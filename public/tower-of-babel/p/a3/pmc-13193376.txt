The tensions between risk and choice: Perspectives of Australian occupational therapists about informed choice when working with older people
Authors: Sathya Viswanathan, Sarah McMullen‐Roach, Scott Weeks, Carolyn M Murray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAged care in Australia is under scrutiny. In 2021, the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety highlighted the need for increased informed choice. Occupational therapists working within residential and community aged care have a responsibility to provide informed choice to their clients. Knowing more about the perspectives of occupational therapists about informed choice with older people will provide insights into practice that may direct future training needs and best practice recommendations. This study therefore aimed to explore perspectives of Australian occupational therapists about informed choice when working with older people.MethodsA qualitative descriptive research design was employed. Through purposive and snowball sampling, 10 Australian occupational therapists who work in the aged care sector were recruited. They worked in both residential and community aged care. Data were collected face‐to‐face and via tele‐conferencing using semi‐structured interviews; data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.Consumer and Community InvolvementConsumers of occupational therapy services were not involved, but this is a recommendation for future research.FindingsThe findings reflected tensions reported in the literature of managing multiple decision‐makers and fluctuating decision‐making capacity alongside professional responsibilities. Theme 1 described methods for raising client awareness of options, negotiating choices, exercising caution, and supporting dignity or risk. Theme 2 described the communication skills employed for promoting informed choice, and Theme 3 described the need for collaborative decision‐making. The final theme outlined the value of workplace policies, procedures, and documents for informed choice.ConclusionOccupational therapists in aged care approach their practice using a person and family centred lens. They employ skilful communication, negotiation, and problem solving. However, tensions exist between managing professional responsibilities around risk and supporting informed choice. Aged care is complex, with ongoing need for training and supervision, and a community of practice for idea sharing and collective problem‐solving.Keywords: advocacy, aged care, decision‐making, dignity of risk, empowerment, informed choice, occupational therapists

INTRODUCTION

Informed choice is essential to health‐care service delivery because it empowers consumers, is collaborative, improves standards of care (Fotaki, 2010, 2013; Ireland‐Blake et al., 2025), and contributes to increased life satisfaction and well‐being (Sikorska‐Simmons, 2007). The concept of ‘informed choice’ is largely underdefined and debated (Harris et al., 2021; Marteau et al., 2001) and differs from informed consent. Informed consent is where the knowledge and expertise of both the health professional and the consumer are shared; after which the risks and benefits of what is being consented to are evaluated, before arriving at a decision (St John et al., 2024). Informed choice on the other hand has two core characteristics: (1) that decisions are made based on quality and sufficient information so that consumers understand the available options, and (2) that decisions are underpinned by the values of the person making the choice (Yuill et al., 2025). Research about informed choice can be found in general medicine, birth care, and mental health (Choi, 2022; O'Brien & Bannigan, 2008; Paterick et al., 2020; Yuill et al., 2025), as well as aged care (Setterlund et al., 2002).

Informed choice is one of the six principles of the Consumer Directed Care (CDC) model, which aims to increase autonomy for recipients of services (Kosiol et al., 2024; McCabe et al., 2018). Autonomy is achieved when there is sufficient flexibility in service provision options for consumers to make decisions based on their values and preferences (Kosiol et al., 2024; Moilanen et al., 2021). From a consumer perspective, informed choice is the process of receiving, understanding, and analysing information to facilitate decision‐making (Davies & Forster, 2022). Sometimes this decision‐making may include choices made in collaboration with family and other stakeholders (shared decision‐making) (Scarinci et al., 2022). In principle, informed choice for older people involves knowing enough about their health situation and the services or treatment options available to independently direct how care is delivered to meet their needs (Zolkefli, 2017). Informed choice in aged care relates to receiving information about systems and structures for in‐home care, residential care, equipment, assistive technology, home modifications, personal care, allied health. and nursing services (Department of Health and Aged Care, 2021).

In aged care, there is growing evidence that the provision of informed choice for older recipients of services has been below expectations (Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety, 2021). This undermining of rights may relate to the vulnerability of the older population, entrenched cultures of ageism and neglect, and systemic issues within aged care services related to funding, staffing, and regulation (Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety, 2019, 2021). In 2018, substandard practices led to the ‘Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety’. Between 2018 and 2019, information and evidence about provision of aged care services were analysed through hearings, community forums, public submissions, and service provider surveys (Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety, 2019, 2021). The final report was issued in May 2021, with 148 recommendations that included improving choice and control, social support, specialist dementia care services, and regulation of restraints. In line with CDC, the Royal Commission placed informed choice and client autonomy as the foundation for many of their recommendations (Department of Health and Aged Care, 2021). With the commencement of the new Australian Aged Care Act (Australian Government, 2024), health professionals and organisations providing aged care services are required to comply with standards where consumer dignity and choice are central (Department of Health, 2021).

Occupational therapists operate under the principle of being client and family centred, which is based on the premise of supporting client autonomy and collaborating with family (Occupational Therapy Board of Australia, 2022). This positions occupational therapists well for supporting and providing informed choice for their older clients, but there are three complexities to consider that require nuanced knowledge and skills (Lawn et al., 2017; Paterson et al., 2005). Firstly, an older person receiving aged care services may be experiencing some cognitive changes, and over 50% of older people living in residential care have a dementia diagnosis (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2025). In this circumstance, the occupational therapist needs to employ strategies to support client memory during decision making and make an assessment of risk (Hoe et al., 2024). Secondly, complexities arise from the presence of multiple decision‐makers (e.g. family) (Ibrahim & Davis, 2013). Finally, there can be competition between the (sometimes parochial) intent of occupational therapists to reduce risk/protect from harm (such as preventing falls) while operationalising person and family centred practice (Durocher et al., 2015; Marsh & Kelly, 2018; Russell et al., 2002). The concept of ‘dignity of risk’ includes recognising and respecting decision‐making abilities, reaching a shared vision for choices and having a firm understanding of legal responsibilities. This approach requires openness to the possibility of positive risk that improves life quality (such as social contact), being able to negotiate, taking a relational perspective, and being prepared for any ethical complexities that may arise (Foundas, 2025; Marsh & Kelly, 2018).

Given the heightened requirement for informed choice for older adults and the complexities described earlier, this research sought to explore the perspectives of Australian occupational therapists about informed choice in aged care. Occupational therapists were asked to provide information about their understanding of informed choice and provide some narratives about their provision of informed choice for their older clients. They were asked about their perspectives on systems, processes, and procedures related to informed choice in their practice. The choices described largely related to older people choosing to receive services (e.g. home modifications), accommodation decisions, and provision of equipment. These findings will inform training needs and any gaps in practice for occupational therapists and make recommendations for strategies that support provision of informed choice with older people. This study therefore aimed to answer the research question:

What are occupational therapists' perspectives about informed choice when working with older people in Australia?.

METHODS

Research design

A qualitative descriptive design was used to explore occupational therapy perspectives (Nayar & Stanley, 2015). This approach was chosen as the research aimed to provide ‘straightforward descriptions’ that remain close to context (Doyle et al., 2020, p. 444; Sandelowski, 2010).

Ethical considerations

Ethics was obtained through the University of South Australia Human Research Ethics Committee (protocol number 205080). The participants received a participant information sheet to read and understand before signing consent. To ensure the anonymity of the participants, only general demographic information is provided, and potentially identifying information has been removed from illustrative quotes.

Sampling and recruitment

Purposive sampling (Patton, 2002) was used to intentionally recruit occupational therapists registered with the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency, who were working in residential or community aged care in Australia at the time of this study and had knowledge and experience in enacting informed choice. The inclusion criteria were deliberately broad to maximise participation. To capture diversity of perspectives, this research aimed for 8–10 participants with variation across employment levels, experience, gender, geographical locations, and employment sectors (Gill, 2020). The study was advertised through social media platforms, such as Facebook, and professional sites, such as LinkedIn and Occupational Therapy Australia. Recruitment also involved inviting occupational therapists within the research teams' professional networks, and snowball sampling through email and word of mouth (Patton, 2002). Participation was entirely voluntary with 10 participants recruited.

Data collection

Data were collected using semi‐structured interviews to capture participants' viewpoints in their language and style (Braun & Clarke, 2013). All interviews were conducted by the first author (SV) between January and July 2023. To provide flexibility for participants' schedules and locations, seven interviews were completed through Zoom Communications™ and three were face‐to‐face.

The first author used a pre‐prepared interview guide (Table 1) with open‐ended questions. The research team developed the questions through discussion and in consultation with literature. Two pilot interviews were conducted with a physiotherapist and a nutritionist who were not eligible to participate in the study; however, they had sufficient experience with informed choice in aged care to provide feedback on the interview guide. This process enabled the first author to hone her interviewing skills and adapt the interview guide in response to feedback and reflection prior to interviewing participants. The interview guide was further revised after reflection from completion of the first interview. Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim by the first author.

Data analysis

Data analysis was completed using six phases of reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022). Firstly, the interview recordings were reviewed several times to ensure transcripts were error‐free (Braun & Clarke, 2022). As well as achieving immersion through transcription, the repeated reading of transcripts sensitised the first author to their content. Next, inductive line‐by‐line coding was conducted using participants' words to stay close to the context where possible (Braun et al., 2019). All transcripts were coded by the first author (SV), with three transcripts coded initially in duplicate by other research team members. Dual coding allowed discussions about different interpretations. The next stage involved the research team sorting coded data into categories to create candidate themes (Braun & Clarke, 2022). The 67 candidate themes were further refined through an iterative process of moving between candidate themes and transcripts to identify patterns across the data. The research team used thematic maps to discuss and further refine themes (Braun & Clarke, 2022). Descriptive summaries of candidate themes were written to ensure there were apparent differences in meanings between developing themes. By the end of this process, each theme had a refined name which described the data represented by the theme (Braun & Clarke, 2022).

Rigour

The credibility of the research was enhanced through regular peer debriefing and reflexivity (Olmos‐Vega et al., 2023; Thomas & Magilvy, 2011). The participants were asked to check individual summaries of their interview transcript to ensure the content resonated with their viewpoints (Johnson et al., 2020; Letts et al., 2007), and they were also provided a copy of the final interpreted themes. A broad inclusion criterion and a reasonably heterogeneous sample supported transferability of the findings (Johnson et al., 2020). Dependability and confirmability were strengthened by maintaining an audit trail of important decisions made throughout the research process and by including participant quotes in the findings (Letts et al., 2007). Procedural rigour was addressed through piloting and practising interview questions before commencing data collection (Letts et al., 2007).

Positionality statement

At the time of the research, the first author (SV) was an occupational therapy honours student who worked part‐time in aged care. Two authors (CMM and SMR) were experienced aged care occupational therapists. SW did not have aged care experience but was interested in the concepts of choice and control in occupational therapy practice. The senior author (CMM) is an experienced qualitative researcher with an interest in both dignity of risk and choice and control. These combined experiences bring some presuppositions in relation to expectations of occupational therapists and the imperative for all older people to be regarded with respect and dignity. To ensure participants were safe to speak openly, all were interviewed by SV who was not known to them, and their identities were not revealed to the research team. Reflexivity and a team approach assisted with managing presuppositions and biases.

FINDINGS

Description of participants

There were 10 participants, including eight females and two males. Four participants worked in residential care settings, five in community settings, and one in both residential and community settings. One participant worked rurally, and five participants held senior positions. The participant experience in the aged care sector varied from 1.5 years to 30 years. The participants were from Western Australia (n = 1), New South Wales (n = 1), and South Australia (n = 8). The participant quotes are used to illustrate themes, and each participant was given a pseudonym and an indication if they worked in community (Comm) or residential aged care (RAC). To protect anonymity, demographic details are not linked to participants.

Overview of themes

Four themes described the perspectives of occupational therapists about informed choice. Overall, these themes reflect the tensions in the literature of managing multiple decision‐makers, cognitive changes in the older person, and professional responsibilities. As a result of these tensions, the participants at times use language suggestive of a power imbalance, which may appear contradictory to their perspectives about informed choice. Overall, informed choice included respecting the right of an older person to have control through providing information, negotiating decisions, and being honest (Theme 1). The second theme described communication skills to enhance informed choice, including provision of clear information, building rapport, sensitivity, and inclusivity. The third theme described participant efforts to collaborate with family and other stakeholders and to advocate for their older clients. The final theme describes policies and procedures that participants used to facilitate informed choice, including risk assessments and documentation processes.

Theme 1 – supporting choice through awareness, negotiation, and caution

This theme had three sub‐themes that described the process of supporting older people to understand the choices available to them. Funds, costs, and risk considerations at times limited available choices for their clients. At times, scenarios arose where clients made choices that participants saw as risky; in which case they were honest and upfront, or attempted to negotiate and/or monitor and observe the situation.

Subtheme 1.1: client awareness of different options

The participants drew on their knowledge and skills to provide relevant information in ways that matched their understanding of the client's health literacy. Sometimes, they used demonstrations and offered different options because they saw this as more empowering than the provision of verbal information alone.

‘I find it's better with the actual item there to trial them than just telling them about it’ (Samuel, Comm)

The participants shared their reasoning for recommendations with clients at the same time as asking clients about their preferences and rationale for these. The participants also asked clients what options they have already tried to avoid making unhelpful suggestions.

Subtheme 1.2: negotiating choices

Where participants found clients choosing options that they believed posed inherent risks to them (e.g. falls risk) or did not align with best practice standards, they would use their skills and knowledge to negotiate and discuss. This conversation often required problem‐solving, thinking outside the square, validating feelings, and providing reassurance.

‘if you can really work with them to show them that you understand, what we're trying to achieve, we understand what that end goal is, they're much more likely to kind of come with you on that journey of exploring all of the options’ (Sonya, Comm)

‘working out what the risks are, and then how can we work around this? How can we make this work if we can … if there's any chance, we will do what we can to make it work and problem solve and come up with solutions’ (Sally, RAC)

‘If a client requests for something that does not fit within policies or standards, I would not go ahead with providing what they are requesting. Instead, I would inform them about other safer alternatives or options. I would also explain the clinical reasoning behind that.’ (Susan, Comm)

Choices discussed were influenced by cost, funding approval, financial affordability, and organisational policies. For example, when considering home modifications, clients were reported to express concerns over the resale value of their house and did not always share the same views as participants about their need for modifications or equipment. Families were often included in discussions and negotiations, which was important but contributed to complexities of negotiating choices (this is further elaborated in Themes 2 and 3).

While participants were strong advocates in supporting informed choice and providing space for discussion and negotiation, they were sometimes conflicted about the need to balance risk and client goals. Bridgette (RAC) used an example of a client who was no longer able to safely use his powered mobility aid in a residential facility (driving too fast and running into people), and so in this case, ‘duty of care’ was enforced ‘for not only him, but also for staff and the other residents involved’ (Bridgette, RAC). Depending on the client's cognitive capacity, the participants reported that the public advocate might become involved with decision‐making.

Subtheme 1.3: being honest, cautious, and risk averse

The participants acknowledged a tension between striving to support client choice and propensity towards risk aversion.

‘[Occupational therapists] have great depth of understanding around what's happening and what could be the potential risks for people, and I think because of that, we tend to err on the side of caution a lot’ (Sally, RAC)

Sophie (Comm) gave an example of a situation where clients have a bed stick or bedrail which is considered ‘high risk’. In this situation, she would respectfully give clients time to think about any alternatives suggested and review them regularly (more information in Theme 4).

‘we just like to keep an eye on and just because you've made a decision, that doesn't have to be your final decision forever’ (Sophie, Comm)

Bridgette (RAC) described another example of balancing between managing risk and safety through closely monitoring the situation and keeping in touch with clients. In this example, the client wanted to access the community in a powered mobility device but had speech difficulties, meaning he could not call for help if needed.

‘We've put stuff [safeguards] in place … the son purchased a, like a GPS tracker … it's only if he becomes stuck and he can push for help … that will send out a signal.’ (Bridgette, RAC)

There was an acknowledgement that in some situations direct communication and honesty was needed regarding concerns about choices made.

‘if I think something … is dangerous, unsafe … I would explain … I'll be honest with you … I'm happy for you to … get a second opinion … but I'm not prepared to do it because of these reasons’. (Thomas, Comm)

Theme 2 – communication is everything

This theme has three subthemes that describe the need for clarity and use of verbal, visual, and non‐verbal communication when working with older people. Building rapport and trust was essential for working out the clients' preferred ways to receive information and allowed for customised communication to suit their preferences and needs. Sensitive and inclusive communication was reported by participants to promote informed choice, which they believed supported psychological well‐being for their clients.

Subtheme 2.1: building rapport and trust

Respecting the right for control was described as ‘the overarching goal of occupational therapy … in aged care’ (Sally, RAC). Building rapport was vital for reaching this goal, connecting with clients and supporting them to feel comfortable and safe. Building rapport was linked to the concept of trust, with Bridgette (RAC) explaining that she would ‘spend time with the person until that person feels comfortable and safe … after we have that connection … then I provide the options.’ The participants believed that for conversations to be effective and honest, clients needed to feel sufficiently connected to their occupational therapist. Having that connection opened a dialogue for negotiation and compromise. Some participants had observed that some older people mistrust health professionals, possibly because of previous negative experiences or worry about being ‘ripped off or … hurt’ (Thomas, Comm). In such circumstances, the participants showed empathy about past experiences and communicated their values and approaches.

‘There is that distrust as much as we like to think we've got great relationships with everyone, and a huge part of what we do is building rapport, ultimately, there are occasions where …, for whatever reason, there is a little bit of mistrust there.’ (Sonya, Comm)

Subtheme 2.2: tailored and sensitive communication

The process of building rapport with clients meant participants could tailor communication to client need depending on their condition, background, literacy levels, and culture. Sensitivity in communication was pivotal to working with the older population, who may feel increasingly powerless with ‘mental health going down’ (Bridgette, RAC). There was a need identified for active listening and empathy for past losses. The participants gave clients time and were careful not to ‘boss them around’ or ‘force’ things (Thomas, Comm).

‘so many losses, so many things taken away … giving that power and choice back gives them that bit more control and that dignity … a bit more self‐esteem rather than feeling like everyone's just coming in and telling me what to do all the time.’ (Sophie, Comm)

As such, the participants aimed to be ‘flexible and adapt how we are providing choices’ (Bridgette, RAC).

‘you need to write things down and you do need to write yes or no questions so that he can actually advocate and speak for himself’ (Amy, RAC)

Bridgett (RAC) also explained the need to check understanding and to not necessarily assume a yes or no response is informed. Providing information through internet links and printed information sheets supported clients to consider their choices in their own time and space. Sending a follow‐up letter to the client as a reminder also supported involving the family, particularly if there were language barriers.

Subtheme 2.3: striving to empower and be inclusive

The participants reported that clients with cognitive changes or language barriers often felt excluded from decision‐making. For this reason, the participants were keen to be inclusive at all stages of informed choice.

‘You have to involve them because I find if you say, “you have to do this”, whether it's professional world, whether it's life, [it] doesn't work, generally very few people like being told you must do this.’ (Thomas, Comm)

The participants explained that they involve older people with mild cognitive impairment in choices through giving sufficient time for them to process information. As such, the participants recommended having inclusive conversations and giving opportunity to demonstrate their understanding of the choices and implications.

‘people will be more accepting of your recommendations and the changes you're making, if it's something that you've consulted on and that fits with them and their lifestyle’ (Rachel, Comm)

Depending on the clients' cognitive capacity, the participants sought consent to gather information from their substitute decision‐makers, which included family members, friends, or neighbours.

‘… they're within their rights to say, I don't want my son knowing, or that's completely fine’ (Sally, RAC)

With this empowerment focus, the participants found the need to advocate for clients to ensure they have a voice when families were not in agreement or did not appear to be acting in the client's best interest. If the client did not speak English, the participants would usually opt to use a professional interpreter rather than family.

‘there's definitely that conflict there within families … our role then actually became educating the family around that decision’ (Amy, RAC)

‘you can sense that some [family] are bit controlling and if I do sense that, then I'll try and talk to the client more and ask them what they think’ (Samuel, Comm)

‘you need to empower the client all the time’ (Thomas, Comm)

Theme 3 – ‘making decisions as a team’

Given the complexities of informed choice with older people experiencing physical and cognitive changes, the participants described efforts to work collaboratively with everyone involved. This theme has two subthemes that describe how the participants saw themselves as advocates, working closely with family members and being mindful of cultural diversity.

Subtheme 3.1: a collaborative process

The participants talked of working in the client's best interest, including recognising client ideas and exploring their goals with due consideration.

‘people have the right to … take risks and do things that are of their choice …’ (Rachel, Comm)

Working collaboratively ensured ‘everybody's on the same page and … aware of those risks … making decisions as a team’ (Sally, RAC). The team included the client, their family/carers, and other health professionals to ensure the client could ask clarifying questions and they were working in the client's best interests.

‘it's always like talking to the person, not about the person, with the family member, but like in collaboration with the family and the resident’ (Maxine, RAC)

The participants regularly met with the client's family and other health professionals involved in their care. This helped to understand the client's background, including medical conditions, available support, support needed, family expectations, and any mismatch between them. Where cognitive changes were an issue, the next of kin or the nominated substitute decision‐maker usually made the final decision.

‘we might know that the person has dementia and often…often it's in a couple. If not, there'll be a son or a daughter …, often where they are present at the interview. So, then they help the client to make an informed choice.’ (Susan, Comm)

Subtheme 3.2: the role of family and culture

The client's family was often pivotal in informed choice and could see the need to balance risk with quality of life.

‘and the daughter in this circumstance said … I'm content for mum to keep making her coffee or tea, it's always been important to her’ (Amy, RAC)

When multiple family members were involved in the decision process, the participants described increasing complexity. This was especially true when clients had cognitive impairment such that they were unable to make decisions for themselves, and family members had different preferences and expectations.

‘she [wife] needed him in there and then the daughter and the son wanted him out and they … were arguing with each other, wanted two completely different things, weren't involving him in any of this decision‐making’ (Bridgette, RAC)

Culture played a part in the family becoming involved in informed choice. The participants described situations where clients from different cultures had complete ‘trust in a particular family member’ (Thomas, Comm) and would only respect decisions if a male family member made them.

Theme 4 – ‘we have to abide by policies and procedures’

This theme consists of three subthemes that describe the policies and procedures that underpin practice for participants. The participants valued training and staying up to date, as well as noting the drive for informed choice through the Australian Aged Care Quality Standards. Even with the focus on informed choice, the participants were required to document risk conversations and to record the discussions, negotiations, and decisions. This finding reflects the ongoing tension and middle ground between health professionals upholding what they see as their professional obligations and upholding their professional values of respecting choice.

Subtheme 4.1: training and referring to evidence

The participants received periodic training in respecting people's space, obtaining consent, legally defensible documentation, home modifications, and tailoring communication. Rachel (Comm) received ‘some training with an external service provider about dignity of risk’. One participant was also part of a social media group about home modifications, through which he improved his awareness of policies and procedures. Another participant accessed government‐based websites and research studies to stay updated about policies and procedures so she could base her practice on the best available evidence.

‘what is the evidence‐based practice here [that can] guide that decision‐making so it's always good to refer back to the evidence’ (Sally, RAC)

Subtheme 4.2: workplace guidance is helpful

The participants spoke of the role of the Aged Care Quality Standards in informing policy to drive practice towards supporting people's choices and goals. Although most participants reported that balancing informed choice with dignity of risk was essential, Bridgette (RAC) describes the tensions in aged care practice that threaten informed choice.

‘we get encouraged to help people make their own decisions, inform choices and … . go ahead with what they want to do, but then at the same time, we also have that duty of care as well, which overrules everything anyway.’ (Bridgette, RAC)

The participants had flow charts within their organisations that guided them on when to involve family or other people and when to document risk conversations.

‘They're there… as a guide often. And because we work for an agency … we have to abide by policies and procedures. The example of the bed sticks is a particularly good one because of the dangers surrounding usage and people have died … none of us want to be responsible for anyone's death.’ (Susan, Comm)

Internal policies and procedures surrounding informed choice were essentially put together based on the Aged Care Quality Standards. Most participants appreciated the role of policies in guiding their practice, especially when the client's choice involved risks. As policies were derived from the Aged Care Quality Standards, the participants found they could use them as a means for defending their decisions and recommendations when discussing options with clients. Although the participants have explained the importance of referring to policies to maintain client safety while providing options, two participants raised ambiguities in policy, such as with scooter use after driver's licence removal (Sally, RAC) and application of public building standards for private dwellings (Samuel, Comm).

Subtheme 4.3: ‘documentation has a big role’

All participants identified the importance of documentation as evidence of conversations about informed choice and associated risks, particularly when negotiation was needed.

‘I think documentation has a big role to play where … reviews are required, where you're in a conversation with someone who may be agreeable in that moment, but you know, in a few months' time might say, … no one told me this.’ (Amy, RAC)

Some participants referred to procedures to document risk conversations using a ‘dignity of risk form’. This form was described as an objective and transparent way of documenting conversations with clients, which is signed by all parties involved in those conversations. In the absence of a designated risk form, these conversations were documented in progress notes. The Aged Care Quality Standards require organisations to have a detailed record of risk conversations for reference if they receive client complaints in the future. The participants described this requirement as worthwhile and improving care standards but also being time‐consuming.

‘I really like that we've just formally implemented that form this year … I think it's a really good way of … giv [ing] people as many opportunities and options as possible, but at the end of the day it's their choice.’ (Sophie, Comm)

DISCUSSION

This research presented four themes that describe perspectives of a sample of occupational therapists working with older people from across both residential and community aged care about informed choice. This research has uncovered how occupational therapists working in aged care settings apply advanced clinical reasoning that involves making professional judgements related to clients' rights to have control (Theme 1), their communication needs (Theme 2), goals (Theme 3), and using tools to guide thinking and uphold professional responsibilities (Theme 4). This reasoning is described by Paterson et al. (2005) as professional artistry.

Ibrahim and Davis (2013) reported that the barriers to enacting the dignity of risk principle in aged care were centred on fluctuating decision‐making ability, having multiple decision‐makers, risk intolerance, and fear about not fulfilling professional responsibilities, which is particularly salient for regulated professions such as occupational therapy. The data collected from10 Australian occupational therapists in 2023 suggest that there has been a shift towards addressing the barriers reported by Ibrahim and Davis (2013). Indeed, the themes developed through this research align to some extent with the solutions to the problem that were proposed by Ibrahim and Davis (2013). These solutions focused on recognising and respecting clients' rights to make decisions (even with fluctuating decision‐making ability), having a shared vision among the team around the client (including family) and the need to manage risk through clear and reasonable actions and processes (Ibrahim & Davis, 2013). However, there is still some way to go to break down parochial cultures and power structures in aged care (Department of Health and Aged Care, 2021; Magee et al., 2025).

Theme 1 highlights the complexities for occupational therapists for providing services to older people that respectfully raise client awareness of the choices available to them while negotiating these choices against the backdrop of a regulated environment and profession. The participants expressed awareness of possible fluctuating decision‐making capacity of clients and the need for continuous assessment concurrent with supporting dignity, empowerment, autonomy, and respect (Zhang et al., 2024). In most cases, the participants explained how they negotiated choices to minimise risk and maximise client control, showcasing highly skilled occupational therapy practice arising from a basis in problem‐solving and collaboration (Laver et al., 2017; Maywald & Stanley, 2014). This finding reflects the ongoing tension and shifting middle ground between health professionals upholding what they see as their professional obligations and upholding their professional values of respecting choice. To further streamline and improve practices, and support the occupational therapy aged care workforce, there is an imperative for greater emphasis on and funding towards increasing the uptake of advanced care planning for decision‐making.

In Theme 2, the participants saw rapport building as a cornerstone for informed choice, and with rapport came the often taken‐for‐granted skill of communication (English et al., 2022). Consistent with recommended processes for communication, the participants gave clients sufficient time to process information using multiple means of conveying information (Moghimi, 2007). Tailoring communication to the client's needs improves absorption of information (Jetha et al., 2021), which was also observed to occur by participants in this research. Similarly, providing good quality written information can support informed choice through giving opportunity to discuss with others and review options in their own time (Baxter et al., 2008; Woolf et al., 2005). Developing trust was another skill presented in Theme 2, closely related to respectful interactions. Evidence suggests that when communication fosters trust, health literacy is enhanced (Brooks et al., 2017).

The findings in Theme 3 in relation to working as a team were encouraging for the implementation of CDC and the application of the Aged Care Quality Standards (Gill et al., 2018). Direct relationships are reported in the literature between enacting informed choice and supporting dignity through combining knowledges and experiences (client, family, and health professional) (Castro et al., 2016; Moghimi, 2007). Working collaboratively creates a safe environment with everyone being aware and contributing to the decision‐making process and goals (Copley et al., 2008; Kuipers et al., 2004; Levack et al., 2009; Tang Yan et al., 2014). Historically, families have been known to get frustrated by the lack of information and inclusivity from health professionals (Petriwskyj et al., 2014). As family are likely to have more contact with the older person, their involvement may improve informed choice through increasing health literacy, client satisfaction, and positive therapy outcomes (Ross & McLaughlin, 2006). However, it must be acknowledged that family involvement is not always constructive because of complex relationships and the potential vulnerability of older people (Arendts et al., 2015; Setterlund et al., 2002).

In Theme 4, the participants explained the systemic, procedural, and political considerations that influence the way they negotiate and direct choices. These considerations included the client's funding systems, financial affordability, and organisational policies. This finding is somewhat consistent with that of Calderone et al. (2022), who found that funding models in residential aged care restricted practice to such an extent that ethical concerns were raised. Similarly, Woolf et al. (2005) found that health professionals were experiencing little time for detailed discussions about informed choice, which could present barriers to client outcomes. The participants appreciated being offered training as a valuable aspect of keeping up to date and seemed to engage positively with organisational tools and processes in place to support them with enacting informed choice. However, there is still a necessity for further training and critical conversations about methods for and tensions related to upholding what are perceived to be professional responsibilities alongside provision of informed choice and autonomy. Past research has raised a lack of training and supervision as an issue (Calderone et al., 2022; William et al., 2013), and this is an area for further consideration and monitoring, particularly for less experienced occupational therapists.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

Although the study aimed to gather perspectives from occupational therapists in all states within Australia, most participants were from South Australia and worked in metropolitan areas. A strength of the sample was the mix of occupational therapists working in community and residential aged care and a mix of gender. As most of the interviews were conducted via Zoom, some of the body language nuances may have been missed. Member checking of the interpreted findings occurred with participants with no requests for changes and feedback that the findings resonated with their experiences. When sharing their perspectives, the participants were not explicitly asked to differentiate between informed choice for people with and without cognitive impairment, as this was beyond the scope of this study.

RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

Future research is recommended that captures consumer and family experiences of receiving informed choice in aged care. There is also scope to explore perspectives of other allied health disciplines. It may be useful to undertake a more nuanced exploration that provides insights into different perspectives about informed choice depending on decision‐making capacity. This research could align with a document or policy analysis of documents or guides that inform practice, such as the dignity of risk tools and other guides that were referred to by the participants.

IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

This research has highlighted the need for occupational therapists to have sufficient time to work collaboratively with clients and their families and to perform the professional artistry (Paterson et al., 2005) required for skilful communication, negotiation, and problem‐solving. There is also an ongoing need for training and supervision and a community of practice for idea‐sharing and collective problem‐solving. It is necessary for occupational therapists and other health professionals to continue having conversations about ways to implement dignity of risk at the same time as upholding professional values and responsibilities when working with older people.
