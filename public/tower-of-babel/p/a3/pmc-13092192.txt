Bidirectional effects of cuminaldehyde–pepsin interaction on enzymatic and antioxidant activity: Potential implications for gastrointestinal health and disorder modulation
Authors: Yunqi Li, Jinping Li, Jianjian Yin, Yingying Wang, Zhen Zhang, Enqiang Linghu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPepsin is the primary proteolytic enzyme in the stomach and plays a pivotal role in gastric protein digestion, with dysregulation closely associated with gastrointestinal functional disorders. Cuminaldehyde, a dietary phytochemical abundant in Cuminum cyminum (cumin), is widely consumed and recognized for its antioxidant and gastrointestinal bioactivities. In this study, we systematically investigated the interaction between cuminaldehyde and pepsin under physiologically relevant conditions to elucidate its implications for gastric digestion, antioxidant function, and gastrointestinal health. Fluorescence quenching decreased with rising temperature (KSV 1.04 × 105 to 0.19 × 105 M−1; kq 1.04 to 0.19 × 1013 M−1 s−1 from 298 to 316 K). Binding constants increased (logKa 4.54–4.98) with binding sites (n) 0.87–1.13. Thermodynamic parameters (ΔH° = 42.59 kJ/mol; ΔS° = 229.74 J/mol·K; ΔG° = -25.86 to -30.00 kJ/mol) indicated spontaneous interaction. Secondary structure analysis showed β-sheet reduction (51.23% to 41.44%) and random coil increase (27.67% to 36.68%). Furthermore, kinetic studies showed that activation energy (Ea) increased (37.33 to 40.23 kJ/mol), while Vmax decreased (0.2419 to 0.1156 μmol/min/mL), confirming inhibitory effects of cuminaldehyde against pepsin. Importantly, the interaction also modulated the functional properties of cuminaldehyde itself, as its radical-scavenging capacity assessed by DPPH and ABTS assays was attenuated upon binding, likely due to hydrogen-bond interactions involving Gly-109. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations confirmed the stability of the cuminaldehyde–pepsin complex and revealed localized flexibility changes within the enzyme structure. In conclusion, these findings demonstrate a bidirectional interaction in the gastric environment, whereby cuminaldehyde modulates pepsin-mediated protein digestion while its antioxidant activity is partially compromised through enzyme binding. This study provides mechanistic insight into phytochemical–enzyme interactions during digestion and suggests potential implications for dietary regulation of gastric function and gastrointestinal disorder modulation.Keywords: Cuminaldehyde, Pepsin, Gastric digestion, Antioxidant activity, Phytochemical–protein interaction, Gastrointestinal health, Enzyme inhibition

Introduction

It is important to investigate the interactions of bioactive compounds with digestive proteases—such as pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin, and other proteinases—to gain essential preliminary information on their metabolism, bioavailability, and distribution in the body, which are critical for evaluating their therapeutic efficacy and informing dosage formulations. Among these enzymes, pepsin plays a central role in gastric protein digestion; however, excessive pepsin activity in the stomach can contribute to gastrointestinal disorders, particularly duodenal ulcers. Modulation of pepsin structure and activity has therefore been proposed as a potential strategy for mitigating pepsin-associated gastric damage. Both hydrochloric acid and pepsin are known to contribute to ulcer formation (Khan, Arora, Kalra, & Arora, 2022), highlighting the clinical relevance of regulating pepsin activity.

The interaction of ligands with pepsin can result in some changes in the structure and activity of this enzyme. For example, Zeng et al. studied the interaction of chlorogenic acid to pepsin by different spectral and molecular docking studies (Zeng, Liang, You, & Qu, 2014). They showed that the fluorescence quenching of pepsin upon interaction with chlorogenic acid was based a static mechanism mediated by hydrophilic forces with a binding constant (Ka) of 1.18 × 105 L/mol at 288 K and the number of binding sites (n) of ∼ 1. The results of the activity assay indicated that chlorogenic acid can inhibit pepsin activity in vitro (Zeng et al., 2014). Molecular docking and multi-spectroscopy analyses on the binding mechanisms of aloe-emodin to pepsin also demonstrated a static quenching mechanism, contribution of hydrophilic forces, the conformational changes of the enzyme, and reduction of pepsin activity down to around 40% with aloe-emodin to pepsin at a molar ratio of 9:1 (Ren et al., 2019). Recently, the investigation of the interaction of apigenin to pepsin by spectroscopic, theoretical, and enzyme activity analyses revealed a static quenching of pepsin, a spontaneous interaction, microenvironmental and structural changes of pepsin, and inhibition of pepsin activity with inhibition rate of around 50% at apigenin concentration of 200 μM (Guo, Gan, Cheng, Cui, & Yi, 2023).

Cuminaldehyde (C10H12O, 4-isopropylbenzaldehyde) is a benzaldehyde derivative with an isopropyl substituent at the para position, widely found as a major component in the cumin plant (Cuminum cyminum) (Singh, Gangadharappa, & Mruthunjaya, 2017). Cuminaldehyde has shown different biological and pharmacological activities, including anticancer, antibacterial, and antioxidant properties (Ghiasi et al., 2021). Moreover, it has been reported that cuminaldehyde with its unique structure can be used as a natural aldehyde to inhibit protein aggregation and associated cytotoxicity (Micsonai et al., 2015). It has also been demonstrated that cuminaldehyde can act as a potential inhibitor of aldose reductase and α-glucosidase (Lee, 2005).

Investigating the direct interaction of pepsin with small bioactive molecules, such as cuminaldehyde, is necessary to understand how these compounds might modulate enzyme function and, consequently, gastrointestinal health. Notably, functional groups of cuminaldehyde that participate in complex formation with pepsin may experience diminished antioxidant activity as a result of enzyme binding (Guo et al., 2023). Similar mechanisms have been reported for other dietary antioxidants: for instance, apigenin, a flavonoid present in vegetables and herbal spices, exhibits reduced antioxidant activity upon interaction with pepsin, mediated by hydrogen bonding between its hydroxyl groups and pepsin amino acid residues (Guo et al., 2023). Aloe-emodin, another bioactive compound, has been shown to undergo comparable activity attenuation upon binding to pepsin (Ren et al., 2019).

Therefore, in this study, we investigated the mechanism of interaction between the dietary phytochemical cuminaldehyde and the digestive enzyme pepsin using multiple complementary approaches as well as theoretical analyses. Another aspect of the study design was to assess the antioxidant activity of cuminaldehyde in both its free form and when bound to pepsin, using established radical-scavenging assays. These experiments were aimed at elucidating the quenching mechanism, binding constants, binding sites, thermodynamic parameters, structural alterations in pepsin, and the effect of pepsin binding on the antioxidant activity of cuminaldehyde. Collectively, this approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the bidirectional interaction between cuminaldehyde and pepsin, with implications for protein digestion, antioxidant modulation, and gastrointestinal health.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pepsin from porcine gastric mucosa (Catalog No. P7012; lyophilized powder; ≥2500 U/mg protein) and cuminaldehyde (Catalog No. 135178; purity 98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Pepsin was dissolved in disodium hydrogen phosphate–citric acid buffer (pH 2.0) to mimic gastric conditions. The concentration of pepsin was determined by measuring absorbance at 280 nm using a molar extinction coefficient of ε₂₈₀ = 51,715 M−1·cm−1, as previously reported (Ying et al., 2015). A stock solution of cuminaldehyde (1 mM) was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). In all experiments, the final concentration of DMSO did not exceed 0.3% (v/v), a level verified to have no detectable effect on pepsin structure or enzymatic activity under the experimental conditions. All other reagents were of analytical grade, and deionized water was used throughout the experiments.

Fluorescence spectroscopic analyses

Fluorescence spectra were recorded on a fluorescence spectrometer (Shimadzu RF-5301 PC Spectrofluorophotometer, Japan) equipped with a 1.0 cm quartz cell, using an excitation wavelength of 295 nm. The slit widths of excitation and emission were fixed at 5.0 nm and 10.0 nm, respectively. A water bath was used to control the temperature of the system at four different temperatures of 298, 304, 310, and 316 to cover a physiologically relevant range. A 2.0 mL aliquot of 2 μM pepsin solution was titrated with incremental additions of cuminaldehyde solution (final concentrations 2–60 μM). The inner filter effect due to cuminaldehyde absorbance was corrected for using the method described previously (Bakar & Feroz, 2019). Synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy (SFS) analyses

Pepsin and cuminaldehyde concentrations were identical to those used for intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy (Section 2.2). The synchronous fluorescence spectra of pepsin with increasing concentrations of cuminaldehyde solution were recorded on a fluorescence spectrometer. The wavelength interval (Δλ) between excitation and emission was set to 15 nm or 60 nm to probe the microenvironment around Tyr or Trp residues, respectively. The excitation and emission slit widths were set to 5.0 nm and 10.0 nm, respectively. Spectra were corrected by subtracting the buffer blank.

CD measurements

Far-UV CD spectra (200–260 nm) of pepsin with and without cuminaldehyde were recorded using a Jasco J-810 spectropolarimeter (Japan). Samples containing pepsin alone or pepsin-cuminaldehyde complexes (molar ratios 1:1 and 1:30) were prepared. Spectra were corrected by subtracting the corresponding buffer and cuminaldehyde blanks. Secondary structure content was calculated from the CD spectra using CDNN software (Micsonai et al., 2015).

UV–Vis measurements

UV–Vis absorption spectra (250–300 nm) of pepsin with and without cuminaldehyde were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-2501PC spectrophotometer (Japan). A 2 μM pepsin solution was titrated with cuminaldehyde (final concentrations 2–60 μM), and spectra were recorded at 310 K. Spectra were corrected against corresponding buffer and cuminaldehyde blanks.

Enzymatic activity evaluation

The catalytic activity of pepsin was assessed using the methodology described in previous reports (Guo et al., 2023; Tong, Prasanna, Zhang, & Jing, 2020). Briefly, pepsin (5 μM) was mixed with two concentrations of cuminaldehyde (10–60 μM) in a citrate phosphate buffer (pH 2.2) and incubated at 310 K for 30 min. No significant degradation of cuminaldehyde can be observed over 30 min (the incubation time used in our enzyme assay). 1.00 mL of casein solution in the range of 0.5–6 g/L was then added to the samples and incubated at 310 K for 10 min, followed by the addition of 2.00 mL of trichloroacetic acid and Fulin reagent, and incubated at 310 K for 20 min. Finally, the absorbance of the samples was read using an UV–Vis spectrophotometer at 680 nm.

Antioxidant assay

The antioxidant activity of cuminaldehyde was determined using ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging assays, as described previously (Guo et al., 2023). Therefore, the antioxidant activity of cuminaldehyde was assessed using “2,2-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS) radical scavenging and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay” (Guo et al., 2023). For this assay, pepsin (25 μM) was added by various concentrations of cuminaldehyde (0, 2, 5, 10, 20 and 30 μM). Then, ABTS scavenging activity of cuminaldehyde was assessed (Guo et al., 2023; Lo, Leung, Fedrizzi, & Barker, 2021). Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation studies

Docking studies were performed using AutoDock software. The crystal structure of pepsin was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 3PEP). The three-dimensional structure of cuminaldehyde (PubChem CID: 326) was downloaded from the PubChem database. The protein receptor was treated as rigid, while cuminaldehyde was treated as flexible during docking. The binding site was analyzed using Discovery Studio and ChimeraX softwares.

MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS package (Lindahl, Hess, & Van Der Spoel, 2001) with the Amber-99 force field (Chen & Pappu, 2007) and the TIP4P water model (Horn et al., 2004). The pepsin and pepsin–cuminaldehyde systems were enclosed in a triclinic simulation box, maintaining a minimum of 1.0 nm between the protein surface and box edges. The systems were solvated with explicit water molecules, and appropriate counterions were introduced to neutralize the charge. Initial energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent method. Equilibration was performed under constant volume and temperature (NVT) for 100 ps using a modified Berendsen thermostat (V-rescale) to maintain the temperature at 310 K, followed by a constant pressure and temperature (NPT) phase for an additional 100 ps, regulated with the Parrinello–Rahman barostat to maintain 1 bar pressure. Following equilibration, production MD runs were performed for 150 ns using a 2 fs integration time step. Trajectory analyses were performed using standard GROMACS utilities, with structural visualization and inspection carried out in VMD and PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. Statistical differences were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Fluorescence quenching spectra of pepsin

Pepsin contains 5 Trp and 16 Tyr amino acid residues, which contribute significantly to the intrinsic fluorescence of pepsin (Shen et al., 2015). The structure of pepsin is analyzed by fluorescence emission intensity. Fig. 1a displays the fluorescence spectra of pepsin in the presence of increasing concentrations of cuminaldehyde at 298 K.

The fluorescence quenching of pepsin at a concentration of 2 μM titrated with different concentrations of cuminaldehyde solution at 298 K (a). The plots F0 /F against [Q] (b), the plots of log[(F0 − F)/F] against log[Q] (c), the plot of lnKb against 1/T (d).

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, with the continuous titration of cuminaldehyde, the fluorescence intensities of pepsin gradually decrease, indicating that cuminaldehyde may interact with pepsin, resulting in the quenching of the intrinsic fluorescence of the protein. This indicates that, microenvironmental changes around the chromophores of pepsin (Trp and Tyr) may occur following the titration of cuminaldehyde (Shen et al., 2015).

The intrinsic fluorescence intensity of proteins can be quenched by several known intermolecular effects, such as collisional interactions and complex formation in the ground state. Such reductions in fluorescence intensity are usually classified as dynamic, static, or mixed quenching. The dynamic quenching mechanism is regarded as a diffusion-mediated interaction between the receptor in the excited state and a small molecule acting as a quencher, without significant photon emission. The static quenching mechanism, on the other hand, primarily arises from the formation of nonfluorescent receptor-ligand complexes in the ground state. Nevertheless, molecular interaction between the receptor and the ligand is necessary in all types of quenching mechanisms. For static quenching, the quenching constants decrease as the temperature of the solution increases, while, in the case of dynamic quenching, the opposite phenomenon is observed (Silva, Cortez, & Louro, 2004). The well-known Stern-Volmer plot typically reveals a linear relationship during either dynamic or static quenching mechanisms (Ciotta, Prosposito, & Pizzoferrato, 2019). Moreover, protein fluorescence intensity can be quenched in the presence of the quencher through a combination of collisions and formation of receptor-ligand complexes, depending on the situation (Ciotta et al., 2019). In this case, the Stern–Volmer plot exhibits a positive curvature at high concentrations of the quencher (Ciotta et al., 2019).

Generally, the Stern-Volmer Eq. (4) could be used to determine the quenching mechanism (Shen et al., 2015).

The corresponding data are tabulated in Table S1.

It is important to distinguish between the KSV and the Ka when analyzing the interaction between cuminaldehyde and pepsin. The KSV value reflects the efficiency of quenching and provides insight into the dynamic or static quenching mechanism. In contrast, the Ka value represents the actual binding affinity between the ligand (cuminaldehyde) and the protein (pepsin), reflecting the strength and stability of the formed complex.

At 310 K the measured Ka (∼104 M−1) corresponds to a dissociation constant Kd ≈ 10−5 M (≈18 μM). Typical gastric pepsin concentrations are in the low tens of micromolar (≈0.5–1 mg·mL−1 ≈ 15–30 μM), so from a purely thermodynamic standpoint the affinity is in the physiologically relevant range and could result in potential interaction under physiological concentrations.

The results indicate that the logKa for the interaction of cuminaldehyde with pepsin increases with the increasing temperature of solution, which may suggest that the formed pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex is more stable at higher temperatures than lower ones. In other words, the affinity of pepsin for cuminaldehyde is low at 298 K and can be increased by raising the temperature of the system. These temperature-related changes in logKa values can be associated with the n values on protein per ligand. The n values increased from 0.87 ± 0.04 at 298 K to 1.13 ± 0.06 at 316 K, revealing that the partial conformational changes of pepsin at high temperatures could facilitate the interaction of cuminaldehyde with pepsin and improve the corresponding binding affinity. The observed fractional values of the n likely reflect an average binding behavior across all enzyme molecules in the system rather than indicating a literal fractional binding site on a single pepsin molecule. This phenomenon is common in spectroscopic binding studies and can result from factors such as heterogeneous binding environments, partial occupancy, or conformational flexibility of the enzyme.

Synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy study

Synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy is regarded as a sensitive method for investigating protein conformational changes because it allows us to study ligand-protein interactions in a non-intrusive manner at low concentrations and, more importantly, under physiological conditions. It is probably useful for probing microenvironmental changes around intrinsic fluorophores, eliminating the need for extrinsic probes and their potential complications (Wani et al., 2021). Trp and Tyr residues are extremely sensitive to the local microenvironment and show an obvious spectral shift (Wani et al., 2021). Therefore, the position of the λma likely depends on the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding these residues, and the fluorescence intensity relates to the extent of exposure. As a result, it may serve as a potential tool for studying protein structure and conformational changes after interaction with substrates.

Therefore, the effect of cuminaldehyde on the synchronous fluorescence spectrum of pepsin is exhibited in Fig. 2.

Synchronous fluorescence spectra of pepsin with various concentrations of cuminaldehyde. (a) Δλ = 15 nm and (b) Δλ = 60 nm; [pepsin] = 2 μM; [cuminaldehyde] = 0 μM–60 μM.

As displayed in Fig. 2a (Δλ = 15 nm), the λmax of pepsin underwent a red shift from 285 nm to 295 nm as the cuminaldehyde concentration increased from 0 to 60 μM. Similarly, for Δλ = 60 nm (Fig. 2b), λmax exhibited a significant red shift from 280 nm to 286 nm over the same cuminaldehyde concentration range.

The observed red shifts may indicate conformational changes in pepsin upon cuminaldehyde binding. The shifts towards longer wavelengths may signify an increase in the polarity and a decrease in the hydrophobicity of the microenvironment surrounding both Tyr and Trp residues. These results support the intrinsic fluorescence quenching data, suggesting the possible involvement of aromatic residues (Tyr, Trp) in the interaction with cuminaldehyde and the resultant alterations in their local microenvironments.

CD spectroscopy measurement

CD spectroscopy, a conventional biophysical method, is commonly employed to explore the secondary and tertiary configurations of proteins (Sekowski et al., 2014; Ying et al., 2015). Peptide bonds show ellipticity changes in the far-UV CD in the range of 200–260 nm (Ying et al., 2015). Therefore, far-UV CD spectroscopy was used to monitor changes in the secondary structure content of pepsin upon interaction with cuminaldehyde. The CD spectrum of pepsin (Fig. 3a) shows a minimum around 208 nm, which is in good agreement with a previous study (Hashemi-Shahraki, Shareghi, Farhadian, & Yadollahi, 2023), revealing a substantial quantity of β-sheet and unordered structures.

CD (a) and UV–Vis (b) spectra of pepsin in the presence of varying concentrations of cuminaldehyde at 310 K.

The native structure of pepsin is composed of 51.23% β-sheet, 5.89% α-helix, 27.67% random coil, and 15.21% β-turn. (Table S2).

After interacting with cuminaldehyde, the CD spectrum of pepsin displayed a significant change, indicating the unfolding of pepsin (Hashemi-Shahraki, Shareghi, Farhadian & Yadollahi, 2023). With the addition of various concentrations of cuminaldehyde, the α-helix and β-sheet contents decreased, while the random coil contents significantly increased. Therefore, pepsin adopted a more unfolded structure with the addition of cuminaldehyde due to the increase in the contents of random coil structures. This outcome indicated that cuminaldehyde could bind to pepsin and reduce the secondary structure fractions of pepsin by disrupting the H-bond networks (Hashemi-Shahraki, Shareghi, Farhadian & Yadollahi, 2023).

The UV–Vis absorption studies are a feasible approach for investigating structural changes and the formation of protein-ligand complexes. Fig. 3b illustrates the alteration in the UV–Vis absorption spectrum of pepsin both with and without cuminaldehyde. The pepsin absorption spectrum displays a peak around 277 nm, corresponding to π → π* transition of aromatic amino acid residues in pepsin (Shen et al., 2015). Upon gradual addition of cuminaldehyde, the absorption peak at 277 nm increases in intensity and undergoes a red shift. This hyperchromicity with a red shift suggests decreased hydrophobicity and increased exposure of aromatic residues in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex (Shen et al., 2015). This data supports the fluorescence quenching data that static quenching occurs during the interaction of cuminaldehyde and pepsin. Also, the structural changes of pepsin around aromatic residues upon interaction with cuminaldehyde align with the findings from synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy. In summary, cuminaldehyde can be regarded as an important small molecule in initiating changes to the secondary structure of pepsin.

Pepsin is a digestive enzyme that hydrolyzes proteins. Its catalytic activity is highly dependent on its three-dimensional structure and the integrity of its active site (Guo et al., 2023).

Both the structure of pepsin and the specific catalytic amino acid residues are essential for its enzymatic function. Small molecules with diverse structures can interact with pepsin through various types of intermolecular forces, potentially blocking the enzyme's active site and inhibiting its function.

We performed global nonlinear fits to competitive, noncompetitive and mixed models. The data fit best with a noncompetitive/ mixed inhibition mechanism. These data indicate that, under our assay conditions, inhibition is dominated by reversible binding rather than stable covalent modification.

Spectroscopic experiments confirmed that cuminaldehyde binding induces microenvironmental changes around critical aromatic residues. Since some of these residues are located near or within the active site, these changes likely contribute to the observed reduction in pepsin catalytic activity. For example, modification of Tyr residues in aspergillopepsin A by p-nitrophenyldiazonium chloride results in a significant drop in enzymatic activity (Tarasova, Kavrenova, & Stepanov, 1981). Additionally, Tyr and Trp residues are recognized as key amino acids in the catalytic site of pepsin, providing hydrogen bonding interactions with water molecules and adjacent residues. Therefore, microenvironmental changes around these residues can significantly affect pepsin's catalytic function. To gain atomic-level insights into the interaction between cuminaldehyde and pepsin and to elucidate the potential mechanism of inhibition, molecular docking studies were performed.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking studies have been widely used to assess favorable binding sites and the types of binding forces. The secondary structure of pepsin is predominantly β-sheet. Additionally, the catalytic site of pepsin is located at the junction of its domains. Therefore, ligand binding near these residues can significantly alter enzyme structure and function. A total of 200 docked conformations of the pepsin–cuminaldehyde complex were generated using molecular docking. Among the predicted binding conformations, the top-ranked pose—characterized by the most favorable binding energy and critical interactions, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts within pepsin's active site—was chosen for further analysis.

The specific interactions stabilizing the complex include hydrogen bonding (Gly-109), pi-sigma (Tyr-75), pi-alkyl (Tyr-75, Trp-39), and alkyl (Leu-112, Ile-120) interactions, suggesting a major contribution from hydrophobic interactions.

In pepsin-inhibitor complexes, water molecules establish H-bond with the −OH group of the conserved residue Tyr-75, and the −OH group of Tyr-75 itself accepts a H-bond from the Nε1 atom of Trp-39 residue. These bonds create a continuous sequence of H-bond residues, Trp 39–Tyr 75–H2O–Ser 35–Asp 32 (active site), connecting the flap to the pepsin catalytic site.

Disruption of the H-bond network involving Tyr-75 (e.g., displacement of the water molecule or altered positioning relative to Trp-39) can increase the distance between the catalytic residues Ser 35 and Asp 32, hindering their interaction and thus inhibiting catalytic activity. Therefore, structural changes of pepsin by cuminaldehyde and the movement of Tyr-75 and Trp-39 residues could be potential reasons for inhibiting the pepsin activity (Andreeva & Rumsh, 2001). These experiments align with enzyme inhibition and synchronous fluorescence analyses of pepsin following the introduction of cuminaldehyde, which illustrated the structural alterations of pepsin near Trp and Tyr residues alongside the inhibition of enzyme function. Thus, the data indicate that the interaction of cuminaldehyde with amino acid residues near the catalytic site of pepsin could be responsible for the inhibition of pepsin activity. However, the binding affinity between the ligand and pepsin is relatively low. Therefore, competitive inhibition may not be the most appropriate or dominant inhibition mechanism in this system. Kinetic modeling suggests a mixed/noncompetitive mechanism, although docking indicates proximity to the active site; therefore, inhibition likely involves both catalytic interference and allosteric effects.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation studies

The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) parameter is typically used in MD studies to explore the structural changes of proteins and protein-ligand complexes over time. Therefore, the stability of pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex in comparison with pepsin-water system during simulation time (150 ns) was evaluated by RMSD analysis. The RMSD plots of pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex and free pepsin are exhibited in Fig. 7a. As for pepsin, from 25 ns to 90 ns, the RMSD remained stable, oscillating between 0.2 and 0.35 nm, reflecting structural rigidity. A notable deviation occurred between 90 ns and 105 ns, where RMSD increased sharply, peaking at 0.52 nm, likely due to transient conformational changes. Subsequently, from 110 ns to 150 ns, the system reached stability, with RMSD fluctuating moderately between 0.3 and 0.4 nm. For the pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex, after an initial equilibration phase (first 37 ns), where RMSD rose to ∼0.28–0.46 nm, the system stabilized between 38 ns and 150 ns, with RMSD values fluctuating within a narrow range of 0.32–0.61 nm. This suggests that the presence of cuminaldehyde induces increased conformational flexibility in the pepsin structure during the MD simulation, potentially due to ligand binding interactions or conformational changes in the enzyme. Specifically, the RMSD of the pepsin–cuminaldehyde complex was found to be higher than that of pepsin alone, which indicates that the pepsin structure experiences greater fluctuations or destabilization when bound to cuminaldehyde. These fluctuations could reflect the binding-induced alterations in the protein's active site or the structural rearrangements of the enzyme to accommodate the ligand. This observation is in good agreement with the spectroscopic data, which suggests that the interaction of pepsin with cuminaldehyde leads to changes in the protein's stability and dynamic properties, supporting the hypothesis of a binding-induced conformational change or partial unfolding of pepsin in the presence of the ligand. The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis can be used to explore the backbone flexibility of proteins after interaction with ligands (Dehdasht-Heidari, Shareghi, Farhadian, & Momeni, 2021). As presented in Fig. 7b, the pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex has higher RMSF values than pepsin around residue numbers of 11, 130, 242, 253, and 317. Also, RMSF values around the residue numbers of 39, 75, 78, 79, 80, 108, 109, 111, 112, 117 and 120 (residues in the binding site) were lower for pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex compared to pepsin. These data show that binding to cuminaldehyde increases the flexibility of specific regions in pepsin, particularly near the N- and C-termini, while decreasing flexibility in and around the binding site residues. The increased flexibility in certain regions, coupled with decreased flexibility in the binding site, supports the notion of cuminaldehyde-induced partial unfolding or conformational destabilization of pepsin, consistent with the CD spectroscopy results. The relatively higher solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) fluctuation in the pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex relative to free pepsin (Fig. 7c) indicates that cuminaldehyde interaction may increase solvent accessibility of some specific residues (regions), potentially reducing the stability of the active site or some other domains. These data align well with prior experimental findings that cuminaldehyde interaction increased pepsin unfolding, which is a possible reason for the decrease in functional activity and stability.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation study for the interaction between pepsin and cuminaldehyde over 150 ns, (a) RMSD, (b) RMSF, (c) Rg, (d) SASA, (e) H-bond analyses.

The radius of gyration (Rg) is also an important factor indicating the compactness and folding state of proteins, which can be examined through MD simulation research (Dehdasht-Heidari, Shareghi, Farhadian, & Momeni, 2021). Rg plots for free pepsin and pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex are shown in Fig.7d. Pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex displayed an average Rg of 2.12 ± 0.03 nm, with fluctuations ranging from 1.98 nm (initial compact state) to 2.24 nm, indicating structural flexibility. The pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex also showed infrequent increases in Rg, such as a transient expansion around 24 ns, suggesting conformational unfolding. In contrast, the pepsin-water system exhibited lower average Rg of 2.05 ± 0.04 nm, with values oscillating between 2.02 nm and 2.18 nm. Therefore, pepsin in the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex exhibits higher average Rg and greater fluctuations than free pepsin. These data indicate that cuminaldehyde interactions decreased pepsin's compactness, potentially unfolding specific conformations and increasing flexibility, which is in good agreement with spectroscopic data. Fig. 7e illustrates the outcome of H-bond analysis between pepsin and cuminaldehyde conducted during 150 ns MD simulation. This information can offer valuable insights into the stability and occurrence of H-bond formation. The H-bond network was calculated using standard criteria, where a hydrogen bond was defined by a donor–acceptor distance of ≤3.5 Å and an angle ≥135°. The H-bond network observed between pepsin and cuminaldehyde varied from 0 to 2, indicating the minimal involvement of H-bonds in the formation of the pepsin-cuminaldehyde complex. Experimental findings indicated that hydrophobic interactions play a primary role in the interaction between pepsin and cuminaldehyde, while hydrogen bonds partially contribute to the formation of this complex. The structural stability of the pepsin–cuminaldehyde complex was further supported by initial and final frames (Fig. S1a), along with the superimposed snapshots taken at regular intervals throughout the simulation (Fig. S1b), which revealed minimal displacement of the ligand within the binding pocket. Based on the distance plot (Fig. S1c) showing the distance between cuminaldehyde and key pepsin residues over 150 ns of MD simulation, a sustained distance ≤1 nm indicates cuminaldehyde forms a stable complex with pepsin.

More specifically, cuminaldehyde consistently remained near pepsin residues (distance <0.4 nm), suggesting stable interactions critical for binding, potentially involving hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic contacts. In contrast, greater distance fluctuations (0.4–1.0 nm) may reflect transient or dynamic interactions. The persistent close interaction implies potential role of specific residues in anchoring the cuminaldehyde within a specific binding pocket, while the dynamic behavior of other residues highlights the flexibility of the complex interface. Notably, no residues sustained distances >1.0 nm, confirming the cuminaldehyde's sustained occupancy near the active site region throughout the simulation.

This aligns with ideal enzyme-inhibitor binding geometry and may suggest potential relevance that warrants further validation.

Antioxidant activity of cuminaldehyde

Aromatic aldehyde compounds have shown potential antioxidant activity (Cahyana, Liandi, Safitri, & Yunarti, 2020; Murti, Goswam, & Mishra, 2013). Cuminaldehyde could regulate hepatic lipid peroxidation and enhance antioxidant levels in models of fatty liver. However, the antioxidant properties of bioactive materials can be downregulated upon interaction with proteins. For instance, the antioxidant properties of aloe-emodin (Ren et al., 2019) and apigenin (Guo et al., 2023) decline upon their interaction with pepsin. Accordingly, we investigated the antioxidant properties of cuminaldehyde upon interaction with pepsin. To address this aim, we performed ABTS and DPPH scavenging activity of cuminaldehyde in the absence and presence of pepsin in vitro. As shown in Fig. 8, the ABTS (Fig. 8a) and DPPH (Fig. 8b) radical scavenging activities of cuminaldehyde increased dose-dependently with its concentration. However, the radical scavenging activities of cuminaldehyde were significantly reduced in the presence of pepsin, indicating that pepsin binding diminishes cuminaldehyde's antioxidant capacity. This data is consistent with earlier studies (Guo, Gan, Cheng, Cui and Yi, 2023, Ren et al., 2019), which found that interaction of bioactive compounds, acting as potential antioxidants, with pepsin significantly reduces their capacity to scavenge DPPH or ABTS radicals. As the functional groups responsible for antioxidant activity (e.g., the aldehyde group) are involved in binding to pepsin (as seen in docking/MD), their availability for radical scavenging is reduced, leading to decreased antioxidant efficacy.

(a) ABTS, (b) DPPH scavenging rate by cuminaldehyde and addition of pepsin. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

This outcome further verifies that interactions between pepsin and cuminaldehyde can occur and may result in the inhibition of the antioxidant activity of cuminaldehyde, probably mediated by hydrophilic and hydrophobic forces, which affect the functional groups of cuminaldehyde and its antioxidant characteristics. When small bioactive materials react with other substances, their structure and chemical characteristics can be modified. Here, we demonstrated that the interaction of cuminaldehyde with pepsin not only changed the structure and activity of the protein but also influenced the characteristics of the bioactive antioxidant cuminaldehyde. Based on the outcomes of experimental and theoretical analyses, it can also be deduced that upon the interaction of cuminaldehyde and pepsin, the oxygen group of cuminaldehyde interacted with the Gly-109 amino acid residue of pepsin (evidenced by molecular docking studies), leading to a probable reduction of its antioxidant properties (Guo et al., 2023). This interaction may influence the therapeutic potential of cuminaldehyde, particularly in oxidative stress-related gastrointestinal conditions where pepsin is active. The reduction in antioxidant activity could limit its effectiveness in neutralizing free radicals within the stomach. However, this effect might be transient or localized, and strategies such as encapsulation (Ghiasi et al., 2021) or chemical modification could potentially overcome this limitation and improve its bioefficacy in gastrointestinal therapeutic applications.

Therefore, it may be discussed that that cuminaldehyde can bind to pepsin under gastric conditions, leading to structural changes in the enzyme. This binding was shown to modulate pepsin activity, suggesting that dietary compounds may influence protein digestion efficiency in the stomach. Additionally, the interaction affects the antioxidant activity of cuminaldehyde, indicating that digestive enzymes can modify the bioactivity of dietary phytochemicals. These findings highlight the broader significance that interactions between food bioactive compounds and digestive enzymes can determine both enzyme function and the bioavailability or bioactivity of nutraceuticals. Therefore, this study provides mechanistic insight into how bioactive compounds survive or are altered during digestion, which is critical for understanding their potential health effects.

Conclusion

This study elucidates a bidirectional interaction between the dietary phytochemical cuminaldehyde and pepsin, a central enzyme in gastric digestion and gastrointestinal function. Through combined spectroscopic analyses, enzyme kinetics, molecular docking, and MD simulations, we demonstrate that cuminaldehyde spontaneously associates with pepsin near key catalytic residues (Trp-39 and Tyr-75), predominantly via hydrophobic interactions. This binding induces conformational perturbations, increases structural flexibility, and suppresses enzymatic activity, suggesting a modulatory effect on gastric protein digestion. Such regulation of pepsin activity may be relevant to gastrointestinal conditions characterized by excessive proteolytic activity and mucosal vulnerability.

Conversely, interaction with pepsin partially compromises the functional properties of cuminaldehyde itself, as evidenced by reduced ABTS and DPPH radical-scavenging capacity. This attenuation is likely mediated by hydrogen-bond interactions involving the reactive carbonyl group of cuminaldehyde and Gly-109, highlighting a functional interplay between enzyme modulation and antioxidant efficacy. MD simulations further confirmed the stability of the cuminaldehyde–pepsin complex over 150 ns and revealed localized increases in pepsin flexibility, providing structural insight into the observed inhibitory mechanism.

The present study was intentionally conducted using a defined binary ligand–protein model to delineate the direct interaction between cuminaldehyde and pepsin. Future investigations may examine cinnamaldehyde independently within a comparable binary system to establish structure–activity relationships, as well as explore ternary pepsin–cuminaldehyde–cinnamaldehyde systems to assess competitive or cooperative binding dynamics under gastric conditions.

Future studies should explore formulation strategies, such as combination with mucoprotective or antioxidant-stabilizing agents, to balance pepsin regulation with preservation of phytochemical bioactivity.

In conclusion, these findings advance mechanistic understanding of phytochemical–enzyme interactions in the gastric environment and underscore their potential implications for gastrointestinal health and disorder modulation. The results suggest that dietary or functional food formulations incorporating cuminaldehyde could influence gastric digestion while simultaneously undergoing bioactivity modification during enzyme binding.
