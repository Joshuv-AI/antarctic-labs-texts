Modeling of hexavalent chromium removal onto natural zeolite from air stream in a fixed bed column
Authors: Elham Rahmanzadeh, Farideh Golbabaei, Gholamreza Moussavi, Ali Faghihi Zarandi, Fatemeh Dehghani, Mahdi Ghorbanian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing use of hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) has exposed large populations to this environmental and occupational carcinogenic agent. Therefore, researchers have been interested in removing this substance through adsorbents. This study aimed to investigate the efficiency of natural zeolite in the direct adsorption of Cr(VI) from airflow and its adsorption modeling. In this study, a nebulizer device produced the Cr(VI) mist. The efficiency of natural zeolite in Cr(VI) adsorption from airflow, modeling of fixed column adsorption, and the effective parameters on adsorption efficiency including the initial concentration of chromium, airflow rate, and adsorption bed depth were studied. To facilitate the prediction of the performance of natural zeolite’s adsorption column, Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, BDST, and Buhart–Adams models were used. The results showed that the adsorption capacity diminished with increased airflow rate and initial concentration, while it increased with elevated height of the adsorption bed. Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, and BDST models corresponded to experimental data with a correlation coefficient of 0.9933, but the information of the Buhart–Adams model had a lower correlation coefficient (around 0.6677). In conclusion, natural zeolite can be used as an efficient low-cost adsorbent for directly Cr(VI) removing from the airflow in a fixed bed column.Keywords: Adsorption, Airborne hexavalent chromium, Nebulizer, Yoon–Nelson model, Thomas model, Buhart–Adams model, BDST modelSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Chemistry

Introduction

Hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)), with acidic properties, is a very strong oxidant, that does not exist in elemental form in nature, but it is produced in various industries1–3. Chromium is used in electroplating, lithography, photography, textile, welding, and cement industries as well as in the production of stainless steel, dyes, wood preservatives, catalyzers, match, explosives, electric plates, and batteries4,5. The toxicity of Cr(VI) is 100–1000 times larger than trivalent chromium and its compounds include chromate, dichromate, and chromic acid6,7. Intake of Cr(VI) can occur through the respiratory and digestive human systems and skin8. Exposure to Cr(VI) has different toxic effects on humans depending on concentration and duration of exposure. One of the most important known effects of Cr(VI) is its carcinogenicity to humans9. Deep wounds in the hands, arms, tongue, and palate, nasal septum perforation, burning sensation and inflammation in the nose and other parts of the upper respiratory tract, asthma, contact dermatitis, damage to the kidneys and liver, and skin irritation are other adverse health effects of Cr(VI)10,11. The threshold limit values for chromium (TVI), according to the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH), National Institute For Occupational Safety And Health (NIOSH), and Occupational Safety And Health Administration (OSHA) standards are 0.05, 0.001, and 0.005 mg/m3, respectively12. Considering the broad range of applications of chromium in industries, different adverse health effects, a large population of workers exposed to Cr(VI), and occupational health and safety regulations, it is very important to control it. A variety of approaches have been utilized to remove heavy metals from the environment, such as anaerobic and aerobic biological, chemical, and physical procedures. Although biological techniques are environmentally friendly and have low operating costs, they are considered one of the high-priority methods, and they often need a large footprint and a long time to remove contaminants. Moreover, bio-techniques are sensitive to hydraulic and organic loading fluctuations, environmental shocks, and toxic elements13,14. Chemical techniques such as electrocoagulation, electrochemical oxidation and reduction, ion exchange, chemical precipitation, photocatalysis, etc. are used for removing heavy metals. However, the use of these techniques is restricted due to some reasons such as the experienced manpower, high cost of construction and operation, excessive consumption of chemicals, high sludge production, and high energy use15–18.

Currently, the industries in which chromium is released in the form of vapor, mist, or fume have used packed bed scrubbers or local exhaust hoods to decrease airborne chromium emissions19,20. Local exhaust hoods can collect the contaminant from the working environment if they are designed and installed properly. However, the use of local ventilation is associated with the release of pollutants into the environment and subsequent problems. Packed bed scrubbers which are mostly used for decreasing airborne chromium (VI) from plating beds, produce chromium-containing wastewater. It is obvious that conventional procedures of wastewater treatment to remove chromium have low efficiency and high operating costs, which are associated with the production of toxic wastewater, and require sanitary landfilling or further treatment21–23. In recent decades, many attempts have been made to produce and utilize inexpensive materials in the adsorption and removal of contamination caused by heavy metals from the air, water, wastewater, and soil24–28. Among the most widely used minerals, zeolites with unique properties have been used as adsorbents. They have a three-dimensional aluminosilicate and porous structure with a cation exchange capacity of up to several milliequivalent per gram. The most abundant type of natural zeolite is Clinoptilolite with the chemical formula KNa2Ca2 (Si29Al7)O72⋅24H2O. It is worth mentioning that Iran has great resources for this material and therefore it is available as a low-cost adsorbent29,30.

In recent years, various studies have been conducted to evaluate different adsorbents for removing chromium from wastewater and liquid environments. Some studies have been focused on the indirect absorption of chromium from airflow (transmission of chromium from air to aqueous environment and then adsorption from the liquid)21,31,32. However, the present study introduces a novel approach. To the best of our knowledge, almost no study has been conducted to investigate the direct adsorption of Cr(VI) from the airflow except for the study by the current research team in 2013 in which activated carbon is used for direct adsorption of Cr(VI) from the air stream33. The current study’s innovation lies in demonstrating the effectiveness of natural zeolite in directly capturing Cr(VI) from the air. This approach eliminates the intermediate step of transferring chromium to an aqueous medium, thus preventing the generation of chromium-contaminated wastewater. By using an inexpensive and naturally available zeolite adsorbent, this study shows the feasibility and efficiency of directly removing airborne chromium. This represents a significant advancement in environmental remediation. Considering the limited studies for controlling airborne Cr(VI) in occupational environments, large numbers of workers exposed, and the adverse effects, the current study was conducted to determine the efficiency and modeling of Cr(VI) adsorption on natural zeolite.

Materials and methods

In this experimental study, the effect of input airflow rate, concentration of Cr(VI), and adsorption bed depth was examined on the efficiency of Cr(VI) adsorption from airflow on natural zeolite adsorbent. Thereafter, adsorption modeling was performed using Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, BDST, and Buhart–Adams models. Finally, the correlation between experimental data and the data obtained from the models was examined.

The adsorbent and chemicals

The zeolite used in this study was prepared from mines in the south of Semnan, Iran. The sample was first crushed by a mill and then granulated using standard experimental sieves (ASTM) with a mesh of 16/20 (0.84–1.19 mm). To remove impurities, it was washed with tap water and distilled water several times and then placed inside an oven for 24 h at 100 ± 5 °C to dry off. A stock solution of chromium 50 mg/L was prepared by dissolving 141.4 mg potassium dichromate (Merck Co., Germany) in distilled water and the required concentrations of dichromate were prepared from this solution. Sulfuric acid 0.2 N and NaOH 1 M solution were used for adjusting pH. A pH meter (Jenway, 3505, England) and Sartorius laboratory balance (R200D, Germany) with an accuracy of 0.01 mg were used to measure pH and mass, respectively.

The characteristics of natural zeolite

Intrinsic properties of zeolite including specific surface area of adsorption as well as the volume and dimensions of pores were measured by BET technique. The experiments of detection of Crystal species and determination of the composition of compounds were performed by XRD and XRF (Xpert MPD model, Phillips, Netherlands). To examine the surface morphology of zeolite, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (XL30, Phillips, Netherlands) was employed.

Adsorption column experiments

Adsorption experiments were conducted in a fixed (column) bed reactor with a downstream and continuous flow, where for keeping thermal conditions constant, the adsorption column was placed inside a closed container equipped with a fan, lighting lamp, heater, and thermostat. The adsorption column was a glass cylinder with an internal diameter of 1.25 cm and a height of 20 cm, where a mesh made of stainless steel was used as the adsorbent holder (Fig. 1).

Analysis

As illustrated in Fig. 1 to measure chromium mist concentration (before and after the adsorption bed), the output contaminant was directed from the adsorption column to three impingers (bubblers). Then, the concentration of chromium trapped inside the bubblers was determined through the colorimetric method, according to the Cr-3500 standard method in the standard methods for the examination of water and wastewater book34.

This method is based on the reaction between Cr(VI) and diphenyl carbaside and the generation of a purple color. The amount of absorption was measured by a spectrophotometer (Unico 4802) at the wavelength of 540 nm. Chromium concentration in the impingers was calculated using a calibration curve of standard Cr(VI) solutions with concentrations of 100–1200 mg/L. Thereafter, its initial and final concentrations and the airflow were determined based on mass balance and volume of the air passing through the adsorption column.

Adsorption column models

Successful design of an adsorption column requires prediction of concentration–time profile (breakthrough curve). Various models are used for this purpose, the most common of which are the Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, Buhart–Adams, and BDST models. In this study, to facilitate the prediction of the performance of natural zeolite adsorption column, experimental data of breakthrough point were corresponded against gas adsorption models of Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, Buhart–Adams, and BDST40.

Yoon–Nelson model

This model was presented by Yoon and Nelson to investigate the behavior of the breakthrough point curve of gases adsorbed on activated carbon. This model is based on the assumption of proportion between the rate of reduction in adsorption probability for each adsorbate molecule with the probability of adsorbate adsorption and the probability of its breakthrough on the adsorbent41. The effect of initial concentration on the efficiency of chromium (VI) adsorption

Cr(VI) adsorption was examined at concentrations of 0.05, 0.15, 1, and 10 mg/m3 with a bed depth of 5 cm and flow rate of 3 L/min. the results obtained from this investigation are provided in Fig. 5. With the increase in the initial concentration, the time for reaching the breakthrough point and saturation was shortened. As shown in Fig. 5, at concentrations 0.15 and 0.05 mg/m3, following 350 h of sampling, the curve did not reach the breakthrough point. However, at concentrations 10 and 1 mg/m3, breakpoint was observed after 27 and 160 h, and its saturation occurred at 58.5 and 219 h, respectively. The results indicate that with increased initial concentration, the driving force of the adsorption process increases, which in turn causes faster saturation of adsorption sites, thereby shortening the time for reaching the breakthrough point44,45. At lower concentrations, the slope of the breakthrough point is smaller and the adsorbent surface becomes saturated after a longer time, suggesting a wide mass transfer zone (MTZ) at low concentrations. However, at higher concentrations, the breakthrough point has a larger slope and its mass transfer zone is smaller. In other adsorption studies, similar results have been obtained46,47. With the increase in the initial concentration of chromium in the adsorption bed, the removal percentage diminishes. However, the output of the bed and adsorption capacity (q) increase, as with elevation of concentration gradient, dispersion coefficient, and mass transfer coefficient, the mass transfer occurs more rapidly45,48,49.

The effect of initial Cr(VI) concentration on its adsorption efficiency at the flow rate of 3 L/min and adsorption bed depth of 5 cm.

The effect of input flow rate on the efficiency of Cr(VI) adsorption

To determine the suitable flow rate of the flow introduced into the adsorption bed, flow rates of 1 and 3 L/min were tested (Fig. 6) (the adsorption bed depth is 5 cm and the initial concentration is 10 mg/m3). As can be observed in Fig. 6, the experiments at the flow rate of 1 L/min did not reach the breakthrough point. Thus, it was decided that the experiments would continue at the flow rate of 3 L/min.

The effect of input flow rate on Cr(VI) adsorption efficiency at the adsorption bed depth of 5 cm and concentration of 10 mg/m3.

The airflow rate is an important parameter in the design of the fixed adsorption column. The effect of airflow rate on the efficiency of Cr(VI) adsorption has been shown in Fig. 6. According to the results of this experiment, at the flow rate of 3 L/min, due to increased airflow rate, shortened retention time and diminished extent of entrapment of Cr(VI) ions by the adsorbent molecules, reaching the saturation point and saturation has occurred more rapidly, thus the slope of the breakthrough curve increases and the removal efficiency decreases. If the retention time of Cr(VI) ions in the adsorption column is not long enough, the ions leave the column before reaching equilibrium.

The experiments progressed at the flow rate of 1 L/min up to 85 h, but the curve did not reach the breakthrough point. At lower flow rates, due to a longer contact time, the contaminant has more opportunity to bond with the adsorbent particles, thereby increasing adsorption efficiency. The input flow rate significantly influences the adsorption capacity, such that with an increased flow rate, the extent of adsorption and entrapment of Cr(VI) ions declines. This can be attributed to the fact that chromium retention time in the adsorption bed is short and part of it leaves the bed before reaching the saturation point. Similar results have been obtained by other researchers21,50–54.

The effect of adsorption bed depth on the efficiency of Cr(VI) adsorption

Cr(VI) adsorption was examined at heights of 2.5, 5, and 10 cm of zeolite adsorbent and with an initial chromium concentration of 10 mg/m3. As shown in Fig. 7, with the increase in the adsorption bed depth, its efficiency increases.

Influence of adsorbent height in the column on efficiency of Cr(VI) adsorption in flow rate of 3 L/min and concentration of 10 mg/m3.

The amount of adsorbent in the adsorption column determines the number of active and available adsorption sites. According to Fig. 7, the time required for reaching the breakthrough point in the bed with heights of 2.5, 5, and 10 cm is 15, 33, and 69 h, and the time required for reaching the saturation point is 22.5, 69, and 127.5 h, respectively. With elevation of the adsorption bed height, chromium retention time in the column and the time required for reaching the breakthrough point are prolonged, and in turn, bed saturation occurs later. Therefore, the specific surface area of the adsorbent increases, while the ratio of C (output concentration) to C0 (initial concentration) declines, thereby increasing adsorption capacity, which is due to increased specific surface area of the adsorbent and adsorption sites.

At lower heights, the dispersion phenomenon is developed in mass transfer, and after the breakthrough point, a sharp rise occurs in the output concentration, and the adsorption capacity diminishes55. With the increase in the adsorption bed in the column, the time of contaminant contact with the adsorbent increases and the breakthrough curve slope decreases, suggesting a wide mass transfer zone at higher heights38,44,56. The results indicate that the optimal efficiency of the adsorbent is obtained with higher heights.

As can be observed in Figs. 5, 6, and 7, all these models had a very high correspondence with experimental data for states in which the breakthrough curve started from the origin of coordinates. However, in cases where the curve has had an intercept, the accuracy of predictions diminished. For this reason, in the curves associated with bed height of 2.5 and 5 cm, the model prediction error has increased occasionally.

As shown in Table 4, with the increase in the initial concentration, KYN and q values increase due to the increased driving force of mass transfer. Further, τ and adsorption efficiency (R) diminished due to the increased rate of bed saturation. With elevation of concentration, more mass of chromium enters the adsorption column, resulting in an increased concentration slope. As a result, adsorption sites are occupied with chromium molecules more rapidly. With the increased height of the adsorption bed, KYN decreases, while τ, q, and R grow due to decreased bed saturation rate, which is in line with other studies35,44,46,55,57.

According to Table 5, in the Thomas model, with increased depth of adsorption bed, due to more adsorption sites for the contaminant, KTh decreases, while q (including adsorption capacity of experimental data and adsorption capacity of the model) and R increase. With the increase in the initial concentration of Cr(VI) entering the adsorption bed, adsorption capacity increases, but efficiency and Thomas constant decreases. This is because with elevated concentration, concentration gradient and mass transfer driving force increase, thus adsorption capacity and breakthrough curve slope also increase. However, as the curve slope is obtained by multiplying the concentration by the Thomas constant, the Thomas constant decreases with increased concentration. The high correlation coefficient between experimental data and model prediction represents suitable correspondence between the model and study results. Therefore, it can be probable that according to the assumption of the Thomas model, adsorption has occurred in a monolayer form in this study. These results are in line with the findings of other researchers35,39.

As can be seen in Table 6, with the increase in the initial concentration introduced into the adsorption bed, the concentration slope increases, while K and adsorption efficiency (R) diminish due to the increased bed saturation rate. Further, N0 (adsorption capacity per volume unit of the bed) and q increase due to elevation of mass transfer driving force. With the increase in the adsorption bed depth, K and N0 decrease due to the reduction of the breakthrough curve and elevation of the adsorbent volume, respectively, but q and R grow due to the increased number of adsorption sites and diminished saturation rate of the bed. These results are in line with the findings of studies conducted in this regard, which is dominant in the overall kinetics of the external mass transfer system44,55.

According to Table 7, in the BDST model, with the increased adsorption bed depth, due to elevation of retention time of the contaminant in the bed and growth of adsorption sites, the coefficient of this model (K), which is a function of curve slope decreases, while q (including adsorption capacity of experimental data and model’s adsorption capacity), N0, and R increase. With the increased initial concentration of chromium (VI) into the adsorption bed, N0 and adsorption capacity increase due to the elevation of mass transfer driving force. However, BDST constant and efficiency decline, which is due to increased saturation rate of the adsorption bed. These results are in line with the findings of other researchers35,39.

According to Tables 4, 5, and 7, the information of Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, and BDST models is matched with the data obtained from performing zeolite experiments with a correlation coefficient of 0.9933. These high values represent a high correlation and relationship between experimental data and the data predicted by the mentioned models. The information of the Buhart–Adams model corresponded with the experimental data obtained from zeolite with a lower correlation coefficient (around 0.6677), as it is used only for the first part of the breakthrough curve.

Scale-up design of adsorption columns for Cr(VI) removal

The scale-up of adsorption processes from laboratory settings to industrial applications involves a detailed and precise approach to ensure efficiency and reliability. One of the primary challenges in scaling up is addressing the discrepancies that arise between lab-scale experiments and industrial-sized columns. These discrepancies necessitate a robust design process that involves calculating key parameters such as adsorbent quantity, column diameter, and column height to optimize the process for industrial applications58.

The scale-up design process begins with lab-scale studies to determine the optimal conditions and parameters for adsorption. Various kinetic models are then applied to analyze the lab-scale data and predict breakthrough curves. In this study, Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, and BDST models corresponded to experimental data with a high correlation coefficient of 0.9933 in the optimum condition and are identified as the best-fitted models for scale-up design. Design parameters identified from the best-fitting kinetic model include design adsorption capacity (qad), superficial velocity (U0), and model equations. These parameters are crucial for calculating the design volumetric flow rate (Qd), adsorbent quantity (Mad), and column height (H), which are essential for the effective scale-up of the adsorption process.

To apply this adsorption system in industrial settings, we propose the implementation of two adsorption columns with identical specifications. While one column undergoes regeneration, the other continues to operate, allowing the treatment process to proceed without interruption. After regeneration, the column is thoroughly backwashed with water. This study shows that after three regeneration cycles, the zeolite loses its effectiveness and needs to be replaced with a new batch.

Environmentally safe disposal of used adsorbents

The secure disposal of chromium-saturated zeolite is crucial due to the significant risk of chromium ions leaching into the environment. Research suggests that Cr(VI)-loaded zeolite can be effectively incinerated at approximately 700 °C for one hour, resulting in its conversion to ash. To evaluate the immobilization of Cr in the ash, subsequent leaching tests can be conducted. These tests involve mixing 5 mg of ash with 25 mL of deionized water, followed by gentle stirring for 24 h. Ideally, the analysis of the resulting filtrate should indicate no detectable levels of Cr(VI) ions60. This outcome would suggest that the ash is safe for repurposing, such as for road construction or use in agricultural fields. Thus, the incineration method not only addresses the disposal of spent adsorbents but also mitigates the environmental impact by preventing the leaching of chromium.

Conclusion

Considering the abundant use of chromium in industries, Cr(VI) adverse effects as well as the legal requirements associated with occupational health and safety, controlling its emission in occupational environments is essential. One way to do this is the adsorption process. This study has shown the efficacy of natural zeolite as a potent adsorbent for directly removing Cr(VI) from air streams in a fixed bed column. The findings indicate that the adsorption capacity of zeolite decreases with increasing airflow rates and initial Cr(VI) concentrations, while it increases with greater bed depth. The modeling outcomes, using Yoon–Nelson, Thomas, and BDST models, exhibited high correlation coefficients, indicating strong alignment with experimental data. Conversely, the Buhart–Adams model presented a lower correlation coefficient, suggesting reduced predictive accuracy for this specific application. This research introduces an innovative approach by using a nebulizer to produce varying concentrations of Cr(VI) in the air and employing natural zeolite for the direct adsorption of Cr(VI) from the air, obviating the necessity to transfer chromium to an aqueous medium. This method simplifies the removal process and avoids the production of chromium-contaminated wastewater. This approach offers a sustainable and cost-effective solution for air decontamination by employing a low-cost and easily available natural adsorbent. The importance of this study resides in its capacity to propel the domain of environmental remediation forward. By providing an effective method for directly removing airborne chromium, this work addresses a critical requirement in occupational health and safety, particularly in industries where Cr(VI) exposure is prevalent. This research underscores the feasibility and effectiveness of natural zeolite as a viable alternative to more complex and costly traditional methods, thus paving the way for enhanced environmental and public health protection.
