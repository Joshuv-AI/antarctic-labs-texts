Engineering the nursing profession: China’s academician system reform and its implications for interdisciplinary health workforce development
Authors: Wan-Qiong Zhou, Cheng-Rui Zhang, Ying Xing, Wei Luan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2025, the Chinese Academy of Engineering’s inclusion of “Nursing” as an independent academician-track discipline marks a historic milestone. While affirming nursing’s disciplinary value and strategic importance to the “Healthy China” initiative, this reform presents a “preparedness crisis.” To succeed, nursing must pivot from soft service models to hard technological innovation, leveraging medical-industrial integration and interdisciplinary talent to meet rigorous engineering criteria.Subject terms: Health occupations, Engineering

In June 2025, the Chinese Academy of Engineering officially released the Implementation Measures for the Election of Academicians in 20251. For the first time, “Nursing” was recognized as an independent disciplinary field within the Department of Medicine and Health and was included alongside Anesthesiology and Rehabilitation Medicine in the Surgery Group. This decision marks a historic shift, breaking traditional boundaries within engineering science and technology, and formally acknowledging nursing as part of the nation’s highest academic platform. It represents not only an official affirmation of the disciplinary value of nursing but also a significant step toward a more diversified and practice-oriented scientific evaluation system in China.

This milestone comes 21 years after the initiation of doctoral nursing education in China, 14 years after the Ministry of Education designated nursing as a first-level discipline2, and 52 years after the establishment of the American Academy of Nursing’s (AAN) academician system. Against the backdrop of China’s national strategy to advance the Healthy China initiative, the elevation of nursing to a core academic discipline holds profound practical significance. Specifically, the “Outline of the Healthy China 2030 Plan” establishes specific targets for optimizing health services, necessitating a paradigm shift from disease treatment to full-lifecycle health management3. This strategic shift creates an urgent demand for technological innovation in chronic disease control and elderly care. Furthermore, the “National Nursing Development Plan (2021–2025)” explicitly mandates the high-quality development of the nursing workforce and the strengthening of nursing research capabilities4. Therefore, the inclusion of nursing in the academician system is not merely an academic honor but a direct institutional response to these governmental directives, aiming to cultivate top-tier scientists capable of leading this critical transformation.

This historic reform signifies not only a major leap in the disciplinary status of nursing but also carries strategic significance in advancing the Healthy China initiative and promoting international academic exchange. As national health priorities shift from disease treatment to health management, the professional value of nursing is becoming increasingly prominent, particularly in areas such as chronic disease prevention and control, elderly care, and community health. The reform of the academician selection mechanism is expected to strongly stimulate innovation in nursing science and technology, while providing a robust talent pool and technical support for building a high-quality, efficient health care service system. From a global perspective, the inclusion of nursing as an academician-track discipline reflects China’s proactive engagement in international health governance. Amid intensifying global competition for nursing talent, this reform enhances China’s influence in the development of nursing standards and technological innovation. By establishing a talent evaluation system aligned with international norms, it also fosters the cultivation of nursing leaders with global vision and supports the dissemination of China’s nursing practice models to the world.

While the elevation of nursing to this new academic status is a cause for celebration and international recognition, we must be sober to realize that this is a great challenge, and even a new crisis. This crisis is not merely about the intense competition individual candidates face, but rather a fundamental “Crisis of Preparedness” for the discipline itself. Currently, a significant disconnect exists between mainstream nursing research, which predominantly focuses on clinical care models and qualitative inquiry, and the CAE’s selection criteria, which demand “significant engineering contributions” and tangible technological outcomes. If the nursing profession fails to rapidly pivot from “soft” service innovation to “hard” technological integration, it risks facing a “hollow victory”: possessing an independent academic seat yet lacking candidates with the requisite engineering credentials to fill it.

The long-standing Fellowship of the American Academy of Nursing (FAAN) provides valuable references for China. Chinese and American academicians selection mechanism is fundamentally different: Chinese Academy of Engineering academician selection led by the state official institutions, is a life-long honor system, to be recommended by the academician or the Association of Science and age (65 years of age or more to be recommended by the two academicians), nationality limitations, every two years, an additional 60–70 people; elected to enjoy the national allowance, about 2000 RMB a month and the policy tilt5, the social status of the administrative position with a high degree of relevance. FAAN is a non-profit organization independent of the government, no statelessness restrictions. In 2025, the proportion of foreign nationals in the list will reach 8% (as of June 2025, a total of 121 people were elected in mainland China)6; the selection of transnational nursing focus on the impact of the selection, no economic treatment or administrative privileges, elected to pay their own dues to maintain the purely honorary status. The core difference between the two is that the Chinese Academy of Engineering is an administrative honor system that highlights the authority of the state, while AAN represents a market-based global academic community. This structural divergence has distinct implications for scientific innovation. The CAE’s state-led model allows for the rapid mobilization of national resources, which is critical for “organized scientific research”-particularly in building high-cost platforms for medical-industrial integration and intelligent equipment R&D. However, this “administrative honor” system carries the risk of entrenching hierarchy, which could stifle the high-risk, disruptive thinking essential for original breakthroughs. In contrast, the AAN model fosters a borderless exchange of ideas and diverse international collaboration, accelerating the global diffusion of nursing standards. To maximize innovation, China’s nursing reform must strike a balance: leveraging the CAE’s resource advantages to build engineering infrastructure while adopting an open, merit-based evaluation culture similar to the AAN to maintain academic vitality.

China’s nursing discipline has made preliminary advances in the domain of medical-industrial integration. Leading institutions such as Huazhong University of Science and Technology and Sichuan University have formed interdisciplinary teams to promote the research, development, and application of intelligent nursing equipment and remote health monitoring technologies. However, the field continues to face substantial challenges. Disciplinary silos have hindered deep integration between nursing and engineering, and interdisciplinary curricula remain underdeveloped. The research foundation is relatively weak, marked by a scarcity of national-level medical-industrial integration laboratories and a low proportion of high-impact scientific research projects in nursing. In 2020, nursing scholars secured only 0.491‰ of all projects funded by the National Natural Science Foundation of China. Additionally, there is a notable shortage of interdisciplinary talent, and existing training mechanisms lack systematic integration of nursing expertise with engineering and technological skills. To address these challenges, it is essential to overcome the current bottlenecks in cross-disciplinary collaboration. Future development should be driven by targeted policy support and enhanced cooperation across academic, clinical, and industrial sectors, thereby fostering innovation and advancing the nursing discipline in the era of technological convergence.

At present, nursing occupies a relatively weak position in the academician selection process of the Chinese Academy of Engineering, primarily due to the limited “engineering visibility” of its medical-industrial integration outcomes. Traditional nursing research remains largely rooted in clinical practice and lacks original engineering breakthroughs, making it difficult to satisfy the Academy’s selection criteria, which emphasize “significant engineering contributions”. To overcome this challenge, the nursing discipline must undergo transformative changes in several key areas.

First, it should prioritize the research and development of intelligent nursing equipment, biosensing technologies, and other high-tech domains. Specifically, the focus should be on emerging fields that directly map onto nursing practice, such as wearable biosensors for continuous patient monitoring, assistive robotics for ageing and rehabilitation, and clinical AI for early deterioration detection. These reflect nursing’s unique proximity to patients and offer concrete entry points for engineering-nursing co-innovation.

Second, dedicated platforms for medical-industrial integration should be established to promote deep collaboration between nursing and engineering-related disciplines such as mechanical engineering, materials science, and artificial intelligence. To navigate existing bureaucratic silos and funding fragmentation, these platforms must be supported by “dual-appointment” systems, allowing nursing scientists to hold joint faculty positions, and dedicated “nursing-tech” transition funds that bridge the gap from laboratory prototype to clinical application.

Finally, the talent cultivation model must be reformed to develop high-level nursing scientists equipped with engineering-oriented thinking.

Only through a paradigm shift from “nursing technology application” to “nursing engineering innovation” can the discipline enhance its academic influence within the field of engineering and gain rightful recognition in national-level scientific evaluation systems. The success of this shift will be marked by an increase in original invention patents, more nursing-led research published in engineering journals, and the clinical commercialization of nursing-developed technologies.
