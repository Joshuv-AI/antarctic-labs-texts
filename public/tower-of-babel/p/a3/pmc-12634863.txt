Impact of enhanced hospital infection prevention measures during-COVID-19 pandemic on the incidence of surgical site infections following spinal instrumentation surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis: A nationwide database study
Authors: Daiyang Yu, Yuri Miyakoshi, Tomoyuki Tanaka, Kunihiko Takahashi, Tetsuya Jinno, Toshitaka Yoshii, Kiyohide Fushimi, Hiroyuki Inose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground contextDespite the implementation of stringent infection control measures during the COVID-19 pandemic, the impact of these interventions on surgical site infection (SSI) rates in spinal instrumentation surgery remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the incidence of SSIs after spinal instrumentation surgeries for lumbar spinal canal stenosis (LCS) before and during the COVID-19 pandemic and to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, including enhanced hospital infection prevention measures implemented during-pandemic, on the incidence of SSIs following spinal instrumented surgery for LCS.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study using the Diagnosis Procedure Combination database, a Japanese national database. We analyzed 42,890 patients who underwent instrumentation surgery for LCS during the prepandemic (2017–2018) and during-pandemic (2020–2021) periods. The primary outcome was the occurrence of postoperative SSIs during hospitalization. We used propensity score (PS) matching to compare SSI rates and conducted multivariate logistic regression analysis to assess the pandemic’s impact after adjusting for confounding variablesResultsResults showed a significant increase in SSI rates during-pandemic, from 1.83% to 2.53% (p<.01). After PS matching, the during-pandemic group continued to show a higher SSI rate (2.44%) compared to the prepandemic group (1.84%, p=.01). Multiple logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the association between the COVID-19 period (prepandemic versus during-pandemic) and SSI incidence remained statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounding variables (p=.01, OR=1.26).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that SSIs following instrumented spinal surgeries for LCS in Japan increased during-pandemic, even with matched patient characteristics. Future research should focus on identifying specific elements of perioperative care that may have been compromised during the pandemic and developing targeted interventions to address these gaps in surgical infection prevention.Keywords: Surgical site infection, Lumbar canal stenosis, Instrumentation surgery, COVID-19, Complication, DPC database

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are major complications in spinal surgeries, and a large population study reported that the total incidence of postoperative SSI was 1.53% [1]. Instrumented spinal surgeries are particularly susceptible to SSIs due to their complexity and invasiveness, and the prevalence of SSIs in such procedures ranges from 2.6% to 3.7% [[2], [3], [4]]. SSIs can lead to prolonged hospital stays, increased morbidity, and higher healthcare expenses, estimated at $26,937-$53,757 per patient [[5], [6], [7]]. The COVID-19 pandemic has undeniably influenced infection control practices across various medical disciplines [8,9]. Healthcare facilities worldwide have implemented comprehensive infection prevention and control protocols to reduce SARS-CoV-2 transmission, particularly in clinical settings. These measures include mandatory personal protective equipment usage, enhanced hand hygiene protocols, and pre-admission screening procedures [10]. While evidence suggests these interventions have been effective in preventing healthcare-associated SARS-CoV-2 transmission, their impact on SSI rates requires further investigation.

Previous studies have reported that enhanced hygiene measures and protocols introduced during the pandemic have led to changes in infection rates for different surgeries [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. For example, a study on cardiac surgeries demonstrated a reduction in SSI rates from 4.3% prepandemic to 1.5% during the pandemic due to improved hygiene protocols [13]. Prior research on SSI rates in instrumented spinal surgeries has shown a decreased trend, a single-center study indicated a reduction in SSI rates from 3.2% to 1% during the pandemic period due to intensified infection control measures [12]. However, other studies suggest that these hygienic measures induced no statistical change in SSI rate after orthopedic surgery [[14], [15], [16]]. The primary objectives of this study are to (1) evaluate the incidence of infection in spinal instrumentation procedures for lumbar spinal canal stenosis (LCS) in the pre-and during-COVID-19 pandemic periods, and (2) assess the impact of enhanced infection prevention protocols implemented during the pandemic on SSI rates following spinal instrumentation procedures.

Methods

Data source

This is a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data obtained from a rigorously maintained national registry, the Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) database [17,18]. DPC covers hospitals in all regions of Japan, and the number of beds included in the DPC database accounted for 56.7% of the total number of beds in Japan, according to a survey conducted in FY2021 [18]. This database contains various information, including patient demographics such as age, Activities of daily living (ADL) score based on the Barthel index (0–20) [19] and gender, as well as surgical information such as diagnoses, complications during hospitalization, and duration of surgery using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes and text data in Japanese.

Approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board (M2000-788). Because the data were anonymized, the ethical committee deemed it unnecessary to obtain informed consent.

Patient sample

Patients who were diagnosed as LCS and underwent instrumented spinal surgery were included in the study. Data from 2017 and 2018 were categorized as the prepandemic period, while data from 2020 and 2021 were considered as the during-pandemic period.

We abstracted the following variables from the DPC database: age, sex, primary diagnosis, primary surgeries, complications, comorbidities, duration of anesthesia, and volume of blood transfusion. The main diagnosis of the patient sample is LCS (M4806). The primary surgeries include anterior lumbar interbody fusion (K1421), posterior or posterolateral fusion (K1422), posterior lumbar interbody fusion (K1423), and combined anterior-posterior vertebral fixation (K1424).

Outcome measures

The occurrence of SSIs during hospitalization [T793, T814].

Statistical analysis

We compared the differences between the 2 groups (prepandemic and during-pandemic groups). We used the chi-square test to compare categorical variables (sex, preoperative comorbidities, surgical approach, emergency surgery, and SSIs), and the student’s t-test was conducted to compare normally distributed continuous variables (age, body mass index (BMI), medical expenses per hospitalization, and anesthesia time). The non-normally distributed continuous variables (smoking index, transfusion volume, and ADL at admission) were analyzed by the Wilcoxon rank sum test.

We performed propensity score (PS) matching after crude data analysis, and we used the same method to compare variables on only eligible patients after PS matching. PS matching is a statistical technique to reduce the bias caused by confounding variables. The logistic analysis was performed on the confounding variables, which include age, gender, BMI, smoking index, surgical approach, ADL score at admission, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, renal disease, malignancy, emergency surgery, blood transfusion volume, and duration of anesthesia.

For PS matching, the surgical approach was dichotomized into 2 groups: procedures including an anterior approach and posterior-only procedures. Patients who have undergone instrumented spine surgery were paired by the one-to-one nearest neighbor-matching method. A caliper of 0.05 was applied to reduce bias and ensure comparability between the groups. Thus, the 2 groups (prepandemic and during-pandemic) with aligned patient backgrounds were formed. After PS matching, we conducted the same statistical analysis on each variable. We then performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis to determine the impact of enhanced infection prevention protocols implemented during the pandemic on SSI rates following spinal instrumentation procedures. Based on the previous study, we included the COVID-19 period status (pre- versus during-pandemic) along with potential risk factors for SSI: Age (years), sex, BMI (kg/m2), smoking index (points), ADL at admission (points), surgical approach, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, renal disease, malignancy, emergency surgery, duration of anesthesia, and volume of blood transfusion [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. The smoking history was quantified using the smoking index, calculated as the number of cigarettes smoked per day multiplied by the number of years of smoking.

All statistical analyses, except for PS matching, were conducted using Stata BE version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station), with p values <.05 considered statistically different. PS matching was performed by JMP 14 (SAS Institute Inc.).

Results

This study included 42,890 patients, divided into a prepandemic group (n=21,719) and a during-pandemic group (n=21,171). The patient characteristics data before matching implies that the prepandemic group had younger patients (prepandemic 70.93 vs during-pandemic 71.55 years old; p<.01). Additionally, the prepandemic group had a smaller BMI (24.30 vs 24.50 kg/m2; p<.01) and a lower smoking index (225.55 vs 240.30; p<.01). There was a significant difference in the distribution of surgical approaches between the 2 periods (p<.01). Additionally, the prepandemic group had a higher ADL score at admission (19.07 vs 18.88; p<.01) and fewer preoperative comorbidities (34.60% vs 35.71%; p<.01). The increased comorbidities included diabetes mellitus (prepandemic vs during-pandemic: 22.58% vs 23.78%; p<.01) and renal disease (3.60% vs 4.55%; p<.01). The other comorbidities are decreased or remained the same level, such as cardiovascular disease (7.77% vs 6.87; p<.01), cerebrovascular disease (0.93% vs 0.85%; p<.01), respiratory disease (3.03% vs 3.00%; p=.86), and malignancy (2.64% vs 2.76%; p=.46). The anesthesia time of patients was also shorter in the prepandemic group (279.20 vs 314.44 minutes; p<.01). Additionally, the medical expenses per hospitalization of the prepandemic group were also lower than the during-pandemic group (14,909 vs 15,102 USD; p<.01). For SSIs, the prepandemic group had a lower SSI rate than the during-pandemic group (1.83% vs 2.53%) with a statistical difference (p<.01). Other variables were not statistically different between these 2 groups (Table 1).

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified several variables associated with the occurrence of SSI. Posterior approach (odds ratio [OR]: 1.50: 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.21–1.86; p<.01), patients with renal disease (OR: 1.46; 95% CI: 1.11–1.93; p<.01), cardiovascular disease (OR: 1.38; 95% CI: 1.11–1.72; p<.01), and diabetes mellitus (OR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.08–1.44; p<.01) were at increased risk for SSI. In addition, higher transfusion volume (OR per 100 mL: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.04–1.06; p<.01) and longer anesthesia time (OR per 10 minutes: 1.02; 95% CI: 1.02–1.02; p<.01) were also significantly associated with increased risk. Emergency surgery (OR: 1.43; p<.01) was another risk factor. Conversely, higher ADL scores at admission (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.93–0.97; p<.01) and female sex (OR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.61–0.80; p<.01) were associated with a decreased risk of SSI. Importantly, undergoing surgery during the during-COVID period was independently associated with an increased risk of SSI (OR: 1.39; 95% CI: 1.22–1.58; p<.01) (Table 3).

Discussions

The results of this study revealed several unexpected findings regarding the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on SSIs in instrumented spinal surgeries in Japan. Contrary to initial expectations, the during-pandemic group showed an increase in SSI rates from 1.83% to 2.53%. This finding contrasts with a previous study that reported decreased SSI rates in spinal surgery during the pandemic [12]. Other SSI studies of orthopedic surgery failed to show a significant reduction in the SSI rate [15,16]. Similar to our result, a retrospective, multicenter analysis study found that some types of SSIs (deep incisional primary, organ-space infection, and superficial incisional) were increased in patients who underwent hip prosthesis surgery after the pandemic [24]. Several factors may contribute to this unexpected increase in SSIs. Firstly, the during-pandemic group had a higher prevalence of preoperative comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus and renal disease, which are known risk factors for SSIs [25]. The higher comorbidity burden in the during-pandemic group (34.60% vs 35.71%) likely increased the overall risk of SSIs. Additionally, the longer anesthesia time observed in the during-pandemic group (314.44 vs 279.20 minutes) may have contributed to increased infection risk, as prolonged surgical duration is a known risk factor for SSIs [3,5]. Furthermore, the pandemic may have led to delays in elective surgeries, potentially resulting in more complex cases being performed later, which could inherently carry a higher risk of SSIs [26].

To eliminate differences arising from variations in patient characteristics, we applied PS score matching to the prepandemic and during-pandemic cohorts and reanalyzed the matched groups. The higher SSI rates persisted even after PS matching and adjustment for potential confounding factors, suggesting that pandemic-related changes in healthcare delivery may have adversely affected surgical outcomes. These findings align with the other study showing poorer outcomes in spine surgeries during the COVID-19 pandemic, including higher rates of morbidity and complications [27]. Several factors may contribute to these findings. First, the systemic adaptations required during the pandemic might have influenced surgical care delivery and outcomes. These adaptations included changes in perioperative protocols, reduced staff availability, and modified hospital workflows, which could have impacted the quality of surgical care [27]. Second, the enhanced infection control measures implemented during the pandemic may not have been as effective as anticipated for preventing SSIs in spinal instrumentation procedures [14]. While we acknowledge that minor protocols might differ between hospitals, fundamental infection control measures against COVID-19 were universally implemented in hospitals throughout Japan. This strongly suggests that the infection prevention measures implemented for COVID-19 were insufficient or ineffective in preventing postoperative SSIs following spinal instrumentation surgery, as these measures were designed to prevent the transmission of an airborne virus, not bacterial infections.

A recent meta-analysis has shown that intraoperative wound irrigation with povidone-iodine solution during spinal surgery effectively reduces the incidence of SSI [28]. Interestingly, recent research on postoperative SSIs after spinal surgery has demonstrated that 86% of SSIs, comprising a wide range of bacterial species, originate endogenously from the patient’s preoperative strains [29]. Collectively, in addition to the essential practices of reducing the bacterial load in the surgical field and body pre- and intraoperatively and preventing postoperative bacterial transmission to the wound, it is necessary to develop SSI prevention strategies tailored to the preoperative microbiota of individual patients. Nevertheless, rather than discontinuing infection control measures implemented during COVID-19 pandemic entirely, a critical evaluation and potential modification of these protocols may be warranted to optimize infection prevention strategies in future surgical practices.

In this study, we found that female sex is a protective factor for the occurrence of postoperative infection, which is consistent with findings from another study [30]. According to the review by Klein et al., complex interactions among genes, hormones, and environmental factors result in sex-based differences in immunity. For instance, androgens, which are present in high concentrations in males, generally suppress immune cell activity [31].

This study had several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis using the DPC database, we relied on pre-existing data, which may lack detailed information on certain potential confounders, such as specific intraoperative infection control practices or postoperative wound care protocols, that could have influenced SSI rates. Furthermore, the database only captures events during hospitalization, meaning any SSIs that developed after discharge were not included. This likely leads to an underestimation of the true SSI incidence. Second, while PS matching reduces bias, residual confounding factors may still be present. To address this, we further adjusted for possible confounders in the multivariate regression analysis, but the during-COVID-19 period remained significantly associated with the occurrence of SSI. Additional studies involving more participants from varied backgrounds would help verify and expand the scope of our observations.

Conclusions

This large-scale, multicenter study demonstrates a significant increase in SSI rates following instrumented spinal surgeries for LCS during the during-pandemic period, despite the implementation of enhanced infection prevention protocols. The elevated SSI rates persisted even after PS matching and adjustment for potential confounding factors. Future research should focus on identifying specific elements of perioperative care that may have been compromised during the pandemic and developing targeted interventions to address these gaps in surgical infection prevention. Additionally, prospective studies examining the effectiveness of specific infection prevention measures in spine surgery could help establish evidence-based guidelines for postpandemic surgical care.
