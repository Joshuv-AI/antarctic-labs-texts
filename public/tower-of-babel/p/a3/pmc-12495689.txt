A review of exercise-induced epigenetic modifications in prostate tissue: implications for gene expression and tumor progression in prostate cancer
Authors: Keng Ye, Zunjin Ke, Weima Gesang, Ke Liang, Melika Malek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical exercise is associated with a lower incidence and development of prostate cancer (PCa), according to epidemiologic research. In particular, it modifies the levels of endogenous hormones such as insulin, testosterone, and insulin-like growth factor. Moreover, physical exercise of various sorts and durations may affect the tumor microenvironment differently. There is growing evidence that physical exercise is associated with epigenetic alterations in prostate tissue, although its consequences on the prostate are yet unknown. It is well recognized that epigenetic changes, including DNA methylation and histone modifications, interfere with important biological functions in malignancies, including tumor development, tissue invasion, and metastasis, and they also increase the likelihood of genetic abnormalities. Notably, exercise has been shown to have anticancer, DNA methylation, histone, microRNA, lncRNA, and apoptosis-inducing properties. Exercise capacity to alter biomarkers of DNA methylation, histone changes, miRNA, lncRNA, and apoptotic induction makes predictive diagnostics possible, enabling patient stratification and early identification of at-risk patients. Thus, this review summarizes research on the molecular processes and therapeutic applications of exercise in prostate tissue, particularly in the context of prostate cancer.Keywords: Exercise, Epigenetic modifications, Prostate cancer, Apoptosis, DNA methylation

Introduction

According to the most recent GLOBOCAN estimates, prostate cancer (PCa) accounted for approximately 1.5 million new cases and over 375,000 deaths globally in 2022, making it one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in men [1]. The survival rate of PCa patients has increased due to early identification and treatment advancements [2]. However, these treatments, such as androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and surgical prostatectomy, can have long-term adverse effects. For example, ADT is associated with loss of bone and lean mass, as well as increased fat mass and metabolic imbalances. Radical prostatectomy often results in urinary incontinence and decreased quality of life [3, 4].

To better understand prostate cancer progression and therapeutic resistance, recent research has turned to the molecular mechanisms driving the disease, particularly epigenetic regulation. Both genetic and epigenetic changes can lead to PCa [5, 6]. DNA methylation changes are routinely observed in preneoplastic lesions, suggesting that epigenetic modifications begin sooner than genetic ones [7]. However, epigenetic changes play a crucial role in both the development and progression of PCa. DNA methylation is a promising source of biomarkers because of several features, including its stability over time, even in fixed samples, its ability to be measured using straightforward, quantitative techniques, and its ability to be found in a variety of bodily fluids, including blood, urine, semen, saliva, and more [8, 9]. In addition to changes in DNA methylation, PCa is associated with the upregulation of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), such as DNMT1, and alterations in demethylating enzymes, contributing to abnormal gene silencing [10]. Furthermore, several investigations have documented that PCa is associated with changes in global levels of histone alterations [11].

Several modifiable and non-modifiable factors are known to contribute to epigenetic alterations in the prostate. Obesity, for instance, creates a pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative environment that can lead to global DNA hypomethylation and promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes [12]. Aging, a major risk factor for prostate cancer, is associated with epigenetic drift, including aberrant methylation patterns and histone modifications [13]. Additionally, smoking and alcohol consumption have been shown to induce DNA damage and disrupt methylation enzymes, while sedentary behavior exacerbates many of these effects by promoting systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [14, 15]. These influences can work synergistically to accelerate prostate carcinogenesis through epigenetic reprogramming. Understanding these factors provides a critical framework for appreciating how exercise may counteract or normalize these epigenetic disruptions, supporting its potential role in prostate cancer prevention and adjunctive care [16].

Given the reversible nature of epigenetic marks, there is growing interest in non-pharmacological strategies, such as exercise, that might favorably influence these processes. Indeed, with the rise of exercise oncology, exercise or physical activity has been given more attention in the context of cancer patient care [17]. Improvements in health-related outcomes for PCa patients are consistently reported by several clinical trials [18, 19] and epidemiological research [20]. Furthermore, studies using preclinical mouse models have shown that an exercise stimulus can lower the number of PCa tumors and delay their growth [21, 22]. These findings offer a compelling mechanistic argument for testing the effects of clinical trials on cancer outcomes. Though several theories have been proposed, the exact pathways via which exercise affects tumor biology remain unclear [23].

When skeletal muscles contract during exercise, they release signaling molecules known as myokines which are a subset of cytokines with autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine effects [24]. These molecules, including IL-6, IL-15, and irisin, play roles in tissue remodeling, metabolism, and immune regulation [25, 26]. Emerging evidence suggests that myokines exert anti-tumor effects by modulating the tumor microenvironment, enhancing immune surveillance, and even directly suppressing cancer cell proliferation [27]. Although most studies have focused on breast and colon cancers, similar mechanisms may influence prostate cancer development through systemic and localized effects [28, 29]. According to research on animals, moderate-intensity exercise may also cause apoptosis and prevent the growth of cancer cells [30].

Obesity, a modifiable lifestyle factor, is associated with a pro-inflammatory and prothrombotic environment that may contribute to the aggressive behavior of prostate cancer [31]. However, the precise mechanisms remain unclear. Exercise can change the biology of PCa and lessen some of the negative consequences of obesity in these individuals, according to mounting observational and experimental data [32]. The capacity of exercise to influence epigenetic alterations including DNA methylation, histone modifications, microRNA (miRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), and triggered apoptosis is one of its many health advantages [33]. These changes are essential for individualized healthcare methods. According to recent research, exercise may have an impact on the prostate tumor tissue’s overall epigenetic changes. Exercise has been shown to reduce the overexpression of DNMTs in prostate tumor tissue and simultaneously increase global DNA methylation, suggesting a normalization of aberrant methylation patterns rather than a simple upregulation of DNMT activity [34]. When combined, these discoveries point to the possibility of an epigenetic connection between exercise and PCa and might constitute a biological mechanism via which exercise inhibits the progression of cancer.

It is important to consider that observed epigenetic changes in exercised PCa models may reflect secondary effects of reduced tumor burden, rather than direct molecular outcomes of exercise [35–37]. Additionally, individuals may vary in their susceptibility to certain stressors or adaptations triggered by exercise, including reduced apoptotic sensitivity or unintended molecular responses. Advanced molecular profiling can help identify such differential responses and guide personalized interventions. Through inducing apoptosis, epigenetic changes, and improving adaptations in white adipose tissue by reducing visceral adiposity and boosting skeletal mass and strength, exercise can act as a focused preventative strategy to reduce these risks [36, 38]. Exercise’s incorporation into individualized healthcare goes beyond athletics to include the treatment of chronic illnesses. Therefore, the purpose of this review is to synthesize current preclinical and clinical evidence on how exercise affects epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, microRNAs, lncRNAs, and apoptosis, in the context of prostate cancer. By doing so, we aim to highlight potential therapeutic and preventive applications within oncology and personalized medicine frameworks.

The beneficial effects of exercise on DNA methylation and histone modifications

Epigenetics is the study of changes in gene expression that are not caused by changes in DNA sequence; in other words, environmental factors and behavior may influence how genes work [39]. Recently, there has been a greater focus on the role that epigenetics plays in the onset and progression of chronic illnesses, such as PCa (Fig. 1). Histone acetylation and DNA promoter methylation are now the best-characterized epigenetic changes linked to PCa [10]. However nothing is known about how exercise affects the epigenetic changes in PCa tumors.

This figure illustrates the major epigenetic mechanisms implicated in prostate cancer development. DNA methylation leads to silencing of tumor suppressor genes. Histone modifications, such as acetylation and methylation, influence chromatin structure and gene accessibility. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression by targeting mRNAs, while long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) modulate transcriptional and epigenetic processes. These alterations collectively contribute to cancer progression

Cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) dinucleotides, which are areas where a cytosine is followed by a guanine residue in the 5′ to 3′ orientation, contain the bulk of DNA methylation marks. Gene silence is often the outcome of CpG island methylation. DNMT enzymes catalyze the insertion of methylation marks [40]. DNA methylation patterns appear to be dysregulated when cancer progresses. For instance, global DNA hypomethylation and site-specific hypermethylation of certain genes implicated in the development of cancer are characteristics of cancer cells [10, 41]. In prostate cancer, these epigenetic processes are often disrupted, leading to altered gene expression and tumor progression.

Both DNA hypo- and hyper-methylation have been linked to the advancement of PCa cells. Chromosome instability, metastatic PCa, and the advancement of the illness have all been linked to global cytosine hypomethylation [42, 43]. A significant percentage of PCa patients also have hypomethylation in repetitive DNA sequences, such as LINE1 [44]. While the bulk of genes in PCa are characterized by site-specific hypermethylation, including more than 50 aberrantly hypermethylated genes, there have been a few particular genes that are hypomethylated [10]. Significantly, early high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia lesions also exhibit many of these methylation events [45, 46], indicating that aberrant DNA methylation alterations take place early in the carcinogenesis process.

To understand how exercise may influence prostate cancer epigenetically, it is essential to first outline the common disruptions in DNA methylation and histone modifications observed in PCa. While DNA methylation changes can silence tumor suppressor genes early in disease progression, histone modifications, such as acetylation and methylation, also regulate chromatin structure and gene expression. These processes are often dysregulated in prostate tumors, contributing to cancer development and resistance to therapy.

Histone proteins interact with DNA and one another in the nucleus to form a structure known as the nucleosome. Changes in several histone-modifying enzymes, including HDAC, histone methyltransferases, and histone demethylases, are frequently linked to the reported histone modifications in PCa [10]. Either arginine or lysine residues can be methylated by histones. The family of protein arginine methyltransferases may mono- or di-methylate arginine, which activates transcription [47, 48]. A wide variety of histone methylation transferases that are members of the SET domain protein family may mono-, di-, or tri-methylate lysine residues [49]. It’s interesting to note that histone methylation marks were once believed to be permanent. The deregulation of histone acetylation in cancer has been the subject of a significant amount of research. A closed DNA structure that is transcriptionally suppressed is created when DNA firmly binds with histones via their protein tails [41]. This is typically accompanied by a significant degree of DNMT-regulated DNA methylation at cytosine residues. Aberrant patterns of histone and nonhistone protein acetylation are also seen in PCa cells. Prostate hyperplasia has been demonstrated to have lower HDAC activity than metastatic prostate cancer cells [50, 51]. A significant drop in cytokeratin 18 staining and an increase in cytokeratin staining indicate that a 4-fold rise in HDAC1 protein in PC3 PCa cells causes a 1.5-fold increase in cell proliferation and an overall loss in cell differentiation [52, 53]. Given that loss of androgen sensitivity is correlated with either enhanced nuclear localization of HDAC4 or accumulation of HDAC1 protein as shown by immunohistochemistry, increased expression of HDAC may be especially significant in the transition to androgen independence [53]. These dysregulated epigenetic patterns form a foundation upon which exercise may exert regulatory effects, as explored in the following sections.

In addition to histone changes, DNA methylation plays a major role in gene silencing and genomic instability in prostate cancer. This mechanism is especially relevant in the early stages of carcinogenesis. Emerging evidence suggests that exercise may help normalize these alterations by modulating DNA methylation patterns and histone-modifying enzymes. For instance, in a study on rats with AT1 prostate tumors implanted which were randomly assigned to exercise training or control, the tumor tissue was examined for histone acetylation, DNA methylation, and microRNA expression. The findings showed that trained and sedentary rats seemed to express miR-27a-5p differently. Additionally, exercise reduced DNMT mRNA expression in prostate tumor tissue and increased global DNA methylation levels, suggesting a normalization of aberrant methylation patterns [35] (Table 1). The relationship between self-reported vigorous physical activity and the development of prostate cancer to a metastatic-lethal phenotype was examined in a Seattle-based cohort of patients with clinically localized prostate cancer and prospective follow-up for outcomes. Prostate cancer tissue samples from patients with different levels of physical activity were analyzed to examine associations between exercise behavior and DNA methylation patterns. According to the results, individuals who engaged in vigorous physical exercise at least once a week in the year before diagnosis (about 79% of the cohort) had a considerably lower risk of developing metastatic, fatal prostate cancer than those who engaged in vigorous physical activity less frequently. Nine differentially methylated CpG sites were identified in the promoter region of the CRACR2A gene, with significantly lower methylation (hypomethylation) observed in men who engaged in vigorous physical activity at least once per week. This hypomethylation was associated with lower risk of progression to metastatic-lethal prostate cancer [54].

Summary of animal studies investigating the effects of exercise on epigenetic and molecular markers relevant to prostate cancer

The ability of exercise training to reduce PCa-induced testicular dysfunction was examined in a study. Fifty male Wistar Unilever rats were used as a model, and they were split into four experimental groups at random [55]. In two animal groups (PCa groups), prostate cancer was induced using a combination of chemical carcinogens and hormonal manipulation. A moderate-intensity aerobic treadmill training program was administered to one PCa group and one control group. The protocol consisted of treadmill running 5 days per week for 13 weeks, at a speed of 18 m/min on a 5% incline for 60 min per session. This regimen corresponds to approximately 60–70% of maximal oxygen consumption, a range commonly classified as moderate-intensity endurance training in rodent models. At the end of the training period, the animals were sacrificed and samples of the testicles, sperm, prostate, and serum were collected for histological and biochemical analysis. Serum testosterone levels, as well as sperm concentration and shape, were measured. According to the findings, PCa had a detrimental effect on testicular function, which was evident in the halt of spermatogenesis. Reduced testis blood flow brought on by oxidative stress-induced DNA damage may also be a factor in germ cell death and the resulting spermatogenic dysfunction. The PCa mice also showed enhanced ketone body metabolism, decreased glycolytic pathway use, and branched-chain amino acid buildup. According to Matos et al. [55], treadmill training activated DNA repair pathways and reversed metabolic alterations associated with PCa in a rat model.

Within the framework of Predictive, Preventive, and Personalized Medicine (PPPM/3PM), exercise has emerged as a potential tool to identify and modulate epigenetic abnormalities such as aberrant DNA methylation and histone modifications in prostate cancer. In PCa, abnormal DNMT and HDAC activity contribute to disrupted gene regulation and cancer progression. Studies have shown that exercise can downregulate DNMT expression and modulate global methylation patterns, potentially restoring a more balanced epigenetic state [35, 55]. In this context, exercise not only serves a preventive function but may also inform personalized interventions by helping to stratify patients based on their epigenetic response profiles to physical activity. Especially in PCa patients, their cytocompatibility facilitates targeted and preventative use. A safe, efficient, and individualized approach to managing bacteria is provided by exercise-based therapeutics that can be tailored for DNA methylation and histone changes.

The beneficial effects of exercise on MicroRNAs in PCa

The discovery of non-coding RNAs, such as microRNA (miRNA), which have significant biological roles, has been a significant breakthrough in molecular biology over the past ten years. Since their discovery, miRNAs have demonstrated several encouraging prospects in a variety of therapeutic applications, including both treatment and diagnostics. Dysregulation of several miRNAs has been documented in prostate cancer, contributing to tumor growth, metastasis, and therapy resistance. These miRNAs may be promising indicators of PCa risk, progression, and possible therapeutic targeting. Likewise, there are significant differences in miRNA expression levels based on muscle disease and training style [56, 57]. Different endurance and strength training methods have been shown to affect the expression patterns of miRNAs in serum and plasma, including miRNA-208b, miRNA-486, miRNA-29b, miRNA-1, miRNA-133a, miRNA-208a, miRNA-206, and miRNA-499 [58, 59]. These miRNAs may also be involved in muscle regeneration [60], myogenesis, and other muscular atrophy models [61]. However, additional research has revealed a distinct miRNA expression profile in some cancer forms, including PCa [62].

Exercise has been shown to influence circulating and tissue-specific miRNA profiles, potentially counteracting the miRNA disruptions seen in PCa. In an F344 rat model of metastatic PCa, the effects of exercise-induced extracellular vesicles (EVs) on cancer development were examined. RNA sequencing analysis revealed significant changes in the RNA content of EVs collected before and immediately after a forced wheel running exercise protocol, in which rats were made to run at a controlled speed on a motorized wheel to ensure uniform exercise exposure. mRNA was the predominant RNA biotype in EVs, with miRNA and rRNA following closely after. Differentially expressed RNAs’ molecular roles mirrored several physiological processes in the parental cells, such as protein folding, metabolism, and immune response regulation brought on by exercise. Exercise-induced EVs administered intravenously to F344 rats with orthotopically implanted syngeneic prostate cancer cells PLS10 showed a 35% decrease in the volume of the main tumor and may have attenuated lung metastases [63]. Examining the effects of exercise training on body composition (muscle strength) and specific myogenic microRNAs (miRNAs) (miRNA-29b, miRNA-1, and miRNA-133) in PCa patients on androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) was another study objective. Blood insulin levels significantly decreased as a result of the ADT and exercise intervention, whereas body composition, free testosterone, IL-6, and myogenic miRNA plasma levels significantly increased [64]. Given that exercise can reshape systemic miRNA profiles, its impact may extend beyond tumor cells to immune cells such as natural killer (NK) cells, which play a critical role in cancer immunosurveillance.

NK cells, a vital component of the innate immune system, are highly responsive to physical activity and are regulated, in part, by miRNA signaling. Following brief bouts of exercise, their numbers can rise significantly in circulation, enhancing the body’s early response against tumor cells. Recent studies have shown that exercise modulates miRNA and gene expression profiles in NK cells, potentially linking these immune shifts to improved cancer defense mechanisms. In a study involving young healthy males undergoing high-intensity interval cycling, brief exercise significantly altered the expression of 23 miRNAs and 986 genes in NK cells. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that many of these miRNA-gene interactions were linked to cancer-related pathways, including p53 signaling and prostate cancer [65]. In another study, hsa_circ_0006620 expressions in PCa cells and tissues were examined using fluorescence in situ hybridization and RT-qPCR after high-throughput sequencing. The downstream targets of hsa_circ_0006620 were identified using the luciferase reporter assay. Hsa_circ_0006620’s involvement in PCa was examined using in vivo carcinogenesis and metastatic tests. The findings demonstrated that PCa cells and tissues had higher levels of hsa_circ_0006620 expression. Downregulating Hsa_circ_0006620 prevented PCa cells from proliferating and migrating both in vivo and in vitro. The luciferase results confirmed that hexokinase 2 (HK2) and miR-502-3p were downstream targets of hsa_circ_0006620. Following hsa_circ_0006620 silencing, PCa cell movement was reversed by HK2 overexpression or miR-502-3p suppression. The study also discovered that, following hsa_circ_0006620 silencing, aerobic glycolysis was reversed by overexpression of HK2 or inhibition of prostate cancer [66]. In personalized medicine frameworks, exercise can be investigated for its predictive ability in detecting aberrant miRNAs and for customizing anticancer therapies. They are also potential options for targeted anticancer therapy and prevention due to their safety and bioavailability.

The beneficial effects of exercise on lncRNAs in PCa

Certain lncRNAs, such as PlncRNA, are overexpressed in PCa and contribute to tumor growth and androgen signaling activation. Meanwhile, aerobic exercise appears to downregulate these oncogenic lncRNAs, offering a potential mechanism for its antitumor effects. In one study, obese mice were used to assess the impact of aerobic exercise in a BPH model. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, lipid and sex hormone metabolites, relative protein expression, PCa-up-regulated long noncoding RNA (PlncRNA), and androgen receptor (AR) mRNA levels were examined in obese C57BL/6J mice in the control group, obesity group (OB), and obesity group plus exercise (OB + E) after eight weeks of training. The findings showed that OB mice’s fat-mass accumulation was delayed by aerobic activity training. OB mice showed a considerable decrease in prostate volume and lumen area, followed by a modest rise after aerobic training. The OB group’s epithelial volume density was greater than the control group’s. Additionally, in OB + E mice, aerobic exercise increased (p < 0.01) blood levels of high-density lipoprotein while decreasing levels of low-density lipoprotein, triglycerides, and free fatty acids. Furthermore, after aerobic activity, the OB + E mice’s estradiol/testosterone (E2/T) hormone ratio resembled that of the control group. Mechanistically, in the prostates of OB + E mice, aerobic exercise inhibited the PlncRNA-AR/androgen signaling pathway through the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B axis [67]. These results highlight how exercise affects lncRNA, which might be used in predictive and preventative medicine to customize anticancer treatments.

The beneficial effects of exercise on apoptosis in PCa

Apoptotic resistance is a hallmark of advanced PCa, with altered androgen signaling and evasion of cell death pathways. Androgen signaling is one route that plays a significant role in PCa. Normal prostate differentiation and function, as well as PCa development and survival, depend on androgens acting through the androgen receptor (AR), a ligand-activated transcription factor that is a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily [68, 69]. In line with this, prostate epithelial cells exhibit shrinkage, increased apoptosis, and decreased cell proliferation when the number of circulating androgens is reduced, either surgically (castration) or by androgen deprivation treatment [70, 71]. The majority of prostate cancer cells, particularly those in late stages, have functioning AR, despite this nonresponsiveness to androgens. This suggests that the AR pathway may be active throughout prostate carcinogenesis. Prostate tumor cells may proliferate more readily in an androgen-depleted environment due to soluble factor-mediated communication between the cells and their surroundings, in addition to these changes that lead to more sensitive AR signaling. It has been hypothesized that growth factors that are typically produced by stromal cells during androgen deprivation can promote the proliferation of epithelial cells by activating AR without the need for a ligand [72]. Numerous recent papers have studied the cross-talk between prostate epithelial tumor cells and the stroma around them [73, 74].

Frequent exercise has been shown to prevent the development of cancer [75]. Exercise lowers the risk of cancer death and recurrence in cancer survivors [76]. The relationship between exercise and a lower risk of cancer and a higher chance of survival is still unclear, though. Exercise may have an impact on the processes controlling the development of cancer cells since serum from healthy persons who exercise lowers proliferation and triggers apoptosis in a variety of cancer cells. Indeed, exercise has been shown to restore apoptotic sensitivity in PCa models by modulating key signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and p53/caspase-3. Serum from patients with advanced prostate or colorectal cancer was examined in a study following a 12-week course of whole-body electromyostimulation training (20 min per session, 2×/week; frequency 85 Hz; pulse width 350 µs; 6 s stimulation, 4 s rest), a safe but efficient resistance exercise for patients with physical weakness. Using cell growth and death tests (5-bromo-2’-deoxyuridine incorporation, cell counting, DNA fragmentation), it was revealed that serum from these advanced cancer patients reduces proliferation and promotes apoptosis of human prostate and colon cancer cells in vitro. Electric pulse stimulation of human primary myotubes simulating exercise showed that myotube media conditioned by electric pulse stimulation also reduces the viability of human cancer cells. Exercise-sensitive genes were found in human PCa cells using gene expression analysis employing a multiplex array of cancer-associated genes and quantitative RT-PCR. These genes may be involved in the exercise-mediated control of malignant cell growth and apoptosis [77] (Table 2).

The tumor suppressor p53, cell cycle, and metabolism are all regulated by the circadian clock [78]. Exercise can help prevent prostate disorders in several ways, but more research is needed to determine how it affects the genes and proteins that control the prostate’s circadian rhythm. In 18-month-old mice that underwent combined training, the investigation confirmed the expression of REV-ERBα (Nr1d1), Bmal1, apoptosis, tumor suppressors, energy metabolism indicators, and androgen receptors in the prostatic microenvironment. There were two sets of C57BL/6 J mice: six-month-old and eighteen-month-old. A combined physical training regimen consisting of 5×/week (3 days aerobic and 2 days strength) was carried out by jogging on a treadmill (40–60% incremental load test) and climbing strength training (40–50% maximal repetition test). The results demonstrated that combined physical activity during aging caused the prostate’s levels of Bmal1 to rise and REV-ERBα to fall. Alongside these findings, the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α proteins decreased, while the PI3K/AKT and p53/PTEN/caspase 3 pathways increased, suggesting the possibility of apoptosis [79]. Growth factors and androgens are key regulators of the lipid metabolism process, and altered lipid metabolism is a significant feature of neoplastic cells. The impact of physical resistance training on apoptosis and lipid metabolism in the adult Wistar rat prostate was examined. Sedentary and physical resistance training were represented by two experimental groups. As part of a physical resistance workout regimen, rats were required to jump in water three days a week for 13 weeks while carrying a weight load affixed to their chests. Rats were put under anesthesia and slaughtered for blood and prostatic analysis two days following the final training session. Exercise was shown to increase feeding efficiency, reduce weight gain, control the serum lipid profile, and alter the levels of free testosterone and insulin-like growth factor-1. Additionally, trained rats’ blood and prostates showed decreased expression of lysosome membrane proteins, increased expression of cluster of differentiation 36, sterol regulatory element binding protein-1, and sterol regulatory element-binding protein cleavage-activating protein. These findings were supported by the fact that trained rats had higher caspase-3 expression and a lower BCL-2/Bax index ratio than sedentary animals [80]. This altered ratio reflects enhanced activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, as an increased BAX/BCL-2 ratio is a well-established marker of pro-apoptotic signaling in prostate cancer cells. In addition, although the intervention lasted 13 weeks, this duration is consistent with other rodent studies that have successfully modeled prostate cancer development and progression within similar time frames. It is considered sufficient to induce measurable molecular and histological changes [81, 82]. However, longer-term interventions could provide additional insights into sustained effects.

Another study examined how voluntary exercise affected the development and spread of prostate PC-3 and human pancreatic Panc-1 cancers in immunodeficient mice. Human pancreatic cancer Panc-1 cells were injected subcutaneously into female severe combined immunodeficient (SCID) mice, whereas human prostate cancer PC-3 cells were put subcutaneously into male SCID mice. Panc-1 and PC-3 tumor development was inhibited by voluntary running wheel exercise for 63 days, beginning one week before the SCID mice received a subcutaneous injection of Panc-1 or PC-3 tumor cells. Both the male and female mice consumed more food and water as a result of the training program. While exercise did not affect body weight, it did reduce the size of the male and female mice’s paradidymis and parametrial fat pads, respectively. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that voluntary running wheel exercise enhanced apoptosis as seen by increased expression of caspase-3 (active form) in the Panc-1 and PC-3 cancers, while also inhibiting proliferation as evidenced by reduced mitosis. The percentage of mitotic cells to apoptotic cells in PC-3 and Panc-1 tumors was reduced by 32% and 38%, respectively, by voluntary running wheel exercise [83]. These results demonstrate the predictive and preventative value of exercise, especially for susceptible groups like PCa patients.

Expert recommendations and outlook

Particularly when included into the PPPM/3PM framework, exercise offers specific chances for innovation in the domains of cancer management. According to the current assessment, the potential of exercise goes beyond its positive effects on general health and offers useful applications that tackle important issues in contemporary healthcare. Future studies should concentrate on predictive diagnostics that use exercise to find early indicators of histones, microRNA, lncRNA, apoptosis, and DNA methylation. These biomarkers can offer vital information about a person’s risk profiles, allowing for the prediction of health issues before they become clinically noticeable. In domains like sports medicine and cancer, where knowledge of vulnerability to injuries or aberrant gene expression may revolutionize cancer treatment approaches, this predictive ability is particularly crucial. Furthermore, further research should be done on the specific preventative uses of exercise. Their capacity to alter histones, microRNA, lncRNA, DNA methylation, and apoptosis makes them a useful treatment for PCa patients. While keeping the precision needed for successful results, this strategy may be expanded for a wider demographic reach. Healthcare professionals may improve preventative techniques that are both economical and environmentally sustainable by customizing exercise interventions to each patient’s requirements and phenotypic characteristics.

Given the promising results from preclinical and observational human studies, further research is warranted to explore the potential of exercise-based personalized treatment plans in precision medicine. To treat a variety of medical diseases, these procedures may be tailored according to metabolic, genetic, and epigenetic aspects. For example, tailored exercise regimens in terms of time and kind may improve performance and recuperation in PCa patients, reduce inflammation and oxidative damage, or trigger apoptosis in PCa patients. Optimizing treatment effects will be ensured by further personalizing these therapies through the integration of sophisticated phenotyping and molecular profiling techniques into clinical settings. Going forward, a multidisciplinary effort involving researchers, physicians, and policymakers is needed to integrate exercise into healthcare. Additionally, to guarantee the safety, effectiveness, and scalability of exercise treatments, defined standards and quality assurance procedures must be developed. In the development of contemporary healthcare, exercise offers a viable and creative way to solve some of the most urgent issues in the field of cancer, as healthcare systems progressively shift toward proactive and individualized methods.

Conclusions

Physical exercise has demonstrated promising effects on key epigenetic mechanisms implicated in prostate cancer, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, microRNAs, lncRNAs, and apoptosis regulation (Fig. 2). Evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies suggests that regular physical activity can modulate these molecular pathways to suppress tumor progression and improve treatment responsiveness. While much of the mechanistic insight comes from animal models, emerging human data point to the potential of exercise as a non-invasive, adjunctive strategy in prostate cancer prevention and care.

Physical exercise modulates several molecular pathways in prostate cancer. It reduces DNMT1 and HDAC1 levels, thereby restoring global DNA methylation and gene expression. It enhances p53 and caspase-3 signaling and alters the BAX/BCL-2 balance, promoting apoptosis. Exercise also upregulates tumor-suppressive miRNAs, such as miRNA-133 and miRNA-29. These changes collectively contribute to antitumor effects in prostate tissue

Future research should prioritize longitudinal clinical studies, standardized exercise protocols, and biomarker validation to confirm these findings and facilitate translation into personalized exercise-based interventions for prostate cancer patients.
