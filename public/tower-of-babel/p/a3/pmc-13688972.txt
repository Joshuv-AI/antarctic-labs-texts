A 3-step static mechanistic modeling approach to predicting transporter- and enzyme-mediated interaction signals of direct oral anticoagulants
Authors: Mostafa ben Ahmed, Anne M. Filppula, Aleksi Tornio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are susceptible to drug-drug interactions involving both transport and metabolism. The relative contribution of intestinal transporters in DOAC pharmacokinetics, however, is uncharacterized, hindering the prediction of their drug interactions. In this study, we examined potential interactions of select precipitants with apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban using a 3-step static mechanistic combinatorial modeling approach. In this approach, we (1) calculated apparent estimates of the fractions of efflux transport (fe) of the 4 DOACs by the intestinal breast cancer resistance protein and intestinal permeability glycoprotein (P-gp), (2) validated our estimates, and (3) used them to simulate unstudied drug interactions with the anticoagulants. We estimated P-gp and breast cancer resistance protein transport to play a similar role in the interactions of apixaban and rivaroxaban (fe_P-gp = 0.21, fe_breast cancer resistance protein = 0.13). Additionally, the role of P-gp was prominent in the interactions affecting dabigatran etexilate (fe_P-gp = 0.61) and less so in those affecting edoxaban (fe_P-gp = 0.38). Using our apparent estimates, we predicted 144 interaction signals of 47 drugs with the 4 anticoagulants. All signals showed potential change >25% in the area under the curve of DOACs, warranting further investigation. Using our combined model, we were able to uncover new drug interaction signals, including previously unrecognized signals by kinase inhibitors.Significance StatementMultiple enzymes and transporters affect direct oral anticoagulant pharmacokinetics. Drugs interacting with the same proteins may increase the levels of direct oral anticoagulants, leading to bleeding complications. Current methods predicting such interactions focus only on individual enzymes or transporters, limiting the prediction of cases where both may be involved. In this study, we combined multiple models to capture the complexity of interactions affecting direct oral anticoagulants. Using our validated model, we predicted 144 drug-drug interaction signals with potential clinical significance.

Introduction

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are widely used for the treatment and prevention of thromboembolic diseases.1 In contrast to warfarin, their elimination is only partially dependent on cytochrome P450 metabolism. DOACs are additionally known substrates of the efflux transporters breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) and permeability glycoprotein (P-gp).2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Bleeding is the most clinically significant adverse effect of DOACs. It is a concentration-dependent effect, which may result from inhibiting DOAC metabolism and transport. This may be confirmed by the collective of in vitro and clinical evidence, which suggests a simultaneous inhibition of DOAC-metabolizing enzymes and efflux transporters in clinically significant interactions, which in turn increases the levels of DOACs.3, 4, 5, 6,8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14

Although the role of metabolizing enzymes in the pharmacokinetic drug interactions of DOACs is well characterized, the exact contribution of intestinal efflux transporters is not clear. Additionally, the common practice of evaluating single enzymes or transporters in static modeling studies further limits the capture of potential drug-DOAC interaction signals where multiple proteins may be involved.

In this work, we focus on predicting potential drug interactions involving apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban as objects of pharmacokinetic drug interactions. We use a systematic 3-step approach where we (1) establish the role of intestinal transporters in drug-DOAC interactions by estimating the fractions of the drugs transported by BCRP and P-gp using clinical observations. We then (2) validate our estimates using additional clinical drug interaction observations and (3) predict new drug interaction signals of DOACs as object drugs (an affected drug in a drug interaction) with select precipitants. In all 3 steps, we use a static mechanistic model which considers the cumulative effect of modulating all potentially involved intestinal transporters and intestinal and hepatic metabolizing enzymes.

Materials and methods

Data and software

In all 3 steps of this study, we used the Certara Drug Interaction Database (DIDB, Certara, accessed between September 2023 and June 2024) to collect clinical drug interaction observations, precipitant maximum plasma concentrations, enzyme and transporter reversible inhibition kinetics, and enzyme time-dependent and induction kinetics. Additional model variables were collected from various sources (Supplemental Tables 1–7). All analyses and calculations in this study were made in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, version 2410 build 16.0.18129.20200).

Step 1: Estimating the role of BCRP and P-gp

To estimate the fractions of DOACs undergoing BCRP- and P-gp efflux transport (fe) in the intestine, we performed a model-based sensitivity analysis (step 1), where we fitted our model to clinical observations from DOAC clinical trials with index inhibitors. The analysis used equation 1 to simulate 9 drug interaction clinical trials that tested clarithromycin, erythromycin, and ketoconazole with DOACs (Supplemental Table 1). The 3 precipitants in this step were selected based on their ability to inhibit DOAC-metabolizing enzymes and transporters to differing degrees (Supplemental Table 7). This allowed the estimation of apparent fe values, which simulate the clinical disposition of DOACs.

In each of the 9 simulations, all parameters were substituted into the model except fe. Each simulation was then equated to the corresponding clinical observation, and the model’s fe of the object drug was tuned to the highest accuracy and least bias in reproducing the observed clinical effect. We used the root mean of squared errors as a measure of model accuracy, the geometric mean of fold errors (GMFE) for bias,15,22,23 and the 100% remainder of absolute bioavailabilities (100%−Fabs) of DOACs as constraints to the unidentified fe values (Supplemental Table 2). In all iterations of this exercise, the sum of fractions transported by BCRP and P-gp equaled the defined constraints (fe_BCRP+fe_P−gp=100%−Fabs). Because of the absence from the available literature, Fa, Fg, and FH were set to 100%, respectively, assuming negligible loss of the object drug caused by absorption, gut metabolism, and hepatic first-pass effect.(5)RMSE=∑r=1n(r)2nWhere r is the error calculated as the difference between a predicted and an observed value,(6)GMFE=∏i=1n10|logf|nand f is the fold error calculated as the ratio of a predicted value to an observed value.

Step 2: fe estimates validation

Following the estimation of apparent fe values, we validated our estimates by measuring their ability to reproduce 28 clinical trial observations of 13 precipitants with DOACs (Supplemental Table 3) (step 2). To simulate the trials, we used the same model described above (eq. 1), and to validate our estimates against trial observations, we used the same measures of accuracy and bias as in step 1. Root mean squared errors = 0 denoted the highest accuracy, and GMFE <2 was our cutoff for acceptable bias. Besides the 28 observations, 22 trial observations were excluded for using DOACs in microdoses.

The validated fe estimates were then used to predict worst-case DOAC interaction signals with a select panel of precipitants.

Step 3: Prediction of worst-case drug interaction signals

In this chapter (step 3), we modeled a select group of drugs as potential precipitants affecting the levels of apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban. The precipitants were selected based on their recently confirmed status as BCRP inhibitors in vitro,24 in addition to their previously known in vitro inhibition of P-gp or CYP3A4, or both.25 Where applicable, metabolites that inhibited any DOAC-metabolizing enzyme were included in the simulations.

The same model described in step 1 above—excluding the enzyme induction component—was used to screen DOACs for drug interaction signals with the 47 drugs. We excluded enzyme induction to give worst-case estimates of the predicted signals.

Model input variables

Aside from the apparent fe variables which were derived and validated in steps 1 and 2, the following sections define other model input variables included in steps 1, 2, and 3.

Precipitants’ input variables

Precipitants’ Cmax, enzyme, and transporter kinetics (Ki, KI, kinact, Emax, EC50) were collected from the DIDB. For Cmax, in steps 1 and 2, priority was given to clinical trials in healthy volunteers where the precipitants were administered in the same doses as the clinical trials (Supplemental Table 3). In step 3, however, the precipitant Cmax was selected to correspond to the highest Food and Drug Administration–approved maintenance dose.

In steps 1, 2, and 3, the effect of metabolites was deliberately limited to hepatic metabolizing enzymes, assuming their formation following the parent drug’s metabolism and therefore their limited access to intestinal metabolizing enzymes and transporters. Additionally, in all steps, when metabolite concentrations were measured following a dose other than the precipitant dosing described above, their concentrations were extrapolated to correspond with the selected precipitant dose, assuming linear pharmacokinetics. Finally, when metabolite concentrations were not measured in humans, animal data describing the metabolite-to-parent ratio were used in the extrapolations. Otherwise, the effect of metabolites was not included when lacking both Cmax and data supporting their extrapolation.

When selecting enzyme and transporter kinetics variables, priority was given to in vitro experiments using regulator-recommended substrates and enzyme systems when applicable.21,26,27 When multiple reports were made of the same in vitro kinetics, we calculated the median as a measure of centrality. We additionally halved the IC50 as a substitute for Ki when Ki was not reported. In dabigatran etexilate models where CES1 and CES2 were involved, to simplify the model, we modeled only the CES enzyme with the least inhibition. This combined the 2 enzymes in a single pathway, showing the least inhibition and the highest conversion of dabigatran etexilate into active dabigatran.

In enzyme induction models, we used protein increase or mRNA increase in place of Emax when a measured Emax was not reported.

Other physiological variables (ie, Qen, Qh), the enzyme induction scaling factor (d), and the fractions of drugs escaping gut metabolism (Fg) were set to their regulator defaults.21,27 Absorption constants (Fa, Ka), enzyme degradation constants (kdeg), and distribution constants (RB, fu,p) were collected from published literature. When unavailable in the literature, fu,p of the metabolite was assumed to equal that of the parent drug. Specifically, however, in step 3, we used the highest recommended21 absorption parameters (Ka = 6 1/h, Fa =1) for the precipitants, and we assumed Fg as 1 as a measure for predicting worst-case drug interaction signals. Detailed references of the precipitant variables used in this study can be seen in the supplementary material.

Objects’ input variables: fm, and Fg

In addition to the 2 transporters (BCRP and P-gp), the 4 object drugs were collectively identified as substrates of 5 metabolizing enzymes.3, 4, 5, 6 These were CYP3A4, CYP2J2, CYP1A2, CES1, and CES2. The fractions metabolized (fm) by each enzyme were gathered from the Food and Drug Administration sponsor reviews.3, 4, 5, 6 In contrast, the objects’ Fg variables were not reported in any literature available to the authors and were assumed as Fg = 1 throughout this study.21

Results

Estimation and validation of the fractions transported of DOACs

Dabigatran etexilate was estimated to have the greatest apparent fe by intestinal P-gp (fe = 0.61) (Table 1).3, 4, 5, 6 The same transporter was estimated to transport edoxaban to a lesser extent (fe = 0.38), whereas it was shown to have the least effect on both apixaban and rivaroxaban (fe = 0.21 for each drug). BCRP was also predicted to play an equal yet lesser role than P-gp in the efflux of apixaban and rivaroxaban (fe = 0.13), whereas it played a minimal role in the efflux of edoxaban (fe < 0.01). Validating the estimated fractions in step 2, edoxaban’s validations reproduced clinical observations with the highest accuracy and least bias (Table 2). This was followed by rivaroxaban and apixaban, whereas dabigatran etexilate’s fe estimates showed the least accuracy and highest bias of all validations. All validations, except 1 pair, nonetheless, fell within acceptable GMFE limits (GMFE < 2) with 21 of the 28 validations showing a 0.8–1.25-fold error. Discussion

In our 3-step approach, we estimated the apparent fe of 4 DOACs by 2 main intestinal efflux transporters. We derived our estimates using 9 clinical observations, and we validated them against 28 clinical trials. We then predicted the worst-case estimates of 188 drug-DOAC interactions where complex mechanisms involving both transporters and metabolizing enzymes were considered. Of our 188 signals, 144 predicted potentially significant drug interactions (area under the concentration-time curve change > 25%).

In our combined model, we included the reversible inhibition of intestinal BCRP and intestinal P-gp, intestinal CYP3A4, and relevant hepatic drug-metabolizing enzymes, and the time-dependent inhibition of the same enzymes. Even though our model (eq. 1) could accommodate multiple gut-metabolizing enzymes, in practice, however, the inclusion of intestinal CYP3A4 was limited by the lack of an experimentally measured Fg. Because of the lack of a validated transporter induction model for BCRP and P-gp, only enzyme induction was included. Furthermore, enzyme induction was only considered in steps 1 and 2 to better match the clinical effects of precipitants when estimating and validating apparent fe values. In practice, this was only relevant for rifampin in step 2. In step 3, however, enzyme induction was excluded because of the absence of several induction parameters from the DIDB. This coincided with our aim to predict worst-case estimates of potential drug interaction signals and to simplify high-throughput drug-drug interaction (DDI) screening, which we accomplished by excluding induction components.

In all 3 steps of this study, we assumed changes in the levels of dabigatran etexilate to correspond with those of active dabigatran. This was because, under physiological conditions, only 7% of absorbed dabigatran etexilate is converted by CES1 and CES2 to dabigatran, comprising 100% of the active drug. Any changes in the levels of dabigatran etexilate, assuming adequate CES activity, should therefore correspond with changes in active dabigatran levels, given the parent drug’s minimal bioavailability. This assumption was supported by clinical pharmacogenetic evidence showing decreased prodrug activation in carriers of CES decreased function alleles.28,29

In our validations (step 2), dabigatran etexilate’s apparent fe_P-gp showed the least validation scores in reproducing the effects of rifampin on dabigatran etexilate (Fig. 1 and Supplemental Table 4). This was likely due to the lack of a transporter induction model and the model’s inability to predict the known P-gp induction by the same drug.30 This demonstrates that our model can be directionally wrong for known transporter inducers. In the same step, 22 clinical trial observations were excluded from fe validations for using DOACs in microdoses or for lacking a suitable Cmax of the precipitant (Supplemental Table 10). Where applicable, we included these trials in our prediction step, where most of our predicted signals (step 3) agreed with the included trial observations (n = 9). This includes an omeprazole–rivaroxaban trial, which showed no clinical inhibition but 1.54 AUCR in our predictions. We suspect that the model–excluded but previously known CYP3A4 and CYP1A2 induction played a role in amplifying our predictions31 (Supplemental Table 9). For these reasons, we consider our predictions of known transporter and/or enzyme inducers to be unreliable, without the inclusion of an induction model. Specifically, these were the predictions of aprepitant, lansoprazole, nifedipine, omeprazole, pioglitazone, rifampin, and rifapentine as inducers of CYP3A4; and omeprazole, rifabutin, and rifampin as inducers of CYP1A2; all of which lacked experimental induction variables in the DIDB.

Among the different precipitant therapeutic groups in our signal predictions (step 3), anticancer kinase inhibitors were the largest group. A scarcity of clinical data is available for our tested kinase inhibitors, having pharmacokinetic DDIs with DOACs. In the only available clinical trial, the authors concluded that the study was limited by a small sample size.32 Potential kinase inhibitor-DOAC interactions may be of special relevance, however, given the growing evidence and guidelines promoting the use of DOACs in the prophylaxis and treatment of cancer-associated thromboembolism.33, 34, 35, 36, 37 This relevance is the product of both the in vitro–confirmed CYP3A and P-gp inhibition by several kinase inhibitors38,39; and current cancer-associated thromboembolism guidelines, which generally recommend against the use of DOACs with strong CYP3A and/or P-gp inhibitors.33, 34, 35 Identifying such clinical interactions is therefore key to avoiding anticancer–DOAC interactions, which may increase bleeding risks in a vulnerable group of patients.

In contrast to the lack of clinical interaction studies with DOACs, some of our kinase inhibitors (step 3) have been the subjects of recent modeling work.40,41 Lafaie et al41 tested 11 drugs in vitro for P-gp–mediated interactions with apixaban and rivaroxaban. Six of the 11 drugs were in common with our studied kinase inhibitors. The authors used the basic model Igut/IC50 >10 to predict potentially significant DDI signals. Consistent with our findings, erlotinib, imatinib, lapatinib, and nilotinib showed signals for potential interactions with both apixaban and rivaroxaban. In contrast, however, our simulations for axitinib and sunitinib predicted an average increase of 57% in the AUCR of apixaban and rivaroxaban, whereas Lafaie et al41 suggested low probabilities of interaction for the same combinations (Igut/IC50 < 10).

In another work, Jin et al40 statically modeled axitinib, erlotinib, and gefitinib with apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban for CYP3A-mediated interactions. Compared with our study, CYP3A was included as a greater fm of apixaban and rivaroxaban, and a lesser fraction in the case of edoxaban. Despite these differences, and the authors not considering transporters, Jin et al’40 predicted signals were nonetheless consistent with our findings.

Our predictive model is a combination of several previous models.15, 16, 17, 18,22 A main strength is its consideration of multiple interaction mechanisms and multiple proteins that may be involved in the disposition of an object drug. Additionally, it includes the effects of precipitant metabolites on hepatic metabolism. Given our validations’ agreement with clinical observations (step 2) and our predictions (step 3) with 9 clinical observations (Supplemental Table 9), as well as recent modeling work, this research builds confidence in static mechanistic models as a DDI prediction tool. Our approach may have also helped characterize the role of intestinal efflux transporters in DOAC pharmacokinetics. All of this consolidates the position of static mechanistic models in the prediction of DDI signals of other drug groups. It should be noted, however, that several limitations are associated with the same models and should therefore be considered when interpreting our results.

One such limitation is that our model could only include mechanistic components known, from in vitro evidence, to affect the pharmacokinetics of DOACs. Unrecognized or unquantified mechanisms were therefore not considered. This especially applies to our apparent fe estimates, where we assumed intestinal P-gp and BCRP to be the only players in DOAC absorption (step 1). Our estimates may have consequently overestimated the transporters’ clinical role at the expense of other unknown mechanisms, which potentially affect DOACs’ absorption, namely Fa, Fg, and FH. This is especially applicable in the cases of apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban, where the sum of our assumed fe estimates for both transporters always equals the 100% remainder of absolute bioavailability (100%−Fabs). In these cases, we predict a true clinical efflux fraction by BCRP and P-gp to be lower than our apparent estimates. The same limitation, however, did not apply to dabigatran etexilate, where only P-gp was considered. This is shown in our fe which was estimated lower than 100%−Fabs. Despite this limitation, in the absence of experimentally measured Fa, Fg, and FH variables, our fe estimates conform to the physiological bioavailability relationship: 100%dose−fetotal≥Fabs.

An added limitation is static models’ capacity to incorporate a single, static precipitant concentration as opposed to a dynamic range. This limits their representation of any drug’s concentration-time relationship, and together with their limited number of physiological variables, may limit their applicability to different disease states and patient populations. Nevertheless, this trade of complexity for a simplified model is likely what distinguishes static mechanistic models as a tool for detecting large numbers of drug interaction signals.

In conclusion, this work has provided methods for estimating the approximate contribution of intestinal efflux transporters in DOAC DDIs by combining comprehensive static mechanistic modeling equations with established clinical observations. We also predicted worst-case drug interaction signals involving DOACs and a diverse group of precipitants that included select kinase inhibitors. A large number of our signals warrant further characterization and quantification to evaluate their clinical actionability.
