Relationship between absorbed dose and SUV derived from SPECT/CT imaging and dose-response in patients with neuroendocrine tumors treated with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE
Authors: Fei Liu, Hongxing Yang, Jianping Zhang, Jindian Li, Ying Qiao, Jie Chen, Shaoli Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) patients treated with peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), and to investigate the relationships among the absorbed dose of the tumor target lesions, standardized uptake value (SUV), and the therapeutic efficacy of the patients. A total of 22 NENs patients who received [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment and underwent consecutive single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography (SPECT/CT) imaging after drug administration were included in this study. After the SPECT/CT imaging, the volume of interest (VOI) of the target organs and the target lesions were delineated using the Hermes Internal Radiation Dosimetry (HIRD) software, and their absorbed doses were calculated. The SUV of the target lesions was measured on the reconstructed SPECT/CT fusion images. We conducted studies on the correlations between the absorbed dose, SUV, and the therapeutic efficacy of the patients respectively.ResultsAfter the patients received [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment in cycles 1 to 4, the absorbed doses of the tumor target lesions were 21.0, 16.0, 11.4, and 8.8 Gy respectively. The results of the correlations between the absorbed dose, SUV, and the therapeutic efficacy of the patients showed that both the absorbed dose and SUV had an “S”-shaped curve relationship with the tumor partial response (PR) rate. In addition, for the 1st to 4th PRRT treatments, the absorbed dose and SUV were linearly correlated with R2 of 0.7, 0.8, 0.8, and 0.9, respectively.ConclusionsThrough the calculation and analysis of the radionuclide therapy dosimetry based on the SPECT/CT images throughout the PRRT treatment of the patients, the absorbed dose and SUV of the patients can provide important dose guidance for radionuclide therapy, which is helpful to improve the treatment efficacy of the patients. Background

[177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) is a type of targeted radiotherapy that combines the radioactive isotope 177Lu with the peptide DOTA-TATE. Once administered, the DOTA-TATE specifically binds to the somatostatin receptors (SSTR) on the surface of neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) cells. The 177Lu then emits β radiation which damages the DNA of tumor cells, ultimately leading to their destruction [1, 2]. The NETTER-1 study demonstrated that [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE shows good efficacy in patients with advanced, progressive, well-differentiated midgut tumors, leading to its approval by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [3]. [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE has been confirmed to be significantly effective for patients with advanced metastatic or inoperable NENs, capable of controlling tumor growth, alleviating symptoms, and extending progression-free survival (PFS), with manageable side effects, making it increasingly recognized by clinicians [4–6]. In contrast to common sites of NENs in foreign populations, the most frequent primary sites of NENs in China are the pancreas (31.5%), rectum (29.6%), and stomach (27.0%), while small intestine (5.6%) and colon (3.0%) NENs are relatively rare [7]. Due to the non-specific clinical manifestations of NENs, over 60% of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [8, 9]. Although [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE has not yet been launched in China, clinical trials for its use in treating advanced NENs patients are being widely conducted.

The standard treatment protocol for [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE is based on the results of NETTER-1 clinical trial, which includes four intravenous injections of a fixed activity (7.4 GBq) with intervals of 8 weeks. Amino acid solutions (lysine/arginine) are used before and after the infusion of [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE to reduce radiation damage to the renal tubules, and antiemetic medications are used prophylactically to alleviate nausea and vomiting caused by the treatment. However, during the clinical practice of [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE, some adverse reactions still occur in NEN patients. Common adverse reactions include hematological toxicity, gastrointestinal reactions, renal toxicity, fatigue, and hair loss. Rare but serious adverse reactions include bone marrow suppression and secondary malignancies [10, 11]. Preliminary studies have shown that when a fixed regimen is used for [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment, there is heterogeneity in organ and tumor absorption doses among patients, leading to different susceptibilities to radiation-related diseases. This heterogeneity is directly related to the drug’s biodistribution and retention time in the organs of interest [12]. Therefore, there are still several details that can be optimized in clinical practice to enhance the efficacy of PRRT and reduce associated toxic side effects.

177Lu is widely used in targeted radionuclide therapy for various types of cancer due to its excellent radionuclide properties. It can emit both β particles and γ rays, where β particles are used for internal radiation therapy, and γ rays are utilized for single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography (SPECT/CT) imaging. Dosimetry calculations for radionuclide therapy based on patient imaging is a complex and emerging research field. Studies have shown that SPECT imaging can accurately calculate the absorbed doses in organs and lesions [13]. Currently, only a few studies have attempted to explore the relationship between tumor absorbed doses during [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment and patient responses to therapy. However, the effective absorbed doses corresponding to the targeted lesions remain unclear. A dosimetry study involving 23 patients with pancreatic NENs (P-NENs) and 25 patients with small intestine NENs (SI-NENs) indicated a dose-response relationship between absorbed dose and tumor shrinkage, which was more pronounced in P-NENs [14]. A retrospective study of 90 patients with gastro-entero-pancreatic NENs (GEP-NENs) found that the radiation dose to the tumors during PRRT could predict radiological responses but not survival rates [15]. Another retrospective study of 37 GEP-NENs patients claimed that the minimum tumor absorbed dose during the first cycle of [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment could predict the prognosis of GEP-NETs patients receiving PRRT, providing a basis for individualized dose-guided treatment strategies [16]. A substudy of the Phase III NETTER-1 trial evaluated the efficacy of [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE in advanced midgut neuroendocrine tumors and found no correlation between optimal tumor size reduction and absorbed dose [17].

The SUV is the ratio of the radioactive activity of the imaging agent taken up by the local tissue to the average injected activity throughout the body, which indicates the degree of the uptake of the radiopharmaceutical by the lesion. It is a semi-quantitative index and may be affected by various factors, including the image acquisition time, the image acquisition protocol, the image reconstruction method, the attenuation correction method, the delineation of the volume of interest (VOI), etc [18]. SUV can serve as a potential surrogate marker for non-invasive assessment of tumor dose, providing rapid reference for clinical treatment decision-making. Currently, the SUV is widely applied in PET/CT, while its application in SPECT/CT is relatively limited. This article for the first time studies the correlation analysis between the SUV in the SPECT/CT scans of patients after PRRT treatment and the treatment efficacy.

In this study, we will analyze the efficacy of NEN patients after receiving four cycles of PRRT, and investigate the relationship between the absorbed dose of target lesions and SUV values, as well as the correlation between absorbed dose and tumor control rate. By performing dosimetry calculations based on SPECT/CT images throughout the PRRT treatment course, we aim to provide important dose guidance for radionuclide therapy, which may help improve treatment outcomes for patients.

Materials and methods

Patients and [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment

A total of 22 patients who received [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment in the Department of Nuclear Medicine of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) from May 2023 to April 2024 were consecutively selected in this retrospective study. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) The patients’ age must be ≥ 18 years; (2) The enrolled patients were those with locally advanced or metastatic GEP-NENs of low or intermediate grade (G1 or G2) that were recently pathologically confirmed by tissue examination and were unresectable; (3) Before receiving [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment, all target lesions (based on RECIST 1.1) of the patients had to be confirmed as positive for SSTR by [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT, and the patients had to have disease progression within 1 year before enrolling in the clinical trial; (4) The patients’ Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score is 0 or 1; (5) The patients’ expected survival is > 12 weeks. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Serum creatinine > 1.7 mg/dL, hemoglobin < 80 g/L, white blood cell < 2.0 × 109/L, platelets < 75 × 109/L, serum albumin < 30 g/L; (2) Positive pregnancy test before medication or female patients during lactation; (3) Patients who had received PRRT treatment before study enrollment; (4) Patients who had undergone the following treatments within 12 weeks prior to the initiation of study treatment: surgery, radical radiotherapy, hepatic artery embolization, radiofrequency ablation of liver metastases. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of FUSCC (2210262-2), and all patients signed the written informed consent forms.

The [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE injection used in this study was provided by the Institute of Nuclear Physics and Chemistry, China Academy of Engineering Physics, with a specific activity of 37 GBq/mg. The radiochemical purity was verified to be > 95% by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) before each administration. The administration activity of the [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE injection was 7.4 GBq per cycle, and the administration interval was once every 8 ± 2 weeks, with a total of 4 administrations throughout the treatment. In case of dose modification toxicity (DMT) during the [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment, dose adjustment was required, including suspension of administration, dose reduction, delayed administration (the longest interval between two administrations being 16 weeks), and permanent discontinuation of the drug. In order to protect the kidneys and reduce the gastrointestinal adverse reactions during the treatment, each patient was given a single intravenous injection of palonosetron and an intravenous infusion of an amino acid solution (L-lysine hydrochloride and L-arginine hydrochloride) 30 min before the treatment. The amino acid infusion lasted for at least 4 h.

Image acquisition, reconstruction and target lesion delineation

Each patient underwent 4 consecutive SPECT/CT imaging sessions at 4 ± 0.5 h, 24 ± 1 h, 96 ± 2 h and 144 ± 4 h after each administration of [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE, including whole-body SPECT imaging and local tomographic CT fusion imaging. For patients with primary pancreatic tumors, the SPECT/CT scanning range was one bed position, covering the abdominal organs. For patients with primary rectal tumors, the scanning range was two bed positions, covering the abdominal and pelvic organs. The SPECT/CT scanning was performed using a Discovery NM/CT 670 (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA), which was equipped with a sodium iodide crystal detector and a low-energy high-resolution (LEHR) collimator, and its main energy window was 113 ± 11 keV. The SPECT acquisition parameters were as follows: matrix size 128 × 128, magnification factor 1, acquisition range 360 degrees, 60 projections, and the acquisition time for each projection was 30 s. After the SPECT acquisition, a low-dose CT scan (120 keV, 20 mAs) was performed for anatomical correlation. The CT scan was reconstructed with a 512 × 512 matrix and a slice thickness of 3.8 mm. To obtain quantitative images, the consecutive SPECT/CT images were reconstructed using the ordered subset expectation maximization standard (OSEM) method (8 iterations and 16 subsets), and photon attenuation and scatter correction were carried out.

The VOI of the target organs (spleen, kidney, liver and bone marrow) and the target lesions were delineated using the Hermes Internal Radiation Dosimetry (HIRD) software (Hermes Medical Solutions, Stockholm, Sweden). The VOI of the liver and the target lesions were semi-automatically delineated on the SPECT/CT fusion images, while the VOI of the remaining target organs were automatically delineated on the CT images. Two physicians with more than 5 years of experience in nuclear medicine and who had received training were responsible for delineating the VOI of all target organs and target lesions. The delineation of VOI refers to the EANM dosimetry committee’s guideline recommendations on dosimetry for 177Lu-labeled SSTR-targeting ligands and PSMA-targeting ligands [13]. The target lesions were determined according to RECIST 1.1 which required that the lesion diameter of ≥ 1.0 cm. Each patient was allowed to select a maximum of 5 target lesions, and all bone lesions were excluded. The target lesions were classified according to the following locations: primary pancreatic and rectal lesions, hepatic metastatic lesions, and lymph node metastatic lesions. The measurement of the target lesions was based on the low-dose CT part of the SPECT/CT scan, and a reduction in diameter of more than 30% was classified as tumor partial response (PR), a percentage decrease in the diameter of the target lesions less than 30% was defined as stable disease (SD) of the tumor lesions [19]. To completely eliminate the factor of potential correlations among lesions within the same patient, we selected only the lesion with the highest absorbed dose from each patient for logistic regression model and dose-response model.

Absorbed dose calculation

The absorbed dose could be calculated in three steps by HIRD: (1) HIRD extracted the time-activity curve (TAC) from multiple dynamic image sequences; (2) HIRD integrated the TAC from the time of injection to infinity to obtain the time-integrated activity curve (TIA); (3) HIRD converted the TIA into a dose distribution map by using the physical properties of the radionuclide and the substance density obtained from the registered CT images. Precise dose calculation was carried out using Monte Carlo simulation, taking into account the photon absorption, scattering and cross-irradiation in the patient’s body. HIRD provided image registration and image reconstruction tools, and the absorbed dose in this article was the average absorbed dose of the VOI calculated by HIRD. For each patient, the SPECT/CT images at multiple time points were registered with the image acquired at the first time point.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were described using the number of observed cases and the frequency of each type. Quantitative data were described using the median, minimum and maximum values, or the mean and standard deviation. The One-way ANOVA was used to compare the differences in the absorbed doses of the lesions between the cycles of PRRT. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Two methods (logistic regression model and dose-response model) were used to model the relationship between the tumor control probability and the absorbed dose. The logistic regression curve and the dose-response curve could show the relationship of the effects produced by a substance (such as a drug or radiation) [20, 21]. Linear fitting was used to model the relationship between the absorbed dose and the SUV. The Origin 2021 software was used for statistical analysis and significance testing.

Results

Patients

Among the 22 patients, there were 15 males and 7 females, with an average age of 52.5 ± 11.3 years (ranging from 30 to 69 years). All of them were pathologically diagnosed with NENs (2 cases were grade G1 and 20 cases were grade G2). The primary lesions of 6 patients were located in the rectum, and the primary lesions of the remaining 16 patients were in the pancreas. 8 patients had their spleens removed due to surgical reasons. The detailed information of the enrolled patients is shown in Table 1. Among them, 18 patients completed the full course of treatment for 4 cycles. A total of 4 patients failed to complete the full cycles of PRRT treatment due to adverse reactions of thrombocytopenia: 2 of them only finished the first 3 cycles and suspended drug administration in the last cycle; the other 2 only completed the first 2 cycles and suspended drug administration in the last 2 cycles. Due to the adverse reaction of neutropenia, the dosage of the last administration for 2 patients was reduced from 7.4 GBq to 3.7 GBq. The average dosages of [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE injected in each treatment cycle for the enrolled patients were 7.6, 7.6, 7.7 and 7.2 GBq respectively. The total number of target lesions was 44 for the 1st and 2nd treatment cycles, and due to the decrease in patients in the 3rd and 4th cycles, the target lesions decreased to 38 and 32 in the 3rd and 4th cycles, respectively .

Basic characteristics of enrolled patients

In this study, hematologic toxicity was the most common adverse reaction. The incidence rates of grade 1/2 nausea, anemia, lymphopenia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia were 27.3%, 54.5%, 31.8%, 36.4%, and 22.7%, respectively. The incidence rates of grade 3 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia were 9.1% and 18.2%, respectively, with no grade 4 adverse reactions observed (Supplemental Table 1). The eGFR of all patients before treatment was > 80 mL/min/1.73m2, and no significant decline in renal function indicators or renal function impairment-related adverse reactions were observed during treatment (Supplemental Table 2). No cases of myelodysplastic syndrome, acute myeloid leukemia, or other secondary malignancies occurred. The patients’ adverse reactions could be managed through symptomatic treatment and/or temporary drug discontinuation with dose adjustment.

Absorbed dose-treatment cycle relationship

The absorbed doses and the average absorbed doses of the tumor target lesions, kidneys, bone marrow, liver, and spleen of the patients after each treatment with [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE are shown in Fig. 1. After the patients received [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment in cycles 1 to 4, the absorbed doses of the tumor target lesions were 20.6, 16.0, 11.4, and 8.8 Gy respectively, showing a gradually decreasing trend, and the cumulative absorbed dose in 4 cycles was 56.8 Gy (Fig. 1A). The p-values between the absorbed dose of the [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE in cycle 1 of the lesion and the absorbed doses of the PRRT in cycles 2, 3, and 4 were > 0.05, < 0.05, and < 0.05 respectively. There were no significant correlations between cycle 1 and cycle 3/4. On the contrary, there was a certain degree of difference. The absorbed doses of the patients’ kidneys were 3.9, 4.0, 3.9, and 3.6 Gy respectively, remaining at the same level. The minimum absorbed dose was 2.4 Gy, and the maximum absorbed dose was 7.4 Gy, with a cumulative absorbed dose of 15.4 Gy in 4 cycles (Fig. 1B). The absorbed doses of the patients’ bone marrow were 0.4, 0.4, 0.3, and 0.3 Gy respectively, showing a gradually decreasing trend (Fig. 1C). The absorbed doses of the patients’ livers were 2.9, 2.7, 2.4, and 2.0 Gy respectively, showing a gradually decreasing trend (Fig. 1D). The absorbed doses of the patients’ spleens were 4.3, 5.3, 5.0, and 5.0 Gy respectively (Fig. 1E). The average absorbed doses of the target lesions, kidneys, bone marrow, liver, and spleen of the patients within 4 cycles were 15.7, 4.0, 0.4, 2.4, and 4.9 Gy respectively (Fig. 1F).

Relationship between absorbed dose and SUV

By comparing Fig. 3A and B, we found that the change patterns of the cumulative absorbed dose and the SUV are quite similar. In order to further study the correlation between the lesion absorbed dose and SUV, we carried out linear fitting on the lesion absorbed dose of each PRRT treatment for the patients and the lesion SUV (per unit body weight) obtained from the SPECT/CT images acquired 4 h after each PRRT injection. The results are shown in Fig. 4. During the 1st to 4th PRRT treatments, the absorbed dose and SUV exhibited a linear relationship, with R2 of 0.7, 0.8, 0.8, and 0.9, respectively. With the increase in the number of treatments, the fitting coefficients gradually increased, and the linear correlation between the two gradually strengthened.

Discussion

[177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE PRRT has great potential in the treatment of metastatic NET, which can achieve true integration of diagnosis and treatment, and has been included in the European Society for Medical Oncolog (ESMO) and European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society (ENETS) clinical guidelines [22]. Gordon et al. published a study on the treatment of 143 patients with advanced NET using [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE. The results showed that the median PFS of the patients was 32.3 months, and the median OS was 72.5 months [23]. However, most of the existing PRRT clinical treatment strategies refer to the regimen recommended by the NETTER-1 clinical trial, and the best treatment effect cannot be obtained. It has been reported in the literature that the cumulative administered dose can be adjusted in the following two ways: (1) Adjust the number of treatment cycles, that is, keep the injection activity of each cycle unchanged, and increase or decrease the number of PRRT treatments according to the patient’s physical condition; (2) Keep the number of treatment cycles unchanged and appropriately adjust the injection activity of each cycle [24, 25].

“Time 0 scan” refers to the scan conducted after the completion of tracer injection and before the patient uses the restroom. This scan captures the initial distribution information of the tracer in the body, providing crucial baseline data for subsequent pharmacokinetic analysis and dose calculation. It aids in more accurately assessing the clearance process of radiopharmaceuticals in the body and improves the precision of dose calculations [26]. However, in this study, the first scan for PRRT treatment was performed 4 h post-administration. Due to limitations in patient compliance and the primary focus on abdominal lesions and organ absorbed dose (without involving whole-body absorbed dose), this scanning protocol was not adopted in the current study.

In PRRT treatment, Dose-Limiting Organs (DLOs) refer to normal tissues or organs that have a high uptake of radionuclides or are sensitive to radiation damage, and their tolerance doses will directly limit the treatment dose. Protecting these organs is the key to ensuring the safety of treatment. According to the EANM dosimetry committee recommendations, the DLOs for PRRT include the kidneys and bone marrow. The total absorbed dose threshold for the kidneys is 23 Gy, while the bone marrow absorbed dose threshold for severe hematologic toxicity is typically considered to be 2 Gy, based on experience with radioiodine therapy [13, 27]. Our study data showed that the average total cumulative absorbed dose for the kidneys in patients receiving standard PRRT treatment was only 15.4 Gy, and the total cumulative absorbed dose for the bone marrow was 1.4 Gy, both within the safe range.

Hematologic toxicity was the most common adverse reaction following PRRT treatment in this study, consistent with previous research findings [28]. Even when the bone marrow absorbed dose was within the safe range, patients could still experience grade 3 or higher hematologic toxicity. In a study of 7 patients with an estimated bone marrow radiation dose of approximately 1.5 Gy, 4 patients developed persistent grade 4 thrombocytopenia and grade 3 leukopenia [29]. Bone marrow dosimetry cannot predict individual patient marrow toxicity, and individual variations further increase the uncertainty in toxicity prediction [28]. In our study, 4 patients were unable to complete all cycles of PRRT treatment due to grade 3 thrombocytopenia as an adverse reaction. 2 other patients required a reduced dose in the fourth treatment cycle due to grade 3 neutropenia. The results indicate that the tumor PR rate (28.1%) in patients who completed four PRRT treatments was significantly higher than in those who completed only two treatments (2.3%). Furthermore, in this study, the absorbed dose of the tumor gradually decreases with the number of PRRT treatments, while the absorbed dose of the kidneys basically remains at about 3.9 Gy (2.4–7.4 Gy) and does not change much with the number of PRRT treatments [30].

In this study, we also proposed two fitting models for the relationship between the cumulative absorbed dose of the lesions in the SPECT/CT images, the SUV of the lesions and the therapeutic efficacy: the logistic fitting model and the dose-response fitting model, and gave the formulas of the fitting models respectively. Logistic fitting is not good at dealing with complex problems, and the dose-response fitting model is mostly based on the study of external irradiation radiotherapy and the target area is fixed, and the studied dose range is also different from that of internal irradiation. When selecting a model, we need to conduct research in combination with clinical practice to explore a more suitable prediction model. In this article, both of the two fitting curves are in an “S” shape, which quantifies the relationship between the absorbed dose and the therapeutic efficacy. To achieve a 90% tumor control rate, two fitting models provide the following key thresholds: the logistic fitting model requires a cumulative absorbed dose of ≥ 149.7 Gy (consistent with peer results of 135.0 Gy [31]) and a SUV of ≥ 44.7 from SPECT/CT imaging (4 h after drug injection of the 4th PRRT), while the dose-response fitting model mandates a lesion absorbed dose of ≥ 96.9 Gy and a SUV of ≥ 29.6 from the same SPECT/CT imaging. It is necessary to clarify that these thresholds should be regarded as exploratory values because the fitting models have not been clinically validated.

Previous studies have shown that performing SPECT/CT imaging at either 4–24 h after treatment is feasible for patients [32–34]. In our study, to achieve higher accuracy in image registration, we selected SPECT/CT images acquired 4 h post-injection as the reference for SUV calculation, with images from the other three time points registered to the 4 h post-injection SPECT/CT images. In our study, both the SUV and the absorbed dose are related to the therapeutic efficacy of PRRT. The SUV at each time point can be converted to %ID and become a point on the TAC. TIA, which is directly related to the absorbed dose, can be obtained by integrating the TAC from 0 to infinity. This explains the linear relationship between SUV and absorbed dose. It should be noted that the SUV and the absorbed dose are different: the SUV is a semi-quantitative parameter at a certain moment, and the absorbed dose is a physical quantity accumulated over time, which is related to the effective half-life of the radionuclide, the radiation type of the radionuclide, etc.

Admittedly, our study can be improved in the following aspects: (1) In this article, the SUV and the absorbed dose we used were obtained based on the conversion of non-quantitative images reconstructed from SPECT/CT images by the HIRD software into quantitative images. During the conversion process, HIRD may bring certain errors (image registration error, image reconstruction error, et al.) to both the SUV and the absorbed dose. In addition, quantitative SPECT/CT may exhibit dosimetric deviations due to its limited spatial resolution, sensitivity, and interference from “motion artifacts” caused by physiological movements. (2) Hematological toxicity was not comprehensively considered in this study. In the future, we will include hematological parameter indicators such as neutrophils, lymphocytes, hemoglobin and platelets, and conduct an in-depth study on the relationship between them and the absorbed doses of organs such as the bone marrow and spleen. (3) The sample size of this article is not large enough. In the future, we will conduct an in-depth study on the relationship between the tumor absorbed dose and its therapeutic efficacy in PRRT patients with a multi-center and large sample size.

Conclusions

In this article, we found that the absorbed dose of the target lesions gradually decreased with the increase in the number of PRRT treatments, while the absorbed dose of the kidneys basically did not change with the number of PRRT treatments. In addition, the results of our study showed that there was a linear relationship between the SUV obtained from the SPECT/CT imaging 4 h after the injection of the PRRT drug and the absorbed dose. This indicates that for patients with NENs receiving [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE PRRT treatment, both the absorbed dose of the lesions and the SUV obtained from the [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE SPECT/CT images are significantly correlated with the tumor control rate. The higher the absorbed dose of the tumor, the higher the SUV of the SPECT/CT scan, and the better the therapeutic efficacy of PRRT. This study starts with dosimetric analysis to provide important dosimetric guidance for formulating treatment plans for NENs patients undergoing [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE PRRT, with the aim of improving therapeutic outcomes.
