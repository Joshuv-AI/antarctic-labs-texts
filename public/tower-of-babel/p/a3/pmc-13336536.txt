Defective Ala Nasi – Johanson Blizzard Syndrome
Authors: C Chandrakala, S Sangeetha Devi, R Lakshmi, B Karpagam, P Raju
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Johanson Blizzard syndrome (JBS) is an autosomal recessive ectodermal dysplasia clinically characterized by defective ala nasi, pancreatic enzyme insufficiency, hypothyroidism, sensorineural deafness, growth retardation, failure to thrive, intellectual disability, imperforate anus, genitourinary abnormalities, low-set ears, aplasia cutis, tooth abnormalities, cardiac defects, micrognathia, and other facial dysmorphism. There is no effective therapy for this condition. Early diagnosis and correction of defects will improve survival. We observed Mongolian spots and a sinus-like opening in the midline of the lumbosacral region as rare manifestations of this syndrome.

A 10-days-old male preterm baby referred from the paediatric ICU for an opinion on skin lesions in the limbs and trunk with a defect in the ala nasi. The child was born out of second-degree consanguinity and was delivered by caesarean section at 37 weeks of gestation for premature rupture of membranes. The mother’s antenatal history was uneventful. On examination, the child was small for gestational age due to intrauterine growth retardation, and there was a defect in the ala nasi on both sides, exposing the nasal cavity and nasal septum [Figure 1a]. The child had hypertrichosis, upsweeping of frontal hair [Figure 1b], low-set ears, microcephaly, micrognathia, epicanthic folds, upslanting palpebral fissures [Figure 1a], well-demarcated hyperpigmentation over the scrotum, Mongolian spots in the lumbo saccral region, and aberrant Mongolian spots around both ankles, the dorsum of both hands, and over the abdomen, hypotonia, and a midline sinus-like opening in the lumbosacral region [Figure 2]. The child had vomiting on and off. There was no history of similar features in the family members and relatives.

On investigations, a complete hemogram, renal function tests, and serum electrolytes were normal. Liver function tests showed elevated total and indirect bilirubin levels. The child had neonatal jaundice and was treated with phototherapy. There was pancreatic enzyme insufficiency with low levels of serum amylase [serum amylase 4 U/L] and lipase [serum Lipase 11 U/L]. Thyroid function tests were normal at 2 weeks of birth. Ultrasound of the abdomen and cranium findings were normal. Regional ultrasound of the pit in lumbosacral region did not show any defects, and MRI was not conducted due to unavailability. Echocardiogram showed an atrial septal defect with a left-to-right shunt. Otoacoustic emission test showed bilateral sensorineural deafness. Brainstem evoked response audiometry was not conducted as it is not available. Genetic study was conducted, which showed homozygous defect in chromosome 15 in URB1 gene [variant: c.488G>A (p.Cys163Tyr)].

The child was diagnosed as a case of JBS on constellation of clinical and investigation findings such as defective ala nasi, facial dysmorphism, upsweeping of frontal hair, hypotonia, sensorineural deafness, atrial septal defect and pancreatic enzyme insufficiency.

JBS is an ectodermal dysplasia that occurs due to mutations in ribosome biogenesis homolog gene located on chromosome 15q15. The URB1 gene encodes E3 ubiquitin ligase, an enzyme involved in the proteolytic pathway.[1] The incidence of JBS is 1:1,25,000 live births. Hypoplasia of the ala nasi with a beak-like nose is the well-marked external feature that helps in suspecting JBS in newborn.[2] Other craniofacial abnormalities described in this condition are microcephaly, micrognathia, maxillary hypoplasia, high-arched palate, upslanting palpebral fissures,[3] long philtrum, absent or poorly developed permanent teeth, and other dental anomalies. Sensorineural deafness occurs in JBS due to defects in cochlea, along with defective development of other ectodermal structures such as the ala nasi and mandible.[4]

Exocrine pancreatic enzyme deficiency, the hallmark feature of JBS, occurs due to progressive destruction of pancreatic acini during intrauterine development.[5] Pancreatic enzyme deficiency with subsequent fat malabsorption, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, and indigestion are the characteristic features of this syndrome. In this condition, as the pancreatic damage extend further, insulin deficiency and diabetes occur in some cases. Other endocrine disorders described in this condition are hypothyroidism and, rarely, hypopituitarism.

Gastrointestinal manifestations occurring in JBS patients are intestinal malabsorption due to exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, severe cholestatic liver disease, liver fibrosis, portal hypertension[3], and imperforate anus. Cardiac anomalies associated with JBS include dextrocardia, anomalous pulmonary venous connection, transposition of great vessels, pulmonary atresia, persistent ductus arteriosus, atrial septal defect, and ventricular septal defect.

Skin lesions commonly reported in this syndrome are aplasia cutis and scalp defects. Few cases have been reported with café au lait macules.[36] We observed Mongolian spots in the lumbosacral region with aberrant Mongolian spots over the abdomen and in a bilateral symmetrical distribution over the ankles, feet, and dorsum of both hands. We also report a sinus-like small opening in the midline lumbosacral region in our case, and neural tube defects were to be ruled out by computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging later.

Management of JBS is challenging as it involves a multidisciplinary approach and lifelong care. JBS is treated symptomatically, as there is no effective treatment. Early intervention with pancreatic enzyme replacement, protein, deficient vitamin and mineral supplementation, hormone replacement for hypothyroidism, hearing aids or cochlear implant for deafness[6], depending upon the severity, correction of cardiac anomalies, and other defects can improve survival. The overall prognosis is poor in JBS due to multisystem involvement. Genetic counselling must be given to the parents as there is a risk of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies. Antenatal screening should be emphasized to diagnose this condition earlier.[7]
