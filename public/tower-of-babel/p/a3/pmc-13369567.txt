Compensation Structures, Job Satisfaction, and Retention of Facial Plastic Surgeons in Academic Practices
Authors: Julia R Brennan, Andrew Chen, Jaymie A Bromfield, Rahul Sharma, Steven Mobley, Douglas M Sidle, James C Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo understand how various compensation models are structured nationwide and to evaluate how these models impact the stability of the academic facial plastic surgeon workforce. We hypothesize that competitive compensation structure plays a critical role in retention of academic departments and aim to gain insights into financial factors at play in sustaining this workforce.MethodsThis cross‐sectional survey study examined responses from a 25‐item electronic questionnaire sent to American Academy of Facial Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery members from July 2023 to January 2024.ResultsOf 59 respondents, 45 (76%) reported employment by academic medical centers, representing 19% of academic facial plastic surgeons in the US. Compensation included production‐based relative value units (RVU) (n = 17, 38%), collections‐based (10, 22%), salary (9, 20%), or a combination of models (9, 20%). In strict RVU models, compensation ranged from $41–91 per RVU (median $60, IQR: $58–66); 84% reported no cap on RVUs, and the median target before incentives was 7500 (IQR: 6690–9000). 56% were satisfied with their income, and 62% were unlikely to leave academia within 5 years, with satisfaction associated with lower intent to leave (p = 0.038).ConclusionFindings show that competitive compensation structures in academic facial plastic surgery are associated with greater job satisfaction as well as lower intent to leave academics. This study provides greater transparency surrounding compensation structure for academic facial plastic surgeons while providing insight into financial factors playing a role in their retention within academia.Level of EvidenceLevel 5.Keywords: academic practice, compensation, job satisfaction, productivity, RVU

Introduction

Across academic medical centers, there is a vast range of compensation models utilized to structure physicians' incomes. Publicly available salary data reflected in the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) or Medical Group Management Association (MGMA) directories do not reflect the nuances in these models including factors such as productivity or collections‐based incentives that may impact overall income [1, 2, 3]. Income models can vary dramatically but share a common goal of attempting to align physician and institutional interests with creating a structure that rewards productivity in clinical care in addition to any academic or research pursuits [4]. The paucity of information surrounding market data creates challenges both for physicians seeking fair compensation and employers seeking to create benchmarks and incentivize staff retention [5].

Facial plastic and reconstructive surgery (FPRS) faces its own unique challenges relative to many other academic specialties in establishing compensation models that reflect the wide variety of care provided. The specialty encompasses surgeries ranging from cosmetic/aging face procedures to facial trauma to post‐oncologic reconstruction. Similarly, there is a diverse payer mix involved. Graduates of FPRS fellowships have job opportunities in a variety of practice environments, with the majority entering into non‐academic positions [6, 7]. The recruitment and retention of academic FPRS physicians is a known challenge for otolaryngology departments, due in part to concerns regarding the ability to offer competitive compensation structures [8]. Among academic FPRS physicians in particular, there is a scarcity of published income data available, and even less information regarding job satisfaction and job retention metrics [9, 10]. This study seeks not only to understand the structure of compensation models nationwide but also to assess their impact on the stability of the academic FPRS workforce.

Understanding determinants of income structure among academic FPRS physicians provides valuable insights for all stakeholders. Current and future job seekers benefit from greater transparency, enabling them to more accurately evaluate their own market worth and consider options for contract structuring. Meanwhile, employers benefit from a better understanding of the budgeting, professional development, and negotiation strategies required to recruit and retain physician employees in academic settings. By analyzing the differences in these compensation models, we can continue to evaluate their effectiveness in serving the needs of academic FPRS physicians and otolaryngology departments nationwide.

Materials and Methods

This was a questionnaire‐based study that received approval from the Northwestern Medical Center Institutional Review Board. An online survey was created using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap, Vanderbilt University; Nashville, TN) and distributed to all members of the American Academy of FPRS (AAFPRS) via email listserv from July 2023 to January 2024 targeting individuals currently holding academic positions. Participants indicated their informed consent when completing the voluntary survey and confidentiality was maintained. Information collected from the survey included practice type, academic ranking, years in practice, compensation details, location of practice, practice details, current and starting salary, and job satisfaction. The full 25‐question survey is included in Data S1.

Descriptive statistics and univariate analysis were performed to evaluate compensation structures and workplace satisfaction. Only participants with numeric responses for dollars per RVU and RVUs before incentive were included for analysis of these variables. Fisher's exact test was used for differences in categorical variables. Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test was used for univariate analysis comparing continuous variables across different categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of a highly likely or likely desire to stay in academia. Only factors that were significantly different across the primary outcome on univariate analysis with an alpha < 0.10 were included in the final multivariable model. Logistic regression modeling was conducted using a Firth's modified score procedure to effectively mitigate analytical biases rooted in small sample sizes associated with this study. Statistical significance was defined as alpha < 0.05. All analysis was conducted in R v4.3.1 (RStudio, Posit Software, PBC; Boston, MA).

Results

Provider Demographics

Of distributed surveys, 59 surgeons responded. 45 surgeons reported an academic practice type reflecting a response rate of 19% (45/235) among academic FPRS physicians, all of whom completed the entirety of the survey. The total number of FPRS physicians working at academic centers (235) was determined based on a manual query of faculty directories at the 159 US medical schools represented in the AAMC medical school member directory for FPRS fellowship‐trained surgeons on active faculty in academic plastic surgery and otolaryngology departments [11]. A total number of 235 FPRS fellowship‐trained academic facial plastic surgeons currently practicing in the US were identified. Out of these, 58 (25%) practice in the Northeast, 79 (34%) practice in the South, 42 (18%) practice in the West, and 56 (24%) practice in the Midwest. The table of academic FPRS surgeons at each medical school and their geographic regions is included in Data S2.

Table 1 illustrates responder characteristics, including region of practice, practice type, forms of compensation, salary data, and RVU metrics, stratified by years in practice. The largest proportion of respondents reported having 20+ years of experience (N = 15, 25%), followed closely by 2–5 years of experience (N = 14, 24%). The majority of respondents practiced in the Midwest (N = 19, 32%) or the South (N = 18, 31%) and worked within an academic practice (N = 45, 76%). Among survey respondents, the Midwest region is proportionally overrepresented and the Northeast region is proportionally underrepresented compared to the study population. However, no region is drastically undersampled. The lower proportional representation of surgeons in the West in survey respondents can be partially attributed to the lower proportion of surgeons in that region in the overall population.

Predictors of Departure From Academia

Table 3 illustrates salary satisfaction, region of practice, and time in practice stratified by respondents' reported likelihood to leave academia. This was only for the 45 academic surgeons included in the study. Most respondents were either very unlikely (N = 13, 29%) or unlikely (N = 15, 33%) to leave academia. Only 1 respondent reported being very likely to leave academia and was notably very dissatisfied with their current salary. Only salary satisfaction (p = 0.013) was significantly associated with a respondent's likelihood to leave academia. Respondents who were very unlikely to leave academia had the highest rate of being very satisfied with their salary (62%).

Discussion

In this national survey of FPRS physicians, our findings provide more insight into the existing compensation structures in academic practices. Notably, these findings supplement the existing literature, including data from AAMC and MGMA, which do not explore the nuances of various compensation models or assess salary satisfaction among respondents. Our findings provide new insight into satisfaction with current compensation structures and how this may impact the ability of academic departments to recruit and retain FPRS physicians.

Factors influencing the practice settings of graduating fellows and practicing physicians are often multifaceted. The 2022 American Academy of Otolaryngology Workforce study reported a 5.6% increase in the number of academic otolaryngologists in the preceding year, with an anticipated growth of 7.2% expected growth in the following year [12]. According to a 20 year review by Lava et al., the majority of fellowship graduates continue to pursue private or community practice. The proportion entering academic practice over the last two decades was reported as 36% (12/33) in 2000, 38% (17/45) in 2010, and 33% (15/46) in 2019 [7]. Interestingly, there has been relative stability in the proportion of FPRS graduates entering academic practice, despite the ongoing growth of academic otolaryngology departments nationwide.

In an anonymous 2017 survey of members of the AAFPRS, academic FPRS physicians cited case complexity, mentorship of resident physicians and medical students, and collegiality and collaboration as their reasons for choosing an academic position [10]. FPRS surgeons in academic departments face unique demands compared to those in other otolaryngology subspecialties. These challenges include the facility costs for cosmetic surgical services, the need for robust alternative patient payment services (i.e., cash‐fee models), and the expense of specialized staff and cosmetic equipment [6]. A section for general comments was included in the current survey study, and the responses reaffirmed these themes. Several academic FPRS physicians reported exorbitant facility fees that exceeded market price, high overhead costs, inadequate cash‐pay systems for cosmetic procedures, and general administrative issues. Collectively, these factors hinder the growth of cosmetic practices and contribute to potential turnover among academic FPRS physicians.

In the previously mentioned 2017 survey of FPRS physicians, the most commonly cited reasons for leaving or considering departure from academic positions were a perceived lack of control over practice and inadequate pay and benefits. In the same study, a supplemental survey of Otolaryngology department chairpersons identified key strategies for retaining FPRS faculty, including offering greater control over their practice and improved compensation [10]. Taken together with the existing literature, our results further support the notion that compensation overall is a critical factor in the retention of FPRS faculty in academic settings.

Tables 3 and 4 present characteristics that may influence the retention of academic FPRS physicians. In our analysis, dissatisfaction with income was associated with an increased likelihood of leaving academia. Respondents who reported being dissatisfied or very dissatisfied with their income were significantly less likely to stay in academia after adjusting for region of practice and incentive compensation structure. Notably, no significant differences in respondents' likelihood of leaving academia were found based on geographic region, years in practice, or compensation structure. These findings contribute to the existing literature by exploring the motivations of the existing academic FPRS workforce and identifying which factors most influence their career decision making. We believe this data provides insight for current and future academic FPRS to better understand their market value, as well as for academic department leaders to better evaluate the considerations and institutional supports that may affect physician recruitment and long‐term retention in academia.

This study had several limitations. Physician compensation data were self‐reported through an online survey distributed to AAFPRS members. Among academic FPRS physicians identified through manual review of medical school faculty rosters, approximately 19% (45/235) responded. This total number also does not account for physicians that work within hybrid models. To address nonresponse bias, reminder emails were sent periodically throughout the study period. However, self‐selection bias and nonresponse bias may still impact the generalizability of these results. Additionally, academic FPRS physicians without active AAFPRS memberships were not able to be contacted through email listserv, which may bias the study sample of survey respondents, which was not able to be drawn from the entire population of facial plastic surgeons in the US. The growth in academic FPRS departments since the survey was conducted also may contribute to the underestimation of the total number of currently practicing academic facial plastic surgeons. Another factor to consider is possible geographic undersampling of competitive facial plastics markets in the Northeast and West regions, where academic facial plastic physicians may hold different views on compensation structure, incentives, and retention. However, this undersampling is to be expected with the sample size of the study and no region is drastically undersampled proportional to each other.

Compensation and satisfaction were assessed in a cross‐sectional manner, which limits the ability to evaluate changes in income or job satisfaction over time. Our results focused primarily on clinical compensation and did not account for other forms of financial support, such as research grants or academic and education salary stipends. Residual confounding may still exist due to unmeasured variables that correlate with physician income, such as protected academic time.

Additionally, we recognize that compensation models at each institution can vary substantially with differing impact of salaried income versus production and collection based contributions, with or without a bonus structure. Differences in the proportion of cosmetic and reconstructive procedures performed across institutions and whether practices are cash‐pay, insurance‐based, or hybrid can also introduce variability in compensation structures for institutions and even individual surgeons at the same institution. As such, our categorical assessment may not adequately reflect the nuanced differences in compensation practices between individual institutions.

Finally, our study targeted academic FPRS physicians. Those working in private practice or hybrid models may have different perspectives, which could offer valuable context for future studies. Demographic analyses within income structures including factors such as race and gender could make a valuable contribution to the literature. However, given the small size of the target population, additional stratification poses a risk of inadvertently compromising respondent anonymity.

An investigation of the impact of additional costs to support academic FPRS practices on department financials and compensation structures in a future study may better elucidate the balance between fair academic FPRS compensation and the unique costs of maintaining a successful FPRS practice.

Conclusion

This study provides an overview of compensation models used by academic FPRS programs across the United States and examines how they may relate to physician recruitment and retention. In doing so, it offers greater transparency around a traditionally taboo topic and initiates a broader conversation on how financial structures influence the ability to build and sustain academic FPRS divisions.
