Aging analysis of latent fingerprint residues by tracking carotenoid and lipid degradation by Raman spectroscopy
Authors: Joao Paulo Santos de Carvalho, Alexandre Silva Santos, Marco Antônio de Souza, Sebastião William da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fingermarks have been crucial in forensic investigations for centuries, aiding in both individual identification and crime scene reconstruction. However, a reliable method for dating fingerprints found at crime scenes remains elusive. This study explores the use of Raman spectroscopy to analyze the chemical composition of latent fingermarks residues and track changes over time, specifically within 90 days of deposition. Fingermarks from three male donors were collected and aged under controlled conditions. Raman spectra revealed several chemical markers, with carotenoids showing a significantly faster degradation rate than lipids, following first-order kinetics and varying among donors. Both carotenoids and lipids underwent molecular changes, with carotenoids shifting from trans to cis-isomers, and lipids from cis to trans. These transformations led to oxidation processes and the generation of by-products. Lipid degradation showed a gradual decrease in unsaturated bonds during the first 40 days, followed by a more rapid decline. Raman data also indicated continuous lipid hydrolysis over time. The study provides insights into the chemical aging of fingermarks and suggests that Raman spectroscopy could become a useful non-destructive method for estimating the age of fingermarks in forensic applications.

Introduction

Forensic science comprises a set of techniques and methods that play a crucial role in solving and combating crimes. It encompasses various disciplines, including physics, chemistry, biology, mathematics, among others1. Among the different types of evidence found at crime scenes, fingermarks are one of the most commonly used forms of technical evidence in criminal investigations2,3.

Latent fingermarks found at crime scenes are often associated with sweat produced by eccrine and/or sebaceous glands3. Eccrine sweat, found on the soles of the feet and palms of the hands, is highly aqueous and contains organic and inorganic compounds, whereas sebaceous sweat, concentrated on the face and scalp, primarily consists of lipids4. Additionally, fingermarks may contain contaminants such as drugs, cosmetics, lotions, food residues, soap, or grease. Identifying the chemical composition of these fingermarks using chemical or physical methods can help profile the donor, potentially leading to the identification of the perpetrator5.

However, one of the greatest challenges in criminal investigation is determining the age of latent fingermarks6,7. Techniques such as ninhydrin powder, iodine fumigation, and silver nitrate are commonly used to develop latent fingermarks8. With technological advancements, research has increasingly focused on methods that analyze the chemical composition and degradation of substances in fingermarks9. Although there are no universally accepted methods for determining fingermark age, estimating the time elapsed since its deposition remains crucial in criminal investigations. The ability to determine whether a fingermark was deposited at the time of a crime or during a legitimate prior visit is a factor that can impact legal outcomes10.

Recent studies have explored various methods for estimating fingermark age by examining the physical and chemical changes that occur over time. These changes influence subsequent enhancement techniques, such as powdering or chemical treatments. Among the non-invasive and practical techniques currently employed is spectroscopy, including Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Mass Spectrometry (MS), and Raman Spectroscopy7,11,12.

Raman Spectroscopy is a powerful analytical technique that enables the identification and quantification of a wide range of materials, both organic and inorganic. These materials can be in solid, liquid, or gas states13. In forensic science, Raman Spectroscopy plays a crucial role in identifying exogenous substances, verifying documents, and analyzing artworks and analyzing artworks and archaeological artifacts14–16.

From the moment a fingerprint is deposited on a surface, its chemical composition begins to change due to environmental exposure and biological activity, potentially affecting the accuracy of age estimation. These modifications occur through both chemical and biological degradation of the compounds present in the fingerprint17,18. Chemical degradation involves processes such as dehydration of aqueous components, isomerization, oxidation, and molecular cleavage, affecting substances like urea, amino acids, squalene, cholesterol, fatty acids, and DNA. Biological degradation, on the other hand, results from bacterial activity on the skin, accelerating the decomposition of fingerprint residues19.

The complexity of determining fingermark age is compounded by the diversity of their chemical composition and exposure to environmental factors such as light, humidity, and temperature7,18,20. Despite these challenges, efforts to develop accurate methods for dating latent fingermarks remain a key focus in forensic research21.

As fingermarks age, their chemical components degrade, altering ridge morphology. Barros et al. analyzed changes in ridge morphology, as revealed by magnetic powder, to evaluate the aging of fingermarks from 20 donors (10 men and 10 women) over 30-days period22. The study demonstrated that magnetic powder was effective in detecting both recent and aged prints. As the fingermarks aged, the ridge patterns narrowed, and the percentage of visible ridges decreased, consistent with previous studies17,23.

Bhargava et al. emphasized the advantages of infrared spectroscopy techniques, highlighting their non-invasive nature and ability to identify the intrinsic chemical compositions of fingermark residues24. Fritz et al. employed FTIR to analyze fingermarks stored under environmental conditions for nine months11. After three months, they observed a general decline in signal intensity and material loss. While volatile components evaporated quickly, more stable components persisted for extended periods, aiding fingermark identification even after decades22.

Archer et al. investigated the variation in lipid composition over time using sebaceous material from fingermarks stored in both light and dark environments25. Their analysis, conducted using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), revealed that fatty acids levels increased during the initial aging phase, while squalene, present in fresh samples, degraded or transformed into other compounds within days, especially when exposed to light. Similarly, Andersson et al. employed Raman Spectroscopy to analyze fingermarks over four weeks, stored in both light and dark environments7. Their analysis identified and tracked the behavior of various compounds such as carotenoids, squalene, unsaturated fatty acids, and protein amino acid segments. While carotenoids and squalene degraded over time, proteins remained relatively stable and less affected by light exposure7.

Although the methods for analyzing fingermarks are non-destructive, contact-free, and practical for forensic applications, no reliable technique currently exists for accurately dating fingermarks found at crime scenes. This represents a significant gap in forensic science, highlighting the need for further research and methodological advancements24. The present study aims to address this challenge by analyzing the degradation kinetics of chemical residues in latent fingermarks after deposition on a suitable substrate, utilizing Raman Spectroscopy.

This study complies with the Phase 1, proof-of-concept investigation, as defined by the “Guidelines for the Assessment of Fingermark Detection Techniques”[25] Phase 1 focuses on the preliminary validation of novel methodologies - in this case, Raman spectroscopy for tracking chemical aging in latent fingermarks. In this phase, the primary goal is to demonstrate the feasibility of a new technique or reagent. Such pilot studies typically involve a limited number of donors (3 to 5) and simple substrates under controlled laboratory conditions. The key objective is to determine whether the technique merits advancement to subsequent phases; thus, statistical validation is not required at this stage.

From these fits, the integrated intensities and vibrational energies of the bands corresponding to each chemical component in the fingerprint were extracted.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that Raman spectroscopy is an effective non-destructive tool for monitoring molecular degradation in latent fingermarks over time. The high molecular specificity of Raman spectroscopy provided valuable insights into the chemical evolution of fingermark components during the aging process, thereby underscoring its potential applications in forensic science.

Notably, carotenoid and lipid degradation exhibited distinct temporal patterns, as identified through spectral analysis. The degradation kinetics of carotenoids followed a first-order reaction model, although significant inter-donor variability was observed. Specifically, donor 01 showed rapid carotenoid degradation, with Raman signals becoming undetectable within 10 days post-deposition. In contrast, donor 03 maintained detectable signals for up to 80 days. Additionally, gradual shifts in vibrational modes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\nu\:(C-C)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\nu\:(C=C)$$\end{document} coupled with the appearance of new spectral features, provided qualitative evidence of carotenoid oxidation. Spectral data revealed that donor 01 exhibited an immediate onset of trans-to-cis isomerization in\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C=C$$\end{document} bonds following fingermark deposition. In contrast, this transition occurred significantly later in other donors.

The observed variability likely reflects differences in initial carotenoid composition, potentially influenced by donor-specific factors including diet, carotenoid intake frequency, and nutritional status, as well as in the physicochemical environments to which they are exposed43–45. The electron-rich polyene chain serves as the primary site of attack and molecular transformation. The type, number, and location of oxygen functional groups, such as hydroxyl, carbonyl, and epoxy groups, as well as unique structure like allenic bonds, significantly modulate the molecule’s radical scavenging capacity and its susceptibility to oxidative degradation45. For instance, a study on orange juice demonstrated that at 60 °C, zeaxanthin degrades 2.7 times faster than β-carotene, which in turn degrades 30% faster than lutein38. Furthermore, a carotenoid’s hydrophilicity dictates its aggregation behavior within the complex fingerprint matrix, which in turn affects its accessibility to oxidants and overall reactivity45.

Although isomerization plays a significant role in carotenoid degradation, oxidation is among the most critical reactions contributing to their degradation46. Upon oxidation, carotenoids undergo molecular degradation, which may occur through polyene chain cleavage, epoxidation of the double bond or reactions in allylic positions47. These processes result in cleavage products such as epoxycarotene, apocarotene and hydroxycarotene48. The sidebands observed around the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\nu\:(C-C)$$\end{document} (~ 1157 cm-1) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\nu\:(C=C)$$\end{document} (~ 1550 cm-1) vibrational modes (See Fig. 6) provide evidence of the presence of these byproducts.

Although Raman spectroscopy has shown promise as a non-destructive tool for monitoring carotenoid degradation in fingerprints, the pronounced inter-donor variability in degradation rates and initial compositions highlights the necessity of comprehensive population studies to establish standardized degradation profiles for fingermarks – a critical requirement toward ensuring the forensic validity of Raman spectroscopic analysis. Establishing such standards would enhance the technique’s potential not only for aging estimations but also for donor characterization based on biochemical signatures.

Raman spectroscopy’s capability extends beyond monitoring carotenoid oxidation in fingerprints to concurrently evaluating lipid oxidation states. This dual-analysis approach provides complete temporal degradation profiles. The dominant lipid degradation mechanism involves autoxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, where molecular oxygen reacts with double bonds via a free radical chain reaction under ambient conditions. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully demonstrated the effectiveness of Raman spectroscopy in tracking the aging of latent fingerprints by monitoring the degradation of carotenoids and lipids, highlighting its potential for forensic applications. The high molecular specificity of Raman spectroscopy allowed us to obtain information about the nature of the chemical changes that occur during molecular degradation. Carotenoids were found to degrade faster than lipids, with significant inter-donor variability. It was found that the degradation kinetics of carotenoids are first-order, with different reaction rates depending on the initial carotenoid composition influenced by factors such as diet and nutritional status. Spectral data further revealed that both carotenoids and lipids undergo oxidative degradation, involving trans-to-cis isomerization at C = C bonds in carotenoids and cis-to-trans isomerization in the hydrophobic chain of the lipids. It was also found that in lipids, the number of unsaturated bonds decreases slightly in the first 40 days and then drops abruptly. Finally, Raman data indicated that lipids are continuously hydrolyzed throughout the aging time. Raman spectroscopy has proven to be a promising technique for fingerprint dating.

While the results of this study are scientifically enlightening, they highlight the need for further studies (e.g., larger donor groups, environmental variability) to address the inherent complexity of fingermark composition and its dependence on extrinsic factors such as diet, health and lifestyle. This foundational research represents a critical step toward future validation (Phase 3) and operational testing (Phase 4), but its current scope precludes direct forensic application without further refinement. Further research is needed to validate the robustness and limitations of the technique to establish standardized degradation profiles for forensic application.

Methods

The fingermarks were obtained by pressing the index finger onto a glass slide coated with aluminum foil. For analysis, three male donors aged between 25 and 54 years were selected. The fingermarks were deposited after each donor washed their hands with liquid (neutral) soap, rubbed their index finger on their forehead, and then pressed it onto the aluminum-coated glass substrate. At the time of collection, the donors faces were free of chemical residues such as sunscreen, moisturizing cream, etc. To monitor the effects of aging on the spectral characteristics, three fingermarks were collected from each donor, totaling nine samples. Furthermore, to avoid potential inhomogeneities in the chemical components present in the fingermarks, 10 Raman spectra (from different points) were acquired from each of the nine fingerprints, resulting in a total of 90 spectra for each day of measurement over 90 days. Finally, an arithmetic mean of the 30 spectra from each donor was obtained and analyzed. As this is a Phase 1 study25, and considering (1) the inherent variability in the initial carotenoid composition, (2) the complexity of the residual matrix in which carotenoids an25 lipids are embedded, and (3) the need to minimize interference from external environmental factors, all experiments were conducted under rigorously controlled laboratory conditions. The samples were stored for 90 days in a dark environment (i.e., without light exposure), at 22 °C, 60–70% relative humidity, and an atmospheric pressure of 0.88 atm. The Raman spectra were obtained using a LabRam HR Evolution spectrometer from Horiba, equipped with a confocal microscope, a CCD (Charge Coupled Device) detector, and a diffraction grating with 1800 lines/mm, installed at the Optical Spectroscopy Laboratory of the Institute of Physics at the University of Brasília – UnB. The measurements were performed using a laser tuned to the 532 nm line (green line) with a power of 5 mW, focused on the sample through a 50X magnification Olympus objective. The spectra were collected in the range of 600 to 1800 cm− 1. For spectral analysis, the LabSpec®5 software was used for baseline correction and normalization of the spectra. Subsequently, the arithmetic means of the ten spectra obtained from each of the three fingerprints of each donor was calculated. This was done using the OriginPro®9.0 software, resulting in three average spectra for each of the three donors. For data analysis, the nine average spectra were fitted using Gaussian + Lorentzian area functions, employing the Peakfit v4 software.
