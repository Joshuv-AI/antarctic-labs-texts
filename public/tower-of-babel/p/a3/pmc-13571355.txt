Exercise-Provoked Wide-Complex Tachycardia and Extreme Duke Score

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 44-year-old man presented with progressive exertional dyspnea, palpitations, and lightheadedness during mild physical activities. He wished to exercise but was concerned about his symptoms. His resting electrocardiogram demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with no ST abnormalities and no preexcitation (Figure 1A). An exercise treadmill test was ordered.

Electrocardiographic Findings Before, During, and After Exercise-Provoked Wide-Complex Tachycardia

(A) Baseline 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus rhythm at 62 beats/min with narrow QRS (86 ms) and no preexcitation or ST abnormalities. (B) Rhythm strip during stress-test recovery showing sustained monomorphic wide-complex tachycardia with left bundle branch block morphology. (C) Spontaneous termination of tachycardia with restoration of sinus rhythm. No antiarrhythmic medications or cardioversion were required. BP = blood pressure; HR = heart rate.

The patient exercised for 9 minutes 23 seconds on the Bruce protocol, achieving 10.77 metabolic equivalents with a peak sinus heart rate of 142 beats/min. No chest pain occurred. The blood pressure response was hypertensive (maximum 219/100 mm Hg). During peak exercise, short runs of wide-complex tachycardia (WCT) were observed. In recovery, the rhythm progressed to sustained monomorphic WCT with left bundle branch block (LBBB) morphology, interpreted as sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) (Figure 1B). The patient experienced lightheadedness but maintained hemodynamic stability. ST-segment depression was present in the lateral leads with ST elevation in aVR. The Duke Treadmill Score (DTS) calculated to −25.3 (highest-risk category).

Vagal maneuvers were attempted without effect. The tachycardia terminated spontaneously without antiarrhythmic medication or cardioversion (Figure 1C). On emergency department arrival, vital signs were stable and physical examination was unremarkable. The patient was admitted for expedited cardiac evaluation and closer monitoring.

Past Medical History

His past medical history includes hypertension on lisinopril and gastroesophageal reflux disease. No prior cardiac history, family history of sudden cardiac death or cardiomyopathy, or substance use.

Differential Diagnosis

The primary consideration was monomorphic VT, which accounts for most WCTs. In a 44-year-old man, VT could arise from ischemic substrate, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, or idiopathic outflow tract origin. The differential also included supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) with functional LBBB aberrancy (orthodromic atrioventricular reciprocating tachycardia with a left-sided accessory pathway [AP] being a classic cause), antidromic reciprocating tachycardia, atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia with aberrancy, and bundle branch re-entrant VT. Surface electrocardiogram criteria alone cannot reliably differentiate these entities.1

Investigations

Laboratory data

High-sensitivity troponin was 8 ng/L initially and rose to 30 ng/L (upper reference limit <15 ng/L). Electrolytes, renal function, and complete blood count were normal.

Coronary angiography

Left heart catheterization demonstrated mild nonobstructive coronary artery disease without flow-limiting stenosis (Figure 2).

Echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance

Echocardiography revealed normal heart structure and function (ejection fraction 60%-64%). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) showed normal function (left ventricular ejection fraction 60%, right ventricular ejection fraction 54%) with no late gadolinium enhancement, normal T1/T2 mapping, and no evidence of a structural substrate for VT.

In the setting of sustained tachycardia without obstructive coronary disease, normal wall motion, and no myocardial scar on CMR, the troponin elevation was attributed to myocardial injury from supply-demand mismatch.2

Electrophysiology study

Electrophysiology study (EPS) was performed on hospital day 4, after coronary angiography, echocardiography, and CMR had excluded an ischemic and structural substrate, as a structural finding would have altered the electrophysiologic approach and any defibrillator decision; the availability of CMR determined the interval. Baseline intervals were normal (atrial-His 60 ms, His-ventricular 48 ms). AV nodal function demonstrated 1:1 anterograde conduction to a cycle length of 270 ms without dual AV nodal physiology. The shortest ventricular pacing cycle length with 1:1 retrograde conduction was 390 ms. Retrograde atrial activation was eccentric and nondecremental, diagnostic of an AP serving as the retrograde limb (Figure 3A). The ventriculoatrial effective refractory period was 340 ms at a drive cycle length of 550 ms. Adenosine during ventricular pacing did not prolong the ventriculoatrial interval or produce block, confirming retrograde conduction via an AP rather than the AV node.

Intracardiac Electrograms During Electrophysiology Study

With isoproterenol, burst ventricular pacing reproducibly initiated monomorphic tachycardia with LBBB morphology at a cycle length of 260 ms, closely mimicking the clinical WCT. A His-bundle electrogram preceded each QRS (His-ventricular approximately 60 ms), confirming supraventricular activation and excluding VT (Figure 3B). Earliest atrial activation was in the proximal coronary sinus (high right atrium 222 ms, His 178 ms, proximal coronary sinus 130 ms, distal coronary sinus 146 ms), localizing the AP to the left posterior mitral annulus (Figure 3B). Programmed ventricular stimulation with up to triple extrastimuli did not induce VT.

Electrophysiology conclusion: Orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia (ORT) via a concealed left posterior AP with functional LBBB aberrancy.

Management

Metoprolol succinate was initiated. Catheter ablation was recommended but deferred to a separate outpatient procedure under general anesthesia given the need for transseptal access and suspected obstructive sleep apnea. The patient was discharged on hospital day 5 in sinus rhythm on metoprolol succinate with outpatient follow-up arranged.

Four weeks later, repeat EPS confirmed the concealed left posterior AP. Transseptal access was obtained under intracardiac echocardiography and fluoroscopic guidance. Using electroanatomic mapping (CARTO, Biosense Webster), the earliest retrograde atrial activation with a fractionated electrogram was identified at the posterior mitral annulus (6:30 clockface position). Radiofrequency ablation at 35 W resulted in loss of AP conduction with transition to a concentric, decremental retrograde pattern consistent with exclusive AV nodal conduction. Additional lesions were delivered to address transient pathway recovery and retrograde conduction remained exclusively nodal. Postablation adenosine during ventricular pacing produced complete ventriculoatrial block, absent before ablation, confirming elimination of the AP.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged the same day in sinus rhythm. At follow-up, he experienced no recurrent symptoms and metoprolol was discontinued. A symptom-limited treadmill exercise test repeated after ablation with comparable workload (11.7 metabolic equivalents, 98% of predicted maximum heart rate), induced no arrhythmia and was negative for ischemia.

Discussion

Exercise-induced WCT is managed as VT until proven otherwise. Here the sustained WCT with LBBB morphology, ischemic-appearing ST changes and extreme DTS suggested high-risk ischemic disease. A systematic evaluation established the correct diagnosis and enabled curative ablation (Table 1).

WCT during exercise

Differentiation of VT from SVT with aberrancy remains imprecise despite established electrocardiogram algorithms.1 Several features in retrospect favored a supraventricular mechanism: the patient’s age, the absence of known structural heart disease, tolerance of a sustained rapid tachycardia, spontaneous termination, and an organized LBBB-pattern QRS rather than the broader morphology common in scar-related VT. None of these is individually decisive, and atrioventricular dissociation, fusion, and capture beats, the most specific surface markers of VT were not evident on his tracings. Initial management was also identical regardless of mechanism: investigate the tachycardia as VT and exclude an ischemic and structural substrate. William et al3 reported 22 patients with WCT presumed to be VT referred for consideration of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator; EPS revealed SVT with aberrancy in all, and device insertion was obviated in 82%. In our patient, the concealed AP made the diagnosis difficult from the surface electrocardiogram alone, and invasive testing was required for correct diagnosis and subsequent curative ablation.

ORT with LBBB aberrancy

In ORT, the circuit proceeds antegradely through the AV node and retrogradely via an AP. With a left-sided AP, functional LBBB forces transseptal ventricular activation, prolonging ventriculoatrial conduction, the phenomenon described by Coumel and Attuel,6 characteristic of ORT with an ipsilateral AP4 (Figure 4). Gu et al5 described 3 of 220 ORT cases requiring LBBB aberrancy for circuit maintenance, all involving lateral Aps. In our patient, the LBBB morphology created a wide QRS mimicking VT, and the rate-related aberrancy resolved immediately on tachycardia termination.

Orthodromic Reciprocating Tachycardia Circuit With LBBB Aberrancy

Schematic of the orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia circuit in radiographic orientation. The antegrade limb (blue) proceeds through the atrioventricular (AV) node, His bundle, and right bundle branch (RBB) to the right ventricle (RV); functional left bundle branch block (LBBB) (dashed red, blocked) forces slow transseptal conduction to the left ventricle (LV), producing wide QRS with LBBB morphology. The retrograde limb (red) conducts from the LV through the concealed left posterior accessory pathway to the left atrium (LA), then reenters the AV node. Because the accessory pathway and the blocked bundle branch are both left-sided, functional LBBB lengthens the re-entrant circuit: the wavefront must cross the interventricular septum before reaching the left-sided pathway, adding conduction time and prolonging the ventriculoatrial (VA) interval and tachycardia cycle length, the phenomenon described by Coumel and Attuel.6 Loss of the ipsilateral bundle branch block, as occurred at termination in this case, shortens the circuit and narrows the QRS. Numbered circles (1-6) indicate the sequence of activation around the circuit. AVN = atrioventricular node; LBB = left bundle branch; RA = right atrium.

Limitations of the DTS during sustained tachyarrhythmia

The DTS combines exercise duration, maximal ST deviation, and an angina index. A score below −11 identifies a high-risk group with substantially reduced 4-year survival.7 Our patient’s score of −25.3 fell in this category. The score was derived and validated for ST deviation during sinus-rhythm exercise and has not been validated when the ST term is generated by a sustained tachyarrhythmia. Here the lateral ST depression recorded during sinus rhythm after a sustained episode of WCT. ST-segment depression is common during SVT and predicts coronary disease poorly: in 1 series of 100 patients it was present in half and carried a positive predictive value of only 6%8 and coronary sinus lactate sampling during SVT has shown no net lactate production to indicate ischemia.9 The normal postablation exercise test supports this interpretation. The decision to proceed to invasive coronary angiography also warrants comment. The sustained WCT, troponin elevation, and lateral ST depression with aVR elevation, a pattern that can accompany left main or multivessel subendocardial ischemia, made exclusion of obstructive disease imperative. In a 44-year-old whose only coronary risk factor was hypertension and who reported no chest pain, the necessity of an invasive study is nonetheless open to debate, and coronary computed tomographic angiography would have been a defensible noninvasive alternative.

Study limitations

The findings derive from a single case.

Conclusions

Exercise-provoked sustained WCT with LBBB morphology and ischemic-appearing stress-test findings was diagnosed as ORT via a concealed left posterior AP. Ablation eliminated AP conduction, and a postablation exercise test confirmed the absence of inducible arrhythmia and ischemia. Once structural and ischemic disease has been excluded, EPS can establish the mechanism and enable curative ablation.

Take-Home Messages

•Exercise-provoked WCT with apparent ischemic ST changes warrants systematic evaluation including electrophysiology study when structural heart disease has been excluded, as SVT with aberrancy can produce a misleadingly extreme Duke Treadmill Score that mimics high-risk ischemic disease.
