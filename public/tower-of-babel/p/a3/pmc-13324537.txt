TRPV4 Activation Promotes Hair Growth via TSLP Signaling in the Skin
Authors: Wook-Joo Lee, Won-Sik Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHair follicle cycling is regulated by complex signaling networks that coordinate stem cell activation and quiescence. Although transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) is expressed in the skin and implicated in cutaneous physiology, its role in hair growth remains unclear. Here, we investigated the contribution of TRPV4 activation to hair growth and its relationship with thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP). Pharmacological activation of TRPV4 using GSK1016790A significantly promoted hair regrowth and elongation in mice, whereas TRPV4 inhibition attenuated these effects. Consistently, Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice exhibited spontaneous hair loss, supporting a role for TRPV4 in maintaining hair homeostasis. TRPV4 activation increased TSLP production in the skin, and exogenous TSLP recapitulated the pro-growth effects of TRPV4 activation. These effects were suppressed by pharmacological blockade of the TSLP receptor, indicating TSLP-dependent signaling. Notably, TSLP restored hair growth in Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice, whereas TRPV4 activation did not, supporting the notion that TSLP acts downstream of TRPV4. In contrast, activation of TRPV3, another TRP channel abundantly expressed in the skin, did not significantly affect hair growth, highlighting the specific role of TRPV4 in this process. Overall, these findings identify a TRPV4–TSLP signaling axis that promotes hair growth and regulates hair follicle function, suggesting a potential therapeutic target for hair loss disorders.Keywords: TRPV4, TSLP, Hair growth, Hair follicle cycling, Skin physiology

INTRODUCTION

Hair plays a crucial role in protecting the scalp from environmental insults, regulating body temperature, and contributing to individual identity. It also serves a sensory function by enhancing the cutaneous perception of tactile stimuli, and plays an important role in sexual and social communication. Collectively, these functions underscore the substantial psychological burden and quality-of-life impact associated with hair disorders, including hirsutism and alopecia (Buffoli et al., 2014). Hair loss can arise not only from aging and genetic predisposition but also from acquired factors, including environmental pollution, psychological stress, and hormonal imbalances linked to dietary changes. Its incidence is further increasing due to nutritional deficiencies associated with excessive dieting, as well as heightened exposure to chemical and physical stressors that compromise hair integrity.

The hair follicle is a dynamic mini-organ of the skin, comprising the outer root sheath, inner root sheath, and hair shaft. It undergoes cyclic transformations through the growth (anagen), regression (catagen), and quiescence (telogen) phases, which are tightly regulated by a complex network of signaling molecules (Muller-Rover et al., 2001; Oshima et al., 2001). Proper coordination of these molecular signals is essential for maintaining hair follicle homeostasis and ensuring the timely activation of hair follicle stem cells. Key signaling pathways regulate hair follicle cycling, particularly the balance between stem cell quiescence and activation. In particular, signaling pathways that balance stem cell quiescence and activation play central roles in hair follicle cycling. Among these, bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), FGF7, and Noggin act in a coordinated manner to regulate phase transitions: BMPs derived from bulge and dermal cells maintain stem cell quiescence during telogen, whereas FGF7 and Noggin promote anagen initiation, with Noggin functioning as a feedback antagonist of BMP signaling (Gazzerro et al., 1998; Hsu et al., 2011; Ito et al., 1999; Plikus et al., 2008). Disruption of these regulatory networks can impair normal hair cycling and contribute to hair loss, highlighting their importance as potential therapeutic targets.

Transient receptor potential (TRP) channels are non-selective, polymodal ion channels that respond to a wide range of stimuli, including temperature, mechanical force, osmotic pressure, and noxious chemical agents (Zhang et al., 2025). Many TRP channels, particularly TRP vanilloid 3 (TRPV3) and TRP vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), are widely expressed in the skin and play critical roles in both skin physiology and pathophysiology (Caterina and Pang, 2016; Luo and Hu, 2014; Toth et al., 2014). TRPV3 has been shown to regulate hair morphogenesis in mice (Cheng et al., 2010), and gain-of-function mutations in human TRPV3 cause Olmsted syndrome, a condition characterized by severe chronic pruritus and skin abnormalities (Lin et al., 2012). In addition, TRPV3 has also been implicated in the modulation of hair follicle cycling (Borbiro et al., 2011; Imura et al., 2007). TRPV4, which is expressed in sensory neurons and keratinocytes, is activated by thermal, mechanical, and chemical stimuli, as well as by inflammatory metabolites (Lee and Shim, 2021; Szabo et al., 2019). Emerging evidence indicates that TRPV4 activation in hair follicle cells promotes the transition from telogen to anagen by enhancing pro-growth signaling while suppressing inhibitory pathways. Consistent with this, pharmacological activation of TRPV4 using the selective agonist GSK1016790A recapitulates these effects in the skin, at least in part, through activation of downstream ERK signaling pathways (Yang et al., 2020). Together, these findings suggest that TRP channels, particularly TRPV3 and TRPV4, may serve as important modulators linking environmental stimuli to hair follicle biology.

Thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) is an epithelial cell–derived cytokine that plays a central role in type 2 immune responses and skin inflammation. Predominantly produced by keratinocytes, TSLP has been identified as a key mediator in the pathogenesis of atopic dermatitis, where it functions as a histamine-independent pruritogenic factor (Ziegler, 2012; Ziegler and Artis, 2010). TSLP signals through its receptor (TSLPR) and has been shown to directly induce scratching behavior in mice, underscoring its critical role in itch and neuroimmune interactions in the skin (Wilson et al., 2013). Beyond its established role in inflammation and pruritus, accumulating evidence suggests that TSLP may also regulate broader aspects of skin physiology.

Concurrently, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of immune mediators in hair follicle biology. Hair follicles are dynamic mini-organs that undergo cyclical phases of growth (anagen), regression (catagen), and rest (telogen), and are highly sensitive to changes in the local immune microenvironment. Disruption of immune privilege and alterations in cytokine signaling can significantly affect hair cycling and follicular function (Arck et al., 2008; Paus and Cotsarelis, 1999). Indeed, cytokine-driven inflammation has been implicated in hair growth dysregulation and in hair loss disorders such as alopecia areata (Gilhar et al., 2012; Ito et al., 2008). In this context, emerging evidence suggests that epithelial-derived cytokines, including TSLP, may contribute to the regulation of hair follicle activity and hair growth (Demehri et al., 2009).

Recent studies further point to a potential mechanistic link between inflammatory signaling and sensory ion channels in the skin. TRPV4, a calcium-permeable ion channel expressed in keratinocytes, has been implicated in skin barrier function and sensory transduction. Notably, our recent study demonstrated that TRPV4 activation modulates TSLP secretion (Lee and Shim, 2021), suggesting a functional interaction between TRPV4 signaling and TSLP-mediated pathways. Collectively, these findings raise the possibility that TRPV4-dependent regulation of TSLP represents a novel mechanism linking cutaneous inflammation to hair follicle function. However, the role of this pathway in hair growth regulation remains largely unexplored.

Despite these advances, the relationship between TRPV4 activation and TSLP signaling in the context of hair growth remains poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear whether TRPV4-induced TSLP production contributes to the regulation of hair growth. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to comprehensively investigate the interplay between TRPV4 activation and TSLP signaling in the skin and to determine how these interactions influence hair growth in mice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Reagents

Mouse TSLP was purchased from ProSpec (catalog #CYT-1135, East Brunswick, NJ, USA). GSK1016790A (catalog #G0798), HC067047 (catalog #616521), and baicalein (catalog #465119) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Animals

All the protocols for the animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Gachon University (GU1-2023-IA0026) and were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by Gachon University. Nine-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Koatech (Pyeongtaek, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). For the generation of Trpv4 homozygous knockout (KO) mice (Trpv4-/-), breeding pairs of heterozygous B6.129X1-Trpv4<tm1Msz> mice were purchased from RIKEN (Wako, Saitama, Japan). The homozygous Trpv4 KO offspring were verified by genotyping using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) protocol provided by RIKEN. All Trpv4-/- mice possessed a homogeneous deletion of TRPV4. All animals showed no noticeable behavioral changes during the study period.

Animal treatment

In 9-week-old C57BL/6 mice, the dorsal hair was shaved. Wild-type (WT) and Trpv4−/− mice received intradermal injections of either GSK1016790A (GSK101, a selective TRPV4 agonist, 30 μM/site, 50 μL, diluted from a 200 mM stock in saline) or mouse TSLP (mTSLP, 100 ng/site, 50 μL, diluted from a 100 μg/mL stock in saline). HC067037 (HC067, a selective TRPV4 antagonist, 20 μM/site, 50 μL, diluted from a 200 mM stock in saline) or baicalein (BCL, a selective TSLPR antagonist, 100 ng/site, 50 μL, diluted from a 100 mg/mL stock in saline) was administered intradermally on a daily basis, 1 h prior to GSK1016790A or mTSLP injection. Intradermal injections were performed using insulin syringes fitted with 31G needles (BD). Fifteen days after injection, mice were sacrificed, and skin samples from the injection sites were collected for further analyses.

Measurement of hair growth area and hair length

On days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15, mice were photographed using a digital camera (iPhone 15 Pro). Hair growth area was quantified by image analysis using ImageJ software (NIH) and expressed in cm². Three hair strands were randomly plucked from each mouse (n = 6), and their lengths were measured to calculate the mean.

Measurement of TSLP

The skin tissue from the intradermal injection site was harvested, homogenized in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently lysed in 1× RIPA buffer for ELISA sample preparation. The levels of TSLP were then measured using a mouse TSLP ELISA kit (catalog #MTLP00; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Statistical analysis

All the data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and the statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software. Comparisons between the two groups were made using unpaired Student’s t-test, while comparisons among more than three groups were made by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test or Tukey’s multiple comparison test. The statistical significance was considered at p<0.05.

RESULTS

Spontaneous hair loss observed in TRPV4-deficient mice

To examine whether TRPV4 contributes to hair growth (Fig. 1A), we assessed the occurrence of spontaneous hair loss in wild-type (WT) and Trpv4-/- mice. No apparent hair loss was observed in the dorsal skin of WT mice (Fig. 1B). In contrast, spontaneous hair loss was evident in a subset of Trpv4-/- mice (Fig. 1C). Quantitatively, all WT mice (10/10) exhibited normal hair growth, whereas 4 out of 8 Trpv4-/- mice developed spontaneous hair loss (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that TRPV4 plays a role in maintaining normal hair homeostasis and prompted further investigation into the underlying mechanisms by which TRPV4 regulates hair follicle function.

(B, C) Representative dorsal skin images of (B) WT and (C) Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice showing normal hair and hair loss phenotypes, respectively. (D) Quantification of hair loss incidence in WT and Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice.

TRPV4 activation promotes hair regrowth and elongation in mice

To investigate the effect of TRPV4 activation on hair growth (Fig. 2A), dorsal hair was depilated, and mice were treated daily with GSK101 (TRPV4 agonist) with or without HC067 (TRPV4 antagonist), according to the experimental schedule shown in Fig. 2B. Hair regrowth was monitored on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15. Representative images show that daily administration of GSK101 markedly accelerated hair regrowth compared with the control group, whereas co-treatment with HC-067 (“GSK101+HC067”) attenuated this effect, resulting in hair growth comparable to controls (Fig. 2C).

TRPV4 activation promotes hair growth in vivo. (A) Schematic illustration of TRPV4 activation by GSK101 and inhibition by HC067 to investigate the effect of TRPV4 modulation on hair growth. The question mark denotes the investigated role of TRPV4 activation in hair growth regulation. (B) Experimental design showing dorsal hair depilation followed by daily treatment with GSK101 (TRPV4 agonist) with or without HC067 (TRPV4 antagonist). (C) Representative images of dorsal skin at indicated time points (Day 0-15). (D) Quantification of hair growth area over time. (E) Hair growth area at Day 15. (F) Quantification of hair length over time. (G) Hair length at Day 15. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. ***p<0.001.

To quantify these observations, hair growth area was measured over time. It was found that hair regrowth became evident from day 9, and by day 15, the GSK101-treated group exhibited a significantly greater hair-covered area compared with both the control and the GSK101+HC067 groups (Fig. 2D, 2E).

In addition, hair length was also measured at the same time intervals. Hair elongation began to increase from day 6, and by day 15, hair length was significantly greater in the GSK101-treated group, whereas this effect was abolished by HC067 co-treatment (Fig. 2F, 2G).

Together, these results demonstrate that TRPV4 activation enhances both hair regrowth and elongation, supporting a critical role for TRPV4 in promoting hair growth.

TSLP promotes hair growth downstream of TRPV4 activation

We have previously reported that TRPV4 activation promotes TSLP production in the skin (Fig. 3A) (Lee and Shim, 2021). To reverify this, we examined whether pharmacological activation of TRPV4 using GSK101 increases TSLP levels. Consistent with our previous findings, GSK101 treatment significantly elevated TSLP levels in the mouse skin, whereas this increase was attenuated by co-treatment with HC067, a TRPV4 antagonist (Fig. 3B). These results support a functional link between TRPV4 activation and TSLP production in the skin (Fig. 3C).

(H) Quantification of hair length over time. (I) Hair length at Day 15. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. *p<0.05, **p<0.01.

To determine whether increased TSLP levels directly affect hair growth, dorsal hair was depilated, and mice were treated daily with mouse TSLP in the presence or absence of baicalein (BCL), a selective TSLP receptor (TSLPR) antagonist, according to the experimental design (Fig. 3D). Hair regrowth was monitored on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15. As a result, it was observed that TSLP-treated mice exhibited markedly accelerated hair regrowth compared with controls, whereas co-treatment with BCL attenuated this effect (Fig. 3E).

Quantitative analysis revealed that hair growth area began to increase from day 12, and by day 15, the TSLP-treated group showed a significantly greater hair-covered area compared with both control and TSLP+BCL groups (Fig. 3F, 3G). Similarly, hair length progressively increased over time, with a significant increase observed at day 15 in TSLP-treated mice, which was markedly reduced by BCL co-treatment (Fig. 3H, 3I).

Therefore, these findings demonstrate that TSLP promotes hair regrowth and elongation in a TSLPR-dependent manner, supporting its role as a downstream effector of TRPV4-mediated hair growth.

TSLP restores hair growth in Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice

To determine whether TSLP can compensate for TRPV4 deficiency in hair growth, we applied the same experimental protocol to Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 4A). Dorsal hair was depilated, and mice were treated daily with GSK101 or mouse TSLP, followed by monitoring of hair regrowth on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15. As a result, while GSK101 had minimal effect in Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice, we found that administration of mouse TSLP markedly promoted hair regrowth, with visible recovery evident by day 12 and further progression by day 15 (Fig. 4B).

TSLP restores hair growth more effectively than TRPV4 activation in Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice. (A) Experimental schematic evaluating whether GSK101 or TSLP treatment affects hair growth in Trpv4⁻/⁻ mice. The question mark indicates the experimental question addressed regarding TRPV4-dependent and downstream signaling mechanisms. (B) Representative images of dorsal skin at indicated time points (Day 0-15) following treatment with control (Con), GSK101, or mTSLP. (C) Quantification of hair growth area over time. (D) Comparison of hair growth area at Day 15. (E) Quantification of hair length over time. (F) Comparison of hair length at Day 15. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ns: not significant.

Quantitative analysis of hair growth area demonstrated a modest increase in the GSK101-treated group compared with controls, although this effect was not statistically significant, whereas the TSLP-treated group exhibited a robust and significant increase in hair-covered area by day 15 (Fig. 4C, 4D). Similarly, hair length measurements showed progressive elongation over time, with a significant increase observed in the TSLP-treated group at day 15, while GSK101 treatment did not produce a significant effect (Fig. 4E, 4F).

Overall, these findings demonstrate that TSLP restores hair growth in Trpv4⁻/⁻mice, indicating that TSLP acts downstream of TRPV4 to stimulate hair growth.

TRPV3 activation does not affect hair growth

To determine whether TRPV3 contributes to hair growth, we evaluated the effects of pharmacological TRPV3 activation using carvacrol in wild-type mice (Fig. 5A). TRPV3 is widely expressed in the skin and has been previously implicated in hair follicle biology, as noted in the Introduction. Dorsal hair was depilated, and mice were treated daily with carvacrol, followed by monitoring of hair regrowth at the same time intervals. As shown in Fig. 5B, no noticeable differences in hair regrowth were observed between the carvacrol-treated and control groups throughout the experimental period.

TRPV3 activation does not significantly affect hair growth in vivo. (A) Schematic illustration of TRPV3 activation by carvacrol in wild-type (WT) mice and its potential effect on hair growth. The question mark denotes the investigated role of TRPV3 activation in hair growth regulation. (B) Representative images of dorsal skin at indicated time points (Day 0-15) following carvacrol treatment or control (Con). (C) Quantification of hair growth area over time. (D) Comparison of hair growth area at Day 15 between control and carvacrol-treated groups. (E) Quantification of hair length over time. (F) Comparison of hair length at Day 15 between groups. ns: not significant.

Quantitative analysis of hair growth area revealed minimal increases over time in both groups, with no significant differences observed at any time point, including day 15 (Fig. 5C, 5D). Similarly, measurements of hair length showed comparable increases in both control and carvacrol-treated mice, with no significant differences at the endpoint (Fig. 5E, 5F).

Collectively, these findings indicate that TRPV3 activation does not significantly influence hair growth, suggesting that TRPV3, unlike TRPV4, is not a major regulator of hair follicle activity under these conditions.

DISCUSSION

As noted, TRPV4 expression in epidermal keratinocytes and skin tissues has been well documented by previous studies using immunostaining and qPCR analyses (Chung et al., 2004; Lee et al., 2005; Moore et al., 2013). In addition, several reports suggest that TRP channels, including TRPV4, may also be present in hair follicle compartments such as the outer root sheath, although follicle-specific enrichment remains to be fully characterized (Atoyan et al., 2009; Nilius and Voets, 2013). In this context, the present study demonstrates that pharmacological activation of TRPV4 markedly promotes hair growth in mice, raising the possibility that TRPV4 contributes to hair growth by modulating hair follicle stem cell activity. Consistent with this notion, a previous report has shown that activation of TRPV4-expressing keratinocytes promotes hair follicle stem cell proliferation and follicle regeneration, underscoring a critical role for TRPV4 in hair cycle regulation. Mechanistically, TRPV4 activation may suppress the expression of Fgf18 and Bmp6, both well-characterized inhibitors of anagen entry, while simultaneously upregulating hair growth–promoting factors such as Fgf7, Wnt16, and Nog (Song et al., 2021), thereby shifting the molecular balance toward pro-anagen signaling. Although keratinocytes likely serve as primary mediators of this response, other TRPV4-expressing cell types within the skin microenvironment, including dermal fibroblasts, adipocytes, and immune cells, may also contribute to sustaining anagen entry by modulating these signaling pathways (Yang et al., 2020). However, the primary focus of the present study was to investigate the relationship between TRPV4 and TSLP. This study was initiated by the unexpected hair growth phenotype observed in TRPV4 knockout mice, which led us to further explore both TRPV4-dependent and TRPV4-independent mechanisms underlying hair growth regulation. Taken together, these findings suggest that TRPV4 activation orchestrates hair follicle stem cell dynamics, promotes anagen entry, and stimulates follicle regeneration, while also highlighting the need to further elucidate the downstream signaling pathways linking TRPV4 to TSLP-mediated hair growth. We next investigated the role of TSLP in hair growth by administering TSLP subcutaneously in mice. Our data demonstrate that TSLP promotes hair growth in both wild-type mice (Fig. 3) and Trpv4-/- mice (Fig. 4) under non-wounded conditions (Fig. 3, 4). This finding is notable, as previous studies have shown that TSLP promotes wound-induced hair growth through direct action on LGR5+ hair follicle stem cells and/or their progeny (Shannon et al., 2022), which are key contributors to hair follicle regeneration and neogenesis (Jaks et al., 2008). Taken together with our results, these observations suggest that TSLP plays a significant role in promoting hair growth across diverse physiological contexts.

It is noteworthy that TRPV4 and TSLP are closely linked together in the skin. Indeed, our recent study demonstrated that activation of TRPV4 under dry skin conditions leads to increased TSLP secretion within the epidermis (Lee and Shim, 2021), suggesting a mechanism through which TSLP levels can be amplified both within the dermis and in hair follicle-associated compartments. We further showed that elevated TSLP acts on TSLPR-expressing mast cells to promote tryptase release, which in turn stimulates keratinocytes via TRPV4 to produce additional TSLP, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop (Lee and Shim, 2021). Although keratinocytes represent a major source of TSLP, additional immune and non-immune cell populations in the dermis, including mast cells, dendritic cells, and fibroblasts, also contribute to local TSLP production (Al-Shami et al., 2005; He et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2013; Pandey et al., 2000; Reche et al., 2001). Collectively, these findings support the notion that TRPV4-driven TSLP signaling constitutes a coordinated signaling network regulating skin homeostasis and hair follicle function.

Importantly, administration of TSLP in Trpv4-/- mice significantly restored hair growth (Fig. 4), indicating that TSLP alone is sufficient to activate hair growth–promoting pathways independently of TRPV4. These findings highlight TSLP as a potential therapeutic factor capable of stimulating hair growth even in the absence of canonical TRPV4-mediated signaling. However, TSLP is also a well-established pruritogenic cytokine, particularly in the context of atopic dermatitis (Wilson et al., 2013). Thus, although exogenous TSLP can promote hair growth, its propensity to induce pruritus may limit its practical therapeutic application. Further studies are therefore required to elucidate the mechanisms by which TSLP promotes hair growth, particularly at the level of the hair follicle, with the goal of identifying alternative targets or agents that can selectively enhance hair growth without eliciting pruritus.

A previous study has reported that activation of TRPV3 can stimulate TSLP secretion (Um et al., 2020). To determine whether TRPV3 activation also contributes to hair growth regulation, we administered the TRPV3 agonist carvacrol in mice. However, no significant changes in hair growth were observed (Fig. 5), suggesting that TRPV3 activation is not sufficient to promote hair growth, at least under our experimental conditions.

TRPV3, which is predominantly expressed in keratinocytes, has been widely implicated in normal hair development. Notably, both loss and overactivation of TRPV3 disrupt hair physiology, indicating that its activity must be tightly regulated (Guo et al., 2023). TRPV3 knockout mice exhibit abnormal hair morphology, including bent and curled hairs, as well as keratinized cyst formation in hair follicles, highlighting its role in maintaining normal hair structure (Moqrich et al., 2005; Song et al., 2021). Conversely, excessive TRPV3 activation, as observed in DS-Nh mice and WBN/Kob-Ht rats, results in a hairless phenotype despite an increased number of hair follicles (Asakawa et al., 2006). These findings collectively indicate that balanced TRPV3 activity is essential for hair follicle homeostasis but does not necessarily drive hair growth. In this context, it is notable that the level of TSLP induced by TRPV3 activation appears to be substantially lower than that elicited by TRPV4 activation. Taken together, these observations suggest that although TRPV3 can modulate TSLP production, its contribution to hair growth regulation is limited (Lee and Shim, 2021). In contrast, TRPV4 appears to play a more prominent role in activating TSLP-mediated signaling pathways associated with hair follicle growth. Accordingly, TRPV4 may represent a more effective therapeutic target than TRPV3 for promoting hair growth.

Despite the findings presented in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Previous in vitro studies have suggested that activation of TRPV4 may induce hair loss in human hair follicle models. Specifically, TRPV4 activation in human systems has been reported to produce effects distinct from those observed in mice, including reduced hair shaft elongation and morphological/structural changes characteristic of catagen follicles (Szabo et al., 2019). Therefore, it remains uncertain whether TRPV4 activation in humans would similarly promote hair growth through increased production of hair growth–related mediators. In addition, human TSLP exists in two isoforms, short-form TSLP (sfTSLP) and long-form TSLP (lfTSLP) (Al-Shami et al., 2005), whereas mouse models do not capture this distinction. Given that sfTSLP is constitutive expressed in human skin (Shannon et al., 2022), it is plausible that sfTSLP contributes to hair follicle regulation in humans. Consequently, the present findings are insufficient to directly predict whether TRPV4-mediated signaling can effectively regulate hair growth in human systems through induction of sfTSLP expression in human. Further studies using human hair follicle models or clinical samples will be necessary to clarify the relevance of this pathway in human hair biology.

In conclusion, this study provides several key findings. We demonstrate, for the first time, that genetic deficiency of TRPV4 in mice results in a pronounced hair loss phenotype, indicating a critical role for TRPV4 in maintaining hair follicle homeostasis in vivo and supporting its utility as an experimental model for alopecia. We further show that administration of mouse TSLP significantly promotes hair growth, providing direct evidence that TSLP can function as a potent inducer of hair follicle activation. Collectively, these results establish TSLP as an important regulator of hair follicle dynamics, capable of activating quiescent follicles and promoting hair growth independently of TRPV4, potentially through both keratinocyte-dependent and -independent mechanisms. Taken together, our findings highlight the significance of TSLP-mediated signaling in hair growth regulation and suggest that modulation of the TRPV4–TSLP axis may represent a promising strategy for the development of novel therapeutic approaches for hair loss. Further studies will be required to determine whether similar mechanisms operate in human hair follicles and to evaluate the translational potential of targeting this pathway in clinical settings.
