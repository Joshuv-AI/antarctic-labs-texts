Optic disc edema during semaglutide therapy: case report and literature review
Authors: Tingting Yang, Aiming Chen, Feng Zeng, Jiayan Liu, Yilali Kamilijiang, Bowei Yuan, Yamei Lu, Guangming Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report fundus characteristics of patients who developed optic disc edema during semaglutide therapy and perform a review of the literature.ObservationsPatient 1 was a 62-year-old female with a 5-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), while Patient 2 was a 51-year-old female with documented T2DM for 6 years. Both patients were enrolled in a multicenter, randomized, open-label, parallel-controlled clinical trial titled 'Comparative Study of the Efficacy and Safety of two Semaglutide Injections in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus'. Baseline fundoscopic examinations prior to trial initiation confirmed the absence of diabetic retinopathy in both cases. Following the initiation of semaglutide therapy for glycemic control, surveillance fundoscopic examinations performed at 28 weeks (Case 1) and 24 weeks (Case 2) post-treatment revealed the development of optic disc edema. Case 1 remained entirely asymptomatic throughout the observation period. For case 1, the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0 logMAR bilaterally. Pupillary reflexes was normal and color vision was unaffected. Optic disc edema with peripapillary hemorrhages and focal yellow-white exudates were detected. Significant increased retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness was detected by OCT in the right eye, while the RNFL thickness of the left eye was normal. Visual fields were grossly normal in both eyes. For case 2, dim vision was reported. Ophthalmic examination revealed the BCVA of both eyes were 0.1logMAR. Pupillary reflexes was normal, and color vision was unaffected. Severe bilateral optic disc edema accompanied by extensive peripapillary patchy hemorrhages and yellow-white exudates were detected. Significant bilateral RNFL thicking were detected by OCT. Bilateral physiological blind spot enlargement were detected by visual field testing. No significant optic nerve functional impairment was observed in either patient. Oral corticosteroid therapy induced substantial resolution of optic disc edema in both patients.Conclusions and importanceDuring semaglutide therapy for glycemic control, clinicians should implement regular ophthalmic monitoring, particularly in the initial treatment phase, to detect potential neuro-ophthalmic complications including optic disc edema.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Semaglutide, Optic disc edema

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential across multiple disease domains, including diabetes mellitus, obesity, cardiovascular disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions.1,2 Semaglutide is a novel, long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist that exhibits potent hypoglycemic effects and offers multiple therapeutic benefits, including weight reduction, lipid metabolism modulation, and neuroprotective properties.1, 2, 3 However, semaglutide is associated with certain safety concerns, including common gastrointestinal adverse effects,4 potential risks of thyroid cancer, and rare cases of acute kidney injury.5 In addition to the well-documented exacerbation of diabetic retinopathy,6,7 ophthalmic adverse drug reactions associated with semaglutide include various retinal complications (retinopathy, vitreous hemorrhage, and posterior vitreous detachment), visual disturbances (blurred vision and decreased visual acuity), allergic conjunctivitis, and even color vision abnormalities.8 Furthermore, emerging case reports have identified non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) as a potential adverse effect associated with semaglutide therapy.9 However, optic disc edema in patients treated with semaglutide were rarely reported.

Here, we report two cases of optic disc edema occurring in Chinese female patients (aged >50 years) with longstanding type 2 diabetes (duration >5 years) participating in a multicenter, randomized, open-label, parallel-controlled clinical trial comparing the efficacy and safety of two semaglutide injections (Registration: CTR20222962). The clinical trial is still ongoing and it designed to enroll a total of 516 patients who receive semaglutide treatment. Both patients developed optic disc edema during semaglutide administration, with onset at 28 and 24 weeks of treatment, respectively.

Case report

The first case involved a 62-year-old female patient with a 5-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The second case involved a 51-year-old female patient with a 6-year history of T2DM. Both patients underwent comprehensive baseline assessments as specified in the protocol for the 'Multicenter, Randomized, Open-Label, Parallel-Controlled Clinical Trial Comparing the Efficacy and Safety of two Semaglutide Injections in T2DM Patients'. Evaluations included: (1) ophthalmic examination: fundus photography, (2) laboratory tests: complete blood count, blood glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), calcitonin, serum amylase, lipase, thyroid function, urinalysis, (3) imaging studies: electrocardiogram (ECG), color doppler ultrasounds (abdominal, urinary tract, thyroid). All assessments were completed prior to semaglutide initiation. Prior to treatment initiation, Patient 1 exhibited elevated metabolic parameters: fasting plasma glucose: 8.05 mmol/L (reference range: 3.9–6.1 mmol/L), HbA1c: 8.8 % (reference range: 3.6–6.0 %). The fasting blood glucose of the second patient was 7.33mmol/L, and the HbA1c was 7.5 %. Before using semaglutide, the fundus examination of both patients showed no microvascular malformation, retinal hemorrhage, or exudation changes, and no optic disc edema was observed (Fig. 1A and B). The semaglutide hypoglycemic regimen was administered as follows: an initial subcutaneous injection of 0.5 mg once weekly, followed by an increase to 1.0 mg once weekly after 12 weeks. The total treatment duration was 32 weeks, with follow-up assessments conducted every 4 weeks, extending to 36 weeks in total.

Comparative fundus photographs before and after semaglutide treatment. (A: Pre-treatment fundus photographs of patient 1. B: Pre-treatment fundus photographs of patient 1. C: Fundus photographs of patient 1 at 28 weeks post-treatment. D: Fundus photographs of patient 1 at 24 weeks post-treatment.)

The first patient developed right-eye optic disc edema with minor peripapillary hemorrhage, detected on fundus examination 28 weeks after initiating semaglutide therapy. Mild optic disc edema was observed in the left eye, accompanied by a small yellow-white retinal exudate superior to the temporal aspect of the disc (Fig. 1C). The patient reported no visual impairment in either eye, and denied experiencing any sensation of obstruction, headache, or ocular/systemic discomfort. Ophthalmic examination: The logarithm of the minimal angle of resolution (logMAR) best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0 bilaterally. Anterior segment examination revealed no abnormalities in either eye. Pupillary diameter and reflexes were normal, and color vision was unaffected. The second patient reported experiencing bilateral visual obstruction 24 weeks after initiating semaglutide therapy. Fundus photography examination demonstrated severe bilateral papilledema, accompanied by extensive peripapillary patchy hemorrhages and yellowish-white exudates (Fig. 1D). The patient denied any generalized discomfort, including headache. Ophthalmic examination revealed logMAR BCVA of 0.1 in both eyes. Anterior segment examination was unremarkable bilaterally. Pupillary diameter and reflexes were normal, and color vision was unaffected.

Fluorescence fundus angiography (FFA) demonstrated marked hyperfluorescence in the right optic disc during the venous phase, with associated mild blocked fluorescence. The left optic disc exhibited relatively normal fluorescence characteristics in the first patient. FFA revealed marked bilateral optic disc hyperfluorescence during the venous phase, accompanied by hemorrhages causing localized fluorescence blockage in the second patient (Fig. 2).

Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) and fluorescein fundus angiography (FFA) images obtained after semaglutide treatment. (A: FAF and FFA of patient 1. B: FAF and FFA of patient 2.)

Visual field testing revealed grossly normal binocular visual fields in the first patient. Visual field testing demonstrated bilateral physiological blind spot enlargement in the second patient (Fig. 3).

Visual field of the two patients after semaglutide treatment. (A: Right eye of patient 1. B: Left eye of patient 1. C: Right eye of patient 2. D: Left eye of patient 2.)

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed a significant increase in retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in the right eye of Patient 1, while the left eye exhibited RNFL thickness within normal limits. OCT revealed significant bilateral RNFL thickening in the second patient (Fig. 4).

Optical coherence tomography images of the two patients post-semaglutide treatment. (A: Right eye of patient 1. B: Left eye of patient 1. C:Both eyes of patient 1. D Right eye of patient 2. E: Left eye of patient 2. F: Both eyes of patient 2.)

Visual evoked potential (VEP) testing demonstrated normal waveform morphology and latency parameters in both patients (Fig. 5).

Visual evoked potential (VEP) recordings obtained after semaglutide treatment. (A:VEP of patient 1. B:VEP of patient 2.)

In addition, patient 2 underwent neuroimaging and lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, including intracranial pressure measurement and antibody testing. The signs identified by contrast-enhanced orbital CT scans were consistent with optic disc edema. The diagnostic workup, including intracranial pressure measurement and antibody testing, revealed no significant abnormalities.

Following the development of optic disc edema in both patients, oral corticosteroid therapy was initiated at an initial dosage of 1.0 mg/kg/day, followed by a gradual tapering regimen over a 3-month treatment course. Following treatment, both patients demonstrated significant resolution of optic disc edema with marked absorption of retinal hemorrhages and exudates (Fig. 6). Additionally, the second patient reported complete resolution of bilateral visual obscuration. Both patients developed varying degrees of corticosteroid-induced metabolic decompensation, including weight gain, moon facies, and hyperglycemia, without evidence of hepatic or renal impairment. These adverse effects resolved following drug withdrawal and supportive therapy.

Discussion

In our case, the patients developed optic disc edema during semaglutide therapy, with presentations ranging from asymptomatic to mild visual obscurations. Following systemic glucocorticoid therapy, we observed marked improvement in optic disc edema with complete resolution of associated retinal hemorrhages and exudates.

Semaglutide, a novel long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist, has emerged as an effective therapeutic option for diabetes management. In recent years, semaglutide has gained widespread clinical adoption. Recent epidemiological data indicates that during the majority of 2023, approximately 1.7 % of the US patient population received semaglutide prescriptions.10 However, ophthalmic adverse drug reactions associated with semaglutide have demonstrated a concerning upward trajectory in recent pharmacovigilance reports. The SUSTAIN-6 cardiovascular outcomes trial demonstrated a statistically significant increased risk of diabetic retinopathy (DR) progression among patients treated with semaglutide compared to placebo (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.76, 95 % CI: 1.11, 2.78).11 Therefore, ophthalmologic monitoring should be considered an essential component of semaglutide therapy, including baseline retinal examination and periodic follow-ups based on individual risk factors.

Optic disc edema caused by semaglutide has been documented in limited case reports. The findings of Cai et al. suggested a modest increase in the risk of NAION among individuals with T2D associated with semaglutide use.12 Recently, Ahmadi et al. documented four male patients who developed NAION with substantial visual impairment during semaglutide therapy.13 Compared to previous studies, our cases exhibited edema accompanied by yellow-white exudates, but without significant visual field defects which can be considered different from NAION.

The potential association between semaglutide use and optic disc edema remains uncertain, with current evidence being insufficient to establish a definitive causal relationship. The association between optic disc edema and increased pressure in the optic nerve sheath space, obstruction of retinal venous return, and exudation around optic disc capillaries suggests that optic disc edema associated with semaglutide may be caused by significant fluctuations in blood glucose and perfusion changes in a short period of time, leading to obstruction of venous return. Previous studies have reported that semaglutide was associated with NAION, and found that non diabetes patients were more likely to suffer from non arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy than diabetes patients.10 Rodent studies have shown that GLP-1 receptor agonists can have a protective effect on retinal lesions through glucose independent pathways.14 These findings suggest semaglutide may affect the eyes through diabetes-independent mechanism. In addition, the expression of GLP-1 receptors in the human optic nerve15 and the increased sympathetic nervous system activity induced by GLP-1 receptor agonists might influence optic nerve head perfusion and potentially increase the risk of optic disc edema.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our case report highlights the importance of vigilant monitoring for potential semaglutide-associated ophthalmic adverse effects in diabetic patients, particularly regarding optic disc edema and visual function changes. Moreover, research results have shown that adverse drug reactions caused by GLP-1 receptor agonists in the eyes often occur within the first year of use.8 Therefore, attention should be paid to progression of ocular health in the early stages of administration.
