Subclinical vascular risk composites and dementia imaging biomarkers 17–20 years later in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA)

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CVD risk factors captured in midlife represent potentially modifiable features of CVD, stroke, and dementia.•Baseline subclinical CVD factors differentially associated with MRI measures collected ∼17–20 years later.•Subclinical arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis factors most associated with downstream brain health.•Findings may highlight subclinical pathways of vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia.

Introduction

Vascular risk factors captured in midlife represent potentially modifiable features of cardiovascular disease (CVD), stroke, dementia, and dementia-related neuropathology [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Many of these factors are also linked to a higher likelihood of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and AD-related dementias (ADRD), consistent with the concept of vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) [[8], [9], [10]] (Fig. 1, Panel A; also see Fig. 1 from Hughes et al. 2024). However, conventional vascular risk factors for dementia are non-specific and provide limited insight into the subclinical structural or functional vascular changes with age that may underlie or contribute to VCID. To better capture the multidimensional nature of vascular aging, we developed four composite factor scores representing distinct physiological domains: arteriosclerosis, atherosclerosis, cardiac function, and blood pressure [12]. These composites integrate multiple subclinical measures, enhancing reliability and mechanistic specificity. This systems-level approach offers both a complementary and refined alternative for studying vascular contributions to neurodegeneration and brain health in aging and Alzheimer’s disease. Using longitudinal data from the Multi‑Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA; Fig. 1, Panel B), we recently demonstrated that these subclinical vascular composites, capturing structural and functional aspects of vascular aging, predict incident CVD and dementia events [12]. We showed that these orthogonal composite factors (atherosclerosis, e.g., arterial wall structural changes, calcification, and plaques; arteriosclerosis, e.g., arterial stiffness and compliance; blood pressure; and cardiac function, e.g., ventricular-arterial coupling and cardiac output dynamics; see Table S1 for all measures) were independently associated with cardiovascular events and dementia beyond conventional risk scores. Notably, composite measures of atherosclerosis and arteriosclerosis, which reflect large‑artery structural changes and plaque burden and metrics of arterial stiffening and microvascular dysfunction, respectively, independently predicted incident dementia over 17 years in models adjusted for dementia risk factors including APOE-ε4 carrier status [12]. Therefore, we hypothesize that these subclinical measures of vascular structure and function could help identify specific pathways underlying VCID beyond conventional dementia risk.

Vascular risk may be particularly relevant for Black and Hispanic individuals in the United States who are at increased risk for stroke and dementia [13] but do not appear to have excess risk for AD pathology [[14], [15], [16]]. MESA provides a unique opportunity to assess longitudinal relationships between subclinical cardiovascular assessments collected at baseline (at ages 45–84 years) and neuroimaging biomarkers acquired ∼17–20 years later in a large, racially, ethnically, and regionally diverse group of older adults free from clinical CVD at baseline. Our a priori objective was to test whether these pre-specified composites measured in midlife are differentially associated with later-life MRI (CVD and neurodegeneration measures; primary) and amyloid PET biomarkers (secondary), and whether these associations vary by gender or race/ethnicity [17].

Methods & materials

Participants

The MESA study enrolled a diverse cohort of 6814 adults aged 45–84 years at baseline (Exam 1, 2000–2002) and has followed them through seven clinical examinations and annual follow-up contacts [17]. MESA participants were recruited from six areas in the US: Baltimore, Maryland; Chicago, Illinois; Forsyth County, North Carolina; Los Angeles County, California; Northern Manhattan and the Bronx, New York; and Saint Paul, Minnesota. All participants self-reported their race/ethnicity as White (38 %), Chinese (12 %), African American/Black (28 %), or Hispanic (22 %) and were free from clinical CVD at baseline. Standardized questionnaires were used to collect data on participants’ demographics including self-reported age, race, ethnicity, education, medical history, household income, health insurance, occupation, and health behaviors. Self-reported race and ethnicity are used as social constructs in our analyses given the increased risk for dementia among Black and Hispanic groups and the need to establish the generalizability of vascular risk factors for dementia and AD/ADRD (Table 1). Self-reported education levels follow the International Standard Classification of Education. DNA was analyzed for APOE genotypes as previously described [18], and APOE-ε4 carriage was defined as the presence of one or more ε4 allele(s). Beginning at follow-up MESA Exam 6 (2016 to 2018; see Fig. 1), and MESA-MIND Visit A (2019–2021), all MESA participants were invited to complete brain MRI who had completed the UDSv3 cognitive battery permitting adjudication of cognitive status as normal, MCI, or probable dementia. PET imaging was offered at three sites (NC, MD, NY) based on site-specific scanner qualification, radiotracer availability, and participant safety as described further below. Clinical and subclinical vascular measures

Participants completed in-person clinical visits and annual follow-up calls over the duration of the study documenting clinical events of hospitalization, cardiovascular events, diabetes, and other related events or changes in medications and health status. Core clinical visit components included measures of anthropometry, seated blood pressure, blood draw, and medication inventory. Subclinical vascular measures include ankle-brachial index, cardiac MRI, arterial waveform analysis, carotid ultrasound, arterial tonometry, arterial flow-mediated dilation, cardiac computed tomography, and electrocardiography [12].

Subclinical cardiovascular risk scores

As previously described [12], a final set of 24 subclinical cardiovascular assessments from MESA Exam 1 (2000–2002) included: carotid ultrasound for plaque, inter-adventitial diameter, and intima-media thickness; coronary calcium by cardiac computed tomography; wave-form analysis from electrocardiogram; augmentation index, large and small artery elasticity, total vascular impedance, estimated cardiac output, and stroke volume by radial artery pulse wave analysis (see Table S1 for a full description of subclinical measures assessed). Each measure was transformed into z-scores, classified and weighted by factor analysis into standardized scaled composites representing four subclinical cardiovascular scores for: atherosclerosis (ATHERO), arteriosclerosis (ARTERIO), blood pressure (BP), and cardiac function (CARDIAC). Higher factor scores indicate greater CVD burden.

Conventional risk scores

Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Aging, and Incidence of Dementia Risk (CAIDE) composite scores were calculated for all participants using published equations for CAIDE [19,20]. CAIDE is comprised of age (<47 years; <53 years; ≥53 years), gender, self-reported years of education (<High School; High School; >High School), elevated systolic blood pressure (≥140 mmHg), body-mass index (>30 kg/m2), total cholesterol (>250 mg/dl), physical activity (active or inactive; defined as ≥500 MET-MIN/WK M-SU), and APOE-ε4 genotype. CAIDE scores were calculated using data drawn from questionnaires and clinical examinations acquired at baseline examination (MESA Exam 1, 2000–2002). In MESA, missing APOE-ε4 observations (n = 61; 4 %) were coded as 0 when calculating CAIDE.

Neuroimaging biomarkers

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were acquired on 3-Tesla (3T) Siemens scanners: Prisma (University of California Los Angeles, Columbia University, Johns Hopkins University, Northwestern University, University of Minnesota) and Skyra (University of California Los Angeles, Wake Forest University). Initial MRIs were collected on 1430 MESA participants between 2016 and 2021. Staff from the University of Pennsylvania Brain MRI Reading Center supervised study quality and trained MRI technologists to perform standardized imaging protocols. Structural MRI brain sequences included 1 mm isotropic sagittal 3D T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery. Other imaging collected for the protocol included axial 2D echo-planar diffusion-tensor imaging (DTI). A detailed brain MRI protocol can be found elsewhere [21]. Study MRI scans were transferred to the Reading Center from the MESA field centers using a HIPAA-compliant transfer image and data system (TRIAD, American College of Radiology, Philadelphia, PA).

MRI Measures: Results from this study include data from the T1, T2, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) MRI sequences. Variables of interest derived from the MRI images included total intracranial volume (ICV), total gray matter volume (GM VOL), bilateral hippocampal volume (HPC VOL), total WMH volume (WMH VOL), and WM fractional anisotropy (WM FA). Automated pipelines were applied for preprocessing structural MRIs, including inhomogeneity correction [22] and extraction of the intracranial brain tissues as well as cerebrospinal fluid using multi-atlas skull-stripping [23]. Anatomical regions of interest were identified using a multiatlas label fusion (MUSE); this method was used to segment GM and WM tissues, with the sum of GM and WM defining ICV [24]. Total intracranial volume was defined as the sum of all GM, WM, and cerebrospinal fluid. Using a deep learning-based white matter lesion segmentation method (WMLS) [25,26], WMH VOL was measured from inhomogeneity corrected and co-registered FLAIR and T1-weighted images [27,28]. The deep-learning model was trained using a separate training set with human-validated segmentation of WMH, applied to participants to calculate binary WMH masks as previously described [29]. MRI brain volume estimates (global and hippocampal volumes) and global WMH VOL were adjusted for total intracranial volume; global WMH VOL was log-transformed prior to analyses to be consistent with prior work [30]. Finally, WM FA is a measure of WM integrity calculated from DTI using automated pipelines [29]. As previously described [31], the degree of anisotropy of the diffusion at a voxel was calculated from diffusion tensor images at each brain voxel. FA maps were subsequently aligned to the T1 image of the subject using FLIRT (FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool), also part of FSL [32]. WM FA raw score data was transformed to z-scores; low WM FA z-scores are interpreted as demonstrating poor WM integrity and indicates WM injury burden in diseases affecting the WM, including cerebral small vessel disease. T1 images were collected on 1430 participants; a subset of participants did not complete T2 FLAIR (n = 16) or diffusion imaging acquisitions (n = 145) and thus were missing WMH and WM FA estimates respectively.

Amyloid PET imaging (Aβ-PET)

Amyloid PET imaging was initiated with [11C] Pittsburgh Compound B [11C]PiB at the Wake Forest site at Exam 6 (2016–2019) and expanded to Columbia and Johns Hopkins with MESA MIND Visit A (2019–2021). [11C]PiB acquisition methods described previously, [19,33,34] were used at both the Wake Forest and Columbia sites. Briefly, following a computed tomography (CT) scan for attenuation correction, participants were injected with ∼10 mCi [11C]PiB and scanned 40–70 min (6 × 5-min frames) post-injection on a 64-slice GE Discovery MI DR PET/CT scanner at Wake Forest and Siemens BioGraph mCT PET/CT scanner at Columbia. Amyloid PET imaging at Johns Hopkins sites incorporated [18F] Florbetaben (FBB): ∼8mCi [18F]FBB and scanned 90–110 min (4 × 5-min frames) post-injection on a Siemens BioGraph mCT PET/CT scanner. Each participant’s CT image was coregistered to their structural MRI, and PET frames were coregistered to MRI space using the affine matrix from the CT-MRI coregistration. Standardized Uptake Value Ratio (SUVr) images were co-registered and resliced to the T1 structural MRI. Aβ deposition (e.g., PiB uptake visualized by PET) was quantified using a voxelwise standardized uptake volume ratio (SUVr), calculated as the PiB SUVr (cerebellar gray reference) signal averaged from a cortical meta-ROI sensitive to the early pathogenesis of AD relative to the uptake in the cerebellum, using FreeSurfer-segmented regions [19,35]. Centiloid (CL) values were calculated to facilitate harmonization across ligands [36]. A CL value of 12.2 was used as a primary threshold to define a lower bound of Aβ-PET positivity, corresponding to moderate-to-frequent neuritic plaques as established by the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s disease (CERAD) [37,38].

Aβ-PET and WMH VOL were inverted in Fig. 1a for visualization purposes only.

Statistical analysis

The analytic sample included MESA participants with baseline subclinical composite scores and follow-up T1 Structural MRI (n = 1420) and amyloid PET (n = 281). Extreme values were identified and excised from outcome-specific analyses (HPC VOL: n = 1; WMH VOL: n = 7) using the is_extreme function (3 × IQR rule) from the rstatix package [39] resulting in a final sample of 1419 and 1407 for assessing HPC VOL and WMH VOL respectively. Chi-squared, Kruskal-Wallis rank sum, and Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine differences in baseline demographic and disease characteristics. General linear models (GLM) assessed relationships between primary exposure variables measuring upstream vascular risk factors (ARTERIO, ATHERO, CARDIAC, and BP) and downstream MRI measures of brain structure, PET measures of global amyloid deposition, and diffusion-weighted WM FA [30]. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess associations between our primary exposure variables and amyloid positivity (see supplementary materials for additional details). Joint (all subclinical factors entered simultaneously; primary) and independent (secondary) GLMs were constructed adjusting for CAIDE (Model 1) and for age, self-reported gender, race, education level, and APOE-ε4 carrier status (Model 2). Models were compared to and/or adjusted for CAIDE given known associations with AD/ADRD neuroimaging biomarkers [19] and our previous work showing that CAIDE is associated with incident dementia over and above either the Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease – Pooled Cohort Equation [40], Framingham Global CVD [41], and Framingham 10-year Stroke [42]. Models adjusting for individual covariates, including components of CAIDE, were not further adjusted for the CAIDE composite to avoid multicollinearity and model overfitting. In post-hoc analyses (see Supplementary Material), we additionally controlled for several other components of CAIDE (e.g., systolic blood pressure, BMI, total cholesterol), family history of AD (Yes/No), smoking status, medication use (CVD medications), and collection site. We also calculated a modified CAIDE, excluding systolic blood pressure, to ensure results comparing factor BP and imaging outcomes were not biased. CAIDE and the modified CAIDE were nearly perfectly correlated (r=0.951, p<.001) and did not impact findings (results are thus not discussed further). In exploratory analyses, we assessed potential interactions between subclinical vascular composite scores and gender, race, and APOE-ε4 carrier status (Yes/No) [40,41]. Likewise, as noted in our prior work, subclinical vascular risk factors (ARTERIO, ATHERO, CARDIAC, and BP) are highly correlated with age with higher levels representing a higher degree of vascular aging and subclinical disease. Therefore, individual and joint (all subclinical factors entered simultaneously) GLMs were constructed before and after adjusting for age in addition to self-reported gender, race, education level, and APOE-ε4 carrier status (data not shown). Models were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR) [43]. GLMs were performed using the base lm function in R. Alpha was set at p < .05.

Prediction modeling

Random Forest (RF; see supplementary material for greater details) [[44], [45], [46]] was used to assess how individual subclinical vascular measures (24 features), collected at baseline (MESA Exam 1, 2000–2002), irrespective of their associate factor, predicted neuroimaging biomarker levels and amyloid positivity ∼17–20 years later at follow-up (MESA Exam 6, 2017–2021). All statistical analyses were conducted in R (RStudio Team, 2020).

Results

Primary analyses

Participants. Participants in MESA with subclinical composite scores and follow-up T1 volumetric MRI (n = 1420) and amyloid PET (n = 281) are shown in Table 1. Overall, the sample had a median age at baseline of 55, 54 % were women, and 26 % were APOE-ε4 carriers. Among participants with brain MRI, 42 % self-reported their race/ethnicity as White, 12 % Chinese, 27 % African American/Black, and 19 % Hispanic (see Table 1). Baseline subclinical factor scores for the current sample with MRI were slightly lower than the full sample of MESA participants on average (see Fig. S1).

Joint linear regression models

In joint linear regression models, where all subclinical factors were entered simultaneously along with the conventional CAIDE risk score (Model 1), subclinical factors were associated with lower GM VOL, greater WMH VOL, and reduced WM FA (Fig. 2a, Table 2; all p < .05). Factor BP was not associated with HPC VOL (p > .05) and only factor ARTERIO was associated with higher Aβ-PET deposition independent of CAIDE (Table 2, p = .030). In fully adjusted joint linear models (Model 2), adjusting for age, gender, race, education, and APOE-ε4 status, factors ARTERIO, CARDIAC, and BP were associated with reduced WM FA (all p < .05; Table 2). Factor ARTERIO was associated with lower HPC VOL, factor CARDIAC with lower GM VOL, and factors ATHERO, CARDIAC, and BP were associated with greater WMH VOL (all p < .05; Table 2). Briefly, CAIDE-adjusted joint regression models explained approximately 3–20 % of the total variance in neuroimaging outcomes, while fully adjusted joint regression models including all subclinical factors, age, gender, race, education, APOE-ε4 status explained approximately 19–43 % of the variance in neuroimaging outcomes (Table S2). Fully adjusted GLM’s controlling for additional health-related covariates (e.g., medication, family history of AD, etc.; see Methods) are provided in Table S3. Effect modification

Linear associations between baseline subclinical vascular composites and downstream neuroimaging measures of overall brain health were largely independent of gender, race, or APOE-ε4 status (data not shown). However, we did observe significant interactions between specific subclinical factor scores, gender, and race/ethnicity, across models, such that: (1) higher factor ATHERO scores and lower GM VOL was stronger in men than in women and (2) higher factor ARTERIO scores and reduced WM FA was stronger in White participants than in Chinese and Hispanic participants (all interaction p < .05). As expected, baseline age was significantly associated with all subclinical factor scores and with neuroimaging measures acquired at follow-up (see Fig. S3; associations were consistent across self-reported racial groups). Specifically, older age (cross-sectional; between-persons) were associated with poorer overall brain health (higher Aβ-PET deposition, lower GM VOL and HPC VOL, greater WMH VOL, and reduced WM FA [all p < .05; Table S5]). In exploratory analyses (data not shown), excluding age from the fully adjusted joint models (all subclinical factors plus age, gender, race, education, and APOE-ε4 status), subclinical factors were significantly associated with brain imaging outcomes with several exceptions. Factor CARDIAC and BP were not significantly associated with Aβ-PET deposition, and factor BP was not associated with HPC VOL (all p > .05). Models excluding age explained 5–28 %.

Predictive modeling

Random forest regression predicting continuous neuroimaging outcomes

Model performance and validation metrics for predicting continuous MRI and Aβ-PET outcomes are detailed in the Supplementary Material (Table S8; Fig. S4). Briefly, subclinical features most accurately predicted global and hippocampal volumes, with lower performance for Aβ-PET deposition. Results were consistent across model specifications before and after imputation status. Random forest classification predicting amyloid positivity

Details of model performance (Table S10) and feature importance (Table S9, Panel B; Fig. S6) in predicting Aβ-PET positivity are provided in the Supplementary Material. Briefly, classification accuracy ranged from 69 to 74 %, with consistent performance across model specifications (Table S11). Age, APOE-ε4 genotype, and vascular features, particularly components of ARTERIO (small and large artery elasticity) and BP (stroke volume and small vessel diameter) emerged as the most predictive subclinical markers.

Discussion

Baseline composites of subclinical cardiovascular factors representing aspects of subclinical vascular aging have been associated with greater CVD events, stroke events, and incident dementia over 17 years of follow-up in MESA [12]. Critically, these subclinical vascular composites have been validated against conventional clinical risk scores for CVD (e.g., Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease–Pooled Cohort Equation [40], Framingham Global CVD [41], Framingham 10-year Stroke [42]) and dementia with CAIDE [20,12]. We showed composites of arteriosclerosis (ARTERIO) and atherosclerosis (ATHERO) at baseline were the most predictive of CVD, stroke, and dementia events over 17 years [12]. Here, we extend this work by evaluating how subclinical CVD composites and their individual components (n = 24) measured at MESA Exam 1 are related to sensitive downstream neuroimaging measures indexing general brain health and amyloid deposition as an AD-specific marker.

Consistent with prior work, we found that factors ARTERIO, ATHERO, and their individual components exhibited the strongest associations with and were most predictive of long-term brain health outcomes. These included reduced global and hippocampal volumes, greater white matter hyperintensity burden and reduced fractional anisotropy, and, to a lesser extent, greater Aβ-PET deposition. Importantly, these findings were independent of CAIDE, a midlife dementia risk score that incorporates vascular, demographic, and lifestyle factors to estimate long‑term dementia risk [12]. Interestingly, factor CARDIAC was associated with smaller hippocampal volume in CAIDE-adjusted models only. Conversely, factor BP showed the opposite pattern: no association in CAIDE-adjusted models, but a positive association with hippocampal volume in fully adjusted models. Additional longitudinal work will be needed to clarify whether these patterns reflect true biological mechanisms, treatment effects, or cohort-specific factors. All factors, including both CARDIAC and BP (BP in particular) were associated with measures of white matter disease (e.g., greater white matter hyperintensity burden and reduced fractional anisotropy) in both CAIDE and fully-adjusted models. These findings are in line with hypothesized pathways linking subclinical cardiovascular disease to age and dementia-related neurodegeneration and eventual cognitive impairment (e.g., Fig. 1A). Further, results suggest more general roles for cardiac functioning and blood pressure over chronological age, whereas markers of arteriosclerosis (e.g., arterial stiffness) may be more directly be linked to dementia-specific outcomes including Aβ-PET deposition, although caution with overinterpretation is warranted [10,12,47,48], as also discussed further below.

Assessing descriptive characteristics of the MESA cohort, we observed that White participants had elevated levels of arteriosclerosis, and CAIDE scores were highest in Hispanic participants as previously reported [49,50]; Black participants exhibited relatively elevated subclinical composite scores compared to all other self-reported racial groups [49,51,52]. Critically, however, linear associations between baseline subclinical vascular composites and downstream neuroimaging measures were largely independent of gender, race, and APOΕ-ε4 carrier status, consistent with prior work [12,34,53,54]. Across models assessed, we observed a single interaction effect for race such that the relationship between higher levels of factor ARTERIO and reduced WM FA was strongest in White (weakest in Chinese) participants. As with race, we observed a single interaction effect when assessing gender. Here, the relationship between higher levels of factor ATHERO and higher levels of factor ARTERIO with lower global gray matter volume was stronger in men compared to women [55]. This finding is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that vascular (atherosclerotic and arteriosclerotic) pathology is associated with reduced brain volume, and that in some cohorts these associations appear stronger in men than in women potentially reflecting greater plaque burden, lower cerebral perfusion, and heightened vascular vulnerability in men, alongside the vasoprotective effects of estrogen in premenopausal women [[55], [56], [57], [58], [59]]. At present, the clinical significance of these interaction effects is uncertain. As noted above, our previous work found that associations between subclinical composite scores and CVD risk and incident dementia were also independent of gender, race, and APOΕ-ε4 carrier status [12]. Future longitudinal work is needed to further assess how gender, race, and APOΕ-ε4 carrier status, in the context of other important demographic and health-related risk factors, may modify long-term relationships between subclinical vascular health and neurodegeneration. The current work suggests the observed associations between subclinical vascular factors and brain imaging are generalizable across these various groups.

Random Forest Modeling of Subclinical Measures and Long-Term Brain Health Outcomes. Cross-validated models predicting MRI-based estimates of global and regional gray matter atrophy performed best overall, followed by measures of white matter brain health indexed by total white matter hyperintensity burden and global estimates of WM FA. Overall, Aβ-PET models were less accurate, and performance varied across iterations of repeated cross-validation (e.g., Fig. S4). Subclinical measures affiliated with composites indexing aspects of: (1) arteriosclerosis (ARTERIO; systemic vascular resistance, estimated cardiac output, carotid distensibility, and small artery elasticity); and (2) atherosclerosis (ATHERO; maximum Intima-media thickness and common carotid intimal-media thickness) were most predictive. Interestingly, two features, estimated stroke volume and small vessel diameter, affiliated with factors CARDIAC and BP respectively, were also consistently among the top ranked predictive features across models. Coronary calcium (ATHERO) was uniquely predictive of global gray matter brain atrophy [60], while small artery elasticity (ARTERIO) and large artery elasticity (ARTERIO), along with small vessel diameter (BP) were ranked relatively higher among subclinical measures predicting hippocampal volume. Though speculative and requiring additional follow-up, differences between global and regional volumetric models may be attributed to and highlight regional differences in blood supply and arterial circulation that differentially contribute to long-term brain health [61].

Amyloid Deposition. Several, though not all, primarily cross-sectional studies have reported modest associations between indices of atherosclerosis and arteriosclerosis and increased amyloid deposition [8,19,54,[62], [63], [64], [65]]. Mechanistically, these associations may reflect hypoxia-induced neuronal loss and impaired clearance of amyloid from the central nervous system [4,5,8,66]. Overall, we observed modest associations with amyloid PET deposition and positivity that were attenuated in fully adjusted models. Midlife indices of arteriosclerosis were modestly associated with higher average Aβ-PET deposition ∼17–20 years later over and above general aspects of cardiovascular health. Arteriosclerosis, specifically, was also the single most predictive factor when Aβ-PET positivity was defined using a more conservative threshold (thus reducing potential false positives regarding amyloid status). Confidence intervals for associations between ATHERO and amyloid PET were wide, suggesting variability or heterogeneity in how vascular atherosclerotic burden relates to amyloid deposition; future work with larger samples and flexible modeling approaches will be important to clarify the link between atherosclerosis and amyloid deposition. While broadly consistent with reports linking arterial stiffness to Aβ deposition on PET imaging [48,67], inconsistencies across studies, together with our finding that PET‑based associations were less robust than those for MRI outcomes, underscore the need for cautious interpretation and additional follow-up. Here, we do not propose that subclinical CVD factors or their respective components are specific markers of amyloid pathology. Rather, we were interested in understanding the underlying age-related vascular co-pathology that may interact with AD pathological processes.

Vascular Aging. In fully adjusted GLMs, as expected, age explained the largest proportion of variance in all imaging outcomes assessed. In exploratory analyses, excluding age from adjusted models, we again observed significant associations between our subclinical composite scores and long-term brain health. Notably, all subclinical factors were positively associated with age; however, age was most strongly associated with factors ARTERIO and ATHERO and at levels comparable to CAIDE (which includes age). Likewise, as noted above, interactions between age and subclinical measures contributed to improved prediction of neuroimaging outcomes in random forest models. Unlike conventional measures, our composites are comprised solely of subclinical cardiovascular metrics – they do not incorporate demographic factors, genetic information, or health status, and are intended to provide a means of dissociating specific subclinical vascular pathways leading to cognitive impairment and dementia. Risk of CVD events, stroke events, cognitive impairment, and poorer brain health outcomes associated with risk of dementia increase with advanced chronological age; however, individuals age at different rates, and it remains important to understand the specific mechanisms underlying age-related CVD phenotypes and risk of dementia.

Limitations & future directions

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations and analytical considerations. First, adjudicated baseline cognitive status was not collected in MESA, precluding adjustment for or stratification by baseline cognition; this is important to note given the ∼17–20-year gap between subclinical CVD assessment and neuroimaging. To address this and better contextualize these associations over time, we are extending this work to examine cognition and cognitive status longitudinally in subsequent MESA follow-up. Second, changes may occur in subclinical CVD over time. To better understand the specific contributions of cardiovascular health to age-related cognitive impairment and dementia, future longitudinal work will develop time-varying scores to account for (e.g., model) biological aging trajectories to identify the earliest stage at which subclinical measures are most predictive of future health outcomes and dementia risk. Third, while the MESA cohort is a large and diverse cohort followed longitudinally for over two decades, this sample is not without selection bias toward healthier participants, contributing to an overrepresentation of healthy individuals, as is common in many imaging studies involving older adults. Fourth, our findings must be evaluated within the context of the MESA imaging cohort as analyzed here, specifically, the smaller number of PET scans and low prevalence of amyloid PET positive participants (∼24–30 % depending on the positivity threshold). Recruitment in MESA for PET and repeated MRI is ongoing, and we are in the process of acquiring sensitive blood-based biomarkers in a large portion of MESA participants (e.g., p-tau217) which will increase statistical power to estimate and predict amyloid deposition [[68], [69], [70], [71]]. Increasing the number of available PET scans and incorporating plasma AD biomarkers will help further confirm observed and hypothesized relationships between subclinical CVD measures and amyloid deposition. Finally, and perhaps critically, both AD and VCID are multifactorial co-pathologies common in age-related dementias; we fully recognize amyloid burden is just one aspect of these complex diseases.

Regarding prediction modeling, we aimed (1) to provide a comprehensive evaluation of subclinical measures in relation to neuroimaging outcomes, and (2) to understand the extent to which patterns of subclinical health are influenced by high-dimensional interactions with age, gender, race, and dementia-related risk factors. We did not perform an exhaustive search for the optimal tuning parameters, nor compare or combine statistical learning models using various algorithms other than the Random Forest – steps which can be taken when building upon this foundational work. Explainable artificial intelligence methods [[72], [73], [74]] can also be utilized to further deconstruct conditional properties inherent to tree-based algorithms (e.g., partial dependence, accumulated local effects) and to leverage participant-level feature importance metrics (e.g., Shapley values) to understand risk at both the group and individual level. Causality cannot be inferred or concluded in this study; our goal was to establish an associative framework connecting aspects of subclinical CVD to brain health and AD/ADRD biomarkers. Future efforts will test the proposed theoretical framework illustrated in Fig. 1 (Panel A) and incorporate both baseline subclinical composites and measures as assessed here, and follow-up examination closer to the time of scan and cognitive adjudication.

Moving forward, it will be important to construct more comprehensive outcome measures that capture both age and dementia-related phenotypes that can be linked to upstream cardiovascular health (e.g., subclinical measures can be used to predict model-based outcomes (e.g., predicted [cardiovascular] age, dementia risk based on predicted probabilities from statistical learning models). The combination of demographics, health-related information, subclinical vascular measures, and neuroimaging outcomes acquired at follow up can also be used to generate a single predicted vascular age score for each participant and serve as a sensitive outcome and as an indicator of overall health linked to SVD. As additional CVD measures acquired at follow-up, closer to the time of scan acquisition become available, we plan to comprehensively evaluate longitudinal trajectories and incorporate causal modeling to clarify the sequence of events from subclinical to clinical CVD that may signal greater risk of cognitive impairment and dementia.

Conclusion

As described in our method validation study [12], subclinical CVD composites derived in MESA, constructed from sensitive physiologic measures for phenotyping vascular health, not commonly acquired in other cohorts, are not designed for use in prediction models or to inform diagnosis. However, the rich repertoire of subclinical measures acquired in MESA provides a novel opportunity to evaluate how specific indices of cardiovascular function contribute to long-term brain health. Subclinical composites of arteriosclerosis (ARTERIO), atherosclerosis (ATHERO), cardiac function (CARDIAC), and blood pressure (BP) have been linked with an increased risk of CVD events and dementia over conventional risk scores. Here we show that composites and measures indexing subclinical vascular health were associated with and predictive of poorer long-term brain health. Consistent with prior work, subclinical measures affiliated with arteriosclerosis (e.g., arterial stiffness) and atherosclerosis were most predictive of neuroimaging outcomes including reduced global and hippocampal gray matter brain volumes and higher white matter hyperintensity burden ∼17–20 years later. Ultimately, our findings further highlight subclinical vascular pathways in aging that may contribute to cognitive impairment and mixed forms of dementia in later life.
