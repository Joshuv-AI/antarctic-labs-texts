A novel nanosensor for in situ detection of EV-miR-107 enables noninvasive tuberculosis diagnosis and treatment monitoring
Authors: Yuan Wu, Shihua Luo, jin Gu, Wei Xu, Min Yang, Jiayu Zhou, Xiaosai Ma, Chonghai Qiu, Muru Xu, Guixian Huang, Liying Zhu, Xunjia Cheng, Hongbo Shen, Qiliang Cai, Feifei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) remains a leading cause of infectious death globally, creating an urgent need for rapid and non-invasive diagnostics. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as promising carriers of disease-specific microRNAs with biomarker potential. This study identifies EV-miR-107 as a candidate host-response biomarker for TB, showing significant upregulation in TB patient plasma and a murine BCG infection model. To utilize this finding, an in-situ detection platform named CLiAN (CHA@Lipo@Aptamer Nanosensor) is developed. It utilizes cationic liposomes functionalized with CD63-specific aptamers and encapsulating catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) probes to enable targeted EV fusion, facilitating efficient EV miRNA profiling within two hours. Clinically, CLiAN detects EV-miR-107 with 95.2% sensitivity (AUC = 0.87), outperforming sputum culture (36.4%) and Xpert MTB/RIF (54.5%). It also reliably monitors treatment response via dynamic changes in plasma EV-miR-107 levels (p = 0.001), correlating with clinical outcomes. This work establishes an integrated framework for TB management—from biomarker discovery to diagnostics and treatment monitoring—paving the way for improved point-of-care TB control.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04780-w.Keywords: Extracellular vesicles (EVs), Tuberculosis diagnosis, Catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA), Liposome-EV fusion, EV-miR-107 biomarker

Introduction

According to the latest Global Tuberculosis Report released by the WHO, tuberculosis claimed an estimated 1.23 million lives worldwide in 2024 [1, 2]. TB ranks among the top ten causes of death globally and stands as the leading cause of mortality from a single infectious agent [3–5]. Scientific advancements in TB control are critical to mitigating its epidemic impact and promoting societal progress [6, 7]. Current laboratory diagnostics primarily include bacterial culture, acid-fast staining smear microscopy, Xpert MTB/RIF testing, interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), and tuberculin skin tests (TSTs)[8–10]. Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture, the diagnostic gold standard, is slow (2∼8 weeks) and detects only 30%∼62% of TB [11]. Acid-fast staining and Xpert MTB/RIF offer results within hours but suffer from suboptimal sensitivity [12, 13]. Additionally, the main samples for these three methods are sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). Sputum testing accuracy is heavily dependent on sample quality, while BALF collection entails invasive procedures under anesthesia, posing inherent clinical risks [14, 15]. Therefore, developing rapid and accurate diagnostic methods for tuberculosis is crucial for effective TB prevention and control.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), lipid bilayer-enclosed nanoparticles (30∼2000 nm) carrying nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, are abundant in bodily fluids (e.g., blood, urine and BALF)[16–18]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), ∼22-nucleotide non-coding RNAs, regulate gene expression and have emerged as diagnostic biomarkers due to their disease-specific expression profiles in EVs [19–21]. For instance, Hu’s study analyzed exosomal miRNAs from 370 participants (including pulmonary tuberculosis [PTB] and tuberculous meningitis [TBM] patients, non-TB disease controls, and healthy controls), identifying six differentially expressed exosomal miRNAs (e.g., miR-20a and miR-191). The findings demonstrated that combining exosomal miRNAs with electronic health records significantly improves TB diagnostic accuracy [22]. Kaushik et al. performed sequencing analysis of exosomal ncRNAs from Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra-, M. bovis BCG-, and Streptococcus pneumoniae-infected THP-1-derived macrophages, along with uninfected controls, and validated miR-185-5p as a differentially expressed candidate biomarker in THP-1 cells, clinical PBMCs, and plasma samples [23]. However, the sequencing results in this study were derived from cell-based experiments rather than clinical samples themselves, thus their potential clinical applicability requires further investigation.

Current methods for EV-miRNA detection primarily fall into two categories [24]. The first involves EV lysis followed by RNA extraction and analysis—a process that is not only technically demanding but also risks causing nucleic acid leakage during EV disruption [25, 26]. Moreover, this approach permits only bulk assay analysis rather than single-EV resolution. The second category employs in situ EV analysis, which comprises three strategies: (1) passive diffusion of detection probes into EVs, which is hampered by prolonged incubation times and low efficiency [27]; (2) pore formation on EV membranes to facilitate probe entry, a technique that risks target leakage and compromises membrane integrity, potentially skewing results [28]; and (3) membrane fusion-mediated probe delivery [29, 30]. The fusion-based approach stands out by enabling single-EV analysis while preserving membrane integrity. It outperforms passive diffusion in both speed and efficiency, as fusion process rapidly delivers probes for EV-miRNA recognition without damaging the EV structure. Currently, this method typically utilizes lipid-encapsulated molecular beacons, though their detection sensitivity remains limited compared to that of enzyme-free, isothermal signal amplification systems such as catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) probes [31]. Furthermore, conventional fusion relies solely on membrane fluidity between bare liposomes and EVs [32]. Functionalizing liposome surfaces with EV-targeting antibodies or aptamers could significantly enhance fusion specificity and efficiency [33].

While most current studies have focused either on screening novel EV‑miRNAs for tuberculosis diagnosis using only qPCR validation, or on developing EV‑miRNA detection methods that rely on pre‑validated biomarkers, an integrated approach that combines the discovery of host‑derived EV‑miRNAs with the development of a corresponding diagnostic method has been lacking[34, 35]. This study identified EV-miR-107 as a potential diagnostic biomarker for tuberculosis through small RNA sequencing of plasma EVs from TB patients and healthy controls, followed by qPCR validation in clinical cohorts. Further in vivo studies revealed that miR-107-enriched EVs are likely derived from lymphocytes in the pulmonary infection microenvironment. These findings demonstrate EV-miR-107 as a potential host-response biomarker for tuberculosis diagnosis and progression monitoring. We subsequently developed a cationic liposome-encapsulated CHA probe functionalized with CD63 aptamers (designated CHA@Lipo@Aptamer Nanosensor, CLiAN). CLiAN delivers CHA probes into EVs via membrane fusion without causing lysis, enabling in situ, signal-amplified detection of EV-miRNA. The resulting fluorescence signals facilitate nanoflow cytometry analysis at single-particle sensitivity. Clinical validation demonstrated that CLiAN achieves 95.2% sensitivity (20/21) in diagnosing tuberculosis using plasma EV-miR-107, with potential utility for treatment response monitoring. Collectively, our findings establish a paradigm for tuberculosis management, spanning biomarker discovery, validation, and clinical implementation to advance TB diagnostics and therapy (Fig. 1).

Methods

Ethical statement and study participant details

All clinical plasma samples used in this study were sourced from Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital and strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines set out in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. This study has been officially approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (Grant No. K21-266). All samples (131 cases in total) were collected anonymously prior to treatment, and participant clinically relevant information was recorded.

Cell culture and EV isolation

Human monocytes cell line THP-1 (RRID: CVCL_0006) and human non-small-cell lung carcinoma cell line A549 (RRID: CVCL_0023) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Both cell lines were regularly authenticated and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. THP-1 and A549 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, C22400500BT) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099141 C) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 atmosphere. After culturing cells in exosome-depleted serum for 48 h, the cell culture supernatant was collected and sequentially centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, 3,000 g for 20 min, and 10,000 g for 30 min, with the supernatant retained after each step. The final supernatant was ultracentrifuged (XNP-100) at 110,000 g for 70 min. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and centrifuged again at 110,000 g for 70 min. Finally resuspend the EV with PBS and store at −80 °C.

EV isolation from clinical plasma samples

Whole blood was collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes and thoroughly mixed with the anticoagulant. The samples were centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min to obtain plasma, which was then diluted with an equal volume of PBS. Subsequent centrifugation steps were performed at 3,000 g for 20 min and 12,000 g for 30 min, with the supernatant collected after each step. The final supernatant was ultracentrifuged (XNP-100) at 135,000 g for 70 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS, centrifuged again at 135,000 g for 70 min, and finally resuspended in PBS for storage at −80 °C.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

EV suspension (10 µL) was loaded onto carbon-coated grids and incubated for 10 min. Subsequently, 10 µL of phosphotungstic acid staining solution was applied for 2 min, followed by removal of excess stain with filter paper. After air-drying for 10∼20 min, samples were examined using a low-voltage transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2, 120 kV) for imaging.

Western blot

EV samples were lysed using RIPA buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, mixed with 5 × loading buffer, and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After rapid blocking for 15 min, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD9 (ab263019, Abcam), CD63 (ab134045, Abcam), and TSG101 (ab125011, Abcam). The following day, membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system after reaction with ECL reagent.

Quantification and size distribution analysis of particles

The EV suspension and liposome was serially diluted and analyzed using nanoflow cytometry (Fuliu, Xiamen) with the following instrument settings: laser power 5 mW, SS decay 10%, sampling pressure 1.0 kPa, and particle count recorded for 1 min (target range: 4,000∼8,000 particles). EV concentration and size distribution were calculated based on calibration with concentration and size standards, accounting for dilution factors.

Small RNA sequencing analysis of plasma EV samples

Total RNA was extracted from plasma EVs using the QIAGEN kit (77144). Briefly, the EVs suspension was lysed with QIAzol, followed by chloroform addition and centrifugation to collect the upper aqueous phase, which was then mixed with absolute ethanol. The mixture was transferred to the RNeasy MinElute spin column provided in the kit, washed sequentially with RWT and RPE buffers, and finally eluted with RNase-free water to obtain purified RNA. The extracted RNA was subjected to quality control with the following criteria: RNA concentration ≥ 1 ng/µL and total RNA ≥ 20 ng. Sequencing libraries were then prepared using PAGE-based miRNA isolation, followed by quality assessment and quantification. Finally, paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 10⁶ extracellular vesicles (EVs) using a QIAGEN extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA (TIANGEN, FP411), followed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis using miRNA-specific forward primers and a poly(A) tailing-based detection system (TIANGEN, KR211). Relative quantification of U6 and target miRNAs was performed using SYBR Green-based qPCR assays, with expression levels calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method.

Preparation of CHA probes

Two hairpin probes, H1 and H2, were designed based on an enzyme-free strand displacement catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) nucleic acid amplification circuit. Their stem-loop structures were predicted using the IDT online tool (sg.idtdna.com). H1 contained a sticky end region complementary to the target miRNA and a sequence partially complementary to H2; the loop region of H2 was complementary to the exposed single-stranded region of H1. The CHA reaction mechanism was as follows: the trigger strand (target miRNA) bound to the sticky end of H1, opening the stem-loop of H1. The exposed single-stranded region then hybridized with the loop region of H2, leading to strand displacement and opening of H2, while releasing the trigger strand for the next cycle. This process ultimately generated a large number of H1-H2 duplex complexes. The DNA sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, purified by HPLC, and dissolved in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). Prior to use, H1 and H2 were separately heated at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by slow cooling to room temperature (approximately 25 °C) for 30 min to allow stable hairpin formation, and were then stored at 4 °C until use.

Agarose electrophoresis

A 3.5% agarose gel was prepared by boiling and cooling to approximately 55 °C, followed by addition of nucleic acid stain and casting. The H1 and H2 probes, along with CHA reaction products, were electrophoresed in 0.5 × TBE buffer at a constant voltage of 150 V for 30 min. Gel images were captured using a gel documentation system.

Fluorescence measurements

The H1 and H2 probes were reacted at a final concentration of 100 nM each with the target analyte in TNaK buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 125 mM NaCl, and 20 mM KCl, pH 7.5) during a 30-minute dark incubation at 25 °C. Fluorescence signals were measured using a multi-function microplate detection platform (Spark, Tecan) with the following parameters: excitation at 460 nm, emission scanning from 505 to 650 nm (3 nm step size).

Liposome preparation

The lipid mixture was prepared by dissolving N-[1-(2,3-Dioleyloxy)propyl]-N, N,N-trimethylammonium chloride (DOTMA), cholesterol (CHO), and DSPE-PEG-2000 (49:49:2 molar ratio) in 20 µL anhydrous ethanol. Meanwhile, 50 pmol each of denatured H1 and H2 probes were mixed in 30 µL TNak buffer. The lipid and probe solutions were combined at a 12.5:1 mass ratio (lipid: probe), then injected into 450 µL DPBS buffer. After 15 min incubation at 25 °C in the dark, CLiN were formed. Subsequent addition of 5 pmol CD63-CHO and 30 min incubation at 37 °C (dark) yielded CLiAN, which were stored at 4 °C protected from light.

Zeta potential measurement of liposomes

Liposome samples were 10-fold diluted with ultrapure water to 1 mL and loaded into a zeta potential cell using a syringe. Measurements were performed using a Zetasizer (Nano ZS-ZEN3600) with the following parameters: 120 s equilibration time and 20 runs per measurement. Each sample was measured in triplicate. Between samples, the cell was sequentially rinsed with anhydrous ethanol and ultrapure water.

CLSM

EVs were labeled with DiD dye, and the CHA probes encapsulated within CLiN/CLiAN emitted FITC fluorescence upon membrane fusion with EVs and subsequent reaction with the target. Briefly, confocal dishes were coated with 200 µL of 1 × poly-L-lysine (PLL) and incubated at room temperature for 15 ~ 30 min, followed by drying at 60 °C for 2 h. After cooling to room temperature, the CLiN-EV or CLiAN-EV reaction mixture was added and allowed to react at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The reaction solution was then removed, and samples were blocked with 1% BSA for 2 min, washed three times with PBS, and fixed with 200 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min. After three additional PBS washes, the dishes were covered with PBS and imaged using a DMi8 microscope (Leica) with FITC and DiD fluorescence channels.

Analyze of EV-liposome fusion by FRET

1 × 108 EVs were resuspended in 400 µL PBS containing 5 µL Dil and 5 µL DiD dye or 5 µL DiD dye only, followed by incubation at 25 °C for 30 min protected from light. Unincorporated dyes were removed using 100 kDa ultrafiltration tubes (3,000 g, 20 min, repeated three times). The labeled EVs were then incubated with liposomes (1 × 108 or 1 × 109) at 37 °C for 2 h to allow membrane fusion. Fluorescence signals were measured using multi-function microplate detection platform (spark, Tecan) with excitation at 480 nm and emission scanning from 525∼700 nm.

Detection of EV-miR-107 with CLiAN

For EV detection, 10 µL CLiN or 11 µL CLiAN was incubated with EVs in 100 µL PBS at 37 °C for 2 h. For the detection of plasma EV samples, a uniform input of 1 × 10⁶ EVs was used. The reaction mixture was then analyzed by nanoflow cytometry under the following settings: laser power 10 mW, SS decay 0.2%, with 1 min acquisition time. The percentage of FITC-positive particles was determined for quantitative analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro‑Wilk test. For data meeting the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, intergroup differences were compared using Student’s t‑test. For data that were normally distributed but had unequal variances between the two groups, Welch’s t‑test was applied. If either group deviated from normality, the Mann‑Whitney U test was used. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

Results

Discovery and validation of EV-miR-107 as a Tuberculosis diagnostic biomarker

During tuberculosis (TB) infection, miRNAs serve as critical regulators of the host immune system, and given their active involvement in disease progression, circulating EV-miRNAs represent potential biomarkers for TB[36, 37]. To identify diagnostic EV-miRNAs, we first established a pilot cohort (4 TB patients and 4 HCs), isolating plasma-derived EVs from both groups. Successful EV extraction was confirmed by electron microscopy for morphology, Western blot detection of markers (CD9, CD63, and TSG101), and nanoparticle tracking analysis for size distribution (Fig. 2A-C). Small RNA sequencing of EVs from the pilot cohort revealed distinct separation between TB and HC groups via principal component analysis, indicating clear intergroup differences (Fig. 2D and S1A). Among the 2,456 differentially expressed miRNAs detected, 878 were exclusive to the TB group, 517 to the HC group, and 1,061 were shared (Fig. S1B). Furthermore, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of target genes of the significantly differentially expressed miRNAs revealed inflammatory signaling pathways associated with “protein phosphorylation”, “FoxO signaling pathway” and “MAPK signaling pathway” (Fig. S1C and S1D). Given that M. tuberculosis infection triggers host inflammatory responses, these findings suggest that plasma EVs carry characteristic miRNAs related to TB infection, highlighting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers for TB. However, these miRNAs are not Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb)-specific but rather reflect host-derived responses to infection. We next validated 14 candidate miRNAs using qPCR in EVs isolated from the discovery cohort (10 TB patients and 10 HCs) (Fig. 2D). Multiple testing correction was applied to further screen candidate miRNAs, yielding a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of 0.05/14 ≈ 0.0036. The relative expression levels of these miRNAs are presented as heatmap (Fig. 2F) and box plot (Fig. S2). To evaluate the diagnostic potential of these EV-miRNAs for tuberculosis, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and visualized the intergroup differences (P-values) along with their corresponding AUC values in a scatter plot (Fig. 2G and H). Notably, EV-miR-107 demonstrated a consistent differential expression pattern between healthy controls and TB patients that aligned with the sequencing data, showing statistical significance (p < 0.0036) with an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.723 ~ 1.000). To assess diagnostic specificity, plasma EV-miR-107 was quantified in ten patients with non-tuberculous respiratory infections (RI). Levels in this disease control group were statistically indistinguishable from healthy controls (p = 0.207) yet significantly distinct from the TB group (p = 0.023), underscoring the specificity of EV-miR-107 as a host-responsive biomarker for tuberculosis (Fig. S3). Our previous work demonstrated that miR-107 plays a role in TB treatment, further supporting its involvement in host immune responses during TB infection[38]. Additionally, interrogation of the EVAtlas database revealed that EV-derived miR-107 is highly expressed in both lymph and plasma (Fig. S4), reinforcing its potential functional relevance in TB progression and its suitability as a host-response biomarker for TB diagnosis.

To further investigate the association between EV-miR-107 and tuberculosis progression, we established a murine infection model via intranasal BCG inoculation (Fig. 2D and I and fig. S5). At 2 weeks post-infection, BCG-infected mice exhibited a 5.9-fold increase in plasma EV-miR-107 levels compared to controls (p = 0.001), demonstrating its utility in detecting mycobacterial infection (Fig. 2J). Surprisingly, we observed a concurrent 5.8-fold elevation of EV-miR-107 in lung-infiltrating lymphocytes, suggesting that the plasma EV-miR-107 likely originates from pulmonary lymphocytes and enters circulation through blood-tissue exchange at infection sites (Fig. 2K). These findings indicate that plasma EV-miR-107, as a host-response biomarker, may reflect TB infection status and possess significant potential for monitoring disease progression.

Design and validation of CHA probes for miR-107 detection

We designed a catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) system for miR-107 (23 nt) detection. Based on sequence analysis and strand displacement circuit principles, two hairpin probes—H1 (45 nt) and H2 (43 nt)—were designed, and their secondary structures were predicted using sg.idtdna.com (Table S1 and Fig. S6). Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed miR-107-triggered CHA amplification, with target-dependent H1/H2 complex formation (Figure S7A). Specificity tests showed that only miR-107 triggered strong fluorescence, whereas single-, double-, and triple-mismatched targets or other miRNAs failed to activate the CHA reaction, confirming high probe specificity (Fig. S7B). Subsequent fluorescence kinetic analysis of the detection system revealed a time-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity, with the signal amplification rate in the CHA system being significantly higher for high-concentration targets compared to low-concentration targets (Fig. S7C). Upon increasing the target concentration from 1 pM to 500 nM, the fluorescence intensity rose progressively from 308 to 7740, reaching a plateau at approximately 200 nM. Below this threshold, the CHA assay displayed a concentration-dependent response. (Fig. S7D). The log-linear range spanned 1 pM ∼ 2.5 nM (I = 39.097 lgC + 409.74, R² = 0.996), and the limit of detection (LOD) was 2.63 pM (Fig. S7E). Finally, stability tests revealed low variability: intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) were 2.36% and 4.17% (1 nM target), and 3.15% and 4.49% (10 nM target), respectively (all CVs < 5%), indicating robust reproducibility (Fig. S7F and fig. S8A and B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that our rationally designed CHA probes provide a robust and feasible platform for sensitive miR-107 detection.

Preparation of liposomes and validation of EV membrane fusion

We first prepared cationic liposomes encapsulating CHA probes (CHA@Lipo nanosensor, CLiN), followed by CD63 aptamer conjugation on the liposomal membrane (CHA@Lipo@Aptamer, CLiAN). The positively charged liposomes and negatively charged EVs facilitate mutual attraction and subsequent membrane fusion through electrostatic interactions. Additionally, the CD63 aptamer modification enhances fusion efficiency by providing specific targeting capability toward EVs (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 3A and S9, transmission electron microscopy confirmed the spherical morphology of the prepared liposomes, with diameters of 50–80 nm. Nanoflow cytometry analysis of five batches of Lipo, CLiN, and CLiAN revealed a gradual increase in particle size consistent with theoretical principles and good batch-to-batch reproducibility (Fig. 3B and fig. S10A-C). In addition, particle size monitoring over 28 days at 4 °C showed that all formulations remained stable, with negligible size variation over time. (Fig. S11). Zeta potential measurements demonstrated progressively decreasing surface charges due to the incorporation of negatively charged nucleic acids, further validating successful assembly (Fig. 3C). Notably, EVs isolated from THP-1 cells, healthy controls (HC), and TB plasma were all found to carry negative surface charges (Fig. S12). To evaluate encapsulation efficiency, we employed a fluorescence-based assay. Nanoflow cytometry showed that 86.7% of liposomes successfully encapsulated FAMt sequences (Fig. 3D), while cholesterol-mediated surface conjugation of sequence achieved 95.2% labeling efficiency (Fig. 3E). Notably, when DNase I was applied to free FTQ probes (dual-labeled with FAM and BHQ), fluorescence intensity increased with enzyme concentration due to probe cleavage (Fig. 3F). In contrast, FTQ probes encapsulated within liposomes exhibited no significant fluorescence increase upon DNase I treatment, matching negative control levels (Fig. 3G). This confirmed effective nucleic acid encapsulation and stable aptamer surface modification, demonstrating successful preparation of CLiAN.

To demonstrate membrane fusion between liposomes and EVs, we first co-incubated 1 × 108 liposomes with 1 × 108 EVs at 37 °C for 0.5, 1, and 1.5 h, respectively. Electron microscopy revealed progressive membrane proximity and fusion events over time (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, FAMt-encapsulated liposomes (FAMt@Lipo) and CD63 aptamer-functionalized counterparts (FAMt@Lipo@Aptamer), both exhibiting intrinsic FAM fluorescence, were then incubated with DiD-labeled EVs for 2 h at 37 °C, demonstrating distinct red-green fluorescence colocalization by confocal microscopy (Fig. 4B and fig. S13). Furthermore, a Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based assay was established to quantify the fusion efficiency. EVs were labeled with equimolar amounts of Dil (donor) and DiD (acceptor) at a particle concentration of 1 × 10⁸. Under excitation at 520 nm, the FRET pair yielded predominant fluorescence emission at 665 nm due to efficient energy transfer. After adding either 1 × 108 or 1 × 109 liposomes and incubating at 37 °C for 2 h, under the same excitation wavelength, the fluorescence signal gradually shifted to 585 nm in a liposome concentration-dependent manner. This shift was likely caused by membrane fusion between liposomes and EVs, which increased the distance between Dil and DiD molecules, thereby abolishing the FRET effect. Based on the fluorescence intensity changes at 665 nm before and after fusion, the fusion efficiency was calculated to be 83.5% using the formula (Fₜ−F₀)/Fₜ. (Fig. 4C and D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that liposomes and EVs can efficiently undergo membrane fusion at 37 °C for 2 h.

In situ detection of EV-miR-107 using CLiAN system

We first incubated CLiN and CLiAN separately with DiD-labeled EVs at 37 °C for 2 hours. CLSM imaging revealed clear green fluorescence colocalization with red fluorescent EVs, confirming both lipid-EV membrane fusion and subsequent CHA reaction. Notably, CLiAN demonstrated slightly stronger colocalization signals than CLiN, suggesting the CD63 aptamer modification enhanced targeting efficiency. (Fig. 5A and D). Subsequently, EV isolated from BCG-infected THP-1 cells were incubated with either CLiN or CLiAN, with nanoflow cytometry analysis revealing a concentration-dependent increase in positive particle percentages. (Fig. 5B and E and S14). The CLiN system showed a linear response (I/I0 = 0.134 lgN + 0.856, R² = 0.994) with a detection limit of 6642 particles/µL, while the CLiAN exhibited superior sensitivity (I/I0 = 0.531 lgN + 0.562, R² = 0.995) with a lower detection limit of 85 particles/µL (Fig. 5C and F). A comparative evaluation of CLiN and CLiAN for detecting varying concentrations of identical EVs revealed that CLiAN yielded higher I/I₀ values and a wider detection range, demonstrating its enhanced sensitivity relative to CLiN (Fig. S15). We further evaluated the detection stability of CLiAN at 1 × 104 and 1 × 106 particles/µL. The intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) were measured at 2.72% and 2.12%, while the inter-assay CVs were 4.79% and 4.8% for 1 × 106 and 1 × 108 EVs, respectively (Fig. 5F and S16). All CVs remained below 5%, demonstrating excellent detection stability of the CLiAN system. Importantly, comparative studies with qRT-PCR using both BCG-infected and uninfected THP-1 and A549 cells revealed consistent detection patterns between methods (R2 = 0.98), suggesting the potential of this novel approach to serve as an alternative to RT-qPCR with promising clinical applications (Fig. 5H and I).

Non-invasive tuberculosis diagnosis using CLiAN platform

To evaluate the applicability of CLiAN for tuberculosis diagnosis via detection of plasma EV-miR-107, we enrolled 26 subjects (13 healthy controls and 13 TB patients) as Test Set 1 (Fig. 1 and Table S2). After isolating plasma EVs and measuring their concentration and particle size, the EV samples were incubated with CLiAN at a fixed input of 1 × 106 EVs. As shown in Figure S17A ~ C, the number of EV-miR-107 positive particles differed significantly between TB and HC (p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis yielded an AUC of 0.91 for diagnostic performance (95% CI: 0.800 ~ 1.000). The optimal cut-off value was determined to be 35.65% based on the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Additionally, plasma EV concentration showed a significant difference between the TB and HC groups (p = 0.011), with an AUC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.585 ~ 0.954), whereas EV particle size exhibited no significant intergroup difference, yielding an AUC of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.457 ~ 0.881). (Fig. S17 B, D and E).

Plasma EV-miR-107 improves TB management

We established test set 2 comprising 77 participants, including 33 Healthy controls, 26 confirmed tuberculosis (TB) patients (21 receiving 0∼12 months and 5 receiving 13∼24 months of anti-TB treatment), 10 nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) patients (6 receiving 0∼18 months and 4 receiving > 18 months of anti-NTM therapy), and 8 bacterial pneumonia (BP) cases (Table S3). Plasma samples were collected from all participants for EV isolation, with subsequent quantification of EV concentration and particle size distribution. EV-miR-107-positive particles were then detected and enumerated using the CLiAN platform under operator-blinded conditions (Fig. 6A and S18A).

Among 21 TB patients treated for 0–12 months, CLiAN-based EV-miR-107 detection achieved a sensitivity of 95.2% (20/21), significantly outperforming sputum-based methods (culture: 36.4% [4/11]; acid-fast staining: 46.2% [6/13]; Xpert: 54.5% [6/11]) and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) assays (culture: 60% [6/10]; acid-fast staining: 30% [3/10]; Xpert: 72.7% [8/11]) (Fig. 6A and Table 1). Concurrently, EV-miR-107 detection, bacterial culture, and Xpert identified 20, 10, and 15 positive cases, respectively. Notably, five cases were exclusively positive by EV-miR-107, whereas no cases were detected solely by either bacterial culture or Xpert alone (Fig. 6B). These findings collectively indicate that EV-miR-107 exhibits higher sensitivity for TB detection. In the test set 2 cohort, the performance of EV‑miR‑107 detection was evaluated by bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations. The AUC for distinguishing TB from the combined NTM, BP, and HC groups was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.748 ~ 0.947, p < 0.001). The AUCs for TB versus NTM, TB versus BP, and TB versus HC were 0.77 (95% CI: 0.567 ~ 0.919, p = 0.015), 0.79 (95% CI: 0.589 ~ 0.923, p = 0.019), and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.769 ~ 0.974, p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 6C and fig. S18B). Within the TB cohort, five patients were subclassified as treatment-naive TB (TN-TB). The assay distinguished TN-TB from the pooled non-TB controls (NTM, BP, and HC) with an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.863 ~ 0.992, p < 0.001), whereas discrimination against NTM alone yielded an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.500 ~ 0.960, p = 0.051). Furthermore, TN-TB was effectively differentiated from the combined HC and BP group who required differential diagnosis, achieving an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.934 ~ 1.000, p < 0.001) (Fig. S18F). Additionally, we explored the correlation between the number of EV‑miR‑107‑positive particles and the cycle threshold (Ct) values of the five probes in the Xpert assay. No significant correlations were found (Probe A: R² = 0.071, p = 0.257; Probe B: R² = 0.090, p = 0.213; Probe C: R² = 0.090, p = 0.198; Probe D: R² = 0.043, p = 0.397; Probe E: R² = 0.030, p = 0.503), indicating that this platform provides a diagnostic approach independent of the Xpert assay (Fig. 6D-H). Disscusion

The CLiAN platform employs cationic liposomes to encapsulate catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) probes, functionalized with EV-targeting CD63 aptamers on their surface. The cationic charge of liposomes enhances membrane fusion efficiency through electrostatic attraction with negatively charged EV membranes and aptamer-mediated specific targeting[33, 39]. This strategy integrates CHA probes within liposomes, where the CHA reaction’s ability to displace targets via H2 hairpin recycling confers superior sensitivity compared to molecular beacons. Based on collision theory, the reaction volume (V) is calculated as V = 1/cNA (where c represents probe concentration at 10 nM and NA is Avogadro’s number), yielding V = 1.7 × 10− 16 L. With measured average diameters of 66.29 nm for plasma EVs and 71.28 nm for CLiAN particles, assuming volume conservation upon fusion, the resultant fused vesicle volume (V = 4/3πr³) approximates 3.4 × 10− 19 L. This confinement elevates intravesicular CHA concentration to 4.9 µM - a 490-fold increase over bulk assay conditions - thereby enhancing molecular collision frequency and theoretically improving both reaction efficiency and detection sensitivity (Fig. S19). The platform’s modular design permits broad applicability: simply designing specific CHA probes and employing distinct fluorophores enables simultaneous detection of multiple miRNA targets. As summarized in Table S4, our in situ EV‑miRNA detection platform does not offer the shortest assay time or the lowest LOD, but it provides practical advantages over faster or more sensitive methods. Its substantially simplified detection probes make it more cost‑effective and operationally streamlined, while achieving turnaround time and LOD comparable to most reported platforms. Notably, previous efforts to develop EV‑miRNA detection methods have largely relied on pre‑validated biomarkers, and EV‑miRNA‑based tuberculosis diagnosis has been mainly confined to PCR‑based approaches. To our knowledge, this study is the first to establish a novel analytical framework using host‑responsive EV‑miRNA indicators for tuberculosis diagnosis and treatment monitoring.

Through pilot and discovery cohort studies, we identified significantly differential expression of EV-miR-107 between TB-infected and healthy groups. To elucidate its association with tuberculosis and origin of elevated EV-miR-107 in TB patients’ plasma, we established a BCG infection murine model via intranasal inoculation. Infected mice demonstrated elevated EV-miR-107 levels in both plasma (vs. controls) and lung lymphocyte-derived EVs, suggesting these lymphocyte-derived EVs likely reflect host immune responses to mycobacterial stimulation. Consequently, EV-miR-107 serves as a reliable host-response indicator for TB infection. During treatment, dynamic bacterial loads and corresponding immune status alterations further modulate EV-miR-107 expression, highlighting its potential for therapeutic monitoring. Notably, Xu’s study found no significant miR-107 expression difference in PBMCs between TB patients and controls, implying peripheral blood cells may not be the primary source[38]. Instead, pulmonary lymphocytes stimulated by Mtb infection likely generate miR-107-enriched EVs that subsequently enter circulation through transcytosis or membrane fusion-mediated tissue-blood exchange[40–42]. In this study, EVs were isolated from approximately 900 µL of plasma via ultracentrifugation and resuspended in 100 µL PBS, yielding a concentration of (5.6 ± 2.3) × 109 particles/mL. Based on the CLiAN platform’s requirement of 1 × 106 EVs per assay, this corresponds to approximately 161 µL of plasma per test. These findings collectively suggest that lymphocyte-derived EVs from the infection microenvironment may serve as effective indicators of tuberculosis infection status, with the added advantage that only a small amount of plasma volumes is required to capture lung lymphocyte-derived EV signatures.

Bacterial culture remains the gold standard for pulmonary tuberculosis diagnosis, yet its sensitivity is suboptimal (58.8%, 10/17). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) demonstrates higher sensitivity (60.0%; 6/10) than sputum samples (36.4%; 4/11). Xpert testing exhibited superior sensitivity (71.4%, 15/21) compared to bacterial culture, particularly in BALF (72.7%, 8/11) versus sputum (54.5%, 6/11). While sputum collection is noninvasive and readily accessible, its diagnostic yield heavily depends on sample quality. Conversely, BALF, though more sensitive, involves invasive procedures with poorer patient compliance and potential risks. In contrast, plasma represents a clinically accessible sample with minimal risk. Plasma EV-miR-107 exhibited outstanding diagnostic performance for tuberculosis (AUC = 0.87, sensitivity = 95.2% [20/21]), effectively distinguishing treatment-naïve TB patients from both healthy controls and bacterial pneumonia cases (AUC = 0.98). This biomarker provides critical early-warning capability prior to bacterial culture results availability while simultaneously facilitating differential diagnosis from bacterial pneumonia. The single negative case (TP8, 34-year-old male) showed only 27.9% residual EV-miR-107 expression at month 11 of treatment, potentially reflecting early therapeutic response within the standard 8∼12 months regimen. These findings establish plasma EV-miR-107 as a promising auxiliary diagnostic biomarker for TB.

Among 26 confirmed TB patients, plasma EV-miR-107 levels decreased significantly post-treatment initiation (p = 0.001). All 21 patients treated for 0∼12 months and 5 patients treated for 13∼24 months showed negative EV-miR-107 results. For instance, one patient at month 15 (33.6%) had negative sputum culture and acid-fast staining with clinical/radiographic improvement, prompting treatment cessation. Another at month 20 (29.1%) had consecutive negative sputum smears, further supporting the correlation between declining EV-miR-107 signals and therapeutic response. In 10 NTM patients, EV-miR-107 showed a decreasing trend (p = 0.219), with all 4 patients treated > 18 months testing negative. Of 6 patients treated 0–18 months, 2 were negative (17.5%, 18.5%)—one with prior TB treatment 30 years earlier, another with antifungal therapy 2 years prior, suggesting potential attenuated EV-miR-107 production upon NTM reinfection. Collectively, plasma EV-miR-107 shows potential as a clinical monitoring tool for TB treatment response, enabling precise therapeutic duration management to alleviate patient burden and reduce global economic impacts. Systematic evaluation of the CLiAN platform’s detection capability through targeted studies is warranted to validate its clinical utility in addressing this unmet need.

This study has several limitations. First, the CLiAN platform requires EV extraction and quantification prior to analysis, which involves relatively complex preparatory steps. Given that EV concentrations from equal plasma volumes show minimal variation, optimization strategies such as microfluidic pore-based processing or plasma dilution could streamline the EV isolation and quantification workflow. Furthermore, the pilot cohort sequencing (TB vs. HC) may not adequately distinguish diseases with overlapping pathophysiology. Ideally, differential diagnosis groups such as NTM and BP should be included. Given the intrinsic limitations of host-response biomarkers, miR-107 may also be altered in other infectious or autoimmune conditions[43, 44]. The current data (Fig. S3) are based on a limited sample size, and further validation in larger, more diverse cohorts is needed. Besides, a single miRNA is insufficiently robust for TB diagnosis. Mining additional miRNAs from the sequencing data, or integrating miR‑107 with pathogen‑specific markers into a biomarker panel, would enhance differential diagnostic accuracy. Finally, although our cohort data revealed a correlation between decreasing plasma EV-miR-107 levels and treatment duration in both TB and NTM patients, consistent with clinical symptom improvement, the lack of longitudinal individual plasma samples restricted our ability to perform detailed therapeutic response evaluations. This limitation underscores the need for systematic sample collection throughout treatment courses in future investigations.

Despite these limitations, the CLiAN platform demonstrates notable advantages: it preserves EV integrity by eliminating lysis and RNA extraction steps, requires minimal plasma volume for single-EV miRNA analysis, and features a simple reagent system with streamlined protocols (2-hour processing time). Through sequencing-based biomarker screening and clinical validation, we identified plasma EV-miR-107 as a promising TB diagnostic marker, showing high sensitivity and accuracy. The platform also detected treatment-responsive EV-miR-107 reduction patterns aligning with clinical improvement in both TB and NTM cases. These findings collectively suggest that CLiAN-based plasma EV-miR-107 detection holds significant potential for optimizing clinical TB management strategies.
