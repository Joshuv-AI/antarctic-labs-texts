High prevalence of antibiotic purchase without prescription in community pharmacies in Bucaramanga, Colombia
Authors: Juan Camilo Caamaño-López, Rafael José Torres-Chaverra, Aylem Nathalia Fonseca-Meneses, Eliat Jabit Pérez-Cujia, Valentina Domínguez-Quintero, Heyleen Yuliana Ortiz-Centeno, Luisa Fernanda Jiménez-Vásquez, Andrés Felipe Trujillo-Mejía, Cristina Isabel Amado-Cabrales, Linda Vanessa Osorio-Cardozo, Jhancy Rocio Aguilar-Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Non-prescription antibiotic purchases are widespread in Bucaramanga communities.•First-line antibiotics like amoxicillin and azithromycin are most commonly bought.•Weak regulation and self-medication highlight the need for multisectoral action.Keywords: Drug resistance, Antibiotics, Epidemiology, Colombia, Community pharmacy

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than half of all medicines worldwide are prescribed, sold, or purchased inappropriately [1]. High levels of inappropriate antibiotic consumption have been strongly associated with the emergence of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms [2,3]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become a critical and growing global public health threat, with the potential to evolve into a pandemic if prioritized, coordinated, and sustained interventions are not implemented [4,5]. Its consequences include increased mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and a significant economic burden on health care systems [[6], [7], [8]]. This burden is expected to disproportionately affect low- and middle-income countries, where increased mortality associated with resistant infections has already been documented [9]. It is estimated that by 2050, AMR could lead to a substantial increase in mortality rates and significant global economic losses [10].

In response, strategies have been proposed at both the community and hospital levels, including antimicrobial stewardship programs, a preference for narrow-spectrum therapies, and optimization of the selection and duration of empirical treatment [11]. The WHO launched a campaign urging countries to promote the rational use of antibiotics and integrate these actions into pharmaceutical policies, emphasizing the importance of strengthening regulatory frameworks, improving infection prevention and control measures, and monitoring antibiotic consumption [12].

Among the key global initiatives is the WHO Global Action Plan on AMR, introduced in 2015 and subsequently translated into National Action Plans in multiple countries, including Colombia [13]. Additionally, in 2018, the WHO expanded its Essential Medicines List to classify antibiotics according to their resistance potential through the AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) framework, thereby promoting appropriate antibiotic use and supporting stewardship strategies [14,15]. This classification is particularly relevant for low- and middle-income countries, because it aims to reduce the use of Watch and Reserve antibiotics while promoting increased use of Access antibiotics [16]. More recently, the WHO AWaRe antibiotic book (2022-2023) has provided practical guidance for the management of common infectious diseases, particularly in ambulatory care settings. This is especially important given concerns about the limited robustness of some national antibiotic guidelines in low- and middle-income countries [[17], [18], [19]].

Despite these efforts, their impact remains limited; antibiotic self-medication continues to be widespread, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. In regions such as sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and north Africa, the prevalence of antibiotic self-medication among adults has been reported to range between approximately 43% and 55.7%, with an increasing trend over time [20,21]. Contributing factors include easy access to antibiotics without prescription, weak regulatory frameworks, limited access to health care services, individual perceptions of mild illness, and prior experience with antibiotic use [21]. Additionally, in low- and middle-income countries, where deficiencies in information on antimicrobial resistance persist, there is a greater propensity for indiscriminate purchase of antibiotics.

Antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) have also been implemented in community pharmacies to reduce the inappropriate dispensing of antibiotics without prescription [22,23]. Although such interventions have been successful in some contexts such as in Saudi Arabia, where indiscriminate antibiotic sales were effectively controlled [24], challenges remain in other settings; for example, in Vietnam, where the stringency of these measures has posed implementation difficulties [25].

In Colombia, the National Antimicrobial Stewardship Plan established guidelines for prescription-based antibiotic dispensing and strategies to address AMR [13]. The Colombian General System of Social Security in Health (SGSSS, for its Spanish acronym) provides health insurance coverage to 99% of the population. Both the contributory and subsidized health insurance schemes include antibiotic dispensing under the Health Benefits Plan (PBS, for its Spanish acronym) [26]. However, there are limited studies evaluating the implementation and impact of policies that restrict over-the-counter antibiotics sales in the country. Wirtz et al. [27] (2013) found that interventions in Colombia were largely limited to media campaigns, with regulatory enforcement mainly concentrated in Bogotá (Capital District) [22,27]. In this city, up to 80% of sampled pharmacies were reported to dispense antibiotics without a prescription [28]. More recent studies in Medellín (Colombia) indicate that 65% of pharmacy personnel reported dispensing antibiotics without a prescription, often because of barriers in access to health care services [29].

Therefore, although regulatory frameworks exist, their enforcement remains limited [13]. The objective of this study was to describe the nonprescription purchase of antibiotics in community pharmacies in neighborhoods of Bucaramanga and identify the factors associated with their acquisition. These results will be essential for proposing actions to prevent AMR.

Methods

A cross-sectional observational analytical study was conducted involving 428 customers of community pharmacies in Bucaramanga, Colombia. Eligible participants were individuals aged 18 years or older who were customers of pharmacies that dispense antibiotics, agreed to participate after providing informed consent, were able to maintain active and adequate communication with the interviewer, and purchased medications for personal use and not through delivery services. Participants were recruited through direct contact at the exit of community pharmacies. Trained members of the research team randomly approached individuals who had just purchased medications and asked whether they had bought an antibiotic. Those who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate and completed a self-reported survey after providing informed consent. Individuals who did not complete the survey in full were excluded from the study.

A total sample of 428 participants was estimated. The sample size was calculated based on an expected proportion of 57.4% [12], with a 95% confidence level and an absolute precision of 5%. A nonprobability convenience sampling method was used. The 10 surveyed neighborhoods are located in seven of the 17 districts of Bucaramanga, Colombia: Cabecera, Real de Minas, Mutis, Conucos, Provenza, Terrazas, Antonia Santos, Sotomayor, Colorados, and El Tejar.

The dependent variable was the purchase of antibiotics with or without a prescription at the previously selected pharmacies. Independent variables included sociodemographic factors (sex, age, socioeconomic status, and educational level), characteristics of the final antibiotic recipient (health insurance scheme, occupation, source that recommended the antibiotic, signs and symptoms prompting antibiotic purchase, and perceived health status), and details of the antibiotic purchased (drug name and antibiotic class). The name of the antibiotic purchased was obtained through direct verification. During the survey, participants were asked to show the interviewer the medication they had acquired, allowing confirmation of the drug name and its dosage form. Antibiotics were classified according to the WHO AWaRe system into three groups based on their resistance potential, spectrum of activity, and therapeutic value: Access, which includes commonly used antibiotics with minimal restrictions; Watch, which comprises higher-risk antibiotics requiring specific prescribing guidance and reassessment at 48-72 hours with support from the antimicrobial stewardship program (ASP) team; and Reserve, which includes last-line antibiotics with restricted use under ASP oversight [16].

Data were collected using a validated printed survey derived from the study “Frequency and characteristics of the purchase of antibiotics without a prescription in pharmacies in Barranquilla and Soledad in the second half of 2017 and first half of 2018” [12]. For survey administration, pairs of research team members were formed; they independently visited the pharmacies and followed a standardized protocol for approaching participants. Data were collected on different days of the week, with an average of 2 hours per day dedicated to survey administration. Each interviewer digitized the data collected in a form designed in Google Forms, and the resulting Excel database was exported to Stata 18.0 statistical software. For statistical analysis, qualitative variables were summarized as proportions, while quantitative variables were summarized using measures of central tendency and dispersion. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of quantitative variables. When normal distribution was not observed, variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges. Bivariate analysis was conducted using the χ² test, t test, and Mann-Whitney U test.

From an ethical perspective, this research adheres to the principles outlined in the Ethics Committee guidelines of the Universidad de Santander and Resolution 8430 of 1993 [30], which sets forth standards for health research in Colombia. The study respected participants’ dignity and well-being, ensured confidentiality, and was classified as risk-free, because it did not involve biological, physiological, psychological, or social interventions. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Additionally, Resolution 1581 of 2012 was observed to guarantee the protection of personal data. The research project was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Universidad de Santander (Minutes No. 10, June 14, 2024).

Results

A total of 428 surveys were conducted in 10 neighborhoods in Bucaramanga: Provenza (n = 156; 36.4%), Cabecera (n = 58; 13.5%), Colorados (n = 45; 10.5%), Mutis (n = 40; 9.3%), Terrazas (n = 34; 7.9%), Real de Minas (n = 32; 7.5%), Conucos (n = 27; 6.3%), Antonia Santos (n = 14; 3.3%), El Tejar (n = 12; 2.8%), and Sotomayor (n = 10; 2.3%). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 84 years, with 50% aged 35 years or older. Women represented 53.3% of respondents. Most participants resided in Provenza (36.4%), had a middle socioeconomic status (70.8%), and reported having higher education (64.3%) (Table 1).

The age of antibiotic users ranged from 2 to 89 years, with 50% being older than 32 years. More than half were workers, and more than half among them were reported to have an average health status as perceived by the buyer of the antibiotic. The most frequently purchased antibiotic group was penicillins (44%), with amoxicillin being the most common agent. More than half of consumers were enrolled in the contributory health insurance scheme. The main reason for antibiotic purchase was sore throat or tonsillitis (27.8%) (Table 2).

Overall, 90.7% (n = 388) of users purchased antibiotics without a prescription, and 50.5% reported routinely taking antibiotics without a prescription (Figure 1). When analyzing the groups of antibiotics most frequently purchased by participants (Table 3), the three predominant antibiotic classes were penicillins, cephalosporins, and macrolides. Within the penicillin group, amoxicillin was the most frequently purchased antibiotic (n = 155). Of these purchases, 94.2% were made without a prescription, indicating a high prevalence of self-medication. This is noteworthy because amoxicillin is a first-line agent for treating a broad range of bacterial infections, and its misuse increases the risk of inappropriate therapy and AMR. In the cephalosporin group, cephalexin was the most frequently purchased (n = 33), of which 84.8% were made without a prescription. This pattern suggests a concerning trend of unsupervised broad-spectrum antibiotic use, which may contribute to the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria. Finally, among macrolides, azithromycin was the most frequently purchased agent (n = 89), and 87.6% of these purchases were made without showing a prescription at the time of purchase.

According to the WHO Access, Watch, and Reserve (AWaRe) classification, there are three categories of antibiotics: Access, Watch, and Reserve antibiotics. Most purchases recorded in this study correspond to Access antibiotics (n = 287; 67%), with amoxicillin being the predominant antibiotic. The Watch group of antibiotics accounted for 139 purchases (32.5%), primarily azithromycin, whereas the Reserve group of antibiotics accounted for two purchases (0.5%), both for meropenem, the only antibiotic sold in this category [16,31].

Among participants who purchased antibiotics without a prescription, 54.6% (n = 212) reported regular self-medication, while only 10% (n = 4) of those who obtained antibiotics with a prescription reported regular self-medication (P < 0.001). No statistically significant associations were found between the purchase of antibiotics without a prescription and variables related to the purchaser (age [P = 0.41], sex [P = 0.66], socioeconomic status [P = 0.08], or educational level [P = 0.62]), nor with variables related to the final recipient of the antibiotic (age [P = 0.17], occupation [P = 0.71], perceived health status [P = 0.35], or health insurance scheme [P = 0.08]).

Discussion

The results showed that most respondents purchased antibiotics without a prescription. This practice represents a significant public health concern and aligns with the WHO warning that “the misuse and overuse of antimicrobials in humans, animals and plants are the main drivers in the development of drug-resistant pathogens.” [32]

Most individuals who purchased antibiotics did so for themselves, suggesting an established pattern of self-medication. Furthermore, half of the respondents reported engaging in this practice regularly, indicating that self-medication has become normalized among a substantial proportion of the population.

Self-medication was primarily motivated by the perception that medical consultation was unnecessary and by a lack of time to seek care. These reasons reflect structural barriers to health care access and an underestimation of the risk. One of the most underestimated drivers of AMR is the lack of effective dispensing control policies at the point of sale, especially in middle-income countries [6].

Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, participants were predominantly from the middle socioeconomic status, and those with an undergraduate education were the most represented group. This finding indicates that inappropriate antibiotic use is not limited to populations with lower educational attainment or socioeconomic status. Similar findings were reported by Carrera-Lasfuentes et al. [33] (2013), who observed a significant increase in antibiotic self-medication as educational level increased, reaching 16.5% among individuals with university education.

A considerable proportion of respondents also stated that they did not know what antibiotics are used for, highlighting deficiencies in health education. Suarez López [34] (2019) reported that many individuals self-medicate because of insufficient knowledge and information about the appropriate use of medications. This issue is compounded by the fact that the main sources of antibiotic recommendations were pharmacists and family members. As Suarez López [34] (2019) also noted, “residents in the municipality of Pasto often receive self-medication advice from neighbors, relatives, and the media—sources that are not considered reliable.”

Regarding the types of antibiotics used, amoxicillin was identified among 36.2% of respondents, followed by azithromycin (20.8%). According to the WHO AWaRe classification, most antibiotics purchased were in the “Access” group, followed by the “Watch” group with minimal use of the “Reserve” group. These findings reveal a concerning pattern of self-medication and misuse of antibiotics among the population [16]. Comparable results have been reported in similar studies. For instance, a study conducted in Medellín by Arboleda Forero et al. [35] (2023) found that “the most commonly self-medicated antibiotic was amoxicillin, followed by azithromycin and cephalexin.” This pattern of antibiotic consumption is also consistent with global evidence. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in sub-Saharan Africa found that amoxicillin accounted for 26.5% of antibiotics dispensed without a prescription, followed by co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) (19.8%) and tetracycline (9.7%) [36]. These findings suggest a widespread trend in low- and middle-income countries, where broad-spectrum, low-cost, and easily accessible antibiotics are commonly used for the empirical treatment of infections, many of which are likely viral in origin.

It is particularly concerning that a large proportion of respondents reported using antibiotics to treat sore throats, colds, and coughs, which are predominantly viral in etiology. This finding aligns with the study by Arboleda Forero et al. [35] (2023), which reported that “the main reason for antibiotic use was the treatment of common cold symptoms.” Similar results were observed in a study conducted in Barranquilla and Soledad (Atlántico, Colombia), where it was observed that the primary symptoms motivating the population to purchase antibiotics without a prescription were sore throat, cold symptoms, and cough [12]. This behavior is particularly concerning in light of the increasing global burden of antibiotic resistance, a major public health threat of the 21st century, with projected mortality exceeding 10 million deaths annually by 2050 in the absence of effective interventions [37].

In addition to the factors identified in this study, a systematic review by Belachew et al. [36] (2021) conducted in low- and middle-income countries found that the sale of antibiotics without a prescription is largely driven by structural and systemic determinants that transcend individual behavior. Among these structural factors are the commercial orientation of pharmacies, where profit motives often outweigh professional and ethical principles; customer pressure, driven by the demand for quick symptomatic relief; weak or absent regulatory enforcement; and insufficient knowledge or inappropriate dispensing practices among pharmacy staff regarding the rational use of antibiotics and their role in health care. These findings are consistent with our observations in Bucaramanga, where many respondents who purchased antibiotics without a prescription reported having received direct recommendations from pharmacists, and where the perception that consulting a physician was unnecessary was a common justification for self-medication. Taken together, this evidence underscores the urgent need to strengthen both regulatory frameworks and educational strategies aimed at pharmacy personnel and the general population. In addition, Belachew et al. [36] suggest that effective interventions should combine continuous professional training on responsible dispensing practices with public awareness campaigns promoting understanding of the risks associated with inappropriate antibiotic use.

These findings highlight the need for a comprehensive, multisectoral approach to address the persistent dispensing of antibiotics without prescription in Colombia, involving all key stakeholders. Although strengthening regulatory enforcement and antimicrobial stewardship interventions in community pharmacies is essential, the central role of patients must also be recognized, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where they actively influence both prescribing and dispensing practices, even for self-limiting conditions such as upper respiratory tract infections. This behavior is associated with limited knowledge of antibiotics, their appropriate use, and antimicrobial resistance, as well as structural barriers and difficulties in accessing timely health care services, which further promote self-medication [38,39]. In this context, it is necessary to combine stricter regulatory strategies and improved training of pharmacy staff with community-based educational interventions and the strengthening of health care professionals’ role in patient guidance, in order to promote the rational use of antibiotics and reduce the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance in the country.

Information bias represents a relevant limitation to the validity of these findings. This type of bias may occur when participants fail to recall the exact medication used, modify their responses because of social desirability, or confuse antibiotics with other medications because of limited pharmacological knowledge. To minimize these distortions, several methodological strategies were implemented, including the use of clear, specific survey instruments with concrete examples of antibiotics, comprehensive interviewer training to ensure standardized, unbiased data collection, and strict confidentiality protocols to encourage honest participation. Additionally, data collection focused on recent antibiotic purchases to reduce recall errors.

In conclusion, findings indicate that purchasing antibiotics without a prescription at community pharmacies in neighborhoods of Bucaramanga is widespread, particularly for first-line antibiotics such as amoxicillin and azithromycin. Most respondents reported self-medicating, frequently justifying this behavior because of lack of time, the perception that medical consultation was unnecessary, and direct recommendations from pharmacists or family members.

These results underscore not only the weak enforcement of national regulations governing antibiotic dispensing but also the urgent need for comprehensive, multisectoral interventions that integrate regulatory, educational, and health care access strategies.
