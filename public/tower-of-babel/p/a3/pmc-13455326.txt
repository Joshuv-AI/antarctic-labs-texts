Ethical challenges to patient autonomy in the era of artificial intelligence: a systematic review
Authors: Sara Mohammadnejad, Afsaneh Raiesifar, Shabnam Bazmi, Fateme Ghonodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly transformed clinical practice by improving diagnostic accuracy and enhancing decision-making processes. However, its growing role in patient care raises profound ethical concerns regarding patient autonomy. This systematic review synthesizes current evidence on how AI may support, challenge, or reshape patient autonomy in healthcare.MethodsThis review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Science Direct for studies published between January 2010 and June 2025. The initial search was performed in September 2024 and updated to June 2025 during the peer review process. Eligible studies included empirical, conceptual, or mixed-methods research that addressed AI applications in healthcare and their implications for patient autonomy. Quality assessment was performed using the STROBE, CASP, and MMAT checklists. A narrative synthesis and thematic analysis were conducted.ResultsOf 472 records screened from the initial search, 11 studies met the eligibility criteria. The updated search (October 2024 – June 2025) identified 3 additional eligible studies, bringing the final total to 14 studies. Seven overarching themes emerged: (1) philosophical and existential dimensions of autonomy, (2) human-centered and autonomy-supportive design, (3) cognitive threats and diminished agency, (4) moral risks and social inequality, (5) covert control and preference manipulation, (6) balancing human and AI authority in clinical decisions, and (7) the need for transparency, dialogue, and adaptive autonomy. Results show that AI can enhance autonomy through shared decision-making and improved information availability, but significant risks exist, including algorithmic opacity, nudging, clinician de-skilling, and increased inequality. The three 2025 studies independently confirmed all seven themes, with Alnawafleh et al. finding that patient autonomy is the predominant ethical concern (67%) in AI-integrated nursing practice.ConclusionAI introduces both opportunities and threats to patient autonomy. Healthcare systems must ensure transparent, human-centered, and ethically governed AI deployment. Autonomy should function as a guiding principle in AI design, training, and regulation. Strong policy frameworks, validated measurement tools, implementation science approaches, and clinical education are essential to ensure that AI serves as a tool for empowerment rather than a source of subtle control.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing medicine, improving the performance of physicians, and delivering quality care to patients [1]. The effectiveness of AI in healthcare delivery is expanding and could soon be used in important aspects of clinical decision-making [2]. The emergence of computer hardware and software in medicine, the increasing use of technology, and the digitization of patient records are important factors in the emergence of AI in healthcare and the clinic. These developments provide new opportunities and challenges in healthcare [3], ranging from identifying new relationships between genetic codes or controlling surgical robots to easily accessing patient medical information [4, 5].

AI ethics is a new branch of digital ethics that has emerged in response to growing concerns about the impact of AI [6]. Over the past five years, guidelines for AI ethics have been published, but further research is needed to develop a global consensus on a coherent standard for the ethics of AI [7]. Several prominent ethical frameworks have been developed to guide responsible AI deployment. The European Commission’s High-Level Expert Group on AI published “Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI” (2019), which identifies seven key requirements including human agency and oversight, transparency, and accountability [8]. The World Health Organization (WHO) released “Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence for Health” (2021), emphasizing six guiding principles: protecting human autonomy, promoting well-being, ensuring transparency, fostering accountability, ensuring equity, and promoting sustainable AI [9]. The IEEE Global Initiative on Ethics of Autonomous and Intelligent Systems proposed “Ethically Aligned Design” (2019), which prioritizes human well-being as a metric for system development [10]. Additionally, the Asilomar AI Principles (2017) and the OECD Principles on AI (2019) provide foundational guidance on safety, fairness, and accountability [11, 12]. Despite these efforts, Hagendorff (2020) found that many of these guidelines lack actionable enforcement mechanisms, particularly regarding the ethical principles of beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice [13]. Autonomy is a core moral value deeply rooted in the ethical, legal, and political practices of many societies, and the development and deployment of AI has raised new questions about the effects of AI on human autonomy [14]. Autonomy has been identified as a critical aspect of justice and well-being, playing a role in individuals’ mental health, and the effects of digital experiences on human autonomy are neither simple nor fixed [15]. The application of AI, from consent to data collection and processing to institutional, social, and other related considerations, must be examined to fully understand the impact of AI on human autonomy, which is yet to be completely understood [16]. Patients prefer the use of AI to help them make judgments and choices that are based on their own free will, but this also comes with significant challenges [17].

Among the most significant ethical challenges associated with the use of AI in healthcare is the lack of transparency of machine learning algorithms—commonly referred to as the “black box” problem. This opacity is thought to prevent patients from fully understanding the rationale behind AI-generated medical recommendations, thereby undermining their capacity to make truly autonomous and informed healthcare decisions [18–20]. The rapid rise of AI in healthcare promises a new era in medicine, but this new technology also raises substantial ethical questions, the most important of which is how to maintain patients’ autonomy [21].

Maintaining patient autonomy is essential for assuring quality of care and fostering confidence in the doctor-patient relationship; hence, the deployment of AI presents several ethical and deontological challenges [22].

Despite a growing body of literature on AI ethics, existing systematic reviews have predominantly examined AI through the lenses of privacy [23], justice and fairness [24], or algorithmic bias [25], with limited and fragmented focus on the specific and nuanced ways AI affects patient autonomy as an independent ethical concern [26, 27]. No comprehensive systematic review to date has synthesized conceptual, empirical, and ethical analyses related specifically to autonomy. This gap underscores the need for a structured appraisal of the challenges and opportunities AI presents for autonomous patient decision-making. Furthermore, while philosophical conceptualizations of autonomy provide essential foundations, translating these principles into measurable, actionable clinical improvements requires integration with patient experience frameworks, psychometric measurement tools, and implementation science methodologies. Recent qualitative evidence has demonstrated that patients’ lived experiences with AI-powered interventions significantly shape their sense of agency and decision-making capacity [28]. Moreover, systematic evaluation of measurement properties—including validity, reliability, and responsiveness—offers methodological guidance for developing instruments that assess patient autonomy in AI-mediated healthcare contexts [29]. Implementation science perspectives further highlight that autonomy-preserving principles must be embedded within clinical workflows, accounting for user acceptance, organizational barriers, and real-world constraints [30]. This review therefore aims not only to synthesize conceptual and ethical analyses but also to bridge the gap between philosophical autonomy principles and their practical operationalization in clinical settings.

This systematic review aims to: (1) synthesize current evidence on AI’s impact on patient autonomy (2), categorize ethical challenges and enabling factors (3), identify measurement and implementation gaps, and (4) provide a framework for ethical AI implementation in healthcare.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a systematic review designed to examine the ethical challenges of patient autonomy in the context of AI applications in the healthcare system. The PRISMA guidelines were used, and we adhered to a predefined protocol developed in accordance with the PRISMA-P standards. The protocol was registered on the PROSPERO website under the identification code CRD1243203, and all methodological steps were clearly documented to ensure transparency and reproducibility. The systematic review was guided by the PICO framework [31], although we acknowledge that for predominantly conceptual and ethical reviews, “alternative frameworks such as PICO (Population, Interest, Context)” or SPIDER (Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research Type) may be more appropriate [32]. The PICO framework was selected to maintain consistency with clinical systematic review standards and to facilitate comparison with other healthcare-focused reviews (Table 1).

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in the electronic databases Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Science Direct [33, 34]. The initial search covered January 1, 2010 to September 1, 2024. Due to the duration of the peer review process, the search was updated to June 15, 2025 using the same databases and identical search strategy to ensure currency. The search was designed and conducted by two medical literature review experts, supported by a research team specializing in medical ethics and systematic reviews.

The time period 2010–2025 was selected based on epidemiological considerations and the evolution of research methodology related to AI in medical sciences. Since 2010, significant advances in machine learning algorithms, the digitization of health systems, and the publication of international guidelines on AI ethics have made studies in this period more scientifically rigorous, more transparently reported, and more comparable.

Using MESH terms, expert opinion, and initial searches, the following keywords were selected: “artificial intelligence,” “autonomy,” “ethics,” “ethical principles,” “disclosure of information,” “privacy,” “health,” “health care systems,” “patients,” and “healthcare.” These were searched alone and in combination using Boolean operators (AND, OR) [35, 36] (Table 2).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: peer-reviewed empirical, conceptual, or mixed-methods studies; focus on AI applications in healthcare; substantive discussion of patient autonomy; published in English; published between 2010 and 2025.

Exclusion criteria: conference abstracts, editorials, letters to the editor, non-human studies, studies not substantively addressing patient autonomy, and studies for which full text was unavailable. Study selection

All records were imported into EndNote to remove duplicates. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two trained reviewers. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was used to collect the following information: author(s), year, country; study design and population; type of AI intervention discussed; key outcomes related to patient autonomy and ethical considerations; and main findings. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers. Thematic coding identified recurring concepts that, after validation, were organized into seven integrated themes: philosophical and existential dimensions, humanistic design, cognitive threats, ethical risks, covert control, human-AI balance, and transparency/dialogue/flexibility.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using validated tools appropriate to each study design: STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology): A 22-item checklist designed to evaluate the completeness and transparency of reporting in observational studies, including cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional designs [39].

CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme): A structured quality assessment tool widely used for evaluating qualitative and conceptual research, consisting of 10 questions that assess methodological rigor, including research design, data collection, ethical considerations, and the clarity and value of findings [40].

MMAT (Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool): A comprehensive tool designed for appraising the methodological quality of studies with diverse designs, including qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods research. It assesses criteria such as appropriateness of the research question, data collection methods, and integration of qualitative and quantitative components [41].

Studies were classified as high quality (meeting ≥ 80% of applicable criteria), moderate quality (meeting 50–79%), or low quality (meeting < 50%). Only high- and moderate-quality studies were retained in the final analysis [42]. Table 3 specifies the assessment tool applied to each study and the resulting quality classification.

Risk of bias assessment

To ensure methodological rigor and minimize the impact of systematic errors, a structured risk of bias assessment was performed for all included studies. Two independent reviewers assessed each study for major types of epidemiological bias, including selection bias, information and measurement bias, confounding bias, and selective reporting bias. Disagreements were resolved by consulting a third senior reviewer.

The quality assessment tools (STROBE, CASP, MMAT) were used as indirect indicators of potential bias by evaluating the clarity of study objectives, the adequacy of statistical analyses, the transparency of data collection procedures, and the appropriateness of sampling strategies. These criteria were used to determine whether each study had a low, moderate, or high risk of bias. The majority of included studies demonstrated acceptable methodological quality. However, some studies showed potential bias due to unclear sampling frameworks, inadequate reporting of AI algorithmic processes, or incomplete reporting of outcome measurements. These limitations were carefully considered in the synthesis and interpretation of results.

Data synthesis

Due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcomes, a narrative synthesis approach was employed. Thematic analysis, following the six-phase framework of Braun and Clarke (2006) [43], was used for qualitative and conceptual studies, encompassing: (1) familiarization with the data (2), generating initial codes (3), searching for themes (4), reviewing themes (5), defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report. Descriptive summaries were used for quantitative studies to report the effects of AI on patient autonomy [44].

Ethical considerations

This review did not involve human participants. All included studies were appropriately cited. The ethical principles of transparency, accurate reporting, and proper acknowledgment of sources were fully adhered to [45].

Results

The initial search (September 2024) identified 472 records from all databases. After removing duplicates (n = 89), 383 records were screened for title and abstract. Of these, 316 records were excluded as irrelevant to the research question. Full-text assessment was performed for 67 records, of which 56 were excluded based on eligibility criteria. This resulted in 11 studies that met the inclusion criteria from the initial search.

The updated search (October 2024 - June 2025) identified 3 additional studies that met all eligibility criteria. Therefore, a total of 14 studies were included in this systematic review. Thematic synthesis

Seven overarching themes emerged from the thematic analysis of the 14 included studies:

Philosophical and existential dimensions of autonomy

Understanding the philosophical foundations of autonomy is essential for evaluating how AI may support or undermine patients’ capacity for self-determined healthcare decisions. Formosa (2021) defined autonomy as the rationality to realize authentic values—a concept directly relevant to whether patients using AI-assisted diagnostic tools are making choices that genuinely reflect their personal values and preferences, or merely accepting algorithmic recommendations [46]. Prunkl (2024) distinguished between authenticity (the internality of values) and agency (the ability to make and execute decisions), highlighting that AI may support informational aspects of patient decision-making while simultaneously eroding the experiential and volitional dimensions of autonomous choice [14]. Laitinen and Sahlgren (2021) emphasized the ability to develop values and rational self-construction as core components of autonomy, arguing that passive acceptance of AI-generated recommendations—without critical reflection— fundamentally contradicts the essence of patient autonomy [16]. Vaassen (2022) further emphasized the importance of the individual’s capacity to shape their own life, which in clinical contexts translates to the patient’s right to actively participate in treatment planning rather than being relegated to a passive recipient of algorithmically optimized care pathways [47].

Synthesizing these philosophical perspectives, we propose a working definition of patient autonomy in AI-enabled healthcare as: “The patient’s informed, reflective, and voluntary capacity to understand, evaluate, and make healthcare decisions that authentically reflect their personal values, preferences, and life goals—free from covert algorithmic manipulation, undue paternalism, or technological displacement of human agency.” This integrative definition encompasses Formosa’s emphasis on authentic value realization, Prunkl’s distinction between authenticity and agency, Laitinen and Sahlgren’s focus on rational self-construction, and Vaassen’s attention to life-shaping capacity. It provides a conceptual foundation for operationalization into measurable constructs for future empirical research.

Implications for patient autonomy: These philosophical perspectives collectively suggest that patient autonomy in the AI era must be understood as more than informed consent; it encompasses the patient’s authentic engagement with, critical evaluation of, and meaningful control over AI-mediated healthcare decisions.

Humanistic design and enabling technology

This theme addresses how technology design can support or strengthen patient self-determination. Calvo et al. (2020) proposed the METUX (Motivation, Engagement, and Thriving in User Experience) model—a theoretically grounded framework based on Self-Determination Theory (SDT) that evaluates how technology design affects users’ basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness across six spheres of experience: adoption, interface, task, behavior, life, and society [15, 48]. The model provides actionable design principles for AI-enabled healthcare tools that support rather than undermine patient self-determination.

Formosa (2021) and Vaassen (2022) identified positive interaction with AI systems and a favorable user experience as factors that promote authentic ethical decision-making [46, 47]. Vaassen (2022) advocated for “glass-box” models—AI systems designed with inherent interpretability, where the reasoning process is transparent and understandable to users, as opposed to “black-box” models whose internal decision-making logic remains opaque [47]. In the context of patient autonomy, glass-box AI tools enable patients to understand why a particular diagnosis or treatment recommendation was made, thereby supporting informed and autonomous decision-making.

Calvo et al. (2020) further emphasized that positive user interface (UI) design should satisfy the three basic psychological needs identified by SDT—autonomy, competence, and relatedness creating technology environments where patients feel empowered rather than controlled [15].

Implications for patient autonomy: These findings demonstrate that patient autonomy is shaped not only by decision outcomes but also by how patients interact with technology. Thoughtful, human-centered AI design can serve as an enabler of autonomy rather than a barrier.

Cognitive threats and weakening of agency

Various studies discussed the risks of reducing agency and autonomous cognition in interactions with AI, with direct consequences for patients’ capacity to make independent healthcare decisions. Formosa (2021) identified the risk of “de-skilling”— whereby prolonged reliance on AI systems may gradually erode both clinicians’ and patients’ cognitive abilities and decision-making skills [46]. When patients consistently defer to AI recommendations without engaging in critical reflection, their capacity for autonomous medical decision-making may atrophy over time.

Laitinen and Sahlgren (2021) described the delegation of cognition to AI as a form of “cognitive heteronomy”—a state in which an individual’s thinking and decision-making processes are externally governed rather than self-directed [16]. In clinical contexts, this manifests when patients accept AI-generated diagnoses or treatment plans without understanding their basis or considering alternatives.

Vaassen (2022) highlighted algorithmic opacity—the concealment of causal pathways within AI decision-making processes—as a fundamental barrier to patient autonomy [47]. When patients cannot trace the reasoning behind an AI recommendation, they cannot meaningfully consent to or refuse it. Vaassen further noted the loss of the intention-outcome relationship: when AI mediates clinical decisions, patients may experience a disconnect between their expressed preferences and the actual care pathway, reducing their sense of agency and control.

Implications for patient autonomy: These cognitive threats suggest that AI, if implemented without adequate safeguards, may gradually transform patients from active decision-makers into passive recipients of algorithmically generated care plans.

Moral risks and social inequality

This theme examines the socio-moral implications of AI-based technologies for patient autonomy, particularly among vulnerable populations. Tiribelli (2023) identified an incomplete understanding of autonomy in aging populations, noting that the use of AI-based health recommender systems for elderly patients may inadvertently lead to imposed decision-making that undermines their independence [22]. Bitterman and Mak (2020) demonstrated that AI-driven disparities in healthcare access can disproportionately affect marginalized communities, reinforcing existing health inequities [49]. When AI systems are trained on biased datasets, their recommendations may systematically disadvantage certain patient groups, effectively creating a two-tiered system in which the autonomy-enhancing benefits of AI are unequally distributed. Laitinen and Sahlgren (2021) raised concerns about the lack of respect for individuality in AI systems, noting that algorithmic categorization may reduce patients to data profiles rather than treating them as unique individuals with distinct values and preferences [16]. Bryson and Winfield (2017) warned about the potential for misuse of accumulated personal data for behavioral prediction and control, which poses a direct threat to individual autonomy and dignity [50]. Community-based governance—a participatory model in which local communities, patient groups, and other stakeholders are directly involved in decisions about how AI technologies are developed, deployed, and regulated within healthcare systems—has been proposed as a mechanism to ensure that diverse values, cultural perspectives, and equity considerations are incorporated into AI governance [9, 51]. Implications for patient autonomy: Without equitable policy interventions, AI may disproportionately erode autonomy among elderly, economically disadvantaged, and culturally marginalized patient populations.

Covert control and preference manipulation

This category examines the impact of covert and guided technology designs on patients’ conscious healthcare decisions. Prunkl (2024) described mechanisms by which AI systems may alter patients’ preferences without their awareness—a process that fundamentally undermines the authenticity requirement of autonomous decision-making [14].

Calvo et al. (2020) and Formosa (2021) identified specific examples of covert influence mechanisms, including autoplay features, implicit prompting, and recommendation algorithms that subtly guide users toward particular choices [15, 46]. In clinical contexts, these mechanisms may manifest as AI systems that present treatment options in ways that nudge patients toward algorithmically preferred pathways—for example, by highlighting certain options more prominently or framing statistical information in ways that favor particular decisions.

Calvo et al. (2020) further warned that recommendation algorithms may alter patients’ conscious goals, creating a situation in which patients believe they are making autonomous choices when, in reality, their decision-making has been systematically influenced by algorithmic architecture [15].

Implications for patient autonomy: These findings raise serious questions about the boundary between “decision support” and “decision steering” in AI-driven healthcare, highlighting the need for transparency in how AI systems present and frame clinical options to patients.

Human–AI balance in decision-making

Research consistently emphasizes that AI should remain an advisory tool rather than a substitute for human decision-making, with direct implications for patient autonomy. Patient autonomy is preserved when three conditions are met: [1] the patient is aware that AI is involved in their care and understands its role; [2] the clinician retains ultimate decision-making authority and can override AI recommendations based on patient preferences and clinical judgment; and [3] the patient has meaningful opportunities to question, discuss, and refuse AI-generated recommendations. Formosa (2021) suggested that delegating less important, routine decisions to AI could free patients and clinicians to devote time and cognitive resources to more significant decisions requiring human judgment [46]. Kim et al. (2024) investigated patients’ preferences regarding AI autonomy levels in healthcare decision-making and found that patients generally prefer AI to function as a “second opinion” rather than a primary decision-maker, and their trust in AI increases when they perceive it as augmenting rather than replacing human clinical judgment [17]. Prunkl (2024) emphasized the importance of maintaining a clear distinction between AI as a tool (which supports human decision-making) and AI as an agent (which makes decisions independently), arguing that conflating these roles poses significant risks to patient autonomy [14]. Calvo et al. (2020) recommended continuous assessment of AI’s impact on patients’ perceived autonomy and agency throughout the care process [15].

Implications for patient autonomy: These findings suggest that preserving the human-AI balance is not merely a technical or organizational concern but a fundamental prerequisite for maintaining patient autonomy in AI-enabled healthcare systems.

The need for transparency, dialogue, and flexibility

Several studies highlighted the interconnected requirements of AI explainability, collaborative human-AI interaction, and adaptive autonomy as essential for preserving patient self-determination. Lu et al. (2024) emphasized that AI decisions must be explainable to patients in accessible, meaningful language—not merely technically transparent—and that creating space for genuine dialogue between the patient, clinician, and AI system is essential for maintaining autonomy [52].

Formosa (2021) proposed “reflective nudging”—a design approach that encourages patients to think critically about AI recommendations rather than passively accepting them—as a way to support autonomous decision-making while still providing beneficial guidance [46]. Lu et al. (2024) further recommended that the level of AI intervention should be adjustable based on the patient’s experience, preferences, and clinical context, ensuring that autonomy support is adaptive rather than static [52].

Additional suggestions included: designing AI interfaces that facilitate meaningful patient-clinician dialogue about AI-generated recommendations; implementing active monitoring of patient experience with AI systems; and ensuring that patients retain the ability to adjust or withdraw from AI-mediated care pathways at any point.

Discussion

The findings of the present review demonstrate that AI in medicine has a dual and multi-layered relationship with patient autonomy: on the one hand, it can help empower patients and enhance autonomous decision-making; on the other hand, it carries the risk of limiting human agency and creating new inequalities. The seven themes resulting from the analysis are discussed below, with emphasis on their clinical implications, connections to external literature, and directions for future research.

Philosophical and existential dimensions of autonomy

In the literature, autonomy refers to the ability to choose freely and rationally. In the context of AI use, this concept is challenged, and it is necessary to redefine the meaning of autonomy in medical sciences in the AI era and to identify instances of preserving and undermining autonomy in clinical settings. Medical staff, and especially physicians, must be equipped to distinguish between genuine patient choices and algorithmic recommendations, because if patient preferences are covertly directed through algorithms, patient autonomy will be substantively undermined—not merely in a procedural or formal sense (e.g., signing a consent form), but in its deeper philosophical meaning encompassing authentic self-governance, reflective endorsement of one’s values, and genuine voluntary choice free from covert algorithmic influence [56]. It is essential that patient well-being remains a fundamental principle and that the medical team balances AI assistance with respect for patients’ decisions [21]. Laitinen and Sahlgren (2021) emphasized the ability to develop rational and self-organizing values, which is in conflict with passive acceptance of AI decisions [16]. Therefore, the introduction of technology into healthcare requires a deep understanding of these philosophical foundations to ensure that patient autonomy is not reduced to the level of formal or procedural choices.

Humanistic design and enabling technology

Although AI is sometimes perceived as a threat to individual autonomy, if designed and used correctly, it can become a tool that empowers patients to make informed and independent decisions [57]. Blease et al. (2019) demonstrated that AI can create the basis for more active patient participation in the treatment process by enabling shared decision-making between the treatment team and the patient [57]. Lu et al. (2024) identified the need for interdisciplinary collaboration in the development and application of AI in healthcare as an important strategy, arguing that maintaining patient autonomy as a non-negotiable priority requires the design and formulation of global regulations as executive guarantees for the ethical use of AI in care [21]. Reddy et al. (2020) emphasized the need for governance frameworks that ensure transparent and human-centered design of AI in health systems [2]. Jia et al. (2025) proposed a delegated autonomy framework suggesting that the level of AI involvement should be calibrated based on specific clinical characteristics. They argue that low-risk, routine clinical tasks—such as automated scheduling, standard laboratory result interpretation, or preliminary image screening—may be appropriately delegated to AI with minimal human oversight. In contrast, high-stakes clinical decisions—including complex diagnostic reasoning, treatment selection for patients with multiple comorbidities, end-of-life care planning, and surgical decision-making—require the active and simultaneous participation of the clinical team, the patient, and AI as a supportive tool.

The criteria for delegating decision-making authority to AI should extend beyond technical accuracy metrics and incorporate factors such as the type of clinical task, the clinical context, the criticality and reversibility of the decision, and the historical error patterns of both the clinician and the AI system [58]. Overall, studies in this field show that patient autonomy is shaped not only by decision outcomes but also by how patients interact with technology and use it.

Cognitive threats and weakening of agency

One of the key findings of the present review is the issue of “cognitive heteronomy.” Overreliance on AI can lead to a decline in both clinician and patient skills. Bitterman et al. (2020) showed that the frequent use of AI in imaging diagnostics can weaken the clinical skills of physicians, and this concern becomes more serious when physicians and patients are not adequately trained to analyze and critique the output of algorithms [49]. To reduce this risk, including digital literacy and ethical principles in the use of AI and fostering critical thinking in the curriculum and training of nurses and other healthcare professionals is essential.

Furthermore, the development of patient education materials that explain AI’s role in their care at appropriate health literacy levels could help maintain patients’ cognitive engagement and autonomous decision-making capacity.

Moral risks and social inequality

While AI can increase the quality of care, it can also lead to the expansion of inequality. Given the public-private partnerships and the role of large companies in health-related AI, strong governance is essential to ensure the public interest, fair distribution of benefits, and social accountability. Guidelines proposed by the World Health Organization and UN agencies aim to align standards, protect human rights, and ensure equitable participation of low- and middle-income countries [9]. Jobin et al. (2019) showed that most ethical guidelines in AI lack enforcement guarantees, which reinforces the risk of inequality in access to technology [7].

Tiribelli (2023) noted that the use of AI technologies for elderly patients can affect their independence and lead to imposed decision-making in this vulnerable group [22].

The impact of AI on patient autonomy is not uniform across populations. Several social determinants of health moderate the degree to which AI supports or undermines autonomous decision-making. Patients with limited education may lack the cognitive resources to critically evaluate AI-generated recommendations, increasing the risk of passive acceptance and decisional dependency [59]. The “digital divide” means that older adults, rural populations, and individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds may have limited familiarity with digital tools, reducing their capacity to engage meaningfully with AI-mediated care [60]. Furthermore, the Western-centric concept of individual autonomy—rooted in Kantian and liberal philosophical traditions— may not align with relational, communal, or family-centered decision-making models prevalent in many non-Western cultures [61]. AI systems designed with Western autonomy assumptions may inadequately serve or inadvertently disrespect patients from cultures where healthcare decisions are made collectively with family members. Language barriers among non-native speakers interacting with AI systems designed primarily in English may further compound these disparities. Without equitable policy interventions, AI may undermine autonomy in certain groups of patients.

Covert control and manipulation of preferences

Medical recommendation algorithms can covertly influence patients’ choices. Shahar (2021) warned that algorithmic bias may lead to “decision steering,” especially when patients are unaware of the decision-making logic [62]. As AI systems become more complex and autonomous, their influence on decision-making unintentionally increases, reducing the patient’s capacity for autonomy [63]. This raises serious questions about the boundary between “support” and “control” in AI-driven decisions. Clarifying liability frameworks, accepting no-fault options for compensation, creating insurance mechanisms and protection funds to ensure protection, and strengthening oversight and accountability are measures that can address these concerns [9]. AI also has an impact on the autonomy of physicians in making decisions regarding patient interventions, though more complementary studies are needed in this area [64, 65].

Human–AI balance in decision-making: implications for patient autonomy

The balance between human and AI authority in clinical decision-making has direct and profound implications for patient autonomy. When AI transitions from an advisory tool to a de facto decision-maker, the patient’s role shifts from an active participant to a passive recipient of algorithmically generated recommendations. This shift fundamentally alters the triadic relationship between clinician, patient, and technology.

Keane and Topol (2018) emphasized that physicians must remain the ultimate supervisors in clinical decision-making, which serves as a safeguard for patient autonomy by ensuring that a human intermediary can translate, contextualize, and adapt AI outputs to the individual patient’s values and circumstances [66]. Kim et al. (2024) demonstrated that patients’ preferences regarding AI autonomy levels are nuanced—patients generally prefer AI to function as a “second opinion” rather than a primary decision-maker, and their trust in AI increases when they perceive it as augmenting rather than replacing human clinical judgment [17]. Recent studies also confirm that patients trust AI more when they do not see it as a replacement for human decision-making [67–69]. Medical education should be designed to ensure that students learn how to use AI as a support tool rather than a substitute for clinical knowledge [21].

Transparency, dialogue, and flexibility

The concept of transparency in AI is not only technical but also means creating a space for dialogue between the patient, the physician, and the algorithm [27]. Although complete transparency may be unattainable, adequate explanation is essential for obtaining informed consent. Transparency and explainability must reach a level of social meaning so that patients can play a more active role in decision-making. Healthcare professionals need to be trained to critically interpret the outputs of AI and present them in a way that enables patients to ask questions and make independent choices [27]. The WHO (2021) emphasized the necessity of “adaptability” of algorithms to different cultural values and needs, demonstrating that transparency and flexibility are key to building trust and strengthening patient autonomy in the age of AI [9].

Patient experience, health literacy, and the operationalization of autonomy

The identified theme of cognitive threats and diminished agency must be understood within the broader context of health literacy and patient experience. Algorithmic opacity does not affect all patients equally; its impact on autonomy is mediated by patients’ health literacy levels, digital competencies, educational backgrounds, and prior experiences with technology-mediated care.

Shankar et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review of qualitative studies examining patient perspectives on AI-powered cognitive behavioral therapy tools and found that patients’ sense of agency and autonomy was significantly shaped by their ability to understand, interpret, and critically evaluate AI-generated recommendations. Patients with higher health literacy reported greater perceived control and more positive engagement with AI tools, while those with limited health literacy experienced confusion, passivity, and reduced decision-making confidence [28].

These findings underscore the need for AI systems to incorporate adaptive health literacy accommodations—such as plain-language explanations, visual decision aids, and culturally appropriate interfaces—to ensure that autonomy-supporting design reaches all patient populations, not merely those with advanced digital and health literacy skills.

The Nutbeam (2000) health literacy model—which distinguishes between functional, interactive, and critical health literacy— provides a useful framework for evaluating the depth at which patients can engage with AI-generated health information [59]. For AI to genuinely support patient autonomy, it must facilitate not only functional understanding (comprehending the information) but also interactive engagement (applying information in dialogue with clinicians) and critical appraisal (evaluating the quality and relevance of AI recommendations to one’s personal values and circumstances). Recent qualitative evidence provides promising initial insights into patient perspectives. Shankar et al. (2025) demonstrated that patients interacting with AI-powered interventions reported complex and nuanced experiences—ranging from empowerment and enhanced self-management to feelings of depersonalization and loss of human connection [29]. Nursing perspectives are particularly critical, as nurses often serve as intermediaries between patients and technology, helping to contextualize AI outputs within patients’ values and care preferences [70].

Measurement challenges and frameworks for assessing patient autonomy in AI-enabled healthcare

A significant gap identified through this review is the absence of validated instruments specifically designed to measure patient autonomy in AI-mediated healthcare contexts. While concepts such as cognitive heteronomy, covert preference manipulation, and diminished agency are theoretically well-articulated, their operationalization into measurable constructs remains underdeveloped. Shankar et al. (2025) provided a comprehensive psychometric evaluation framework in their systematic review of instruments measuring caregiver burden, assessing validity (content, construct, criterion), reliability (internal consistency, test-retest), responsiveness, and interpretability [28]. This rigorous methodological approach is directly applicable to the development of autonomy assessment instruments in AI-enabled healthcare.

We propose that future research should develop and validate a multidimensional Patient Autonomy in AI-Mediated Care Scale (PAAMCS) capturing the following domains: (1) informational autonomy—the degree to which patients understand AI’s role in their care and the basis for its recommendations; (2) decisional autonomy—the extent to which patients perceive genuine choice and freedom to accept, modify, or reject AI-generated recommendations; (3) relational autonomy—the quality of the patient-clinician-AI triad, including perceived support, trust, and communication; (4) reflective autonomy—the patient’s capacity for critical evaluation of AI outputs in light of personal values and preferences; and (5) experiential autonomy—the patient’s subjective sense of agency, control, and self-determination throughout the AI-mediated care process.

Existing instruments such as the Autonomy Preference Index (API) [71], the Health Care Autonomy Scale, and the Perceived Autonomy Support: Health Care Climate Questionnaire [72] provide foundational measurement constructs that could be adapted for AI-specific contexts.

Detection of autonomy erosion indicators in clinical settings could include: patient inability to explain why a particular treatment was recommended; declining rates of patients asking questions about AI-generated recommendations; increased passive acceptance of algorithmic suggestions without discussion; patient-reported feelings of loss of control or confusion; and clinician over-reliance on AI outputs without patient consultation.

Implementation science perspectives: from principles to practice

Translating autonomy-preserving principles into sustainable clinical practice requires systematic implementation approaches. Sumner et al. (2025) demonstrated through a mixed-methods evaluation of telemedicine implementation that successful digital health adoption depends on workflow integration, clinician buy-in, institutional support, and user experience optimization—factors equally critical for autonomy-preserving AI deployment [30].

Drawing on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [73] and the Normalization Process Theory (NPT) [74], we propose the following operational recommendations for autonomy-preserving AI implementation: At the organizational level: establishing AI ethics committees within healthcare institutions with patient representation; developing institutional policies requiring “autonomy impact assessments” before deploying new AI tools; and creating feedback mechanisms allowing patients to report perceived autonomy violations. At the clinical workflow level: integrating “AI disclosure moments” into clinical encounters where clinicians explain AI’s role in recommendations; designing clinical decision support systems with mandatory “pause points” requiring patient input before AI recommendations are finalized; and implementing shared decision-making protocols specifically adapted for AI-augmented clinical encounters.

At the training and education level: incorporating AI ethics and autonomy-preservation modules into medical, nursing, and allied health curricula; developing continuing education programs on critical appraisal of AI outputs for practicing clinicians; and creating patient education materials explaining AI’s role in their care at appropriate health literacy levels.

At the policy and regulatory level: mandating algorithmic transparency requirements for clinical-grade AI systems; establishing patient rights frameworks specific to AI-mediated healthcare; requiring post-market surveillance of AI tools’ impact on patient autonomy and decision-making quality; and developing liability frameworks that clarify responsibility when AI recommendations lead to adverse outcomes.

These recommendations move beyond philosophical principles to provide actionable, measurable implementation targets that healthcare systems can adopt, evaluate, and refine over time.

A recent systematic review by Giorgetti et al. (2026) examined the role of AI in shared decision-making and synthesized evidence across multiple ethical dimensions, including transparency, fairness, reliability, trust, and patient autonomy. While their scope is broader—encompassing a wide range of ethical principles—ours intentionally and narrowly focuses on patient autonomy as a distinct ethical construct [75]. This difference in scope explains the discrepancy in the number of included studies. Importantly, despite this difference, both reviews converge on two important findings: first, that algorithmic transparency is a prerequisite for preserving patient agency; and second, that ambiguous AI systems pose significant risks to autonomous decision-making. This convergence across independent reviews with different scopes strengthens the validity and generalizability of our conclusions.

Comparison with 2025 literature

The updated search (June 2025) identified three new systematic reviews that were published after our initial search. These studies provide independent validation of our findings:

Alnawafleh et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review of 33 studies in nursing practice and found that patient autonomy was the predominant ethical concern (67%) in AI-integrated clinical settings [53] a finding that directly aligns with our Themes 3 (cognitive threats) and 6 (balancing human-AI authority). The convergence between their larger dataset (33 studies) and our focused analysis (14 studies) suggests that our thematic framework captures the essential dimensions of autonomy even with a narrower scope.

Blackman et al. (2025) offered a novel philosophical argument comparing AI’s “black box” problem to the concept of idiopathy in traditional clinical reasoning [54]. This perspective challenges the assumption that lack of explainability is unique to AI, and provided external support for our Theme 5 (covert control and preference manipulation). Their work suggests that the autonomy threats we identified may be exacerbated by AI but are not entirely novel – rather, AI amplifies existing epistemic vulnerabilities in clinical decision-making.

Froicu et al. (2025) a PROSPERO-registered systematic review of 15 studies in oncology – documented specific challenges to informed consent in AI-assisted clinical decision-making [55]. Their findings independently support our Theme 1 (philosophical dimensions) and Theme 4 (moral risks), particularly regarding the difficulty of obtaining truly informed consent when algorithmic opacity prevents full disclosure of decision-making processes.

Validation from updated 2025 literature

Our updated search (June 2025) identified three additional studies (Alnawafleh et al., 2025; Blackman & Veerapen, 2025; Froicuet al., 2025) [53–55]. These studies did not introduce new themes beyond the seven identified in our original synthesis but independently confirmed and strengthened our existing framework. Therefore, our core conclusions remain valid and are further supported by the most recent literature.

Strengths and limitations

This review has strengths, including its comprehensive search across five major databases, adherence to PRISMA guidelines, registered protocol, independent dual screening and data extraction, and the integration of diverse conceptual and empirical perspectives into a coherent thematic framework. Additionally, the updated search (June 2025) ensured currency of the evidence base.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the heterogeneity of study designs (conceptual, empirical, and mixed-methods) precluded quantitative meta-analysis, and the relatively small number of included studies (n = 14) reflects the nascent state of research specifically addressing the intersection of AI and patient autonomy. The low inclusion rate (2.3% of screened records) may be attributed to the interdisciplinary nature of the topic and the stringent requirement that studies substantively address both AI applications in healthcare and patient autonomy.

Second, the included studies were predominantly conceptual and philosophical in nature, with limited empirical evidence from patient-reported outcomes, clinical trials, or implementation studies. This means that the identified themes, while theoretically robust, have not been extensively validated through empirical investigation.

Third, the majority of included studies originated from Western countries (79%), limiting generalizability to non-Western healthcare contexts where cultural conceptualizations of autonomy may differ significantly.

Fourth, the exclusion of grey literature, non-English publications, and conference abstracts may have introduced publication bias and limited the comprehensiveness of the evidence base, potentially missing relevant research from non-English-speaking countries.

Fifth, the rapid evolution of AI technologies means that findings may become outdated quickly; however, our updated search (June 2025) partially addresses this concern.

Sixth, the absence of validated instruments for measuring patient autonomy in AI-mediated healthcare contexts limited our ability to quantitatively assess the magnitude of AI’s impact on autonomy.

Seventh, the use of the PICO framework—originally designed for clinical intervention reviews—may not have been the optimal framework for this predominantly conceptual and ethical review. Alternative frameworks such as PICo or SPIDER may have been more appropriate for capturing the full scope of the literature [32].

Despite these limitations, this review provides a comprehensive and structured synthesis of the current evidence, identifies critical gaps, and offers a foundation for future empirical research, instrument development, and policy development.

Conclusion

This systematic review synthesizes evidence from 14 studies (11 from the original search and 3 from the 2025 updated search studies) yielded seven integrated themes revealing that AI simultaneously creates opportunities for enhanced autonomous decision-making through improved information access, shared decision-making tools, and personalized care pathways—and poses significant threats including algorithmic opacity, cognitive de-skilling, covert preference manipulation, and exacerbation of social inequalities.

Several critical insights emerge from this synthesis. First, patient autonomy in the AI era must be reconceptualized beyond traditional informed consent to encompass authentic engagement, critical evaluation capacity, and meaningful control over AI-mediated healthcare decisions. Second, the impact of AI on autonomy is not uniform; it is significantly moderated by patients’ health literacy, digital competence, socioeconomic status, age, cultural background, and the specific clinical context. Third, current research remains predominantly conceptual and philosophical, with a critical need for empirical studies— particularly qualitative investigations of patient lived experiences and the development of validated instruments for measuring patient autonomy in AI-enabled healthcare. Fourth, translating autonomy-preserving principles into practice requires systematic implementation science approaches, including workflow integration, clinician training, institutional governance, and patient empowerment strategies.

Healthcare systems must ensure that AI deployment is transparent, human-centered, and ethically governed. Autonomy should function not merely as a desirable outcome but as a foundational guiding principle embedded in AI design, clinical training, regulatory frameworks, and institutional policies. Strong, enforceable policy frameworks—combined with robust clinical education, adaptive health literacy accommodations, validated measurement tools, and ongoing monitoring of patient experiences—are essential to ensure that AI serves as a tool for patient empowerment rather than a mechanism of subtle control.

Future research should prioritize: (1) developing and validating multidimensional instruments for measuring patient autonomy in AI-mediated care; (2) conducting qualitative studies capturing diverse patient, clinician, and stakeholder perspectives; (3) evaluating the effectiveness of autonomy-preserving AI design principles through implementation studies; and (4) examining cultural variations in autonomy conceptions and their implications for global AI governance in healthcare.
