Deciphering novel genotypic biomarkers and functional alterations in double strand break repair pathways for Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Chao-Hsuan Chen, Chia-Wen Tsai, Wen-Shin Chang, Ming-Kuei Lu, Der-Yang Cho, Da-Tian Bau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by dopaminergic neuronal loss and α-synuclein aggregation, with evidence implicating impaired DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair in disease pathogenesis. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the association between genetic polymorphisms in DSB repair pathway genes, including XRCC4, XRCC5, XRCC6, XRCC7, LIG4, MRE11, RAD50, and NBN, and PD risk, along with corresponding mRNA expression and DNA repair capacity. A total of 123 clinically diagnosed PD patients and 492 age- and sex-matched healthy controls of Taiwanese ancestry were genotyped, and transcriptional and functional assays were performed in 52 healthy controls. Nominally significant associations with PD risk were identified for four SNPs: XRCC6 rs5751129 (p = 3.55 × 10⁻⁵), XRCC4 rs28360071 (p = 0.0181), NBN rs2735383 (p = 0.0213) and RAD50 rs17772583 (p = 0.0411). Among these, XRCC6 rs5751129 remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Compared to individuals with the wild-type TT genotype, carriers of the heterozygous variant (TC) and homozygous variant (CC) genotypes exhibited increased PD risks, with odds ratios of 1.86 (95% CI, 1.09–3.17) and 13.82 (95% CI, 2.74–69.54), respectively. Cumulative analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in PD risk with multiple high-risk genotypes (p for trend = 0.0025). Functionally, the XRCC6 rs5751129 CC variant was associated with reduced mRNA expression (p = 0.0001) and impaired NHEJ and DSB repair capacity (p = 0.0183). These findings suggest that genetic variants in DSB genes XRCC6, XRCC4, NBN and RAD50 are associated with PD susceptibility in a Taiwanese population and provide preliminary functional evidence that XRCC6 variants contribute to compromised DNA repair. Together, the results highlight a critical role of inherited DSB repair deficiencies in PD etiology and suggest potential avenues for personalized risk prediction and prevention.Keywords: Double Strand Break, Comet, Genotype, Non-Homologous End-Joining, Parkinson’s Disease, Polymorphism, DNA Repair CapacitySubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Genetics, Neurology, Neuroscience

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is one of the most rapidly expanding neurological disorders worldwide, pathologically characterized by the progressive degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons and the aberrant accumulation of α-synuclein aggregates1,2. According to the Global Burden of Disease 2021 report, the global age-standardized incidence and prevalence of PD were estimated at 15.63 and 138.63 per 100,000 individuals, respectively3. Clinically, PD is common neurodegenerative condition that causes profound functional impairment and contributes substantially to the growing global health burden due to its combination of motor, non-motor, and cognitive manifestations1. The hallmark motor symptoms include resting tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability4. Advancing age is the biggest risk factor for PD and men have a higher incidence than women. Current evidence suggests that PD arises from a multifactorial interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental exposures5. Several modifiable risk factors have been proposed, including a sedentary lifestyle and environmental exposure to pesticides, solvents, and air pollution2. Interestingly, cigarette smoking has been reported to be inversely associated with PD risk2. However, the contribution of environmental risk factors to PD remains to be fully established.

The potential role of genomic instability in PD pathogenesis remains insufficiently elucidated. A growing body of evidence indicates that deficiencies in DNA repair mechanisms play a pivotal role in PD pathogenesis6–9. Neurons are particularly vulnerable to DNA damage due to their high oxidative metabolism, elevated generation of reactive oxygen species, and limited regenerative capacity10. In PD, mitochondrial dysfunction and aberrant dopamine metabolism generate excessive oxidative stress, which in turn promotes various forms of DNA lesions, including both single-strand and double-strand breaks (DSBs)11. Inefficient repair of such lesions may cause genomic instability, activate apoptotic cascades, and ultimately result in neuronal loss and neurodegeneration12. Several PD-associated genes, such as leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2)13,14, PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)15,16, Parkinson disease protein 7 (PARK7, DJ-1)17, and poly(ADP-Ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1)18,19, are directly involved in DNA damage responses and repair signaling. Moreover, increasing evidence links polymorphic variants in DNA repair genes, particularly those involved in nucleotide excision repair (NER)20,21, and base excision repair (BER) pathways20,22 with PD risk.

The neurodegeneration-related protein αSyn has recently been implicated in DNA damage responses23,24. αSyn colocalizes with DNA DSB repair factors within the nuclei of both human and mouse neurons25. Notably, αSyn knockout models exhibit elevated DSB accumulation and impaired DNA repair efficiency, defects that can be reversed by reintroduction of human αSyn into murine cortical neurons26. Collectively, these findings indicate that defective DNA repair capacity not only contributes to neuronal vulnerability and progressive degeneration but may also represent a genetic determinant influencing individual susceptibility to PD. Elucidating the molecular interplay between DNA repair pathways and neurodegenerative mechanisms may thus yield important insights into the pathogenesis and progression of PD.

To date, however, no studies have investigated the relationship between PD risk DSB repair gene polymorphisms in Taiwanese. In this study, the genetic polymorphisms in key DSB repair pathway genes were systematically investigated, including non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and the MRN complex (MRE11, RAD50, and NBN). NHEJ is the predominant DSB repair pathway, and MRN complex is a primary sensor of DNA DSBs and essential in both NHEJ and homologous recombination (HR) repair pathways of DSBs27,28. The SNPs were chosen based on one or more of the following criteria: (i) prior evidence of functional relevance (e.g., effects on gene expression or protein function), (ii) reported associations with cancer or neurological disorders, (iii) adequate minor allele frequencies in East Asian populations, and (iv) their relevance to the biological aims of the present study. Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of genetic variants in DSB repair genes, including XRCC4, XRCC5, XRCC6, XRCC7, LIG4, MRE11, RAD50 and NBN, on susceptibility to PD. The secondary aim was to assess the biological plausibility of the identified significant variant(s), specifically whether the variant allele influences gene expression and DSB repair capacity. To our knowledge, this study represents the most systematic and comprehensive evaluation to date of the associations between DSB repair-related genetic polymorphisms and PD risk.

Materials and methods

Study population

According to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), PD is categorized under codes G20, G21, and G22 within the World Health Organization classification system29. All PD patients were clinically diagnosed by experienced neurologists. Each patient completed a structured questionnaire, with assistance from relatives or legal guardians when necessary, and provided a peripheral blood sample for genetic analysis. A control group consisting of four times as many healthy, non-PD individuals were carefully selected from the Health Examination Center database. Each control was individually matched to a PD cases by sex and age (± 3-year). All participants were of Taiwanese ancestry to ensure genetic homogeneity within the study cohort. The study design and experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of China Medical University Hospital (CMUH109-REC2-133). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardian(s).

DNA extraction, genotyping design and settings

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood leukocytes using the QIAamp Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) and stored at -80 °C, with all procedures rigorously standardized and routinely implemented30. Table 1 summarizes the detailed information for each analyzed polymorphic site, including the corresponding forward and reverse primer sequences, restriction enzymes, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) fragment sizes obtained after enzymatic digestion, together with relevant literature references31–37. The specific polymorphic loci of XRCC4, XRCC5, XRCC6, XRCC7, LIG4, MRE11, RAD50, and NBN are illustrated in Fig. 1. The overall genotyping call rates for all analyzed SNPs were 100%. To assess genotyping accuracy, approximately 10% of the healthy control samples the case samples were randomly selected to be genotyped in duplicate, yielding 100% concordance. Genotyping was performed without knowledge of case-control status to minimize potential bias.

mRNA expression of XRCC6, XRCC4, NBN, and RAD50 genes

To examine the association between mRNA expression levels and putative high-risk DSB repair genotypes, RNA was extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 52 controls. Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA-containing tubes and processed promptly to minimize RNA degradation. Total RNA, including mRNA, was extracted from PBMCs using a commercially available RNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) as previously described38,39. The concentration and purity of extracted RNA were assessed by spectrophotometry and the Agilent TapeStation prior to downstream applications. Quantitative analysis of gene expression was performed using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) with an FTC-3000 instrument (Funglyn Biotech Inc., Toronto, ON, Canada). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. The primer sequences used for amplification were as follows: for XRCC4, forward 5’-AGCAGCCGCTATTACCGTATCTT-3’ and reverse 5’-GTGCCAGTGTCATCATCAAATCG-3’37; for XRCC6, forward 5’-CGATAATGAAGGTTCTGGAAG-3’ and reverse 5’-CTGGAAGTGCTTGGTGAG-3’37; for RAD50, forward 5’-CTTATACAGGACCAGCAGGAAC-3’ and reverse 5’-CCTTTCTGTCGCCCTAATGC-3’40; for NBN, forward 5’-CCACCATTGTCCTAGCTACTTG-3’ and reverse 5’-CTTGACTGGAACTCCCTTCTTG-3’40; and for Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), forward 5’-GAAATCCCATCACCATCTTCCAGG-3’ and reverse 5’-GAGCCCCAGCCTTCTCCATG-3’37. All reactions were conducted in triplicate, and the relative mRNA expression levels were calculated as the mean values from three independent experiments, normalized to GAPDH expression. Relative mRNA expression levels were quantified using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. Regarding the reference gene, GAPDH was used as the internal control because of its widespread use and reported stable expression in PBMC cells under comparable experimental conditions. In addition, Ct values for GAPDH showed minimal variation across samples in the present study, supporting its suitability as a reference gene.

NHEJ repair capacity

The NHEJ repair capacity of PBMCs from 52 controls was evaluated using host-cell reactivation assays. Briefly, the luciferase reporter plasmid pGL3 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was linearized with Hind III to measure NHEJ activity. The linearized plasmid DNA was purified through phenol/chloroform extraction followed by ethanol precipitation, then dissolved in sterilized water. Briefly, the PBMCs were seeded at a density of approximately 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and transfected with 1 µg of plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To account for variability in transfection efficiency, a co-transfected control GFP plasmid was included, and NHEJ repair activity was normalized accordingly. After 48 h of incubation, the transfected cells were harvested, and luciferase activity was quantified to determine NHEJ repair efficiency, following procedures previously described36,38.

Single cell neutral comet assay

For the neutral comet assay, PBMCs were treated with 100 µM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and subsequently incubated for either 30 min–24 h. After incubation, the cells were trypsinized, washed, and resuspended in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), as described previously41. A 10 µL aliquot of the cell suspension was embedded in the middle layer of a three-layer agarose gel (80–100 µL low-melting-point agarose per layer). The prepared slides were immersed in ice-cold lysis buffer (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 1% Triton X-100, and 1% sodium lauryl sarcosinate; pH 7.5) for 1 h. Following lysis, slides were equilibrated for 30 min in running buffer (90 mM Tris, 90 mM boric acid, 2 mM EDTA, pH 7.5), then subjected to electrophoresis at 0.8 V/cm for 25 min at 4 °C. After electrophoresis, the slides were rinsed with double-distilled water, fixed in 100% ethanol, and stained with 12.5 µL of 200× SYBR Green I. For each subject, a minimum of 100 cells was analyzed to determine comet moments following 30-minute and 24-hour H₂O₂ treatments. Comet moments were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). To minimize potential bias, comet scoring was performed in a blinded manner, with the experimenter unaware of the genotype information during image analysis. Individual DSB repair capacity was calculated using the following formula: (Comet moment₃₀min−Comet moment₂₄h)/Comet moment₃₀min × 100%. The mean DSB repair capacity of all wild-type samples was designated as 100%, serving as a normalized reference for comparing relative repair efficiencies among different genotypes.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was conducted using the STRING software (v12.0, accessed on Oct 27, 2025) to examine the functional interactions among DSB genes42. The analysis was restricted to interactions derived from Homo sapiens, with a minimum interaction score threshold of 0.900 for network construction. In the resulting networks, nodes represent proteins and edges represent predicted interactions between them.

Statistical analysis

To confirm that the control population conformed to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, a goodness-of-fit test was applied to evaluate potential deviations in genotype frequencies of polymorphic loci. Comparative analyses between PD cases and controls, quantitative mRNA expression levels, and subgroup differences in NHEJ and DSB repair capacities, were performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests. The distribution of genotypes among subgroups was assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test with Yates’ correction (for n ≥ 5) or Fisher’s exact test (for n < 5). Normality of mRNA expression and DNA repair capacity was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which indicated no significant deviations, supporting parametric analyses. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) correction according to the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with the significance threshold set at q = 0.0543. Associations between specific genotypes and PD risk were evaluated by calculating odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using logistic regression analysis.

Individuals harboring the heterozygous variant ID and homozygous variant DD genotypes demonstrated 1.43-fold (95%CI = 0.94–2.18, p = 0.1225) and 2.48-fold (95%CI = 1.26–4.91, p = 0.0131) elevated risks of developing PD, respectively, compared with those carrying the wild-type II genotype. Under the dominant genetic model, carriers of the combined ID + DD genotypes exhibited a 1.58-fold increased risk of PD (95%CI = 1.06–2.35, p = 0.0297) compared to individuals with the II genotype.

For NBN rs2735383, the genotype frequencies among controls were 37.8% GG, 48.4% CG, and 13.8% CC, whereas the corresponding frequencies among PD patients were 26.8%, 51.2%, and 22.0%, respectively. Individuals carrying the homozygous CC genotype showed a 2.24-fold increased risk of PD (95%CI = 1.25-4.00, p = 0.0091) compared to those with the wild-type GG genotype. Under the dominant model, carriers of either CG or CC genotypes exhibited a 1.66-fold higher risk of developing PD (95%CI = 1.07–2.57, p = 0.0301) relative to the GG carriers.

For RAD50 rs17772583, the genotype frequencies among controls were 42.3% AA, 45.1% AG, and 12.6% GG, whereas the corresponding frequencies among PD patients were 31.7%, 48.8%, and 19.5%, respectively. Individuals homozygous for the GG variant demonstrated a 2.06-fold increased risk of PD (95%CI = 1.15–3.70, p = 0.0208) compared with those carrying the wild-type AA genotype. Under the dominant model, carriers of either AG or GG genotypes showed a 1.58-fold higher risk of PD (95%CI = 1.04–2.40, p = 0.0418) relative to AA carriers.

Cumulative effects of multiple DSB repair genotypes on PD risk

PD susceptibility was subsequently evaluated according to the cumulative number of risk alleles across the four significant SNPs carried by each individual. The ORs for PD among individuals carrying one, two, three, or four of these genotypes were 2.19, 2.73, 4.48, and 2.70, respectively (95%CI = 0.96–4.99, 1.28–5.83, 2.03–9.86, and 1.02–7.22, respectively), demonstrating a significant dose-dependent elevation in PD risk with an increasing number of susceptible genotypes (p for trend = 0.0025; Table 5). This cumulative effect was further supported when combined individuals with one or two PD-susceptible genotypes (OR = 2.53, 95%CI = 1.21–5.27, p = 0.0165), and those with three or four susceptible genotypes (OR = 3.93, 95%CI = 1.83–8.43, p = 0.0004), with a significant trend across subgroups (p for trend = 0.0009, Table 5). These findings indicate a dose-dependent relationship between the number of high-risk DSB repair genotypes and PD susceptibility.

Susceptible genotype-based mRNA expression

The relationship between PD-susceptible genotypes (XRCC4, XRCC6, RAD50, and NBN) and their corresponding mRNA expression was further investigated. Genotype-based mRNA expression levels were analyzed in 52 control subjects. For XRCC4 rs28360071, 32 individuals carried the II genotype, 16 carried ID, and 4 carried DD (Fig. 2A). No significant differences were observed in XRCC4 mRNA expression among the three genotype groups (all p > 0.05, Fig. 2A). In contrast, for XRCC6 rs5751129, mRNA expression was slightly reduced in 14 CT carriers compared with 32 TT (wild-type) individuals, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1763). Notably, the 6 CC carriers exhibited a marked decrease in XRCC6 mRNA expression compared with TT carriers (p = 0.0001), and expression levels also differed significantly between CT and CC groups (p = 0.0019, Fig. 2B). For RAD50 rs17772583 and NBN rs2735383, no significant differences in mRNA expression were detected across genotype groups (all p > 0.05, Figs. 2C-D). These findings suggest that XRCC6 rs5751129 exhibits a genotype-dependent effect on transcript levels.

Susceptible genotype-based NHEJ repair capacity

Next, the impact of PD-susceptible genotypes (XRCC4, XRCC6, RAD50, and NBN) on NHEJ repair capacity was assessed using PBMCs from 52 controls. No significant differences in NHEJ efficiency were observed among genotype groups for XRCC4 rs28360071, RAD50 rs17772583, or NBN rs2735383 (all p > 0.05, Fig. 3A and C, and 3D). In contrast, carriers of the PD-susceptible CC genotype at XRCC6 rs5751129 exhibited a markedly reduced NHEJ repair capacity compared with individuals harboring the wild-type TT genotype (p = 0.0183, Fig. 3B). These results indicate that the CC variant of XRCC6 rs5751129 is functionally associated with impaired NHEJ repair efficiency.

Discussion

Among neurodegenerative disorders, PD has shown the most rapid rise in incidence and associated disability in recent years, establishing it as a leading cause of global morbidity44. Impaired repair of DNA DSBs can generate substantial genomic instability, which is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to PD pathogenesis. Despite this, few studies have systematically investigated the role of DNA repair pathways and associated genes in PD susceptibility. Most prior research has focused on oxidative stress-related BER mechanisms20,22, while the involvement of DSB repair genes and functional DSB repair capacity in PD remains largely unexplored.

The MRE11, RAD50, and NBN genes encode the components of the nuclear MRN heterotrimeric complex, a primary sensor of DNA DSBs27,28. The MRN complex not only detects DNA damage but also orchestrates the selection of repair pathways, and mediates intracellular DNA damage responses via extensive protein-protein interactions45. Germline bi-allelic pathogenic variants in NBN, RAD50, or MRE11 cause rare autosomal recessive syndromes characterized by defective DNA repair46. NHEJ represents the predominant DSB repair mechanism in both quiescent and proliferating human cells47, and is essential for V(D)J recombination during immunoglobulin and T cell receptor gene maturation in adaptive immunity48. A deeper understanding of NHEJ has implications not only for improving cancer therapies involving radiation or DSB-inducing chemotherapeutics but also for maintaining genomic stability, contributing to cancer prevention and healthy aging47. DSB repair genes are interconnected within a complex regulatory network (Fig. 5). Predictive analysis places NBN, XRCC4, XRCC6, and RAD50 at the central hub, surrounded by XRCC5, Lig4, XRCC7 (PRKDC) and MRE11, and several additional DSB repair-related proteins including DNA cross-link repair 1 C (DCLRE1C), paralog of XRCC4 and XLF (PAXX), cell cycle regulator of NHEJ (CYREN), Zinc Finger SWIM-Type Containing 7 (ZSWIM7) and SWIM-Type Zinc Finger 7 Associated Protein 1 (SWSAP1) (Fig. 5).

Although the precise contributions of individual DSB repair genes to PD pathogenesis remain to be fully defined, the current study identified four genes, XRCC6, XRCC4, NBN, and RAD50, as potential PD susceptibility genes (Tables 3 and 4). Furthermore, evaluation of the combined effects of these variants demonstrated an additive impact on PD susceptibility, highlighting the cumulative contribution of multiple DSB-related genotypes to disease risk (Table 5). Notably, the XRCC6 rs5751129 SNP exhibited the strongest association with PD risk under both additive and dominant genetic models. However, the homozygous variant CC genotype was observed at a very low frequency in the study population (n = 2 in controls and n = 6 in PD patients), which may result in inflated odds ratios and wide confidence intervals. Therefore, the association involving this homozygous rare genotype should be interpreted with caution. Validation in larger, independent cohorts will be essential to confirm this association.

Few studies have aimed at identifying genotypic markers of PD susceptibility, and genotype-phenotype correlations remain largely unexplored. In the present study, genotype-dependent mRNA expression levels and DSB repair capacity were assessed for the four SNPs that showed nominal significance. The XRCC6 rs5751129 SNP showed consistent results with previous reports that normal tissues harboring the C allele exhibit lower XRCC6 mRNA and protein levels than those carrying the T allele49,50, and similar observations have been reported in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia tissues34. This SNP resides within the promoter region of XRCC651,52, suggesting that this genetic variation may affect mRNA expression transcriptionally. Carriers of the C allele exhibit reduced mRNA expression, leading to heritable reductions in DNA repair capacity relative to T allele carriers, and hence increased PD risk. The functional assays (Figs. 3B and 4B) provide preliminary evidence supporting this notion, and the risk genotypes (CT and CC) exhibit reduced NHEJ and DSB repair efficiency, contributing to increased PD susceptibility. PBMCs were used as an accessible and well-established surrogate model to evaluate systemic DNA repair capacity associated with specific genotypes, rather than to model neuron-specific biology directly in this current study. PBMCs are not neuronal cells and do not recapitulate the DNA repair capacity of central or peripheral nervous system cells. However, due to ethical and practical limitations, neuronal tissues or patient-derived neuronal cells were not available for functional assays in this study. Importantly, the functional studies are interpreted as reflecting genotype-dependent differences in DNA repair capacity at the systemic level. We should also emphasize that this study is primarily a genetic association analysis. Genotypes represent inherited, stable genetic information that is consistent across tissues, including PBMCs and brain cells. Therefore, the observed associations between genotypes in DSB repair genes and PD are independent of tissue origin. Nevertheless, future studies employing neuronal models, including induced pluripotent stem cell-derived dopaminergic neurons, for functional assays, will be critical for establishing the role of DSB repair capacity in PD pathophysiology. In addition, mRNA expression and DSB repair assays were performed only in healthy controls. The use of healthy controls for genotype-phenotype correlation analyses is a well-established practice in the field. In contrast, DNA repair capacity in patients with PD may be influenced by disease status, medication use, or disease-related cellular stress, making such samples less suitable for isolating genotype-dependent effects. Future investigations incorporating PD patient-derived cells will be necessary to elucidate the genotype-dependent effects on DNA repair capacity in PD and to directly compare DNA repair capacity between patients and healthy controls.

The present study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small for functional assays and for analyses involving low-frequency SNPs such as the homozygous CC genotype of XRCC6 rs5751129, which may result in inflated odds ratios and wide confidence intervals. The limited sample size also precluded stratified analyses. Second, although major risk factors for PD including age and sex were matched and adjusted for in logistic regression, other unknown risk factors may have confounding effects. Third, mRNA expression and DNA repair capacity were assessed in PBMCs, which, while commonly used for genotype-phenotype correlation studies, may not fully reflect altered phenotypes in brain cells. Fourth, only PBMCs from healthy controls were used for functional assays. The genotypic effects in PD patients warrants further investigation. Fifth, the study population was restricted to self-reported Taiwanese ancestry. Although genome-wide ancestry markers were not available, the high ethnic homogeneity of the Taiwanese population minimizes population stratification bias; however, this homogeneity may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Finally, while the genetic association analysis systematically evaluated multiple DSB repair genes, functional validation focused primarily on four nominally significant SNPs. Future studies should extend functional assays to additional DSB repair genes to further elucidate their roles in PD pathogenesis.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study identified novel predictive markers in XRCC4, XRCC6, RAD50, and NBN associated with increased susceptibility to PD. Notably, the high-risk XRCC6 genotype was also functionally linked to impaired DNA repair capacity in cellular assays. These findings provide critical evidence supporting the role of inherited factors in PD pathogenesis. Elucidating the contribution of the DSB repair pathway to PD etiology may offer a novel framework for future strategies in early detection and prediction of PD.
