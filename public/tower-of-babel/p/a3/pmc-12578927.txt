Optimum incision geometry in side-to-side anastomosis
Authors: Kourosh Hasanpour, Sepehr Mirlohi, Sadegh Dehghani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), the use of a saphenous vein (SV) graft to the left anterior descending (LAD) artery is prevalent. While end-to-side anastomosis (ESA) is considered standard practice, several studies have highlighted the effectiveness of side-to-side anastomosis (SSA). Optimizing the cross-sectional geometry is one of the factors influencing the success of the anastomosis. Among the possible configurations, an elliptical cross-section is the most suitable option because of its smooth perimeter and its ability to prevent stress concentration. The optimal diameter ratio for this elliptical cross-section can be determined through an optimization process. This study investigated the impact of punch geometry on the side-to-side anastomosis (SSA) at a 90-degree grafting angle, specifically examining the residual stress and strain caused by the grafting of two arteries. The analysis was conducted both without and under blood pressure at the junction and considered different ratios of elliptical punch diameters. According to the simulation results, the lowest residual stress and the lowest stress induced by blood pressure occur at the junction of the two arteries when using an elliptical punch with a larger diameter ratio in the circumferential direction of the LAD.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Interventional cardiology

Introduction

Myocardial ischemia caused by coronary artery obstruction is one of the main causes of death in developed countries, accounting for 85% of deaths caused by heart disease1. Management strategies for ischemic heart disease include noninvasive primary prevention through lifestyle changes, secondary prevention strategies (such as blood pressure control, lipid and diabetes mellitus management, and the administration of antiplatelet agents, β-blockers, and renin‒angiotensin‒aldosterone system antagonists), as well as invasive revascularization by percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery2,3. However, according to US and European guidelines, CABG surgery remains the gold standard treatment for conditions involving multiple obstructed coronary arteries or obstruction of the LAD artery, as well as for patients with reduced left ventricular function. This is despite advancements in prevention strategies, optimization of medical therapy, and PCI technologies4,5.

Among the criteria for selecting a graft in bypass surgery is its long-term patency rate. Various arteries can be used for grafting to the LAD; however, the right internal mammary artery (RIMA) and left internal mammary artery (LIMA) demonstrate the highest patency rates over a 10-year period. In contrast, the most common venous graft is the saphenous vein (SV), which fails in 40–50% of patients within 10 years6.

Most surgeons use the saphenous vein (SV) due to its accessibility and the reduced number of incisions required during harvest for bypass surgery. Additionally, the SV is more resistant to deformation during cutting and is less susceptible to vasospasm7. In contrast, bypass surgery using the left internal mammary artery (LIMA) and right internal mammary artery (RIMA) is more technically challenging, and the available length of the graft is more limited8.

Beyond the type of graft, the connection type (anastomosis) between the graft and the target artery significantly affects the success of the procedure. The side-to-side anastomosis (SSA) and end-to-side anastomosis (ESA) are two common types used in coronary grafts. The ESA has been the gold standard, and most studies on anastomosis have focused on optimizing the ESA graft angle to reduce stress at the junction9.

Figure 1 illustrates the three main types of anastomosis: end-to-end, side-to-side, and end-to-side. Although less commonly performed than ESA, the end-to-end anastomosis provides the advantage of greater blood flow into the artery; however, a potential disadvantage is the mismatch that can occur between two arteries of different sizes.

Three main modes of connection: (a) SSA, (b) ESA, (c) EEA.

For the side-to-side anastomosis (SSA), blood flow can pass in both directions. Provided there are no issues with venous valves and the artery is healthy, this type of anastomosis has few complications.

However, studies on the SSA have proven its feasibility and effectiveness compared to the ESA. Clinical studies conducted on 92 patients (61 treated with SSA and 31 with ESA) revealed that the SSA technique required fewer stitches to achieve hemostasis. After one year, the patency rate was 92.9% for SSA compared to 81% for ESA. Furthermore, the deformation of the LAD artery caused by SSA is less than that caused by ESA10.

To simulate, analyze, and optimize artery grafting, a mechanical description of the arterial tissue is required. This necessitates a constitutive model that accurately represents the material behavior11.

Initially, isotropic constitutive models were used to describe arterial mechanics12. However, following microstructural studies that identified the significant role of collagen fibers, anisotropic models were developed to more accurately capture arterial behavior13,14.

For this study, the anisotropic hyperelastic model developed by Holzapfel et al. (often referred to as the HGO model) was used to simulate the anastomosis of the LAD and SV. This model represents a multilayer artery with distributed collagen fibers, accounting for their three-dimensional orientation and treating the tissue as an incompressible material14,15.

To simulate the mechanical behavior of a coronary artery, the material parameters for its constitutive models must be determined from experimental test data. Uniaxial tensile tests performed on each layer of the artery in both the circumferential and axial directions enable the determination of these material parameters for individual layers16,17.

The diameter and thickness of the LAD and SV, which are essential for graft simulation, vary based on age and sex. Perry et al. used echocardiography to measure the diameter and thickness of the LAD in 210 patients and 30 healthy individuals18. In a separate study, angiography was performed on 167 patients (109 men and 58 women) aged 31–84 years to determine LAD dimensions19. Furthermore, samples were taken from 22 patients during surgery to measure the diameter and mechanical properties of the SV16.

This study investigates the effects of incision shape on stress and strain distributions at the side-to-side anastomosis (SSA) junction, both without and under blood pressure, using numerical analysis.

For bypass grafting, surgeons can utilize arteries sourced from the arm or chest, as well as veins from the leg—most commonly the saphenous vein (SV). The left anterior descending (LAD) artery is characterized by high blood pressure, stiffness, and reactivity to vasoconstrictive drugs. Consequently, the graft vessel connected to it must also possess these mechanical properties.

Kato et al. successfully demonstrated the viability of using a venous conduit for SSA in coronary artery bypass grafting, supported by initial and midterm angiographic findings10. The angiographic results presented in Fig. 2 show that arterial deformation in the SSA is lower than in the end-to-side anastomosis (ESA).

Material parameter identification

Notwithstanding patient-specific variations in the material characteristics of the saphenous vein (SV) and left anterior descending (LAD) artery due to factors such as age and gender, the present analysis focuses on determining the distribution of maximum principal stress and strain. The dependence of these values on material properties and geometrical dimensions constitutes a distinct area of inquiry and is the focus of a complementary study.

To identify the material parameters for the LAD and SV, the three-layer arterial structure was replaced with an equivalent single layer to reduce computation time and improve convergence. The material parameters for the LAD were identified using force-displacement graphs from uniaxial tensile tests performed in the circumferential and axial directions on samples from a 72-year-old male. Similarly, parameters for the SV were identified using force-displacement graphs from uniaxial tensile tests in both directions on a sample from a 68-year-old male.

The dimensions of the modeled samples were based on actual artery measurements. The LAD sample had a length of 14 mm, a width of 5 mm, and a thickness of 0.87 mm. The SV sample had a length of 17 mm, a width of 5 mm, and a thickness of 0.37 mm.

SSA simulation

Donovan et al. reported the saphenous vein (SV) to have an outer diameter of 5.4 mm and a thickness of 0.37 mm, while Perry et al. reported corresponding values of 4.5 mm and 0.87 mm for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery16,18.

The LAD and SV were modeled with a length of 30 mm and an incision area of 4.5 mm2. The geometry incorporated different diameter ratios for the arteries, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Geometry of incision at the junction of two arteries.

As shown in Fig. 6, dimension A represents the diameter of the elliptical incision along the axial direction of the LAD and the circumferential direction of the SV, a result of the 90-degree grafting angle. Dimension B represents the diameter along the circumferential direction of the LAD and the axial direction of the SV. The ratio of A to B is defined as λ.

The connection region between the LAD and SV is a 0.3 mm wide strip around the perimeter of both incisions. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the SV is grafted onto the LAD. The simulation consists of three steps:

A hexahedral (hex) element with a regular mesh distribution was used for the analysis. A mesh independence study was conducted to ensure stability in the results for maximum principal stress () and displacement, which determined an optimal global element size of 0.35 mm. Due to stress concentrations and the connection geometry, critical elements were identified around the incision site. By partitioning these areas and implementing local mesh refinement, the number of critical elements was reduced.

Conclusion

In this work, a side-to-side anastomosis (SSA) at a 90-degree junction angle is examined to determine the optimal diameter ratio for an elliptical incision connecting the saphenous vein (SV) to the left anterior descending (LAD) artery. The optimal ratio was determined based on the criteria of maximum principal stress and logarithmic strain.

According to stress diagrams obtained from the incision edge between the SV and LAD, the most favorable diameter ratio for the elliptical incision is 0.9, as it results in the lowest stress on the edge of the LAD. This outcome is attributed to the greater stiffness and strength of the SV compared to the LAD, as well as the minimal variation in principal stress values across different elliptical incision diameters.
