Efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors according to programmed cell death‐ligand 1 expression in patients with non‐small cell lung cancer and brain metastasis: A real‐world prospective observational study
Authors: Takeshi Masuda, Yukari Tsubata, Kojirou Hata, Mika Horie, Katsuyuki Kiura, Nobuhiro Kanaji, Takuya Inoue, Masahiro Kodani, Masaaki Yanai, Kakuhiro Yamaguchi, Naoko Matsumoto, Masahiro Yamasaki, Nobuhisa Ishikawa, Ken Masuda, Nagio Takigawa, Shoichi Kuyama, Tetsuya Kubota, Kazuya Nishii, Katsuyuki Hotta, Noboru Hattori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStudies have shown the antitumor efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) in patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and brain metastases (BM). However, it is unclear whether the efficacy of ICI is similar between patients with and without BM. It is yet unclear whether the efficacy of ICI in patients with BM increases with higher levels of programmed cell death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) expression, as observed in patients without BM.MethodsWe compared the outcomes of ICI treatment between patients with and without BM using a cohort containing 1741 prospectively enrolled patients with lung cancer. We investigated whether there were differences in the outcomes of ICI based on PD‐L1 expression levels between these patients.ResultsWe enrolled 240 patients with NSCLC with or without BM who were treated with ICI or both chemotherapy and ICI. There were no significant differences in overall survival (OS) between all patients with or without BM (p = 0.489). However, OS was significantly shorter in patients with BM than in those without in the PD‐L1 ≥ 50% group (16.5 M vs. 30.6 M, p = 0.003) but not in the PD‐L1 ≥ 1% or negative group. BM was an independent poor prognostic factor for OS (hazard ratio: [95% confidence interval], 2.045; [1.058–3.953], p = 0.033) in the PD‐L1 ≥ 50% group.ConclusionOur study indicated that the outcomes of patients with or without BM treated with ICI were not significantly different. The efficacy of ICI in patients with PD‐L1 expression ≥50% would be lower in patients with BM than in those without.Keywords: brain metastasis, immune checkpoint inhibitor, non‐small cell lung cancer, programmed cell death‐ligand 1

INTRODUCTION

In the clinical course of patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the proportion of patients with brain metastases at some point is reported to be more than 25%. 1 Owing to the presence of the blood–brain barrier in the brain, the penetration of cytotoxic chemotherapeutic drugs into metastatic brain lesions is poor, and the antitumor efficacy of chemotherapy is poor. 2 The large molecular weight of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which do not pass through the blood–brain barrier, and the unique tumor immune microenvironment of the brain, 3 which has few inflammatory cells, indicate that the efficacy of ICIs is also poor. 4 , 5 However, a phase II study has shown that pembrolizumab had similar antitumor efficacy on brain metastasis and extra central nervous system (CNS) lesion in patients with NSCLC having brain metastasis, programmed cell death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) ≥1% expression, and no neurologic symptoms or corticosteroid requirement. 6 In addition, another phase II study of atezolizumab and chemotherapy showed that this treatment resulted in stable brain metastasis (intracranial median progression‐free survival [PFS]: 6.9 months, systemic median PFS: 8.9 months). 7 Moreover, a pooled analysis of several prospective trials that enrolled patients with asymptomatic stable brain metastases reported the antitumor efficacy of ICI in patients with brain metastases. 8

However, it is unclear whether the antitumor efficacy of ICI is similar in NSCLC patients with or without brain metastasis. Pooled analysis showed that PFS was shorter and the response rate was lower in patients with brain metastasis than in those without. 8 Furthermore, several retrospective studies have shown that antitumor efficacy was inferior in the brain metastasis group. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In contrast, several retrospective studies have shown similar antitumor efficacies in patients with NSCLC with or without brain metastases. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16

The efficacy of ICI treatment is better in patients with NSCLC having high PD‐L1 expression than in those with low or negative PD‐L1 expression. 17 However, it is unclear whether the efficacy of ICI increases with higher PD‐L1 expression levels in patients with brain metastases, because of the unique tumor immune microenvironment of the brain, which contains few inflammatory cells.

The aim of this study was to investigate whether the antitumor efficacy of ICI was similar in patients with NSCLC with or without brain metastasis in a real‐world setting. Thus, we compared the outcomes of ICI treatment between these patients using a CS‐lung registry cohort containing 1741 prospectively enrolled patients with lung cancer. 18 Additionally, we investigated whether there was a difference in the efficacy of ICIs based on PD‐L1 status levels between these patients.

METHODS

Study population and design

Patients were recruited from a prospective lung cancer patient registry (CS‐Lung‐003). 18 This registry prospectively enrolled patients from March 2017 to September 2020 and includes related prospective observational studies to clarify multiple clinical practice points in lung cancer treatment, one of which is this study. The inclusion criterion for this study was a histologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC. The patients who received ICI treatment or a combination of chemotherapy and ICI treatment. Patients in whom brain metastases could not be evaluated and those with driver mutations were excluded. The data for this study were collected in August 2021. The ethics committees approved the study protocol (no. 1703‐055; Institutional Review Board of Okayama University Hospital), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

We conducted this study in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations.

Data collection

We collected the following data: age, sex, smoking history, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS), histological type, clinical stage, T classification, number of metastatic site, PD‐L1 tumor proportion score (TPS), line of treatment, type of treatment, characteristics of brain metastasis, treatment for brain metastasis, specimen, and antibody used to investigate PD‐L1 expression.

Efficacy and assessments

Tumor response was assessed using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors. 19 PFS was the time from treatment initiation to the occurrence of death and progression of disease. Overall survival (OS) is defined as the time from the initiation of treatment to death. The tumor response, including brain metastasis, was evaluated by the attending physician for each case. Adverse events were summarized by type and grade using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events. 20

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were presented as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables and means for continuous variables. The objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) were calculated, and the one‐sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. These were compared between two groups using Student's t‐test and chi‐squared tests. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to analyze the PFS and OS. Ninety‐five percent CIs of the survival function and median survival times were calculated. Furthermore, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted on PFS and OS to identify prognostic factors. The adverse events observed during treatment are summarized by type. Parameters with a p‐value less than 0.05 in the univariate analysis were selected for inclusion in the multivariable analysis. Statistical significance for all tests was defined as a two‐tailed p‐value of <0.05. All patients who met the inclusion criteria in the Lung Cancer Patient Registry (CS‐Lung‐003) were enrolled. To evaluate the treatment effects based on brain metastasis with adjustment for known prognostic factors, multivariate Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed. The following variables were included in the hazard models for OS: performance status, smoking status, histology, clinical stage, PD‐L1 TPS, and treatment line. Data analyses were performed using the JMP Pro 16 software (SAS).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 327 patients who underwent ICI treatment were recruited from 1741 patients in the CS‐lung registry. 18 Twenty cases in which the presence of brain metastasis could not be evaluated and 67 cases with driver mutations were excluded. In total, 240 patients were included in the analysis. Of these, 52 were brain metastasis‐positive, and 188 were brain metastasis‐negative (Figure 1). The clinical characteristics of patients who were brain metastasis‐positive and metastasis‐negative are shown in Table 1. The proportion of patients with stage IVB disease was significantly higher in the group with brain metastases than in the group without brain metastases; however, other factors did not differ between the two groups. In the brain metastases group, the proportions of patients with multiple brain metastases or symptoms due to brain metastasis before ICI administration were 50% and 23.1%, respectively. Regarding radiotherapy for brain metastasis prior to ICI treatment, 15.4% of patients received whole brain radiotherapy and 19.2% of patients received local radiotherapy. The radiotherapy was performed before or just before ICI treatment in 21.1% and 15.4% of all patients. The specimen information used to investigate PD‐L1 expression was obtained from 96 patients. Of these 96 patients, 62, 16, and 18 underwent bronchofibroscopy, CT‐guided biopsy, and surgery, respectively. Information regarding the number of brain metastases was not obtained for the 18 surgically resected tissues. In our study, the antibody for PD‐L1 staining was obtained from 95 cases, and the 22C3 antibody was used in 94 cases.

Tumor response

The ORRs of all the patients are shown in Table 2. The ORR in patients with and without brain metastasis was 44.4% (95% CI, 36.7–51.6) and 42.5% (95% CI, 28.5–57.8), respectively. The DCR was 70.2% (95% CI, 62.9–76.6) and 67.5% (95% CI, 52.0–79.9), respectively. There was no significant difference in the ORR and DCR between the two groups. The ORRs in patients who were PD‐L1‐negative, ≥1%, or ≥50% are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The ORR and DCR were also not significantly different between the two groups.

OS and PFS analyses of patients with or without brain metastasis

The median study follow‐up was 15.9 (0.2–67.6) months. At the time of data cutoff, 107 patients (56.9%) in the no brain metastasis group and 31 patients (59.6%) in the brain metastasis group had died. The Kaplan–Meier curves of OS between patients with and without brain metastasis are shown in Figure 2. The median OS was not significantly different between the brain metastasis group and the no brain metastasis group (18.1 vs. 20.3 M, respectively, p = 0.489) (Figure 2a). In the subgroups with PD‐L1‐negative and PD‐L1 TPS ≥1%, there were no significant differences in OS between the two groups (Figure 2b,c). In contrast, in the subgroup of PD‐L1 TPS ≥50%, OS was significantly longer in patients without brain metastasis than in those with brain metastasis (16.5 vs. 30.6 M, respectively, p = 0.003) (Figure 2d). At the time of data cutoff, 105 patients (70.0%) in the no‐brain‐metastasis group and 28 patients (71.8%) in the brain metastasis group showed disease progression. As shown in Figure 3, the PFS in both groups was not significantly different regardless of PD‐L1 expression.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses for OS

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to identify the poor prognostic factors for OS. ECOG PS, smoking status, histology, clinical stage, PD‐L1 TPS, and treatment line, but not brain metastasis, were independent risk factors for OS in all patients. Multivariate analysis showed that the ECOG PS, clinical stage, and treatment line were independent risk factors for OS (Table 3). Subsequently, we performed Cox regression analysis for OS in the PD‐L1 TPS subgroup. In the subgroup with PD‐L1 TPS ≥50%, brain metastasis was an independent poor prognostic factor (Table 4). In the subgroup of PD‐L1 TPS <1% or ≥1%, this observation was not shown (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). In the PD‐L1 ≥50% group, the proportion of patients with the number of metastasis site ≥3, pulmonary, pleura, liver, adrenal grand, and skin metastasis was found to be significantly higher at baseline in the brain metastasis group than in the no brain metastasis group (Supplementary Table 4). In addition, in the PD‐L1 TPS >1% group, the proportion of patients with T3 or T4 and the number of metastasis site ≧3, pulmonary, pleura, liver, adrenal grand metastasis were found to be significantly higher at baseline in the brain metastasis group than in the no brain metastasis group (Supplementary Table 4). In the PD‐L1 TPS <1%, the proportion of patients with bone metastasis was found to be significantly higher at baseline in the brain metastasis group than in the group without brain metastasis (Supplementary Table 4).

Safety results

The incidence of immune‐related adverse events and events requiring steroid administration was not significantly different between patients with and without brain metastasis (Supplementary Table 7).

DISCUSSION

We enrolled patients with NSCLC who were treated with ICIs from 1741 patients prospectively registered in the CS‐lung 003 study. 18 There were no significant differences in OS between patients with NSCLC with or without brain metastasis. However, OS was significantly shorter in patients with brain metastasis than in those without in the PD‐L1 ≥50% group but not in the PD‐L1 ≥1% or PD‐L1‐negative groups. In addition, brain metastasis was an independent poor prognostic factor for OS in the PD‐L1 ≥50% group. The proportion of patients with disease progression in the CNS as initial PD was significantly higher in all patients with brain metastases than in those without.

The outcomes of ICI treatment between patients with and without brain metastasis were not significantly different in our cohort enrolled in the CS‐lung 003 study. In addition, two previous phase II studies have shown the antitumor efficacy of ICIs in brain metastases. 6 , 7 Moreover, several retrospective studies have shown that the OS or PFS after ICI treatment was not significantly different between patients with NSCLC with or without brain metastasis. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 These observations suggest that the outcomes of the ICI treatment were similar between the two groups. However, three studies showed that the efficacy of ICI treatment was inferior in patients with brain metastasis compared with that in those without brain metastasis, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 and two studies showed that the presence of brain metastasis was an independent poor prognostic factor. 10 , 11 The difference in the outcomes of ICI treatment between patients with and without brain metastasis could be caused by the varying rates of symptomatic brain metastasis among the studies. In a previous report showing that brain metastases were a poor prognostic factor, the proportion of patients with symptomatic brain metastases was 40%. 10 In our study, the percentage was 23.1%, while in other studies, it ranged from 12.0% to 26.9%. 9 , 11 , 15 , 16 Another factor is whether brain metastases in patients with NSCLC were newly discovered or confirmed to increase prior to ICI administration. Brain metastases, being a poor prognostic factor in two studies, were active brain metastases. 10 , 11 When treating cases of symptomatic brain metastasis or those that increase in size before ICI therapy, attention should be given to the fact that the outcomes may not be the same as in those without brain metastasis.

To date, it is unclear whether brain or non‐brain metastases are the initial PD sites in cases with or without brain metastases. Although the efficacy of ICIs between patients with and without brain metastasis was not significantly different in our study, the proportion of patients who underwent PD for brain metastasis was significantly higher in the brain metastasis group than in the no brain metastasis group. In subgroup analysis, this result was also found in the PD‐L1 TPS ≥1% and ≥50% groups. This finding suggests that the efficacy of ICIs for brain metastases is inferior to that of ICIs for other lesions. A phase II study showed that intracranial PFS was 2 months shorter than systemic PFS. 7 Thus, attention should be paid to the PD of brain metastases in patients with brain metastases during ICI treatment. In addition, opportunities for local treatment should not be missed.

The therapeutic efficacy of ICI was higher in patients with high PD‐L1 expression. 17 Therefore, we examined whether there was a difference in the outcome of ICI treatment between patients with and without brain metastases according to the PD‐L1 TPS. The results showed that OS of patients with brain metastasis was significantly worse than those without in the subgroup with PD‐L1 TPS ≥50%. This may be because of the discordance between PD‐L1 expression in brain metastases and other metastatic lesions. In fact, reports have shown that this discordance is present in 20% of cases. 21 Therefore, even if PD‐L1 expression in lesions other than the brain is greater than 50%, PD‐L1 expression in brain metastases may not be greater than 50% in some cases. Furthermore, it has been reported that the number of inflammatory cells is lower in brain metastases than in non‐brain metastases. 4 , 5 Therefore, ICI outcomes may differ between patients with and without brain metastases. However, a report showed no difference in the efficacy of pembrolizumab in patients with or without brain metastasis with PD‐L1 TPS of 50% or higher. 16 The difference between this study and ours may also be due to differences in PD‐L1 expression in metastases and the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration. In addition, we should consider the difference in the number of patients with brain metastasis and other metastases and the T factor of the primary lesion, which would be associated with OS 22 , 23 between patients with and without brain metastasis. In the PD‐L1 ≥1% and ≥50% group, the proportion of patients with the number of metastasis site ≥3 and pulmonary, liver, and adrenal grand metastasis was significantly higher at baseline in the brain metastasis group than in the no brain metastasis group. In addition, the proportion of patients with T3 or T4 was significantly higher at baseline in the brain metastasis group than in the no brain metastasis group in the patients with PD‐L1 TPS ≥1%. These results suggest that the number of metastatic sites and the size of the primary lesion would not affect the results, showing that in the PD‐L1 ≥50% group but not the PD‐L1 ≥1% group, OS was significantly shorter in the brain metastasis group than in the no brain metastasis group. In the future, we should prospectively examine the difference in ICI outcomes in the subgroup with PD‐L1 TPS ≥50% in a cohort with a larger number of patients.

The safety results were not significantly different between the groups with and without brain metastases. We were concerned about the increase in the proportion of CNS‐related adverse events. However, the proportion of adverse events, including CNS events, was not different between the two groups. A pooled analysis including a prospective study and retrospective studies showed similar results. 8

Our study has several limitations. First, the treatment line and treatment with the anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 antibody alone or in combination with chemotherapy were not uniform across all patients. Second, in this study, PD‐L1 expression in tumors was not measured in 17.9% of the patients. Third, we could not clarify why the difference in OS between patients with and without brain metastasis was larger than that in PFS in the high PD‐L1 group. Therefore, our findings should be confirmed in a prospective study with a larger number of patients. Fourth, in our study, we compared the OS between patients with and without brain metastasis who were treated with ICIs only. Therefore, whether the efficacy of other treatment modalities differs between patients with and without brain metastasis should be investigated. Fifth, this study included only Japanese patients. Our results should be validated in patients without North Asian populations. Finally, we considered that PD‐L1 expression and the number of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes in brain metastases are important for investigating the efficacy of ICI treatment for brain metastases. However, the number of cases whose brain metastasis tissue sample was available was only one case. Therefore, we were unable to investigate the association between PD‐L1 expression and the number of TIL in brain metastasis and ICI efficacy for brain metastasis.

CONCLUSION

Our study indicated that the outcomes of patients with or without brain metastasis treated with ICI were not significantly different in a real‐world setting. In contrast, the efficacy of ICI in patients with PD‐L1 expression ≥50% would be lower in patients with brain metastasis than in those without brain metastasis. The proportion of patients who experienced brain metastasis progression as the initial PD was significantly higher in the brain metastasis group than in the no brain metastasis group. Thus, attention should be given to the PD of brain metastases in patients with brain metastases during ICI treatment.
