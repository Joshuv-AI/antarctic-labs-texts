Nephrectomy and high-salt diet inducing pulmonary hypertension and kidney damage by increasing Ang II concentration in rats

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant risk factor for pulmonary hypertension (PH), a complication that adversely affects patient prognosis. However, the mechanisms underlying this association remain poorly understood. A major obstacle to progress in this field is the lack of a reliable animal model replicating CKD-PH.MethodsThis study aimed to establish a stable rat model of CKD-PH. We employed a combined approach, inducing CKD through a 5/6 nephrectomy and concurrently exposing the rats to a high-salt diet. The model's hemodynamics were evaluated dynamically, alongside a comprehensive assessment of pathological changes in multiple organs. Lung tissues and serum samples were collected from the CKD-PH rats to analyze the expression of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), evaluate the activity of key vascular components within the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), and characterize alterations in the serum metabolic profile.ResultsAt 14 weeks post-surgery, the CKD-PH rats displayed significant changes in hemodynamic parameters indicative of pulmonary arterial hypertension. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), a prevalent global health concern, has attracted significant attention in recent years due to its growing public health burden. The reported prevalence of CKD ranges from 10 to 13% [1]. Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a well-established complication of CKD and a strong predictor of poor patient prognosis [2, 3]. The incidence of PH progressively increases with CKD severity, with reported rates of approximately 6% and 26.6% in stages 1 and 5, respectively [4]. Notably, PH affects a significant proportion (22%) of patients with CKD and is associated with a heightened risk of hospitalization and mortality [5]. Even in non-dialysis CKD patients, the prevalence of PH remains concerningly high, reaching up to 32% [6]. Despite its clinical significance, the etiology and pathogenesis of CKD-related PH (CKD-PH) remain largely unknown. Further research is crucial to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving CKD-PH and identify potential treatment strategies. Building upon established CKD modeling approaches, this study presents the first successful construction of a CKD-PH model. This novel model offers a valuable tool for investigating the mechanisms by which CKD leads to PH. It offers a distinct advantage over traditional PH models induced by hypoxia, Su5416 + hypoxia, monocrotaline, or left pulmonary artery ligation.

Compelling evidence suggests that the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) plays a significant role in the pathological processes underlying CKD. Inhibition of RAAS activity has demonstrably yielded meaningful clinical benefits in patients with CKD [7–9]. Similarly, the RAAS and the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) are recognized as critical contributors to the development of PH through their influence on endothelial dysfunction and vascular remodeling [10–12]. Notably, elevated RAAS activity has been observed in patients with PH, while inhibition of RAAS has been shown to improve survival rates in PH cohorts [13, 14]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that Angiotensin II can induce the migration of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) [15]. Additionally, research suggests that reduced SNS activity achieved through percutaneous pulmonary artery denervation (PADN) leads to a downregulation of pulmonary artery pressure and promotes exercise endurance capacity [16]. Based on this established body of evidence, we hypothesize that alterations in RAAS and SNS activity may be implicated in the pathophysiological processes of both CKD and PH. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this involvement remain to be fully elucidated.

This study aims to establish a CKD-PH rat model to investigate the temporal changes in CKD and PH parameters, as well as to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms involving the RAAS in CKD-PH development. By exploring the role of the RAAS system in CKD-PH progression, this research seeks to provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CKD-PH. These insights hold the potential to inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies for disease management.

Methods

Establishment of CKD-PH rat model

All animal experiments described in this study were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: 2021247). CKD-PH rats were established using previously established methods [17, 18], which involved a combination of 5/6 nephrectomy and a high-salt diet. Specific pathogen-free male Sprague Dawley rats weighing 280 ± 20 g (8–9 weeks old) were obtained from the Guangdong Provincial Medical Experimental Animal Centre and housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade animal room of the State Key Laboratory of Respiratory Disease, Guangzhou Medical University. As illustrated in Fig. 1, at 8 weeks old, the rats were randomly divided into sham and 5/6 nephrectomy groups. The rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction, then 2.5% for maintenance). Nephrectomy was performed after shaving and disinfecting the surgical area with 75% alcohol. A 1.5-cm incision was made through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and muscles to expose the left kidney. The upper and lower poles of the left kidney were then removed. One week after surgery, the right kidney was completely removed. In the sham group, the same incision was made without nephrectomy. Seven days after surgery, rats in the 5/6 nephrectomy group were fed a high-salt diet (8%), while rats in the sham group received a normal-salt diet (0.4%).

Protocol for the establishment of CKD-PH rat model

Hemodynamic and histological measurements

After anesthesia, hemodynamic parameters were measured in rats using a BIOPAC MP150 data acquisition system (BIOPAC Systems, Inc., Santa Barbara, CA), including right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respectively. The ratio of RV mass to the mass of the left ventricle plus septum (RV/(LV + S)) was calculated to assess right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH). In addition, the kidney, liver, and spleen were weighed. The lung, kidney, and heart were then formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, and stained with H&E and Masson's trichrome (KGMST-8003) to examine any pathological changes in these tissues. Pulmonary vascular remodeling was evaluated by determining the ratio of wall thickness to total vascular diameter (WT%) and the number of vessels per square millimeter (mm2).

Immunofluorescence staining

To determine the expression of ACE2, CD31, and α-SMA in lung tissue, immunofluorescence staining was performed using previously established methods [19]. Briefly, lung tissue slides underwent dewaxing, dehydration, and antigen retrieval before incubation with primary antibodies followed by secondary fluorescent antibodies. The expression of each protein was quantified using a case viewer. The primary antibodies used were ACE2 (Abmart, Cat.#T55787M), CD31 (MLBIO, Cat.#ml058803), and α-SMA (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. #A5228).

Measurement of myocardial cell cross-sectional area

To assess the degree of ventricular hypertrophy, the cross-sectional area of myocardial cells was measured. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained tissue sections were analyzed using the iViewer 6.0 slicing analysis software (Unic Technologies Inc., Beijing, China). For each rat, 50 circular-shaped myocardial cells were randomly selected, and their cross-sectional areas were measured. The average value of these 50 cells was calculated to represent the mean cross-sectional area of myocardial cells for that particular rat. This method of quantifying hypertrophy by measuring the cross-sectional area of myocardial cells is a commonly used approach. Randomly selecting a sufficient number of cells and averaging their areas enhances the representativeness and accuracy of the measurement.

Van Gieson staining

To visualize collagen deposition within the vascular wall, Van Gieson staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections of blood vessels. Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with Mayer's hematoxylin for nuclear staining. Subsequently, the sections were stained with Van Gieson solution (acid fuchsin and picric acid) to stain collagen fibers red/pink. After dehydration and clearing, the sections were mounted and examined under a light microscope. Collagen content within the vascular walls was quantified by measuring the percentage of red-stained area relative to the total vessel area usingiViewer 6.0 slicing analysis software (Unic Technologies Inc., Beijing, China).

Echocardiographic assessment

Stroke volume, cardiac output (CO), ejection fraction (EF), and the ratio of pulmonary acceleration time to pulmonary ejection time (PAT/PET) were measured using the same protocols described previously [20].

Isometric tension measurement of isolated pulmonary artery

Isolated pulmonary artery (PA) rings were prepared according to previously described methods [21, 22]. The PA rings were allowed to equilibrate and achieve optimal stretch in Krebs–Henseleit solution (containing 118.0 mM NaCl, 25.0 mM NaHCO3, 4.7 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgSO4, and 11.1 mM glucose) bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 for 100 min. To assess PA ring viability, 60 mM KCl was used to induce contraction. Once the PA rings reached their maximum contractile amplitude, 10 μM Ach (acetylcholine) was applied to evaluate endothelial integrity. Phenylephrine (1 μM) was then used to induce optimal contraction in a calcium solution. After phenylephrine-induced contraction, the PA rings were allowed to return to their baseline tension. Finally, to evaluate the effect of plasma on contraction, PA rings were incubated with plasma from both sham and 5/6 nephrectomy groups. Angiotensin II (Ang II) was used as a positive control.

Metabolomics analysis

Blood was collected from the caudal vein using 5 ml EDTA tubes. The plasma was obtained through low-temperature centrifugation at 5,000 g for 10 min. To investigate the plasma metabolomics profile of the CKD-PH rat model, the LC–MS/MS technique was employed using a high-resolution mass spectrometer Q Exactive HF system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Data were collected and further analyzed using the R 4.1.1 DeSeq2 package. Significant differential metabolites were identified through univariate analysis, based on an absolute log2 fold change (FC) greater than 1 and a p-value less than 0.05 (sham vs. 5/6 nephrectomy group). The edgeR and limma algorithms were also used to select more reliable differential metabolites. The differential metabolites were integrated and ranked using robust rank aggregation (RRA). The candidate metabolites were analyzed and annotated using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). The raw metabolomics data for this study is available in the Supplemental Materials.

Measurements of plasma renin, Ang II, aldosterone and Ang (1–7)

The listed ELISA kits (Renin, MEIMIAN Co. Ltd, Beijing, China, Cat.#: MM-034R2; AngII, MLBIO, Cat.#: ml058803; Aldosterone, MEIMIAN Co. Ltd, Beijing, China, Cat.#: MM-0555R2; and Ang(1–7), Shanghai Jianglai Industrial Limited By Share Ltd, China, Cat.#: JL21363-96 T) were used to measure the concentrations of renin, Ang II, aldosterone, and Ang(1–7) in plasma samples prepared as described above, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Western blot

To obtain whole-tissue extracts, tissues were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (50 mmol/L Tris pH 7.4, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS) supplemented with Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; 100 mmol/L GBCBIO), protease inhibitor cocktail (E2, Roche), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (E3, Sigma) in a 100:2:1:1 ratio (RIPA: PMSF: E2: E3). After three rounds of homogenization, tissue extracts were cleared by centrifugation at 4 °C. Protein concentration was then quantified using the Bradford assay. Equal amounts of soluble proteins were mixed with SDS sample buffer, boiled, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% dry milk in PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-ACE2, Abcam; anti-Actin, Abcam) diluted in PBST containing 1% BSA (Sigma). Following three washes with PBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies. After five washes with PBST, protein bands were visualized using the ECL system (Millipore).

Statistical analysis

In this study, "n" refers to the number of animals, and data were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD). One-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Student's t-test was used for two-group comparisons. In cases with significant deviations from normality, non-parametric tests (such as the Mann–Whitney U test) were applied. Receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the diagnostic accuracy of candidate metabolites. Statistical analysis of metabolomics data was performed using the R 4.1.1 DeSeq2 package. Statistical significance was set at *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01.

Results

5/6 Nx and high-salt diet induced progression of chronic kidney disease

Compared to the sham group, serum creatinine and urea nitrogen levels in the 5/6 Nx group progressively increased over time, indicating progressive renal dysfunction (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, the 24-h urine protein level in the 5/6 Nx group was highest at 8 weeks, followed by a slight decrease at 14 weeks (Fig. 2A). This suggests potential early protein leakage followed by a possible compensatory mechanism. Consistent with these findings, Fig. 2C revealed significant glomerular swelling and sclerosis, dilated renal tubules, and prominent interstitial fibrosis in the 5/6 Nx group at 8 weeks. Notably, prolonged treatment with 5/6 nephrectomy and high-salt diet appeared to induce a transition, with swollen and sclerotic glomeruli gradually reverting to a more normal size albeit with structural abnormalities. Simultaneously, renal tubules exhibited progressive dilation of the inner lumen, accompanied by persistent renal interstitial fibrosis.

Masson's trichrome staining was performed to assess the extent of renal fibrosis. In this technique, collagen deposition appears blue, and fibrin stains red. As expected, Fig. 2D demonstrates a progressive expansion of the blue-stained area in the 5/6 Nx and high-salt diet groups at both 8 and 14 weeks, indicative of renal fibrosis progression. Interestingly, kidney weight in the 5/6 Nx 8-week group was significantly lower than the sham group (Fig. 2B) (P < 0.05), suggesting initial kidney atrophy. However, the kidney weight in the 14-week group recovered remarkably, indicating potential compensatory growth (P < 0.05).

5/6 Nx in combination with a high-salt diet induced pulmonary hypertension and cardiac dysfunction

This study evaluated typical hemodynamic indices of PH in the established CKD-induced PH rat models. As shown in Fig. 3A-B, the RVSP and the RV/(LV + S) ratio progressively increased over time, supporting the successful development of the model. Notably, the 5/6 Nx-14 week group exhibited significantly higher RVSP and RV/(LV + S) ratios compared to the sham group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, systemic blood pressure (mean arterial blood pressure(mABP), SBP, and DBP) with significant difference was significantly elevated in the 5/6 Nx groups compared to the sham group (Fig. 3C, P < 0.05). Interestingly, unlike observations in other PH models, we did not detect significant smooth muscle media wall thickening or remodeling of the distal pulmonary vasculature in the 5/6 Nx and high-salt diet-induced CKD-PH rats. This is evidenced by the lack of significant differences in the percentage of wall thickness (WT%) and vessel numbers per mm2 (Fig. 3D). Similarly, immunofluorescent double staining for α-SMA (red) and CD31 (green) revealed no significant differences in pulmonary artery smooth muscle medial wall thickness between groups (Fig. 3E).

Cardiac function was evaluated using a multifaceted approach, including hemodynamic indices, echocardiographic analysis, and pathological examination. Echocardiography provided data on both left and right heart function. Left ventricular function parameters, including left ventricular EF, CO, and SV) were assessed, along with right heart function measurements like PAT/PET. As shown in Fig. 3G–I, SV, HR, and CO were all significantly higher in the 5/6 Nx-14 week group compared to both the sham group and the 5/6 Nx-8 week group (P < 0.05). Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system dysregulation in CKD-PH rat models

Plasma Ang II concentration exhibited a strong upward trend, while Ang (1–7) levels steadily decreased during the 5/6 Nx + high-salt diet treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). Conversely, renin and aldosterone (ALD) levels were significantly elevated in both the 8-week and 14-week 5/6 Nx + high-salt diet groups compared to the sham group, with slightly lower levels observed in the 8-week group compared to the 14-week group (Fig. 4A). To investigate the influence of the RAAS on pulmonary vascular tone, we assessed the isometric tension of isolated pulmonary arteries (PAs). Interestingly, plasma from the 14-week 5/6 Nx group significantly induced pulmonary vasoconstriction in PA rings, an effect not observed with sham group plasma (Fig. 4C, E). Notably, losartan (an Ang II receptor blocker) significantly inhibited the 14-week plasma-induced constriction of PA rings (P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. 4F, G, ACE2 expression was prominent in endothelial cells. However, treatment with 5/6 Nx and a high-salt diet resulted in a significant reduction of ACE2, suggesting its suppression in the CKD-PH rat model. Western blot analysis of ACE2 in lung tissue (primarily containing lung endothelial cells) further confirmed the downregulation of ACE2 in the 5/6 Nx-8 week and 14-week groups compared to the sham group (Fig. 4H).

Identification of potential differential metabolites

Untargeted metabolomics was employed to identify and analyze plasma metabolites in different groups. This approach detected a total of 12,819 metabolites, of which 2,092 were identified as differentially expressed based on a threshold of |log2 fold change (FC)|> 1 and p-value < 0.05. Among these differential metabolites, 1,460 were upregulated (represented in pink) and 632 were downregulated (represented in blue) in the 5/6 Nx groups compared to the sham group (Fig. 5A). Utilizing the edgeR and limma packages in R software, another set of differentially expressed metabolites was identified, revealing 1,070 and 972 upregulated metabolites, respectively (Figs. 5B-C, represented in pink). All differential metabolites were integrated and ranked using Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA), resulting in the identification of 400 upregulated metabolites in the operated groups (Fig. 5D). A Venn diagram analysis revealed 377 commonly upregulated differential metabolites identified using all three algorithms (Fig. 5E). Enrichment analysis against the KEGG database identified 17 upregulated differential metabolites (Fig. 5F). These findings provide a comprehensive picture of the metabolic changes associated with the development of CKD-induced PH.

Functional analysis of the identified differential metabolites

Cluster analysis identified 17 differential metabolites exhibiting dramatic increases in the treated groups (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, these metabolites displayed strong correlations with each other, with significant negative correlations observed between C00165, C11115, C17755, and other metabolites (coefficient < 0). Further analysis revealed the expression patterns of these 17 metabolites in sham and treated groups (Fig. 6C). Table 1 provides the corresponding KEGG IDs for these metabolites. Non-parametric tests confirmed statistically significant differences between the sham and treated groups for the top five upregulated metabolites (C00165, C00836, C11115, C17755, and C05932) (P < 0.01, Fig. 6D). Notably, all five metabolites demonstrated significant AUC-ROC values (AUC > 0.85, P < 0.05) in discriminating the sham group from the treated groups (Fig. 6F). KEGG pathway analysis revealed the involvement of 83 signaling pathways associated with these five metabolites, including the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (specifically aldosterone synthesis and secretion). Time-series analysis categorized all the metabolites into four clusters (Fig. 6E). Interestingly, C00165 (diacylglycerol, DAG) resided in cluster 1, suggesting a consistent and continuous accumulation of C00165 throughout the observation period (Fig. 6E). The Kruskal–Wallis test further confirmed this observation (Fig. 6G).

Discussion

In this study, we successfully established a rat model of CKD-PH using a combined approach of 5/6 nephrectomy and a high-salt diet. Firstly, we observed characteristic changes indicative of CKD, including increased urinary protein excretion, blood urea nitrogen, and serum creatinine levels. These findings were accompanied by classic pathological alterations consistent with previous reports [23–25]. Secondly, this animal model displayed typical hemodynamic indices of PH, such as elevated RVSP and RVH) in this animal model. However, unlike other PH subtypes, this model lacked classic pulmonary vascular remodeling, suggesting a distinct pathogenesis for CKD-PH. Thirdly, we identified cardiac dysfunction in this CKD-PH model, characterized by decreased PAT/PET, an increase in blood pressure, HR, SV, and CO, along with cardiomyocyte enlargement. Despite these observations, our results demonstrated persistent activation of the RAAS in the CKD-PH rat model, ultimately leading to pulmonary artery constriction. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying RAAS involvement in CKD-PH, we employed untargeted metabolomics and identified 17 upregulated differential metabolites. Among these, DAG was selected based on KEGG pathway analysis. Notably, our findings highlight the potential for elevated DAG levels, induced by RAAS activation, to play a crucial role in the development of CKD-PH.

CKD-PH has become a research hotspot in recent years due to its high prevalence [4]. However, knowledge regarding the development of animal models for CKD-PH remains limited. Previous studies have frequently employed classic rodent models of CKD, including those induced by 5/6 nephrectomy [23], adenine [26], high-salt diet [27], and the combination of 5/6 nephrectomy and high-salt diet [28]. Additionally, activation of the RAAS has been demonstrated to contribute to high blood pressure and cardiac remodeling in both CKD patients and animal models [29–33]. We acknowledge that compensatory hyperfiltration in the remnant kidney may lead to increased sodium delivery to the macula densa, potentially inhibiting renin release. In a model where 5/6Nx rats received a high-salt diet for 2 weeks, the remnant kidney of 5/6Nx rats exhibited lower renin levels than sham rats, which were appropriately reduced by salt loading. However, our study's model shows increased renin levels in 5/6Nx rats. This inference is based on several reasonable assumptions [17]. The metabolic clearance rate for renin falls by about one half with complete nephrectomy. However, the renal function (GFR) with subtotal nephrectomy of the degree employed in the remnant kidney model in rats would be about one third of that of the intact rat. Therefore, the metabolic clearance rate for renin would remain greater than one half the control value. Since the decrease in nephron number is of a greater degree, the similar steady-state plasma renin activities suggest a higher secretory rate per residual nephron. The higher renin content in residual nephrons compared to controls becomes evident since the total renal content of renin is similar between intact control and partially ablated kidneys, despite the latter having fewer nephrons [34]. Moreover, increased renal sympathetic nerve activity and angiotensin II feedback could counteract this inhibition and promote renin secretion under high salt conditions and reduced renal mass [35].

This study employed a combined approach of 5/6 nephrectomy and high-salt diet intervention to generate a rat model of CKD-PH. After a 14-week modeling period, rats displayed hallmark characteristics of CKD, including increased urinary protein excretion, elevated serum creatinine and urea nitrogen levels, and distinct pathological changes within the kidney. Additionally, they exhibited signs of PH, such as elevated RVSP and RVH, and cardiac remodeling. These findings suggest the successful establishment of a CKD-PH rat model. However, vascular remodeling, a well-established and characteristic pathological feature of PH, was notably absent in this model [36, 37], indicating that the pathogenesis of CKD-PH may differ from other PH subtypes, warranting further investigation into its underlying mechanisms.

The RAAS is a hormonal cascade regulating blood pressure, body fluid balance, and electrolyte homeostasis. Evidence from experimental animal models and human cohort studies suggests an imbalanced RAAS contributes to the development and progression of chronic kidney disease [8, 38–40]. Conversely, RAAS inhibition demonstrably suppresses CKD progression [41–45]. Intriguingly, RAAS involvement in pulmonary hypertension development has also been documented [46]. Patients with idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH) typically exhibit increased expression of renin, angiotensin I and II, and ACE [13]. An increasing body of studies reports that RAAS blockers can ameliorate monocrotaline-induced RAAS upregulation and right ventricular hypertrophy [13, 47, 48]. A recent study showed that sacubitril/valsartan, combining a neprilysin inhibitor and angiotensin receptor blocker, significantly improved pulmonary artery pressure in heart failure patients with reduced ejection fraction [49]. Similarly, the ACE inhibitor Captopril has been shown to improve the prognosis of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (SKLRD-Z-202513 COPD)-related PH [50, 51]. Notably, increased renin and angiotensin II levels have been reported in patients with CTEPH, ultimately enhancing pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cell (PASMC) migration [15]. However, the specific role of RAAS in CKD-PH remains largely unexplored. Our CKD-PH rat model, established using 5/6 nephrectomy and a high-salt diet, exhibited an imbalanced RAAS. Upregulated renin, aldosterone, and angiotensin II levels, alongside downregulated Ang-(1–7) and ACE2, suggest that RAAS activity is essential for CKD-PH development. The RAAS system maintains a delicate balance between the detrimental ACE1-Ang II-AT1R axis and the beneficial ACE2-Ang-(1–7)-Mas axis. Suppressed ACE2 or aggravated ACE1 activity has been implicated in PH [52]. The ACE1-Ang II-AT1R arm promoted vasoconstriction, proliferation, and growth, while the ACE2-Ang-(1–7)-Mas arm counteracted these effects [53]. In the present study, we observed decreased ACE2 expression in the pulmonary vascular endothelium and lung tissue of our CKD-PH rat model. This may lead to an inability to counteract the continuous enhancement of the ACE1-Ang II-AT1R axis, potentially contributing to sustained vasoconstriction in the pulmonary vasculature of these rats.

To elucidate the potential role of RAAS in CKD-PH development, we employed metabolomics, a powerful and innovative diagnostic tool gaining traction in clinical and biomedical research. This emerging technique has been extensively used to analyze metabolite profiles in CKD rat models. For instance, one study identified the erythrocyte-specific sphingosine kinase 1 (eSphK1)/Sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) axis as an inhibitor of Ang II-induced CKD [54]. Another study reported the identification of 291 differential metabolites in the serum of a CKD rat model, with polyamine and glycine-conjugated metabolites linked to CKD development and severity [55]. Additionally, enrichment of trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) was observed in the serum of CKD patients [56], with the TMAO inhibitor linaclotide shown to lower uremic toxins (BUN, Cr) and reduce kidney fibrosis in a 5/6 nephrectomy-induced CKD mouse model [57]. Furthermore, metabolomic profiling revealed ketoacid-induced reductions in indoxyl sulfate, betaine, choline, and cholesterol in an adenine-induced CKD rat model [58], while organic anion transporter 1 (OAT1) was found to modulate interorgan communication in a nephrectomy-induced CKD rat model [59].

This study integrated metabolomics data to identify differentially expressed metabolites in the 5/6 Nx group. Metabolites with relatively higher expression levels were identified as diacylglycerol (DAG), sphinganine, xanthurenic acid, o-propanoylcarnitine, β-D-glucuronoside, N-acetyl-L-phenylalanine, p-hydroxy phenylacetylglycine, indoxyl, 5-hydroxy indoleacetyl-glycine, n-Succinyl-L-glutamate 5-semialdehyde, salicyluric acid, cinnamic acid, 5-methyl-2-furaldehyde, indolin-2-one, alpha-methylstyrene, 3-carbamoyl-2-phenylpro-pionic acid, and dopamine quinone. Notably, DAG exhibited a progressive increase in the group continuously receiving a 5/6 Nx and high-salt diet.

A recent report implicated DAG in angiotensin II (Ang II)-mediated aldosterone production [60]. This lipid second messenger influences aldosterone production through regulation of protein kinase C and calcium homeostasis [61]. Arachidonate and hydroxy eicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs), metabolized by DAG in glomerulosa cells, enhance aldosterone synthesis by regulating Ca2+ homeostasis [62]. Moreover, DAG inhibition blocks Ang II-induced aldosterone generation [63]. Interestingly, aldosterone has been linked to the development of pulmonary hypertension (PH). In a monocrotaline (MCT)-induced rat PH model, elevated aldosterone levels stimulated the expression of aquaporin 1 (AQP1) and proliferation of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs). Spironolactone (SP), an aldosterone antagonist, demonstrated a clear therapeutic effect in this model [64]. Additionally, combination treatment with SP and ambrisentan was superior to ambrisentan alone in improving exercise tolerance, reducing B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels, and improving WHO functional class in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension [65]. Similarly, elevated aldosterone correlated with worsened hemodynamics and pulmonary vascular remodeling in MCT and SU-5416/hypoxia-induced PH models, and these effects were reversed by SP treatment [66]. Furthermore, SP has been shown to exert anti-inflammatory effects in the MCT-induced PH rat model by downregulating the expression of xeroderma pigmentosum group B complementing protein (XPB) [67].

Diacylglycerol, a fatty acid metabolite, has been implicated in both pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure [68]. Notably, DAG appears to play a key role in modulating pulmonary artery tone through a mechanism involving transient receptor potential canonical channel 6 (TRPC6) and cellular calcium homeostasis [69–72]. Suppression of DAG blocked Ang II-induced constriction of human and rat pulmonary arteries [73]. Furthermore, DAG has been shown to enhance mechanosensitive cation currents and cellular Ca2+ influx in human pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (PAECs) mediated by TRPC6 [74]. Interestingly, our analysis of the KEGG database revealed an association between DAG and aldosterone synthesis and secretion. This suggests a potential role for the RAAS in CKD-PH pathogenesis through DAG.

In conclusion, we successfully established a CKD-associated PH rat model using a combined treatment of 5/6 nephrectomy and a high-salt diet. This animal model exhibited characteristic features of CKD, including impaired kidney function, elevated pulmonary artery pressure, and deteriorated heart function. Notably, during the 14-week treatment course, a significant increase in pulmonary artery pressure was observed in the absence of pulmonary vascular remodeling. We further confirmed the activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system in this CKD-PH rat model. Finally, untargeted metabolomic analysis revealed time-dependent upregulation of diacylglycerol.

In summary, our CKD-PH rat model, established using 5/6 nephrectomy and a high-salt diet, exhibited activation of the RAAS. This activation, likely a response to the combined effects of low renal mass and high salt intake, led to elevated levels of renin, angiotensin II, and aldosterone, ultimately contributing to hypertension and PH. The potential mechanisms by which the low renal mass-salt method activates the RAA system include Hyperfiltration, Increased Sodium Delivery, Renal Sympathetic Nerve Activity and Feedback Mechanisms. Furthermore, our data revealed increased DAG levels in this model. These elevated DAG levels could enhance aldosterone synthesis via protein kinase C activation, further exacerbating the condition by promoting vasoconstriction and fluid retention. We observed a positive correlation between Ang II and DAG levels with model duration, suggesting their potential roles in promoting aldosterone accumulation and PA constriction, ultimately leading to CKD-associated PH. These findings provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of CKD-associated PH and may hold promise for the development of targeted therapies for this subtype of PH.

Perspectives

This study characterizes the development of PAH in CKD-PH rats. Key features include elevated pulmonary artery pressure, increased serum levels of vasoconstrictive substances, and downregulation of pulmonary endothelial ACE2 expression. Our findings suggest that the rise in pulmonary artery pressure may be attributed to the increase in vasoconstrictive serum metabolites. Therefore, therapeutic strategies for CKD-PH may benefit from interventions that normalize the RAAS and address the metabolic abnormalities associated with chronic kidney disease.

Novelty and relevance

What is new?

We established a rat model of chronic kidney disease with pulmonary arterial hypertension (CKD-PH) by performing 5/6 nephrectomy surgery and administering a high-salt diet. The model exhibits hallmark pathological features, principally manifested by elevated pulmonary artery pressure.

What is relevant?

Therapies that normalize the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and address the metabolic abnormalities associated with chronic kidney disease hold promise for intervening in CKD-PH.
