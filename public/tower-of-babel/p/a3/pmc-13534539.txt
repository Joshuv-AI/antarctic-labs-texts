Quantitative characterization of crack coalescence mechanisms in granite at the mesoscopic scale using deep learning
Authors: Peng Xiao, Peng-Zhi Pan, Zida Liu, Diyuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study quantitatively characterized the crack coalescence mechanisms in granite at the mesoscopic scale using deep learning and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The meso-morphology of the crack surface created by crack coalescence was acquired by SEM. The fractal dimension of the morphology image was computed. Mesoscopic tensile and shear morphologies on the crack surface were quantitatively characterized by deep learning. The results showed: (1) The fractal dimension of the crack surface morphology caused by shear coalescence (1.927) was the highest, followed by that induced by tensile-shear coalescence (1.901), while the lowest was caused by tensile coalescence (1.871). (2) Shear morphology proportions were 14.3, 29.1, and 55.8% for crack surfaces from tensile coalescence, tensile–shear coalescence, and shear coalescence, respectively. (3) The crack coalescence behavior on the specimen surface was linked to that inside the specimen. A high ratio of shear to tensile strain on the sample surface was associated with a large proportion of shear morphology inside the specimen. (4) The meso-mechanisms of crack coalescence and crack propagation were compared, and crack coalescence exhibited more pronounced shear characteristics than crack propagation. The findings of this study offered new insights into the mesoscopic mechanisms of crack coalescence.

Introduction

Natural rock usually includes various scales of discontinuities such as fissures, joints, cavities, bedding planes, faults, and other natural weaknesses1. The existence of these defects affects the stability of rock masses since new cracks typically initiate from or near the defects due to construction disturbances2,3. The initiated cracks gradually evolve into longer cracks, thus weakening the mechanical properties of the rock mass and resulting in the instability of rock structures4. Consequently, it is very important to investigate the crack behavior of rock.

A common method for studying crack behavior is to prefabricate artificial fissures in specimens and observe the cracks originating from them. Initially, rock-like materials were applied to replace the natural rock to investigate the crack behavior due to the simplification of specimen preparation5,6. However, rock-like materials cannot fully replicate the crack behavior of rock materials due to their differences in physical and mechanical properties and composition7,8. With advancements in techniques, scholars have progressively adopted the rock materials with prefabricated fissures to explore crack behavior9–11.

According to the experimental studies12–14, for specimens with a single fissure subjected to compression, as the loading progressed, the initial crack formed, and then the secondary crack appeared. The secondary crack overlapped with the far-field crack at the specimen end, leading to the eventual failure of the specimen. For multi-fissure specimens, apart from the appearance of initial and secondary cracks, crack coalescence behavior was observed between pre-existing fissures15,16. Crack coalescence describes the connection of pre-existing fissures through the formation of new cracks. Crack coalescence involves the propagation and linkage of multiple fundamental cracks, and its behavior is more complicated17–19. Accordingly, it is necessary to investigate crack coalescence.

Recently, rock specimens with double fissures have been widely applied to study the crack coalescence behavior of rock. For example, Wong and Einstein20 observed the fracture process of marble with double fissures and summarized nine crack coalescence modes. Yang et al.21 analyzed the crack coalescence stress in sandstone with double coplanar fissures. Yang et al.22 studied the crack behavior and failure strength of fissured sandstone and analyzed the relationship between peak strength and bridge angle. Le et al.23 studied the crack behavior of limestone-like samples with double fissures and analyzed the coalescence types. Miao et al.24 used the fissured marble specimen in infilling conditions to explore the impact of infilling on crack coalescence stress. These studies have analyzed the crack coalescence behaviors in various fissured rocks with different fissure geometries. However, most of these studies primarily summarized the coalescence modes and mechanical properties of fissured specimens, whereas few explored the underlying mesoscopic mechanisms of crack coalescence. Moreover, many previous studies primarily focused on analyzing the crack behavior on the specimen surface, and crack behavior within the specimen interior was rarely investigated.

Crack propagation and coalescence usually result in the formation of a crack surface in fissured specimens, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Observing and analyzing the crack failure generated by crack coalescence can provide insights into the mechanisms of crack coalescence. For example, Shen et al.25 analyzed the crack surface caused by crack coalescence and found that it was rough and stepped. Tian et al.26 used the video microscope to observe the crack surface caused by crack coalescence in three types of granites and analyzed the influence of grain size on crack coalescence. Xu et al.27 compared the crack surfaces of specimens with penetrating and non-penetrating fissures, and they found that the crack surfaces in specimens with non-penetrating fissures were more complex. These studies reflected the importance of crack surfaces formed by crack coalescence in understanding the mechanisms of crack coalescence, but they did not systematically analyze the crack surfaces. Furthermore, few researchers analyzed the meso-morphology of the crack surface. Analyzing the meso-morphology of crack surfaces resulting from crack coalescence can reveal the meso-mechanisms of crack behavior within the specimen interior. Currently, the mesoscopic mechanisms of crack coalescence remain unclear and require further exploration4, particularly regarding the quantitative analysis of these mechanisms. To address this limitation, the present study focuses on exploring the mesoscopic mechanism of crack coalescence from the perspective of crack surface morphology. By applying deep learning to quantify the morphological features of crack surfaces, the study enables a quantitative analysis of the meso-mechanisms underlying crack coalescence within the specimen.

In this study, granite samples were used as testing materials. Rock blocks were processed into prismatic samples, and fissure pairs with different bridge angles were prefabricated on the sample. Uniaxial compression tests were performed on fissured specimens. The fracture process of the specimen was monitored by digital image correlation (DIC), and the strain on the specimen surface was analyzed. The crack coalescence modes were analyzed. Meso-morphology of the crack surface was acquired by scanning electron microscope (SEM) technology. The morphological features were quantitatively analyzed by a deep learning technique to characterize the meso-mechanism of crack coalescence. Moreover, the strain on the specimen surface and the characterization results of mesoscopic morphology were analyzed to investigate the relationship between crack behavior on the specimen surface and that within the specimen.

Rock tests

The Zhangzhou granite from Fujian, China, was selected as the testing material. The properties of rock are referred to in the study29. The rock block was processed into a prismatic shape, and the specimen dimensions were 60 mm × 30 mm × 120 mm. Two fissures were created in the sample. The prefabricated fissures of the samples were completed by using water jet cutting, and no additional defects were observed after cutting. Figure 2(a) exhibits the geometry of the fissure pair. The fissure length (a) is 10 mm. The ligament length (2c), i.e., the distance between the tips of the two fissures, is 14 mm. The fissure inclination (α) is 45°. Six bridge angles (β = 0°, 45°, 90°, 120°, 150° and 180°) are considered. When the bridge angle was 45°, the two fissures were coplanar. Moreover, intact samples without fissures were also processed. Before testing, the sample surface was treated with speckle. Figure 2(b) shows the surface of the sample after speckle treatment. The camera was used to capture speckle images during the specimen failure process. These speckle images were then processed using the VIC system. The VIC system was developed by Correlated Solutions company, and it was a non-contact optical measurement system based on digital image correlation technology. VIC system can perform high-precision measurement and visualization of the displacement field and strain field on the specimen surface. The INSTRON-1346 device was applied to conduct mechanical tests on rock samples. The loading machine applies pressure from top to bottom on the sample, which is the compressive stress in vertical direction. During testing, the crack development on the sample surface was recorded using a camera. In this study, the term “fissure” represents an artificial defect, and the term “crack” refers to a crack produced in the specimen during the loading process.

Crack coalescence mode

The crack coalescence mode is significantly associated with the propagation of fundamental crack. Understanding the propagation of fundamental crack is crucial. According to the crack classification by Wong and Einstein31 and Yang et al.32, the crack types near the double pre-existing fissures were observed and analyzed. This classification method is also applicable to brittle materials such as ceramics, glass, and concrete. Seven categories of cracks were summarized. Figure 4 shows these crack categories. These cracks were mainly classified into two categories of tensile cracks, two categories of mixed tensile-shear cracks, and three categories of shear cracks. According to Fig. 4, the categories of cracks near double fissures with different bridge angles were summarized. Figure 5 shows the diagram of cracks. When bridge angles were 0° and 45°, there was no direct crack coalescence. This was mainly because cracks initiating from the inner fissure tips tended to propagate vertically or along steeply inclined paths. Under these two bridge-angle conditions, the bridge inclination was not coplanar with the crack propagation direction. As a result, coplanar cracks between the two fissures were difficult to form, and direct crack coalescence did not occur.

When the bridge angle was 90°, tensile coalescence occurred between the double pre-existing fissures. When the bridge angle was 120°, mixed tensile-shear coalescence occurred. When bridge angles were 150° and 180°, shear coalescence occurred. Compared with fundamental crack propagation, the existence of a neighboring fissure had a significant effect on shear crack propagation.

Mesoscopic failure analysis

Mesoscopic morphology acquisition

In the last section, the mechanical parameters, fracture process, damage development, and crack coalescence mode of fissured specimens were analyzed. These analyses reflect the failure behaviors of fissured specimens on macroscopic scale. When the bridge angle is different, the crack coalescence mode is different, resulting in a different failure mode of specimen. Exploring the mesoscopic mechanism of different crack coalescence modes is essential. Crack coalescence can result in the formation of a failure surface. This surface facilitates mesoscopic mechanism analysis based on fracture morphology. For simplicity, in the following analysis, the term “crack surface” is used to represent the macroscopic surface caused by crack propagation and coalescence during the test, as displayed in Fig. 6(a).

Mesoscopic morphology of the crack surface was obtained using the SEM technique. When the bridge angles were 90°, 120°, 150°, and 180°, the crack coalescence produced the crack surface in the region between double fissures. The mesoscopic morphologies of crack surface in fissured specimen with bridge angles of 90°, 120°, 150°, and 180° were obtained. When the bridge angle is 120°, as displayed in Fig. 5(d), crack coalescence occurs at two locations, and two crack surfaces form. Both surfaces show the mixed tensile-shear coalescence. “120-left” represents the crack surface on the left side, and “120-right” represents the one on the right side.

During SEM image acquisition, the magnification was 50×, the field of view of each image was 5.58 mm × 5.58 mm, and images had a resolution of 1536 × 1536 pixels. Starting from the upper-left corner of the crack surface, one SEM image was collected first, and then the specimen platform was moved by 5 mm to acquire the next adjacent region. This procedure was repeated until SEM images of all regions on the crack surface had been collected.

Figure 6(b) shows the two crack surfaces, and the mesoscopic morphologies of both crack surfaces are collected. Figure 6 shows the mesoscopic morphology of crack surfaces in fissured specimens with bridge angles of 90°, 120°, 150°, and 180°. Fractal dimension calculation

After collecting the mesoscopic morphology images, the fractal dimension was applied to analyze the overall feature of mesoscopic morphology of the crack surface in fissured specimens with different bridge angles. The fractal dimension of mesoscopic morphology was calculated using the box-counting method. The box counting method is the most common fractal dimension calculation method33–35. It divides the image into a series of grids or boxes and calculates how many boxes contain the image structure. When the box size is reduced, the number of boxes filled at each scale is recorded, and the fractal dimension is obtained by fitting a linear equation to the data at different scales. Figure 8 shows the fractal dimension of the crack surface. Fractal dimension is usually associated with the complexity of morphological image. The larger the fractal dimension, the higher the complexity of morphology image36–38. The fractal dimension in fissured specimen with a 90° bridge angle is the smallest, with a value of 1.871. The fractal dimension in fissured specimen with a 180° bridge angle is the largest, with a value of 1.939. The fractal dimension of crack surface morphology created by shear coalescence is the largest, followed by that induced by tensile-shear coalescence, and the smallest is that induced by tensile coalescence. Mesoscopic morphology of crack surface caused by shear coalescence is more complex than that caused by tensile-shear coalescence, while the morphology resulting from tensile coalescence is the simplest. Moreover, the maximum difference in fractal dimension of crack surfaces among the tensile, mixed, and shear coalescence is 0.068. Previous studies have reported that the differences in fractal dimension among SEM images of different fracture surfaces are usually small39–42. Thus, the result of this study is consistent with previous reports.

Deep learning model

Fractal dimension can describe the overall morphological characteristics of crack surface. However, the local morphological features cannot be reflected by the fractal dimension. Crack propagation, crack coalescence, and rock failure are strongly associated with the failure of mesoscopic grains inside rock. Mesoscopic tensile and shear morphologies can reflect the failure of grains. Tensile morphology is accompanied by intergranular (IG) fracture, and shear morphology is associated with transgranular (TG) fracture. Deep learning is a powerful tool to process images, and it can extract the image features and identify image types43,44. The deep learning model developed using tensile and shear morphology images can quantitatively identify whether a given morphology image represents a tensile or shear fracture. Li et al.45 previously established an image database of tensile morphology and shear morphology of Zhangzhou granite by performing direct tensile and shear tests and observing the failure surfaces. They used the image database to develop the AlexNet model to identify the tensile morphology and shear morphology. The granite samples used in this study have the same composition and grain size as the samples in Li et al.45. Therefore, the database established by Li et al.45 was used to develop a deep learning model to characterize tensile and shear morphologies. There are 248 tensile morphology and 232 shear morphology images in the database.

These images are cropped into sub-images with a FOV of 1.86 mm. After cropping, there are a total of 2232 tensile morphology images and 2088 shear morphology images. The database is split into training (70%), validation (20%), and testing (10%) sets. Table 4 shows the number of training, validating, and testing images. To improve the recognition accuracy, we modified the AlexNet model and proposed the SEMAlexNet model. Compared with the AlexNet model, the proposed SEMAlexNet incorporates several structural enhancements tailored for SEM images of rock. Figure 9 shows the SEMAlexNet model structure. Mesoscopic morphology identification

This study follows the same training parameters as those used by Li et al.45 to train the SEMAlexNet model. The model training was conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB memory), an Intel Core i7-11700 K CPU, and 32 GB RAM. The deep learning framework was PyTorch 2.2.1, and CUDA 12.1 was used for GPU acceleration. During training, the input image size was set to 512 × 512, the number of epochs was 40, the batch size was 16, and the learning rate was 0.001. The model parameters were updated using the stochastic gradient descent optimizer, with the momentum factor set to 0.9. Figure 10 shows the loss and accuracy curves.

After training, the testing images were used to verify the capability of the SEMAlexNet model. In the testing images, the SEMAlexNet model achieved an accuracy of 96.5%, a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 92.9%, a Matthews correlation coefficient of 93.1%, a precision of 99.2%, a recall of 93.3%, and an F1 score of 96.2%. SEMAlexNet model exhibits good performance in identifying both mesoscopic tensile morphology and shear morphology. The morphology images of crack surface in fissured specimens with bridge angles of 90°, 120°, 150°, and 180° were input into the trained SEMAlexNet model. The SEMAlexNet model can judge whether the input image is tensile morphology or shear morphology. The mesoscopic tensile and shear morphologies on crack surface were identified.

Crack behavior on specimen surface and within specimen

Exploring the relationship between crack behavior on specimen surface and that within the specimen interior is necessary. Tensile cracking is accompanied by horizontal displacement, creating tensile strain on the specimen surface. Shear cracking is accompanied by dislocation displacement, resulting in shear strain on specimen surface. The tensile strain and shear strain on specimen surface can be calculated using VIC software. As displayed in Fig. 12, when the specimen approaches failure, strain concentration on specimen surface becomes pronounced. The horizontal strain (exx) and shear strain (exy) on specimen surface are calculated. The positive sign of exx represents tensile strain, and the negative sign of exx represents compressive strain. The positive and negative signs of exy correspond to different shear directions. If the upper edge moves to the right relative to the lower edge (clockwise), the shear strain (exy) is positive, as shown in Fig. 12(d). Discussion

Crack coalescence mode analysis

In this study, the crack coalescence behavior between double fissures was analyzed across various bridge angles: 0°, 45°, 90°, 120°, 150°, and 180°. At small bridge angles, such as 0° and 45°, there was no direct crack coalescence between pre-existing fissures. This was mainly because the propagation direction of cracks near pre-existing fissures was vertical or had a steep inclination. At small bridge angles, the crack propagation was not coplanar with the rock bridge, making it difficult for cracks from one fissure to coalesce with those from another20,49. At the bridge angle of 90°, tensile coalescence occurred. At the bridge angle of 120°, the mixed tensile-shear coalescence occurred. These observations aligned with previous research findings24,50, which identified that a 90° bridge angle mainly showed tensile coalescence, while a 120° bridge angle often resulted in mixed tensile-shear coalescence (Fig. 15). The mesoscopic morphology of the crack surface in bridge angles of 90° and 120° was quantitatively characterized. The results indicated that the meso-mechanisms of both tensile coalescence and mixed tensile-shear coalescence were dominated by tensile fractures. For the bridge angles of 150° and 180°, such fissure pair geometries had not been investigated in previous studies. Through crack trajectory analysis, crack coalescence modes in bridge angles of 150° and 180° were classified as shear mode. Meso-morphology analysis suggested the meso-shear fractures were predominant (with a proportion exceeding 50%) on the crack surface, which confirmed the reasonability of shear coalescence.

Overall, with increasing bridge angle, the crack coalescence mode progresses from no coalescence to tensile coalescence, then to mixed tensile-shear coalescence, and ultimately to shear coalescence. However, this trend shows slight differences from previous studies. For example, Zhang et al.50 performed rock tests on specimens with bridge angles of 30°, 60°, 90°, and 120°. They observed a distinct pattern: as the bridge angle increased, the mode of coalescence changed from no direct coalescence to shear coalescence, and eventually to tensile or a mixed mode of tensile-shear coalescence. The differences in this trend were primarily due to differences in the bridge angle settings. Specifically, the crack coalescence modes observed in this study for small bridge angles (≤ 45°), as well as for bridge angles of 90° and 120°, were consistent with those reported in earlier research. However, earlier studies included a 60° bridge angle and confirmed it as a shear coalescence mode. In contrast, this study did not incorporate a 60° bridge angle but instead included bridge angles of 150° and 180°, which were verified as shear coalescence. The different settings of the rock bridge resulted in differences in the variation law of crack coalescence modes with the increase of bridge angle.

Mechanism analysis of crack coalescence and propagation

In the double-fissure specimen, different bridge angles result in different crack coalescence modes. Crack coalescence involves the propagation and linkage of multiple fundamental cracks. These fundamental cracks include tensile cracks, mixed tensile-shear cracks, and shear cracks. The propagation and linkage of these different cracks result in different crack coalescence modes. Crack coalescence is more complex than crack propagation. Comparing the mesoscopic mechanisms of crack coalescence and crack propagation is necessary. A typical tensile crack and a mixed tensile-shear crack were selected in a fissured specimen with a bridge angle of 0°. A typical shear crack was selected in a fissured specimen with a bridge angle of 45°. After specimen failure, the propagation of these cracks created a crack surface on the specimens. The meso-morphology of the crack surface was collected. Fractal dimension of meso-morphology was calculated, and meso-tensile and meso-shear fractures on the morphology image were quantitatively characterized.

Figure 16 shows the quantified results of crack surface morphology caused by the propagation of tensile crack, tensile-shear crack, and shear crack. Figure 17 compares the fractal dimension and the proportion of mesoscopic shear fracture in crack surfaces caused by crack coalescence and crack propagation. In tensile, mixed tensile-shear, or shear modes, the fractal dimension of the crack surface resulting from crack coalescence is greater than that resulting from crack propagation, as depicted in Fig. 17(a). Crack surface morphology caused by crack propagation is relatively simple. In the fundamental crack propagation, the mesoscopic shear fractures account for 8.3% in the tensile crack, 21.3% in the mixed tensile-shear crack, and 29.9% in the shear crack. The propagation mechanisms of all fundamental cracks are mainly mesoscopic tensile fracture, which corresponds to the previous results of Liu et al.29. Compared with the propagation of fundamental cracks, mesoscopic shear fractures play a greater role in the crack coalescence behavior. In tensile, mixed tensile-shear, or shear modes, the proportion of mesoscopic shear fracture on the crack surface resulting from crack coalescence is greater than that resulting from fundamental crack propagation, as displayed in Fig. 17(b). The mesoscopic mechanism of crack coalescence behavior is more complex than that of fundamental crack propagation. Crack coalescence exhibits more pronounced shear characteristics than crack propagation. This characteristic is most apparent in shear coalescence and shear crack propagation. The mesoscopic shear fracture is dominant in the shear crack coalescence, but the mesoscopic tensile fracture is dominant in fundamental shear crack propagation. During the loading process, two initial cracks undergo shear slip. The cracks are simultaneously affected by shear slip of the two initial cracks during the penetration process, thus exhibiting strong shear characteristics. During the expansion process, the cracks are less influenced by the initial crack slip, and therefore the shear characteristics are weak.

Limitations and future works

The crack coalescence modes of fissured granite samples with different bridge angles were analyzed. Through a quantitative study of the meso-morphology of the crack surface, the mesoscopic mechanisms of crack coalescence were characterized. The relationship between crack coalescence behavior on the specimen surface and that within specimen was analyzed. This study selected a 45° fissure inclination and focused solely on the impact of bridge angle on crack coalescence behavior. Fissure inclination, fissure length, and bridge length influenced crack coalescence behavior, except for bridge angle. However, these factors were not considered in this study. At the same bridge angle, variations in fissure length, bridge length, and fissure inclination lead to different geometries of fissure pairs, resulting in different crack coalescence patterns. Considering fissure inclination, fissure length, and bridge length is essential for quantitatively characterizing the meso-mechanisms of crack coalescence from the perspective of crack surface morphology in the future. Such an analysis can provide comprehensive insight into the influence of fissure pair geometry on the crack coalescence mechanism. Additionally, external environmental factors, such as temperature, also impact crack coalescence patterns. Future research should consider the effects of environmental factors on crack coalescence behavior and mechanisms.
