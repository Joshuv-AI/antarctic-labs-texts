Promising tools into oxidative stress: A review of non-rodent model organisms
Authors: Yuhao Zhang, Yun Li, Tianyi Ren, Jin-ao Duan, Ping Xiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxidative stress is a crucial concept in redox biology, and significant progress has been made in recent years. Excessive levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can lead to oxidative damage, heightening vulnerability to various diseases. By contrast, ROS maintained within a moderate range plays a role in regulating normal physiological metabolism. Choosing suitable animal models in a complex research context is critical for enhancing research efficacy. While rodents are frequently utilized in medical experiments, they pose challenges such as high costs and ethical considerations. Alternatively, non-rodent model organisms like zebrafish, Drosophila, and C. elegans offer promising avenues into oxidative stress research. These organisms boast advantages such as their small size, high reproduction rate, availability for live imaging, and ease of gene manipulation. This review highlights advancements in the detection of oxidative stress using non-rodent models. The oxidative homeostasis regulatory pathway, Kelch‐like ECH‐associated protein 1-Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Keap1-Nrf2), is systematically reviewed alongside multiple regulation of Nrf2-centered pathways in different organisms. Ultimately, this review conducts a comprehensive comparative analysis of different model organisms and further explores the combination of novel techniques with non-rodents. This review aims to summarize state-of-the-art findings in oxidative stress research using non-rodents and to delineate future directions.Keywords: Oxidative stress, Reactive oxygen species, Redox signaling pathway, Antioxidant mechanism, High-throughput screening, Non-rodent model organisms

Introduction

Oxidative stress, a crucial concept in redox biology, is customarily thought of as caused by a disequilibrium between reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and the antioxidant system [1,2]. However, this general and vague overview makes the term "oxidative stress" overused in a way that transcends redox biology [3], which is frequently mentioned as an explanation for most manifestations of organismal subhealth that are of remote connection. Subsequent research has shown that redox signaling, induced by oxidizing stimuli based on specific oxidants such as H2O2, has a critical regulatory role in maintaining normal cellular physiological activity [4]. Such ROS-induced physiological regulation is even more pronounced during the juvenile development of animals compared to the subsequent growing phase [5]. Recent cutting-edge research has progressively advanced the understanding of oxidative stress, aiming to differentiate between oxidative stress within the normal physiological range and excessive oxidative damage [6]. The former has become a hotspot of current research, while effectively and precisely mitigating the oxidative damage caused by the latter is also challenging. Presently, research on oxidative stress has entered a complex and multifaceted stage, underscoring the importance of selecting appropriate animal models for research.

Rodent models, commonly used as animal models in pharmaceutical research, are often criticized for high experimental costs and ethical concerns [7]. By contrast, non-rodent model organisms, including Danio rerio (zebrafish), Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), and Drosophila melanogaster (Drosophila), possess the strengths of small size, high reproductive rate, transparency, short life cycle, availability of real-time imaging, and ease of gene editing. Meanwhile, maintenance and experimental costs, as well as ethical concerns, are greatly minimized. In this context, using non-rodent models has improved the efficiency of various research in the field of oxidative stress. For instance, non-rodents preloaded with redox-sensitive fluorescent probes through a transparent carcass enable direct and rapidly in vivo ROS detection and real-time imaging [8]; specific expression of genetically encoded redox biosensors by gene editing in non-rodents enabled spatiotemporal monitoring of ROS [9]. In addition, redox proteomics enabled wide-scale detection of specific cysteine oxidation in non-rodents, thus further revealing the chemical nature of redox signaling regulation [10]. In addition, non-rodent model organisms are often used to evaluate antioxidant activity. Notably, high-throughput screening platforms can even be constructed based on non-rodents, thus significantly improving the screening efficiency of antioxidants in the future. In general, there is growing evidence that non-rodent model organisms are emerging as powerful tools for oxidative stress, of which the recent research frontiers urgently need to be reviewed.

This review focuses on commonly used and state-of-the-art techniques for detecting oxidative stress in non-rodent models and categorizes them with a new perspective. It presents their differences in the conservation of the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway and the progress of cutting-edge research on multiple regulations of the Nrf2-centered pathway. A critical discussion of non-rodents in evaluating antioxidant activity is also presented. Finally, three non-rodents are comprehensively analyzed, and the combination of novel techniques with non-rodents is given to improve research efficiency further. This review aims to summarize the current advances in the research of oxidative stress with non-rodents and to provide directions for future research.

Oxidative stress

The term "oxidative stress" is crucial in redox biology, evidenced by its substantial presence in over 20,000 entries on PubMed in recent years, a number that continues to grow. However, there has been a notable tendency toward its indiscriminate overuse, along with the similarly pervasive term "ROS." In the past few years, a series of landmark reviews in redox biology have been published to provide a systematic and critical review of the basic concepts and frontiers of the field. One notable example is a Consensus Statement published in 2022, which lays out foundational guidelines for various critical concepts in redox biology and rational research methodologies [11]. Therefore, this section commences with an updated introduction to oxidative stress, anchored on the content provided in the Consensus Statement and focusing mainly on non-rodent models.

Distinction of the concepts

In 1985, "oxidative stress" was defined as "a disturbance in the prooxidant-antioxidant balance in favor of the former". In contemporary research, "prooxidant" is often substituted with "ROS, " commonly perceived as a singular entity and frequently cast as the antagonist. However, with the advancing field of "redox signaling", there is a growing recognition that the traditional concept of "oxidative stress" requires refinement. Therefore, understanding "ROS" is crucial for comprehending "oxidative stress" in redox biology.

Reactive oxygen species

Indeed, "ROS" encompasses a broad spectrum of species, including free radicals and non-radicals, all derived from oxygen and significantly more reactive than oxygen. Importantly, these species exhibit diverse physicochemical properties such as half-life, subcellular localization, and migration distances. It is advisable to refer to specific species as "oxidants" rather than lumping them under the blanket term "ROS".

For instance, when targeting substances like methionine, different oxidants interact with distinct secondary rate constants, varying dramatically—up to 11 orders of magnitude in neutral pH water. These substantial differences result in varied roles for different oxidants within the body's physiological pathways. Among them, H2O2 has a relatively stable half-life, and its propagation distance of more than 1 μm is a standard distance between organelles. Interestingly, a specific aquaporin, peroxiporin, on the cell membrane can conduct H2O2 across the cell membrane. More importantly, the reactivity of H2O2 towards cysteines on specific proteins is far superior to that of proteins in general. Current research has shown that H2O2 can react with specific cysteines on target proteins by thiolating them to form sulfenate, which can go on to generate intramolecular or intermolecular disulfide bonds or glutathionylation [13]. Thus, H2O2 can alter the activity of target proteins using these specific thiol group modifications and act as a signaling agent. Interestingly, the two post-translational modifications mentioned above can be reduced to the thiolate state by thioredoxin (Trx) and glutaredoxin (Grx), respectively [14], and Trx and Grx themselves can be reduced again by the Trx system (containing the Trx reductase, the TrxR) and glutathione (GSH) system. The above may be the basis of the biological response of H2O2 to regulate redox signaling. In particular, it should be noted that reactions (6), (7), and (8) are the three ways in which H2O2 consumption is essential (with emphasis on the role of the enzyme) to maintain the homeostasis of H2O2 levels. From the above conclusions, it can also be inferred that if the intra- and extra-cellular balance of H2O2 is disrupted, for example, by a weakening of its sink mechanism, leading to excessively high levels of H2O2, which may consequently lead to its modification of erroneous protein targets and the generation of more potent oxidants, thus resulting in the disruption of various biomolecules. Normal levels of H2O2, on the other hand, act as normal signaling to maintain normal cellular function.

Oxidative eustress and oxidative distress

Understanding the distinction of ROS mentioned above, particularly the role of H2O2 signaling, provides a deeper insight into oxidative stress. Modern redox biology classifies oxidative stress into two categories: oxidative eustress and oxidative distress. The former denotes the physiological adaptation of the body's normal redox state, often called the "Golden Mean" or the "Goldilocks Zone" [6]. Maintaining a stable and normal cellular level of H2O2 is critical for preserving the redox balance within the optimal range, often called the "Golden Mean". Cells that can maintain this specific normal level of H2O2 are considered to be in a state of oxidative eustress. Conversely, cells that exceed a certain threshold of H2O2 enter a state of oxidative distress. Determining this threshold is a complex issue, given the variability of redox conditions among different cellular contexts. Sies outlined the relationship between intra- and extracellular concentrations of H2O2 and their corresponding cellular responses, thereby clarifying the subtle distinction between oxidative eustress and oxidative distress [15].

Why non-rodent models?

Previous distinctions between ROS and oxidative stress indicate that current research into oxidative stress remains challenging due to the intricate nature of the redox system in the human body. From the perspective of parsing the for resolving redox signaling, measuring specific oxidants is critical, especially H2O2. To some extent, the goal is to achieve precise quantitative, spatio-temporal, dynamic, tissue-specific, and cell-specific detection for ROS. Many current probes for detecting ROS rely on fluorescence visualization. The transparent bodies of zebrafish and C. elegans embryos/larvae make them excellent in vivo models for employing fluorescence imaging techniques (the availability of Drosophila is discussed in this review). It also saves much time when taking isolated tissues, cells, and organelles. Some common maneuvers to detect ROS in frozen sections or tissue homogenates push the results into error. Because disrupted membranes may lead to elevated levels of Fe2+, the resulting ROS production is far from the in vivo results, as shown in the reaction (9). For cellular models, commonly used cell culture conditions are characterized by relatively high O2 concentrations and lower antioxidants relative to in vivo. This results in the utilization of cultured cell models that produce more ROS compared to in vivo models. In addition, simple in vitro assays in test tubes make it difficult to simulate the sophisticated redox system in vivo, and finally, for rodent models (mice and rats), which also serve as in vivo models, the cost of laboratory culture and ethical concerns about rodent models cannot be ignored, in addition to the potentially complex and fallacious manipulations described above. Ethical concerns about rodent models cannot be ignored.

A prevalent case in research is the evaluation of the activity of antioxidants. Currently, commonly available methods involve both in vitro chemical assays and cellular or animal models. Multiple in vitro assays, generally classified as single electron transfer (SET) and hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), are systematically reviewed by Gulcin [16]. These assays are, to some degree, simple and time-saving. However, in vitro chemical assays can barely mimic the human body's internal physiological and dynamic environment. The differences in effective concentrations between in vitro and in vivo cannot be ignored. Cellular models also serve as test models for in vitro evaluation. Nevertheless, the variability of cellular models determines dosages of antioxidants and oxidative inducers, thus leading to an inferior standardization and uniformity of the evaluation methods and a possible lack of correlation between experimental results. Moreover, as the most commonly adopted rodent and non-human primate models, more troublesome problems become the obstacle, encompassing experimental ethics, significant expenditure, and long testing time [17,18]. Zebrafish, Drosophila, and C. elegans are systematically discussed in this review as they share strengths such as low cost, short life cycle, high genomic similarity, and potential for high-throughput screening for novel antioxidants.

Detection of oxidative stress in non-rodent models

Generalized ROS and redox detection

As indicated in Table 1, most ROS exhibit short lifespans, and their generation rates, reactions, and diffusion constantly fluctuate, leading to low steady-state levels. This dynamic nature poses a significant challenge for ROS detection. This challenge is further compounded in vivo, particularly in non-rodent systems, where the intricate redox signaling system should be considered holistically. Previous efforts, including electron spin resonance/electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR/ES) and LC-MS methods, have been used for ROS detection [19,20]. Here, we discuss the detection techniques compatible with the non-rodent model, which involves a variety of fluorophore-based visualization principles and, in particular, a variety of genetically encoded redox biosensors that can be assembled in systems where expression is possible.

Dihydrofluorescein

2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH), commonly recognized as a small-molecule fluorescent probe, is typically used in its diacetate form (DCFH-DA). This form allows it to enter cells quickly due to its attached diacetate moiety, which is later cleaved by cytosolic lipases. Once inside the cell, DCFH remains sequestered until it reacts with ROS, transforming into DCF and emitting intense fluorescence. Therefore, DCFH-DA is a fluorescent probe known for its efficient cell-penetrating properties.

DCFH-DA is widely utilized in zebrafish embryonic and larval models to detect ROS. In most cases, it is used to measure overall ROS levels rather than specific oxidants, providing a useful albeit less precise measurement for research where precise quantification of ROS levels is not the primary focus. For example, zebrafish, like Drosophila and C. elegans, are frequently employed in screening potential antioxidants, especially those derived from natural products. Moreover, due to their aquatic nature, zebrafish are commonly used in toxicological research concerning water pollution, investigating various potentially harmful substances affecting aquatic organisms, such as the recent research on triphenyltin [8].

DCFH-DA is widely used for ROS detection in C. elegans, which has been reviewed elsewhere [21]. It is important to note that the lysis of nematodes releases Fe2+, which can result in inaccurate detection of ROS. DCFH-DA is commonly employed to detect various tissues in Drosophila larvae, including the midgut, brain, and embryonic cells. This preference may stem from adult Drosophila tissues possessing complex structures and higher densities, which can lead to reduced transmittance and imaging quality. By contrast, the thinner walls of larvae and embryonic bodies mitigate these concerns [22,23].

Concerns about DCFH-DA were elucidated early on. DCFH-DA lacks assay specificity and can respond to a wide range of oxidants, including H2O2, HO•, and ONOO−, which differs from its initial claimed specificity in response to H2O2. More importantly, the intermediate step in the reaction of this probe also primarily involves the reaction of DCF radicals with O2, which can lead to subsequent H2O2 production, thereby affecting the measurements [24]. Overall, this technique must be implemented with careful attention (avoiding light), and the results obtained from DCFH-DA measurements must consider the effects of these limitations mentioned above.

CellROX®

CellROX® is a typical commercially available ROS detection probe. According to its construction guidelines, it was originally designed as a generalized ROS detection. Like the principle of DCFH-DA, CellROX® is cell-membrane permeable and produces bright and stable fluorescence after oxidation by ROS in the cell and remains in the cell. CellROX® is similar in principle to DCFH-DA. Nevertheless, according to the developer, CellROX® has more advantages over DCFH-DA. The prominent fact is that CellROX® is available in three color forms: CellROX® Green, CellROX® Orange, and CellROX® Deep Red. This extends the spectral range of ROS detection probes. After all, most current ROS assays are developed with green fluorescence, which can lead to an overlap of spectra when dual or multiple probes are required.

The oxidative stress status of non-rodent models can be revealed through CellROX®. For instance, zebrafish embryos were exposed to different concentrations of cisplatin for 96 h and then exposed to CellROX® Green. The results showed that most of the CellROX® fluorescence signals were localized to ionocytes compared to other skin cells [25]. This result may be related to ionocytes being enriched in mitochondria, where the electron transport chain (ETC) is the main ROS-generating site. In addition, CellROX® Deep Red has been used to investigate the ROS levels in C. elegans undergoing nutrient deprivation (starvation for 24 h). The results showed that ROS levels were significantly elevated in C. elegans with daf-12 or din-1 mutants after starvation. Meanwhile, well-fed and wild-type (WT) C. elegans starved for 24 h and showed comparable ROS levels. Nevertheless, well-fed mutants did not lead to a dramatic increase in ROS levels. These results suggest that antioxidant response may protect against nutrient deprivation in C. elegans [26]. In other cases involving nutritional supplements, it was claimed that cellular mitochondrial ROS levels were detected in C. elegans using CellROX® Green [[27], [28], [29]]. The body of Drosophila has a thick cuticle, but its compound eye structures or wings allow light to pass through. Researchers can utilize dissected Drosophila eye or wing discs for fluorescence detection, and both CellROX® and DCFH-DA have been used in this way in larval Drosophila [30,31].

Finally, as a commercially available fluorescent probe, CellROX® does not provide an exact molecular formula. However, some researchers have hypothesized that it may belong to the DCF-like family of fluorophores based on its excitation/emission spectra [32]. Moreover, there are some differences between CellROX® Green, CellROX® Orange, and CellROX® Deep Red, which can be found in commercially available information or partly reviewed by another research group [32].

Redox detection: roGFPs alone, Grx1-roGFP2, and novel sensors

Genetically encoded redox protein sensors are remarkable tools for ROS detection, especially when an expression system is present. Compared to the previous small molecule redox fluorescent probes, its outstanding advantage is that spatio-temporal control of sensor expression owing to the inducible promoter can be realized. Dynamic detection based on the reversibility under changing cellular redox status can also be realized. Zebrafish, C. elegans, and Drosophila are all excellent gene editing model animals that provide excellent expression systems for genetically encoded redox sensors, which are highly recommended in this review. Redox fluorescent protein (rxFP) essentially senses the state of oxidative stress in cells through the thiol/disulfide system, thereby changing the structure of the fluorescent protein and further changing the interaction between the chromophore and the surrounding environment [33]. roGFP is a commonly used redox sensor protein and the basis for modifying other new fluorescent proteins. Unlike the intensiometric rxYFP, roGFP is a ratiometric fluorescent probe, which can reduce the impact of differences in protein expression levels in different systems. Notably, roGFP alone or fused with non-peroxidase thiol exchange domains senses the redox state of the entire thiol pool of the cell rather than some specific oxidants [34]. This is the basis for roGFPs alone and Grx-roGFP2 to be classified in this section. The application of various genetically encoded redox sensor proteins, including roGFP2 and Grx1-roGFP2, was systematically reviewed by Breus and colleagues [35].

Recently, Hamre et al. made Grx1-roGFP expressed in zebrafish mitochondria or cytosol to investigate GSH: GSSG ratio changes during zebrafish embryogenesis. The results showed that the GSH: GSSG ratio was almost identical in different organs during zebrafish development [36]. Bazopoulou and colleagues used WT C. elegans expressing Grx1-roGFP2 to investigate the effect of ROS on redox states during early life. The results suggest a transient elevation of GSSG: GSH early in nematode life development and a switch in their redox states during adulthood [37]. Liu et al. constructed roGFP transgenic Drosophila bearing a mitochondrial or matrix target signal (MTS) (UAS-MTSroGFP2) and a cytoplasmic version (UAS-roGFPR12) based on the UAS-GAL4 system and verified the responsiveness of these redox protein sensors to redox changes after expression [38]. Nevertheless, targeting Grx1-roGFP2 to mitochondria in some non-mammalian organisms does not seem promising. Interestingly, Albrecht et al. reversed the domain order of Grx1-roGFP2 to generate roGFP2-Grx1 [39]. The latter one has better mitochondrial targeting ability and overcomes the limitation that Grx1-roGFP2 can only be targeted to mitochondria of mammalian model organisms, making it possible to observe the mitochondrial redox homeostasis of non-mammalian model organisms. Notably, the fly lines that express roGFP2-Grx1 are available as well [40]. In addition, the endoplasmic reticulum is also an important subcellular compartment for the target of genetically encoded biosensors, as redox homeostasis is strictly regulated during the formation of inter- and intra-molecular disulfide bonds during protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum [41]. Kirstein et al. used variants expressing roGFP and Hyper in the endoplasmic reticulum of C. elegans to detect redox status and H2O2 levels. The results showed that the endoplasmic reticulum of young nematodes presents an oxidizing environment, which gradually turns into a reducing state during aging [42]. Recently, researchers have developed a new redox-sensitive biosensor, Grx1-roCherry, based on red fluorescent protein, which can sensitively detect the change signal of 2GSH/GSSG in zebrafish tissues. However, Grx1-roCherry is an intensiometric biosensor, and its readouts depend on the expression level of the protein in the cell and lack quantitative properties [43].

As mentioned previously, roGFP is often designed into different cellular compartment versions. Notably, different subcellular compartments (endoplasmic reticulum, cytosol, and mitochondria) can have significantly different redox potentials. The midpoint potential of the protein sensor typically needs to be close to the steady-state redox potential of the cellular compartment, as they can be designed to fit this.

Specific/selective oxidants detection

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

Amplex Red®

As a commercially available fluorescent probe, Amplex Red® (N-Acetyl-3,7-dihydroxyphenoxazine) has an unconcealed chemical structure and reaction mechanism. Amplex Red® can act as a peroxidase substrate and react with H2O2 to generate highly fluorescent resorufin [44]. Notably, although some single electron oxidants can also oxidize resorufin, the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)/H2O2-mediated resorufin reaction is extremely efficient. Thus, this method is generally regarded as an ultrasensitive and specific detection of H2O2. In a notable case, Amplex Red® was used to quantify H2O2 in C. elegans, and the specific fluorescent response did not appear without the HRP substrate. In contrast, it was detected after the addition of HRP [45]. However, Amplex Red® is usually used to detect extracellular H2O2 levels. In contrast, when Amplex Red® is used to detect intracellular H2O2 levels, resorufin radicals may mediate the formation of O2•− and H2O2, confounding the results of the peroxide assay [46]. Therefore, Amplex Red® is commonly used to detect extracellular H2O2 levels or in isolated mitochondria. For instance, Amplex Red® detected ROS production in isolated mitochondria from different α1-subunits of voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs), including D-type, T-type, and cacophony in a previous Drosophila case [47]. In addition, based on the strong sensitivity of Amplex Red®/HRP to H2O2, this technology can be further extended and designed to detect different targets. For example, H2O2 is generated during the catalytic oxidation reaction of cholesterol oxidase. Based on this principle, a detection technology for cholesterol can be designed to convert cholesterol ester, the main form in the blood, into free cholesterol through cholesterol esterase, which is evidenced by research in zebrafish embryos [48].

MitoB

Boronate-based small molecule fluorescent probes show high selectivity to H2O2 [49], of which MitoB is a typical case. MitoB, or MitoBoronic acid, contains a lipophilic triphenylphosphonium cation moiety, which allows it to accumulate rapidly in the negatively charged mitochondrial matrix. The arylboronic moiety of MitoB can selectively react with H2O2 to generate MitoP (or MitoPhenol), a chemical with biological stability. Finally, LC-MS/MS is used to accurately detect the MitoP/MitoB ratio in the extracted sample, thereby revealing the H2O2 levels in the mitochondria [13,50]. Here, we focus on applying MitoB in Drosophila as the detailed protocol is provided by Cochemé and colleagues [51]. Similarly, they used MitoB to measure total mitochondrial H2O2 levels in Drosophila that increased with age and were not modulated by interventions that modulated lifespan [52]. In addition, one concern is that MitoB can also react with ONOO− to generate MitoP, and Drosophila does not express a NO synthase that generates NO. The research showed no clear sign of ONOO− production in Drosophila, and l-NAME, which is often used to inhibit NO synthase, did not affect the oxidation of MitoB [53]. Therefore, the detection results of MitoB in Drosophila can be considered to be an accurate reflection of H2O2 levels. In addition, the application of MitoB in C. elegans was completed by Cochemé colleagues as well [53].

roGFP2-Orp1

Genetically encoded biosensors can achieve the specific detection of H2O2 levels. To be specific, by coupling the redox sensor protein with the H2O2-sensitive thiol protein, genetically encoded protein sensors with sensitivity and specificity to H2O2 can be produced. When roGFP2 is coupled with peroxidases, such as Orp1 or Tsa2, roGFP2-Orp1 and roGFP2-Tsa2ΔCR can be obtained, respectively [54,55]. For example, roGFP2-Orp1 was generated by fusing roGFP2 with the yeast oxidant receptor peroxidase. This biosensor has been widely used in three non-rodent model organisms: zebrafish, fruit flies, and nematodes. For example, Panieri and colleagues used a zebrafish line expressing roGFP2-Orp1 in different cellular compartments to monitor oxidative stress. The results showed that in zebrafish endothelial cells, different subcellular compartments displayed different redox states under acute or gradual ROS elevation [56]. The same transgenic zebrafish line was subsequently used to study metabolic pathways and antioxidant responses, also by Panieri et al. [57]. In addition, Terzi et al. examined retinal ganglion cells expressing roGFP-Orp1, where dynamic levels of H2O2 could also be sensitively detected [58]. In addition, roGFP2-Orp1 and Grx1-roGFP2 were also expressed in Drosophila neurons to examine the relationship between redox state and Aβ neurotoxicity. The results showed that neuronal EGSH, not changes in H2O2 concentration, are associated with Aβ42-mediated neurotoxicity in vivo [59]. Detailed information on the use of roGFP-Orp1 to monitor H2O2 levels in Drosophila can be found in the work of Barata et al. [60]. Finally, studies expressing roGFP2-Orp1 in different subcellular compartments of the C. elegans germline support these results showing that spatial differences in H2O2 levels are present within the C. elegans germline under normal conditions [61].

HyPer

HyPer is constructed based on the insertion of cpFP into the redox active regulatory domain OxyR of E. coli [62]. Various HyPer series and roGFP2-based H2O2 biosensors have been implemented in zebrafish, which was systematically reviewed by Breus and colleagues [35]. It is worth noting that the latest version, HyPer7, is ultrasensitive, ultrafast, and pH-stable to H2O2. The researchers injected HyPer7 (H2O2-sensitive) and HyPer7-C121S (H2O2-insensitive) into zebrafish embryos at the one-cell stage and performed a tail fin amputation assay on 2 dpf zebrafish. The results showed that HyPer7 could display the H2O2 gradient at the wound site with a large signal intensity while the HyPer7-C121S did not, which confirmed the ultrasensitivity of HyPer7 for specific oxidation detection [9]. Conventional HyPer has been used to investigate the H2O2 levels in different growth stages of C. elegans [5]. By contrast, conditions with excessively high levels of H2O2 are unsuitable for using HyPer7 as these can be completely oxidized, whereas regular sensor proteins, such as HyPer1-3, roGFP-Orp1, and HyperRed, are recommended. For example, a transgenic zebrafish line expressing Hyper3 has been generated earlier and exhibits significantly faster redox dynamics compared to Hyper2, as well as a more extended dynamic range [63].

Superoxide anion (O2•−)

Hydroethidium and novel probes

Dihydroethidium (2,7-diamino-10-ethyl-9-phenyl-9,10-dihydrophenanthridine, DHE) is commonly used to detect O2•−. Its mitochondrial-targeted version, MitoSOX, incorporates an added triphenylphosphonium group. They have been used to detect O2•− in non-rodent species, including zebrafish [64,65], C. elegans [21,66], and Drosophila [67,68]. However, the shortcomings of DHE have been clarified. Another product of DHE oxidation, ethidium cation (E+), has similar properties to the red fluorescent product being detected, 2-hydroxyethidium (2-OH-E+), which produces spectral overlap and interferes with the result. Both oxidation products readily bind to DNA, again amplifying the fluorescent signal. Additionally, DHE is autoxidative and photosensitive, which is similar to DCFH-DA. NeoD and its mitochondria-targeted version, MitoNeoD, are chemically based on p-HE, which claims to possess more excellent selectivity for O2•− and does not intercalate into DNA [69]. The probe is currently being tested for in vivo O2•− detection in rodents at the time of development and can be subsequently tested in non-rodent models.

In addition, HKSOX-1 and its coupled dimethyl iminodiacetate version HKSOX-1r to enhance its cellular uptake and retention, as well as the mitochondria-targeted version HKSOX-1m, are three of the novel O2•− detection probes currently available. After exposure 72 h post-fertilization (hpf), zebrafish larvae preloaded with HKSOX-1r to phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) as well as antimycin A, bright green fluorescence was captured by confocal microscopy in both cases, suggesting that HKSOX-1r is capable of effectively detecting the generation of O2•− in vivo [70].

Another novel "turn-on" type, the two-photon fluorescent probe 6-(dimethylamino) quinoline-2-benzothiazoline (HQ), has been developed to track O2•− in organisms. It was reported that 72 hpf zebrafish (preloaded with HQ) exposed to PMA and 2-Methoxyestradiol (2-ME) could also show strong green fluorescence. This demonstrates the sensitive detection of O2•− by HQ in zebrafish [71].

It is worth noting that HKSOX-1r and HQ induced O2•− production in zebrafish when assayed in vivo by exogenous chemical induction, such as adding PMA as described above. A similar approach does not seem to induce O2•− production sustainably, potentially making the fluorescent probe assay results misleading. Non-rodent models are excellent tools for expression systems, and genetically encoded oxidant generators are a more recommended option, e.g., KillerRed [72], killerOrange [73], SuperNova [74], and miniSOG [75]. They enable in vivo site-specific O2•− induction compared to exogenous chemical induction. KillerRed has been expressed in zebrafish and nematodes to induce O2•− production [[76], [77], [78]].

It is worth noting that in addition to the above common biosensors, additional genetically encoded biosensors are used to detect ROS or redox status. In addition to GSH:GSSG mentioned above, NADPH/NADP+ or NADH/NAD+ are also common biomarkers used to detect redox status, which is summarized by Zou et al. [79]. In their work, genetically encoded biosensors with different spectral properties, including iNap1, SoNar, and HyperRed, were expressed in combination in zebrafish models to detect NADPH/NADP+ or NADH/NAD+ levels, as well as H2O2. In addition, roGFP variants constructed by replacing positively charged amino acids near the disulfide bond in roGFP1 can improve its sensitivity to the redox potential of the surrounding environment [80]. One of these variants, roGFP1-R12, has been expressed in C. elegans to detect in vivo redox status [81]. Finally, Radhakrishnan et al. designed rogRFP2, an improved red emission for quantitative live-cell imaging, by using roGFP2 as a Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) donor and screening a library of red fluorescent protein acceptors and linkers. It has a wider signal dynamic range than the currently commonly used red fluorescent redox sensors. By expressing rogRFP2 in Drosophila, a readily detectable ratiometric signal was obtained in the diamide-exposed adult fruit fly retina compared to the dithiothreitol-exposed group [82].

On the other hand, non-rodents are not the only expression carrier of genetically encoded redox sensors. Rodent models (mice) that can be used to report redox status have also been developed. For example, transgenic mice models expressing Grx1-roGFP2 in the mitochondria and cytosol of cardiac myocytes showed distinct EGSH signal intensities in different subcellular compartments. Different drug treatments in cardiac myocytes also resulted in independent compartment responses [83]. Wendt et al. used intracranial AAV (adeno-associated virus) deliveries to induce the expression of roGFP1 or rogRFP2 in the cortex of adult mice [84]. More cases on the expression of genetically encoded redox sensors in mice models are reviewed by Marta et al. [85]. The more complex physiological system of mice, compared to that of the three non-rodent species, offers several advantages for studying genetically encoded redox sensors. This includes the expression of roGFP1 in various organs such as the brain, placenta, lungs, liver, kidneys, pancreas, and skin of mice [86]. This broader detection range of redox sensors in mice, compared to non-rodent models with simpler physiological structures, enhances their utility in experimental settings. Additionally, when using redox biosensors to detect oxidative stress in disease models, mice offer superior disease modeling capabilities, resulting in more accurate and reliable findings. While mice lack the transparent optical properties of larval zebrafish, Drosophila, or C. elegans, frozen sections are typically used for subsequent fluorescence imaging of tissues expressing redox sensors. Notably, during the freezing and sectioning of mice, tissues can be treated with the fast-acting membrane-permeable alkylating agent N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) to 'lock' the biosensor molecule in its reduced state [87]. This approach is crucial for future studies utilizing mouse models to express redox biosensors. However, a common limitation arises when plasmids are microinjected into nuclei to generate transgenic mice: some mice exhibit mosaicism in the germline, resulting in heterogeneity among individual biosensor-expressing cells [85]. This variability poses a challenge for the consistent use of redox biosensors in mouse models.

Ultimately, genetically encoded redox sensors are expected to be applied to human studies. A common approach involves the use of human cell lines expressing redox biosensors, such as the widely used HEK293 or HeLa cell lines [[88], [89], [90]]. Notably, Trautsch et al. developed a protocol for expressing Grx-roGFP2 in human embryonic stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes and engineered human myocardium [91]. This research was the first to demonstrate the feasibility of cell-type-specific GSH/GSSG imaging using genetically encoded redox sensors in human heart muscle. These findings hold significant potential for future organ- or tissue-specific redox status detection in human research.

Oxidation of biomacromolecules

Biomarkers

The fluorescent probes and genetically encoded redox biosensors discussed earlier offer direct detection capabilities for ROS to a certain extent. Indirect determination of ROS presence can also be achieved through their impact on various biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, and DNA. These biomolecules undergo oxidation, producing detectable reactants commonly referred to as biomarkers. When cellular ROS levels exceed a threshold, biomolecules may undergo irreversible oxidative modifications, such as protein carbonylation, which can impair normal protein functions. Consequently, biomarkers obtained from these measurements can indicate the extent of oxidative damage to cells to some degree. Table 2 represents the detection of common oxidative damage biomarkers using the three non-ergodic models and the corresponding methods. Notably, the degree of carbonylation content of proteins, which are the main targets of oxidative damage attack by ROS, is often used as a biomarker for detection. The most common method is to use the reaction with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) to produce a stable 2,4-dinitrophenyl (DNP) hydrazone, which is then detected spectrophotometrically or by immunoblotting (OxyBlot detection kit). An alternative CyDyeTM-hydrazide-based technology, tested in C. elegans [92], claims to have superior detection performance to traditional OxyBlot. In addition, the choice of antibody is crucial for both ELISA and immunostaining. For example, a newly designed antibody has been reported to detect 8-OHdG in a compassionate and specific manner, which functions in both cells and tissues [93]. To detect similar biomarkers, it is vital to address the low specificity, cross-activity, and background activity of antibodies [11].

Moreover, these biomarkers are frequently employed to assess the correlation between oxidative damage and related diseases. For instance, protein carbonylation has been linked to various diseases such as neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, multiple sclerosis, diabetes mellitus, sepsis, rheumatoid arthritis, and obesity. Similarly, 8-OHdG, associated with oxidative DNA damage, has been implicated in several diseases, particularly cancer [34,111]. According to the 2022 consensus statement, the measured level of biomarkers represents a balance between the production and removal of biomarkers [11]. These biomarkers are recognized as suitable indicators for evaluating antioxidant activity because they directly mirror the outcomes of oxidative damage. However, it is crucial to consider elevated levels of oxidative damage resulting from sample isolation or analytical procedures. This consideration is particularly important when working with non-rodent species.

Protein cysteine oxidation

As mentioned in section 2.1.1, oxidation of specific Cys residues on specific proteins (by H2O2) is an important chemical mechanism of redox signaling. The particularity of redox-sensitive cysteine is reflected in its unique chemical properties: cysteine is easily oxidized, especially its deprotonated form thiolate (RS-), a massively active nucleophile. Chemical mechanism of redox signaling based on cysteine oxidation. GSH: Glutathione; Grx: Glutaredoxin; Trx: Thioredoxin.

These specific cysteine residues possess unique characteristics. Unlike cysteines buried within the protein structure, exposed cysteines are known to be more involved in transmitting redox signaling. Intriguingly, partially exposed cysteines can be oxidation targets, shielding adjacent crucial amino acids from oxidation. It suggests that clustered cysteines are more prone to oxidation. Furthermore, the reduction potential and the presence of nearby charged amino acids can influence the redox sensitivity of these cysteine residues [112].

It is essential to find those oxidized proteins and identify their crucial cysteine. One effort is to use redox proteomics technology. Earlier, OxICAT was developed based on the combination of isotope-coded affinity tag (ICAT) technology and differential thiol trapping technique. It can accurately quantify the percentage of cysteine oxidation in many proteins while capturing those key redox-sensitive cysteine residues [113]. Based on OxICAT, the researchers measured the thiol redox status of approximately 140 different proteins at different time points in the life cycle of C. elegans. The results showed that the thiol oxidation level would gradually increase in adult C. elegans (WT). On the 15th day, the oxidation status of the aged worm was already significantly higher than the level on the second day. In addition, the protein oxidation level of L4 larval worms during the developmental period was also significantly increased and then decreased sharply on the second day of adulthood [5]. These results suggested that C. elegans experiences increased oxidative stress activity during development and aging. Interestingly, subsequent research in Drosophila revealed that no significant changes in thiol redox state were detected during aging, but rather that fasting had a significant effect [114]. This result seems to challenge the free radical and aging theory previously shown in the nematode model and more research needs to be invested in revealing the difference in the aging mechanisms of these two model organisms. Recently, the cysteine proteome of C. elegans was globally investigated through proteomics. Specifically, the activity of three different redox forms of cysteine (reduced, sulfenylated, and sulfinylated cysteines) was investigated after H2O2 exposure. Changes in the redox status of critical cysteines are essential for regulating stress response pathways. The results indicated that the P38 MAPK signaling pathway can regulate the oxidative stress response of C. elegans to a large extent. Two key cysteine residues of ROS-sensing proteins in the p38 MAPK pathway have been identified: the transmembrane ER kinase/RNase IRE-1 (Cys663) and the MAPKKK NSY-1/ASK1 (Cys364 and Cys905). The redox-sensitive cysteine residues of SEK1 and PMK1, two downstream proteins in the pathway, are located at Cys213 and Cys173, respectively [10].

The other important issue is how H2O2 selectively and efficiently oxidizes redox-sensitive cysteine residues. Increasing evidence has demonstrated the indirect electron transfer function of peroxiredoxin (Prx), called the “redox relay” [115,116]. Recent research has shown that a Drosophila cytoplasmic Prx, Jafrac1, can act as an H2O2 sensor, temporarily oxidize, and then transmit the oxidation signal to Mekk1. A similar redox relay also occurs in human cells where the interaction happens between MEKK4 (the homolog of Mekk1) and Prx2 [116]. After appropriate exercise, the latest redox proteomics results indicated that cysteine's redox status significantly differs in prdx-2 mutants and wild-type C. elegans. This conservative cytoplasmic Prx2 plays a key role in the beneficial adaptive response of C. elegans to physiological redox stress [117].

Another prominent cysteine-rich protein, Keap1, is mentioned here due to its crucial role in regulating redox homeostasis via interaction with Nrf2. Yamamoto et al. systematically reviewed the research of various key cysteines in Keap1 [118]. In the next section, we focus on conserving the Keap1-Nrf2 system in different model organisms.

Antioxidant mechanisms in non-rodent models

Faced with the potential oxidative stress risks in nature (toxic chemicals, ionizing radiation, ultraviolet light), animals must develop antioxidant systems to maintain oxidative homeostasis. In this case, animals have inherited certain antioxidant mechanisms from their ancestors to resist the risks. For example, mammals inherited the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1-NF-E2-related factor 2 (Keap1-Nrf2) mechanism. Zebrafish, C. elegans, and Drosophila have a high degree of genetic homology with humans and have evolved conservatively in many key metabolic signaling pathways, especially the antioxidant one. Over the past two decades, researchers have identified homologs of Keap1 and Nrf2 in these three non-rodent species. Differences in antioxidant mechanisms across various animal species have been gradually investigated. These advancements leverage the superior research benefits provided by three non-rodent species. Apart from their high gene homology advantages, these species are characterized by small size, simple structure, and fully resolved genomes, facilitating efficient gene editing operations. By integrating a suite of loss-of-function (LOF) tools, alterations in animal phenotypes or physiological and biochemical levels can be swiftly identified. Meanwhile, the transparent characteristics of the whole body provide a basis for the intervention of various fluorescent technologies to efficiently detect gene expression levels or key protein nuclear translocation, significantly improving the research efficiency of antioxidant mechanisms.

There are differences in the "Keap1-Nrf2" antioxidant signaling pathway because of ortholog (due to species formation) and paralog (due to gene duplication) between the three species of ungulates as well as mammals. This provides an obstacle to the extrapolation of the research results obtained in zebrafish, C. elegans, and Drosophila to human beings, and it is therefore necessary to organize the differences in Nrf2 biology between species.

Distinction of Nrf2 in different non-rodent models

Nrf2 (encoded by the Nfe2l2 gene) is an essential member of the mammalian Cap’n’collar (Cnc) basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors family. Other members include Nrf1 (encoded by Nfe2l1), Nrf3 (encoded by Nfe2l3), and Bach1 and Bach2 (transcriptional repressors). Among them, the role of Nrf2 in mammals has been investigated thoroughly. As a multifunctional transcription factor, the functions of Nrf2 include but are not limited to energy metabolism, iron metabolism, amino acid metabolism, DNA repair, and stem cell regulation, and the most important function is to regulate redox balance [119,120]. Mammalian Nrf2 contains 7 functional domains. Among them, the role of Neh2 is to bind to the Keap1 Kelch-like structure since its N-terminus and C-terminus have two important motifs, DLG (low affinity) and ETGE (high affinity), respectively. It also includes a lysine-rich α-helix for Keap1-dependent polyubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2. Interestingly, it was found that the distance between the DLG and ETGE motifs in the Nrf2 Neh2 domain of various vertebrates is highly conserved (45–47 amino acids) [121], which may be consistent with the theory that Nrf2 and Keap1 dimers bind in a hinge and latch model. Neh4 and Neh5 domains contain CREB binding protein binding sites, which play a role in transcriptional activation [122]. Neh7 can bind to retinoid acid receptor alpha (RARα). The N-terminus of mammalian Neh6 contains a DSGIS motif, which can be phosphorylated by glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3) to recruit the E3 ubiquitin ligase adaptor, β-transducin repeat-containing protein (β-TrCP), thereby achieving subsequent degradation. In addition, another DSAPGS motif on the Neh6 domain can recruit β-TrCP but does not require the assistance of GSK-3. The Neh3 domain at the C-terminus of Nrf2 is the transactivation domain. The CNC domain and basic region in the Neh1 domain play a role in binding to DNA, and a leucine zipper region is used to dimerize with small Maf (sMaf) proteins. It is noteworthy that the CNC domain and basic region are highly conserved in multiple animals (mammals, zebrafish, Drosophila, and C. elegans) compared to other domains in Nrf2. Nrf1, a member of the same family as Nrf2, regulates oxidative stress responses through similar pathways, primarily due to their highly comparable domain structures. However, unlike Nrf2, which is localized in the cytosol under normal conditions, the N-terminal endoplasmic reticulum binding region of Nrf1 anchors it to the ER membrane. Notably, Nrf1 and Nrf2, along with Nrf3 and NF-E2, comprise the four Nrf genes specific to vertebrates. In zebrafish, which are also vertebrates, two highly conserved Nrf1 homologs, Nrf1a and Nrf1b, exist. The distinction between them lies in the fact that Nrf1a contains a conserved ER binding domain but lacks the conserved DLG and ETGE motifs, while Nrf1b exhibits the opposite characteristics [121].

Kobayashi and colleagues have made a groundbreaking contribution to Nrf2 biology in zebrafish. Compared with C. elegans and Drosophila, the homolog of mammalian Nrf2 in zebrafish is significantly more conserved. It was initially believed that the nrf2 gene encoded Nrf2 in zebrafish. Still, recent research has found a new paralog of Nrf2 in zebrafish [123], indicating that both Nrf2a (encoded by nrf2a) and Nrf2b (encoded by nrf2b) exist in zebrafish. Subsequent research has shown that nrf2a and nrf2b in zebrafish are co-orthologs of human Nfe2l2 [124]. The researchers used morpholino-modified anti-sense oligonucleotides (MOs) to perform Nrf2a/Nrf2b knockout experiments in zebrafish and found that the genes regulated by the two genes were highly dissimilar, with only minimal overlap [124]. In comparing the two domain structures, Nrf2a exhibits greater conservation, whereas Nrf2b lacks the transcription activation motif within the Neh4 domain entirely, and the phosphorylation site within Neh6 shows very low conservation [121]. The function of zebrafish Nrf2b may not be conserved with that of mammalian Nrf2. It has a negative regulatory effect on some p53 and heme oxygenase 1. Research has shown that Nrf2a is essential in regulating oxidative homeostasis in the late development of zebrafish [125]. It is effective to investigate pleiotropic transcription factors such as Nrf2 with LOF methods. In addition to the MO mentioned above, TILLING (targeting induced local lesions in genomes) has been used to achieve the point mutant nfe212afh318 of zebrafish Nrf2a [126]. The exposure to ten-spice phytochemicals was followed by detecting changes in antioxidant activity, thereby characterizing the role of Nrf2 [127]. However, the residual Nrf2 activity in the nfe212afh318 mutant larvae used in the above research may affect the experiment, which leads to more profound suspicion of the results. The solution is that CRISPR/Cas9 can be employed to construct a null mutant of zebrafish larvae Nrf2, and one example is the Nrf2 frameshift mutant line nfe212ait321 [128]. In this case, the Nrf2 gene was effectively knocked out to investigate the antioxidant mechanism of soy-derived equol, and the results indicated that the antioxidant activity of equol was Nrf2-independent.

McGinnis and colleagues have made a groundbreaking contribution to the research of the homolog of Nrf2 in Drosophila. The three isoforms of Cnc discovered earlier, CncA, CncB, and CncC, are characterized by including progressively more N-terminal sequences. Later research also found more Cnc variants based on this trend. Nevertheless, CncC is the transcript variant that regulates oxidative homeostasis. Compared with Skn-1 in C. elegans, the conservation of Drosophila CncC is higher but lower than that of zebrafish Nrf2a. From the domain structure of CncC, it has highly conserved DLG and ETGE motifs (100 aa apart) to achieve binding with Drosophila Keap1 (dKeap1, the ortholog of human Keap1) dimer. However, CncC possesses an endoplasmic reticulum binding N-terminal homology box 1 (NHB1), entirely different from mammalian and zebrafish Nrf2, which lacks the ER binding motif.

Skinhead-1(Skn-1) is an ortholog of Nrf2 in mammals, which plays a crucial role in regulating oxidative homeostasis in C. elegans. Skn-1 is less conserved compared with zebrafish Nrf2 and Drosophila CncC. To be specific, neither of the two isoforms of Skn-1 (Skn-1a and Skn-1c) has the conserved ETGE and DLG motifs nor the leucine zipper domain, which means that Skn-1 cannot form a dimer with sMaf but binds to DNA as a Skn-1 monomer.

Distinction of “Keap1” in different non-rodent models

The domain structure of the mammalian Keap1 protein has been entirely resolved. In addition to the N-terminal region (NTR) and C-terminal region (CTR), Keap1 also includes three functional domains. Among them, the broad complex-tramtrack-bric a brac (BTB) domain can achieve self-homodimerization, thereby binding to Cullin3 (Cul3) and recruiting E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, causing ubiquitination of Nrf2 and subsequent degradation (through 26s proteasome). The BTB domain has a Cys151, which is critical for the activity of Keap1 under oxidative conditions. Double glycine repeats (DGR) as Kelch repeat domain bind to Nrf, which can be achieved by the two Kelchs of Keap1 homodimer binding to the DLG and ETGE motifs of Nrf2, respectively. Nrf2 activation can be achieved through the separation of this motif from Keap1. The current mainstream activation mechanism is through the hinge and latch model. The low-affinity DLG (latch) separates from Kelch, while the high-affinity ETGE (hinge) and Kelch always remain bound. This hypothesis has been supported by NMR spectroscopy titration experiments [129]. Recent research employing a mathematical model based on Nrf2's half-life, maximal activation, and response steepness to investigate the Keap1-Nrf2 system quantitatively has demonstrated that Nrf2 activation can occur without the dissociation of DLG [130], which differs from the hinge and latch model. Finally, the intervening region (IVR) combines the BTB with the DGR. Meanwhile, Cys273 and Cys288 on the IVR are both critical sensor cysteines (including the Cys151).

Keap1 is characterized as a typical cysteine-rich protein, and differences in homologs between species primarily manifest in the cysteine residues within each domain. Keap1a and Keap1b in zebrafish are co-orthologs of Keap1. The former lacks Cys273, while the latter lacks Cys288. In recent research, Koboyashi's team found that zebrafish with Keap1b knockout (keap1bit308) can still grow normally since the remaining Keap1a can inhibit Nrf2 activity. Recently, Koboyashi's team constructed zebrafish with Keap1a knockout (keap1ait302) and created homozygous Keap1a/Keap1b double knockout zebrafish through hybridization [131]. In this case, Nrf2 is overexpressed in the zebrafish, and feeding defects and death (one week) occur. Back to the original Keap1a and Keap1b, having one of the two (carrying only one sensor cystine) seems sufficient for fish Keap1 to function. However, a subtle distinction exists between the two. Keap1a is unique to fish and certain amphibians, whereas Keap1b is a vestigial paralog found in mammals, characterized by a lower affinity for Cul3 [132]. In essence, this condition facilitates easier induction of Nrf2 under Keap1b sequestration, thereby promoting antioxidant effects. In recent research, Yumimoto and colleagues engineered mutations in mammalian Keap1 to resemble Keap1a (resulting in weaker Nrf2 induction) observed in zebrafish. Interestingly, mice carrying these mutations perished upon exposure to sunlight-level ultraviolet radiation [133].

Drosophila Keap1 (dKeap1) serves as the homolog of Keap1, sharing similar functions and domain structures. Like its counterpart, dKeap1 comprises three functional domains: BTB, IVR, and DGR. However, a notable distinction lies in the absence of several cysteine residues in dKeap1, including Cys226, Cys241, Cys249, Cys257, Cys319, Cys368, and Cys395 [134]. However, the two sensor cysteines in the IVR domain, Cys273 and Cys288, are conserved in dKeap1, which ensures its normal function [121]. In addition, it is noteworthy that dKeap1 also contains a long C-terminal tail, which mediates its nuclear import and chromatin binding [135].

Compared to mammals, zebrafish, and Drosophila, C. elegans stands out as unique regarding the "Keap1" protein because it lacks a true ortholog of Keap1 [121]. However, in C. elegans, there exists a negative regulatory system analogous to Keap1-Nrf2, which involves WD40 repeat protein-23 (WDR-23) recruiting the cullin 4/damaged DNA binding protein 1 (CUL4/DDB1) complex. This recruitment leads to the proteasomal degradation of Skn-1, detailed in section 4.3.

Basic “Keap1-Nrf2” system in different organisms

In mammals, Nrf2 binds to Kelch on the Keap1 homodimer through the DLG and ETGE motifs on Neh2, a negative regulatory mechanism that aims to prevent overactivation of Nrf2. As mentioned, Keap1KO (Nrf2 overexpression) mice exhibit neonatal lethality and feeding defects. Another case is the tumor cells where Nrf2 is persistently activated, leading to therapeutic resistance [136]. Under normal conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytosol to prevent its nuclear translocation and over-activation. This negative regulation recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex by binding the Keap1 BTB domain to Cul3, promoting the ubiquitination of Nrf2, which the 26S proteasome will degrade. However, when cells are under oxidative stress stimuli, multiple cysteine thiols of Keap1 (especially sensor cysteines) become interaction sites, leading to changes in Keap1 conformation. In this context, Nrf2 translocates into the nucleus, binds to small musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma (small Mafs, sMaf) (MafK, MafF, MafG in mammals), activates antioxidant response elements (AREs), and increases the expression of phase Ⅱ detoxification genes, such as NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) gene, glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) gene, and UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs) gene.

The "Keap1-Nrf2" system in zebrafish is highly conserved, and its primary oxidative homeostasis regulatory mechanism is almost the same as that of the mammals mentioned above. A slight difference is that fish has a special sMaf-MafT, a possible ortholog of mammalian MafF. Meanwhile, zebrafish also have MafG and MafK. In addition, like mammals, the Nrf2/AREs/sMaf system of zebrafish will only activate the expression of phase Ⅱ detoxification genes, among which representative ones are gstp1, gclc, and nqo1.

The mechanism by which the dKeap1-CncC system regulates oxidative homeostasis remains conserved in Drosophila. The function of Maf-S in Drosophila has been identified, and it binds to the CncC ZIP domain to form a dimer and activate ARE transcription. Unlike mammals and zebrafish, Drosophila CncC regulates detoxification genes at all phases, including phases Ⅰ, Ⅱ, and Ⅲ (which is reviewed by Loboda et al. [134]).

Compared with mammals, zebrafish, and Drosophila, the regulatory mechanism of oxidative homeostasis in C. elegans is still the most unique. Due to the lack of a leucine zipper domain, Skn-1 does not form a dimer with sMaf as in mammals but activates transcription as a monomer by itself. Like Drosophila, C. elegans Skn-1 can regulate detoxification genes at all stages. In addition, due to the lack of keap1 ortholog, the negative regulation of Skn-1 can recruit Cul4/DDB-1ubiquitin ligase through WDR-23 to promote its proteasomal degradation, thereby achieving a similar effect to Keap1. However, this negative regulation mechanism is carried out in the nucleus and applies to non-oxidative stress conditions. Under oxidative stress conditions, Skn-1 activity must be accumulated to achieve antioxidant effects, which involves multiple other signaling pathways to co-regulate Skn-1 activity, detailed in section 4.4.4.

Nrf2-centered system and interplay with other regulatory pathways

Multiple regulation of Nrf2-centered pathway

Many regulatory pathways can interplay with the Nrf2-centered system, primarily falling into two categories. One category involves direct regulation of Nrf2 transcriptional activity, while the other involves modulation of Keap1, thereby indirectly influencing Nrf2 transcriptional activity. Phosphorylation serves as a significant mechanism for regulating Nrf2 activity. As discussed in section 4.1, the DSGIS motif at the N-terminal of Neh6 of Nrf2 is significant for GSK-3β-dependent phosphorylation and subsequent ubiquitination by β-TrCP. In other words, GSK-3β-mediated phosphorylation of Nrf2 inhibits its transcriptional activity. The PI3K/Akt pathway can negatively regulate GSK-3β, indirectly increasing Nrf2 activity [137]. Once activated, GSK-3β phosphorylates Fyn kinase, a Src family member, at threonine residues, leading to its nuclear accumulation, favoring Nrf2 nuclear export [138]. Earlier research showed that AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) can also directly phosphorylate Nrf2 at Ser558 residue (Ser550 in mice) inserted in the nuclear export signal, favoring Nrf2 nuclear accumulation [139]. Recent research has shown that AMPK can phosphorylate and activate Nrf2 at three sites, Ser374, Ser408 and Ser433, and can regulate the transcription of Nrf2-specific target genes (HMOX1, Akr1c14) [140]. In addition, protein kinase C (PKC) can directly phosphorylate the Nrf2 Neh2 domain Ser40 site, thereby increasing the nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity of Nrf2. Recent research showed this transcriptional activity is associated with c-Src kinase activation [141]. Casein kinase II (CK2) can phosphorylate Neh4 and Neh5 of Nrf2 and increase the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 [142]. P38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) can phosphorylate Ser215, Ser408, and Ser577 of Nrf2 to reduce its nuclear accumulation, which also plays a negative regulatory role [143]. Recent research has shown that Sirt1, an important multifunctional regulatory factor in Sirtuins, can deacetylate and reduce ubiquitination levels of Nrf2, activating the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway. Interestingly, Nrf2 also regulated Sirt1 at protein expression and deacetylase activity levels, forming a positive feedback loop [144]. Recently, p97, a ubiquitin-targeted ATP-dependent segregase that regulates proteostasis, was shown to negatively regulate Nrf2 by extracting ubiquitinated Nrf2 from the Keap1-Cul3-Rbx1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex and promoting its proteasomal degradation [145]. Interestingly, Nrf2 and p97 can form a negative feedback loop, thereby achieving bidirectional negative regulation of Nrf2 [146].

The second synergistic effect is to regulate Keap1 to indirectly regulate the transcriptional activity of Nrf2. In the canonical NF-κB pathway, an inhibitor of κB (IκB) kinase (IKK) phosphorylates IκB protein (IκBα), thereby promoting its ubiquitination and degradation by the proteasome. As a result, P65/P50 (NF-κB) originally bound by IκBα can undergo nuclear translocation and activate transcription [147]. The binding between Keap1 and IKK can be similar to that between Keap1 and Nrf2, inducing IKK ubiquitination and degradation. Thus, Nrf2 and IKK compete with Keap1 for binding. In addition, Neh4,5, as mentioned in section 4.1, can bind to CBP as a transcriptional co-activator. CBP can also serve as a transcriptional co-activator of NF-κB. As a result, the two again produce competitive binding on CBP binding [148]. Therefore, the NF-κB and Keap1-Nrf2 pathways can also produce crosstalk. Finally, the autophagy pathway mediated by SQSTM1/P62 can also interplay with the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway. Phosphorylated p62 (Ser407 and Ser403) can localize to the ubiquitinated cargo, and then p62 is phosphorylated again (Ser349), thereby binding to Keap1 and inducing its degradation by autophagy [149,150]. As a result, the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 is increased.

In addition, as discussed in the previous section, persistent high expression of Nrf2 is common in tumor cells. Keap1 deficiency can lead to overexpression of Nrf2, resulting in subsequent lethality in animal models. Ong et al. also observed a similar phenomenon. Interestingly, their research also showed that the loss of Keap1 causes transcription factor EB (TFEB)/transcription factor binding to IGHM Enhancer 3 (TFE3)-dependent lysosomal biogenesis. These results indicate that the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway plays a regulatory role in lysosomal biogenesis [158]. Drosophila

The Drosophila CncC Neh6 domain has a DSAVS motif similar to the mammalian DSGIS, which provides a phosphorylation site for GSK-3. However, unlike mammals, Drosophila does not have the conserved DSAPGS motif, of which the phosphorylation is GSK-3-independent. In general, the Drosophila GSK-3 ortholog, Shaggy (Sgg), and the β-TrCP ortholog, slimb (Slmb), may regulate the transcriptional activity of CncC through phosphorylation and subsequent ubiquitination degradation, thereby achieving the regulation of oxidative homeostasis. In addition, Slmb/β-TrCP can regulate other oxidative stress response genes, such as apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1)/Ask1, a redox-sensitive serine/threonine kinase that regulates oxidative homeostasis. Interestingly, Lithium can improve the stress resistance of Drosophila by inhibiting GSK-3 and activating Nrf2 while also improving its lifespan and health span, showing its potential as a drug that prolongs lifespan [160]. Lithium is an essential GSK-3 inhibitor drug, and its pharmacological mechanism is to inhibit the activity of GSK-3 (GSK-3β′s inhibitory phosphorylation at Ser9) by competing with Mg2+ as an inhibitor [161,162].

The homolog of SQSTM1/P62 in Drosophila is ref(2)P, the only known autophagy receptor for ubiquitinated proteins in Drosophila. In the autophagy pathway, P62-type selective autophagy receptors (SARs) and poly-ubiquitinated cargo, autophagosomal LC3/Atg8 proteins are cross-linked and degraded in lysosomes. SARs recognize and bind to LC3 through their LC3-interacting region (LIR). In a recent research, Bhattacharjee et al. generated a Drosophila ref(2)PLIRm stock by using CRISPR-Cas9 homology-directed repair to replace the endogenous LIR, thereby disrupting the binding of ref(2)P/SQSTM1 to Atg8a, leading to its accumulation. However, excessive accumulation of aggregates did not affect the health and lifespan of Drosophila, as the accumulated ref(2)P/SQSTM1 bound to Keap1, thereby activating the CncC pathway and enhancing the flies’ ability to resist oxidative stress [163]. This result is interesting since it suggests that an aggregation-induced antioxidant response can compensate for the potentially harmful effects of impaired autophagy of ubiquitinated proteins.

Fs(1)h, the only member of the Drosophila BET family of proteins (bromodomain-containing), was found to be a negative regulator of CncC in Drosophila, which can prevent the activation of target genes by interacting with acetylated lysines in CncC. Importantly, this negative regulatory effect is independent of Keap1. Keap1 and Fs(1)h inhibitors can be used synergistically to promote the transcriptional activity of Nrf2 and improve the antioxidant capacity of Drosophila [164].

C. elegans

An important way that C. elegans regulates oxidative homeostasis is through the insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling (IIS) pathway and the Skn-1/Nrf2 system [[165], [166], [167]], which is illustrated in Fig. 8. Among them, WDR-23, mammalian DDB1-CUL4 associated factor 11 (DCAF11)/WDR23, is considered a negative regulator of Skn-1. However, this important cullin-RING ligase (CRL) substrate receptor has two spatially distinct isoforms, a cytoplasmic WDR-23A and nuclear WDR-23B. Recently, the two isoforms were shown to have opposite regulatory effects on Skn-1. WDR-23A can activate Skn-1 activity in the cytoplasm, while WDR-23B induces the ubiquitination and degradation of Skn-1 in the nucleus. The researchers also discovered a new substrate of WDR-23A/B, GEN-1, and the regulatory effects of the two isoforms of WDR-23 on it were similar to those of Skn-1. The positive and negative regulatory effects were related to the mono- and poly-ubiquitination of WDR-23A/B, respectively [168]. Even though the spatial laws of the regulatory effects of the two isoforms of WDR-23 have been solved, the temporal laws (front and back regulation) still need further investigation.

Nrf2 biology and aging in non-rodent models

In 2013, López-Otín et al. proposed a series of evaluation criteria for the manifestation of aging cells, including loss of proteostasis, genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, mitochondrial dysfunction, deregulated nutrient sensing, cellular senescence, stem cell function, and altered intercellular communications [169]. These are also known as the "Hallmarks of Aging", and the loss of Nrf2 contributes to this process, leading to a series of age-related diseases [170]. In earlier studies, Sykiotis et al. have found that Keap1 loss-of-function in male Drosophila can increase their lifespan, indicating the impact of enhanced Nrf2 signaling on lifespan. Male keap1 heterozygotes in female Drosophila did not produce significant differences in oxidative stress resistance or lifespan. Meanwhile, both male and female flies overexpressing CncC have improved Paraquat resistance [171]. Although there are certain gender differences, this study still shows the regulatory role of CncC/Nrf2 in the aging process. Earlier, Bishop et al. found that the increase in the lifespan of diet-restricted C. elegans requires the expression of skn-1 in a pair of ASI neurons in its brain. This pair of ASI neurons instructs the increase in metabolic activity of peripheral tissues of C. elegans, thereby extending its lifespan [172]. Subsequent research by Tullet et al. directly demonstrated that Skn-1 has an independent effect on the extension of C. elegans lifespan, which differs from the ASI-mediated lifespan extension mechanism under dietary restriction. Interestingly, the extension of C. elegans lifespan by the persistent activation of Skn-1 can be independent of DAF-16, and the effect of Skn-1 itself on the extension of C. elegans lifespan results from the reduction of IIS when expressed transgenically [173]. Numerous research have explored the extension of lifespan in C. elegans via the Skn-1 pathway, as reviewed by Blackwell et al. [174].

C. elegans and Drosophila are extensively used in aging research, not just in studies related to Nrf2, primarily due to their shorter lifespans. Drosophila lives for 70–90 days, while C. elegans has a lifespan of only 12–22 days under appropriate culture conditions [175,176]. By contrast, zebrafish and mice have lifespans that span several years [177,178]. Notably, there is a scarcity of research on Nrf2-dependent aging processes in zebrafish. It is important to acknowledge that the role of the Keap1-Nrf2 system in aging remains poorly understood. Simple Keap1 loss-of-function or Nrf2 gain-of-function manipulations appear insufficient to fundamentally alter the biological limits of lifespan. In fact, persistent Nrf2 activation is often disrupted in tumor cells, leading to chemotherapy resistance [179]. Nevertheless, C. elegans and Drosophila possess relatively conserved oxidative stress response mechanisms (Skn-1 and CncC, respectively) and offer the advantage of being well-established aging models, laying a strong foundation for future research.

Screening antioxidants with non-rodent models

Zebrafish, Drosophila, and C. elegans are commonly utilized models for assessing antioxidant activity, following a structured workflow outlined below. The first step involves selecting appropriate model organisms, which may include wild-type strains or mutants of non-rodent animals at different developmental stages. The second step entails constructing an oxidative stress model typically induced by exogenous chemical reagents. Next, in step three, potential antioxidants are administered to the organisms under controlled conditions, with careful consideration of exposure methods, concentrations, and durations. The timing of antioxidant supplementation, whether before, during, or after oxidative stress induction, is a critical factor. Step four focuses on evaluating antioxidant efficacy. This involves assessing levels of ROS, endogenous antioxidants (such as antioxidant genes, antioxidant enzyme activity, and the ratio of reduced to oxidized glutathione), and biomarkers of macromolecular oxidative damage. Step five involves measuring the organisms' resistance to oxidative stress through tests such as lifespan analysis, assessment of malformations, and monitoring cell death. Finally, step six, though optional, involves probing into the mechanisms of antioxidant action. This targets major oxidative stress response pathways in non-rodent species, such as Nrf2, CncC, and Skn-1.

Some well-known low molecular mass compounds (LMMCs) antioxidants, such as Vitamin C and Vitamin E, have been reported to exhibit favorable activity in zebrafish [180], Drosophila [181], and C. elegans [182]. The efficacy of similar positive antioxidants in small non-rodents has validated the rationale for using these animals as potential evaluation models for antioxidant activity. Their small size, high reproductive rate (ample experimental samples), transparency, low breeding costs, and minimal ethical concerns enhance research efficiency and enable high-throughput screening of antioxidants. The effectiveness of antioxidant supplementation in clinical treatment is questioned, and the reasons for its lack of effect are reviewed by Barry [208]. One possible reason is its interference with the normal physiological role of ROS. As mentioned previously, ROS, particularly H2O2, is a crucial signaling molecule essential for maintaining cellular oxidative homeostasis. Researchers have investigated another well-known small molecule thiol antioxidant, GSH, and its precursor, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), in C. elegans. The results showed that long-term consumption of GSH or NAC in healthy C. elegans inhibited Skn-1-mediated transcription and accelerated aging [209]. Considering that the endogenous GSH biosynthesis network of C. elegans is conserved compared with mammals [210], such findings have a certain degree of extrapolation to humans. The fact that long-term supplementation of small molecular weight thiol antioxidants has resulted in negative feedback does not mean that all antioxidants are doubted. Thiol antioxidants are sacrificial agents that are important components of oxidative homeostasis regulation in the human body and can be produced endogenously. Long-term supplementation of thiol antioxidants may interfere with the normal regulatory capacity and thus produce negative feedback. On the other hand, such research results have certain implications for the subsequent development of antioxidants and clinical treatment plans, namely, how to maintain normal redox homeostasis in the human body and enable antioxidants to reach specific lesions while effectively reducing oxidative damage.

Comprehensive analysis

Comparison of model organisms in redox biology research

This review presents key areas of redox biology research, including the detection of oxidative stress, mechanisms underlying oxidative stress, and the screening of antioxidants, using non-rodent models. Comparing different animal models in redox biology research is a broad and complex topic. The advantages of non-rodent models in specific redox biology studies are driven by several common factors. Fig. 9 highlights a comparison of these factors across various model organisms used in research.

Multiple factors of model organisms to be considered in redox biology research.

In oxidative stress detection, whether through redox-sensitive small molecule fluorescent probes or genetically encoded redox biosensors, the transparency of non-rodent models such as C. elegans and larval zebrafish facilitates in vivo imaging. Conversely, the heavy pigmentation and opaque structure of adult Drosophila present certain limitations. However, all three non-rodent models offer a wide array of strains expressing genetically encoded redox sensors, providing more experimental options compared to rodent models. Additionally, when detecting redox biomarkers, the advantages of these non-rodent models in terms of size, population quantity, and ease of maintenance significantly enhance research efficiency relative to rodent models.

Conversely, an important oxidative stress response mechanism, the Keap1-Nrf2 system, has been reviewed. The degree of conservation of this system is evidently higher in zebrafish than in Drosophila, and higher in Drosophila than in C. elegans. This suggests that research findings on the Keap1-Nrf2 system derived from zebrafish models are more favorable to human extrapolation. However, from the perspective of studying the mechanisms of oxidative stress, Drosophila offers significant advantages as a widely used gene editing tool, attributable to its simple genome and lack of genetic redundancy [211]. Furthermore, Drosophila possesses a fully sequenced genome and advanced genetic tools, making nearly all aspects of its biology well-established and understood [212].

Finally, the evaluation of antioxidant activity is also critically reviewed. All three non-rodent species have been widely used to evaluate the activity of antioxidants, and the research efficiency has been greatly improved. However, considering their simpler physiological structure and lower degree of conservation compared with rodent models, the activity evaluation results obtained in rodents are still more accurate. Here, the use of non-rodent species for primary activity screening is recommended. In this regard, the construction of a high-throughput screening platform using non-rodent species is a promising goal, especially the zebrafish and C. elegans. Zebrafish is one of the most widely applied fish model organisms for scientific research internationally, and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) ranked zebrafish as the third among experimental vertebrate model organisms, just after mice and rats, due to its advantages over others [213]. In addition, zebrafish have a more conserved antioxidant system than Drosophila and nematodes. A new method for antioxidant discovery is to design or screen active molecules through the protein-protein interaction inhibition principle of Keap1-Nrf2 [214]. The conserved Keap1-Nrf2 system of zebrafish provides a more suitable activity evaluation tool, which is more suitable for the research. Zebrafish is a superior vertebrate model organism suitable for high-throughput screening in a microplate. The sample size of zebrafish is only 0.1%–1% of that of mice, and the single ovulation of female fish is more than 200 with slight individual differences [215], which ensures the high efficiency of pharmacological activity screening. C. elegans is a small invertebrate genetic model with tiny size, high reproductive capacity (every 3–4 days), and short life cycle (about 2–3 weeks) [216]. C. elegans usually feeds on E. coli, making it easy to culture and maintain. It is primarily hermaphroditic and can obtain progeny of uniform genetic background by self-fertilization and spawning [217]. To summarize the facts above, the expenditure and time spent on the cultivation and experiments are extremely low when C. elegans models are employed with fewer ethical worries, making C. elegans models superior model organisms for high-throughput screening [218].

Combination of novel techniques and non-rodent models

Zebrafish

Recently, researchers developed a deep learning-based platform for zebrafish phenotypic screening, specifically designed for multi-parameter cardiac analysis using dynamic cardiac videos of zebrafish larvae. This platform facilitated the screening of 347 natural product compounds, leading to the identification of cyanidin chloride. This compound exhibited a potent inhibitory effect against doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity. Further investigation into the protective mechanism of cyanidin chloride revealed its strong antioxidant properties, which can inhibit lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial damage. Molecular docking and thermal transfer experiments additionally demonstrated that cyanidin chloride inhibits the Keap1-Nrf2 protein-protein interaction, suggesting a potential mechanism for its antioxidant activity [219].

Zebrafish is a superior vertebrate model organism for high-throughput microplate screening [220]. Developing a high-throughput screening platform for zebrafish models is a promising application, especially when combined with many current analysis, detection, and delivery techniques. These provide the basis for the update and iteration of high-throughput screening platforms for zebrafish models.

Delivery techniques facilitate the safe and effective delivery of vulnerable zebrafish larvae. A light-driven technique can transport zebrafish larvae in a microfluidic environment and avoid causing damage to the larvae when transported in an imaging platform by traditional techniques [221]. The detection technique accurately and efficiently detects the relevant parameters of the zebrafish model. An automated and noninvasive technique can detect the heartbeat of zebrafish embryos [222]. Last but not least, the analysis technique comprehensively analyzes the relevant parameters. An Argus system can automatically perform quantitative analyses of the behavior of zebrafish, which is an application of the zebrafish model in behavioral neuroscience [223]. From the perspective of behavior detection, a 3D high-throughput analysis technique can analyze zebrafish swimming paths, which is a more specific detection object [224].

Drosophila

In Drosophila transgenesis, fluorescent markers are a popular and powerful technique. However, the densely pigmented and opaque structural characteristics of the Drosophila cuticle prevent visible light from directly penetrating the interior tissues. Additionally, tissue-induced light scattering and optical aberrations can lower signal strength and alter spatial resolution, making it challenging to image entire brain networks [225]. Therefore, tissue sectioning is necessary for further examination. FlyClear, a novel, unique tissue clearance and decolorization technique, permits the long-term retention of endogenous fluorescent signals and the elimination of overall pigmentation for all Drosophila stages. It compensates for the limitations of low transparency of Drosophila and allows it to observe cells and tissues fluorescently [225,226].

In the Drosophila model of gain-of-function or loss-of-function circumstances, such as inhibition of endogenous antioxidants, the RNAi technique is frequently employed to examine the activity of individual genes. It uses the GAL4/upstream activating sequence (UAS) system to regulate the expression of RNAi structures, the developmental stage specifically depleting the transcriptional product of the target gene [227]. Traditional RNAi technique has numerous disadvantages, including false positive outcomes for highly expressed genes due to inefficiency, false adverse consequences due to off-target effects or primary promoter leakage, and the inability to target multiple genes, which pose a severe obstacle to gene editing in Drosophila. The PNP vector-based transgenic RNAi system enables precise regulation of highly expressed genes, reduces the likelihood of basic promoter leakage, and allows for the simultaneous knockout of multiple genes, providing great convenience in investigating the function of protein complexes [228]. Nonetheless, the constraints of standard gene editing approaches of the RNAi and mutant cloning classes cannot be circumvented, i.e., there is a considerable delay once functional genes are eliminated. Due to the rapid development and short lifespan of Drosophila, the time lag may lead to inaccurate results and misclassification of gene functions. In response, Anchor Away can directly target proteins, allowing for rapid and effective LOF by isolating the target protein in another cell compartment and preventing it from carrying out its specialized physiological activity [229].

C. elegans

C. elegans is suitable for high-throughput screening due to its small size, short life cycle, and ease of maintenance. Traditional agar plate-based testing of C. elegans is time-consuming [230]. However, higher analytical efficiency and more accurate analytical results can be obtained by combining C. elegans models with novel techniques for analysis.

C. elegans models are frequently applied for toxicity assessment, and how to improve the efficiency and accuracy of traditional assays is a crucial issue. Combining conventional toxicity detection and a novel efficient screening platform in C. elegans integrates both strengths, thus providing one solution. For example, an integrated automated high-throughput microplate toxicity screening platform is equipped with a microplate culture system, microfluidic chips, and a computerized worm identification technique. Remote control and online worm monitoring can also be achieved via the Internet. Such a toxicity screening platform effectively improves analytical efficiency, making it a future application for high-throughput toxicity screening [230]. Moreover, an enhanced toxicity assessment platform based on C. elegans has been developed to rapidly assess acute toxicity and developmental and reproductive toxicity. It significantly improves the validity and sensitivity of C. elegans for rapid toxicity assessment [231].

It is indispensable to judge various phenotypes such as the survival, development, death, and other behaviors of C. elegans when performing activity/toxicity screening assays. However, previous phenotypic screening of C. elegans has relied chiefly on manual work, and an automated and efficient phenotypic screening platform is becoming urgently required. Fortunately, an improved Wide Field Nematode Tracking Platform (WF-NTP) can analyze multiple behavioral parameters of hundreds of worms simultaneously, screening for phenotypes. It provides an automated means of assessing C. elegans fitness and improves experimental efficiency significantly [232]. Additionally, an open-source robotic image capture platform, WormBot, can perform 144 parallel C. elegans survival and behavioral phenotype experiments. It creates the possibility of achieving low-cost, simple, and automated C. elegans survival analysis [233]. Last but not least, a method for automatic phenotyping of C. elegans embryos based on a high-throughput screening microfluidic platform is provided to investigate the embryogenesis of C. elegans exposed to various compounds. It opens new avenues for large-scale developmental biology research and rapid high-throughput screening in C. elegans embryos [234].

Conclusion and outlooks

This review examines recent progress in detecting oxidative stress using non-rodent models, presented through a novel categorization framework. It systematically reviews the Keap1-Nrf2 oxidative homeostasis regulatory pathway and various Nrf2-centered pathways in diverse model organisms. Additionally, it critically evaluates antioxidant screening methodologies in non-rodents. Ultimately, the review conducts a comprehensive comparative analysis of different model organisms and explores integrating innovative techniques with non-rodent models to enhance research efficacy.

In the future, leveraging non-rodent models for high-throughput screening will require realizing their potential availability. Automated high-throughput screening can be achieved by integrating various innovative technologies, encompassing delivery, detection, and analysis processes. This approach particularly applies to zebrafish and C. elegans, utilizing their capabilities as preliminary screening tools. Additionally, this paper presents feasible options for Drosophila decolorization (FlyClear) and knockout technologies (Anchor Away) to enhance the potential for high-throughput screening in Drosophila. Such advancements promise to significantly improve the efficiency of oxidative stress research in the future.
