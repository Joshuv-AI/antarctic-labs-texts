Effect of Diltiazem Versus Placebo on Microvascular Dysfunction Assessed By Repeated Continuous Thermodilution Measurements: Results of the EDIT‐CMD Trial
Authors: Tijn P J Jansen, Annemiek de Vos, Suzette E Elias‐Smale, Valeria Paradies, Regina E Konst, Caïa Crooijmans, Aukelien C Dimitriu‐Leen, Laura Rodwell, Angela H E M Maas, Pieter C Smits, Niels van Royen, Peter Damman
Published: 2023

Despite the absence of large randomized placebo‐controlled trials, expert consensus recommends the use of calcium channel blockers in patients with angina and no obstructive coronary artery disease (ANOCA) with proven vasomotor dysfunction. 1 Vasomotor dysfunction includes vasospastic disease and coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) characterized by a decreased microvascular vasodilatory capacity or increased resistance. We have recently shown the diagnostic value of invasive continuous thermodilution assessed absolute flow (Q) and absolute resistance (Rμ) for microvascular dysfunction in ANOCA. 2 The automated design of this method is easy to use and leads to increased reproducibility as compared to bolus thermodilution, making it ideal for sequential intrapatient comparisons and therefore for treatment evaluation such as calcium channel blockers at baseline and follow‐up. 3 , 4 We report on the effect of the calcium channel blockers diltiazem versus placebo on microvascular dysfunction assessed by continuous thermodilution at repeated coronary function testing (CFT).

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The protocol was approved by all the local institutional review boards. All patients gave written informed consent. All data were entered into an electronic database (Castor EDC). EDIT‐CMD (Efficacy of Diltiazem to Improve Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction: A Randomized Clinical Trial) is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04777045).

This is an additional analysis of the randomized, placebo‐controlled trial in patients with ANOCA with evidence of coronary vasomotor dysfunction, in which we assessed the effect of diltiazem versus placebo on microvascular function by repeated CFT: the EDIT‐CMD trial (clinicaltrials.gov: NCT04777045). In EDIT‐CMD, we performed a clinically indicated CFT in 126 patients with ANOCA and then randomly assigned 85 patients with confirmed coronary vasomotor dysfunction, defined as presence of epicardial or microvascular coronary spasm and/or CMD (coronary flow reserve <2.0 or index microvascular resistance ≥25), to receive either oral diltiazem or placebo in increasing dosage up to 360 mg/day. After 6 weeks of study treatment we performed a follow‐up CFT. The results of the effect on spasm and bolus thermodilution coronary flow reserve and index microvascular resistance are presented previously 5 ; however, the initial/index and follow‐up CFT also included continuous thermodilution assessed hyperemic Q and Rμ according to previously described methodology. 2 In short, a dedicated monorail double‐lumen coronary infusion catheter (RayFlow, Hexacath, Paris, France) was advanced over a pressure/temperature‐sensor equipped coronary wire, and positioned in the proximal segment of the left anterior descending artery. Hyperemia was induced using automated saline infusion of 20 mL/min, after which the distal and infusion steady state temperature was acquired. Distal and aortic pressures were recorded continuously throughout the measurement. Q and Rμ were automatically calculated using the Coroventis Coroflow system.

In total, 74 patients underwent the second CFT, of whom 66 patients underwent both continuous thermodilution measurements (32 placebo and 34 diltiazem). Eight patients did not undergo continuous thermodilution because of 5Fr guiding use, excluding microcatheter use.

The change from baseline in the hyperemic Q and Rμ values to follow‐up was compared between the placebo and diltiazem group by fitting a linear regression model. In addition to treatment group, respective baseline value of Q or Rμ and CMD status at baseline were included as covariates in the model. Baseline characteristics were not different between the treatment groups. The mean age was 58 (±9) years, 65% of patients were female, and 64% had CMD. There were no statistically significant or clinically relevant differences for hyperemic Q and Rμ between the groups (Figure). Mean hyperemic Q slightly decreased from 0.18 mL/min to 0.17 mL/min in the diltiazem group and slightly increased from 0.18 mL/min to 0.19 mL/min in the placebo group (relative difference in change from baseline: −0.025 mL/min [95% CI, −0.053 to 0.03]). Mean Rμ changed from 516 to 504 WU in the diltiazem group and from 527 to 506 WU in the placebo group (relative difference in change from baseline: 9.0 WU [95% CI, −86.76 to 104.75]).

The current EDIT‐CMD substudy shows that first, diltiazem results in a comparable effect on microvascular function when compared with placebo in patients with ANOCA, as measured by the continuous thermodilution method. There were consistent results in the total ANOCA population as well as patients with ANOCA with a confirmed CMD endotype (data not shown). As previously reported there was no improvement in complaints or quality of life. 5 Therefore, additional studies are necessary to determine the optimal pharmacological therapy for the CMD endotype. Second, intrapatient serial continuous thermodilution measurements are feasible and safe, and the current substudy shows the potential of serial continuous thermodilution to assess treatment effects on microvascular (dys)function. As current consensus documents highlight, evidence‐based treatments are necessary for the patient population with ANOCA which is characterized by worse clinical outcomes and a high angina load.

In conclusion, diltiazem shows no effect on hyperemic absolute coronary flow and microvascular resistance as assessed by repeated continuous thermodilution in patients with ANOCA.
