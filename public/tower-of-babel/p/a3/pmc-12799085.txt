Impact of Hyaluronic Acid Around Dental Implants: A Systematic Review
Authors: Shruti Ananya, Pooja Palwankar, Ruchi Pandey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:Hyaluronic acid (HA), a naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan, has gained attention in dental implantology due to its bioactive, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing properties. This systematic review evaluates the current evidence regarding the application and efficacy of HA around dental implants, focusing on its impact on peri-implant tissue health, osseointegration, and prevention of peri-implant diseases.Materials and Methods:A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and Web of Science databases for studies published from 2016 to 2024. Inclusion criteria: Encompassed human studies, animal studies, and in vitro studies. The Cochrane risk of bias 2 tool and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale were used to assess the risk of bias. A total of 12 studies met the inclusion criteria.Results:It is demonstrated that HA application positively influences soft tissue healing, reduces inflammation, and may support early osseointegration. Despite promising findings, heterogeneity in HA formulations, application protocols, and follow-up durations limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Further long-term, standardized clinical trials are needed to validate its routine use in implant dentistry.Conclusion:This review highlights HA’s potential as a valuable adjunct in implant therapy, especially for enhancing peri-implant tissue health and managing early peri-implant disease.

INTRODUCTION

Implantology has significantly evolved, transforming oral rehabilitation by improving functional, aesthetic, and psychosocial outcomes for partially or fully edentulous patients. Advances in osteo-implantology and implant-supported prostheses have enabled shorter loading times, greater patient satisfaction, and better bone preservation compared to traditional prostheses. The most crucial aspect of implant success is the healing between bone and implant, that is, osseointegration.[1]

Osteointegration is a crucial component of implant therapy effectiveness and is impacted by a number of implant-related factors, like material composition, macro- and microgeometry, and most importantly, surface modifications. Sandblasting and acid-etching are two conventional techniques which have been modified and long been used to provide surface roughness, improving bone apposition and providing better osseointegration.[2,3,4]

In order to further encourage bone-to-implant contact (BIC), coating of implant surfaces employing bioactive materials like calcium phosphate (CaP) is encouraged, owing to recent progress in material science. Surface hydrophilicity has become a crucial element in enhancing the biological response at the interface between the implant and bone. Because of these developments, a wide range of implants that are sold commercially and have different surface topographies, chemistries, wettability, and structural designs have been developed.[2] Despite the fact that there are many surface modifications, there is still no universally accepted method for improving osseointegration. As a result, research is still ongoing to compare and assess the effectiveness of various implant surface treatments.[1]

One such biomolecule which can aid in improving osseointegration by modifying the surface characteristics of implants is hyaluronic acid (HA), which is a naturally occurring nonsulfated glycosaminoglycan having a high molecular weight of 4000–20,000,000 Da.[5]

HA is a polyanionic disaccharide unit of glucuronic acid and N-acetyl glucosamine, which are joined by alternating bl-3 and bl-4 bonds. HA is the key element in the periodontal tissues like gingiva, cementum, alveolar bone, and periodontal ligament. Hyaluronan may help regulate the inflammatory response. In chronically inflamed tissues such as inflamed gingiva during the postoperative phase after implant or sinus lift surgery, high-molecular-weight HA produced by synthase enzymes in the gingiva, periodontal ligament, and alveolar bone is significantly broken down into pieces of lower molecular weight.

As an adjuvant treatment for peri-implantitis, HA exhibits notable anti-inflammatory qualities. The pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin (IL)-1β, a major modulator of tissue degradation, is significantly reduced by the subgingival injection of 0.8% HA gel after mechanical debridement. Better clinical results, such as decreased probing depth and blood on probing, are correlated with this biochemical improvement. According to these results, HA is essential for regulating the host’s inflammatory response and fostering a more salubrious peri-implant environment.[6,7] By dramatically increasing the number and differentiation of human osteoblasts and actively promoting the development of mineralized bone nodules in vitro, HA has a direct osteogenic effect. This suggests that its activity is bioactive rather than passive, essentially supporting the underlying cellular functions necessary for bone healing and regeneration.[5]

In operations such as sinus augmentation, HA promotes bone repair. Stronger BIC is shown by noticeably increased implant stability when added to a bone transplant. HA speeds up the development of new, denser bone, which produces more predictable results in complex grafting, according to radiographic and histological evidence.

On titanium implants, HA coatings greatly improve osseointegration.[8,9] These coatings increase the durability of primary and secondary implants, suggesting an early and more favorable bone healing response. Additionally, implants coated with HA show decreased marginal bone loss during loading, indicating that the coating enhances biological activity at the implant–bone interface to promote greater long-term bone stability.[7]

HA helps modulate the inflammatory response by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor [TNF]-α). This is essential for preventing and managing early-stage peri-implant diseases like mucositis. It helps in soft tissue healing by promoting fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and collagen formation. It enhances soft tissue integration around the implant, contributing to a stable and healthy mucosal seal. It also enhances osteoblastic activity around implants.

This systematic review focuses on the impact of HA on clinical outcomes such as implant survival rate, peri-implant soft tissue health, bone loss, inflammation, probing depth, and patient satisfaction around dental implants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The following keywords or, in the instance of the PubMed database, medical subject headings (MESH) were used to search for pertinent published studies in Ovid, Google Scholar, and PubMed:(“hyaluronic acid” OR “hyaluronic acid gel” OR “hyaluronic acid injection” OR “low molecular weight hyaluronic acid” OR “high molecular weight hyaluronic acid”) AND (“dental implants”) AND (“osseointegration” OR “healing” OR “stability” OR “regeneration”) AND (“comparative study” OR “clinical trial” OR “in vitro studies” OR “animal studies”)

The published data for last 10 years (2016–2025) were considered.

A literature search was carried out using the following primary eligibility criteria in compliance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines: only English, human studies, animal studies, in vitro studies, technical notes, letters to editors, instructional course, and articles in language other than English were not included [Figure 1].

STUDY SELECTION

To find studies pertinent to the effects of HA surrounding dental implants, a two-step screening procedure was used.

Step 1: Screening for titles and abstracts: In order to get rid of duplicates, every document that was taken out from the electronic databases was first loaded into a reference management program. Two reviewers (SA and RP) separately assessed the remaining abstracts and titles using predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. At this point, studies that blatantly failed to meet the requirements were eliminated.

Step 2: Full-Text Screening: After passing the first screening, the articles were reviewed in their entirety. To determine final eligibility, the same reviewers (SA and RP) evaluated each full-text publication separately. A third reviewer was consulted to settle any disputes between the reviewers.

RISK OF BIAS ASSESSMENT

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the scientific integrity of the nonrandomized studies that were included. With a possible score of 9, this measure evaluates the danger of bias in three areas: outcome/exposure (maximum 3 stars), comparability (maximum 2 stars), and selection (maximum 4 stars). Three of the ten evaluated studies were classified as having a moderate risk of bias (5–6 stars) and seven as having a low risk of bias (≥7 stars). Low-risk studies showed sound outcome evaluations, appropriate group comparability, and effective selection procedures. On the other hand, studies classified as moderate frequently lacked adequate information about follow-up adequacy or control group comparability. The necessity for cautious analysis of pooled results is emphasized by the variation in methodological rigor among studies. The overall reliability of the evidence was supported by the fact that most of the included studies had low-to-moderate methodological quality.

As opposed to other surface modifications, Yurttutan et al.[2] revealed that titanium implants with HA produced noticeably better results. In a sheep model, HA-coated implants demonstrated improved reverse torque resistance, increased implant stability quotient (ISQ), and increased BIC at 1 and 3 months after implantation. According to these results, HA coatings can greatly improve dental implants’ mechanical stability and early osseointegration.[2]

HA coating significantly enhances the biological performance of 3D-printed PEEK surfaces, as demonstrated by Abdulghafor and Amin.[13]At both 7 and 21 days, HA-coated PEEK disks exhibited markedly higher osteoblast viability, proliferation, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, and mineralization compared to uncoated and control groups. These findings suggest that HA coating serves as an effective surface alteration, promoting osteogenic differentiation and improving the dental implant material bioactivity, thereby supporting better implant integration.[13]

Abhigna et al.[14] assessed the impact of locally injecting HA gel into osteotomy sites and discovered significant advantages prior to implant placement. After 3 months, the HA-treated sites demonstrated a larger improvement in secondary implant stability (ISQ) and a considerably lower marginal crestal bone loss when compared to the control group. According to these results, using HA prior to implantation promotes early osseointegration and aids in maintaining peri-implant bone.[14]

In their 2019 study, Kaya et al.[15] investigated the application of HA in conjunction with collagen membrane and xenograft (HAXC) to treat peri-implant dehiscence defects and discovered that HA offered little further benefit. At 6 months, the HAXC group’s vertical bone height (VBH) growth was somewhat greater than that of the xenograft and collagen membrane (XC) group, but the difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, only medium defects treated with XC had a significant decrease in VBH over time, suggesting that HA did not significantly improve clinical results in this situation.[15]

Yazan et al.[16] investigated the impact of HA on the osseointegration of dental implants using a rabbit model. HA was applied to the implant surfaces prior to placement, and bone healing was assessed histologically and histomorphometrically at 4 and 8 weeks. The HA-treated group demonstrated more extensive osteoid tissue and new bone around the bone–implant interface in contrast to the control group, but the difference was not statistically significant.[16]

Lupi et al.[17]compared covalently linked hyaluronan-coated titanium dental implants with traditional sand-blasted and acid-etched titanium implants in a randomized clinical trial with 30 patients and 106 implants. During a 36-month follow-up period, the primary outcomes assessed were implant success, wound healing, and marginal bone resorption. According to the study, there was no statistically significant difference in clinical success or bone resorption between the test and control groups, indicating that HA coating did not offer any extra long-term therapeutic advantages in this situation.[17]

Mohammad and AlGhaban[18] evaluated the impact of HA on the BIC in rabbits, with a specific focus on inflammatory response. Immunohistochemical analysis was used to detect the expression of TNF-α, a key inflammatory marker, around the implants. Findings revealed that HA significantly lowered TNF-α levels, indicating its potential to reduce inflammation and enhance bone healing.

Shamma et al.[19] investigated the impact of HA paired with biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) on bone healing surrounding dental implants. After applying the composite to the implant sites, radiography and histology techniques were used to assess bone repair. Findings revealed that the HA–BCP combination significantly improved new bone formation and BIC compared to BCP alone.

A randomized controlled experiment was carried out by Sánchez-Fernández et al.[6] to examine the use of HA gel as an adjuvant in the treatment of peri-implantitis. Either mechanical debridement with HA or debridement with a placebo gel was administered to thirty patients. Probing depth, bleeding on probing, and levels of the inflammatory marker IL-1β in the crevicular fluid were all considerably reduced in the HA-treated group at 6 months. According to the study’s findings, HA can effectively reduce inflammation in peri-implantitis.[6]

In a lab study, Bonifacio et al.[10] investigated how HA and a combination of amino acids affected human osteoblasts, which are cells that make bone. The scientists discovered that administering these compounds to the cells greatly enhanced their capacity to proliferate, become active, and build mineralized structures—all of which are essential processes in bone regeneration and repair. The findings imply that amino acids and HA may be useful ingredients in biomaterials intended to promote bone formation.[10]

A clinical investigation by Omr et al.[11] assessed the effects of HA addition to a bovine bone graft for upper jaw sinus augmentation procedures. Twenty patients had sinus grafts using either bone alone or HA plus bone combination and then concurrent implant insertion. After 6 months, the HA group showed better outcomes, such as increased implant stability, increased bone density on radiographs, and more new bone development in histological analysis, suggesting that HA improves the efficacy of bone grafts.[11]

In a randomized clinical trial, Hamdy et al.[12] contrasted a novel HA-coated implant surface with a traditional SLA surface. The posterior upper jaw was implanted with 26 implants. Along with decreased bone loss around the implant neck, the HA-coated implants demonstrated noticeably higher stability values both during installation and at the 6-month follow-up. According to the results, a HA coating may enhance osseointegration’s rate and caliber.

QUALITY ASSESSMENT

A quality evaluation was carried out employing appropriate tools based on the study design. The Cochrane risk of bias 2 tool assessed randomized controlled trial (RCTs) across five domains, assigning an overall bias rating (low, some concerns, or high) [Table 2]. For observational studies, the NOS was applied [Table 3]. The included studies’ overall quality varied. Due mostly to restrictions in blinding or outcome reporting, the majority of RCTs showed a high to low risk of bias. The dependability and strength of the data supporting the stated effects of HA surrounding dental implants were interpreted using these evaluations.

Synthesis of Results: An initial assessment of data homogeneity was conducted due to variations in study design, interventions, outcomes, and follow-up periods. Meta-analysis was considered appropriate when clinical and methodological homogeneity was sufficient—particularly regarding patient populations, outcome measures (e.g., probing depth, bone loss, and implant stability), and intervention types (e.g., HA gel or HA-coated implants). In such cases, standardized mean differences or risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals were computed using RevMan software.

A meta-analysis was deemed unsuitable due to substantial heterogeneity in study models, outcome measures, and intervention methods. Instead, a narrative synthesis was conducted by categorizing studies based on the intervention type and outcomes and then systematically comparing results. The review highlighted consistent patterns, contradictions, and context-specific findings regarding the effects of HA around dental implants

DISCUSSION

Numerous clinical, in vivo, and in vitro investigations have examined the use of HA surrounding dental implants, which collectively demonstrate its advantages. In vitro findings of Abdulghafor and Amin.[13] indicate that HA greatly enhances cell viability, proliferation, differentiation, and antimicrobial properties, especially when combined with substances like selenium or employed on novel surfaces like PEEK. These results suggest that the effectiveness of HA may vary depending on the context, including the type of defect, the mode of distribution, and the biological milieu.[13]

Further information about HA’s osteoconductive and healing-supportive qualities can be found in studies that concentrate on implant stability and bone regeneration. In comparison to other surface modifications, Yurttutan et al.[2] observed better ISQ, BIC, and reverse torque values in HA-coated implants. In a similar vein, Abhigna et al.[14] found that local HA injection prior to implant placement improved secondary stability and decreased marginal bone loss.

Mohammad and AlGhaban,[18] and Shamma et al.[19] reaffirmed HA’s function in profound healing around dental implants and encouraging quicker tissue healing, with results that are on par with those of conventional therapies over the long run.

However, not all results point to a significant additional benefit. Kaya et al.[15] discovered that the HA group with XA and collagen membrane did not prove to be beneficial compared to XA and collagen membrane alone.

Similarly, Yazan et al.[16] failed to show significant difference in bone formation between the two study groups at 2 months in rabbits when analyzed histologically and histomorphometrically. Study by Lupi et al.[17] did not show differences in crestal bone level at 36 months in comparison to uncoated implants.

According to the NOS assessment, seven studies were categorized as low risk of bias, indicating high methodological quality and reliability. Three studies showed a moderate risk, suggesting some limitations but still providing useful insights. Overall, the majority of studies demonstrated acceptable to strong quality, supporting the credibility of the evidence. The majority of studies with low-to-moderate risk indicate the effective role of HA around dental implants.

CONCLUSION

Recent research highlights the many uses of HA in implant therapy, emphasizing its advantages for long-term health, healing, and peri-implant stability. HA promotes early osseointegration, improves BIC, and stabilizes both soft and hard tissues. Its anti-inflammatory properties, which are shown by decreases in TNF-α and IL-1β, support soft tissue healing while shielding neighboring tissues from inflammation and oxidative stress.

Increased mechanical resistance, better BIC, and increased early implant stability are further confirmed by preclinical and in vivo research, indicating faster and more robust integration with soft tissues and bone for HA-coated implants. All of these results point to HA as a useful supplement in implant procedures, with encouraging potential for extending implant life and lowering early problems.
