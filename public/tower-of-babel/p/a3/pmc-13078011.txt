Translation of expanded CGG repeats in LRP12 associated oculopharyngodistal myopathy
Authors: Chengcheng Li, Jil A Daw, Sara K Pittman, Connor J Maltby, Hidetoshi Sakurai, Peter K Todd, Conrad C Weihl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOculopharyngodistal myopathy (OPDM) is characterized by ptosis, ophthalmoparesis, dysphagia, and distal weakness. Myopathological features include rimmed vacuoles and intranuclear inclusions. OPDM is associated with a pathogenic CGG repeat expansions in the 5’UTR of LRP12, NOTCH2NLC, GIPC1, RILPL1 and ABCD3. Translation of the repeat in the glycine reading frame has been demonstrated for expansions in FMR1, NOTCH2NLC and GIPC1. To assess for a similar phenomenon with LRP12, we expressed normal or expanded CGG repeats in the context of the 5’UTR of LRP12, upstream of a green fluorescent protein (GFP) in the three repeat reading frames. Repeat dependent translation occurs exclusively in the glycine reading frame. However, unlike other CGG repeat disorders, there is no proximal AUG, or near-AUG cognate initiated polyglycine (polyG) open reading frame in LRP12. Instead, our results support a model in which repeat-associated non-AUG (RAN) mediated polyG translation may initiate within the arginine reading frame and then undergo a + 1 translational frameshift into the glycine reading frame. LRP12-associated polyG products form intranuclear SQSTM1/ubiquitin positive inclusions that are cytotoxic and alter the nuclear lamina architecture in transfected cells. While FMR1-associated polyG inclusions are cytosolic, LRP12-associated polyG inclusions are nuclear in transfected skeletal muscle. LRP12 expansion carrier iPSC derived myotubes exhibit SQSTM1 positive intra- and peri- nuclear inclusions when compared with control patient myotubes, suggesting that polyG expression can occur in patients. Together, these findings provide evidence of RAN translation and polyG-toxicity in LRP12-associated OPDM pathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02272-4.Keywords: CGG repeat expansion, Polyglycine, RAN translation, Oculopharyngodistal myopathy, LRP12

Introduction

Oculopharyngeal distal myopathy (OPDM) is an autosomal dominantly inherited degenerative myopathy characterized clinically by ptosis, ophthalmoparesis, dysphagia, and distal myopathy [38]. Myopathologic features include variation in myofiber size, rimmed vacuoles and filamentous intranuclear inclusions [38]. OPDM was initially described in four Japanese families and has since been described in multiple patients in other countries with the largest cohorts residing in East Asia [30, 38]. Whole genome sequencing of OPDM patients revealed a CGG repeat expansion in the 5’-untranslated region (5’UTR) of the LRP12 gene in 22 unrelated patients [14]. Notably, the CGG repeat expansion in LRP12 (OPDM1) only accounted for 25% of OPDM patients [14]. Subsequent studies identified expanded CGG repeats within the 5’UTR of GIPC1, NOTCH2NLC, RILPL1 and ABCD3, as well as the long non-coding RNA NUTM2B-AS1, expanding the genotypic spectrum to OPDM1-6 [8, 9, 11, 36, 37]. In the case of LRP12, the CGG repeat within the 5’UTR expands from a normal repeat range of 13–45 to a range of 85–289 repeats with most patients having a repeat size between 125 and 150 [14].

The pathogenic mechanism for a CGG repeat expansion in the 5’UTR of LRP12 is not established. The CGG repeats could potentially trigger silencing of LRP12 protein expression from the expanded allele resulting in haploinsufficiency, expression of expanded toxic CGG repeat RNA species, or translation of toxic polypeptides through an upstream open reading frame or via repeat-associated non-AUG (RAN) translation [6, 22, 23]. All three of these mechanisms are thought to play active roles in the pathogenesis of a similar CGG expansion in the 5’UTR of FMR1, which causes both Fragile X Syndrome (due to CGG repeat methylation and gene silencing) and fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome (FXTAS) (which results from expanded transcribed CGG repeats). In FXTAS, the CGG repeats form RNA foci and sequester relevant RNA binding proteins, such as hnRNPA2/B1 and the Drosha/DGCR8 complex [31, 33]. In parallel, expanded transcribed FMR1 CGG repeats support RAN translation of multiple proteins, the most abundant of which is a polyglycine containing protein (FMRpolyG) that accumulates in neuronal intranuclear inclusions in patients [34]. This unusual process was originally described at CAG repeats in the Spinocerebellar Ataxia type 8 locus and subsequently similar phenomena were identified in multiple neurodegenerative disorders, including C9orf72 repeat expansions associated with ALS and CAG repeat expansions that cause Huntington’s disease [4, 5, 24, 40]. In the context of FMR1 and FXTAS, RAN translation predominantly initiates from an upstream ACG near-cognate start codon through a cap-dependent scanning mechanism [17, 32, 34]. Intriguingly, elimination of this upstream ACG is sufficient to prevent repeat toxicity in both Drosophila and mouse models of disease, and antisense oligonucleotides targeted to this ACG codon block RAN translation from FMR1 and suppress toxicity in patient derived iNeurons [29, 32, 34].

In the case of OPDM, intranuclear RNA foci containing CGG repeat expansions have been demonstrated in the skeletal muscle of patients with NOTCH2NLC, GIPC1, LRP12 and ABCD3 [8, 9, 14]. Additionally, the CGG repeat expansion in the 5’UTR of NOTCH2NLC has an upstream AUG start codon distinct from the canonical AUG start codon that is associated with NOTCH2NLC expression [7]. This upstream AUG generates a unique polyglycine (polyG) containing protein termed upstream of N2C polyG-containing protein or uN2CpolyG [7] whose production is required for toxicity in a transgenic mouse model of the NIID. In OPDM2, the expanded GGC repeat in GIPC1 also undergoes RAN translation into a toxic polyG protein that accumulates in SQSTM1 positive intranuclear and cytoplasmic inclusions and which initiates at an upstream CTG near-cognate start codon [15].

The spectrum of phenotypes associated with CGG repeat expansion diseases ranges from OPDM to central nervous system symptoms such as leukoencephalopathy, ataxia and peripheral neuropathy [38]. Patients with FXTAS do not typically have muscle involvement whereas patients with CGG repeat expansions in NUTM2B-AS1 or NOTCH2NLC can manifest with central nervous system symptoms and myopathy [14, 25]. In contrast, patients with CGG repeat expansions in GIPC1, RILPL1, and ABCD3 typically exhibit isolated OPDM. While initial studies suggested that LRP12 similarly exhibits predominantly isolated OPDM phenotypes, recently patients with motor neuron disease have been identified with intermediate length CGG repeat expansions (< 100 with a mean of 76) in LRP12 expanding the phenotypic spectrum of LRP12-associated myopathy to include amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [12, 20]. How a CGG repeat within the 5’UTR of several genes leads to ataxia, a mixed presentation of central nervous system disease and myopathy, ALS or pure myopathy is currently unclear. In the present study, we evaluate whether an expanded CGG repeat in the 5’UTR of LRP12 can be translated into polypeptides.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

The 5’UTR of human LRP12 fused to GFP (with its initiation ATG omitted) was synthesized by GeneWiz (GeneWiz). This fragment was then inserted into an engineered pcDNA3.1 (+) with 4 EarI sites mutated, via HindIIII/XbaI. To facilitate insertion of different CGG repeats, an EarI site was then introduced immediately after the 9 CGG by site-directed mutagenesis (Takara Bio). The construct was named LRP12-9X-GFP. The reading frame of the repeat relative to GFP was shifted by removing nucleotides from the 3’ end of 5’ UTR sequence. To make LRP12-48X-GFP, LRP12-86X-GFP, and LRP12-124X-GFP, different sizes of CGG repeats were transferred from donor constructs via EarI sites and inserted into LRP12-9x-GFP in each of three different reading frames. Mutations of AGG and GTG nucleotides were generated using site-directed mutagenesis kit (Takara Bio). The resulting plasmids were designed AGG > TAG and GTG > TAG. Expanded repeats were then introduced as described above. The pCAG-tdTomato plasmid, used as a transfection marker, was purchased from Addgene (83029). Plasmids containing repeat sequences were cloned and propagated at 30 °C to prevent instability and repeat size was confirmed by restriction digest and gel electrophoresis. Other constructs were handled at 37 °C. All vector sequences were confirmed (Genewiz and Plasmidsaurus).

Cell lines and culture conditions

HEK293, HeLa, and U2OS cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were passaged upon reaching confluence. For experiments requiring transfection, cells were trypsinized and reseeded 24 h before transfection. Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, cells were harvested for immunocytochemistry and western blot at 48 and 72 h post transfection.

Control induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) lines ANI.1 and BJFF.6 were obtained from The Genome Engineering & Stem Cell Center at Washington University School of Medicine and cultured following standard protocols. For the OPDM1 disease iPSCs, S21004OPDM clone#1 and clone#2 were generated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells obtained from an OPDM patient using an episomal vector system, as described previously [28]. The patient is a 39-year-old male who was diagnosed with OPDM based on his clinical symptoms and a CGG repeat expansion in the LRP12 gene. Human iPSCs were cultured and maintained in mTeSR1 (STEMCell Technologies) on Matrigel (Corning) coated tissue culture plates at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity. Medium was changed daily. Cells were passaged every 4–6 days using ReLESR (STEMCell Technologies). iPSC cells were differentiated into skeletal muscle cells using Skeletal Muscle Differentiation Kit (AMSBIO) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded onto Collagen I/Laminin-coated plates at a density of 2700 cells/cm2 and cultured in the differentiation medium provided with the kit. Differentiation was carried out for up to 10 days, during which multinucleated myotubes were observed.

Western blot and lysostaphin treatment

HEK293 and HeLa cells were scraped in PBS and centrifuged at 4 °C. The cell pellet was lysed using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.25% Na-deoxycholate, and 1 mM EDTA) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Millipore Sigma). Protein concentration was determined using BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and boiled for 5 min with Laemmli buffer. Samples were then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred from gels to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). After blocking for 1 h in 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight (4 °C) with anti-GFP (1:500, SANTA CRUZ) and anti-GAPDH (1:1000, Cell Signaling). The anti-mouse and anti-rabbit HRP conjugated secondary antibody were used, respectively. Immuno-positive bands were visualized by a chemiluminescent method (ECL, Amersham).

For cells transfected with a Gly-124X-GFP construct, cell extract with determined protein concentration was incubated with lysostaphin (Prospec) for 1–5 min at 37 °C. Equal volume of Laemmli buffer was added to the mix and proteins were analyzed by the above western blot procedure.

Immunocytochemistry

HeLa and U2OS cells transfected with various constructs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 24, 48 and 72 h post transfection. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.5%Triton X-100, blocked. iPSC-derived myotubes at day 10 of differentiation were fixed with 2% PFA for 10 min, gently rinsed with PBS, and simultaneously blocked and permeabilized with 5% normal goat serum (NGS) and 0.07% Triton X-100 in PBS for 45 min. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C followed by secondary antibodies (and DAPI for myotubes) for 60 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies included anti-SQSTM1 (1:500, Proteintech), anti-ubiquitin (1:200, Dako), anti-Lamin B1 (1:500, Abcam), and MF-20 (1:500, DSHB). Fluorescent secondary antibodies were Alexa Fluor® 488, 555, and 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells on coverslips were mounted in Mowiol with DAPI and imaged on a fluorescence microscope (Nikon 80i); cells in wells were imaged directly in PBS using an inverted microscope (Accu-Scope EXI600). To measure the % of cells with aggregates, images of random fields were taken and cells with aggregates were counted via ImageJ. Similarly, to quantify the percentage of nuclei containing SQSTM1 positive inclusions in iPSC-derived myotubes, 5–10 random fields of view were imaged and analyzed.

Cell viability assay

Cell death in transfected HEK293 and HeLa cells was determined via Live-or-Dye™ Fixable Viability Staining Kit (Biotium, Inc.). Cells were harvested at 72 h after transfection and then stained according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, cells were detached using trypsin and centrifuged. Cells were then washed with PBS solution and mixed with Fixable Dead Cell Dye immediately. After 30 min incubation, cells were washed and fixed with 2% PFA. Results were revealed by MACS Flow Cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec). The data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (TreeStar Inc).

Muscle electroporation

Per mouse TA muscle, 40 ug endotoxin-free plasmid diluted in PBS was injected using a 0.5 ml syringe. Muscle electroporation was immediately performed by inserting the electrodes into the injected TA muscle. The electroporation parameters were set to: voltage, 75 V; pulse length, 50 ms; number of pulses, six pulses; pulse interval, 200 ms; desired field strength, 200 V/cm (BTX ECM830 Electro Square Porator). The mouse TA muscle was then isolated at 7 days post electroporation. To visualize the expression of delivered plasmids, 10 µM sections of TA muscle were affixed to slides, treated with ice cold acetone and mounted with Mowiol plus DAPI. Specimens were examined using a fluorescent microscope (Nikon 80i).

Statistics

For all quantitative measurements, data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test; comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey or Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test, using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Differences were considered significant if p was less than 0.05.

Results

To explore the possibility of RAN translation at CGG repeats in LRP12-associated OPDM, we modified a GFP expression plasmid so that the 5’ UTR of a non-expanded human LRP12 cDNA that contains 9 CGG repeats was cloned upstream of GFP abolishing the start codon yet maintaining one of the three unique reading frames of the repeat (Fig. 1a). Any GFP expression from these constructs would thus be AUG-initiation independent. HEK293 cells were transfected for 48–72 h with one of six plasmid constructs containing an unexpanded 9X or expanded 124X in the arginine, alanine or glycine reading frame. Only the LRP12-124X-GFP in the glycine reading frame (Gly-124X-GFP) generated detectable punctate GFP positive intranuclear inclusions by fluorescent imaging or detectable products by immunoblotting using a GFP antibody of transfected lysates (Fig. 1b–c). Consistent with GFP being expressed from a non-AUG initiation site and carrying an expanded polyG repeat, treatment of the lysates with lysostaphin, a glycine-glycine protease [1, 13, 34], decreased the high molecular weight GFP to an unfused size of 27 kDa (Fig. 1d). Unlike the 5’UTR of FMR1, NOTCH2NLC, or GIPC1 which have upstream ACG, AUG or CTG codons in their polyglycine reading frames, respectively [7, 15, 32] to support polyG reading frame translation, the LRP12 5’UTR lacks a direct upstream cognate or near-cognate start codon in the glycine reading frame (Fig. 2a). Recent studies using an expanded CGG in the 5’UTR of FMR1 suggest that FMRpolyG translational initiation can also occur via an upstream ACG in the arginine frame resulting in an arginine to glycine + 1 translational frameshift within the repeat [35]. Consistent with this idea, we identified two near-cognate start codons, AGG and GTG, directly upstream of the CGG repeat within the arginine frame LRP12 5’UTR (Fig. 2a). Mutating either of these sites to the stop codon TAG abolished polyG-GFP expression in HEK293 cells (Fig. 2b). These results suggest that LRP12polyG production may initiate translation in the arginine reading frame and subsequently undergo a frameshift within the repeat to generate a protein in the glycine reading frame (Fig. 2c).

To determine whether LRP12polyG production and inclusion formation are dependent on repeat size, we generated constructs with CGG repeat expansions carrying 48 or 86 repeats in addition to the unexpanded 9 repeat and 124 repeat constructs. Similar to Gly-124X-GFP, both Gly-48X-GFP and Gly-86X-GFP constructs generated higher molecular weight forms of GFP in HEK293 cell lysates 72 h after transfection (Fig. 3a). Fluorescent microscopy of HEK293 cells from similar transfections at 24 h found that longer expansions with 48, 86 and 124 CGG repeats in the glycine frame generated punctate GFP positive inclusions (Fig. 3b). Quantitative analysis revealed a repeat length-dependent increase in inclusion frequency (Fig. 3c), with the Gly-124X-GFP construct producing significantly more inclusions than the Gly-48X-GFP and Gly-86X-GFP constructs. In addition, a trend toward increased inclusion formation was observed for the Gly-86X-GFP repeat construct compared with the Gly-48X-GFP construct, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07). The number of transfected cells with GFP positive inclusions following Gly-124X-GFP increased in a time dependent manner that peaked at 48 h post transfection (Fig. 3d). By 72 h, there was a decrease in cell viability in Gly-124X-GFP as compared with unexpanded Gly-9X-GFP in both transfected HEK293 and HeLa cells (Fig. 3e–f).

Prior studies in FXTAS described alterations in the nuclear lamina in both patient iNeurons and in FMR1 CGG repeat transfected cells [2, 32]. To evaluate the nuclear morphology in LRP12polyG inclusion containing cells, we transfected HeLa cells with Gly-124X-GFP for 24 and 72 h and immunostained for the nuclear envelope protein Lamin B1. In contrast to cells without intranuclear LRP12polyG inclusions, the nuclear lamina was fragmented and disrupted in Gly-124X-GFP expressing cells with intranuclear LRP12polyG inclusions (Fig. 3g–h).

To evaluate the intracellular localization of LRP12polyG inclusions, we transfected U2OS and HeLa cells with LRP12-124X-GFP and as a control, FMR1-99X-GFP. LRP12-124X-GFP inclusions were predominantly intranuclear whereas FMR1-99X-GFP inclusions were commonly found both intranuclear and cytoplasmic (Fig. 4a–d). Some LRP12-124X-GFP and FMR1-99X-GFP co-localized with the protein aggregate markers SQSTM1 or ubiquitin. Surprisingly, when FMR1-99X-GFP, LRP12-124X-GFP or LRP12-86X-GFP expression constructs were electroporated into mouse tibialis anterior muscle, FMR1-99X-GFP polyG inclusions were sarcoplasmic whereas LRP12polyG-GFP inclusions were exclusively myonuclear (Fig. 4e–g).

To explore intranuclear inclusion formation in OPDM1 patients, we generated myotubes from iPSCs derived from two control individuals and two independent clones from a patient carrying an LRP12 repeat expansion. Ten-day-old differentiated myotubes were multinucleated and expressed myosin heavy chain (Fig. 5a, left). In contrast to the nuclear membrane defects observed in HeLa cells, iPSC-derived myotubes did not display evident nuclear membrane defects. Discussion

This study provides evidence that expanded CGG repeats within the 5’UTR of LRP12 support RAN translation of a polyglycine-containing peptide which we term LRP12polyG. RAN translation of polyglycine containing proteins occurs in other CGG repeat disorders, including FXTAS, NOTCH2NLC-associated NIID and more recently OPDM2 associated with CGG repeat expansions in GIPC1 and these products are thought to contribute to disease pathogenesis [7, 15, 32, 34]. However, unlike the polyG peptides generated in FMR1, NOTCH2NLC and GIPC1, which predominantly utilize either an proximal AUG codon or a AUG near-cognate codon to create an upstream open reading frame [7, 15, 34], the polyG peptide in LRP12 appears to initiate RAN translation in the arginine frame, followed by a + 1 frameshift that redirects elongation into the glycine frame to produce the polyG peptide. However, the precise initiation site cannot be definitively determined. This mechanism closely parallels the frameshift model proposed by Wright et al., who showed that CGG repeats can drive ribosomal frame shifting from an arginine frame into the glycine frame to generate chimeric R/G peptides in FMR1 [35]. Our findings extend this paradigm to the CGG repeat context of LRP12, demonstrating that arginine frame initiation followed by a frameshift may represent a more generalizable strategy for polyG production across multiple repeat-expansion disorders.

Our study did not identify RAN translation products in any of the other reading frames, which is distinct from FMR1 and NOTCH2NLC where RAN translation has been demonstrated in the polyalanine reading frame [21, 34]. While this distinction may reflect differences in the sequences surrounding the CGG repeat in each condition (Fig. 2b), more sensitive RAN translation measures may be required to confirm our negative results in relation to polyarginine and polyalanine peptide production from LRP12.

Prior studies suggest that expression of polyG containing proteins is cytotoxic in a repeat length-dependent manner for FMR1polyG, uN2CpolyG and GIPC1polyG proteins [6, 15]. Our study supports that LRP12polyG is similarly toxic in transfected cells. Polyglycine proteins elicit toxicity through multiple mechanisms that are at least in part mediated by the amino acid sequences surrounding the polyglycine stretch [32]. Alterations in cellular proteostasis pathways occur in other CGG repeat disorders and would be consistent with the degenerative pathology seen in OPDM [10, 27]. In addition, alterations in nuclear architecture or defects in nucleocytoplasmic shuttling are seen in multiple repeat expansion disorders, including FXTAS and NIID [32, 39]. Consistent with this, we observed alterations in the nuclear lamina in LRP12polyG transfected cells.

One feature seen in OPDM1 is intranuclear SQSTM1 positive aggregates and filamentous inclusions in affected muscle [38]. This pathologic finding has been demonstrated in all OPDM subtypes although its specificity for OPDM in patient muscle tissue is less clear [26]. In the case of FMR1, NOTCH2NLC- or GIPC1- associated repeats, the polyG product co-localizes with SQSTM1 and ubiquitin positive intranuclear inclusions [7, 15, 34]. As generation of antibodies to pure polyglycine stretches are challenging due to low immunogenicity, these prior studies generated antibodies to amino acid residues upstream yet in frame of the polyglycine expansion (e.g. uN2CpolyG or uGIPC1polyG) [7, 15]. We found that OPDM1 patient iPSC derived myotubes had an increase in SQSTM1 positive intranuclear and perinuclear inclusions similar to that seen in OPDM1 patient muscle. Whether these inclusions are truly LRP12 derived polyG proteins cannot be directly determined at present, as no reliable antibody against LRP12polyG is available. Generation of a unique antibody against LRP12polyG protein would be required to address this question. Nevertheless, OPDM1 patient iPSC derived myotubes will be a powerful tool to understand how expanded CGG repeats in LRP12 mediate cellular toxicity.

Our findings demonstrate that polyG expression in the context of LRP12 creates nuclear/myonuclear inclusions. In contrast, polyG expression in the context of FMR1 is largely cytosolic/sarcoplasmic in skeletal muscle - which is different than what is observed in transfected neurons or in patient brains [19, 35]. While both FMRpolyG and LRP12polyG contain expanded polyglycine stretches, the surrounding neo-amino acids are unique between FMR1 and LRP12 and these surrounding sequences likely modify polyG’s toxic properties [32, 35]. Sequence features, such as potential NLS motifs in regions flanking LRP12polyG, could influence intracellular localization; however, we are unable to address this in the current study. Studies aimed at understanding the determinants of polyG containing protein localization and behavior seem likely to advance our understanding of the differential toxicity in these genetically similar disorders.

Disruption of the nuclear lamina following expression of LRP12polyG in tissue culture cells is similar to that observed for polyG-containing proteins from FMR1 and NOTCH2NLC [32, 39]. In the case of NOTCH2NLC, nuclear lamina disruption was accompanied by alterations in nucleocytoplasmic transport of mRNA [39]. Disrupted nucleocytoplasmic shuttling is an emerging pathogenic mechanism in several protein aggregate disorders including those associated with RAN products such as the dipeptide repeats generated by C9ORF72 [16]. Whether disrupted nucleocytoplasmic transport is detrimental in the context of multinucleated myofiber is not established.

Our study does not preclude the possibility that OPDM1 toxicity is mediated by mechanisms unrelated to RAN translation. Specifically, expanded RNA mediated toxicity has been suggested for CGG repeat disorders [38]. In the case of LRP12-associated disease, LRP12 transcript positive RNA foci are present in patient tissue and iPSC derived neurons. These foci sequester RNA binding proteins such as MBNL1 [20]. Moreover, while polyG proteins appear necessary for disease pathogenesis in model systems of CGG repeat expansion disorders, evidence in FXTAS model systems suggests additional roles for repeat RNA in both inclusion formation and disease pathogenesis [3, 18]. Intriguingly, one previous study identified LRP12 immuno-positive inclusions that co-localized with CGG repeat RNA, suggesting that a polyarginine polypeptide may be present since it would be in frame with the full length LRP12 protein [20]. Whether the LRP12polyG polypeptide colocalizes with CGG repeat RNA is not yet known. Future studies will be needed to understand the contribution of RNA vs. polyG mediated toxicity in OPDM.
