AXL and MERTK facilitate tissue repair in severe acute pancreatitis via a CCR5-dependent neutrophil and macrophage crosstalk
Authors: Bin Li, Xiuli Zhang, Song Liu, Xiaoyu Guo, Wanyi Lu, Kaixin Peng, Rujuan Liu, Zhigao Chen, Liang Li, Guoyong Hu, Sohail Husain, Xingpeng Wang, Li Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) is a potentially life-threatening inflammatory disorder of the exocrine pancreas, characterized by massive cell death, which drives the progression and resolution of the disease. However, little is known about the key regulators in the tissue microenvironment that mediate tissue damage and repair. In this study, we discovered that AXL and MERTK in macrophages are responsible for tissue repair and pancreatic inflammation following SAP. Targeted deletion of Axl and Mertk in myeloid cells resulted in impaired phenotypic switch towards pro-resolving macrophage. This impairment is partly due to an accumulation of Cxcr2+ neutrophils and its interaction with Mrc1+/high macrophages likely via CCL4-CCR5 axis. Pancreatic tissue repair was effectively restored by CCR5 inhibition. Collectively, we identify a CCR5-dependent pathway orchestrated by AXL and MERTK in macrophages, which offers a pharmacological target, to promote tissue repair in SAP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02412-8.

Introduction

Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), accompanied by systemic inflammatory response syndrome and multiple organ failure, has a mortality rate of up to 30% and currently lacks specific drug therapies [1, 2]. Patients with SAP, often develop additional local complications during the resolution stage, leading to significantly prolonged recovery times and extended hospital stays [3]. The progression and outcomes of the disease are closely related to the extent of tissue damage and repair. Previous studies showed that SAP is characterized by rapid and massive cell death [4, 5]. Almost all the known pattern of cell death pathways, such as the classical pathways of apoptosis and necrosis, as well as newly identified pathways like necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, have been investigated [6–12]with clear correlations to the severity of SAP. Most studies have focused on the crucial regulators primarily in pancreatic acinar or ductal cells, and have shown how the activation of cell death pathways in these cells is tightly regulated, resulting in the identification of potential therapeutic targets for the early treatment of SAP. However, the narrow therapeutic window time calls for exploration of the drug that can limit tissue damage and facilitate tissue repair at the later stage of this deadly disease.

Pancreatitis is also characterized by massive infiltration of immune cells, such as neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells or T/B cells [4, 13]. During acute pancreatitis (AP), the infiltration of inflammatory cells can directly act on pancreatic acinar cells or participate in pancreatic tissue damage and repair through the release of inflammatory cytokines [5, 14]. Several recent studies have shown dynamic changes in macrophage phenotypes during the injury and reparative phases following AP. M2 polarization of macrophages during the reparative phase promote pancreatic tissue regeneration, and activated macrophages may create a specific immune microenvironment for tissue repair after pancreatic injury, aiding the regeneration of damaged acinar cells [15, 16]. Manohar et al. recently identified a new class of M2 macrophages expressed MHCIIhiLy6Gchi during the recovery phase from mild AP, while an MHCIIhiCD54hiLAP-TGFhi subset is associated with ongoing injury from SAP [17]further highlighting the crucial role of macrophages in mediating pancreatitis severity and tissue repair following SAP. However, it remains largely unknown which regulators or sensors expressed in these immune cells within the microenvironment orchestrate the signals from the activation of cell death pathways, particularly those involving apoptotic cells, to sense and respond to tissue damage and repair.

Accumulative evidence suggests that several receptors, such as CD36, TIM-1, TAM receptor tyrosine kinases, and integrins, are expressed in macrophages or neutrophils, where they sense signals from dying apoptotic cells, either alone or with phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) [18, 19]. TAM receptor tyrosine kinases consist of three homologous receptor tyrosine kinases: TYRO3, AXL and MERTK. These receptors were activated by binding to their specific ligands, growth arrest-specific protein 6 (GAS6) and protein S (PROS1), along with phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) on the outer membranes of apoptotic cells to participate in the clearance of these cells, a process known as efferocytosis [20, 21]. Bosurgi et al. elegantly demonstrated that AXL and MERTK-mediated clearance of apoptotic cells, in conjunction with IL-4 or IL-13, promotes tissue repair and remodeling in the lungs post-helminth infection or in the gut post-colitis induction [22]. Most recently, the same group demonstrated that distinct apoptotic cells recognized by macrophage AXL and MERTK determine the functional diversification of these cells during liver injury and repair [23]. Some studies have indicated that AXL and MERTK negatively regulate inflammatory responses [24]while we previously reported that global and bone marrow cell-specific deletion of AXL and MERTK protect against pancreatic necrosis through selectively limiting CXCL2-related neutrophil infiltration into the pancreas [25]. However, it remains unknown whether AXL and MERTK, and in which specific immune cell types, mediate tissue damage and repair in SAP by sensing apoptotic cells.

In this study, we found that AXL and MERTK, but not TYRO3 and other receptors sensing apoptotic cell signals, were selectively expressed in pancreatic macrophages, and the levels of AXL and MERTK in pancreatic macrophages are correlated with the extent of tissue damage and repair in SAP. Furthermore, Axl and Mertk deficiency in macrophages delayed pancreatic tissue repair without affecting tissue damage, leading to the accumulation of neutrophils and impairing the phenotypic switch of macrophages towards a pro-resolving status. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed an enhanced ligand-receptor interaction between Cxcr2+ neutrophils and Mrc1+/high macrophages likely related to the CCL4-CCR5 axis. CCR5 inhibition facilitates pancreatic tissue repair after SAP, serving as a promising therapeutic strategy for SAP treatment, with a broader therapeutic window.

Results

AXL and MERTK are selectively expressed in pancreatic macrophages, and their levels correlate with the extent of tissue damage and repair during severe acute pancreatitis

Receptors that sense the signals from apoptotic cells, including TAM receptors (TYRO3, AXL, and MERTK) are primarily expressed in innate immune cells, particularly macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs) [21, 25]. To assess their expression pattern in the immune cells of the pancreas, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing using CD45+ cells sorted from the pancreas at baseline (without pancreatitis) and Day 4 post-pancreatitis induction. Unsupervised Seurat-based clustering, combined with established immune cell-specific markers, identified six major immune cell types: B cells, DCs, macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, neutrophils and T cells (Fig. 1A). Axl and Mertk were selectively expressed on pancreatic macrophages, while Tyro3, Timd4, Adgrb1, and Itgb3 were minimally expressed on both pancreatic macrophages and infiltrating immune cells. Additionally, Cd36 and Itgav did not exhibit a cell-specific expression pattern; they were expressed not only on pancreatic macrophages but also on DCs and/or NK cells (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, we observed that upon pancreatitis induction, Axl and Mertk in macrophages were further upregulated, while their levels in other immune cells remained undetectable (Fig. 1C). When the six major immune cell types were further divided into several sub-clusters, we observed that Axl and Mertk expression across four macrophage sub-clusters exhibited distinct expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1A and B). Next, we examined the dynamic changes of AXL and MERTK in pancreatic macrophages over the course of SAP by flow cytometry (Fig. 1D). We found that their expression levels up-regulated on Day 2, then gradually declined from day 4 to day 14, and reached levels similar to those seen at baseline by Day 14 (Fig. 1E). Since two distinct macrophage clusters were observed in Fig. 1D, we re-analyzed the data by separately gating these two clusters: F4/80high and F4/80mid (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Specifically, the AXL and MERTK expression patterns in F4/80high macrophages showed distinct dynamics: AXL began to upregulate on Day 4 and remained elevated until Day 8, while MERTK started increasing on Day 2 and maintained high levels through Day 14 (Supplementary Fig. 1D and E). In contrast, the expression levels of AXL and MERTK in F4/80mid macrophages were consistent with their expression shown in Fig. 1E (Supplementary Fig. 1F and G). Additionally, we assessed the temporal expression profiles of Axl and Mertk in pancreatic tissue by targeted qPCR and observed significant upregulation of Axl and Mertk expression at Day 2, followed by gradual decline during the resolution phase (Fig. 1F). The findings were consistent with the observations in pancreatic macrophages, specifically F4/80mid population. Collectively, these results suggest that AXL and MERTK in macrophages play a role in mediating tissue repair in SAP.

Myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk impairs pancreatic tissue repair, leading to the accumulation of apoptotic cells during severe acute pancreatitis

To determine the role of AXL and MERTK in macrophages on tissue damage and repair, we obtained mouse lines with Axl and Mertk deletions from Dr Carla Rothlin at Yale University School of Medicine [22, 23]. Given that AXL and MERTK are predominantly expressed in pancreatic macrophages, studies have shown that pancreatic macrophages in response to pancreatitis are mainly replenished by circulating monocytes from the bone marrow [26, 27]. Myeloid-cell specific deletion of Axl and Mertk (AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ) was generated by crossing Axlf/fMertkf/f mice with a Cre line targeting myelomonocytic cells (LysM-Cre). SAP was induced by hourly injections of caerulein for two consecutive days, with pancreatic damage assessed on Day 2, and tissue repair evaluated on Day 4, 6, 8, 14, 21. We found that myeloid-cell specific deletion of Axl and Mertk (AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ) did not affect pancreatic damage on Day 2, but significantly delayed tissue repair following caerulein-induced pancreatitis, as assessed by histological scores of lobular integrity, acinar de-differentiation, and inflammatory infiltrate (Fig. 2A and B). Furthermore, we found that the restoration of Amy2a, which encodes amylase for acinar cells and Krt19, known as CK19 for ductal cells, was markedly delayed in Axl- and Mertk-deficient mice (Fig. 2C). Next, we evaluated pancreatic fibrosis using Masson’s trichrome staining and observed that myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk resulted in a delayed differences of fibrosis, starting on Day 6 (Fig. 2D). To exclude the model-specific effects of caerulein-induced experimental pancreatitis on AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice, we established another experimental model of SAP by retrograde biliopancreatic infusion of sodium taurocholate (NaT). The extent of tissue damage was assessed one day after pancreatitis induction, and tissue repair was evaluated four days after pancreatitis induction (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Similarly, the results showed that myeloid-cell specific deletion of Axl and Mertk did not affect pancreatic damage on Day 1, but significantly delayed tissue repair in the pancreas following NaT-induced pancreatitis on Day 4 (Supplementary Fig. 2B and C). Consistent with caerulein model, we observed a significant upregulation of Axl and Mertk at the tested time points in NaT model (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Collectively, these data strongly suggest that AXL and MERTK derived from circulating monocytes play a crucial role in pancreatic tissue repair after SAP.

Since AXL and MERTK in macrophages have been shown to mediate the phagocytosis of apoptotic cells to promote tissue repair [20, 22]we next performed immunostaining of CD45 and cleaved caspase-3. Our results showed that myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk resulted in sustained immune cell infiltration in the pancreas and the accumulation of apoptotic cells across all the time points tested (Fig. 2E and F), which in turn sustained the inflammatory responses and unresolved tissue injury during SAP.

Myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk impairs phenotypic switch towards reparative macrophages in the pancreas

Studies have suggested that the macrophage phenotypic switch is crucial for creating a supportive microenvironment necessary for pancreatic tissue repair following SAP [15, 28] and AXL and MERTK are known to induce a reparative macrophage phenotype and gene signature through the phagocytosis of apoptotic cells [22, 29, 30]. Therefore, we sorted CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages from the pancreas of AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ and WT mice to examine the changes of pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, and pro-reparative gene expression during the first week of SAP (Fig. 3A). We found that pancreatitis induces up-regulation of pro-inflammatory genes, such as Il1b, Il6 and Tnf starting on Day 2, with the levels gradually declining and approaching baseline by Day 8. However, myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk significantly up-regulated those genes to a greater extent, starting on Day 2 and continuing across all the time points tested (Fig. 3B). On the other hand, the reparative genes, including Mrc1, Retnla and Arg1 in the sorted macrophages were impaired since Day 4, although Mrc1 and Arg1 were transiently up-regulated on Day 2 in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice (Fig. 3C). Next, using flow cytometry, we further analyzed changes of macrophage phenotype over the course of SAP. The number of iNOS+ (M1) macrophages in the pancreas did not differ between two groups, although the number in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice exhibited a decrease on Day 2 (Fig. 3D), suggesting that AXL and MERTK have limited role in mediating the M1 macrophage phenotype. On the other hand, the number of Arginase+ (M2) macrophages in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice was up-regulated at Day 2, but remained consistently lower than their WT counterparts starting on Day 4 (Fig. 3E), highlighting a crucial role of AXL and MERTK in promoting M2 macrophage phenotypic switch. Similar results in Arg1 gene expression were observed in the sorted macrophages (Fig. 3C). Taken together, these data suggest that due to insufficient phagocytosis, myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk impairs macrophage phenotypic switch towards a reparative status in the pancreas, but ultimately hindering pancreatic tissue repair, though not affecting tissue damage.

Myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk enhances the crosstalk between Cxcr2+ neutrophils and Mrc1+/high macrophages via CCL4-CCR5 axis

Effective tissue repair after injury depends on a well-coordinated interactions between the local immune microenvironment and tissue-regenerative responses [27, 31]. We next investigated the interactions between pancreatic infiltrating immune cells, particularly neutrophils and macrophages. To explore these interactions, we sorted CD45+ immune cells for single-cell RNA-sequencing, from the pancreas of AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ and WT littermates on Day 4 after the induction of SAP (Fig. 4A). Dimensionality reduction using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis and unsupervised clustering, along with integrating established canonical markers of immune cells with SingleR, revealed 17 cell types, including B cells, DCs, monocytes, macrophages, NKs, neutrophils, and T cells (Fig. 4B). The expression of selected marker genes confirmed the specificity of each marker for each cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Differential proportional analysis showed that among those 17 cell clusters, only Cxcr2+ neutrophils were markedly elevated in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Using flow cytometry, we further examined the dynamic changes of infiltrating immune cells in the pancreas over the first week after SAP. Our results verified that the number of pancreatic infiltrating neutrophils only on Day 4 were significantly elevated in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice, whereas at other tested time points nor the number of other immune cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and DCs at all the time points remained no differences (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 3C and D), suggesting a well-organized and effective clearance of pancreatic infiltrating neutrophils is crucial for pancreatic tissue repair after SAP.

Next, we employed CellPhone DB232 to examine the interaction between Cxcr2+ neutrophils and other innate immune cells, including the sub-clusters of monocytes, DCs, and macrophages. Notably, Cxcr2+ neutrophils showed stronger interactions with two clusters of macrophages, CD163+Mrc1+ and CD163−Mrc1high macrophages, as suggested by the number of enriched ligand-receptor pairs (Fig. 4E), and those predicted interactions remained identical between AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ and their littermate controls (data not shown). Among the predicted ligand-receptor pairs, three potentially interesting pairs, including CCL4-CCR5, IL1B-ADRB2 and PTPRC-CD22 were selected for further verification using in situ RNA hybridization. We observed that the number of RNA dots for CCL4 and CCR5 were much higher than those for PTPRC, CD22, IL1B, and ADRB2 (Fig. 4F). Additional analysis on single-cell RNA-seq dataset showed that Ccl4 and Ccr5 were expressed across various innate immune cell sub-clusters, including sub-clusters of monocytes, DC, macrophages and Cxcr2+ neutrophils. Notably, two Mrc1+/high macrophages, along with Siglech+ DC and two monocyte populations, showed selectively highly expression of CCR5 (Supplementary Fig. 4A). The co-localization analysis further revealed that, the number of double-positive dots for CCL4_CCR5 (Fig. 4G), but not PTPC_CD22 or IL1B_ADRB2, were significantly increased in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice (Supplementary Fig. 4B and C). Collectively, these data indicate that myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk enhances the crosstalk between Cxcr2+ neutrophils and Mrc1+/high macrophages, most likely via CCL4-CCR5 axis.

CCR5 inhibition accelerates pancreatic tissue repair and inflammation resolution

Given that an enhanced crosstalk between Cxcr2 + neutrophils and Mrc1+/high macrophages were via CCL4-CCR5 axis, we hypothesized that impaired pancreatic tissue repair, due to myeloid cell-specific Axl and Mertk deficiency may be compensated through disruption of the enhanced interaction between those immune cells by inhibiting CCR5. We tested the therapeutic effects of CCR5 inhibition on pancreatic tissue repair after SAP. Maraviroc (50 mg/kg), an FDA-approved CCR5 selective antagonist [33] was intraperitoneally given daily starting right after the induction of SAP and pancreatic tissue repair were evaluated on Day 4 after the induction (Fig. 5A). Pancreatic histopathological scoring revealed that CCR5 inhibition facilitated pancreatic tissue repair in both WT and AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice (Fig. 5B and C). Pancreatic immune cell infiltration assessed by CD45 immunostaining, was similarly reduced in both groups with CCR5 antagonist. More importantly, we found that with CCR5 inhibition, CD86+ macrophages (indicative of M1 macrophages [34]) in the pancreas remained unchanged in WT mice while were significantly reduced in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice, and ARG1+ macrophages were increased in WT mice while restored in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice. Pancreatic neutrophil infiltration assessed by Ly6B.2 immunostaining, was increased in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice, but was markedly decreased with CCR5 antagonist in AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ and WT mice (Fig. 5D and E). These data suggest that during SAP, a subset of macrophages expressed CCR5 can likely be reprogramed towards pro-reparative phenotype in the presence or even absence of Axl and Mertk.

In addition, CCR5 inhibition resulted in a reduction of pancreatic fibrosis and myofibroblast activation, assessed by Masson’s Trichrome staining and αSMA immunostaining, respectively in both groups (Fig. 6A and B). Expression of ECM remodeling, including Mmp9 and Adam8, was similarly reduced (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these results demonstrated that CCR5 inhibition accelerated pancreatic tissue repair and promoted the resolution of pancreatic inflammation after SAP, further highlighting an alternative therapeutic strategy to overcome the impaired clearance of neutrophils mediated by macrophage AXL and MERTK deficiency.

Discussion

Patients with SAP are characterized by pancreatic necrosis and persistent organ failure, which are two determinants of disease severity and outcomes. SAP also accompanied by more local complications, experience significantly prolonged recovery time and extended hospital stay [3, 35–37]. In this study, we demonstrated that Axl and Mertk were selectively and inducible expressed on pancreatic macrophages and play a crucial role in mediating tissue repair after SAP. Using myeloid cell-specific Axl and Mertk deficient mouse model, we demonstrated that the impaired pancreatic tissue repair following SAP was due to the accumulation of neutrophils, resulting in the defective phenotypic switch of macrophages towards a pro-resolving status in the pancreas. Furthermore, CCR5 inhibition facilitates pancreatic tissue repair. The findings from the current study provide a valuable window of opportunity for interventions aimed at facilitating pancreatic tissue repair and recovery after the induction of pancreatitis. Bosurgi et al. have shown that IL-4 or IL-13, not alone but in combination with apoptotic neutrophils, is sufficient to induce a tissue repair-related gene signature in macrophages. They further demonstrated that genetic ablation of Axl and Mertk in macrophages impaired tissue repair programs in the lungs after helminth infection and in the gut after colitis [22]. Chang et al. also demonstrated that monocyte-derived macrophages, through AXL and MERTK-mediated erythrocyte engulfment and hematoma clearance, contribute to neurological recovery after intracerebral hemorrhage [29]. We previously showed that global and bone marrow-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk protected against pancreatic necrosis [25]. However, the current study did not address whether AXL and MERTK in macrophages directly interact with pancreatic parenchymal cells, such as pancreatic acinar or ductal cells. Future studies are needed to investigate or identify direct interactors expressed in immune cells that regulate pancreatic cellular injury and regeneration.

Pro-reparative macrophage phenotypic switch is crucial for creating an essential microenvironment to support pancreatic tissue repair after severe acute pancreatitis [15, 28]. Macrophage AXL and MERTK have been shown to facilitate tissue repair via the phagocytosis of apoptotic cells, which induces a reparative macrophage phenotype during several disease conditions, such as intracerebral hemorrhage, lung helminth infection and colitis [22, 29]. More recently, Liebold et al. have shown that macrophage AXL and MERTK selectively phagocytosed apoptotic neutrophils, and to a lesser extent, apoptotic T cells, and therefore to induce distinct macrophage profiles [23]. Consistently, we showed that in the context of SAP, myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk leads to insufficient phagocytosis of neutrophils (but not other immune cells), which in turn impairs the macrophage phenotypic switch towards a reparative status in the pancreas. By employing scRNA-seq, we selectively observed the accumulation of Cxcr2+ neutrophils on Day 4 post induction of SAP. Using flow cytometry, we further verified that the accumulation of neutrophils on Day 4, but not other time points tested or other immune cells in the pancreas is the most important factor that contribute to impaired tissue repair due to AXL and MERTK deficiency. This observation further highlights that a well-organized and efficient clearance of pancreatic infiltrating neutrophil is critical for restoring tissue homeostasis after pancreatitis. Several studies in heart and liver have also shown that neutrophils play a crucial role for tissue regeneration after injury [38, 39]. Recent studies have highlighted the heterogeneity of neutrophils in both normal and diseased conditions across various systems [40–42]. Therefore, future studies are required to decipher the heterogeneity and functionality of pancreatic neutrophil during SAP, to better understand their kinetics in mediating pancreatic tissue damage and repair.

Furthermore, we identified that the impaired pancreatic tissue repair capacity due to Axl and Mertk deficiency can be compensated by CCR5 inhibition. Recent studies have shown that suppressing CCR5 alleviated cardiomyocyte loss and myocardial injury [43]and genetic deletion of CCR5 subsequently attenuates the development of insulin resistance [44]. We found CCR5 antagonist accelerated pancreatic tissue repair and promoted the resolution of pancreatic inflammation after SAP, especially when macrophage AXL and MERTK were deficient. More importantly, the CCR5 antagonist is an FDA-approved drug [33] that could significantly shorten the translational timeline for use in acute pancreatitis. Since Moreno et al. found that global deletion of CCR5 exacerbates pancreatic injury in the acute phase of caerulein-induced AP [45]the use of CCR5 inhibitors should be administered within the appropriate therapeutic window. Collectively, the findings from our current study suggested a therapeutic strategy for facilitating pancreatic tissue repair, offering unique advantages for improving the management of SAP.

In summary (Fig. 7), our findings demonstrated that AXL and MERTK derived from monocytes are responsible for tissue repair without affecting pancreatic damage following SAP. Myeloid cell-specific deletion of Axl and Mertk impaired the clearance of neutrophils, resulting in macrophage phenotypic switch towards pro-reparative status in the pancreas. Further, we identified enhanced crosstalk between Cxcr2+ neutrophils and Mrc1+/high macrophages likely via the CCL4-CCR5 axis in macrophage Axl- and Mertk-deficient mice. Finally, we proposed a novel therapeutic strategy, the use of an FDA-approved CCR5 inhibitor, which has great potential for clinical application in treating SAP through facilitating pancreatic tissue repair.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J (catalog SM-001) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). Mice with conditional deletion of AXL and MERTK in myeloid-specific macrophages (AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ) was generated on the C57BL/6J background, and they were acquired from Dr. Carla V. Rothlin, Yale School of Medicine, Yale University. Floxed littermates (Axlf/fMertkf/f) were used as controls for AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ mice. All mice were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with free access to standard food. All mice used in this study were 8- to 12-week, and both male and female mice were evenly used in all experiments. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital (2019-A019–01) and Peking Union Medical College Hospital (XHDW-2023-154).

Induction of severe acute pancreatitis and treatments

Caerulein hyperstimulation pancreatitis model was induced by 10 hourly i.p injections of 100 µg/kg caerulein (HY-A0190, MedChemExpress) per day for 2 consecutive days; Biliary acute pancreatitis was induced by retrograde biliopancreatic ductal infusion of sodium taurocholate (NaT, 2.5%, 2 µl/g)46; mice were sacrificed humanly at indicated time points. In some experiments, 50 mg/kg Maraviroc (HY-13004, MedChemExpress) or vehicle was administrated by i.p injections daily immediately after the last caerulein injection of severe acute pancreatitis model.

Histology, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Pancreatic tissues fixed with 4% formalin were embedded with paraffin. Tissues were then sectioned into 4 μm slices for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining using standard procedures. Histology score of pancreas tissues was evaluated as previously reported [47]. In brief, 10 random images from each slide were scored from 0 to 5 for lobular integrity, acinar de-differentiation and immune cell infiltration in a blinded fashion by two experienced pathologists. Pancreatic histological scoring system for tissue repair is listed in Supplementary Table 1. For immunohistochemistry, pancreatic sections were dewaxed, rehydrated and then subjected to antigen retrieval by EDTA solution (E673004, Sangon Biotech). Sections were subsequently incubated with antibody against Cleaved Caspase-3 (9661, Cell Signaling Technology), CD45 (70257, Cell Signaling Technology), αSMA (ab5694, Abcam), ARG1 (93668s, Cell Signaling Technology), CD86 (19589s, Cell Signaling Technology) and Ly6B.2 (MCA771GA, BioRad), visualized using an immunohistochemistry kit (PK4001, Vector Laboratories), and imaged by microscope (DFC550, Leica).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was purified from pancreatic tissue or tissue-derived macrophages using Trizol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen). RNAs (500 ng) were then reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix kit (RR036A, Takara). RT-PCR was performed by TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ kit (RR420A, Takara) using a QuantStudio 7 Flex RT-PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Expression of mRNA levels were calculated by comparative CT method (2−ΔΔCT) and normalized to Rplp0. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Leukocyte isolation from pancreas

Pancreatic tissue was minced and digested for 20 min at 37 °C with digestion buffer containing 2 mg/ml collagenase type IV (C5138, Sigma Aldrich). Tissue fragments were resuspended in ice-cold stopping buffer containing 10% FBS in 40 mL PBS, and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Cells were centrifuged and red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed with 1 mL ACK lysing buffer (A10492-01, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 min. The remaining leukocytes were washed twice with FACS buffer containing 2% FBS in PBS for single cell RNA sequencing analysis or flow cytometry.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

CD45+ cells from pancreatic leukocytes were sorted using a MagniSort™ Mouse CD45 Positive Selection Kit (8802 − 6865, Invitrogen). Dead cells were removed using a Dead Cell Removal Kit (130-090-101, Miltenyi Biotec). Single cells were encapsulated into droplet emulsion using GemCode Technology (10× Genomics) according to manufacturer’s specifications. cDNAs were reverse transcribed, barcoded and amplified for library construction using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v3.1 (1000268, 10× Genomics). cDNA libraries were sequenced across an Illumina Nova 6000 PE150 platform in paired-end reads.

Raw reads were demultiplexed using Cell Ranger v7.0.1 and aligned to the mm10 reference genome using STAR aligner. Gene count matrices were generated according to the alignment results. Ribosomal genes were filtered from the detected gene matrix and the cells of low quality were removed according to the following criteria: the number of genes < 800 or > 6000, the number of unique molecule identifiers (UMIs) > 30,000 and the percentage of mitochondrial genes > 10%. The remaining data was normalized and log transformed by scanpy package (SCANPY: large-scale single-cell gene expression data analysis) in python3. The highly variable genes were identified and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with the default parameters. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for data visualization in two dimensions and “Louvain” algorithm was applied to identify the cell clusters. The cells markers for each cluster were identified using the “rank genes groups” and the cell types were annotated based on the top expression genes of each cluster. The cell count for each cell type within each sample were calculated and differential proportion analysis was performed to detect changes in proportions of cell populations between Axlf/fMertkf/f and AxlLysMΔMertkLysMΔ group. CellPhoneDB is a novel repository of ligands, receptors and their interactions, which is integrated with a statistical framework that predicts enriched cellular interactions between two cell types from single-cell transcriptomics data [32]. Ligand-receptor interactions were identified using the CellphoneDB (version 4.1.0, https://www.cellphonedb.org/) with default parameters and visualized by heatmap and dotplot using R package. The original data were submitted to the NCBI’s Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under accession PRJNA1147010.

Flow cytometry and fluorescence-activated cell sorting

Pancreatic leukocytes or BMDMs were incubated with Fc block (553142, BD bioscience) for 10 min in FACS buffer. For surface staining, cells were incubated with the following antibodies: Pacific blue-CD45 (clone S18009F, Biolegend), FITC-CD11b (clone M1/70, eBioscience), PE-F4/80 or PE/Cy7-F4/80 (clone BM8, Biolegend), PE/Cy7-AXL (clone MAXL8DS, Biolegend) and APC/Cy7-MERTK (clone DS5MMER, Biolegend). For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Kit (554714, BD bioscience), and then stained with PE-iNOS (clone CXNFT, eBioscience) and APC-Arginase (clone AlexF5, eBioscience). Cells were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software, version 10.8.1 (Tree Star). To acquire pancreatic macrophages, CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ cells were sorted using a BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences).

Masson’s trichrome staining

Pancreatic fibrosis was determined using a trichrome staining kit (ab150686, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA in situ hybridization and quantification

RNA in situ hybridization was performed using a RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (323100, Advanced Cell Diagnostics [ACD]) for formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the slides were treated with hydrogen peroxide solution, target retrieval reagents and protease IV in a hybridization oven. Slides were then incubated with the following ACD mouse probes: Ccl4, Ccr5, Ptprc, Cd22, Il1b and Adrb2. After hybridization, slides were subjected to six amplification steps using amplification buffers. Images were captured using Nikon AXR microscope at 60× magnification. Image analysis was performed using the open-source software CellProfiler [48]. We designed a CellProfiler pipeline to quantify the number of RNA dots in each fluorescence channel and their colocalization, as previously reported [49]. Colocalization analysis was conducted using the RelateObjects module in CellProfiler to quantify the number of double-positive RNA dots in each image.

Statistical analyses

Data are shown as mean ± SD. All statistical analysis was performed using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software). Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t test. To compare data from multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post test was performed. In experiments with more than one independent variable, comparisons were assessed by two-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparison test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis and graphs were made with Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). No statistical method was used to pre-determine the sample size. No experimental data were excluded.
