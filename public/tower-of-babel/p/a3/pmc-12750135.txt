Systemic Autoimmune Rheumatic Disease Risk: Association With Long‐Term Exposure to Fine Particulate Matter
Authors: Mareva Geslin, Julien Vachon, Naizhuo Zhao, Elhadji Anassour Laouan Sidi, Sonia Jean, Audrey Smargiassi, Sasha Bernatsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a possible trigger of systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARDs). We investigated SARDs risk related to long‐term exposure to PM2.5 and its components (ammonium, black carbon, mineral dust, sea salt, nitrate, sulfate, organic matter), the composition of which may affect toxicity.MethodsWe assembled an open cohort of Quebec adults (without SARDs) using administrative health data from April 2000 to December 2019. Our SARD case definition included physician billing and hospitalization diagnostic codes for systemic lupus, dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis, Sjögren disease, and undifferentiated connective tissue disease. Estimates of mean annual PM2.5 and its components were available from modeling using satellite aerosol optical depth images and ground‐based observations. Exposures were assigned to each resident based on six‐character postal codes, updated over time. Cox models (adjusted for age, sex, year, socioeconomic status, Local Service Network, and urban/rural region) generated hazard ratios (HRs) for PM2.5 and its seven components. Quantile‐based g‐computational models with similar adjustments were used to estimate marginal HRs for the mixture of PM2.5 components.ResultsWe studied 7,482,397 Quebec residents. Over 98,039,305 person‐years, 55,267 SARDs cases were identified. Using quantile g‐computational models, the adjusted SARDs HR for a one‐decile increase in PM2.5 components was 1.01 (95% confidence interval 1.00–1.02). Among the seven components, ammonium contributed most to SARDs risk.ConclusionThis large general population cohort study suggests that ambient PM2.5 (and ammonium in particular) may be associated with SARDs incidence.

INTRODUCTION

Air pollution is a global public health problem, and among its components, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is considered a major environmental risk factor. 1 These particles, which have a median aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 μm, are of very diverse composition that depends on their sources. 2 Because of their small size, PM2.5 can penetrate the respiratory tract and reach the bloodstream, causing systemic effects such as cardiovascular diseases and possibly birth outcomes, diabetes, dementia, and rheumatic diseases. 3 , 4

Among rheumatic diseases, systemic autoimmune inflammatory diseases (SARDs) are of particular concern because they can lead to devastating organ damage and even death. Their etiology remains incompletely known. 5 SARDs include conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic scleroderma (SSc) and other inflammatory conditions of the joints and other organs. 6

Several studies have investigated the potential links between ambient PM2.5 and the incidence of certain SARDs and other rheumatic diseases. We previously demonstrated that industrial air pollution emissions were associated with SARDs risk using Ontario health administrative data. 7 We also found that exposure to PM2.5 components was associated with rheumatoid arthritis risk in Ontario. 8 Although the literature generally reported positive associations between PM2.5 and SARDs, there have been some potential discrepancies in results. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 These may be due to differences in study design and populations studied or regional differences in PM2.5 composition. Most epidemiologic studies of SARDs have only considered total PM2.5 concentrations. However, increasingly recognized is that particle size and chemical composition may have a distinct and important role in terms of toxicity. 10 , 13 , 14 Our objective was to evaluate (1) long‐term exposure to outdoor PM2.5 and its components and (2) the occurrence of new SARDs cases in a cohort of Quebec adults.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

We constructed a retrospective population‐based open cohort from the Quebec Integrated Chronic Disease Surveillance System (QICDSS). 15 The QICDSS provides demographic data (sex, age, and death date, where relevant) on all provincial beneficiaries of Quebec's health plan, which provides universal insurance for visits to physicians and emergency departments as well as hospitalizations. The QICDSS has information on six‐character postal codes throughout time. For our study, individuals reaching the age of 18 years during the follow‐up period were enrolled if, in the previous 4 years, they lived in the province of Quebec and they had no physician billing or hospitalization diagnostic codes for SARDs (see Case definition section). Data for years lived in health regions 17 and 18 (Nunavik and James Bay Cree Lands, respectively) were excluded due to systematic incompleteness data in these areas (related to greater use of nursing stations rather than physician visits for ongoing care). Individuals were followed until the earliest of the following: date of SARD diagnosis, death, emigration out of the province, or end of follow‐up (December 31, 2019).

Case definition

New SARDs cases were identified based on two or more relevant International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnostic codes relating to physician claims eight weeks or more apart but within two years or one or more relevant ICD diagnostic codes relating to hospitalization. The ICD‐9 code was 710.x, and ICD‐10 codes were M32.1, M32.8 to M32.9, M33.x, M34.x, M35.0, M35.5, and M35.9. These ICD codes include SLE, Sjögren disease, SSc, polymyositis, dermatomyositis, and undifferentiated connective tissue diseases; all ICD codes are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. The algorithm used to identify cases has been validated and has shown high sensitivity and specificity. 16

Exposure estimation

Long‐term exposure to PM2.5 and its components was estimated with the use of annual concentrations at the residential six‐digit postal codes of the individuals throughout their follow‐up. If an individual moved during the course of a year, different exposure values were assigned based on the time‐varying postal codes.

Annual concentrations of PM2.5 and its components (version V4.NA.03) were obtained from the website of the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group https://sites.wustl.edu/acag/datasets/surface-pm2-5/). PM2.5 was estimated at 1‐km2 resolution by combining satellite aerosol optical depth data, Goddard Earth Observing System (GEOS) chemical transport modeling, and ground observations. 17 , 18 The proportions of seven main PM2.5 components (ie, black carbon [BC], sulfate [SO4 2−], ammonium [NH4 +], nitrate [NO3 −], organic matter [OM], sea salt [SS], and mineral dust [DUST]) were then calculated using the GEOS modeling results and calibration with ground observations. 18

Covariates

Because SARDs risk varies with age and sex, 16 we adjusted for these two covariates. Previous studies also suggest that socioeconomic status (SES) can influence the SARDs risk. 12 To adjust for SES, we used the Pampalon material deprivation index, an indicator of lack of financial resources. 19 This index is available for Canadian census dissemination areas (DAs), which comprise units of 400 to 800 individuals across several postal codes. 20 The DA value of the Pampalon material deprivation index for the year closest to the census year was assigned to each individual's follow‐up years. The Local Service Network (LSN) region of residence was also used as a covariate to account for regional variations in local health services throughout the follow‐up. There are 93 LSN regions in Quebec, 21 each including multiple postal codes. In additional analyses, we used Statistics Canada's classification system (ie, major census metropolitan areas, medium and small urban areas, and rural areas) to assign residences as either urban or rural regions. 22 We adjusted for urban versus rural regions for more than one reason. First, there may be effect modification, as the distribution of PM2.5 components here may differ by urban versus rural region. Also, our definition of SARDs onset requires access to medical care, which often differs by urban versus rural area.

Statistical analyses

Correlations between PM2.5 (total mass concentration) and its components were assessed with Pearson tests after verifying the normality of the distribution of these variables. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between time‐varying mean annual concentrations of pollutants (ie, PM2.5 and its seven components separately) and overall SARDs risk. PM2.5 models were built with age (in days) as the time axis and were incrementally adjusted for covariates. The first model was adjusted for sex and calendar year (to account for time trends). Models were then further adjusted for SES (ie, Pampalon deprivation index) and for LSN regions (Supplementary Table S2). This model was also used to separately estimate associations with each of the seven most important PM2.5 subcomponents. We verified the Cox proportionality assumption for all variables using stratification. All HRs were reported per increase of one interquartile range (IQR) of PM2.5 or components. Next, nonlinear models with cubic spline functions were generated to confirm the linearity of the models. Finally, we used quantile g‐computation with adjustment for all demographic and regional variables to estimate the marginal HR and 95% CI for time to SARD onset with an increase of one decile in all the seven components (ie, the mixture of the seven most important component). 23 All analyses were performed with R version 4.2.0 (2022‐04‐22, RStudio version 1.1.456).

We also stratified analyses by urban versus rural area, biologic sex, and SES. Also, we used spline functions to assess the relationship between our exposure and outcome. In exploratory analyses we looked at the two most common SARDs under study, SLE and Sjögren disease. Finally, the main g‐computation methods presented effects for a decile difference in PM2.5, but for a greater contrast, we presented the risk difference between people exposed to the highest decile of PM2.5 components and those exposed to the lowest decile.

RESULTS

A total of 7,499,351 individuals were included in the cohort, of whom 16,570 were subsequently excluded because they consistently lived in health regions 17 and 18 throughout the follow‐up. There were few missing data among exposure variables (<1% of the database); thus, a case‐complete approach was used. Analyses were performed for a total of 7,482,397 individuals, representing 98,039,305 person‐years of follow‐up (Supplementary Figure S1).

Table 1 and Supplementary Table S3 summarize the main characteristics of the population. The cohort was 51% female. The cohort included individuals from 92 LSNs and 16 health regions of Quebec (Montreal being the most populous with 27% of the population), with the majority living in large urban areas (50%) (Supplementary Table S3).

During follow‐up, 55,267 new SARDs cases were identified for an incidence of 5.6 cases per 10,000 person‐years. Among those identified, the majority of individuals were women (70%) and more than 50 years of age. Many resided in major urban centers, particularly in the Montreal region (26%) (Supplementary Table S4).

Table 2 and Supplementary Tables S5 and S6 show PM2.5 exposure levels and their components during the follow‐up. Across all years, OM was the main component of PM2.5 (mean 3.80 ± 1.38 μg/m3), whereas SS, NH4 +, DUST, and BC each accounted for <10% of the mixture. Pearson correlation tests showed that PM2.5, NH4 +, NO3 −, OM, and SO4 2− were positively correlated. Correlations were highest for nitrogen derivatives and SO4 2− (Supplementary Table S7).

The best‐fitting Cox model of the association between PM2.5 and SARD was the one fully adjusted for age, sex, year, SES, and LSN (model 3), with the exposure–response curve consistent with linearity, as the difference in the Akaike information criterion was negligeable between a linear and nonlinear model (Supplementary Table S8). Associations between PM2.5 and SARD were positive in all adjusted models (HRs ranging from 1.002 to 1.070 per IQR) (Table 3). Most adjusted estimates of the association between PM2.5 components and SARD onset were also positive, with the exception of those for SS and SO4 2−. However, 95% CIs of the estimates of association for all individual PM2.5 components (except NO3 − and OM) overlapped and included the null value (Table 4).

In the quantile g‐calculation model, the adjusted HR per one‐decile increase in all PM2.5 components was 1.01 (95% CI 1.00–1.02). The risk difference in SARDs onset between people exposed to the highest decile of PM2.5 components and those exposed to the lowest decile PM2.5 was 1.4 cases per 10,000 person‐years. The index weights for NH4 + (0.38), mineral DUST (0.29), OM (0.18), and NO3 − (0.16) were positive (suggesting a positive correlation with SARDs risk), whereas those for SO4 2− (0.46), BC (0.40), and SS (0.14) were negative (Table 5).

In stratified analyses, the adjusted HR for urban (metropolitan area) residents was 1.071 (95% CI 1.032–1.111) per IQR increase of 2.1 μg/m3. For rural (nonmetropolitan area) residents, the adjusted HR was 1.020 (95% CI 0.982–1.060) per IQR increase of 3.0 μg/m3. The adjusted HRs stratified by biologic sex were as follows: for men, 1.059 (95% CI 0.998–1.125) per IQR of 3.3 μg/m3; for women, 1.025 (95% CI 0.985–1.067) per IQR of 3.3 μg/m3.

Detailed estimates of HRs for upper and lower SES, adjusted for year, sex, LSN, and age (as the time axis), by quintile are provided in Supplementary Figure S2. Estimates for the lowest SES quintile were lower than those for the four other quintiles. For SES quintiles 1 to 4, the adjusted HR was 1.055 (95% CI 1.019–1.093) per IQR of 3.1 μg/m3. When looking at the two most common SARDs, the adjusted HR per IQR for SLE (n = 6,187) was 1.082 (95% CI 0.986–1.189), and the adjusted HR per IQR for Sjögren disease (n = 3,268) was 1.414 (95% CI 1.003–1.298) (Supplementary Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

We provided a comprehensive report of SARDs risk and both total PM2.5 and its main components, as well as modeling of the combined effects. Our results suggest positive associations between SARDs risk and PM2.5 components and the combined mixture (of seven components).

Based on the g‐computation model, we noted a 1% increase in SARDs risk per decile increment in PM2.5 components; we also observed a 2% increase in SARDs risk per IQR increase in PM2.5 (2000–2012). Zhao et al reported a 12% increase (HR 1.12; 95% CI 1.06–1.35) in SARDs risk for each 3.97‐μg/m3 increase in PM2.5. 12 Our results add to the mounting evidence that PM2.5 health effects extend far beyond the lung. Though exact mechanisms remain to be completely elucidated, it is known that PM2.5 has systemic proinflammatory effects and oxidative stress reactions. 3 PM2.5 composition may contribute to variation in this toxicity across regions and time.

Most epidemiologic studies of PM2.5 have considered total levels, not chemical composition. Our quantile g‐computation models showed that the largest index weights in the positive direction were for NH4 +, indicating that of the seven PM2.5 chemical components, NH4 + had the most effect. This result is consistent with a previous study in which NH4 + contributed the most to interstitial lung disease onset in patients with SARD. 24 NH4 + has been associated with other health outcomes, such as diabetes 25 and asthma. 14 NH4 + sources include diesel vehicles and industrial activities, nitrogen fertilizers and livestock farming, burning of crop residue, and grassland fires and wildfires. 26 As vehicle and industrial emissions become more regulated in the developed world, these other sources of PM2.5, particularly forest fires, are likely to be increasingly important.

Our study has several strengths, such as the use of a very large population‐based sample to limit selection bias. 15 Our case definition was based on both physician billing and hospitalization data, which improves sensitivity compared to only using one source. 16 We used the most advanced methods to assign exposures at the six‐digit postal codes level and included sophisticated modeling to allow us to study PM2.5 chemical component effects.

However, there are potential limitations. First, exposure misclassification is still possible, especially in rural areas, where a single postal code may cover large areas. Additionally, individuals do not remain at their residence all the time, and PM2.5 exposures can occur at work or during commutes. There is the possibility of residual confounding given the lack of individual data on some factors (such as smoking) in administrative health data. However, PM2.5 levels in Canada are negatively related to smoking; controlling for smoking would thus likely increase the association between PM2.5 and SARD. 27 We used validated approaches to identifying SARDs with administrative health data, but no definition is perfect. 28 Moreover, SARDs are relatively uncommon, so even with a very large general population cohort, the precision of estimates can be limited, particularly for results stratified by region, sex, and SES. We do note similar estimates for men versus women. With stratification by SES, the point estimate related to the lowest quintile was a bit lower than the other quintiles, but (as in all of the stratified analyses) the 95% CIs were less precise. It should be kept in mind that our indicator of SES is a regional variable; thus, results should be interpreted with caution.

The stratified HR point estimates were smaller for rural versus urban regions, and the CIs were wider. This could be due to misclassification of exposure (likely higher in rural areas due to larger postal code areas) as well as the absolute smaller number of cases in rural areas.

As another potential limitation, we really do not know precisely the date of disease onset (we only have the date that the individual met our criteria for SARDs ascertainment). However, this is true for essentially all studies of SARD epidemiology. We note that we did not adjust for frequency of ambulatory visits or comorbidity, nor did we use a negative control design. As a final potential limitation, there is no consensus as to which exposure period is most important, though it seems reasonable to hypothesize that the five years prior to case ascertainment might be more important than more remote exposures.

Future studies could assess associations between specific PM2.5 subcomponents and the severity and progression of SARD. Studies of PM2.5 pollution and SARDs in children and adolescents would also be of interest. 29

This large population‐based cohort study suggests that exposure to PM2.5 and to the mixture of its components may be associated with the development of SARDs. It reinforces that effects of exposure to PM2.5 may extend beyond the lungs, potentially to the immune system. Our study sheds light on the epidemiology of SARDs, which has for so long remained difficult to characterize.
