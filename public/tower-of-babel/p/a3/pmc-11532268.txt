Protocol for the generation of single-nuclei RNA-seq libraries and quantification of heterogeneous cell types activated during social interaction
Authors: Hailee Walker, Nicholas A Frost
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryQuantifying immediate early gene expression as a marker of cellular activity in single-nuclei RNA sequencing data allows for the identification of neurons involved in specific behaviors. Here, we present a protocol for generating single-nuclei libraries from the mouse brain and identifying active cell populations following social interactions. We describe steps for the dissection, preparation, and analysis of the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum. This protocol has the potential to be modified for any brain region or behavior of interest.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Walker and Frost.1Subject areas: Neuroscience, Behavior, Systems Biology

Before you begin

The activity of heterogeneous cell types in distributed brain regions including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) underlies complex behaviors including social interactions. Cortical microcircuits are comprised of diverse cellular populations with distinct functional roles in processing and communicating information. Quantifying the contribution of different cell types during social interaction therefore may be critical to understanding the computations and output of the circuits which underlie prefrontal regulation of normal social behavior2,3,4 as well as pathologically altered behaviors observed in disease states.5 This quantification has been hampered by technological limitations on the number of cell types which can be simultaneously assayed within the same animal during behavior. Previous studies have overcome this to examine the activation of different cell populations following seizure.6 We modified this approach and demonstrated that heterogeneous cell populations recruited during social interaction can be identified through quantification of immediate early genes (IEGs) coupled with transcriptional identification of different cell types following behavior.

This protocol describes the dissection and sample preparation of the PFC, hippocampus and cerebellum used to identify heterogeneous neuronal ensembles involved in social behavior.1 We describe our method to identify populations of neurons specifically activated during social behaviors; however this protocol could be used following other behaviors and for different brain regions.

Step-by-step method details

Below we describe a social behavior assay and the preparation of samples from brain regions involved in this behavior for snRNA-seq. Steps 1–2 describe the social interaction assay with novel juveniles. Steps 3–12 describe dissection of the PFC, hippocampus and cerebellum; however, any brain region of interest could be collected for the next nuclei isolation steps. For the nuclei isolation, steps 13–21 describe lysis of the tissue, 22–27 describes myelin removal, and 28–30 describes the final resuspension and preparation of samples for library construction for snRNA-seq (step 31). The remainder of the protocol describes read alignment, quality control and our analysis for identifying socially responsive cells.

Social investigation of novel juveniles

Timing: 1 h (varies by number of mice)

Experimental mice are exposed to a social interaction assay with sequential exposure to novel juveniles or to control cage lid opening.

1.Allow social mice to engage in sequential 5-min episodes of social interaction with novel conspecifics.

Allow social mice to engage in sequential 5-min episodes of social interaction with novel conspecifics.

Note: We use sex-matched, juvenile (< 6 week) conspecifics though mice of other ages could be used.

Note: As mice tend to interact most during the initial period of contact, we introduce a new novel juvenile every 5 min (and remove previous juvenile). Any fighting or aggression towards the novel juvenile should be noted as this may result in activation of alternate ensembles not directly related to social behavior.

Note: We have noted that lid removal results in a persistent increase in exploratory behavior and for this reason we remove the lid briefly every 5 min in control (non-social) mice.

2.Following completion of five 5-min interaction episodes, leave mice undisturbed in their home cage for an additional 10 min prior to dissection.

Following completion of five 5-min interaction episodes, leave mice undisturbed in their home cage for an additional 10 min prior to dissection.

Note: We have also extended the post-social interaction time prior to dissection to 35 min and immediate early genes can still be detected following sequencing, however, the number of nuclear IEG transcripts decreases in this interval.

CRITICAL: The beginning of the behavioral assay should be offset by 10 min per experimental mouse to provide sufficient time to complete each dissection.

Note: We routinely collect 8 samples from up to 4 mice in one morning and achieve high sample quality throughout nuclei isolation and library prep.

Expected outcomes

This protocol should produce suspensions of isolated nuclei that are ready for use in single-nuclei or bulk RNA-sequencing. The suspensions should be free of debris or clumping of nuclei. Additionally, samples should have little transcriptional perturbation allowing for the future identification of cells which were activated by social interactions through the quantification of immediate early genes.1

Limitations

While this protocol has been successfully reproducible for the brain regions which we have used it for, there are potentially regions of interest which may contain too few cells or cell types more sensitive to the dissociation process. This may result in unsuccessful isolation of a sufficient number of nuclei for sequencing. Additionally, we did not attempt to enrich our samples for any particular cell type as other protocols have done. Theoretically, the nuclei suspensions from this protocol could be FACS sorted to isolate cell types of interest or cells with a particular reporter such as GFP.15

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Samples will not filter through the Flowmi cell strainer at the end of Lysis (related to step 21).

Potential solution

Some regions, particularly those which are more myelinated, may be difficult to filter. In our experience this is often true for the cerebellum and spinal cord. In this case a new Flowmi strainer can be used for every 100 μL of sample. Additionally, for samples being produced from large sections of tissue this may occur. In this case the sample could be split into multiple tubes prior to lysis and then combined at the end of sample preparation. This improves lysis of the tissue through the 3 lysis buffer steps.

Problem 2

The nuclei pellet isn’t visible following the sucrose cushion step (related to step 24).

Potential solution

For small brain regions the nuclei pellet is often very difficult to see with the naked eye following the sucrose gradient. It is important to load samples in the centrifuge so that the pellet location is known following spinning. The supernatant should be removed from the opposite side of the tube as the pellet and a small amount (∼50 μL) can be left in the tube to prevent aspiration of the pellet. There are typically still enough nuclei in the pellet to target the desired number of nuclei in downstream snRNA-seq.

Problem 3

Dirty sample containing debris when examined under microscope for nuclei counting following nuclei isolation (related to steps 24–27).

Potential solution

For samples from larger brain regions such as the cerebellum we have observed that efficient removal of the myelin layer following the sucrose gradient becomes more difficult. We believe this is due to the region containing more myelin, which is more difficult to remove via pipetting. This results in the nuclei sticking together upon resuspension and the appearance of non-nuclei debris when the samples are examined for nuclei counting (Figures 7B and 7C). In some cases, the nuclei and remaining myelin can stick strongly and prevent resuspension, which will make the sample unusable (Figure 7B). We have found that using one pipette tip to first scoop much of the myelin layer off followed by a second clean pipette tip to pipette off the remaining myelin. Additionally, slowly pipetting off the sucrose supernatant from the tube while removing the myelin seems to improve the efficiency of sample cleanup.

Problem 4

Low nuclei yield from samples (related to step 28).

Potential solution

Nuclei yield largely depends on the size of the region you have dissected but will also vary by performance of the isolation protocol. In the case the low nuclei yield is due to the region size, bilateral regions from the same mouse or from multiple mice can be pooled into one sample prior to homogenization and nuclei isolation. Low yield may also occur in the case that nuclei have not pelleted well or have been aspirated during pipetting steps. To reduce this, ensure that all tubes are placed in the centrifuge in a manner that allows identification of where in the tube the pellet will attach. Then supernatant should be slowly aspirated from the opposite side of the tube.

Problem 5

Clumping of nuclei or a high count of “live” cells (related to step 28).

Potential solution

Nuclei will be counted as “dead” cells by the Countess3 cell counter. In the case many cells are showing up as “live” there may be an issue of insufficient lysis or clumping of nuclei. If cells were insufficiently lysed and there are intact cells, samples may have been insufficiently homogenized. In this case samples should be homogenized for a longer period of time. If nuclei are clumping together, myelin may have not been completely removed during the sucrose gradient making the nuclei sticky. Additionally, if myelin was successfully removed additional BSA can be added to the nuclei resuspension buffer. We have tested up to a concentration of 1%.
