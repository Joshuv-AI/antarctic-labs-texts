Brain Connectivity Gradients Alterations in Discordant Cerebrospinal Fluid Profile for Alzheimer's Disease Biomarkers
Authors: Lorenzo Pini, Chiara Tuzzato, Alessandra Griffa, Lorenza Brusini, Federica Cruciani, Gilles Allali, Maurizio Corbetta, Gloria Menegaz, Ilaria Boscolo Galazzo, Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlzheimer's disease (AD) is a heterogeneous disorder characterized by brain accumulation of amyloid‐beta (Aß, simplified as A for the AD biological model) and tau (T) proteins, with Aß emerging first. However, a significant proportion of individuals exhibit discordant biomarkers' profiles, such as elevated phosphorylated tau181 (p‐tau181) with normal Aß42 from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), posing diagnostic and mechanistic challenges. This study investigated whether functional and structural brain connectivity can distinguish individuals with discordant CSF profiles (A−T+) from those with concordant patterns (A+T+), hypothesizing that distinct connectivity patterns may reflect early divergent pathophysiological processes. Data from cognitively unimpaired or mildly impaired individuals in the ADNI3 repository were analyzed, selecting those with resting‐state functional MRI (rsfMRI) and/or diffusion MRI (dMRI) within 18 months of CSF testing for Aß and p‐tau181. Participants were grouped into A−T+ or A+T+ groups. Structural and functional connectivity gradients were generated for each participant and summarized using a Euclidean distance measure from reference gradient templates derived from cognitively unimpaired individuals without pathology (A−T−). We applied linear mixed models and analysis of variance to assess connectivity‐based gradient differences between A−T+ and A+T+ groups, adjusting for relevant variables. Classification analyzes using logistic regression and support vector machine, along with feature importance via the Boruta algorithm, evaluated the discriminative power of gradient connectivity profiles. Multimodal integration was performed using partial least square canonical analysis (PLSC), and relationships between gradients and cognition were assessed via UMAP‐based dimensionality reduction and bootstrapped linear regressions. Results were compared with a classical network analysis examining within‐ and between‐network connectivity differences. Among 424 participants, n = 67 were classified as A−T+, n = 106 as A+T+, and n = 56 as cognitively healthy A−T−. The remaining 195 participants (n = 86 A+T+ and n = 109 cognitively impaired A−T−) were not included. A−T+ individuals (age = 75 ± 8.2) exhibited less cognitive impairment but greater functional connectivity gradients' distance to the reference templates (false discovery rate‐corrected p < 0.05) in the temporo‐occipital axis compared to A+T+ (age = 76.1 ± 7.7). Structural connectivity differences were not significant. FC‐based models classified A−T+ and A+T+ with good accuracy (AUC = 0.77), loading on the same temporo‐occipital regions, unlike SC (AUC = 0.52). The posterior brain involvement in A−T+ was confirmed by PLSC analyzes. A+T+ individuals showed a significant relation between cognitive scores and functional connectivity, primarily mapping the default mode network (DMN). A shift was observed in relation to executive functions and functional connectivity in A−T+. Discordant CSF profiles (A−T+) exhibit distinct functional connectivity patterns, particularly in posterior brain regions, compared to concordant CSF patterns (A+T+), which are characterized by a significant cognitive‐DMN connectivity association. These results suggest that CSF p‐tau181 accumulation in the absence of Aß42 may be associated with specific functional trajectories, suggesting specific pathophysiological patterns.Keywords: atypical AD, brain connectivity, diffusion MRI, resting‐state fMRI, tau accumulation

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) cases are expected to quintuple in the coming decades (Livingston et al. 2020). Significant progress has been made in pharmacology, although some criticisms exist (Pini, Lista, et al. 2024). Such progress has been significantly propelled by the availability of cerebral in vivo markers for misfolded proteins such as amyloid‐beta (Aß) and tau. These biomarkers have been crucial in assessing the efficacy of disease‐modifying compounds and enhancing our knowledge of the pathophysiological mechanisms linked with AD. A notable framework that has driven these advancements is the ATN scheme introduced by Jack et al. (2016). This model shifted the research approach for AD from a clinical perspective to a biological framework, focusing on abnormalities in Aß (the A in the model), tau (the T), and neurodegeneration (the N) outcomes. Recent updates relegate the role of N as second‐tier of the disease, highlighting that A and T are necessary and sufficient to identify AD neuropathological changes (ADNC) (Jack et al. 2024). According to this recent revised model, Core 1 biomarkers are represented by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), such as Aβ42, phosphorylated and secreted AD tau p‐tau181. Although reflecting general ADNC, these markers map onto either the Aß or AD tauopathy pathway and define the initial stage of AD detectable in vivo identifying the presence of both symptomatic and asymptomatic AD (Jack et al. 2024).

While Aß accumulation (low Aβ42 or Aβ42/40 ratio in the CSF) along with an increased concentration of p‐tau has been proposed to support the diagnosis of AD in a clinical setting (Dubois et al. 2021), normal CSF Aβ combined with increased p‐tau is not uncommon, presenting a diagnostic challenge. Vos et al. (2024) recently highlighted the clinical importance of individuals labeled as A−T+, referred to within the construct of suspected non‐Alzheimer pathology (SNAP). This CSF profile can be found in up to 20% of cognitively unimpaired (CU) older adults (Ebenau et al. 2020; Kern et al. 2018). However, the underlying aetiologies that lead to this discordant profile are still unclear. Although it is anticipated that CSF Aβ42 levels may be abnormal while p‐tau181 remains within the normal range, reflecting the different temporal onset of these biomarkers, the reverse scenario is more challenging, as elevated p‐tau181 is considered relatively specific to AD (Leuzy et al. 2025). Recently, Erickson et al. assessed longitudinal rates of change in cognitive function, Aß accumulation, metabolism and hippocampal atrophy showing that CU and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) individuals with SNAP (A−T+) profile did not show higher rates of cognitive deterioration, Aβ accumulation, or neurodegeneration compared with A−T− individuals. The authors challenged whether the A−T+ profile should be interpreted similarly to CSF biomarker‐negative results (Erickson et al. 2023), contrary to the pathological interpretation of the A−T+ profile. Thus, there is a lack of consensus regarding the pathological status of these individuals in the absence of significant cognitive deficits.

Among the various biomarkers that could help better characterize individuals with concordant or discordant ADNC profiles, brain connectivity may play a pivotal role. Aβ and tau accumulation are closely linked to alterations in structural (SC) and functional connectivity (FC) (Vogel et al. 2023). An intriguing hypothesis suggests that brain connectivity serves as a biological pathway for the cortical spread of extracellular (A) and intracellular (T) molecular alterations (Warren et al. 2013). This assumption is supported by numerous findings, dating back to the pioneering work of Buckner et al. (2005). Several studies demonstrated a close correspondence between the molecular distribution of misfolded proteins and connectivity alterations of a specific neural network known as the default mode network (DMN), even in the AD prodromal stages (Palmqvist et al. 2017). Notably, the strength of DMN connectivity is related to memory performance in patients (Smallwood et al. 2021). Accordingly, plasma biomarkers (mainly p‐tau181) demonstrated significant mediating effects on brain functional connectivity across different Aβ stages. Similarly, misfolded proteins propagation can be predicted by the structural connectome (Gherardini et al. 2023).

Detection of FC and SC can be performed noninvasively with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques. FC is generally assessed by analyzing blood oxygenation level‐dependent (BOLD) signals through resting‐state functional MRI (rsfMRI) and reflects the synchronization of neural signaling (Biswal et al. 1995). SC is evaluated by assessing the displacement of water molecules in the brain as a proxy for axonal pathways by means of diffusion‐weighted MRI (dMRI) scans. The resulting tractogram, generated from dMRI data, represents the brain's structural connectome, mapping the brain network of structural connections. Different approaches exist to investigate FC and SC properties, such as gradient analysis (Margulies et al. 2016). Brain gradients capture the functional and structural relationships between regions, and how they are interconnected and interact. Unlike conventional connectivity analyzes that focus on discrete connections or networks, gradient mapping captures continuous macroscale organizational principles of the cortex. This framework seems particularly sensitive to subtle, spatially distributed alterations of preclinical AD and allows direct comparison between structural and functional modalities within a unified space.

Based on these premises, we explored FC and SC in individuals with a discordant CSF profile, namely those showing evidence of tau accumulation in the absence of Aβ deposition. We conducted a gradient analysis, hypothesizing that such discordant profiles would reveal distinct connectivity alterations indicative of an underlying coherent pathophysiological mechanism, rather than reflecting a heterogeneous pattern. Recently, we have found a significant relationship between the ATN scheme and dynamic FC. Patients within atypical ATN trajectories, characterized by neurodegeneration but low amyloid accumulation (defined mainly by PET and MRI), showed significant alterations between the posterior cingulate cortex and the temporo‐occipital axis (Pini, Brusini, et al. 2024). We expect to extend these findings, reporting a specific connectivity fingerprint in individuals with discordant CSF profiles, in direct response to the call put forth by Leuzy and colleagues to investigate cases of high CSF p‐tau181 levels in the context of normal CSF Aβ42 (Leuzy et al. 2025).

Methods

The workflow of the analysis is depicted in Figure 1. All the analyzes were run on Python 3.9 through an ASUS TUF Dash F15 machine (12th Gen Intel(R) Core (TM) i7‐12650H 2.30 GHz) running on an Ubuntu 20.04.6 LTS (Focal Fossa) environment, except for the preprocessing, which was performed on a dedicated server. Participants from the ADNI3 repository (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/) were included if rsfMRI and/or dMRI and CSF data (for both Aβ and p‐tau detection) were available within an 18‐month temporal window. Data were included from a preselected dataset that underwent quality checks after preprocessing, excluding participants with at least one frame showing excessive motion (translation > 3 mm or rotation > 3°). Socio‐demographic (age, education, and gender), genetic (APOE; both ε4 allele heterozygous and homozygous carriers), cognitive performance (Mini Mental State Examination [MMSE] and composite scores for memory, executive function, language and visuospatial domains) and clinical stage variables were retrieved from the database for these subjects. Individuals were considered within the AT biological continuum. Specifically, participants were classified based on Aβ42 and p‐tau181 CSF levels, according to previous recent studies (Arranz et al. 2024; Oberstein et al. 2022; Eratne et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2021; Kurihara et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025). Cut‐off values for classifying individuals as A+ and T+ were lower than 980 pg/mL and higher than 24 pg/mL for Aβ42 and p‐tau181 levels, respectively. Cut‐offs, quantified with the Roche Elecsys immunoassay, are reported in ADNI protocol (adni.loni.usc.edu), congruently with previous literature (Chen et al. 2025; Yosypyshyn et al. 2023; Van Harten et al. 2022; Blennow et al. 2019; Cumplido‐Mayoral et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2022; Willemse et al. 2018). CU individuals with an A−T− profile were considered as the control group and served to compute the connectivity gradients' templates used to align the individual gradients of the sample. For the aim of the study, we considered only individuals with an A+T+ or an A−T+ profile, irrespectively of their clinical status (CU, subjective memory complaint [SMC], early‐MCI, late‐MCI). Thus, we included individuals from the preclinical to the MCI stage (stage I–III), excluding patients with dementia (stage IV–VI) (Cohen et al. 2022).

Analytical workflow of the study. Individuals were retrieved from the ADNI3 repository with available resting‐state fMRI, diffusion MRI and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) data for amyloid and phosphorylated tau detection. CSF data were used to stratify individuals according to amyloid (A) and tau (T) status. For each individual, functional and diffusion data were used to build connectivity matrices fed into a diffusion embedding algorithm for gradient identification. Two different levels of analysis were performed. Functional and structural gradient differences were assessed at the parcel level; the linear association between a low‐dimensional gradient space and cognitive data was assessed.

Differences in cognitive, socio‐demographic and genetic characteristics between A−T+ and A+T+ groups were assessed by means of an independent t‐test for continuous variables or χ 2 for categorical values. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging Processing

RsfMRI data (repetition/echo time [TR/TE] = 3000/30 ms, FA = 90°, FOV = 220 × 220 × 163 mm, 3.4 mm isotropic voxel size, 197–200 volumes) were first minimally preprocessed according to the following steps: (i) removal of the first five volumes to allow signal stability, (ii) interleaved slice‐timing correction, (iii) motion realignment (with MCFLIRT algorithm) (Jenkinson et al. 2002), (iv) and 4D mean intensity normalization. The six motion parameters and their derivatives, mean white matter and CSF signals and a linear trend component were then linearly regressed out from the minimally preprocessed data. White matter and CSF probability masks were derived from the tissue segmentation of the corresponding T1‐w scans and linearly registered to the rsfMRI native space (Boundary‐Based Registration algorithm with 6 DOF), after erosion and binarization with a threshold of 0.8. Finally, rsfMRI residuals were band‐pass filtered (0.01–0.08 Hz) and spatially normalized to the 2‐mm MNI space using a linear and a nonlinear registration (FLIRT and FNIRT) with a 6‐mm FWHM kernel smoothing application. Connectivity matrices were computed using Schaefer parcellation including 100 parcels grouped into 7 resting‐state networks (Schaefer et al. 2018). For each parcel, the mean time series was extracted by averaging the preprocessed rsfMRI signals across all voxels within the parcel. For each pair of parcels, the Pearson correlation coefficient between their mean time series was calculated. Correlation coefficients were then organized into a 100 × 100 connectivity matrix per subject, where each element (i, j) corresponds to the functional connectivity between parcel i and parcel j. This matrix is symmetric and contains values ranging from −1 to 1.

Diffusion‐Weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging Processing

Single shell dMRI images (TR/TE = 7200/56 ms, FOV = 232 × 232 × 160 mm, 2 × 2 × 3 mm spatial resolution, b‐values = 0 and 1000 s/mm) were preprocessed as follows: (i) brain extraction and Eddy currents correction using FSL (Andersson and Sotiropoulos 2016); (ii) denoising using the Dipy library (https://dipy.org) to apply a principal component analysis (PCA)‐based denoising algorithm with automatic PCs classification grounding on the Marcenko–Pastur distribution (Veraart et al. 2016) (the radius of the 3D sliding window was set equal to 2); (iii) b0 volumes were averaged and registered to the corresponding T1‐w image using the epi_reg routine in FSL (Jenkinson et al. 2002), and then linearly registered to the MNI space; (iv) the same linear transformation was applied to all the other dMRI volumes, and the result obtained was further nonlinearly registered to the MNI space through ANTs software (Avants et al. 2011) to correct for EPI‐induced distortions. The dMRI b‐vector directions were rotated accordingly.

The structural connectome construction was performed using MRtrix3 (https://www.mrtrix.org/). Constrained spherical deconvolution was calculated on the preprocessed dMRI data. The T1‐w images were registered to the dMRI in the MNI space and three‐tissue anatomical segmentation was performed using the FSL fast tool. The 5ttgen script in MRtrix3 was then used to perform a five‐tissue segmentation based on the Schaefer atlas. Subsequently, anatomically constrained tractography with 2 million streamlines was performed using the iFOD2 algorithm and dynamic seeding with streamlines truncated at the gray‐white matter interface. A step size of 0.3 mm and a maximum length of 300 mm for each streamline were chosen. Finally, the Spherical deconvolution Informed Filtering of Tractograms (SIFT2) (Smith et al. 2015) was applied to reduce biases. The resulting set of streamlines was used to construct the structural brain network of each subject. In more detail, the individual SC was built by counting the number of streamlines connecting pairs of regions from the Schaefer atlas, ignoring self‐connections, resulting in a 100 × 100 undirected weighted matrix with a zeroed diagonal.

Gradients Computation

Gradient mapping is a novel data‐driven dimensionality reduction technique that identifies principal axes (gradients) of variation in brain connectivity patterns. This approach reveals continuous spatial trends in connectivity organization, rather than discrete networks.

The control group served as a reference for gradients' alignment. Specifically, a group‐average connectivity matrix was computed from this sample for both the structural and functional connectomes. The group‐average connectivity matrices were then independently fed into a diffusion embedding algorithm by means of the brainspace suite (https://brainspace.readthedocs.io/) (Vos De Wael et al. 2020). For FC, we considered the full connectivity matrix, while for the structural connections we considered only the intrahemispheric connections (separately for the right and left hemispheres) and discarded the interhemispheric connections. This choice was motivated by excluding from gradient analysis the main left–right component that would result from the SC matrix, not informative in the AD context. For SC we used the normalized angle as a metric for building the affinity matrix and set the sparsity parameter to 0 (due to the intrinsic sparsity of the SC matrix). For FC we employed the normalized angle metric, included the full matrix (no a priori threshold), and set the sparsity parameter to 0.9 to build the affinity matrix. In line with previous studies, we decided to consider the first three gradients from each modality (FC and SC) to promote comparability between our study and the others in the same field (Ottoy et al. 2024).

The gradients were then computed from each individual included in the study groups (A−T+ and A+T+) and matched to the template gradients from the control group using the Procrustes analysis. From such individual gradients, we computed a gradient distance metric for each subject and each modality (structural and functional connectivity, respectively), defined as the Euclidean distance between parcel‐wise gradient values with the template gradients computed from the control sample. Comparing Gradients Between Groups

GD values from A+T+ and A−T+ groups were compared by means of analysis of variance (ANOVA) performed at parcel‐wise level, separately for FC and SC. The value of each parcel was compared between groups. The corresponding p‐values were corrected for false discovery rate (FDR) and considered significant at a corrected threshold p < 0.05. Two different models were applied: Model 1 (ANOVA) investigated gradient differences between groups, while Model 2 (ANCOVA) included as covariates variables significantly different between groups. The same models were applied by means of a linear mixed model (LMM), considering the clinical stage (CU, SMC, early‐MCI, late‐MCI) as random intercept and slope. p‐values were considered significant with an FDR‐corrected p < 0.05.

For a deeper understanding of the difference in terms of connectivity gradient, we perform two additional analyzes, one related to the classification task (concordant vs. discordant CSF profiles) and a second related to feature importance. For the classification analysis we run a logistic regression model splitting the set into a test and training set (0.25 vs. 0.75 ratio, respectively). We used the GD values within each parcel as a predictor. The parameter C (spanning from 0.01 to 10 with steps of 0.1) was validated in the training test with a 5‐fold cross‐validation. The best model (C) was applied to the test set to evaluate the ROC curve. The top 20% coefficients were plotted at surface level to highlight the parcels mostly contributing to the classification. To evaluate the robustness of our analysis we run a support vector machine (SVM) with hyperparameters validation in the same training set (with a linear kernel and C parameter spanning from 0.01 to 10 with steps of 0.1). Finally, we run the Boruta algorithm to evaluate feature (GD values parcel‐wise) importance and agreement with the aforementioned algorithms.

Multivariate Structural and Functional Gradients

A partial least square canonical (PLSC) analysis was performed to assess the covariates across functional and structural gradients. The gradients from functional matrices were matched with gradients from the structural connectivity according to the Kuhn–Munkres algorithm (Kuhn 1955), using absolute Pearson's correlation as the distance metric between each FC and SC gradient from the reference control group. Subsequently, data were z‐scored across subjects and fed into a PLSC analysis (one PLSC for each SC‐FC pair from the Kuhn–Munkres algorithm). Significance was evaluated through a permutation approach on the loadings (n = 1000; p < 0.05). Scores from each individual (resulting from the projection of individual FC and SC gradients onto the first latent component identified by each PLSC analysis), were compared by means of a nonparametric analysis (U Mann–Whitney test), separately for the gradients considered in the analysis.

Connectivity Gradients and Cognitive Performance

A data reduction approach for the FC and SC gradients was separately performed through the UMAP algorithm (McInnes et al. 2018). Specifically, gradient data (represented for each gradient as an i × e matrix, where i is the number of individuals from both A+T+ and A−T+ groups, while e = 100 represents the number of brain parcels) were z‐scored (across subjects; independently for each parcel) and fed into the UMAP analysis. Data were reduced from an i‐dimensional space to a two‐dimensional (2D) space for each modality (FC, SC) and each gradient, according to our previous publication (Pini et al. 2024).

We assessed the difference between groups in these new latent spaces, which help to summarize individuals' gradient values within low‐dimensional non‐linear topological manifolds. First, mixed ANOVAs were performed separately for each gradient, considering both the dimension of the 2D space (within) and groups (between) as factors. The significance was set to p < 0.05 and Bonferroni corrected for the number of comparisons (latent spaces).

Secondly, we assessed the associations between individuals' gradient values in the latent spaces and cognitive performances for the cognitive domains considered in the analysis (n = 4), separately for SC and FC. A bootstrapping approach (n = 1000) was used to compare results from a linear regression model using cognitive scores as dependent outcomes and the 2D‐UMAP gradient space values as independent variables. Linear regressions were run separately in the two study groups (A+T+ vs. A−T+). The two groups' bootstrapping distributions of r values were compared by means of a permutation test. Specifically, the observed difference in means was compared to a null distribution generated by randomly shuffling group labels 1000 times and recalculating the difference each time. The two‐tailed p‐value was computed as the proportion of permuted differences greater than or equal to the absolute observed difference. Further, 95% confidence intervals were computed, and significance was set to p < 0.05 corrected for multiple comparisons (number of cognitive tests × gradients). Bootstrapping comparison was performed only if the cognitive‐gradient linear regression performed on at least one of the subgroups was significant.

Network Analysis

Finally, we conducted a “classical” network analysis to compare within‐ and between‐network connectivity values from FC and SC data between CSF concordant A+T+ and disconcordant A−T+ groups. For each subject's SC matrix, we calculated the average number of streamlines connecting parcels within the same network and between different networks. This process generated a symmetric 7 × 7 matrix per subject, with the diagonal representing within‐network connectivity and the off‐diagonal elements representing between‐network connectivity. For the SC, in this analysis both intra and interconnectivity measures were included. We applied the same method to FC, determining the average connectivity strength both within and between networks. To analyze the data, we used ANOVA and LLM, running both Model 1 and Model 2 as described above. In the LLM, the diagnosis label (CU, SMC, early‐MCI or late‐MCI) was included as a random factor for both intercept and slope. p‐values were corrected for FDR and considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of n = 424 non‐demented individuals were retrieved from the ADNI3 dataset based on the availability of CSF information and rsfMRI and/or dMRI scans (age = 73.4 ± 7.7 years; female: 56%; education = 16.6 ± 2.4 years). N = 106 were classified as A+T+ (age = 76 ± 8 years; female: 58%; education = 16 ± 3 years) and n = 67 as A−T+ (age = 75 ± 8 years; female: 54%; education = 17 ± 2). All these were included in the analysis. Among individuals classified as A−T− (n = 165), n = 56 participants were CU and included as the reference group (age = 72 ± 8 years; female: 57%; education = 17 ± 3 years). The remaining A+T− individuals (n = 86) were not considered in this study. These ratios were in line with previous studies on A−T+ individuals classified through CSF (Oberstein et al. 2022; Delvenne et al. 2025).

CSF discordant (A−T+) and concordant (A+T+) individuals were comparable for age, sex, and education, while significant differences were reported for the APOE‐4 (heterozygous or homozygous carriers) frequency and for the MMSE score (Table 1). These variables were considered as covariates for the Model 2 analysis. Similarly, the A+T+ showed lower cognition within each domain (memory, executive functions, language, and visuospatial scores; p < 0.05; Table 1).

Structural and Functional Gradients in Cognitively Unimpaired A−T− Individuals

Based on the selection criteria (paragraph 2.3), three main gradients were retained for both SC and FC matrices. The first three gradients from FC explained more than 64% of variance, while the first three SC gradients explained more than 45% of the variance (left: 48%; right: 46%) (Figure S1). A graphical representation of the SC and FC gradients for the control group is depicted in Figure 2. FC gradients reflect the synchronization patterns of neuronal activity between cortical regions, whereas SC gradients reflect the architecture of white matter streamlines connecting different regions.

Template connectivity gradients from A−T− cognitively unimpaired individuals. A group‐average connectivity matrix for both functional (left panel) and structural (right panel) connectomes was computed from the sample diagnosed as cognitively unimpaired and negative for both amyloid and p‐tau CSF accumulation. The first three gradients were considered and plotted onto the surface. Brain surfaces were colored according to the respective connectivity gradients, with blue and red representing the two extremes of each gradient.

The FC gradients were in line with the previous literature (Margulies et al. 2016); the axis of the first gradient mapped from visual (on one end) to sensorimotor (on the other) cortices (30% variance). The second gradient described a cortical axis spanning from multimodal to unimodal cortices (DMN‐like pattern; 22% variance), while the third gradient mapped mainly to the temporal lobe (12% variance).

For SC, the first gradient mapped on an anterior–posterior cortical axis (left: 19% variance; right: 18%); the second gradient captured a white matter streamline pattern moving from the sensorimotor cortex to the posterior cingulate cortex (16% variance for both left and right); the third gradient showed a latero‐medial variation with an extreme of the axis located in the occipito‐parietal regions and the retrosplenial cortex (left: 13% variance; right: 12%). Overall, the SC patterns reflect the different segments of the corpus callosum, as shown in our previous publication (Pini et al. 2025).

Gradient Distance's Differences Between A+T+ and A−T+ Individuals

For FC gradients, n = 2 individuals from the A+T+ group and n = 6 from the A−T+ group were not considered due to missing rsfMRI data. A significant difference was reported between the two AT groups in multiple parcels (GD values) mapping to the temporo‐occipital cortex, with A−T+ showing significant (FDR‐corrected) higher GD values, suggestive of greater distance from the controls' template. Model 1 applied by both ANOVA and LMM reported significant differences in parcels mapping to the temporal gyrus, fusiform cortex, lingual gyrus, intracalcarine cortex, cuneal cortex, and occipital pole. Results were confirmed by Model 2 (ANCOVA and LMM), suggesting a different connectivity trajectory of A−T+ individuals from controls, as compared with A+T+ individuals, when controlling for MMSE scores and genetic (APOE) variables (significantly different between groups; see Table 1). For the LMM, only APOE status was included as a covariate, since clinical status (modeled as a random intercept and slope) is closely aligned with MMSE scores. Results are shown in Figure 3. A post hoc analysis (Model 1) was carried out to assess whether these differences were driven by a specific gradient. The first gradient showed significant differences between the two groups surviving multiple comparison thresholds within similar parcels. Significant differences in the same regions were reported for the second gradient not surviving FDR‐correction (Figure S1).

Predicting CSF Profile Based on GD

After validation of the C parameter in the training test, we applied the logistic regression model in the test set reporting an AUC of 0.77 for classifying concordant and discordant CSF profiles through GD from the FC. The analysis of the coefficients showed that the 20% top parcel mapped to the posterior cortex, congruently with the parcel‐wise GD analyzes (Figure 4). These results were validated when applying a SVM (AUC = 0.78) and by the Boruta algorithm, identifying the most relevant parcel in the same brain posterior location. Multivariate Functional‐Structural Connectivity Patterns

This analysis was performed on n = 150 individuals (A+T+: n = 92; A−T+: n = 58), based on the contemporary availability of rsfMRI and dMRI data. The gradient distance matching based on the Kuhn–Munkres algorithm revealed a correspondence between the variance explained by FC and SC gradients and the similarity of the components: the first FC gradient was matched with the first SC gradient (absolute‐r = 0.64), the second FC gradient with the second SC gradient (absolute‐r = 0.52), and the third ones were matched together (absolute‐r = 0.61) (Figure 5).

The PLSC analysis on the first FC and SC gradient revealed a component predominantly loading on the temporo‐occipital cortex on one extreme and somatomotor‐frontal area on the other extreme (Figure 5). Relationship Between Functional Connectivity Gradients and Cognitive Performance

For the 2D UMAP latent space from the first FC gradient, we found a significant group × space interaction effect (F = 10.2; p = 0.002), surviving the multiple comparison correction threshold. Post hoc analysis showed that the second element of the UMAP space was significantly different between groups (T = 2.66; p = 0.009), suggesting that the data reduction approach was able to summarize the A+T+/A−T+ differences of the first FC gradient into a low‐dimensional space. On the contrary, the 2D UMAP latent spaces for the second and third FC gradients did not show significant interaction (differences) between groups (p > 0.37).

The first gradient did not show significant linear regression results for memory, language, and visuospatial abilities in either A+T+ or A−T+ groups (p > 0.13 for all models). On the contrary, executive functions were significantly associated with the first gradient's latent space in the A−T+ group (r 2 = 0.14; p = 0.019), but not in the A+T+ group (p = 0.280). The bootstrapping comparison showed that the distribution of r 2 values was significantly higher in the former group (p < 0.0001, difference = 0.13), suggesting a small to medium yet statistically significant association between the first gradient with executive functions in A−T+. The second FC gradient's latent values showed an opposite pattern. In A+T+ individuals, we found a significant association with memory (r 2 = 0.27; p < 0.0001) and executive functions (r 2 = 0.24; p < 0.0001), not reported in the A−T+ group (memory: r 2 = 0.095; p = 0.064; executive functions: r 2 = 0.032; p = 0.412). Significant bootstrapping results were reported (memory: p < 0.0001, difference = 0.14; executive functions: p < 0.0001, difference = 0.19), revealing a significant association of these domains with the second gradient in A+T+ individuals. The second gradient's latent values were also significantly associated with language functions in both groups (A+T+: r 2 = 0.15; p < 0.0001; A−T+: r 2 = 0.14; p = 0.018), with no group differences assessed by the bootstrapping (p = 0.257, difference = 0.012). The visuospatial domain was significantly related with this gradient in the A+T+ group (r 2 = 0.13; p = 0.003), while only a trend was reported in A−T+ individuals (r 2 = 0.09; p = 0.08), with no significant bootstrapping differences (p = 0.160, difference = 0.015). The third FC gradient's latent values showed no significant association with either executive functions or visuospatial abilities. However, memory and language functions showed an opposite group pattern, with the former associated with the FC gradient in the A+T+ group (A+T+: r 2 = 0.10; p = 0.009; A−T+: r 2 = 0.032; p = 0.409), and the latter in the A−T+ individuals (A+T+: r 2 = 0.009; p = 0.657; A−T+: r 2 = 0.13; p = 0.021). This pattern was confirmed at the bootstrapping level (memory: p < 0.0001, difference = 0.05; language: p < 0.0001, difference = 0.13). Significant results are shown in Figure 6.

Relationship Between Structural Connectivity Gradients and Cognitive Performance

No group‐differences were reported for the 2D UMAP latent spaces derived from the SC gradients, in line with the parcel‐wise analysis (p > 0.28 for all the group comparisons). For the first gradient, no significant associations were reported for executive, language, and visuospatial abilities (p > 0.08 for all comparisons). Only a significant but weak association was reported with memory in the A+T+ group (A+T+: r 2 = 0.08; p = 0.022; A−T+: r 2 = 0.09; p = 0.08), not significant at the bootstrap level (p = 0.063). The same pattern was observed for the second gradient and memory (A+T+: r 2 = 0.08; p = 0.023; A−T+: r 2 = 0.10; p = 0.06), but significant at the bootstrap (p = 0.0002). No significant associations were reported for the remaining domains (p > 0.08 for all comparisons). Finally, the third gradient showed a significant association with language again in the A+T+ group only (A+T+: r 2 = 0.12; p = 0.003; A−T+: r 2 = 0.05; p = 0.23), although the group difference from the bootstrap analysis did not survive the multiple comparison correction (p = 0.010).

Discussion

In this study, we decomposed individual‐level high‐dimensional data including both FC and SC into their main axes of variance (gradients) and compared their expression in individuals with discordant Aβ42 (normal) and p‐tau181 (elevated) profiles. From a healthy aging reference population, we identified an overall unimodal‐transmodal functional gradient closely aligned with previous literature (Ottoy et al. 2024). Likewise, the occipital and temporal functional gradients were consistent with previous findings (Ottoy et al. 2024), suggesting a high reproducibility of these connectivity patterns across populations. The structural gradients also closely matched those reported by the literature (Ottoy et al. 2024).

The main findings of this study are as follows: (i) significant FC gradients' differences between A−T+ and A+T+ groups, primarily centered around occipital and lateral‐temporal regions, in line with our previous findings (Pini, Brusini, et al. 2024); (ii) absence of echoed effects for the SC gradients; (iii) group‐specific associations between cognitive performances and connectivity gradient mapping.

Although A−T+ individuals exhibited less cognitive impairment, their FC gradient profiles deviated more from the control template compared to A+T+ individuals, with pronounced alterations in occipito‐temporal connectivity patterns. Notably, we found that this approach provided higher sensitivity in capturing group‐level deviations. Indeed, conventional FC analyzes, examining pairwise connectivity strengths, did not capture subtle and spatially distributed alterations in network architecture.

A key question raised by the present finding is whether posterior connectivity alterations in A−T+ can be considered a “general” fingerprint highlighting specific connectivity vulnerability in the absence of Aβ42 accumulation, or whether they are linked to a specific co‐pathology occurring in these individuals. The posterior pattern found in this work in A−T+ individuals is partially in line with previous rsfMRI and tau‐PET studies. Ottoy and colleagues recently conducted a gradient study to explore the relationship between tau‐PET distribution and functional/structural connectivity in AD. While patients showed a main contraction of the unimodal‐transmodal functional axis (DMN‐like regions), linked to the distribution and progression of tau, the authors also observed connectivity changes along the visual gradient. Accordingly, distinct patterns of tau accumulation were reported in the temporo‐occipital and frontoparietal cortices in AD compared to controls (Ottoy et al. 2024), suggesting that tau may build up in specific regions reflecting not only multiple potential origins (epicenters), but also heterogeneities in individual functional and microstructural vulnerabilities. Similarly, Vogel et al. identified distinct tau profiles in AD, challenging a single model of spatiotemporal evolution of tau pathology progression in AD and suggesting that variations in the pathological cascade of AD can be expressed along distinct axes and subtypes. Among these, a posterior tau phenotype was associated with slower cognitive decline compared to all other subtypes. These individuals exhibited considerable tau pathology in posterior (including occipital) brain regions with relatively less medial temporal lobe and frontoparietal binding (Vogel et al. 2023). Similarly, previous literature described common variations in visual and temporo‐occipital tau pathology spanning preclinical and symptomatic AD (Pikkarainen et al. 2009; Alafuzoff et al. 2008), and in up to 50% of the cognitively intact elderly sample (McKee et al. 2006). Using a data‐driven approach, we recently reported dynamic FC changes in patients with an atypical ATN profile within temporo‐occipital regions, consistent with the findings of the present study (Pini, Brusini, et al. 2024). That patients' group included both MCI and demented patients with a clinical diagnosis of AD yet characterized by high neurodegeneration and low amyloid levels.

Taken together, these results suggest that a posterior subtype may, at least partially, signify the existence of a posterior cortical network selectively vulnerable to tau pathology independently from amyloid pathology. These results were confirmed by the PLSC analysis, which showed that when both FC and SC patterns were aligned along a posterior gradient, a significant difference between the CSF groups emerged. This vulnerability might be decoupled from amyloid accumulation, resulting in alterations of brain connectivity in those regions. Recently, Frisoni et al. suggested a revised amyloid cascade model in AD where the penetrance of the amyloid cascade assumes different probabilistic weights, proportional to the concomitant presence of genetic risk factors for AD (Frisoni et al. 2022). Some evidence is linked with the A−T+ profile, found in up to 45% of individuals without dementia (Pascoal et al. 2020; Leuzy et al. 2020). This condition undermines the initiating role of Aβ in the amyloid cascade. Wuestefeld et al. observed that, in CU individuals, age‐related tau deposition independent of Aβ occurs not only in the medial temporal lobe but also in neocortical regions (both in vivo and ex vivo). This was also observed in individuals with low Aβ load, suggesting that tau pathology can exert downstream effects on brain function and cognition (Wuestefeld et al. 2023) which can be captured by connectivity alterations in both temporal and cortical regions, as suggested by our analysis.

However, a key question remains open. Specifically, based on the evidence provided, further studies should assess whether the spatial distribution of alterations observed along the posterior connectivity gradients in A−T+ align with regional patterns of tau accumulation as assessed by tau‐PET. Exploring this topographical correspondence could help clarify whether specific connectivity axes are preferentially vulnerable to tau pathology independently of amyloid status.

Our results could also suggest the contribution of a common co‐pathology to possibly heterogeneous SNAP aetiologies. SNAP can coexist with various non‐AD pathologies, including limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy neuropathological change (LATE‐NC) (Nelson et al. 2019). Recent research has suggested that LATE might account for 15%–20% of all clinically diagnosed AD cases (Nelson et al. 2019). Early retrospective autopsy studies found that TDP‐43‐opathies mimic the AD clinical syndrome (Brenowitz et al. 2014; Pao et al. 2011; Josephs et al. 2017), and although LATE‐NC can exist in tandem with other pathologies, most commonly ADNC (Attems and Jellinger 2006), it is a separate pathology and syndrome. Recently, Tazwar et al. demonstrated a lower transverse relaxation rate by combining ex vivo MRI and histopathology, in a spatial pattern mainly involving the occipito‐temporal cortex in LATE (Tazwar et al. 2022). Moreover, Young and colleagues in a preliminary study investigated pathological progression patterns of TDP‐43 in several neurodegenerative diseases. They reported a subtype labeled corticolimbic predominant with greater TDP‐43 deposition in temporal regions and the occipital cortex (Young et al. 2023). Further studies should assess whether SNAP would represent a clinical entity characterized by a higher probability of LATE pathology.

Different patterns between concordant and discordant CSF profiles were confirmed when assessing the relationship with cognitive scores. The DMN‐like functional gradient showed a significant association with memory, language and executive functions in the A+T+ group but not in the A−T+ group. Conversely, the temporo‐occipital functional gradient showed an association with executive functions in the A−T+ group. Although modest, the effect was statistically significant and remains relevant given the early clinical stage of this group, where ceiling effects may reduce potential associations. Of note, no significant association was found between the first FC gradient and visuospatial abilities. These scores were similar across groups, suggesting a potential “ceiling effect” that may have limited the ability to detect subtle, yet potentially meaningful, group‐level differences. Moreover, it is plausible that this gradient captures cognitive domains beyond visuospatial abilities, as suggested by its mild association with executive functions. This interpretation aligns with previous evidence indicating that visuospatial impairments may emerge later in the disease course or involve distinct network alterations not fully reflected by this gradient (Pini et al. 2021).

Finally, no significant differences were found in SC gradients between the two groups, highlighting that SC patterns may be less sensitive to capture differences in AD biomarker profiles at the level of granularity assessed in this study. This lack of significant SC gradient differences might reflect the early stage of the disease in the sample, consistent with evidence that in the AD pathophysiological cascade functional alterations emerge early within specific networks (Palmqvist et al. 2017), preceding structural changes (both GM and WM). Thus, functional alterations may be more sensitive to distinguish CSF‐concordant from CSF‐discordant groups, while SC differences are expected to appear mainly when compared with healthy controls. Additionally, null SC results may partly reflect the tractography pipeline. Given that the ADNI3 dataset lacks reverse phase‐encoding images for the correction of susceptibility‐induced geometric distortion, diffusion data were registered to their respective anatomical scan before performing tractography to correct for EPI‐induced susceptibility artifacts (Nir et al. 2017). This step, however, may have masked subtle group differences (Greene et al. 2018).

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of inherent limitations. Future research should investigate whether including critical AD brain structures in the connectivity analyzes, such as the hippocampus and amygdala, could reveal more pronounced differences between groups. Although the sample size was drawn from one of the largest AD imaging and biomarker repositories, the final cohort was relatively small and unbalanced with respect to the A−T+ group, due to the strict requirement for both rsfMRI/dMRI and CSF data. Moreover, our study included individuals spanning the clinical continuum from cognitively unimpaired to those with MCI. Future research should investigate whether stratification by clinical diagnosis or the inclusion of additional biomarkers (e.g., neurodegeneration profile) would further validate and extend these results. Finally, different markers (e.g., p‐tau217) or different ratios (e.g., p‐tau181/Aβ42 or Aβ42/40) should be applied to confirm these results. Despite these challenges, it is noteworthy that the gradients extracted from our sample align well with findings from independent studies conducted on different cohorts (Ottoy et al. 2024). Similarly, several strengths should be noted. Our study advances the field of gradient‐based applications by assessing the relationship between connectivity gradients within the biological continuum of the AT(N) model. Finally, we included data from both SC and FC to provide a comprehensive perspective of changes occurring in individuals with tau abnormalities but divergent amyloid accumulation. Further studies are needed to validate these findings in different cohorts and using histopathological data.
