The interaction of top–down and bottom–up attention in visual working memory
Authors: Weixi Zheng, Yanchao Sun, Hehong Wu, Hongwei Sun, Dexiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the interplay between top–down and bottom–up attention in visual working memory (VWM) is crucial, although the specific challenges arising from this interaction remain ambiguous. In this study, we address this complexity by examining how cue informativeness and probe status of the salient items influence this interaction. Through three experiments, we manipulated top–down attention by varying probe frequencies using pre-cues and bottom–up attention by varying the visual salience of memory items. Experiment 1 explored cue informativeness at 100% and 50%, while Experiments 2 and 3 maintained cue informativeness at 80% and 50%. Additionally, Experiment 1 tested a few of the salient items, Experiment 2 excluded them, and Experiment 3 tested half of them in each cue condition. Across all experiments, we consistently observed cueing benefits for cue-directed items, albeit with costs to non-cued items. Furthermore, cue informativeness and the probe status of salient items emerged as critical factors influencing the interaction between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM. These findings underscore the pivotal roles of cue informativeness and salient item relevance in shaping the dynamics of top–down and bottom–up attention within VWM.Keywords: Visual working memory, Top–down attention, Bottom–up attention, Visual salience, Cue informativenessSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Visual working memory (VWM) is crucial in cognitive processes, facilitating the temporary storage and manipulation of visual information during ongoing tasks1,2. However, VWM has a limited capacity, posing challenges for effective attentional selection3,4. Attention selection in VWM involves both top–down and bottom–up mechanisms5–8. Top–down attention is driven by task demands or individual goals, while bottom–up attention is triggered by sensory inputs or unexpected external stimuli9,10. Theoretical frameworks11–14 propose that top–down attention guides the selection, prioritisation, and manipulation of information stored in memory, while bottom–up attention determines which information is initially encoded into memory. These mechanisms interact synergistically to facilitate information processing and memorization. Previous studies have demonstrated that both forms of attention can enhance VWM performance15–21.

Investigations into the effect of top–down attention often utilise methodologies such as the cueing paradigm, employing cues presented either before (pre-cue) or after (retro-cue) memory items to indicate which items are more likely to be tested19,22,23. Pre-cues have been found to enhance VWM performance for cued items but may compromise others17,19,22,24–26. Conversely, retro-cues improve VWM performance for cued items without necessarily incurring such costs20,27–31. These findings suggest distinct underlying mechanisms for attention selection during VWM encoding and maintenance. Pre-cueing enables attentional anticipation before VWM encoding, whereas retro-cueing enables controlled spatiotopic access to representations recently encoded and maintained in VWM32,33.

Researchers have examined the effect of bottom–up attention on VWM by manipulating item intrinsic properties such as colour, shape, and spatial location. Increasing the visual salience of an item’s intrinsic properties has been shown to improve encoding into VWM, enhancing retention and recall of that information15,34–38. However, this advantage for salient items comes at a cost to the recall of other items within the memory set. A comprehensive review39 highlights that when items in the memory set possess equal salience, estimated VWM capacity surpasses scenarios where one item is more salient than the others. Furthermore, a stronger salience advantage is associated with lower overall item recall.

Although previous research has addressed the roles of top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM, it remains unclear how these two forms of attention affect the selection of information for encoding and maintaining in VWM. Some studies suggest that salient perceptual features can automatically capture attention and access VWM irrespective of top–down processes40, while others argue in favour of top–down attention actively suppressing of salient items perceived as task-irrelevant41–43. Our study aims to explore the conditions governing the suppression or non-suppression of salient items, with a particular focus on cue informativeness—the amount of information cues provide.

Cue informativeness, measured as the percentage of trials on which the cue accurately points to the target, influences how attention is allocated. It has been shown that participants can strategically control the allocation of attention to the target according to the informativeness of the cues. Specifically, they respond more correctly and faster with highly informative cues than less informative cues44. Riggio and Kirsner has directly explored the relationship between top–down central cues and bottom–up peripheral cues in covert attention orientation by analyzing participants’ reaction times in an attention task45. They manipulated the informativeness and validity of central cues and the validity of peripheral cues in the attention task, hypothesizing that central cues depend on top–down processes while peripheral cues involve bottom–up processes. Their findings revealed that the effects of the central and peripheral cues were independent and additive. The cue informativeness modulate the effect of central cues but not the peripheral cues, suggesting that the top–down and bottom–up attention influence performance in distinct information processing stages, following an addictive factor model.

Based on previous findings, we aim to investigate whether cue informativeness could explain the different interactions between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM using a VWM change detection task. We hypothesise that highly informative cues may enhance VWM performance by effectively selecting pertinent information, while less informative cues may lead to ineffective filtration of irrelevant stimuli. To test this hypothesis, we adopted a pre-cue paradigm to explore how cue informativeness influences the interaction between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM. Three experiments were designed. Experiment 1 manipulated cue informativeness (100%-informative vs. 50%-informative) and the presence of salient displays (salient vs. non-salient). Both conditions included neutral cue trials as a baseline, while the 50%-informative condition featured invalid cue trials. Salient items were consistently located at neutral and invalid cue positions and occasionally probed. We hypothesised that in the 100%-informative condition, salient but task-irrelevant items would not affect VWM performance of the cued item but would impair performance of the neutrally cued item. Conversely, in the 50%-informative condition, the salient task-irrelevant item was expected to impair VWM performance across all cue conditions.

Since the 100%-informative cues were fully informative, their effects may stem from the deterministic nature of 100%-informative cues. Experiments 2 and 3 further explored the effect of cue informativeness, with cues being either 50% or 80% informative. Cue validity was manipulated to be valid, invalid, or neutral. Experiment 2 assessed cue informativeness when none of the salient items were tested, while Experiment 3 investigated the effect of cue informativeness when half of the salient items in each cue condition were tested. If the differences in the interactions of top–down attentional cues and salient displays in VWM lie in the deterministic nature of 100%-informative cues, we should not find a three-way interaction in both Experiments 2 and 3. If the differences in the interaction rely on the high informativeness, we should observe the three-way interaction in both Experiments 2 and 3. This interaction can be influenced by whether the salient item was probed. We predicted that interactions between pre-cues and salient displays rely on the informativeness of cues, with higher informative cues effectively suppressing salient but task-irrelevant items, while less informative cues would not.

Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, we manipulated cue informativeness and the presence of salient displays to explore the interaction between how top–down and bottom–up attention influence VWM. Pre-cues were either 100% informative or 50% informative. To discern the impact of attentional cues and visual salience on various types of items, we conducted three-way repeated measures ANOVAs to analyse the data.

Method

Participants

Sample size determination was performed using G*Power 3.146, with a predicted medium effect size (f = 0.25). A priori analysis was conducted for a two within-factors design (informativeness: 100% and 50%; salient display: salient and non-salient) in a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with four measurements of one group of participants. To achieve 80% power at a 0.05 significance level, the recommended sample size was approximately 24 individuals. We recruited 24 undergraduate students (female: 9; mean age: 19.54 ± 1.47) for this experiment, compensating them with 10 RMB upon completion. This sample size aligns with previous studies utilising a cueing paradigm30,47–49. All participants, native Chinese speakers, were recruited from Weifang Medical University. They reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and none had colour blindness. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants based on the protocol approved by Weifang Medical University’s Ethics Committee. We confirm that all methods for the present study were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Equipment and materials

This experiment was performed using E-prime 2.050 on a Lenovo 19-inch screen with a resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels at a 60-Hz refresh rate. Stimuli were presented against a white background (RGB: 255, 255, 255) with a visual angle of 1.5°, viewed from approximately 57 cm. Each trial involved four shapes randomly selected without replacement from a set of six shapes (circle, triangle, flag, cross, diamond, and star; see Fig. 1). In non-salient trials, all the stimuli were black. In salient trials, the salient stimulus was chosen from five colours (red (RGB: 255, 0, 0), yellow (RGB: 255, 255, 0), green (RGB: 0, 255, 0), blue (RGB: 0, 0, 255), and pink (RGB: 255, 0, 255)), while the other three stimuli were black (RGB: 0, 0, 0). The attentional cues used were similar to those used in previous studies22,31. As shown in Fig. 1, the informative cue was characterised by the illumination of two contiguous sides of the central square, forming an arrow. In contrast, the neutral cue involved the entire square brightening, providing no spatial indication regarding the probable location of the probe.

Schematic illustration of the trial procedure. Initially, three random numbers were presented, followed by a fixation cross. Subsequently, either an informative or uninformative pre-cue was presented. Then, four memory items were displayed, followed by a probe item presented at one of the memory item's locations. Participants were required to press one of the two keys on the keyboard to indicate whether the shape of the probe item matches the shape of the memory item at that location. In Experiment 1, when an informative cue was presented, the cue-directed item was tested either with a probability of 100% or 50%. In experiments 2 and 3, the informative cue directed item was tested either with a probability of 80% or 50%. When uninformative cues (neutral cues) were presented, the items were tested with a probability of 25%, in all of the three experiments. The different types informative cues were blocked in each experiment, with uninformative cues inserted within each informative cue blocked. As seen in the figure, the informative cue was an arrowhead pointing to one of the four item positions, while the uninformative cue was a bold quadrangle pointing to all of the four item positions.

Design and procedure

The experiment manipulated cue informativeness (100%-informative and 50%-informative), cue validity (valid, neutral), and salient display (salient and non-salient) as within-subject factors. In the 100%-informative condition, cue validity was either valid or neutral. Participants were informed that when an informative cue (1.3°×1.3°) was presented, the cue-directed item would be tested with a 100% probability, while an uninformative cue (1.3°×1.3°) indicated that each item would be tested with a 25% probability. In the 50%-informative condition, cue validity was separated into valid, invalid, and neutral. Participants were informed that when an informative cue was presented, the cue-directed item would be tested with a 50% probability (valid cue), while the other three items together would be tested with a 50% probability (invalid cue). When an uninformative cue presented, each item would be tested with a 25% probability. The 100%-informative and 50%-informative conditions were blocked, with each containing two blocks of 60 trials. Within each block, the salient display and non-salient display conditions were systematically presented.

Figure 1 illustrates the experimental procedure. Each trial began with the presentation of three random numbers for 1500 milliseconds (ms). Participants were instructed to repeat the numbers aloud throughout the trial to suppress the verbal encoding, with experimenter oversight to ensure compliance. Subsequently, a fixation cross appeared at the centre of the screen, which remained on the screen throughout the trial. Next, an informative or uninformative cue (1.3°×1.3°) was presented for 100 ms. After a 500-ms interval, four memory items were simultaneously presented at the corners of an invisible square (3.6°×3.6°) on the screen for 500 ms. Visual salience in the memory display was manipulated by inserting a color singleton among black items. As shown in Fig. 1, the red item compared to the other three black items were more salient in color. Following a 1000 ms delay, a black test item appeared at one of the four item locations for 2000 ms. Participants were required to press one of two buttons on the keyboard to indicate whether its shape matched the memory item at that location, aiming for quick and accurate responses. In cue type (valid, invalid, and neutral), 50% of the probes had a ‘Yes’ answer, while the other ‘50%’ had a ‘No’ answer, randomly distributed across the experiment. All probes with a ‘No’ answer were new items. The experiment trials were preceded by 18 practice trials, with feedback regarding their task performance in these practice trials.

The experiment comprised four blocks of 60 trials, totalling 240 trials. Each block in the 100%-informative condition included 30 valid and 30 neutral trials, randomly presented. In the 50%-informative condition, each block contained 20 trials of each cue condition (valid, invalid, and neutral), also randomly presented. Prior to the experimental trials, the participants were informed whether they would undertake a 100%- or 50%- informative block, with the allocation counterbalanced across participants. In each block, half the trials contained a salient item, while the other half did not. When an informative cue was presented, it never indicated a salient item. In the valid-cue condition, the salient items were never tested. In neutral and invalid cue trials, the shape of the salient items was occasionally tested to assess participants’ ability to suppress bottom–up attention. The invalid/neutral and salient/non-salient trial conditions intermixed in each of the 100%- and 50%-informative conditions. Specifically, in the 100%-informative condition, six neutral trials tested salient items, while in the 50%-informative condition, four invalid trials and six neutral trials tested salient items. However, participants were not informed whether salient items were tested. On average, participants spent approximately 30 minutes completing the experiment.

Results

The data were analysed using JASP (version 0.18.3.0)51,52, with accuracy and reaction time as dependent measures. To ensure comparability across different cue conditions, accuracy and reaction times related to salient items were excluded from analysis. Reaction times with incorrect responses were also excluded from the reaction time analysis responses. An exclusion rate of incorrect response (plus those exceeding the time limit) was observed at 18% (8% from the 100%-informative condition; 10% from the 50%-informative condition).

To explore how cue informativeness modulates the interactions between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM, a three-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed on accuracy and reaction time with cue informativeness, cue validity and salient display as within-subject factors. Additionally, two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted separately for both 100%-informative and 50%-informative conditions to further reveal interactions between cue validity and salient display. Sets of t-tests were also conducted to compare performance across trials with salient and non-salient items as probes.

The analysis employed a combination of frequentist statistics and Bayes Factor (BF) analysis using JASP (version 0.18.3.0) to overcome potential limitations of traditional frequentist methods and provide additional insights for interpretation53,54. BF10 was used to assess the strength of evidence for the alternative hypothesis compared to the null hypothesis and facilitate tests of equivalence between conditions or groups55. All possible models were assessed. The reported results include the most likely model relative to a null model (included subject and random slopes). For the analysis of effects in Bayesian ANOVAs, the “Across matched models” option was selected to detect the higher-order interaction effects56. BF10 below 1/3 were typically interpreted as genuine null effects (i.e., substantial evidence in favour of the null model). BF10 below 3 suggests weak evidence. BF10 between 3 and 10 indicates substantial evidence in favour of the alternative model. BF10 between 10 and 100 suggests strong evidence. BF10 above 100 indicates decisive evidence in favour of the alternative model. For frequentist statistics, a Holm correction was used for multiple comparisons.

The modulation of cue informativeness on cue validity and salient display

Accuracy of the valid-cue condition (M = 0.95, SE = 0.02) was better than that of neutral cue condition (M = 0.79, SE = 0.02). Furthermore, significant two-way interactions were found between cue informativeness and cue validity (F(1,23) = 18.86, p<0.001, η2p = 0.45, BFincl =134.71), and between cue informativeness and salient display (F(1,23) = 4.47, p = 0.046, η2p = 0.16, BFincl =1.30). The interaction between cue validity and salient display was not significant as indicated by the frequentist statistics (F(1,23) = 0.01, p = 0.906, η2p = 6.202×10−4, BFincl =0.28). As the post hoc analysis of the main effect of cue informativeness indicated that accuracy in 100%-informative condition was worse than that of the 50%-informative condition, we further untangled the cue informativeness and cue validity interaction to identify the exact nature of the effect for cue informativeness. The result showed no significant difference on accuracy in valid trials between 100%-informative and 50%-informative conditions (t (23)= 1.93, pholm = 0.060, Cohen’s d = 0.25). However, accuracy was worse in the 100%-informative condition than 50%-informative condition for the neutral cue condition (t (23)= − 4.88, pholm <0.001, Cohen’s d = − 0.64).

Additionally, a significant three-way interaction was also observed between cue informativeness, cue validity, and salient display (F(1,23) = 6.78, p = 0.016, η2p = 0.23, BFincl =27.85). To delve into the three-way interaction, separate two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted on accuracy for both 100%-informative and 50%-informative conditions, with cue validity and salient display as within-subject factors.

For the 100%-informative condition, the BF analysis indicated that the most likely model included main effects of cue validity, salient display, and their interaction (BF10 = 1.401×108 relative to a null model). The analysis demonstrated significant main effects of cue validity (F(1, 23) = 95.00, p <0.001, η2p = 0.81, BFincl = 9.652×106) and salient display (F(1, 23) = 5.37, p = 0.030, η2p = 0.19, BFincl = 0.92). Specifically, the accuracy was higher in the valid-cue condition (M = 0.97, SE = 0.02) than the neutral-cue condition (M = 0.76, SE = 0.02). Additionally, higher accuracy was also observed in the non-salient condition (M = 0.87, SE = 0.01) compared to the salient condition (M = 0.85, SE = 0.01). The analysis also revealed a significant interaction between cue validity and the salient display (F(1, 23) = 8.50, p = 0.008, η2p = 0.27, BFincl =15.68). Post hoc comparisons, adjusted for multiple comparisons, showed no effect of salient display on accuracy in the valid-cue condition (t (23)= 0.55, pholm = 0.585, Cohen’s d = 0.07, BF10= 0.35); however, accuracy was worse in the salient than in the non-salient condition for the neutral-cue trials (t (23)= − 3.71, pholm =0.001, Cohen’s d =− 0.47, BF10= 5.06).

For the 50%-informative condition, including the invalid cue condition in the analysis, the BF analysis indicated that the most likely model included only main effect of cue validity (BF10 = 468344.59 relative to a null model). A significant main effect of cue validity was observed (F(2, 46) = 25.60, p <0.001, η2p = 0.53, BFincl =459306.14). Post hoc comparisons indicated higher accuracy in the valid-cue condition (M = 0.94, SE = 0.03) compared to the invalid-cue (M = 0.72, SE = 0.03; t(23)= 7.15, pholm <0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.44, BF10 = 1.195×106) and neutral-cue (M = 0.83, SE = 0.03; t(23)= 3.42, pholm < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.73, BF10= 247.38) conditions. The accuracy was also higher in the neutral-cue condition than in the invalid-cue condition (t(23)= 3.73, pholm <0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.80, BF10= 662.63). However, neither the main effect of the salient display (F(1,23) = 4.06, p = 0.056, η2p = 0.15, BFincl = 0.86) nor the interaction between cue validity and the salient display (F(1,23) = 1.33, p = 0.275, η2p = 0.06, BFincl=0.32) were significant.

Discussion

In Experiment 1, we manipulated cue informativeness and salient display to investigate the collaboration of top–down and bottom–up attention mechanisms in shaping VWM performance. Three-way repeated measures ANOVAs were conducted on accuracy and reaction times to analyse the modulation of cue informativeness on the interaction between cue validity and salient display in VWM. The results indicated that valid cues significantly improved VWM accuracy and reaction times, while invalid cues notably impaired accuracy and prolonged reaction times, particularly evident in the 50%-informative condition. This suggests that a valid pre-cue can enhance VWM performance but at the expense of others, aligning with previous findings17,19,22,24–26.

Moreover, there was a significant interaction between cue informativeness and cue validity. Accuracy remained consistent between 100% and 50% informative conditions during valid trials, but significantly declined in the 100%-informative condition during neutral trials. Conversely, reaction times were expedited in the 100%-informative condition during valid trials, with no significant differences observed during neutral trials between the two informativeness levels. This interaction suggests that the highly informative cues enhance effectiveness by both promoting the response speed on valid trials and impairing performance of the neutral trials.

More importantly, there was a three-way interaction on VWM accuracy between cue informativeness, cue validity, and salient display. In the 100%-informative condition, the presence of a salient yet untested item (i.e., salient but irrelevant item) impaired VWM accuracy under neutral cue conditions but not under valid cue conditions. However, this interaction was absent in the 50%-informative condition, suggesting that cue informativeness moderates the interplay between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM.

Additionally, comparing salient and non-salient probes in salient display conditions showed that the accuracy of salient probes was worse and the reaction time was shorter compared to non-salient items for the neutral cue trials in both 100% and 50% informative conditions. This suggests that the salient feature of an item disrupts its VWM performance when its salient feature is irrelevant to the task demands.

In summary, the cue informativeness modulated the interaction between cue validity and salient display on accuracy measurement. However, the observed difference in interactions between the 100%-and 50%-informative conditions may be attributed to the deterministic nature of 100%-informative cues. As elucidated by Riggio and Kirsner45, in their Experiment 3, a fully informative cue ensures participants’ certainty regarding the stimulus occurrence, rendering peripheral cues such as item visual salience ineffective if outside the focus of attention. To further explore these effects, Experiments 2 and 3 adjusted cue informativeness levels (80% and 50% informative) and included variations in salient probes to provide deeper insights into their impact on VWM performance.

Experiment 2

When the pre-cue is 100% informative, participants are certain about the location of the stimulus occurrence, rendering the visual salience of the items ineffective45. To mitigate the deterministic effect or certainty associated with 100%-informative cues, in Experiment 2, we manipulated cue informativeness (80% and 50%). By lowering the informativeness of the cues from 100 to 80%, we aimed to reduce the degree of certainty participants had regarding the stimulus location. In addition to cue informativeness, we manipulated cue validity (valid, invalid, neutral) and salient display (salient, non-salient) to further investigate the modulation of cue informativeness on the interaction between cue validity and salient display. In this experiment, none of the salient items were tested.

Method

Participants

Sample size determination for Experiments 2 and utilised G*Power 3.146, estimating a medium effect size (f = 0.25). A priori analysis was conducted for a three-way within-factors design (informativeness: 80% and 50%; cue validity: valid, invalid, and neutral; salient display: salient and non-salient) in a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with 12 measurements of one group of participants. To achieve 80% power at a 0.05 significance level, a sample size of approximately 12 individuals was recommended. Consequently, we recruited 24 undergraduate students (female: 20; mean age: 19.42 ± 0.83) for participation in this experiment, offering them 10 RMB as compensation upon completion. All participants were enrolled from Shandong Second Medical University (formerly Weifang Medical University) and provided written informed consent in accordance with the university’s Ethics Committee protocol. They were all native Chinese speakers and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision; none had colour blindness.

Equipment and materials

Experiment 2 employed identical equipment and materials as Experiment 1.

Design and procedure

Experiment 2 employed a within-subjects factorial design, incorporating cue informativeness (80% and 50%), cue validity (valid, invalid, neutral), and display saliency (salient, non-salient) as factors. In the 80%-informativeness condition, participants were informed that when an informative cue was presented, the item indicated by the cue would be tested with an 80% probability, while the remaining items would collectively be tested with a 20% probability. On neutral trials, each item would be tested with a 25% probability. The instructions for the 50%-informativeness condition were identical to those used in Experiment 1. In both the 80%-informative and 50%-informative conditions, cue validity was categorised into valid, invalid, and neutral levels.

Each participant completed four blocks of 60 trials each, totalling 240 trials. The 80%-informative and 50%-informative conditions were allocated into separate blocks, each comprising two blocks. Within each 80%-informative block, there were 32 valid, 8 invalid, and 20 neutral trials, randomly interspersed throughout the block. Each 50%-informative block included 20 trials for each cue condition (valid, invalid, and neutral), randomly presented within the block. Within each block (both 80%- and 50%-informative conditions), half of the trials featured a salient item, while the other half did not. Consistent with Experiment 1, the pre-cue never pointed to a salient item when a valid informative cue was presented. The pre-cue and salient items were counterbalanced across the four item positions. Notably, the salient items were not tested in Experiment 2. The trial procedure was the same as that of Experiment 1.

Discussion

In Experiment 2, we expanded our investigation into the dynamics of top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM by manipulating cue informativeness, cue validity, and salient display. This experiment aimed to isolate the effects of cue informativeness by ensuring that salient items were never tested and setting cue informativeness levels at 80% and 50%. The results demonstrated significant main effects of cue validity on both accuracy and reaction time, mirroring the findings of Experiment 1. Valid cues consistently enhanced VWM performance and response speed compared to neutral cues, while invalid cues detrimentally affected performance and reaction times.

Moreover, an interaction between cue validity and salient display emerged, particularly impacting accuracy. This interaction revealed that the presence of salient yet irrelevant items impaired accuracy specifically in invalid-cue trials, while valid and neutral cues provided protection against this distraction for the cued item. However, non-cued items were not shielded from distraction, resulting in impaired VWM performance. Importantly, this protective effect of attentional cues for the cued items was evident across both 80% and 50% informativeness conditions.

In summary, Experiment 2 did not replicate the influence of cue informativeness on the interplay between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM. Nevertheless, the findings offer valuable insights into how cue validity and salient display interact to influence VWM performance when salient items are entirely irrelevant to the task.

Experiment 3

In Experiment 2, none of the salient items were tested, implying that these items might have been treated as distractors and consequently filtered out of the focus of attention before information processing. To delve deeper, in Experiment 3, we manipulated cue informativeness (80% and 50% informative), cue validity (valid, invalid, neutral), and salient display (salient, non-salient) to further investigate how cue informativeness modulates the interaction between cue validity and salient display. In this experiment, half of the salient items were tested. This setup introduced uncertainty for participants regarding the relevance of both the pre-cues and salient items to the final probe stage. This uncertainty allowed us to test how top–down and bottom–up attention interact in VWM in accordance with the task set.

Method

Participants

For this experiment, 24 undergraduate students were recruited (all females, mean age: 19.24 ± 0.95 years). Participants received 10 RMB as compensation upon completion. All participants were native Chinese speakers and enrolled at Shandong Second Medical University (formerly known as Weifang Medical University). They provided written informed consent following the university’s Ethics Committee protocol, reporting normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no colour blindness.

Equipment and materials

Experiment 3 used identical equipment and materials as those in Experiments 1 and 2.

Design and procedure

Experiment 3 closely mirrored the experimental design and procedure of Experiment 2 with some key distinctions. In the valid cue condition of Experiment 3, half of the salient items served as the cued items. Additionally, within each cue condition (valid, invalid, and neutral), half of the salient items were tested. Despite these variations, the trial procedure remained identical to that of Experiments 1 and 2.

Discussion

Experiment 3 aimed to deepen our understanding of the interaction between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM by manipulating cue informativeness, cue validity, and salient display. Unlike Experiments 1 and 2, Experiment 3 introduced a crucial variation: the salient items were counterbalanced among valid, invalid, and neutral trial conditions in both the 80% and 50% informative conditions, with 50% of the salient items being tested.

The primary finding of Experiment 3 highlighted a significant main effect of cue validity on accuracy. Specifically, valid cues enhanced VWM performance compared to neutral cues, while invalid cues impaired it. This outcome resonates with previous studies and echoes the findings of Experiments 1 and 2, reinforcing the robustness of the cue validity effect.

Comparing across experiments unveils intriguing nuances. Experiment 1 unearthed a significant three-way interaction, indicating that 100% valid cues could effectively suppress the influence of irrelevant salient items when only a few salient items were probed. In Experiment 2, both 50% and 80% valid cues exhibited proficiency in mitigating interference from irrelevant salient items when none of the salient items were probed. However, Experiment 3 solely demonstrated a cue validity effect without any interaction between salient display and cue validity, nor any effect of salient probes.

In summary, the findings from Experiment 3, in conjunction with those from previous experiments, suggest that the properties of cues (cue informativeness, cue validity) and salient display may intricately regulate the impact of top–down and bottom–up attention on VWM.

General discussion

This study examined the interplay between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM, focusing on how cue informativeness influences this interaction. Three experiments were conducted, varying cue informativeness, cue validity, and salient display. In Experiment 1, cue informativeness was manipulated to be either 100% or 50% informative. Both Experiments 2 and 3 maintained cue informativeness levels at either 80% or 50%. Additionally, while salient items were occasionally tested in Experiment 1, they were never tested in Experiment 2; in Experiment 3, half of the salient items in each cue condition were tested. Across three experiments, consistent cueing benefits were observed for cue-directed items, accompanied by cost for non-cued items, as evidenced by both accuracy and reaction time measurements. This underscores the predominant role of top–down attention in enhancing VWM performance, aligning with previous research17,19,22,24,57 that suggests informative pre-cues help prioritize task relevant information, enhancing its representation in VWM.

Importantly, we found that cue informativeness and the probe status of salient items affect how top–down and bottom–up attention interact in VWM. When cues were fully informative (100% in Experiment 1), valid pre-cues protected items from distraction by irrelevant salient items. Similarly, when salient items were never tested (Experiment 2), both highly (80%) and less informative (50%) cues effectively protected items from distraction. However, when the pre-cues were not fully informative and the salient items could potentially be tested (Experiment 3), only a main effect of cue validity was observed, suggesting that the influence of cue informativeness on the interaction between top–down and bottom–up attention may diminish in this context.

The findings from Riggio and Kirsner45 highlight the distinct roles of top–down and bottom–up attentional cues in information processing. Bottom–up attentional cues influence early perception processing, while top–down attentional cues influence the late perception or decision stages. Building on this, our study explored how these attentional mechanisms operate in VWM, particularly focusing on the influence of cue informativeness and the probe status of salient items. We observed that when pre-cues were fully informative or when salient stimuli were completely untested, the visual salience of an item did not affect the processing of valid cue-directed items. This suggests that when participants were certain about which items would be tested, they could effectively allocate attentional resources to relevant items, suppressing the salient items. Conversely, when cues were only partially informative or when salient stimuli were potentially being tested, the uncertainty of the pre-cues and the potential testing of salient probes led participants to adopt a safer attention strategy. In this case, both the pre-cues and the salient features of the item acted as attentional cues, competing for the limited resources. The salient item did not diminish the benefit of top–down attention; instead, it slightly diverted attention from the pre-cued location, allowing invalidly cued trials to benefit from the salient display’s redistribution of attention. The reason we did not find a benefit of visual salience for the salient item in these conditions may be attributed to the very few of the salient trials.

It is interesting to note that the reaction times were visibly slower in Experiment 2 compared to Experiment 3 overall. This difference in reaction times can be explained in terms of how the task demands of testing saliency might have interacted with the other factors and performance. In Experiment 2, where salient items were never tested, the task may have involved more rigorous filtering processes, requiring participants to consistently ignore the salient distractors. This increased demand could lead to slower reaction times as participants exert additional cognitive effort to maintain focus on relevant items. Conversely, in Experiment 3, where there was uncertainty about whether salient items would be tested, the mixed demands might have resulted in more efficient processing strategies, hence faster reaction times. The difference in reaction times might stem from the possible use of different strategies across experiments, and it remains an open question for future study.

Additionally, van Ede et al.58 adopted a retro cue paradigm also examine the effect of top–down (goal-directed) and bottom–up (stimulus-driven) attention selection in VWM. Their findings suggest that top–down attention primarily enhances memory accessibility, while bottom–up attention primarily improves memory quality. In our study, we adopted a pre-cue paradigm while manipulating the salient display, revealing the influence of cue informativeness and the probe status of salient items on how top–down attention shapes VWM performance. This underscores the distinct effect of pre-cues and retro-cues in VWM. Previous research indicates that pre-cueing allows for attentional anticipation before VWM encoding, whereas retro-cueing enables controlled spatiotopic access to representations recently encoded and maintained in VWM32,33.

Nevertheless, these findings contribute to the ongoing debate on the interaction of top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM, highlighting the modulation of interaction dynamics by cue informativeness and the probe status of salient items. Previous studies present inconsistent findings, with some suggesting that salient items can capture attention and be encoded into VWM even when they are irrelevant49,59 or less relevant60 to top–down task goals, while others propose that top–down attention can actively suppress the irrelevant items41–43. This study adds to this debate, emphasizing the role of cue informativeness and salient items probe status in modulating this interaction.

The findings support the idea that both top–down and bottom–up attentional processes contribute to VWM11–14 and illustrate the dynamic interplay between these processes. The observed effects suggest that cue informativeness modulates the degree to which top–down attention can effectively prioritise relevant information and shield against distraction from irrelevant items. Importantly, the modulation of this interaction by cue informativeness is further influenced by the probe status of salient items, underscoring the relevance of bottom–up item features to task demands. When salient items are irrelevant to task demands, they can be suppressed by top–down attention. However, when they are relevant to the task or when attentional cues are less informative, suppression of salient items becomes more challenging. The top–down and bottom–up attention could influence different information processing stages or different VWM45.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings consistently demonstrate cueing benefits for cued items across all experiments, albeit with a cost to non-cued items. Furthermore, we identified cue informativeness and the probe status of salient items as crucial factors shaping the interaction between top–down and bottom–up attention in VWM. These results underscore the critical roles played by cue informativeness and the relevance of salient items in modulating the dynamics of top–down and bottom–up attention within the context of VWM.
