Genetic diversity and comprehensive evaluation of physicochemical traits in Phyllanthus emblica L. for breeding and resource utilization
Authors: Jianchao Wang, Muhammad Moaaz Ali, Yongjie Wu, Ge Zhang, Xiaoyan Zhang, Lixue Xie, Lijie Zhang, Jianqing Chen, Tao Li, Faxing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhyllanthus emblica L. is a nutritionally and medicinally valuable fruit tree with extensive germplasm diversity. This study evaluated 61 genetically diverse accessions collected from China and South Asia for 27 physicochemical traits, including 13 physical and 14 chemical attributes, to assess genetic diversity and identify elite germplasm for breeding. Substantial phenotypic variation was observed, with coefficients of variation (CV) ranging from 5.13 to 98.09% for physical traits and 1.51–59.82% for biochemical traits. Notably, single fruit weight (CV: 98.09%), flesh stickiness (63.65%), crude fat (59.82%), and polysaccharide content (50.12%) exhibited high variability. Fifteen traits conformed to a normal distribution and were categorized into five probability-based grades. Correlation analysis showed that pericarp strength was significantly positively correlated with soluble solids, titratable acid, total flavonoids, polyphenols, and tannin content, while flesh hardness correlated positively with crude fiber and total sugar. Principal component analysis (PCA) reduced the 27 traits to 12 representative indicators, accounting for 82.54% of total variation. Hierarchical clustering grouped the germplasms into three classes: Class I (19 accessions) with high antioxidant contents and strong textures; Class II (2 accessions) with the largest fruit size and high flesh toughness; and Class III (40 accessions) with high moisture content and lower values for most traits, suitable for juice processing. A comprehensive biochemical evaluation model was constructed using PCA-derived weights, and the top-performing accessions—PEF30, PEF41, PEF35, PEF17, and PEF31—demonstrated superior nutritional quality based on total sugar, polyphenols, vitamin C, and flavonoid contents. These accessions are promising candidates for functional food development, fresh consumption, and cultivar improvement. This study provides a robust analytical framework and theoretical foundation for the classification, conservation, and targeted utilization of P. emblica germplasm in breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07161-x.Keywords: Amla, Fruit quality, Diversity analysis, Tropical and subtropical fruit, Resource conservation

Introduction

Phyllanthus emblica L. (synonym Emblica officinalis Gaertn [1]), commonly known as Yuganzi (Chinese), Yougan (Chinese), Amla, or Indian Gooseberry [2], is a tropical and subtropical tree belonging to the family Euphorbiaceae. Native to tropical Southeast Asia, it is widely distributed across various tropical and subtropical regions, including China, India, Indonesia, Pakistan, Uzbekistan, Sri Lanka, and Malaysia [1, 3, 4]. P. emblica is a medicinal and edible plant regarded as a vital economic resource. The World Health Organization has classified it as one of the three most important health-care plants cultivated worldwide [5]. It has been extensively utilized in traditional medicine systems such as Traditional Chinese Medicine and Indian Ayurveda and is now widely incorporated into modern medicine [6, 7]. The fruits of P. emblica are rich in a variety of bioactive compounds, including organic acids, terpenes, flavonoids, polysaccharides, tannins, alkaloids, phytosterols, amino acids, and vitamins [8]. These compounds contribute to its numerous medicinal properties, such as antioxidant [4, 9], anti-cancer [10–14], antiviral [15–17], hepatoprotective [18, 19], antibacterial [14], and anti-diarrheal [20, 21] effects. These attributes underscore the plant’s extensive application prospects in medicine, health foods, and other related fields.

With rising consumer interest in natural functional foods and dietary supplements, there is renewed interest in P. emblica as a high-value horticultural crop [22]. However, the species’ wide geographical distribution has led to the evolution of diverse germplasm with significant variation in fruit traits, including size, texture, flavor, and biochemical composition [23]. A thorough understanding of this diversity is essential for resource conservation, germplasm utilization, and genetic improvement efforts. P. emblica is distributed across subtropical and tropical regions, spanning 70°E–122°E longitude and 1°N–29°N latitude. Its broad geographical range encompasses diverse ecological types, numerous varieties, complex phenotypic traits, and significant genetic variation [24]. While studies in India and Southeast Asia have described some aspects of genetic diversity and phytochemical profiles, comprehensive evaluations integrating both physical and biochemical fruit quality traits remain limited [9, 11].

The study of genetic diversity is essential for conserving biological diversity and ensuring the sustainable utilization of biological resources [25, 26]. Currently, research on P. emblica germplasm resources is limited to a few regions, with no comprehensive evaluation system in place [27]. This gap underscores the urgent need for systematic and comprehensive assessments. Since the 1980 s, researchers at the Fruit Research Institute of the Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences have systematically investigated and collected diverse P. emblica varieties and wild germplasm across China and internationally. These efforts led to the establishment of China’s first national-level germplasm resource protection platform for P. emblica: the Phyllanthus emblica Germplasm Resource Sub-Center at the National Tropical Plants Germplasm Resource Center. Located in Zhangzhou, Fujian Province, this center houses over 60 genetically diverse resources, including accessions from Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan, and international sources such as India and Myanmar [5]. It provides a robust foundation for the protection, identification, and innovative utilization of P. emblica germplasm resources. Additionally, it offers abundant research materials for in-depth studies on the genetic diversity of this species. However, systematic evaluation of these resources based on both physical (e.g., fruit texture, size, weight) and chemical (e.g., vitamin C, polyphenols, flavonoids) quality traits is still lacking.

P. emblica is a characteristic fruit tree with significant potential for development. Its germplasm resources are abundant, exhibiting considerable genetic variation in fruit quality. However, fruit quality is a multifaceted concept influenced by sensory attributes (e.g., size, shape, peel color, flavor, taste, juiciness, crispness, firmness) and agricultural practices, as well as the fruit’s nutritional composition [28, 29]. The evaluation of fruit quality is critical for identifying superior resources and advancing the breeding of new varieties [30, 31]. Physicochemical quality is a key metric and serves as the foundation for assessing fruit quality [32, 33]. Fruit texture is an essential component of sensory quality, reflecting characteristics such as softness, hardness, brittleness, roughness, tenderness, density, and looseness. It is primarily determined by mechanical and geometric properties, which influence consumer preferences [34–36]. Despite its importance, current analyses of P. emblica fruit quality largely emphasizes vitamins, trace elements, volatile oils, and phenolic acids, based on a limited number of germplasm resources [37]. The integration of texture characteristics with physicochemical quality evaluation has been insufficiently explored. A comprehensive and objective assessment of the physicochemical quality of P. emblica resources, coupled with the identification of superior germplasm, will provide a solid theoretical foundation for the functional development of P. emblica fruit and the breeding of improved varieties.

Therefore, this study aimed to (i) assess the genetic diversity of 61 P. emblica germplasm accessions based on a wide range of physical and biochemical traits, (ii) explore relationships among these traits through correlation analysis, (iii) streamline trait evaluation through principal component and cluster analysis, and (iv) establish a comprehensive model to identify high-quality germplasm for breeding and conservation purposes. We hypothesized that significant genetic variation exists among the selected germplasm, and that integrating physical and biochemical quality traits would enable effective screening of superior resources. This work is expected to offer strategic insights for optimizing germplasm utilization and cultivar development, particularly under the subtropical conditions of Fujian Province.

Materials and methods

Plant material and chemical reagents

Mature fruits of P. emblica were harvested between October 2022 and December 2023 from the P. emblica Germplasm Resource Sub-Center, part of the National Tropical Plants Germplasm Resource Center, located in Zhangzhou, Fujian, China (E: 119°19ʹ59ʺ, N: 26°7ʹ48ʺ, altitude: 20 m). The area belongs to the south subtropical marine monsoon climate, with an average temperature of 21.4 °C, an average rainfall of 1,453 mm, an average relative humidity of 82%, and an average annual sunshine of 2,185.2 h. Fruits were carefully selected to ensure uniform maturity and to exclude specimens with mechanical damage, pest infestations, or disease symptoms. To enhance accuracy and reliability, the selection process was conducted collaboratively by at least two trained professionals. All plant materials were collected under permits granted by the Phyllanthus emblica Germplasm Resource Sub-Center. This study did not involve any human or animal subjects. Botanical identification of all accessions as Phyllanthus emblica L. was confirmed by taxonomists at the Fruit Research Institute, Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences, based on morphological traits as described by Gantait et al. [1] and Wu et al. [38]. Voucher specimens for each accession are maintained at the Germplasm Sub-Center herbarium.

The 61 germplasm accessions used in this study were all asexually propagated and grafted onto rootstocks of the same P. emblica variety, ensuring uniformity across all samples. There were no differences in rootstock genotype among accessions. Each germplasm was grafted during the same time period and cultivated under identical field management conditions. Each accession was represented by three trees planted in a randomized block design to minimize environmental variation.

For sampling, fruits were harvested from all three trees per accession. A total of 150 fruits were collected per accession and divided into three groups of 50 fruits each, representing three biological replicates. Equal representation from each tree within the accession ensured that each replicate reflected biological variability rather than tree-specific differences. The weight, vertical diameter, and transverse diameter of each fruit were measured, along with soluble solids content and texture. After deseeding, flesh tissues were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C in ultra-low-temperature freezers.

Materials from two production cycles were combined to analyze chemical quality indices. The 61 germplasm samples originated from 58 locations in China (Fujian: 26, Guangdong: 30, Guangxi: 2, Yunnan: 1) and international representatives from India and Myanmar (1 accession each). Details of the sample origins are provided in Table S1, and fruit morphological diversity is shown in Fig. 1. Hereafter, Phyllanthus emblica fruits are abbreviated as PEF.

Determination of physical quality

The weight of individual fruits was determined using an electronic balance (AW 220, Shimadzu, Japan). Vertical and transverse diameters were measured with an electronic digital vernier caliper, and the ratio of these two dimensions was calculated to determine the fruit shape index.

For texture analysis, 10 fruits were randomly selected from each germplasm resource. A TMS-PRO food texture analyzer (Food Technology Corporation, USA) was used to assess the mechanical properties. The equatorial section of each fruit was punctured using a P/2 probe, with an initial force of 0.750 N, a testing speed of 250 mm/min, and a puncture depth of 2–5 mm. This procedure was performed to evaluate pericarp strength, rupture depth, brittleness, and toughness. Each parameter was measured three times per fruit.

Following the pericarp analysis, the pericarp was shaved to a thickness of approximately 1 mm at the equatorial section. The same puncture procedure was then applied to evaluate flesh hardness, brittleness, toughness, adhesion, and stickiness. Similar to the pericarp measurements, each parameter was repeated three times per fruit to ensure accuracy and reliability.

The puncture force-running distance characteristic curve for P. emblica fruit (PEF) is illustrated in Fig. 2A and B, defining various texture indices for the pericarp and flesh. In Fig. 2A, pericarp texture indices include pericarp strength (N), represented by the force at the highest point ‘c’ on the curve, reflecting the hardness and resistance to chewing or breaking. Rupture depth of the pericarp, defined as the transverse distance between points ‘a’ and ‘b,’ indicates the elastic resistance to deformation. Pericarp toughness (mJ) is calculated as the area enclosed by the coordinate zeros, points ‘a,’ ‘b,’ ‘c,’ and the axes, representing the tissue’s ability to resist fracture under deformative force. Pericarp brittleness (N‧mm−1), the ratio of the force at point ‘c’ to the rupture depth, describes the propensity for fracture under minimal deformation. In Fig. 2B, flesh texture indices include flesh hardness (N), represented by the force at point ‘c,’ indicating firmness, and flesh brittleness (N‧mm−1), the ratio of the force at point ‘c’ to the rupture depth, reflecting the tendency to fracture. Flesh toughness (mJ) is determined by the area enclosed by the coordinate zeros, points ‘a,’ ‘b,’ ‘c,’ and the axes, indicating resistance to fracturing. Flesh adhesion (N), measured as the force at the lowest point of the curve (point ‘e’), represents the force needed to separate the flesh from a contact surface, while flesh stickiness (mJ), defined by the area formed by points ‘d,’ ‘e,’ and ‘f’ with the axes, reflects the work required to separate the flesh from the contact surface after rupture. These indices provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating the mechanical properties of PEF and their implications for physical and sensory quality.

Determination of biochemical attributes

The soluble solids content was measured using a digital refractometer (PAL-1, ATAGO, Japan). Crude protein content was determined through the Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method (China GB 5009.5–2016), with samples digested using a sulfuric acid-catalyst. Crude fat content was determined by Soxhlet extraction method (China GB 5009.6–2016). Moisture content was evaluated using a modified oven-drying method (China GB 5009.3–2016). Crude fiber content was determined by the paradigm washing method (China GB/T 5009.10–2003). The total sugar content was determined using the acid hydrolysis phenol-sulfuric acid colorimetric method (China GB/T 15672 − 2009), with glucose as the standard substance. The standard equation for this method was y = 195.97x-1.7239 (R²=0.9907). The titratable acidity content was assessed through the acid-base titration method (China GB/T 12293 − 1990). The sugar-acid ratio was calculated as the ratio of total sugar to titratable acidity. Total flavonoid content was measured using spectrophotometry [39], with rutin as the standard substance, and the standard equation was y = 1477.1x + 9.5888 (R²=0.9974). Total polyphenol content was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method [40], using gallic acid as the standard substance; the standard equation was y = 151.38x-0.7263 (R²=0.9983). The total triterpene acid content was assessed using the vanillin-glacial acetic acid method (China DB34/T 485–2014), with oleanolic acid as the standard substance, and the standard equation was y = 308.81x + 1.1352 (R²=0.9991). Total polysaccharide content was determined by the phenol-sulfuric acid method (China NY/T 1676–2008), using glucose as the standard substance, with the standard equation y = 90.11x + 1.8503 (R²=0.993). Tannin content was measured using the phosphomolybdic acid-phosphotungstic acid colorimetric method (China NY/T 1600–2008), with gallic acid as the standard substance, and the standard equation was y = 138.51x + 0.6951 (R²=0.9992). Vitamin C was quantified using 2,6-dichloroindophenol titration (China GB/T 5009.86–2016) [41]. In the standard equation for various determination methods, x represents the absorbance value, while y denotes the concentration of the corresponding standard substance. Each index was measured three times to ensure reliability.

Normality test and frequency distribution of physicochemical attributes

The significant value of Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) normality test of each physicochemical quality trait was greater than 0.05, which was the criterion for normal distribution. Traits that followed a normal distribution were categorized into five grades (grade I, grade II, grade III, grade IV, and grade V) based on four-point values: X-1.2818 S (point value 1), X-0.5246 S (point value 2), X + 0.5246 S (point value 3), and X + 1.2818 S (point value 4), where X represents the mean of the trait and S denotes the standard deviation. This categorization facilitated an approximate distribution of 10%, 20%, 40%, 20%, and 10% for grades I to V, respectively.

Comprehensive evaluation of biochemical attributes

The biochemical quality, encompassing nutritional indicators of 61 PEF samples, was systematically evaluated. The analyzed indicators included soluble solids, crude protein, crude fat, moisture, crude fiber, total sugar, titratable acid, sugar-acid ratio, total polysaccharide, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, total triterpenic acid, tannin, and vitamin C. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on each indicator to identify key variables contributing to overall quality variation.

The principal component score coefficients derived for each variable served as weights. These weights were applied to the standardized values of the corresponding variables to compute the linear weighted sum, enabling the construction of expressions for the principal component scores (Eq. 1). Statistical analysis

All 61 P. emblica accessions were grafted during the same time period onto rootstocks of the same variety to ensure consistency across samples. Each accession was represented by three clonally propagated trees grown under identical field conditions in a randomized block design. For each production cycle, 150 fruits were collected per accession (50 fruits per tree), and divided into three biological replicates (n = 3). Data were recorded as means of replicates. To reduce environmental influences, fruits were collected from different trees for each replicate.

Data organization and analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel 2016 and SPSS 27.0, including calculations of descriptive statistics, normality tests, genetic diversity indices (Shannon-Wiener index and Simpson index), principal component analysis (PCA), cluster analysis, and correlation analysis. All data were standardized using Z-scores before multivariate analysis. Cluster analysis was conducted using Ward’s method with squared Euclidean distance. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient in R 4.3. OPLS-DA and permutation tests were carried out using SIMCA 14.0 for group discrimination. Visualization tools included SPSS (histograms), SIMCA (PCA and clustering), and R (heatmaps and correlation plots).

Results

Descriptive analysis of physical quality

The descriptive analysis of the physical quality of PEF is provided in Table 1 and Table S2. The coefficient of variation (CV) and genetic diversity indices (H, D) for the fruit’s physical qualities revealed significant variability, reflecting considerable genetic diversity. The CVs for the 13 traits ranged from 5.13 to 98.09%, with an average of 33.53%. Notably, the CV for the fruit shape index was below 10%, while the CVs for the other 12 traits exceeded 10%. Traits with the highest CVs included single fruit quality (98.09%), flesh stickiness (63.65%), and flesh toughness (54.05%). These results indicated substantial genetic variation in these traits, highlighting the diversity among different germplasm resources. The values for H and D ranged from 3.86 to 4.10 and 0.9681 to 0.9836, respectively, signifying a high level of diversity.

Descriptive analysis of physical quality of P. emblica fruit

The variation in pericarp strength and flesh hardness of the fruit at maturity ranged from 8.52 N to 17.47 N and 5.73 N to 13.49 N, respectively. Both pericarp strength (12.86 N) and flesh hardness (8.66 N) were higher than those of many popular fruits, such as peach, apple, and pear, indicating that the fruit had a higher degree of hardness. Increased hardness requires more force for chewing or crushing, which is an important factor influencing the fresh fruit market. The material selected for this study, PEF61, is the fresh variety ‘Fubing 5 Hao,’ developed by the Fruit Research Institute of the Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences. This variety exhibits the lowest pericarp strength (8.52 N), rupture depth of pericarp (0.54 mm), and pericarp toughness (2.68 mJ) among all the resources examined. It also has lower flesh hardness (6.32 N), which enhances its chewiness for fresh consumption, while still providing adequate protection and freshness for storage. This variety has been successfully promoted in Fujian Province, China, filling a gap in the local market for fresh PEF.

Descriptive analysis of biochemical attributes

The descriptive analysis of the chemical quality of PEF is presented in Table 2 and Table S2. The coefficient of variation (CV) for the chemical quality of the fruits was lowest for moisture, at just 1.51%. In contrast, all other quality indices had CVs greater than 10%, with an average CV of 28.51%. Notably, crude fat (59.82%) and polysaccharides (50.12%) had the highest CVs, reflecting the distinct characteristics of different germplasm. The genetic diversity index (D) and the information index (H) ranged from 0.9778 to 0.9836 and 3.96 to 4.11, respectively, indicating a high level of genetic diversity. Fruit sugar-acid ratio ranged from 0.93 to 5.27, with an average value of 2.17, which was significantly lower than the optimal value. Therefore, the objectives for superior plant screening should focus on germplasm that contains higher levels of total sugar, polysaccharides, and along with lower acid content, which are crucial for the development of fresh fruit varieties.

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis revealed significant relationships among physical qualities, chemical qualities, and their interactions (Fig. 4, Table S3). Notably, pericarp strength showed a significant positive correlation with rupture depth of the pericarp, pericarp toughness, pericarp brittleness, flesh hardness, flesh brittleness, and flesh stickiness. It was also significantly positively correlated with soluble solids, titratable acid, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, and tannin content, but negatively correlated with moisture.

Correlation analysis of physicochemical qualities of P. emblica fruit. Different colors in the figure represent the Pearson correlation coefficient. Red indicates a strong positive correlation, and blue indicates a strong negative correlation. The deeper the color in the circle and the larger the area, the greater the absolute value of the correlation coefficient between the qualities

Rupture depth of the pericarp was significantly negatively correlated with total triterpene acid content. Pericarp brittleness was significantly or highly significantly positively correlated with soluble solids, total sugars, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, tannins, and vitamin C, and negatively correlated with moisture and total triterpene acid. Flesh hardness showed significant positive correlations with crude fiber and total sugar content, while flesh adherence was positively correlated with soluble solids and crude protein. Flesh stickiness, on the other hand, was significantly negatively correlated with soluble solids, crude protein, crude fat, moisture, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, and tannin content.

These correlation results provide a foundation for identifying key factors influencing fruit quality characteristics and for improving overall fruit quality.

Cluster analysis

The cluster analysis results, presented in Fig. 5, revealed that all physicochemical qualities could be grouped into three categories. Category 1 included moisture, flesh stickiness, single fruit mass, longitudinal fruit diameter, transverse fruit diameter, and fruit shape index, which are primarily associated with the physical qualities related to fruit appearance. Category 2 encompassed total triterpenic acid, pericarp brittleness, flesh brittleness, crude fiber, total polysaccharides, vitamin C, total sugar, sugar-acid ratio, crude fat, flesh adherence, and crude protein. Category 3 comprised rupture depth of the pericarp, pericarp toughness, pericarp strength, flesh hardness, titratable acid, tannin, soluble solids, total flavonoids, and total polyphenols. Notably, traits with strong correlations were clustered into the same category.

The analysis further categorized the 61 germplasm samples into three distinct classes (Fig. 5; Table 4). Class I consisted of 19 germplasm characterized by strong pericarp strength, pericarp brittleness, and flesh adhesion, accompanied by high levels of soluble solids, crude fat, total sugars, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, and tannins. These germplasms are particularly promising for antioxidant-rich supplement production. Class II included PEF54 and PEF56, which exhibited the largest single fruit mass and the greatest longitudinal and transverse diameters. This class also demonstrated the highest flesh toughness and the lowest flesh brittleness, significantly differing from the other two classes. Additionally, Class II displayed higher rupture depth of the pericarp, pericarp toughness, flesh stickiness, titratable acid, and sugar-acid ratio. Their firmness and size make them suitable candidates for commercial distribution and consumer preference. Class III comprised 40 germplasm characterized by higher moisture content, which significantly distinguished this class from the other two, while all other indices were at lower or the lowest levels. However, their higher water content may be advantageous for juice extraction or hydration-based products. This classification not only reflects phenotypic clustering but also helps prioritize accessions for specific breeding goals, such as enhancing antioxidant content, improving mechanical texture, or tailoring to different end-use markets.

Principal component analysis

Based on the principal component variance contribution ratio (Table S4), nine principal components were extracted to replace the original quality indices, with a cumulative variance contribution ratio of 82.541%. According to the principal component loading matrix (Table 5), the first principal component showed high loadings for total flavonoids (0.711), total polyphenols (0.801), and tannins (0.679). These are active ingredients with significant physiological functions, making the first principal component suitable to be named the “efficacy factor.” The second principal component exhibited higher loadings for pericarp strength, pericarp toughness, flesh hardness, and flesh toughness, all of which reflect the fruit’s resistance to external forces. Hence, it was named the “fruit hardness factor.”

Discussion

The comprehensive assessment of 61 P. emblica germplasm resources revealed substantial variability in both physical and biochemical fruit traits. This phenotypic diversity indicates a rich genetic reservoir, essential for breeding programs targeting specific fruit qualities. High coefficients of variation (CV > 30%) observed in traits such as fruit weight, flesh stickiness, and crude fat suggest these traits are under strong genetic influence and subject to considerable selective pressure, a common pattern in domestication studies of fruit crops [26]. Recent studies have highlighted the significant genetic diversity within P. emblica populations across different climatic regions. For instance, populations from dry climate areas exhibited higher genetic diversity compared to those from wet climates, suggesting environmental factors like precipitation and altitude play crucial roles in shaping genetic variation [43]. This environmental influence on genetic diversity underscores the importance of considering ecological factors in germplasm conservation and breeding strategies.

Sugar-acid ratio is one of the frequently used indexes to measure fruit flavor [41, 44, 45], a sugar-acid ratio between 10 and 20 is generally associated with a more favorable taste. In this study, fruit sugar-acid ratio was significantly lower than the optimal value. Therefore, the objectives for superior plant screening should focus on germplasm that contains higher levels of total sugar, polysaccharides, and along with lower acid content, which are crucial for the development of fresh fruit varieties.

The mechanical properties, particularly pericarp strength and flesh hardness, are critical to the texture and handling of fruits [46, 47]. The structure and composition of the cell wall play a crucial role in determining the mechanical properties of the fruit flesh, which directly influence its texture [48, 49]. These properties are influenced by the arrangement and compactness of the cell wall, as well as the number, size, and thickness of the cells [50]. Previous studies have demonstrated that changes in fruit texture are closely linked to the cell wall polysaccharides [50–52]. Our study showed that P. emblica has significantly higher pericarp and flesh hardness than common temperate fruits like apple and pear. These traits not only influence consumer acceptability but also affect post-harvest shelf life and mechanical resistance. Similar observations have been reported in other fruits, where enhanced mechanical properties contribute to extended shelf life and reduced post-harvest losses [53].

Correlation analysis revealed strong positive relationships between pericarp strength and phytochemical contents such as total flavonoids, polyphenols, and tannins. This supports the hypothesis that structural reinforcement of the fruit may be associated with biochemical investments in phenolic compounds, known to cross-link with cell wall components and enhance tissue rigidity [54]. Similarly, the positive correlation between flesh hardness and crude fiber aligns with findings in other fiber-rich fruits, where lignocellulosic content significantly contributes to firmness [2]. The findings align with previous studies, such as the observation that pericarp hardness in wine grapes is proportional to phenolic substances and anthocyanin content [55].

The use of PCA was effective in reducing the complexity of the dataset and identifying 12 core traits accounting for most of the variance. These included both physical traits (e.g., pericarp toughness, flesh brittleness) and nutritional traits (e.g., total polyphenols, sugar-acid ratio), which are consistent with previous findings on multidimensional trait evaluation in fruit quality studies [56]. PCA has been widely utilized in fruit quality assessment, providing a comprehensive understanding of trait interrelationships and aiding in the selection of superior cultivars [57, 58]. The classification of germplasm into 3 distinct clusters further highlights the genetic divergence among accessions. Class I germplasms, rich in antioxidant compounds (polyphenols, flavonoids, vitamin C), are prime candidates for functional food development. Class II, with large and firm fruits, may be better suited for commercial fresh markets due to consumer preferences for size and texture. Class III, with high moisture content, may be optimized for juice processing, as water content influences extractability and yield. Such clustering aligns with previous studies where germplasm grouping based on quality traits facilitated targeted breeding and utilization strategies [59].

Importantly, P. emblica is globally recognized for its medicinal and therapeutic potential. Its high titratable acidity, abundant vitamin C, and rich profile of polyphenols and tannins give it exceptional antioxidant, hepatoprotective, and immunomodulatory properties [11, 60]. The trait combinations identified in our study offer tangible opportunities for value-added product development. For instance, germplasms with high vitamin C and acidity (e.g., PEF30, PEF41) are suitable for functional drinks or antioxidant-rich tonics. Accessions with elevated polyphenol and tannin levels can serve as raw materials for nutraceuticals aimed at liver health, glycemic control, and oxidative stress reduction. Germplasms with moderate sugar-acid balance and improved texture offer potential for traditional processed products such as candies, herbal teas, or dried fruit snacks. Such applications align with global trends favoring natural, plant-based health products, and reflect growing demand in both pharmaceutical and food industries [3, 23]. The strategic use of germplasm based on integrated trait profiling, as demonstrated here, enhances the value chain of P. emblica from farm to industry.

OPLS-DA enhanced the interpretability of multivariate models by maximizing variation between specified groups while minimizing variation within individual replicates, without compromising predictive capability [61]. The construction of this model using PCA weights offered a robust framework for identifying elite germplasm. The top-ranked accessions—PEF30, PEF41, and PEF35—showed promise for further agronomic trials and molecular studies. Integrative models that balance trait contributions objectively are increasingly favored in germplasm evaluation, as they provide a holistic approach to cultivar selection [60].

Overall, our findings provide scientific insights into the physicochemical diversity of P. emblica, supporting both fundamental research in plant biology and applied breeding strategies. Future work should focus on linking these traits to genetic markers through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and exploring the environmental interactions influencing trait expression. Additionally, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying trait variation will further enhance the efficiency of breeding programs aimed at improving fruit quality and adaptability.

Conclusions

The analysis of 27 physicochemical qualities of P. emblica revealed significant genetic diversity, with physical qualities having an average CV of 33.53%, and chemical qualities showing a CV of 28.51%. Fifteen traits conformed to a normal distribution, providing valuable insights into the variability of P. emblica germplasm and establishing a probabilistic basis for trait grading and classification. Correlation analysis identified strong associations, with pericarp strength being positively correlated with soluble solids, titratable acid, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, and tannins, while negatively correlated with moisture content. Flesh hardness was positively correlated with crude fiber and total sugar content, indicating potential co-selection opportunities for texture and nutritional quality. Principal component analysis (PCA) effectively reduced the 27 evaluated physicochemical qualities to 12 key traits, including total polyphenols, moisture, flesh hardness, flesh toughness, pericarp toughness, flesh brittleness, total sugar, sugar-acid ratio, fruit shape index, crude protein, crude fiber, and crude fat. This reduction simplifies the assessment of P. emblica germplasm, enhancing the efficiency and precision of breeding and selection processes. A comprehensive evaluation model for the biochemical attributes of PEF was established, with the formula F = (0.31261Z1 + 0.14967Z2 + 0.11732Z3 + 0.08683Z4 + 0.07899Z5)/0.74542. Based on this model, the top 10 P. emblica germplasm resources, ranked by their comprehensive scores, were PEF30, PEF41, PEF35, PEF17, PEF31, PEF53, PEF9, PEF29, PEF24, and PEF40. These germplasm resources exhibited superior biochemical attributes and antioxidant-related potential, and are therefore recommended for further evaluation, product-oriented screening, and potential use in targeted breeding programs and value-added processing applications.
