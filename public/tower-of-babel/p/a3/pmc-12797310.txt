Cerebral arterial air embolism complicating robotic guided lung biopsy: A call for vigilance
Authors: Marc Assaad, Venkateswara Kollipara, Nathalie Abi Hatem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Flexible bronchoscopy has been extensively studied and is associated with a relatively low incidence of mortality and morbidity. Robotic bronchoscopy, a more recent advancement, has demonstrated non-inferiority to transthoracic needle lung biopsy and maintains an excellent safety profile. Vascular air embolism, while exceedingly rare, has been infrequently reported as a complication of therapeutic bronchoscopy. Despite its rarity, this complication carries a considerable risk of morbidity and mortality. Although several patient- and procedure-related risk factors have been identified, the infrequency of these cases presents challenges in elucidating the mechanisms involved and establishing optimal diagnostic strategies. Herein, we report what we believe to be the first documented case of cerebral arterial gas embolism following robotic-guided lung biopsy.

Introduction

Flexible bronchoscopy remains a cornerstone in the diagnostic evaluation of airway pathology, with decades of research affirming its favorable safety profile. Since 1974, multiple retrospective and prospective studies [1], have consistently demonstrated a low incidence of major complications estimated between 0.5 % and 1.1 % and an associated mortality rate of approximately 0.02 %–0.04 % [[2], [3], [4]]. The largest of these investigations, comprising 20,986 bronchoscopic procedures, provides the most robust data to date [3]. Notably, the highest complication rates are observed with transbronchial biopsies [1], whereas bronchoalveolar lavage is associated with the lowest risk, documented at 0.7 % [1]. Major complications of flexible bronchoscopy include hemorrhage, pneumothorax, respiratory failure, hypoxemia, pulmonary edema, respiratory infections, and bronchospasm among others [1,5].

Vascular air embolism (VAE) represents a rare yet potentially fatal complication, most commonly encountered during surgical, laparoscopic, and endoscopic procedures, with reported mortality rates ranging from 21 % to 50 % [6]. Its true incidence in the context of medical interventions is however likely underestimated, as clinical manifestations can often be subclinical [7].

While the risk of VAE is well established in endovascular procedures [6], its occurrence as a complication of fiberoptic bronchoscopy is rarely documented. In one series involving 772 bronchoscopies, VAE was reported in only a single case. Other data indicate an incidence of 2.65 per 100,000 hospitalizations related to bronchoscopy [6] and 0.02 % in additional reports, while another study observed 2 cases in 10,702 flexible bronchoscopies (0.019 %) [5]. Air embolism has also been reported following transbronchial needle aspiration at rates of 0.02–0.07 % according to Ragey et al. [8]. Among therapeutic bronchoscopic interventions, Argon plasma coagulation (APC), Nd-YAG laser therapy, and transbronchial biopsies are most frequently associated with the occurrence of VAE(6) [9]. In comparison to transbronchial biopsies, transthoracic needle biopsy (TTNB) or computed tomography (CT)-guided lung biopsy appears to carry a higher risk of air embolism. This is exemplified by a survey involving 60 patients, in which 15 % experienced air embolic events [10].

Additional risk factors identified in the literature that predispose patients to vascular air embolism during bronchoscopy include positive pressure ventilation, positioning of the needle tip near pulmonary veins, presence of cystic or cavitary lesions, underlying vasculitis, air trapping, obstructive lung disease, emphysema, air insufflation, Valsalva maneuver, episodes of coughing, a wedged bronchoscope, and tuberculous granulomas favoring venous sclerosis [2,3,9,11,12].

The clinical manifestations of air embolism exhibit considerable variability, ranging from an asymptomatic state in minor cases to profound cardiovascular collapse and death in severe instances [13]. Systemic air embolism may present as cerebral arterial gas embolism (CAGE), characterized by neurological deficits such as altered mental status, headache, seizures, motor or sensory impairment, and coma [2,13,14]. Coronary vessel involvement can result in electrocardiographic changes including ST-segment deviations, as well as tachyarrhythmias, bradycardia, and asystole. When air is present in the right heart chambers, it may precipitate obstructive physiology and acute cor pulmonale in addition to a decrease in pulmonary venous return, decrease in left ventricular preload and subsequent decrease in cardiac output [13]. Mesenteric artery involvement was associated with pneumatosis intestinalis [5].

Robotic bronchoscopy represents a recent advancement in the field of lung nodule biopsy. Current evidence suggests that robotic guided lung biopsy is associated with a lower complication rate compared to TTNB [15]. Nevertheless, comprehensive data detailing the incidence and spectrum of complications linked to this novel modality remain limited. In this report, we present what we believe to be the first documented case of CAGE and coronary artery air embolism following robotic lung biopsy.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old African American male with a history of obstructive sleep apnea managed with positive airway pressure, gastroesophageal reflux disease, hypertension, and biopsy-proven sarcoidosis initially diagnosed via mediastinoscopy with biopsy of mediastinal adenopathy in the 90s, being evaluated for worsening parenchymal infiltrates. The patient had not exhibited pulmonary involvement at the time of his original diagnosis and did not receive systemic therapy for sarcoidosis. He developed bilateral upper lobe parenchymal consolidations in 2022 that were initially followed on repeat imaging given the absence of symptoms.

Findings remained stable until recent chest imaging revealed a significant progression of bilateral upper lobe consolidations with worsening confluent opacities in both upper lobes, including a right upper lobe (RUL) lesion measuring 4.4 × 5.7 cm, a left upper lobe (LUL) cavitary lesion, as well as previously noted calcified mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy (see Fig. 1a 1b). The patient was intubated and sedated under general anesthesia, with mechanical ventilation set at tidal volumes of 8–10 mL per kilogram of ideal body weight and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 7 cm of water, in accordance with the Pinehurst protocol. Robotic bronchoscopy was performed utilizing ION technology, supplemented by fluoroscopic guidance and confirmation with radial endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS). Both the (LUL) and the (RUL) lesions were biopsied using forceps, a cytology brush, and a 21-gauge fine needle. Following tissue sampling, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed using 60 ml syringe with 40 ml of normal saline, succeeded by a push of 20 ml of air connected to the proximal portion of the ION catheter. Contents were slowly injected and aspirated. The return was cloudy, and blood tinged. Hemostasis was maintained by leaving the ION catheter in situ temporarily for tamponade, until the absence of bleeding was confirmed.

The procedure was well tolerated, and the robotic part was concluded. However, during the withdrawal of the robotic bronchoscope, the patient developed marked bradycardia, with the heart rate dropping to 20 beats per minute. Administration of epinephrine resulted in prompt improvement of the heart rate to the 60s. No drop in blood pressure was noted as systolic blood pressure remained in 90–100s. Oxygen saturations remained >95 %. An immediate airway evaluation with a flexible bronchoscope revealed no evidence of significant airway hemorrhage.

At that time, ST elevations were noted on the monitor. Cardiology was promptly called into the room. Subsequent electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated ST-segment elevations in the inferior and anteroseptal leads (see Fig. 2). Plans were made for emergent cardiac catheterization. Before proceeding with cardiac catheterization, the decision was made to extubate the patient and assess him neurologically. Extubation was performed; neurological assessment revealed right upper extremity motor weakness and encephalopathy, prompting activation of a stroke protocol.Fig. 2ECG demonstrating ST segment elevations in inferior and anteroseptal leads.Fig. 2

Laboratory investigations showed a troponin level of 1271 ng/L and an NT-proBNP of 354 pg/mL. An urgent head and chest CT scan did not demonstrate any evidence of air embolism or acute bleeding.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit and was evaluated by neurology. Fibrinolytic therapy was considered in the light of the initial neurological findings; however, its administration was ultimately deferred due to the patient's rapid clinical improvement throughout his neurological evaluation. He remained encephalopathic and this was attributed to his recent anesthesia. Left heart catheterization performed around 90 minutes post procedure revealed non-obstructive coronary artery disease involving a distal branch of the right coronary artery, which was supplied by collateral vessels. ST elevations had resolved at that time. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a normal left ventricular ejection fraction of 65 %, preserved right ventricular function, and no regional wall motion abnormalities. There was no evidence of intracardiac shunt, septal defect, or patent foramen ovale (PFO). Of note, cardiac windows were limited, and transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) was not performed.

Subsequent carotid duplex ultrasound revealed mild bilateral atherosclerotic plaques without hemodynamically significant stenosis. During hospitalization, the patient remained encephalopathic, experienced a single convulsive seizure episode on day 1 post procedure and received anticonvulsive therapy. On day 2 post procedure, he demonstrated progressive neurological recovery that led to complete resolution of symptoms by day 3. The patient was managed with high-flow oxygen via nasal cannula at 100 % and 20 L per minute given hyperbaric oxygen therapy was not available onsite. Transfer to a facility that could offer it was considered however it was not pursued due to the clinical improvement. The patient was subsequently discharged on day 4 post procedure with outpatient follow-up, having returned to his pre-procedural baseline.

Histopathological analysis of the biopsy specimens revealed non-caseating granulomas, supporting the diagnosis of worsening parenchymal involvement related to the patient's underlying sarcoidosis.

Although imaging did not reveal the presence of air emboli, the abrupt clinical deterioration and swift resolution of both ST-segment elevations and stroke-like symptoms, in conjunction with negative findings on ischemic workup, strongly suggest the occurrence of air embolism following transbronchial biopsies and instillation of air with BAL. It is postulated that performance of distal transbronchial biopsies could have disrupted fibrotic lung parenchyma with damage to capillary beds including pulmonary veins and venules. Fibrotic lung parenchyma could have created traction of broncho vascular bundles following lung biopsies, creating negative pressure and the addition of air with BAL could have introduced air directly into pulmonary veins and venules, promoting air embolism. Having high positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and positive pressure ventilation also could have contributed to the formation of air embolism.

Discussion

We report, to our knowledge, the first documented case of air embolism presenting as both CAGE and coronary artery air embolism mimicking ST-elevation myocardial infarction following robotic-guided lung biopsy. The patient's symptoms resolved completely with high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy and without lasting sequelae. A review of the literature reveals twelve previously reported cases of CAGE associated with therapeutic fiberoptic bronchoscopy [2]. Similar to our case, several of these patients experienced bradycardia—thought to be secondary to right coronary artery involvement [5,16]—as well as seizures and/or hemiplegia [5,8,9,11,12,14,16,17]. Additional notable clinical and laboratory findings have included retinal gas bubbles observed via ophthalmoscopy, Liebermeister's sign (pallor of the tongue due to lingual artery embolism), elevated hematocrit ratio [13,14] and mill wheel murmur appreciated by esophageal stethoscope [7].

Vascular air embolism (VAE) may be classified as either venous or arterial. When systemic manifestations such as acute neurological changes are observed, the possibility of a paradoxical embolus must be considered [13]. Air bubbles as small as 30–60 μm have the potential to interrupt coronary or cerebral blood flow [2]. While human data is lacking, it is estimated that an air volume of 200–300 mL, or 3–5 mL/kg, is typically fatal [7]. The precise mechanism underlying VAE during lung biopsy and therapeutic bronchoscopy remains incompletely understood. It is postulated that lung biopsy can create a broncho-vascular fistula, and that wedging of the bronchoscope may generate a pressure gradient sufficient to permit air entry into the pulmonary venous system once the absorptive capacity of the alveoli is exceeded [2,6]. The presence of an intracardiac shunt, such as a patent foramen ovale (PFO), can facilitate the passage of air bubbles into the left heart chambers, particularly when right-sided pressures are elevated (coughing) and left-sided pressures are decreased under mechanical ventilation [2]. Intravascular air bubbles not only disrupt distal perfusion but also provoke an inflammatory response in the affected vascular territory [13].

With regard to diagnostic modalities for VAE, the optimal approach remains a subject of debate. TEE is regarded as the most sensitive diagnostic tool; however, its use is limited by factors such as cost and the need for specialized operator expertise. Other potential modalities include precordial doppler ultrasound, contrast-enhanced transcranial doppler, end-tidal nitrogen monitoring (which is not widely available), end-tidal carbon dioxide, and pulmonary artery catheterization [7]. It is important to note that CAGE cannot be excluded based solely on normal brain imaging, as pathological changes may be subtle or transient; thus, negative imaging does not rule out the diagnosis [6,13]. Whether CT or brain MRI is superior for detecting VAE remains uncertain [2]. In the present case, our patient demonstrated acute findings on MRI but not on CT, paralleling observations reported by Ragey et al. [8]. Among twelve previously documented cases of CAGE following therapeutic bronchoscopy, nine exhibited imaging findings consistent with air embolism [2].

Management of air embolism remains complex and is largely supportive. In severe cases, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, administered at pressures higher than atmospheric, is the recommended intervention [2,6,13]. High-flow nasal cannula oxygen, capable of delivering 100 % inspired oxygen (FiO2), serves as an alternative, facilitating nitrogen washout and potentially mitigating endothelial injury, reperfusion damage, and cerebral edema [2]. Patient positioning strategies, such as the Durant maneuver (left lateral decubitus with head down) or the reverse Trendelenburg position, have been suggested [3,6]; however, concerns exist regarding the potential exacerbation of cerebral edema with a lowered head position. Some experts advocate for the supine or left lateral decubitus position instead [2,13]. Additional measures, including intravascular volume expansion to enhance venous return and the use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), have also been employed [6,13]. Current literature does not demonstrate a benefit from anticoagulation or corticosteroid therapy in the treatment of air embolism [13,14].

A complete neurological recovery was achieved in our patient following treatment with high-flow nasal cannula oxygen. Notably, Tsuji et al. reported a similar case in which full neurological function was restored within 1 h [5]. In a systematic review encompassing 12 cases of CAGE after therapeutic bronchoscopy, six patients received hyperbaric oxygen therapy, resulting in complete neurological recovery in three cases. The remaining six patients were managed with high flow oxygen: among these, four experienced either significant disability or death [2].

Conclusion

Cerebral air embolism remains a rare complication of diagnostic bronchoscopy. However, the advent of novel, robotic techniques that allow access to more distal airways and on higher level of PEEP, may disrupt the pulmonary vascular bed and could potentially increase that risk. We urge clinicians and bronchoscopists to maintain a high index of suspicion for air embolism when hemodynamic instability arises during robotic bronchoscopy. Procedural modifications—such as avoiding air injection following BAL especially post transbronchial biopsies, might mitigate this risk. This case highlights the need to critically evaluate and refine procedural performances to minimize the occurrence of this rare but potentially fatal complication.
