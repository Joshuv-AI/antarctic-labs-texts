Pooling data from studies on prolonged grief: the Multi-region Archive of Research data on Bereavement and Loss from Empirical Studies (MARBLES) project
Authors: Paul A Boelen, Samet Baş, Laura T Bolaséll, Richard A Bryant, Gizem Cesur-Soysal, Maarten C Eisma, Barbara Goodfellow, Jos De Keijser, Anette Kersting, Emrah Keser, Maja O'Connor, Holly G Prigerson, Bibi Schut, Iris Vissers, Suqin Tang, Julia Treml, Lonneke A Van Tuijl, Jianping Wang, Lisanne Asberg, Franziska Lechner-Meichsner, Chusi Xie, Xin Xu, Lonneke IM Lenferink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction: Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) was newly included in the 11th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) and the text revision of the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR). There is a need to increase our understanding of the prevalence, nature, and risk factors of this condition. Research in this area can be advanced by pooling existing datasets.Objective: The MARBLES (Multi-region Archive of Research data on Bereavement and Loss from Empirical Studies) project was initiated to create a consortium of researchers conducting studies on PGD. Its goal is to harmonize and combine data from separate studies into a unified, individual participant data-archive, boosting bereavement research.Methods: To date, data from 16,666 bereaved individuals have been compiled from 32 datasets across nine countries. All datasets include data on PGD symptoms and sociodemographic and loss characteristics; most also include data on emotion regulation and concurrent symptoms. For preliminary analyses presented in this paper, data on sociodemographic and loss-related characteristics and on PGD, posttraumatic stress, and depression symptoms were harmonized.Results: Regarding the preliminary analyses, participants’ mean age was in their mid-40s, about 70% of the participants were female, one in four participants had lost a partner, and one in four participants experienced an unnatural loss. Analyses (with multiple imputation) indicated that 18.3% met criteria for probable PGD (ICD-11).Conclusion: The MARBLES archive demonstrates that it is feasible to build a FAIR archive of PGD symptom data. Although the archive can already be used to study the prevalence, pathogenesis, and predictors of PGD symptoms and associated emotional problems, it is intended as an evolving resource. Its potential will expand as additional datasets are added and use of the archive grows. Future extensions should prioritize the inclusion of underrepresented groups (e.g. migrants) and currently missing social, cognitive, and neurobiological variables.KEYWORDS: Prolonged grief disorder, pooled data, data harmonization, data archive

Introduction

Grief is a normal response to the death of a loved one. Longitudinal research has shown that, apart from normal pain and grief, most people do not experience long-term emotional problems following loss (e.g. Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018; Nielsen et al., 2019). Yet, in a significant minority, loss precipitates severe mental health problems including persistent disabling grief, also termed prolonged grief (PG). Prolonged grief disorder (PGD), characterized by such grief reactions, is newly included in the 11th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11; WHO, 2022) and the text revision of the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of mental disorders (DSM-5-TR; APA, 2022). PGD is defined as present when an individual experiences separation distress (yearning, preoccupation) and accompanying symptoms (anger, difficulties accepting the death) that exceed expected social, cultural or religious norms and cause significant distress and functional impairments at least 6 months (as per ICD-11) or 12 months after a loss (as per DSM-5-TR) (Prigerson et al., 2021). Epidemiological studies indicate that a maximum of 5% of bereaved adults develop this condition (Boelen & Adamkovič, 2024; Rosner et al., 2021; Shevlin et al., 2023; Treml et al., 2024). Importantly, deaths of close persons can not only precipitate PGD but may also give rise to bereavement-related depression (characterized by dysphoria and lack of interest in previously fulfilling activities) and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD; characterized by reexperiencing memories, images, and emotions associated with threatening circumstances connected with the loss and the tendency to avoid cues reminding of those circumstances; Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2021). Furthermore, losses, particularly sudden deaths, are known to elicit first episodes of several other mental health conditions (Keyes et al., 2014). Consequences of loss thus constitute an important public health issue (Killikelly et al., 2021; Lichtenthal et al., 2024). A central aim of bereavement research is to understand and predict which individuals are at risk of developing PGD or other forms of psychopathology following loss (Killikelly et al., 2025).

Challenges in bereavement research

Despite evidence that emotional consequences of loss pose a substantial mental health burden, knowledge about the prevalence of PGD remains limited. For example, prevalence data and estimates are scarce for individuals with a refugee background, persons exposed to multiple or cumulative losses, and non-Western populations, as well as for individuals in clinical settings. Further, there is limited knowledge about the extent to which prevalence estimates vary as a function of the use of self-report versus interview-based assessments (see also Eisma, 2025). Although a growing number of studies have begun to examine PGD prevalence in some of these groups (e.g. in patients, see Haneveld et al., 2022, and in refugees, see Lechner-Meichsner et al., 2024), further research is needed to obtain representative prevalence estimates from different populations and contexts. With regard to the phenomenology and nature of emotional distress following loss, knowledge is limited about, e.g. the co-occurrence of PGD with other mental health conditions and about which mental health symptoms following loss most clearly distinguish between healthy and unhealthy grief and best predict different grief trajectories over time.

Challenges also remain with regard to risk factors. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Buur et al. (2024) concluded that elevated risk for PGD was associated with higher levels of pre-loss grief and depression, sociodemographic factors such as lower educational attainment, lower income, and female gender, personality-related characteristics including an anxious attachment style, and loss-related features such as unexpected death, violent or unnatural death, and the loss of a child or partner. Another systematic review and meta-analysis focusing specifically on PG symptoms following violent loss identified similar risk factors and additionally found associations with unemployment, absence of a family with children, exposure to multiple losses and additional traumatic events, and concurrent health problems (Heeke et al., 2019). Despite the importance of this work, existing literature on risk factors for PG and other adverse bereavement outcomes has limitations. For instance, studies in this area are predominantly cross-sectional and there is still limited knowledge about longitudinal and prospective predictors of PG. In addition, little is known about the interplay among risk factors, including the extent to which specific factors moderate or mediate the effects of others.

In addition to relatively static risk factors, a growing body of research has focused on psychological variables mediating bereavement outcomes. Cognitive behavioural theories (e.g. Boelen et al., 2006) propose that poor adaptation to loss is associated with difficulties integrating the loss into existing autobiographical memory, maladaptive cognitions and appraisals, and the use of avoidant coping strategies. According to the biobehavioural model proposed by Shear and Shair (2005), PG and related difficulties are linked to a failure to update the ‘working model’ of the relationship with the deceased. Based on their cognitive–attachment model, Maccallum and Bryant (2013) suggested that PG is associated with a merged identity with the deceased. In addition, the dual process model of grief posits that emotional disturbances following loss are related to disruptions in the oscillation between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping (Stroebe & Schut, 1999). Empirical research informed by these theoretical frameworks is increasing, and several of the processes identified in these models have been examined extensively (e.g. negative appraisals and avoidance; e.g. Smith & Ehlers, 2020, 2023). However, there remains limited knowledge regarding the distinction and overlap between variables and mechanisms proposed across these models, the dynamic interplay between variables over time (e.g. oscillation between orientations toward different stressors), and the ways in which variables operating at different levels of analysis (e.g. personality characteristics, cognitions, memory processes) interact to influence bereavement outcomes. Moreover, the impact of these variables is likely moderated by sociodemographic and loss-related factors (e.g. the effectiveness of specific coping strategies may differ between men and women), yet these moderating effects have received little empirical attention to date (cf. Xie et al., 2024).

The MARBLES project

In the last few decades, significant advances in understanding the nature, prevalence, and risk factors of PGD and related mental health problems have been made. Nonetheless, there remains an urgent need to further knowledge to answer questions like: What are the prevalence rates, patterns, and temporal relationships of PGD and co-occurring mental health problems, and how do these differ across different bereaved subgroups, cultures, and contexts? How do different categories of risk factors, including more ‘static’ sociodemographic variables and malleable psychological processes (including cognitive, behavioural, and emotional-regulatory processes distinguished in theories of grief), work together in affecting bereavement outcome? What trajectories can be identified in the development of grief over time and how do risk factors influence trajectories? Answers to these questions are essential for enhancing the early identification of individuals at increased risk of mental health problems following loss and to foster the provision of timely preventive care tailored to their specific risk profiles (cf. Boelen, 2026).

The MARBLES1 (Multi-region Archive of Research data on Bereavement and Loss from Empirical Studies) project was initiated to contribute to the advancement of knowledge about psychopathology following loss. The project aims to establish an international consortium of researchers conducting cross-sectional and longitudinal studies on PG and to standardize, harmonize, and integrate raw data from samples collected in these separate studies into a unified individual participant data archive. This infrastructure enables systematic investigation of the prevalence, pathogenesis, and predictors of PGD across diverse types of losses, circumstances, and contexts. The MARBLES project is grounded in FAIR data principles. Accordingly, the dataset is Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable for researchers seeking to conduct in-depth analyses of these issues. The intended and anticipated value of MARBLES lies in its capacity to overcome the fragmentation, limited statistical power, and methodological heterogeneity that have constrained prior research.

In this paper, we describe the methods and approach we used up until now to gather and harmonize individual- and item-level data on PGD and associated constructs. We also provide information on the datasets generated so far. To provide an impression of the data currently included in the archive and to illustrate the types of research questions for which it may be used, we present selected results from preliminary, illustrative analyses of the prevalence and correlates of PGD, PTSD, and depressive symptoms based on data contained in the archive. We end with a reflection on challenges we faced when we started building the archive, research topics that could be examined in future work, and ways to contribute to and use the MARBLES archive as it grows.2

Methods

Data sources

Datasets are eligible for MARBLES if they (i) are from studies that have undergone independent ethical review (or local equivalent), (ii) include at least one measure of PGD symptoms, (iii) are performed with adults (≥18 years), and (iv) have individual- and item-level data available for pooling. Supplementary Table 1 describes sample characteristics, recruitment strategies, inclusion criteria, and eligibility criteria of the 32 datasets included in the MARBLES archive so far. The MARBLES initiators (the present paper’s first and last authors), started with combining data from some of their own projects (Dataset 1-5). Next, we contacted researchers from prior collaborations (e.g. Dataset 6, 9–13), and subsequently other researchers active in PGD research (e.g. Dataset 14–30), while gradually adding more of the initiators own projects (Dataset 7, 8). At this stage of the project, we did not yet employ a systematic approach to including new datasets, but instead contacted researchers based on existing contacts and recent studies, because the research questions the archive aims to address are still broad. Now that the archive has been established, we are exploring more systematic approaches to acquiring additional datasets (e.g. through targeted solicitation of studies with relevant populations and/or variables).

Data standardization, harmonization, and pooling

Participating researchers contributed raw data without any personal, identifying information. Datasets included at least one measure of PGD symptoms, information on sociodemographic variables and characteristics of the loss, concurrently assessed mental health outcomes (e.g. depression, PTSD), and other variables assessed in the study. If deemed eligible, variables in the study were standardized and harmonized (cf. Kassam-Adams et al., 2020). By standardization, we refer to the process of establishing consistent variable numbering, names, labels, and response coding (i.e. numeric values) that are applied uniformly to each new dataset. (A periodically updated ‘data dictionary’ is available on the MARBLES website; https://www.uu.nl/en/research/the-marbles-project). Harmonization refers to the process of achieving common item responses for a given construct, from different instruments used to measure that construct. At this stage of the project, we have harmonized socio-demographic and loss-related variables (see below) and data from instruments assessing symptoms of PGD, PTSD, and depression.

Drawing on procedures from the Child Trauma Data Archives (Kassam-Adams et al., 2020), we applied a three-step method to achieve expert-reviewed, item-level semantic harmonization. To illustrate, first, we aligned the symptom-level diagnostic criteria for PGD across the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 (e.g. the DSM-5-TR symptom ‘Identity disruption [e.g. feeling as though part of oneself has died] since the death’ was aligned with the ICD-11 symptom ‘Feeling one has lost a part of one’s self [C7]’). Second, we mapped semantically similar items from available PGD measures onto these consolidated symptom categories. For example, the items ‘I feel that a part of me died along with the deceased’ (ICG-R) and ‘Do you feel confused about your role in life or feel like you don’t know who you are [i.e. feeling that a part of yourself has died]?’ (PG-13) were both mapped onto this category. Finally, an expert panel reviewed the proposed symptom concepts and item wordings. The panel comprised PB and LM, initiators of the archive, clinical researchers experienced in bereavement research and care, and bachelor-level psychologists familiar with literature on PGD criteria. Moreover, recommendations by Sekowski and Prigerson (2021) on mapping PGD questionnaire items onto diagnostic criteria were taken into account.3 Similar procedures were applied to map items assessing PTSD and depression symptoms onto PTSD and depression criteria as defined DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. Here, we also considered the harmonization of PTSD and depression measures performed via an expert consensus process at the Child Trauma Data Archives (available from https://childtraumadata.org/use-data/harmonization).

For most PGD, PTSD, and depression measures, items were scored on 5-point scales representing increasing severity of symptoms. Some measures employed a 4-point scale, which we converted to a 5-point scale.4 In addition to these Likert-type scores, we also recoded the scores into a dichotomous variable (0 = symptom absent, 1 = symptom present), with presence defined as the two highest response categories on the 5-point scale.5 (Harmonization tables for the PGD, PTSD, and depression items are available at the MARBLES website; https://www.uu.nl/en/research/the-marbles-project.)

Constructs and measures

Starting with pooling data from our own research, data were categorized into sociodemographic variables, loss-related variables, key outcomes of PGD, depression, and PTSD symptoms, and cognitive behavioural emotion regulatory variables. With the newly added datasets, categories were added. Supplementary Table 2 provides an overview of measurement instruments and constructs included across the datasets. Instruments are also briefly described below. Categories and concepts are likely to expand as the archive continues to grow.

Sociodemographic variables

The participant’s age and sex could be reliably derived from the different datasets. Other variables were coded differently across sets and harmonized for use in the pooled dataset. Education was coded variably, due to different countries having different education models; for most datasets, education could be recoded in years of schooling and/or a dichotomous variable of (i) highest completed education being college/university or (ii) lower than college/university. Some other variables were not consistently measured across studies but were included in the pooled dataset, given that the amount of available data on these variables is expected to increase as new datasets are added. For instance, several datasets included information about marital status, ethnicity, race, religious affiliation, and employment status.

Loss-related variables

Cause of death was typically assessed using multiple categories such as illness, accident, suicide, homicide, and sudden medical events. Given its importance in bereavement research, we have, for now, retained several categorizations in the MARBLES archive. For a substantial portion of the pooled data, cause of death can be classified into four categories: (i) natural causes, (ii) suicide, (iii) accident, and (iv) homicide. For nearly all datasets, a broader dichotomization into (i) natural or (ii) unnatural causes is possible. A similar approach was taken for the relationship to the deceased. For a large portion of the data, relationship could be categorized into four groups: the deceased was a (i) partner, (ii) child, (iii) parent, or (iv) another close person. For nearly all data, this information could be dichotomized into (i) loss of a partner or child, or (ii) loss of another close person. Time since loss was typically recorded in months and was available for nearly all cases in the archive. As with other variables, we included some loss-related variables that were only available in a limited number of datasets, based on their potential value as the archive grows. These included the unexpectedness of the death, the age of the deceased, and the degree of closeness to the lost person.

PGD, PTSD, and depression symptoms

Instrument to assess PGD symptoms include: (i) the Inventory of Complicated Grief including the original version (Prigerson et al., 1995) and shortened version used in Dataset 6 (O'Connor et al., 2010), (ii) the extended, 29, 30, or 32 items ICG, sometimes called the Inventory of Complicated Grief-Revised (Prigerson & Jacobs, 2001) or Inventory of Traumatic Grief (Boelen et al., 2003), (iii) the Prolonged Grief-13 (PG-13; Prigerson & Maciejewski, 2006) and its revised version, the PG-13-R (Prigerson et al., 2021), (iv) versions of the Traumatic Grief Inventory (TGI), including its 18-item self-report version (TGI-SR; Boelen & Smid, 2017), the 22 item TGI-SR + (Lenferink et al., 2022), and the clinician administered version (TGI-CA; Lenferink et al., 2023), and (v) the International ICD-11 Prolonged Grief Disorder Scale (IPGDS; Killikelly et al., 2020).

Measures of PTSD include: (i) the PTSD symptom scale (PSS; Foa et al., 1993), (ii) PTSS Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015), (iii) PTSS Checklist-Civilian version (PCL-C; Ruggiero et al., 2003), (iv) Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (Mollica et al., 1992), and (v) Impact of Event Scale in its original version (IES; Horowitz et al., 1979) and revised version (IES-R; Weiss & Marmar, 1997).

Measurements of depression symptoms include: (i) the Beck Depression Inventory, version I (Beck, 1961) and II (Beck et al., 1996), (ii) the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD, Hamilton, 1960), (iii) the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001), (iv) the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (QIDS; Rush et al., 2003), (v) Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS; Zung, 1965), and depression subscales of the (vi) Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), (vii) Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS, Zigmond & Snaith, 1983), and (viii) Symptom Checklist 90 (SCL-90; Derogatis, 1994).

Other outcome variables

Given that the distinction between healthy and unhealthy grief largely depends on its impact on functioning, various datasets contained measures of functional impairment, including the Work and Social Adjustment scale (WSAS; Mundt et al., 2002), and quality of life, including the Short Form health survey (SF-36; Ware & Sherbourne, 1992) and the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0; WHO, 2010).

Different datasets also assessed generalized anxiety (e.g. Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 [GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006], HADS [Zigmond & Snaith, 1983], State-Trait Anxiety Inventory [STAI; Spielberger, 1983], Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale [SAS; Zung, 1971]), anger (e.g. Posttraumatic Anger Questionnaire [PAQ; Orth & Maercker, 2009], State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory–2 [STAXI-2; Spielberger, 1999]), and broader psychopathology (e.g. Symptom Checklist-90 [SCL-90; Derogatis, 1994], Kessler Psychological Distress Scale [K10; Kessler et al., 2003], Patient Health Questionnaire-4 [PHQ-4; Kroenke et al., 2009]).

Positive outcome variables in some datasets include indices of mental health, such as the Mental Health Continuum–Short Form (MHC-SF; Lamers et al., 2010), posttraumatic growth measured by the Post-Traumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) in both its original 21-item version (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996) and the abbreviated 10-item version (Cann et al., 2010), as well as life satisfaction assessed by the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS; Diener et al., 1985).

Incidentally, only in one or two datasets, data were available on other outcomes that we decided to include in the archive based on their relevance to research aims and the likelihood of recurrence in future datasets. These outcomes included alcohol use, smoking history, separation anxiety (Dataset 13), suicidality (Dataset 8), loneliness (Dataset 6), chronic nonspecific arousal/stress (Dataset 16), and bereavement related guilt (Dataset 18).

Cognitive behavioural and emotion regulatory variables

The first datasets included in MARBLES were collected for research on cognitive behavioural variables as defined in Boelen et al.’s (2006) model. Therefore, different sets included data obtained with the Grief Cognitions Questionnaire (GCQ), including its original 38-item version (Boelen & Lensvelt-Mulders, 2005) and shorter version (Doering et al., 2021). Several sets included the Experienced Unrealness Scale (EUS; Boelen, 2010) and the Depression and Anxious Avoidance in Prolonged Grief Questionnaire (DAAPGQ; Boelen & van den Bout, 2010). As the archive grew, data have been added on variables originating from other grief-related theoretical frameworks, including ‘grief-rumination’ (Utrecht Grief Rumination Scale, UGRS, Eisma, Schut, et al., 2014) and ‘typical beliefs’ (Typical Beliefs Questionnaire, TBQ, Skritskaya et al., 2016).

Additionally, several datasets included concepts related to self-identity, such as event centrality measured by the Centrality of Event Scale (CES; Berntsen & Rubin, 2006), self-esteem assessed with the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES; Rosenberg, 1965), and self-concept clarity measured by the Self-Concept Clarity Scale (SCCS; Campbell, 1990).

More general regulatory variables (not specifically tied to loss) have also been included in some datasets, including: (i) experiential avoidance, measured by the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire versions 1 (AAQ-1; Hayes et al., 2004) and 2 (AAQ-2; Bond et al., 2011); (ii) difficulties with emotion regulation, assessed using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) in both its 36-item version (Gratz & Roemer, 2004) and 16-item version (Bjureberg et al., 2016), as well as the 10-item Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ; Gross & John, 2003); (iii) worry, assessed using the Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ) in its 16-item (Meyer et al., 1990) and 8-item (Crittendon & Hopko, 2006) versions; (iv) rumination, assessed with the Ruminative Response Scale (RRS), a 22-item measure that includes the frequently used distinct scales of ‘brooding’ and ‘reflection’ (Treynor et al., 2003), and the Rumination-Reflection Questionnaire (RRQ; Trapnell & Campbell, 1999); and (v) responses to positive affect, measured by the Responses to Positive Affect questionnaire (RPA; Feldman et al., 2008).

Incidental cognitive behavioural and emotion regulatory variables represented in single datasets included autobiographical memory characteristics (Dataset 22), coping self-efficacy and just world beliefs (Dataset 5), coping styles (Dataset 6), early maladaptive schemas as defined in schema therapy (Dataset 24), and meaning reconstruction (Dataset 19).

Trait and personality variables

While the archive gradually grew, data related to trait and personality variables were also added. Particularly pertinent to grief is that several datasets included measures of attachment, such as the Adult Attachment Scale (AAS; Collins & Read, 1990), the Experiences in Close Relationships scale, including its revised version (ECR-R; Kooiman et al., 2013) and short form (ECR-S; Wei et al., 2007), and the Relationship Questionnaire (RQ; Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994). Additionally, datasets included measures of the Big Five personality traits, such as the Ten-Item Personality Inventory (TIPI; Gosling et al., 2003) and the NEO Personality Inventory-Revised (NEO PI-R; Costa Jr & McCrae, 2008). Several datasets included only the neuroticism items from these measures. Further trait-like characteristics considered in several datasets included optimism (Life Orientation Test [LOT]; Scheier et al., 1994), intolerance of uncertainty (Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale [IUS-12]; Carleton et al., 2007), trait mindfulness (Mindful Attention Awareness Scale [MAAS]; Brown & Ryan, 2003), trait resilience (Resilience Evaluation Scale [RES]; Van der Meer et al., 2018), and a sense of coherence (Sense of Coherence Scale [SOC]; Antonovsky, 1993).

Miscellaneous variables

Miscellaneous variables present in individual datasets included different aspects of social support and relationships, measured using the Interpersonal Support Evaluation List (ISEL; Cohen & Hoberman, 1983; Dataset 13), the Crisis Support Scale (Joseph et al., 1992; Dataset 6), an ad hoc Social Integration Scale constructed for Dataset 13, and the Quality of Relationships Inventory (QRI; Pierce et al., 1991; Dataset 21). Other variables included prior life events, assessed with the Life Events Checklist (LEC; Weathers et al., 2013; Dataset 25) and the Life Events Scale (LES; Van der Velden et al., 1992; Dataset 31), and variables conceptually related to the bereaved person’s ongoing relationship with the deceased, including unfinished business, measured by the Unfinished Business in Bereavement Scale (UBBS; Holland et al., 2018; Dataset 18) and continuing bonds, measured with the Multidimensional Continuing Bonds Scale (MCBS; Keser & Işıklı, 2021; Dataset 20).

Data storage

MARBLES data are stored at Yoda, a research data management service that enables storage, sharing, archiving, and publishing of research data (https://www.uu.nl/en/research/yoda). Yoda software was developed at Utrecht University but is now widely used by different organizations inside and outside the Netherlands.

Preliminary analyses

For illustrative purposes, we conducted several basic analyses to provide information about the participants in the archive, including (i) sex, (ii) age, (iii) dichotomized education (i.e. college/university vs. other), (iv) dichotomized kinship (loss of partner/child vs. other close person), (v) dichotomized cause of death (natural vs. unnatural cause), and (vi) months passed since the loss. In addition, we examined descriptive statistics of scores on items mapping onto ICD-11 criteria for PGD, depression, and PTSD. Further, we examined to what extent summed scores on ICD-11-based PGD, depression, and PTSD symptoms differed as a function of the aforementioned six participant characteristics. Finally, we calculated how many people met criteria for probable ICD-11-based PGD, and examined whether probable caseness differed as a function of participant characteristics.6 For brevity, we did not consider DSM-5-based symptoms.

All analyses were performed after imputation of missing data. Thirty-six percent of the values were missing (in part because widely used scales, such as the ICG, do not capture certain symptoms of ICD-11-based PGD, including ‘blame’ and ‘inability to experience positive mood’). To address this, we selected data on six participant characteristics as well as ICD-11-based symptoms of PGD, PTSD, and depression, and applied multiple imputation. This procedure allowed us to retain all participants and maximize the use of available information. The pooled dataset comprised 32 datasets currently included in the MARBLES archive, each characterized by structural (i.e. planned) missingness arising from study-level differences in measurement protocols. Because missingness was determined by study design rather than participant-level factors, the data were assumed to be missing at random. Under this assumption, we employed multiple imputation via chained equations using the mice package in R (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Specifically, the two-level predictive method was applied (e.g. 2 l.pmm). This method allowed us to impute missingness while accounting for the study-level difference. Given the 36% missingness rate, 36 imputed datasets were generated, each with 50 iterations, to ensure adequate efficiency and stability for subsequent pooled analyses. The imputation model included all six socio-demographic and loss-related variables mentioned at the beginning of this section as auxiliary variables, given their established associations with grief-related symptoms and relatively low missingness. For items measuring symptoms of PGD, PTSD, and depression, only those with a correlation greater than 0.1 were selected as auxiliary variables. This criterion ensures that the imputation process relies on variables with a certain degree of predictive relevance, thereby effectively filtering out noise interference. One PGD item (i.e. ‘Response exceeding cultural norms’) was excluded from the imputation because of extreme missingness rate (97%) and high multicollinearity with other variables. The quality of the multiple imputation was assessed by visualizing convergence plots for stability and distribution plots to verify that the imputed values followed patterns consistent with the original dataset. All subsequent analyses described below were performed on the 36 imputation-based datasets and results are reported based on pooled estimates, using Rubin’s rules (Rubin, 2018). Specific results for different datasets as well as convergence plots and distribution plots are available upon request from PB.

Results

Study features and sample characteristics

As summarized in Supplementary Table 1, of all 32 sets included so far, 12 are longitudinal with 2–11 waves; 20 are cross-sectional. Data were gathered in nine countries. The total sample size (for all wave 1 data) is N = 16,666. Basic characteristics of the samples (before imputing missing data) are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Across all sets and considering available data, the average age varies from around 30 (Datasets 16, 21, 22) to a little over 70 (Dataset 6). In nearly all sets, the majority of participants are women. Dichotomized education varies, from only 3.9% having had college or university (Dataset 14) to 93.1% (Dataset 28). Time since loss varies as well, from 3.9 months (Dataset 30) to 128.1 months (Dataset 15). Considering dichotomized cause of the death, some projects were exclusively focused on the impact of unnatural deaths (plane crash, Dataset 5; homicidally bereaved, Datasets 9 and 10). In all other projects, most participants experienced a natural loss, typically due to illness or old age. The relationship to the deceased, dichotomized as loss of a partner or child versus another close person, also varied across datasets. In Dataset 24, only 1.5% had experienced the loss of a partner or child, compared to 87.9% in Dataset 13. Table 1 shows the pooled descriptive statistics for all study variables based on the imputation-based datasets. Participants had a mean age in the mid-forties, the majority were female, and educational level was evenly distributed across the two categories. Approximately one-third had lost a parent, one-quarter a partner, and one-tenth a child. In three-quarters of cases, the death was due to natural causes.

Correlates of summed PGD, PTSD, and depression symptom scores

We examined whether total symptom levels of PGD, PTSD, and depression differed as a function of socio-demographic and loss-related characteristics, using Welch’s analysis of variance (accounting for unequal variances across groups) for categorical variables and Pearson correlations for continuous variables. Table 3 summarizes the pooled results. Across all three symptom clusters, women reported higher scores than men. Symptom severity also differed by relationship to the deceased. Post hoc tests (Supplementary Table 4) showed that participants who lost a child reported the highest symptom scores, significantly higher than several other groups. For cause of death, unnatural cause was associated with higher symptom scores than natural cause across all outcomes. Percentage and correlates of probable PGD caseness

Overall, 18.3% (95% CI [16.8%, 20.0%]) of participants met ICD-11 criteria for probable PGD (n = 3058 of 16,666). We examined whether participants who did vs. did not meet these criteria differed in terms of the six socio-demographic variables and loss characteristics, using distinct logistic regression analyses. Table 4 summarizes outcomes. Age did not differ between groups. Women were more likely than men to meet these criteria, and individuals with a university education were less likely to meet these criteria than those with other educational backgrounds. Loss characteristics were also associated with probable PGD caseness. Participants bereaved due to unnatural causes of death were more likely to meet criteria for probable PGD than those bereaved due to natural causes. The relationship to the deceased mattered as well: losing a child or partner was associated with a higher likelihood of probable PGD than losing other kinship relationships. Finally, individuals who met criteria for probable PGD had, on average, a shorter time since loss than those who did not. To allay concerns that the results were influenced by the type of measurement instrument used to assess PGD symptoms, we reran all analyses while controlling for the PGD measures used across the different datasets in the archive. Specifically, we dummy coded the PGD instruments and included them as covariates in the models to adjust for potential instrument-related variability. Discussion

Current status and anticipated development of the archive

In the MARBLES archive, we aim to harmonize and combine individual- and item-level data collected in studies on PGD from around the world. The development of the archive was initiated to maximize the utility of existing research data and to enable large-scale investigations into the nature, prevalence, and risk factors of PGD and related mental health conditions. Furthermore, by bringing together researchers worldwide to develop a FAIR dataset accessible to researchers globally, including those from low and middle-income countries, we aim to advance bereavement research in accordance with open science principles. We actively seek to further expand the archive, both in the number of datasets it encompasses and the range of variables included. Because the archive began with the integration of data from studies focusing on cognitive–behavioural variables, it currently includes substantial information on maladaptive appraisals, coping behaviours, and other emotion regulation processes that are central to cognitive–behavioural models of grief (e.g. Boelen et al., 2006). During the development of the archive, constructs and questionnaires that appeared for the first time in a new project were selectively incorporated. Constructs were added when they were considered relevant to the aims of the MARBLES project (i.e. improving the prediction of PGD) and likely to recur in future datasets. Constructs were not included when they related to a very specific research question not aligning with the objectives of MARBLES and when they would likely be infrequently investigated in the future.

Some important categories of variables are still absent, and it would be beneficial to be able to incorporate them into the archive in the future. For instance, from the perspective of dual-process models of psychopathology (e.g. Beevers, 2005), PGD is influenced by both fast, associative, automatic information processing and slower, deliberate, controlled processing. To date, the MARBLES archive primarily includes variables reflecting the latter. It still lacks variables related to automatic, implicit, and intuitive processing (e.g. cognitive biases, implicit associations, emotional conditioning). Similarly, neuropsychological variables (e.g. executive functioning, attention, memory) and neurobiological variables (e.g. brain changes, neuroendocrine activity) are not (yet) included in the archive. Furthermore, although there is considerable research on social support, other relevant variables related to social functioning – such as social deprivation resulting from bereavement, changes in identity and social roles, and socio-cultural and religious factors – are not yet included in the archive. The lack of data on these variables in the MARBLES archive partly reflects the fact that these topics have received relatively limited research attention to date. Indeed, data collection on implicit, neuropsychological, and social functioning is, for logistical and medical-ethical reasons, more challenging to conduct than survey-based research. To advance a more comprehensive understanding of PGD, it would be valuable for future research to focus on these domains and to eventually integrate obtained data into the MARBLES archive.

Preliminary analyses and preliminarily findings

One study has been published based on part of the MARBLES archive, examining latent trajectories of PGD symptoms from the second half year of bereavement until five years post-loss (Pociunaite et al., 2023). Further work is underway, using machine learning to predict PGD symptom severity based on different categories of risk-factors (Xie et al., 2024; in preparation) and using network analyses for a cross-cultural comparison of PG, posttraumatic stress, and depression symptoms networks (Lechner-Meichsner et al., 2025).

The exploratory analyses in the present paper (intended to characterize participant characteristics and levels of PGD and associated mental health symptoms of individuals so far included in the archive) shed some further light on the prevalence of PGD, depression, and bereavement-related PTSD. Mean PGD severity scores and symptom endorsement turned out to be higher in the MARBLES sample compared to those observed in representative samples from e.g. Germany (Rosner et al., 2021), Ireland, and the UK (Hyland et al., 2024). This is not entirely surprising given that selective samples of, e.g. people bereaved by an unnatural cause, are somewhat overrepresented in the MARBLES archive. Findings on the relative prevalence of different symptoms were broadly consistent with prior evidence that yearning is the most common PGD symptom (e.g. Rosner et al., 2021; Treml et al., 2024). Notably, symptoms of depression were more commonly endorsed than those of PTSD, and, for example, suicidal ideation was endorsed by approximately one in eight individuals. Regarding correlates of PGD severity, these preliminary findings align with prior evidence showing higher symptom levels among women, individuals with relatively lower education, those who lost a partner or child, and in cases of death due to unnatural causes (e.g. Buur et al., 2024). The archive offers valuable opportunities to pursue further research into correlates and predictors of PGD, PTSD, and depression symptom scores.

Overcoming challenges in extending the MARBLES archive

While many researchers have so far been positive about sharing their data for the MARBLES archive, we encountered several obstacles in the process of encouraging researchers to share data. Three main barriers mentioned by researchers where that (1) sharing data is possibly not permitted by the local institutional review board, (2) sharing data requires considerable effort for which the capacity is currently lacking, and (3) data might still be utilized for own research. These were also commonly mentioned barriers for sharing data among psychotrauma researchers (Prakash et al., 2023).

First, some researchers are hesitant to share data because they are uncertain whether the obtained ethical approval permits it. Whether authors are allowed to share data with other researchers depends on many factors, varying across countries and institutions. In principle, we encourage researchers to inform participants (prior to data collection) about which data will be shared, with whom, for what purpose, by what means, and for how long. An example of participant information and consent form wording on data sharing is included in Supplementary Material A in Pociūnaitė-Ott and Lenferink (2025). If consent for data sharing has not been obtained, it is often still permissible to share de-identified research data with others. A general rule for effective de-identification is that no combination of data points (e.g. age, cause of death, relationship to the deceased) should create a unique, identifiable profile. For example, clustering age data and time since loss may be one way to reduce the risk that specific individuals may be identifiable based on the data. For further guidance, we refer researchers to their local data protection officer and to other papers part of this special issue (e.g. Kassam-Adams et al., 2025).

Second, some researchers lack time and resources to share data. Funding agencies and academic journals increasingly request researchers to share research data through secured data repositories, resulting in annotating and sharing data to become a more standard research practice. Embracing this movement towards more open science calls for adopting new research practices that costs time and effort, which are sparse. Several calls for action have been published showing ways forward in changing psychotrauma research practices to become more FAIR (for instance see Figure 1 in Kassam-Adams & Olff, 2020; Figure 2 in Prakash et al., 2023; Table 2 in Sadeh et al., 2023). At MARBLES, we have so far been able to offer researchers contributing data some support to prepare their data for inclusion in the archive.

Third, some researchers indicated that they did not want to share data yet, because they first wanted to publish additional research output based on these data. Importantly, when researchers contribute data to MARBLES, this does not refrain them from using these data in any way they want. In principle, datasets from one researcher cannot be used by another unless this has been explicitly agreed upon – for instance, through a preregistration listing all parties as co-authors. Analyses using MARBLES data should always be based on at least two datasets. The latter also addresses concerns some researchers have that, by sharing their data, others might use it to verify or scrutinize previously published work based on that data.7

Contributing to the MARBLES archive

Researchers interested in sharing their data are invited to contact the MARBLES key team (https://www.uu.nl/en/research/the-marbles-project). If the basic conditions for data sharing are met (i.e. the dataset includes a measure of PGD symptoms and was collected in an Institutional Review Board-approved study) the data-sharing process involves several steps. First, contributors are requested to prepare their data by removing all personal identifiers and ensuring that variables have clear values and labels. If contributors have limited resources to do so, members of the MARBLES core team can provide assistance. Second, the MARBLES team harmonizes the variables in the set with those in the archive and specifies, in a data transfer agreement, which data are shared and any conditions attached to their inclusion in the MARBLES archive. Third, the shared dataset is securely stored in Yoda (see Section 2.4 above). Fourth, the dataset is merged into the MARBLES archive. As a further step, we plan to assign a Digital Object Identifier (DOI) to each contributed dataset, including the contributors’ names, which can be cited in subsequent research using the MARBLES archive.

Reusing data from the MARBLES archive

We aim for the MARBLES archive to be FAIR for scientific researchers who have clear and well-considered research questions that ultimately contribute to improving bereavement research and care. Reusing data from the archive consists of four steps. Step 1 includes sharing the request for reusing the archive with the MARBLES key team. The key team checks whether requested data are actually available in the archive. So far, this has been a manual process; as the archive grows, we will add tools and metadata to make its contents more easily searchable for interested users. In Step 2, we ask researchers who want to make use of MARBLES to preregister their planned study (including the hypotheses, methods, and analytic plan) via publicly available websites such as the Open Science Framework or AsPredicted. Templates for preregistration of secondary analyses are available (Mertens & Krypotos, 2019; Van den Akker et al., 2021). Step 3 consists of completing a data request form, available from the MARBLES website. This includes similar information on hypotheses, methods, and analyses of the study plus a detailed overview of which data are requested. Step 4 is taken by the MARBLES team and includes sharing the data with the researchers. When researchers eventually publish a study based on the MARBLES archive, they are requested to cite the current paper as well as the papers from the original studies from which the data were derived (see Supplementary Table 1). In addition, each dataset will be assigned a DOI, and researchers are requested to cite the DOIs of all datasets used in their analyses.

Limitations

There are limitations to the MARBLES project. First, we were able to obtain original questionnaires in nearly all, but not 100% of cases. Therefore, for a small portion of the data archive, we relied entirely on the information provided by the providers and visible in the datasets for variable scoring and labelling, without being able to perform even a minimal check on it. Second, not only does the archive encompass a limited number of variables (as noted above), there are also limitations to the samples that are so far included, that must be considered when using the data for further analyses. For instance, some groups (e.g. non-Western samples, very recently bereaved, people who suffered loss in natural disasters) are underrepresented. As noted, expanding the set to obtain more diversity in sample composition is an important goal. Third, we used an established three-step procedure to harmonize items (O'Neil et al., 2025). Although this procedure allows for optimal use of available data to assess symptoms of (among others) PGD, it does not yield a perfectly consistent representation of symptoms across all datasets. For example, we interpreted items reflecting ‘avoidance of loss-related cues’ as indicators of the ICD-11 symptom ‘denial’ (given that most instruments – discussed above – did not include a measure of ‘denial’); moreover, most instruments did not assess ‘blame,’ resulting in substantial missing data for this symptom. Due to the suboptimal representation of PGD symptoms and the consensus-based selection of items, the validity and reliability of the harmonized data are inevitably more limited than those of data obtained with instruments specifically developed to assess these symptoms. Future work may also use mathematical procedures for analytic harmonization of item scores (cf. Morgan-López et al., 2022).

A fourth somewhat related limitation is that the archive focuses on PGD, in accordance with DSM and ICD criteria. Although there is considerable consensus that these are valid representations of disordered grief, these criteria do not encompass all possible manifestations typically associated with unhealthy and healthy grief. This limits the scope of studies that can be performed with the archive. Another limitation is that the included data are almost entirely based on questionnaires rather than clinical interviews, which likely contributes to an overestimation of symptom scores. A sixth limitation is that the MARBLES archive still has limited metadata and to gain insight into the content, one must for now turn to large files on its website or to the MARBLES core team. While this should not discourage researchers from using the data, we aim to facilitate its use by gradually increasing the availability of metadata. A final important point, which is less a limitation of the MARBLES archive itself than of its utility, is that journals may be increasingly reluctant to accept analyses based on data that are more than, for example, five years old. At present, this applies specifically to JAMA Psychiatry. At the very least, when drawing on older data – such as those in MARBLES – it is essential to specify the years from which the data originate and to justify their use in light of the research objectives (cf. Ketchen et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Notwithstanding these considerations, the development of the MARBLES archive is one of a growing number of initiatives demonstrating that making data related to stressful life events more FAIR is feasible and valuable. To our knowledge, this is the first initiative exclusively focused on PGD. The MARBLES archive offers added value beyond individual datasets by enabling harmonized analyses across large and heterogeneous samples. This facilitates the detection of small-to-moderate effects, the examination of interaction effects, and robust subgroup analyses that are typically underpowered in single-site studies. Importantly, MARBLES enables the study of both relatively rare outcomes and rarely examined, specific subgroups, such as individuals bereaved by uncommon causes of death or those confronted with multiple losses. Moreover, by promoting the re-use of existing high-quality data, MARBLES represents an efficient and responsible use of research resources, maximizing scientific yield while reducing redundancy in data collection. Together, these features provide a strong foundation for the development and validation of prediction models aimed at estimating PGD risk with improved generalizability. Considering PGDs recent inclusion in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, the archive is a valuable and timely resource to promote robust research to enhance our understanding of PGD and associated emotional responses to loss. This, in turn, should ultimately inform improvements in bereavement care – ranging from public health initiatives to highly specialized services.

In several PGD measurement instruments, items were scored on 5-point Likert scales with anchors ranging from 1 = never to 5 = always (e.g., the ICG, ICG-R, and TGI-SR). These analyses were not performed to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the prevalence and correlates of ICD-11-based PGD, PTSD, and depression symptoms. Instead, they were meant to illustrate the potential applications of the archive. Having another researcher reanalyze a dataset – to verify whether the original analyses and results were reported correctly – is an important and valuable element of open science. However, because this could also discourage researchers from sharing their data, we have decided not to allow this within the framework of this project.
