Clinical and Genetic Characteristics of Free Sialic Acid Storage Disorder
Authors: Zoe Wolfenson, Gabriella Grois, Ruth F Hailemeskel, Marla Sabaii, Laryssa A Huryn, Wadih M Zein, Tanya Lehky, Audrey Thurm, Lisa Joseph, Eva H Baker, Gilbert Vezina, Keith Hyland, Laura Pollard, Ellen Macnamara, Andrea Gropman, May C Malicdan, William A Gahl, David R Adams, Lynne Wolfe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFree sialic acid storage disorder (FSASD) is a lysosomal storage disorder that results from biallelic pathogenic variants in the SLC17A5 gene. This gene codes for sialin, a 12‐transmembrane domain protein that exports the charged sugar N‐acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac; sialic acid) out of the lysosome. Dysfunctional sialin causes accumulation of free sialic acid within lysosomes and a range of clinical manifestations, such as intellectual disability, facial dysmorphisms, and increased urinary excretion of free sialic acid. These findings, along with characteristic brain abnormalities on MRI, make the diagnosis of FSASD. Despite recognition of the clinical and imaging phenotype, the natural history of FSASD has not been extensively elucidated. Therefore, we prospectively characterized the clinical, molecular, laboratory, and imaging findings of eight children with FSASD in order to pursue biomarker discovery with collaborators in a consortium of FSASD investigators. Our cohort displayed a high prevalence of ophthalmologic and auditory abnormalities, including myopia, exotropia, and abnormal ABR. Prominent features include impaired CNS myelination, a very thin corpus callosum, documentation of varying levels of intellectual disability, elevated urine, plasma, and CSF free sialic acid levels, and essentially normal endocrine, hematologic, and immunologic parameters. The consistent finding of delayed but progressive myelination suggests that quantitative assessment of myelination by MRI and 1H MRS should be added to the list of potential clinical outcome measures for future clinical trials.Keywords: free sialic acid storage disorder, lysosomal storage disease, rare disease, sialic acid, white matter

Introduction

Free sialic acid storage disorders (FSASD) represent a rare group of lysosomal storage diseases caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in SLC17A5 [1, 2, 3]. This gene codes for sialin, a 12‐transmembrane domain protein that exports the charged sugar, N‐acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac; sialic acid), out of the lysosome [2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Dysfunctional sialin causes accumulation of free sialic acid within lysosomes, resulting in a range of clinical manifestations [9].

Disease severity manifests as a continuous clinical spectrum inversely correlated with the amount of residual sialin activity associated with the SLC17A5 mutation [2, 3, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Three recognized FSASD subtypes [1, 10] include Salla disease [3, 15], intermediate severity FSASD [11, 16], and infantile free sialic acid storage disease (ISSD) [17, 18]. Salla disease is the most attenuated form of FSASD, with onset of clinical findings near the first year of life and a roughly normal life expectancy [3, 15]. Phenotypic characteristics include developmental delay, facial coarsening, mild‐to‐moderate psychomotor delays, truncal ataxia, hypotonia, and spasticity [9]. ISSD is the most severe form, with onset of clinical findings at birth and a life expectancy that does not exceed early childhood [17, 18]. ISSD presents with severe developmental delay, increasingly coarse facial features, moderate‐to‐severe psychomotor delays, hypotonia, spasticity, ataxia, hepatosplenomegaly, cardiomegaly, and failure to thrive [9]. Intermediate severity FSASD is between that of Salla disease and ISSD [11, 16]. Although phenotypes vary greatly from one patient to the next, all FSASD subtypes share the hallmark features of neurodegeneration, cognitive impairment, and hypomyelination evident on brain imaging [1, 11, 14].

The rarity of FSASD and its variable presentations contribute to delayed or missed diagnoses. Biochemical analysis of increased free sialic acid in urine and the molecular confirmation of biallelic SLC17A5 pathogenic variants remain critical for diagnosis [1, 2, 3, 9, 10, 14]. However, the natural history of FSASD remains incompletely understood and is crucial to improving patient care and developing targeted interventions, since there is no approved treatment [19].

To address this gap, we describe a cohort of eight individuals with FSASD, capturing a comprehensive view of their clinical, biochemical, radiologic, and genetic profiles. We seek to contribute to a broader understanding of FSASD, fostering collaborations among clinicians, researchers, and patient advocacy groups to enhance outcomes for affected individuals.

Materials and Methods

Patients

Participants were enrolled in NIH protocol 14‐HG‐0071, “Clinical and Basic Investigations into Known and Suspected Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation” (http://clinicaltrials.gov/ NCT02089789), approved by the National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI) Institutional Review Board. Parents provided written informed consent. Recruitment efforts were supported by the Salla Treatment and Research (STAR) Foundation, a patient advocacy group providing information about FSASD community support, emerging research efforts, and treatment prospects. Eligibility to participate in the protocol required a biochemical and/or molecular diagnosis of FSASD. Enrolled children were admitted to the NIH Clinical Center for a week of comprehensive and multidisciplinary evaluations in 2022–2023. The cohort comprised eight patients, including four females and four males, aged 3–9 years. Participants were from multiple countries, including the United States (N = 4), Norway (N = 2), Dominican Republic (N = 1), and Brazil (N = 1).

Clinical Phenotyping

Clinical evaluations consisted of laboratory studies, imaging, consultations, and sedated procedures. Blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were collected, analyzed, and stored; fibroblasts were cultured from a skin biopsy. Imaging and other procedures included brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H MRS), electromyogram (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCV), awake and sedated eye examinations, optical coherence tomography scans (OCT), behavioral determination of pure tone thresholds, tympanometry, distortion product otoacoustic emissions, auditory brainstem‐evoked potentials (ABR), echocardiogram, electrocardiogram (EKG), routine and overnight electroencephalograms (EEG), abdominal ultrasound, liver elastography, dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA), bone age, and skeletal survey. Consultations included audiology, genetic counseling, neurology, neurodevelopment, nutrition, occupational therapy, ophthalmology, physical therapy, and physiatry.

Genetic Studies

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using standard protocols. To confirm the variants in the SLC17A5 gene, PCR products amplified by specific primers (sequences available upon request) were purified enzymatically using the ExoSAP‐IT Express PCR Product Cleanup Reagent (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 75001.200.UL) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The purified PCR products were subjected to cycle sequencing using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 4337457). The resulting sequencing reactions were purified using the BigDye XTerminator Purification Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 4376485) following the manufacturer's protocol. Capillary electrophoresis was performed using the SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer System (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. A35645). Sequence data were analyzed with Sequencher software (Gene Codes, version 5.4.6, build 46 289).

Sialic Acid Determination

CLIA‐certified biochemical measurement of free and total sialic acid was performed clinically on urine by the Greenwood Genetic Center (Greenwood, SC) via HPLC analysis of 1,2‐di‐amino‐4,5‐methylenedioxybenzene dihydrochloride (DMB) conjugated sialic acid, and on CSF by LabCorp (Burlington, NC). Plasma sialic acid was measured on a research basis using UPLC‐MS/MS.

Case Reports

Patient 1

At 8 months, an MRI revealed markedly delayed myelination, as well as atrophy of the brainstem, cerebrum (including the corpus callosum), and optic nerves. A gastrostomy tube was later placed. Genetic testing revealed compound heterozygous variants in the SLC17A5 gene (Table 1). This child requires full assistance for activities of daily living.

Patient 2

A 3‐year‐8‐month‐old mixed ethnicity male presented with global developmental delay, sialorrhea, spastic diplegia, and a history of bilateral myringotomy tube and gastrojejunostomy tube placement. He was delivered at 37 weeks to nonconsanguineous parents and had an older sister diagnosed with FSASD. Symptoms were initially recognized at 4 months of age, and he was assessed at 7 months of age for abnormal movements and low muscle tone. Head‐dropping episodes started at 5–6 months of age. He was diagnosed with global developmental delay and spasticity at age 15 months. Two emergency department visits at age 14 months resulted in G‐tube placement; another emergency department visit at age 25 months for a spastic right hip resulted in a diagnosis of spastic diplegic cerebral palsy. Developmentally, he required assistance for activities of daily living. Most of the prior testing was unremarkable, except for an EEG revealing a slow background for age.

An extensive NIH evaluation at age 3 years 8 months found mildly elevated liver enzymes. Brain MRI demonstrated atrophy involving all parts of the brain, especially the cerebrum, and markedly delayed myelination. Structural abnormalities of the cerebellum suggested a minor variant on the Dandy‐Walker continuum. The cognitive mental age approximated 1 month.

Patient 3

This 4‐year‐8‐month‐old female from the Dominican Republic presented with global developmental delay, truncal hypotonia with extremity spasticity, bilateral clubbed feet, and moderate hearing loss. The pregnancy was unremarkable until 36 weeks' gestation when abnormally small femurs were recognized on ultrasound. The proband was delivered at 38 weeks with clubbed feet, a spontaneous Babinski reflex, and poor trunk control. Development progressed normally until 6 months when hypotonia was noted. At 7 months, an MRI revealed delayed myelination and a thin corpus callosum. At 12 months, right hemiparesis was recognized and supported by rehabilitation therapy. At approximately 18 months, she was diagnosed with FSASD. Developmentally, this child required full assistance for most activities of daily living and partial assistance for eating. Most prior testing was unremarkable, except for high sialic acid, generalized osteopenia in the lower extremity long bones, and an abdominal sonograph revealing an enlarged liver.

An extensive NIH evaluation at age 4 years 8 months revealed inferior T‐wave abnormalities on ECG, wandering atrial pacemaker at a rate of 100, slow conduction velocities in the right median, fibular, and tibial motor nerves, potential concerns for seizures, mild sleep apnea, and abnormal ABR suggesting potential retro‐cochlear dysfunction. Brain MRI demonstrated a thin corpus callosum and small optic nerves, without widespread brain atrophy; myelination had progressed compared to 7 months but remained markedly delayed. The cognitive mental age was consistent with 10 months.

Patient 4

This 5‐year‐10‐month‐old white female presented with global developmental delay, chronic otitis media, gastroesophageal reflux disease, recurrent nausea and vomiting, hypotonia, spasticity, seasonal eczema, and chronic constipation. She was born without complications at 39 weeks to non‐consanguineous parents. At 6 months, she had poor head control, hypotonia, and lower limb spasticity. At 8 months, she was diagnosed with nystagmus by her ophthalmologist. At 10 months, brain MRI revealed delayed myelination and a suggestion of atrophy, including a thin corpus callosum. At 16 months, she was diagnosed with FSASD, as well as having middle ear effusions and difficulty with lateral tongue movement and speech. Bilateral myringotomies were performed, along with a frenectomy. Prior evaluations had revealed eustachian tube dysfunction. Developmentally, she required assistance for activities of daily living.

An extensive NIH admission at age 5 years 10 months found mild cognitive dysfunction, abnormal clotting factors, elevated anti‐thyroglobulin antibodies, reduced visual acuity, cochlear auditory dysfunction, and mild osteopenia. Brain MRI demonstrated widespread brain atrophy with relative sparing of the cerebellum; myelination had progressed in comparison to 10 months but remained markedly delayed. The cognitive mental age approximated 10 months.

Patient 5

This 6‐year‐old male from Brazil presented with epilepsy, neuro‐psychomotor developmental delay, recurrent otitis media, adenoid hypertrophy, truncal ataxia, global apraxia, myoclonic spasms, and sleep apnea. The pregnancy was unremarkable until 37 weeks when premature rupture of the membranes prompted an urgent cesarean section. The proband's disease course began at 4 months with abnormal lower limb movements and signs of seizures. At 6 months, symptoms progressed to hypotonia and developmental motor delays. At 11 months, he was diagnosed with significant adenoid hypertrophy requiring surgical removal. Developmental milestones were delayed, and he required assistance with activities of daily living. Brain MRI at ages 18, 24 months, and 4 years 5 months demonstrated atrophy that initially involved the cerebrum but later involved all parts of the brain, slightly worsening during that time span. Myelination was markedly delayed but also progressed over that period. EEGs documented epileptiform activity.

An extensive NIH evaluation at age 6 years 1 month revealed abnormal ABRs bilaterally, enlarged spleen, and bilateral coxa valga. Brain MRI showed further progression of atrophy involving all parts of the brain, and further progression of myelination, which remained markedly delayed. The cognitive mental age was approximately 10 months.

Patient 6

A 6‐year, 11‐month‐old white male presented with global developmental delay, dysmorphic features, feeding difficulties, truncal hypotonia, extremity spasticity, and a history of x‐linked ichthyosis. He was born at 36 weeks through an uncomplicated pregnancy with non‐consanguineous parents. There was one sibling, who was unaffected. Developmental delays were noted at 6 months, with abnormal eye movements. Brain MRI was performed at ages 17, 23, and 26 months. These demonstrated a thin corpus callosum and small optic nerves but not brain atrophy. Myelination progressed over this time frame but remained markedly delayed. A lesion in the left superior temporal gyrus, perhaps representing cortical dysplasia or a hamartoma, was unchanged. At 2 years of age, additional symptoms appeared with unsteady gait, mild truncal hypotonia, and global developmental delay. Genetics studies revealed variants in SLC1745 (Table 1). He was admitted to the ED for numerous illnesses. He required assistance with all aspects of daily living.

An extensive NIH evaluation found hyper‐trabeculation of the left ventricle, bone age delayed by 3.4 standard deviations below the mean for 7 years, mild scoliosis and kyphosis. Brain MRI demonstrated new atrophy of the cerebellum, some improvement of the corpus callosum, and no change in the left superior temporal gyrus lesion. Myelination progressed slightly but remained markedly delayed. The cognitive mental age was between 1 and 2 years.

Patient 7

A 7‐year‐1‐month‐old male of Indian ancestry presented with global developmental delay, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), hyperopia with astigmatism, ataxia, eczema, esotropia, and gastro‐esophageal reflux. He was conceived through IVF and born at term. He received phototherapy for 24 h after birth due to jaundice. At 1 year of age, he was delayed in walking and was diagnosed with hypotonia, limb length discrepancy, and heel cord contractures, prompting placement of ankle‐foot orthotics. At 4 years, significant gross motor and language delays were documented, and he was diagnosed with ADHD. Brain MRI at 23 months demonstrated a thin corpus callosum and delayed myelination. Developmentally, the child was independent with respect to activities of daily living and limited walking and speech; he attended regular rehabilitation sessions.

An extensive NIH evaluation found low thyroid stimulating hormone and mild neuropathy with slow conduction velocities. Brain MRI demonstrated slight progression of myelination, although still markedly delayed, and a thin corpus callosum, without generalized brain atrophy. The estimated IQ was 54, corresponding to a cognitive mental age of approximately 4 years.

Patient 8

This 9‐year‐5‐month‐old white female from Norway presented with developmental delays, mild scoliosis, and lower extremity spasticity. She had an uncomplicated term birth to non‐consanguineous parents. At 3 months, concerns began with delays in head control and rolling. Following a skin biopsy and urine evaluation, she was diagnosed with FSASD at 2 years of age and genetic testing confirmed this finding a year later. Brain MRI at 22 months demonstrated mild cerebral atrophy with disproportionately thin corpus callosum and markedly delayed myelination. She required partial assistance with activities of daily living and receives weekly speech therapy.

On NIH evaluation at age 9 years 1 month, she had axonal sensorimotor neuropathy, mild elevation of clotting factors, bilateral optic atrophy, optic nerve head cupping, mild myopic astigmatism, and potential retro‐cochlear dysfunction. Brain MRI demonstrated no change in the atrophy; myelination had distinctly progressed but remained markedly delayed. Adaptive functioning was impaired in all areas, with adaptive skills generally in the 3–4 years old range.

Results

Molecular Findings

Eight individuals with confirmed biallelic pathogenic variants in SLC17A5 were admitted to the NIH Clinical Center. We identified nine variants that is, four substitutions, two nonsense variants, one frameshift, and two splice variants (Table 1). The most common variant, Arg39Cys (c.115C>T; NM_012434), a founder variant originating from the Salla region in Finland, occurred in six patients; two patients were homozygous for this variant. Compound heterozygosity was observed in Patients 2, 3, and 6. Patient 6 carried a complex allele containing c.509T>C and c.511G>A in cis and inherited from the father. It is possible that these alleles in close proximity occurred as a single variant. Most variants were classified as pathogenic based on ACMG guidelines (Table 1).

Clinical Characteristics

Birth and Development

Four of the eight patients were delivered between 36 and 38 weeks' gestation due to premature rupture of membranes, premature labor, or other abnormalities. Two deliveries required C‐section. One patient experienced asphyxia at delivery, requiring a NICU stay and the use of CPAP. Normal birth weight, length, and occipital‐frontal circumference for gestational age were seen in all. Except for one patient with bilateral clubbed feet, significant dysmorphology was not appreciated at birth.

Developmental concerns began between 3 and 10 months, primarily with motor milestone delays in all patients (Table 2). Four patients had delayed ambulation but were walking by their NIH visit; the other four were not yet ambulatory.

Neuroradiology and Spectroscopy

All eight patients underwent brain MRI and 1H MR spectroscopy. On MRI, markedly delayed myelination and a thin corpus callosum were observed in all individuals, and at least some degree of brain atrophy was superimposed in five individuals. The infants had myelination patterns similar to that expected of newborns; in the older children (ages 7–9 years), myelination corresponded to a 4–6 month age. Other abnormalities included a discrete intraparenchymal lesion (possibly cortical dysplasia or hamartoma) in one patient and a minor variant on the Dandy‐Walker continuum in another child. The other three metabolites (myo‐inositol, creatine, and glutamine+glutamate+GABA) were elevated at three of the four locations (left centrum semiovale, midline parietal grey matter, and pons). At all four voxel locations, the FSASD cohort demonstrated that NAA increased with age, and that the increase was roughly in parallel with the NAA increase seen in the reference cohort; increasing NAA during childhood reflects normal maturation.

Neurology

Nerve conduction studies, performed on all eight patients, revealed abnormalities in five, including sensorimotor neuropathies (n = 2) and isolated neuropathies involving the fibular motor nerve (n = 2) and median motor nerve (n = 1). The oldest participant, age 10, had the most severe findings with a combined axonal and demyelinated sensorimotor neuropathy. A second participant with a neuropathy, age 7, had milder findings. The youngest three participants in the group, ages 3–4, had normal findings or minimal abnormalities (Table S10).

Ophthalmology

Awake and sedated ophthalmic examinations, performed on the eight participants, revealed a range of abnormalities. Early onset myopia (n = 5), exotropia (n = 5), end‐gaze nystagmus (n = 2), decreased visual acuity (n = 6), and astigmatism (n = 5) were the most common findings. Mild optic nerve atrophy was observed in two children, while cortical visual impairment was proposed as the cause of reduced visual function in four patients. Less frequent findings included staphyloma, convergence insufficiency, small optic nerve, tilted optic nerves, optic nerve head cupping, and peripapillary atrophy.

Audiology

Audiologic assessments, including tympanometry, otoacoustic emissions and auditory brainstem responses (ABR) were performed on all eight participants. Behavioral hearing thresholds could be established reliably for only four participants and were of limited utility. Tympanometry results were unremarkable for three participants, while others exhibited flat type B tympanograms (n = 2) or negative pressure (n = 2). Abnormal tympanogram results influenced the ability to collect meaningful data from otoacoustic emissions. However, of the patients with normal tympanograms (n = 3), two had unremarkable otoacoustic testing and one had evidence of low to mid frequency peripheral hearing loss. Seven of the eight patients presented with abnormal ABR findings, including abnormal absolute and interpeak latencies indicative of retrocochlear dysfunction.

Cardiorespiratory

Echocardiograms were unremarkable in seven patients, while one patient had mitral valve thickening and regurgitation. One child underwent an extensive cardiology evaluation to determine risk for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy based on EKG and echocardiogram findings. Electrocardiogram results were unremarkable in five children, but three patients had abnormalities of minimal significance, including nonspecific T‐wave abnormality, precordial transition, and T wave flattening inversion with possible right ventricular hypertrophy.

Sleep studies were performed in seven of the eight children and revealed evidence of obstructive sleep apnea (n = 2), periodic limb movement (n = 2), and nocturnal movement consistent with epileptiform waveforms (n = 1). Two individuals were unable to tolerate flow sensors, resulting in incomplete sleep data.

Gastrointestinal

Abdominal ultrasounds were normal for two of the five individuals tested. Abnormal results included one patient each with mild splenomegaly, mild hepatomegaly, and minimally coarsened echogenicity of the liver.

Skeletal

Bone age was delayed in four of the eight subjects tested. Complete skeletal surveys identified abnormalities in six subjects, including minimal/mild scoliosis (n = 3), bilateral hip subluxation (n = 3), coxa valga (n = 4), and diffuse osteopenia (n = 3). Findings noted only once included straightening of lumbar lordosis, bony overgrowth on digits 2–5 of the right foot, erosion of the tip of the distal phalanx on the first toe, and periosteal reaction involving the distal margins of proximal phalanges 2–5 bilaterally.

Laboratory Results

Urine and Plasma Sialic Acid

Free sialic acid in the urine of the children with FSASD ranged from 743 to 2815 nmol/mg creatinine (Table 5); these measurements were consistent with the recognized finding that values are highest just after birth [4]. Overall, free sialic acid levels averaged 2.0 times the upper limit of normal or 3.3 times the middle of the control range. The values put our patients in the category of intermediate severity FSASD [4]. Urinary total sialic acid reflected the elevated free sialic acid levels.

CSF Laboratory Values

CSF was obtained under anesthesia from seven of the eight children with FSASD. Free sialic acid concentrations were mildly elevated in every case; total CSF sialic acid concentrations were generally at the upper limit of normal (Table 6). CSF IgG Index and protein, glucose, and white blood cell counts were essentially normal. CSF amino acids and lactate were normal in all seven patients. Five children had their CSF examined for oligoclonal bands; there were none in any patient. Given the normal WBC counts, protein, IgG index, oligoclonal bands, and neopterin levels, we could not identify signs of neuroinflammation in this cohort. CSF neurotransmitters, methyltetrahydrofolate, pterins, amino acids, and lactate were assessed in seven of the eight patients and no significant abnormalities were identified (Table S11).

Endocrine Laboratory Values

Endocrine studies were essentially normal (Table S1) except that Patient 2 had a PTH of 118.6 pg/mL (control range 15–65) and Patient 3 had a TSH of 11.74 mcIU/mL (control range 0.70–4.17).

Hematologic Laboratory Values

Comprehensive hematologic evaluation did not identify anemia, clotting, or bleeding abnormalities (Tables S2, S3, and S4). There were no significant deficiencies in CBC values, coagulation factors, clotting times, or fibrinogen, ferritin, or transferrin levels. Iron levels were normal except that Patient 3 had an iron concentration of 30 mcg/dL (control range 50–120) and an iron saturation of 8% (control range 20–50). Lupus anticoagulant was negative in the seven patients in whom it was measured (data not shown).

Protein Glycosylation Results

N‐linked glycosylation was assessed by measuring the sialylation of transferrin; an increase in the amount of hyposialylated forms is consistent with a Congenital Disorder of Glycosylation (CDG). All eight patients had normal amounts of sialylated transferrin isoforms (Table S5). O‐linked glycosylation was assessed by measuring the sialylation status of apolipoprotein CIII, which was normal in all eight patients (Table S5).

Immunology/Inflammation

Comprehensive evaluations of immune system function revealed normal quantitative immunoglobulins, vaccination titers and EBV & CMV exposures. Immunoglobulins G, A, and M were normal or only mildly elevated (Table S6). Immune responses to selected antigens are shown in Table S7. In general, antibody production to Haemophilus influenza and rubella was protective, while the response to naturally occurring viruses (e.g., EBV and CMV) was variable. Anti‐pneumococcal antibodies to 22 serotypes were variably present in seven of the eight children, but Patient 5 had values below the reference range for all subtypes (data not shown). Only Patient 3 had anti‐meningococcal IgG titers (serogroups A, C, Y, and W‐135) within the reference range (data not shown).

Systemic markers of inflammation (ESR, CRP, C3, and C4) and autoimmunity (ANA) were normal (Table S8). Patient #1 had a slightly elevated ESR, a significantly elevated CRP, and a slightly elevated ANA (data not shown). Rheumatoid factor levels were normal in all eight patients. Lymphocyte phenotyping, performed on six patients, revealed non‐specific variability that was not clinically significant (data not shown).

Other Laboratory Values (Data Not Shown)

Routine biochemical assessments, including serum electrolytes, glucose, creatinine, ammonia, liver function studies, lipid panel results, albumin, calcium, phosphorus, total protein, creatine kinase, amylase and lipase were essentially normal in all eight children (data not shown). In four of the eight patients, serum magnesium was between 1.8 and 2.0 mg/dL (normal range, 2.1–2.8). Serum homocysteine was normal in all eight patients, ammonia concentrations ranged between 19 and 38 mM (control, 18–72), and methylmalonic acid was normal except in patient 1, whose concentration was 0.9 nM (normal < 0.4). Free carnitine averaged 35 nM (normal range 22–66) and was normal in all patients. Acylcarnitines averaged 8 nM (normal range 4–28) and were normal except in Patient 1, whose concentration was 3 nM. Plasma amino acid concentrations were essentially normal in all seven of the patients tested and urine amino acid concentrations were normal in all six of the patients tested (data not shown). One patient had non‐specific excretion of some urine amino acids in a non‐fasting sample. Vitamin A, Vitamin E, and 25‐hydroxy Vitamin D levels were at or above the normal range for all 8 children.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of eight patients' age 3.1–9.5 years with Free Sialic Acid Storage Disorders (FSASD) and confirmed biallelic pathogenic variants in SLC17A5. Detailed clinical, biochemical, and genetic evaluations expanded our understanding of early FSASD phenotypes and pathophysiology and identified diagnostic and management challenges.

Genetic analysis revealed considerable allelic heterogeneity within this small FSASD cohort, with nine distinct pathogenic variants identified. The p.Arg39Cys variant, associated with Salla disease, accounted for 6 of the 16 alleles detected. This Finnish founder‐effect variant is responsible for the Salla disease phenotype. Our cohort illustrates that additional disease‐associated variants exist in the broader, non‐Finnish population. The clinical heterogeneity in our cohort suggests that larger and longer studies will be required to understand both global FSASD allele prevalence and allele severity patterns for genotype–phenotype correlation studies.

Consistent with prior reports, early developmental delay and intellectual disability were universal in our cohort, with variable severity. Delayed myelination was seen on brain MRI in all patients, and atrophy was observed in the majority of patients (Figure 1). A very thin corpus callosum was a universal and striking finding in all patients. These brain abnormalities likely contribute to the significant cognitive and psychomotor impairments observed in FSASD and highlight the utility of brain MRI in early diagnosis.

Although our cohort was small, a total of 19 brain MRI exams were performed. These exams consistently showed severely delayed myelination. However, they also showed that myelination of structures occurred in the expected order, with normal‐appearing myelin laid down and without discrete lesions to indicate demyelination. Myelination progressed within individuals (where follow‐up images were available) and across the group (when images were ordered by patient age). The 1H MRS findings, especially the low choline in white matter, argue against demyelination or inflammation contributing to the observed neurological impairments and are also consistent with delayed myelination.

Two conclusions derive from the MRI imaging and MR spectroscopy results. First, FSASD needs to be included in the differential diagnosis for CNS hypomyelination in children with developmental delay, including pediatric leukodystrophies and CNS hypomyelination syndromes. Second, the consistent finding of progressing myelination suggests that quantitative assessment of myelination by MRI and 1H MRS should be added to the list of potential clinical outcome measures for future clinical trials.

While there did not appear to be a specific early developmental milestone finding, significant motor delays in the first year of life in the context of global developmental delays should be an indication for genetic testing. With only half of the eight children having acquired ambulation, and some not able to sit up independently, motor delays are noteworthy for general severity. In addition to variable but very early onset motor, language, and cognitive delays that require longitudinal evaluation for indications of stagnation and/or regression, our cohort also displayed a high prevalence of ophthalmologic and auditory abnormalities, including myopia, exotropia, and abnormal ABR. These findings suggest that routine ophthalmologic and audiologic evaluations should be integral to FSASD care to address sensory deficits and improve quality of life. Furthermore, the identification of peripheral neuropathies, as well as splenomegaly and hepatomegaly in the older patients, may be useful in identifying optimal timing of future treatments.

Biochemical analyses confirmed elevated free sialic acid levels in urine, plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), consistent with the hallmark lysosomal storage defect of FSASD. The elevated urinary free sialic acid levels, ranging from 743 to 2815 nmol/mg creatinine with the highest levels observed in younger patients (Table 5), highlight the diagnostic utility of urinary free sialic acid measurements. Nevertheless, free urinary sialic acid may be fully normal or only slightly increased in some patients [20].

CSF free sialic acid levels were also elevated and may provide a biomarker to differentiate FSASD from other disorders of sialic acid metabolism [13]. Plasma levels are also increased but show variability. These findings emphasize that while urinary sialic acid testing remains the main diagnostic tool, plasma and CSF free sialic acid measurements may provide additional insights into disease burden.

Several laboratory evaluations, including hematologic, immunologic, and endocrine studies, yielded minimal or no abnormalities; these may be useful for determining general health status and safety but can probably be discarded as potential therapeutic outcome measures.

Limitations

Despite the depth of our characterizations and analyses, this study is limited by its small sample size and the young age of the participants. Nevertheless, the results identified several quantifiable outcome measures for therapeutic intervention in young FSASD patients that is, the myelination status, 1H MRS measurable metabolites, and neuropsychiatric parameters such as Vineland assessments and cognitive testing; organomegaly, peripheral neuropathy, and progression of white matter disease can be followed later in life. Additional longitudinal studies are essential to understand the natural history of FSASD, including the timing and trajectory of neurodegeneration, sensory deficits, and systemic complications.

Conclusions

This study highlights the spectrum of clinical and biochemical manifestations of FSASD in childhood, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis, comprehensive phenotyping, and multidisciplinary care. By expanding our understanding of this rare disorder, we lay the groundwork for improved diagnostic tools, personalized management strategies, and the development of targeted therapies. Collaboration across clinical, research, and advocacy communities will be essential to translate these findings into meaningful outcomes for patients and families.
