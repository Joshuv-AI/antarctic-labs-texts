Cost‐Effectiveness of Venetoclax‐Based Treatment in Treatment‐Naïve Fit Patients With CLL Without TP53 Aberrations
Authors: Lars Holger Ehlers, Mark‐David Levin, Marjolein van der Klift, Morten Berg Jensen, Hoa Thi Tuyet Tran, Arnon P Kater, Carsten Utoft Niemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe GAIA‐CLL13 trial showed that venetoclax‐obinutuzumab (Ven‐O) based regimens, with or without ibrutinib, offer superior efficacy compared to chemoimmunotherapy (CIT) with regards to progression free survival (PFS) in fit, treatment‐naïve (TN) CLL patients. However, their higher costs warrant a cost‐effectiveness evaluation. This study assessed the cost‐effectiveness of Ven‐O versus venetoclax with rituximab (Ven‐R), venetoclax‐obinutuzumab‐ibrutinib (Ven‐O‐I), and CIT in fit, TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations across the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark. A state‐transition Markov model including later line treatment was applied to estimate costs, life years (LYs), and quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) over a 38‐year horizon. Results were sensitive to long‐term OS assumptions. In the base‐case analysis, extrapolating OS and PFS for each treatment separately, all venetoclax‐based treatments appeared cost‐effective compared to CIT in all three countries. ICERs for Ven‐R, Ven‐O, and Ven‐O‐I versus CIT were €35 840, €32 513, €30 331 for the Netherlands, €39 881, €38 099, €26 381 for Norway, and €34 010, €37 804, €33 215 for Denmark. In the sensitivity analyses, however, cost‐effectiveness was lost when only allowing separate OS and PFS extrapolations for statistically significant differences at 60‐month follow‐up. Furthermore, cost‐effectiveness results were sensitive to varying assumptions about national willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) thresholds, IGHV‐status, and time horizon. In conclusion, venetoclax‐based treatments may be considered a cost‐effective treatment option for fit, TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations in the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark, but the pricing process for targeted agents should take the uncertainty about cost‐effectiveness into account when negotiating pricing of medication. Longer follow‐up data is needed to address the uncertainties.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is the most prevalent leukemia in Western countries, predominantly affecting older populations [1]. The incidence rises with age, resulting in increased incidence and prevalence due to the demographic shift toward aging societies [2]. Despite significant advances in therapeutic options, the management of CLL remains a critical clinical and economic challenge [3]. Current treatment strategies are tailored based on patient fitness, genetic markers, and disease characteristics. In line with international clinical guidelines and national treatment guidelines for treatment‐naïve (TN) patients without del(17p)/TP53 mutation, targeted therapy with a BCL‐2 inhibitor‐based therapy in combination with an anti‐CD20 antibody such as venetoclax plus obinutuzumab (Ven‐O) or in combination with a BTK inhibitor (BTKinh) such as ibrutinib plus venetoclax with an alternative in terms of BTKinhs until progression or toxicity. For fit patients with IGHV‐mutated CLL and without TP53 aberrations, chemoimmunotherapy (CIT) including fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab (FCR) or bendamustine and rituximab (BR) remains an alternative treatment option to the targeted approaches [4]. These approaches offer distinct efficacy and safety profiles, which must also be carefully weighed against their financial implications. Today, clinical practices vary between the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark [5, 6, 7].

The phase 3, open label GAIA‐CLL13 randomized controlled trial (RCT) demonstrated the superior efficacy of venetoclax‐based regimens, including Ven‐O and venetoclax plus obinutuzumab and ibrutinib (Ven‐O‐I) but not venetoclax plus rituximab (Ven‐R), as compared to CIT in fit TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations [8]. These findings highlight the potential of targeted therapies to improve progression‐free survival (PFS) with similar or less toxicity. However, the introduction of targeted therapies has been associated with substantially higher treatment costs, necessitating health economic evaluations to ensure value‐based decision‐making. A decision‐analytic model incorporating modern survival analysis methods provides the most robust assessment of the expected value for money, supporting evidence‐based decision‐making and resource prioritization [9, 10]. In particular, subgroup analyses, such as those stratified by immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region (IGHV) mutation status, may provide critical insights into health economic evaluations for specific patient populations [2].

To date, no published studies have comprehensively evaluated the cost‐effectiveness of Ven‐O, Ven‐R, or Ven‐O‐I, relative to CIT in fit TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations, stratified by IGHV‐mutation status. Furthermore, there is limited evidence on the cost‐effectiveness of these therapies across diverse healthcare systems, such as those in the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark.

The objective of this study is therefore to assess the cost‐effectiveness of Ven‐O, Ven‐R, or Ven‐O‐I compared to CIT for fit TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations in these three countries. Using quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) as the primary measure of effect, this analysis provides critical data to inform clinical guidelines and policy decisions.

Methods

Decision Analytic Modelling

A state‐transition Markov model was constructed to estimate the costs, life years (LYs), and QALYs associated with the use of Ven‐O, Ven‐R, Ven‐O‐I, and CIT as first‐line treatments for fit TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations. The decision analytic modelling followed international guidelines for health economic evaluation [9, 11, 12, 13] using a similar model structure and methodological approach as Slot et al. 2023 [14] (Supporting Information S1). A limited societal perspective with all costs expressed in 2024 euros (€) was applied in all analyses [15, 16, 17]. The primary outputs of the models—total QALYs, LYs, and costs—were used to calculate incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios (ICERs), expressing the lifetime additional cost per QALY gained for Ven‐O, Ven‐R, or Ven‐O‐I compared to CIT.

The model simulates a hypothetical cohort of fit TN CLL patients to project long‐term disease progression and outcomes [9]. To predict disease evolution following first‐line treatment, patient pathways were modeled through mutually exclusive health states: treatment‐free after first‐line therapy, second‐line treatment (Ven‐R or ibrutinib), and death. Disease progression was assumed to transition patients directly to second‐line treatment. Tunnel states were incorporated for second‐line treatment to reflect the influence of patient history on transition probabilities, thereby addressing the memoryless property of standard Markov models [9]. To estimate the differences in costs and consequences between the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark, a separate version of the model was constructed for each country, and for each country, separate analyses were performed for all fit TN CLL patients, patients with IGHV‐unmutated CLL, and patients with IGHV‐mutated CLL, respectively. The simulation was conducted in 28‐day cycles over a 38‐year horizon, after which all patients were assumed to have died. Half‐cycle corrections were applied. The state‐transition model was developed using TreeAge Pro Healthcare software (version 2023, R2.0).

The hypothetical cohort mirrored the trial population of GAIA‐CLL13, with a median age of 61 years, cumulative illness rating score (CIRS) < 6, while being restricted to the subpopulation without TP53 aberrations (Supplementary S10) [8].

Costs were estimated separately for each country following principles according to national health technology assessment guidelines [11, 12, 13, 38]. Data for resources and unit costs were derived from a combination of sources, including the national current medicinal product (list) prices, national diagnosis‐related group (DRG) tariffs, laboratory databases, and published literature (Supplementary S3–S5).

The outcomes were discounted using country‐specific annual rates: 3% for costs and 1.5% for effects in the Netherlands [11], 4% for Norway [12], and 3.5% for Denmark until year 35, then 2.5% [22], in accordance with each country's national guidelines for health economic evaluation [11, 12, 13, 38].

For the Netherlands, we assumed a willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) threshold range of €20 000–€50 000 per QALY [18], and for Norway, a WTP range of 500 000–800 000 NOK per QALY [12] (€42 000–€67 000) (converted using an exchange rate of 11.8754 NOK/€ on December 20, 2024) [11, 12]. Thus, in these two countries, WTP depends on disease severity, and uncertainty in estimating this severity can lead to the same treatment being evaluated against either end of the range with potential implications for cost‐effectiveness [11, 12]. In the base‐case analysis, the higher WTP threshold for each of these countries was used as the reference value reflecting the threshold we consider most likely to be applied by the national HTA bodies for CLL patients. For Denmark, no official WTP threshold exists, and we applied the WHO recommended methods of estimating WTP from national gross domestic product (GDP) per capita [19, 20] approximating €66 000 per QALY (converted using an exchange rate of 7.46 DKK/€ on December 20, 2024).

For the estimation of drug costs, we applied the assumption of an average body surface area of 1.79 m2 for CIT and rituximab dosing [39]. For hospital‐related costs, it was assumed that patients would have regular visits during treatment, including physician consultations, telephone contacts, nurse visits, and blood tests (Supplementary S6). The time required for these activities was estimated per 28‐day treatment cycle, based on annual salaries for physicians, nurses, and laboratory technicians. Costs for blood tests were obtained from laboratory databases specific to each country. Patient time and transportation expenses were included in accordance with national guidelines and cost estimates. Additionally, a computed tomography scan of the neck, chest, and abdomen was assumed prior to initiating second‐line treatment. End‐of‐life costs were not included in the analysis (Supplementary S5,S6).

Costs and disutility were included for all grade 3 and 4 AEs if the absolute difference between treatment groups exceeded 2 percentage points. For these AEs, costs were based on grade 3–4 events, while disutility was based on grade 1–4 AEs [8]. Since the clinical trials did not stratify AEs by genetic risk factors, it was assumed that the reported AEs were representative of the fit CLL patient independent of IGHV‐mutational status (Supplementary S7).

Quality‐of‐life (QoL) weights for CLL‐specific health states were sourced from published literature. The progression state was assumed to apply to all health states after the first progression. For AEs, a QoL decrement was subtracted from the health state utility value for the duration of the AE (Supplementary S8,S9).

Finally, as part of the validation process, all clinical CLL experts reviewed the model assumptions about costs, QoL AEs, and commented on the structural design of the economic model.

Survival Analysis

To ensure an accurate estimation of overall survival (OS) and PFS beyond the duration of the trial, survival analysis modelling was applied using National Institute for Health and Care Excellence's (NICE) recommended methods [10]. A range of parametric and flexible parametric methods were tested, with model selection guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) measures to determine the best fit for the observed trial period (Supplementary S11,S12). Fitted and extrapolated functions were plotted, compared to background population data, and visually inspected by all authors to ensure plausibility. Flexible parametric survival (FPR) models were chosen for OS, while flexible parametric (FP) models were used for PFS (Figure 1). These generally led to more conservative extrapolation curves than other parametric methods (see Supplementary S11). FPR models incorporate background mortality by combining expected population hazards with excess disease‐related hazard [10]. Second‐line treatments were included based on published evidence on OS and PFS, with data from Aarup et al. (2020) [40] informing the use of ibrutinib as a second‐line treatment and the MURANO study (2020) [41] supporting Ven‐R as a second‐line option. Extrapolation of OS and PFS for these treatments followed the same methods as those applied to first‐line therapy. The same OS function was used in the model regardless of IGHV status due to the absence of published literature. All survival analysis was done in Stata (version SE/18.0).

Sensitivity Analyses

The impact of the assumptions about long‐term overall and progression free survival benefits in the model was investigated through scenario‐analyses, (which is a type of sensitivity analysis, where multiple assumptions are changed simultaneously). As base‐case analysis, the absolute observed OS and PFS data from the GAIA‐CLL13 phase III RCT was applied for the first 60‐months [8]. All extrapolated OS and PFS functions were incorporated for simulations beyond 60 months (Supplementary S11–S13). As scenario analysis 1, the OS function for CIT was applied for all treatment arms. This scenario was made to investigate the impact on the ICERs from eliminating the assumption of first line OS differences. As a scenario analysis 2, the Ven‐O extrapolation of OS was applied across all venetoclax‐based treatments. Regarding PFS, the extrapolation from the CIT arm was applied to both CIT and Ven‐R, whereas the Ven‐O extrapolation was used for both Ven‐O and Ven‐O‐I. This scenario was made because the 4‐year follow‐up OS and PFS data from the GAIA‐CLL13 phase III RCT could be interpreted to reveal such a pattern [8] (Supplementary S12). Furthermore, a scenario analysis 3 with similar assumptions as scenario 2 was made, but where the OS for CIT was assumed for all treatment arms. This scenario corresponds to only including statistically significant differences at 60‐month follow‐up.

Further scenario/sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of different sets of assumptions on the cost‐effectiveness results, including (a) narrowing the time horizon to 10 years, (b) incorporating end‐of‐life costs, (c) using a 48‐month time horizon for OS and PFS extrapolation instead of the base‐case of 60 months, and (d) delayed initiation (12 month) of second‐line therapy.

Probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) with 10 000 s‐order Monte Carlo simulations were conducted assigning beta distributions to transition probabilities, utility and disutility values, while gamma distributions were applied to costs. Where available, the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to reflect uncertainty. In cases where CIs were unavailable, ±20% of the mean values were applied. Additionally, one‐way deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSA) were performed on all variables to test the robustness of the results and explore the impact of individual parameter variations (data not shown).

Results

Base‐Case Scenario Cost‐Effectiveness Analysis

In the base‐case scenario, all venetoclax‐based treatments appeared cost‐effective compared to CIT in all three countries (Figure 2 and Supplementary S14). Estimated ICERs for Ven‐R, Ven‐O, and Ven‐O‐I versus CIT were €35 840, €32 513, €30 331 for the Netherlands, €39 881, €38 099, €30 685 for Norway, and €34 010, €37 804, €33 215 for Denmark (Table 2). Although ICERs were similar for Ven‐O, Ven‐O‐I, and Ven‐R, indicating that they all provide value for money, LYs and QALYs gained were different between treatment arms. Estimated QALYs for CIT, Ven‐R, Ven‐O, and Ven‐O‐I were 5.97, 7.61, 8.17, and 9.46, and LY 8.08, 10.49, 11.16, and 12.75. Results were sensitive to time horizon showing that a long‐term perspective was needed to demonstrate cost‐effectiveness (Supplementary S15).

Alternative Scenario Cost‐Effectiveness Analyses

In scenario analysis 1, assuming similar OS, only Ven‐O‐I remained cost‐effective compared to CIT (Table 2). Results from scenario analysis 2 were similar to base‐case. In scenario 3, where we assumed no OS differences between all treatments and only included the PFS differences that were statistically significant at 60‐month follow‐up, CIT was cost‐effective. Thus, cost‐effectiveness of venetoclax‐based treatment was lost under conservative survival assumptions. ICERs in this scenario were estimated for Ven‐O and Ven‐O‐I versus CIT to €134 825 and €197 619 for the Netherlands, €134 958 and €182 332 for Norway, and €143 908 and €205 836 for Denmark (Table 2). Ven‐R was dominated by CIT (i.e., same effectiveness but more expensive). Key assumptions driving cost‐effectiveness results are summarized in Supplementary S15.

For the IGHV‐unmutated population, Ven‐O‐I was cost‐effective in the base‐case and scenario 1, Ven‐O in scenario 2, and CIT in scenario 3 (assuming only statistically significant PFS differences and the same OS extrapolations for all four treatment arms, Supplementary S14). For the IGHV mutated patients, Ven‐O was consistently cost‐effective due to a high number of QALYs gained over a lifetime. Late initiation of second‐line therapy was associated with cost‐savings in all treatment regimes, thus only affecting the ICERs marginally (Supplementary S15).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the cost‐effectiveness of first‐line Ven‐O, Ven‐R, Ven‐O‐I, and CIT for fit CLL patients without TP53 aberrations, while also including the costs and effects of second‐line treatments. Furthermore, this study provided a cross‐country comparison of cost‐effectiveness in the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark, offering insight into how results may vary across different healthcare systems.

Our base‐case analysis suggests that venetoclax‐based treatments may be considered cost‐effective compared to CIT in all three countries for fit TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations; however, this result is sensitive to assumptions about the extrapolation of OS and PFS. In this base‐case analysis, we have followed international health economic guidelines on extrapolating, evaluating and selecting models, choosing cut points, and integrating external data, which include extrapolating also statistically non‐significant differences in OS and PFS [10, 11, 12, 13]. Some studies indicate long‐term survival for CLL patients may approach survival curves for the background population [42], however, other studies indicate this may not be the case, among other things due to infections [43]. In health economic evaluation, survival analysis modeling is required to estimate lifetime benefits and costs [11, 12, 13], yet, survival analysis modeling remains an area of uncertainty and controversy with ongoing methodological advancements [10]. Therefore, we have conducted different scenario analyses to address this uncertainty. Health economists recommend extrapolation of each treatment arm separately to reflect potential treatment effects that may manifest beyond the trial horizon, accommodate non‐proportional hazards, and enable lifetime cost‐effectiveness assessments [10, 44, 45, 46]. This approach allows modeling of clinically plausible delayed benefits but also carries the risk of overestimating treatment effects if not sufficiently justified or validated. In essence, this may entail extrapolating unproven OS benefits. Conversely, some clinical experts may argue against extrapolation of treatment effects that have not been demonstrated with statistical significance in a clinical trial to avoid bias from assumptions unsupported by trial data. To add the approach as a scenario analysis is a way of handling the uncertainty and analyzing what the consequences are, and it aligns closely with traditional evidence hierarchies and clinical reasoning among health professions [47]. However, it may underestimate the true value of novel therapies, particularly in hematologic malignancies where small or immature sample sizes often preclude statistical significance despite clinically meaningful trends. Furthermore, it may ignore emerging evidence suggesting that survival benefits can accrue over extended periods.

A limitation to the study is that we did not have access to separate survival data reflecting IGHV‐mutational status. This may bias the results on the importance of IGHV‐status. Another key limitation concerns central model assumptions, particularly those related to economic factors, including confidential pricing agreements and WTP thresholds. We believe, CLL will be classified as a moderate/serious disease in the Netherlands and in Norway corresponding to high WTP thresholds, but we have shown lower WTP thresholds in graphs as well [12, 18, 19, 20, 21]. To address these uncertainties, we have tried to be conservative in all model assumptions about clinical benefits and costs. Especially, a limitation pertains to the inclusion of only severe AE's from treatment. In our calculations, only acute grade 3–4 AE's during treatment are included. This approach likely underestimates the long‐term toxicity burden of CIT, potentially biasing results against venetoclax‐based treatments. Conversely, late toxicities of novel agents are also uncertain. Consequently, this aspect of the analysis represents a strong conservative approach as potential quality‐of‐life deterioration from long‐term side‐effects has not been included. Thus, the model may not fully capture the full effect of toxicity the first year [48]. Additionally, this analysis does not account for productivity gain from improvement upon treatments. Given that venetoclax‐based treatments may be less burdensome and associated with fewer long‐term AE's, there could potentially be a societal gain from venetoclax‐based treatments compared to CIT, particularly for patients of working age who remain in the labor force. Productivity loss is not included in Danish and Norwegian guidelines for health economic evaluations, however, accepted in Dutch guidelines [11, 12, 13, 18]. Finally, a model‐related limitation is the uncertainty regarding the time interval for initiating second‐line treatment. The model assumes immediate initiation following disease progression, whereas, in clinical practice, the delay may extend up to six months or even a year. This assumption was made as a simplification of the model.

The main strength of this analysis is the use of robust data from the GAIA‐CLL13 trial, complemented by validation sources for second‐line treatment data [8, 40, 41]. This ensures that the model is based on high‐quality, clinically relevant evidence. Additionally, the inclusion of three European countries—the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark—enhances the generalizability of the findings across different healthcare systems and health economic guidelines. Despite differences in health care systems, price levels, and WTP thresholds between the three countries, we find similar economic results. Thus, these differences in health care systems and costs don't seem to explain the observed differences in current clinical guidelines between the three countries. Furthermore, our analysis underscores the importance of subgroup‐specific evaluations, as our results revealed potential differences in cost‐effectiveness depending on IGHV‐mutational status. Surprisingly, we found Ven‐O consistently more economically favorable in mutated patients. This finding is driven by extrapolation rather than observed trial differences. It can mainly be explained by more QALY gain from longer survival driven by lower disease progression; however, as seen from comparison of the survival curves from the trial with up to 5 years follow up [8] as compared to the extrapolated PFS curves (Supplementary S13), this gain is based on long‐term extrapolations with high uncertainty. These findings highlight the necessity of stratifying analyses by IGHV‐status to ensure that treatment recommendations align with the specific characteristics of each patient subgroup. By incorporating this level of detail, we can provide more precise guidance on treatment strategies, which is particularly valuable for optimizing outcomes in both IGHV‐mutated and unmutated populations. Further health economic research on the impact of IGHV‐mutational status on cost‐effectiveness is needed when more information on survival differences is available.

To date, no health economic analyses have specifically examined fit, TN patients with CLL without TP53 aberrations. However, similar studies on related populations, such as Slot et al. (2023) and Chatterjee et al. (2023) [14, 49], have found that Ven‐O is a cost‐effective treatment for TN, unfit CLL patients without TP53 aberrations compared to CIT. These findings align well with the results of the present study, supporting the robustness of economic evaluations across the patient populations.

The implications of our study suggest that targeted therapies, such as Ven‐O, are more effective and may be considered cost‐effective, however with major uncertainties due to the long‐term extrapolations needed of OS and PFS. This has already been leading to an increasing preference for targeted treatments in clinical guidelines [1, 50], Health economic evaluations provide useful insights into the value for money of new interventions by assessing their expected long‐term economic, clinical, and quality‐of‐life outcomes for the average patient. These findings are important for guiding resource allocation and informing decision‐making in healthcare systems with finite budgets. In this study, results suggest that pricing and reimbursement negotiations at the national level should carefully consider the uncertainty of cost‐effectiveness. Given the uncertainty in cost‐effectiveness, price negotiations and/or flexibility in pricing agreements such as outcome‐based agreements or indication‐specific pricing may be necessary to ensure value‐based access across different healthcare systems. Moving forward, further research is needed to refine our understanding of long‐term cost‐effectiveness of targeted treatment regimens across different subpopulations and settings.

Conclusion

Venetoclax‐based treatments may be considered cost‐effective compared to CIT for fit TN CLL patients without TP53 aberrations in the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark; however, uncertainty about cost‐effectiveness should be taken into account when negotiating prices and reimbursement at the national level. Longer follow‐up data from real world settings are needed to address the uncertainty.
