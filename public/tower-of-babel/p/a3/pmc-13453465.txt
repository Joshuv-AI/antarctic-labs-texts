Proteomics-based clustering for higher risk of venous thromboembolism in patients with nephrotic syndrome
Authors: Xin Liu, Jing Ma, Yanshuang Lyu, Xiaomeng Zhang, Wanlu Song, Peiran Yang, Zhenguo Zhai, Guohui Fan, Dingyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNephrotic syndrome (NS) carries a high and heterogeneous risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE). Hypercoagulability has been considered as key factors, but in-depth molecular mechanisms remain unrevealed.ObjectivesWe aimed to identify patients with NS who are under higher risk for VTE and explore key pathways and molecules using proteomic profiling.MethodsSerum proteomic data from 49 patients with NS were analyzed via unsupervised consensus clustering (top 1000 variable proteins, K = 2) and gene set variation analysis. Differentially expressed proteins were identified, and a protein–protein interaction network was constructed to pinpoint hub proteins. The levels of hub proteins were compared between the clusters, and their correlation with clinical biomarkers of renal function were analyzed. The cumulative incidence of VTE over 2 years was the primary end point. Trajectory analysis was performed to reveal the dynamic changes of renal function of each cluster.ResultsUnsupervised clustering stratified 2 clusters. Cluster 2 had numerically higher prevalence of both baseline and cumulative VTE than in cluster 1 (43% vs 11%; P = 0.13 for baseline VTE; and 58% vs 22%; P = .074 for cumulative VTE) and had enriched activity in extracellular matrix organization and cell–cell interaction pathways. Differentially expressed protein analysis found 145 upregulated proteins in cluster 2, and protein–protein interaction network analysis identified 9 candidate hub proteins (CTNNB1, VCAN, POSTN, SDC1, MMP2, SERPINC1, APOA1, PCSK9, and LIPC), all significantly elevated in cluster 2, and MMP2 and POSTN were associated with markers of tubular injury.ConclusionSerum proteomics analysis identified a group of patients with NS who were potentially at higher risk of developing VTE. Extracellular matrix and endothelial glycocalyx damage might play an important role in VTE development among patients with NS.Keywords: nephrotic syndrome, embolism, endothelial barrier

Introduction

Nephrotic syndrome (NS) is a clinical syndrome caused by various primary or secondary renal diseases, characterized by massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema, and hyperlipidemia [1,2]. Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is one of the most common and severe complications of NS, as pulmonary thromboembolism is potentially life threatening; thus, prophylactic anticoagulation is recommended in NS management [1]. The overall incidence of VTE was ∼7% to 37% in patients with NS [3], indicating a high heterogeneity in VTE risk between different clinical conditions, including pathological manifestations. Moreover, anticoagulation would also increase the risk of bleeding, as those patients are often required to have renal biopsy for etiological diagnosis. Guidelines have been recommending serum albumin level as the major indicator of VTE prophylaxis in patients with NS [4], but recent evidence and prospective highlighted the need for more precise identification among patients with NS who have particularly higher risk of VTE [[5], [6], [7]].

Hypercoagulable state and endothelial injury are key elements of the Virchow triad of VTE pathogenesis [8]. Imbalance of the coagulation and fibrinolytic proteins that caused by the breakdown of the permselectivity barrier of the glomeruli capillary wall in the kidney has been acknowledged as a major mechanism of VTE in NS, together with platelet hyperactivation and hypercholesterolemia, creating a hypercoagulable state that promotes thrombus formation [3,9]. Recently, markers of endothelial injury, including von-Willebrand factor (VWF), circulation endothelial cells, and soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule-1, and markers of endothelial glycocalyx degradation have been revealed elevated in pediatric patients with NS, patients with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and patients with minimal change diseases [[10], [11], [12]]. Although the existing evidence emphasizes a comprehensive underlying cause of VTE in NS, more in-depth molecular mechanism and the connection between hypercoagulable state, endothelial injury, and other unrevealed pathways have not been clearly investigated.

Clustering of proteomics has been used to identify potential disease phenotypes, providing substantial clues on the association between distinct clinical features and outcomes [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. It has been conducted in kidney diseases such as pediatric NS and chronic kidney disease, for precise diagnosis and prognosis prediction. In this study, we performed unsupervised clustering on serum proteomics data from clinically diagnosed patients with NS, irrespective of VTE occurrence, combined with gene set variation analysis (GSVA). Afterward, the intercluster differences in VTE incidence were explored to identify key protein signatures associated with high VTE risk, aiming to promote an accurate risk assessment strategy to improve VTE prophylaxis in patients with NS.

Methods

Patients and ethics approval and sample collection

The study population was all Han Chinese, including 49 patients clinically diagnosed with NS, and age and sex-matched healthy control (HC)-paired serum samples (n = 15) were collected. Demographic features of patients and HCs are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The HC group was used as a reference group for proteomic comparison. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 1975, the research protocol and informed consent documents were approved by the Ethics Committees of the China-Japan Friendship Hospital (Ethics Approval No. 2023-KY-318). Those without VTE at NS onset had pathological diagnosis by ultrasound-guided percutaneous renal biopsy after admission. All patients were followed up to 2 years. All first VTE events were recorded as study end points and were confirmed by objective imaging–based methods, including vascular ultrasonography and computed tomography pulmonary angiography, as appropriate. Recurrent VTE events were not included in the present analysis.

In this study, baseline VTE was defined as VTE already present at the time of baseline serum collection. Follow-up VTE was defined as the first incident VTE occurring during follow-up among patients without VTE at baseline sampling. Accordingly, cumulative VTE referred to all first VTE events identified during the study period, including both baseline and follow-up VTE. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, pathological subtypes, and treatments were collected. Laboratory parameters included serum albumin, lipid profiles, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), immunoglobulin, coagulation-related parameters, and 24-hour urinary protein excretion.

During hospitalization, 2 mL of serum was collected from patients with NS before renal biopsy or corticosteroid application. Blood samples were subsequently stored at −80 °C. The entire sample collection process took <1 hour. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all serum samples and were used to evaluate the stability of the proteomic workflow.

Proteomic sample preparation

A high-depth serum proteomics workflow was applied for sample processing. For each serum sample, 50 μL was collected, and low-abundance proteins were enriched using a magnetic bead–based method. The enriched proteins were subsequently lysed, reduced, and alkylated, followed by enzymatic digestion at 37 °C using 10 μL of a trypsin/Lys-C mixed enzyme working solution. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 solid-phase extraction cartridges, lyophilized, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. Peptide concentrations were measured using the BeyoBCA assay and normalized to 100 ng/μL for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Peptide samples were separated using a Vanquish Neo Ultra High Performance Liquid Chromatography nano–liquid chromatography system. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. Peptide separation was performed using a trap–analytical column configuration, with the analytical column temperature maintained at 55 °C and an injection amount of 200 ng. Eluted peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Astral high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Mass spectrometry was performed in positive ion mode using a data-independent acquisition (DIA) strategy. The MS1 scan range was m/z 380 to 980, and MS2 spectra were acquired using a multiwindow DIA approach for protein identification and quantification.

Mass spectrometry data processing and protein identification

Raw mass spectrometry data were analyzed using DIA-NN software (version 1.8.1) with a library-free search strategy. Database searching was performed against the UniProt human proteome database (uniprotkb_proteome_UP000005640_human_83385_20250224.fasta). During data processing, the Match Between Runs function was enabled. A false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% was applied at both the peptide and protein levels. The resulting protein identification and quantification data were used for subsequent analyses.

Proteomic quantification and data preprocessing

Relative protein quantification was performed using DIA-NN with the MaxLFQ algorithm, yielding a total of 4032 nonredundant proteins. Redundant protein entries were merged by retaining the record with the highest total intensity. Protein intensities were log2 transformed. Nonpositive values (corresponding to undetected signals) were treated as missing values. Because MaxLFQ performs cross-sample normalization, the resulting intensity matrix was subsequently median-centered across samples for downstream analysis. Only proteins quantified in at least 2 experimental groups, with valid quantitative values in no <30% of samples within each group, were retained, and proteins with zero variance across samples were excluded. Given the left-censored missing-not-at-random nature of LFQ data, missing values were imputed using the within-group MinProb method (q = 0.01, tune.sigma = 0.3) [17]. Consensus clustering and downstream statistical analyses were performed using the filtered and imputed protein expression matrix.

Consensus clustering analysis

Based on the protein expression matrix, the SD of each protein across NS samples was calculated, and the top 1000 proteins with the highest SD were selected for unsupervised clustering. Consensus clustering was then performed using the R package ConsensusClusterPlus [18] (version 1.68.0; R Studio). Hierarchical clustering was applied with Pearson correlation as the distance metric. The maximum number of clusters was set to K = 6. A total of 1000 resampling iterations were conducted, with 80% of samples and 80% of features randomly selected in each iteration (pItem = 0.8; pFeature = 0.8). To evaluate the robustness of the clustering results, additional consensus clustering analyses were performed using the top 1200 and 1300 most variable proteins. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the consensus matrix. Principal component analysis (PCA) was subsequently performed to reduce dimensionality and visualize the overall distribution of different molecular subgroups in low-dimensional space.

GSVA enrichment analysis

GSVA (version 1.50.5) was used to evaluate differences in pathway activity among molecular subgroups. Gene sets for Gene Ontology Biological Process were obtained from MSigDB (Gene Matrix Transposed file), and pathways containing at least 10 genes were included in the analysis. GSVA was performed using a Gaussian kernel to generate pathway enrichment score matrices for each sample. Based on the consensus clustering results (cluster 2 vs cluster 1), differential analysis of GSVA scores was conducted using the limma package (version 3.62.0), with statistical significance estimated by empirical Bayes methods. Pathways with |log2 fold change (FC)| of >0.2 and adjusted P values of < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Differential expression analysis

Differential protein expression between cluster 2 and cluster 1 was analyzed using the limma package. Proteins with an FDR, adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, of <0.05 and |log2 (FC)| of >0.585 were defined as differentially expressed proteins.

Protein–protein interaction analysis

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks of upregulated differentially expressed proteins were constructed using the STRING database (version 11.0). Network visualization was performed using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.0). Hub proteins were further identified using the Cytoscape plugin cytoHubba [19], based on topological analysis using the Degree and Edge Percolated Component algorithms.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians with IQRs, and categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages. Group comparisons based on consensus clustering results were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Associations between protein expression levels and clinical parameters were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis, with correlation coefficients and corresponding P values reported.

Longitudinal follow-up indicators, including serum albumin, eGFR, and 24-hour urinary protein excretion, were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with cluster, time (in months), and the cluster × time interaction as fixed effects and subject-specific random intercepts as random effects. Time was modeled as a continuous variable and was calculated from the first available measurement date for each variable within each patient. The models were fitted using all available follow-up measurements without additional imputation of missing longitudinal outcome values. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to describe the cumulative incidence of VTE events during follow-up, excluding patients who had experienced VTE at baseline. Patients who remained free of documented VTE were censored at the date of last follow-up; this included patients lost to follow-up. Differences between molecular subgroups were compared using the log-rank test. All statistical tests were 2 sided, and P vale of < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.3.3; R Studio), and data visualization was conducted using ggplot2 (version 4.0.1).

Results

Molecular heterogeneity of patients with NS revealed by consensus clustering and PCA

The characteristics of HCs and patients with NS are tabulated in Supplementary Table 1. PCA was first performed using the protein expression profiles of all samples. In the reduced-dimensional space, patients with NS were clearly separated from HCs, indicating overall differences in proteomic patterns between the 2 groups (Figure 1A). To explore molecular heterogeneity within patients with NS, consensus clustering was conducted based on the top 1000 most variable proteins among NS samples. According to the consensus matrix heatmap (Figure 1B) and the cumulative distribution function curves (Figure 1C), the optimal number of clusters was determined to be K = 2. Therefore, patients with NS were classified into 2 molecular subgroups (cluster 1 and cluster 2). Similar clustering patterns were observed when the analysis was repeated using the top 1200 and 1300 most variable proteins, supporting the robustness of the 2-cluster solution (Supplementary Figure 1). PCA was then performed the second time among only samples of patients with NS. The 2 molecular subgroups showed a clear separation in the low-dimensional space (Figure 1D), which was consistent with the consensus clustering results.

Baseline clinical characteristics in NS clusters

Considering the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics (Table), the 2 clusters were similar in age (57 [42-61] vs 60 [41-70] years; P = .50) and sex (male: 89% vs 75%; P = .70). Membranous nephropathy and minimal change diseases were the predominant pathological manifestations in both clusters, with statistically nonsignificant difference (P = .80). Most baseline laboratory parameters showed no significant differences between the 2 clusters, including serum albumin, lipid profiles, renal function indices (serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, and eGFR and 24-hour urinary protein excretion). Notably, urinary β2-microglobulin levels were slightly higher in cluster 2 than in cluster 1 (0.02 vs 0.00; P < .05).

Incidence of VTE in NS clusters

Both baseline VTE and cumulative VTE (all first VTE events identified during the study period, including baseline and follow-up events) were numerically higher in cluster 2 than in cluster 1, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (cluster 2 vs cluster 1: 43% vs 11%; P = .13 for baseline VTE; and 58% vs 22%; P = .074 for cumulative VTE) (Table). Supplementary Figure 2 shows the follow-up incidence of VTE among baseline VTE-free patients, which was slightly higher in cluster 2 than in cluster 1, but the difference was not statistically significant (log-rank P = .362). Detailed clinical characteristics of the VTE events, including VTE type, timing relative to NS diagnosis, and available anatomical site information, are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Trajectories in renal function between NS clusters

Serum albumin levels increased in both clusters but remained consistently higher in cluster 2 than in cluster 1 over time (cluster-by-time interaction P = .031) (Figure 2A). eGFRs demonstrated significant differences in trajectories between the 2 clusters (Figure 2B) and were consistently lower in cluster 2 (cluster-by-time interaction P = .044). Regarding 24-hour urinary protein excretion, both subgroups showed a decreasing trend during follow-up, with an overall lower level in cluster 2 (Figure 2C).

GSVA reveals distinct pathway activities between NS clusters

GSVA was performed to compare pathway activities between the 2 NS clusters. Pathways included extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, cell–cell signaling, calcium-dependent cell–cell adhesion via plasma membrane cell adhesion molecules, chemokine-mediated signaling, and monocyte chemotaxis were identified to be more active in cluster 2 (Figure 3A). Meanwhile, cluster 2 showed lower activity in pathways associated with cytoskeletal organization and cellular energy metabolism, including regulation of actin cytoskeleton organization, actin filament–based movement, microtubule cytoskeleton organization, cellular respiration, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate synthesis.

Differential protein expression between the NS clusters

Differential protein expression analysis was performed between the 2 clusters (cluster 2 vs cluster 1). By using an FDR of <0.05 and |log2(FC)| of >0.585 as the selection thresholds, a total of 972 differentially expressed proteins were identified, of which 145 were significantly upregulated in cluster 2, and 827 were significantly downregulated (Figure 3B).

PPI network analysis of upregulated proteins and identification of candidate hub proteins

The PPI network construction on significantly upregulated differential proteins using the STRING database is shown in Figure 3C. Topological analysis of the PPI network was subsequently performed using the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape software. Based on Degree and Edge Percolated Component algorithms, the network nodes were ranked, and high-scoring proteins were selected (Figure 3D). By intersecting the results from both algorithms, 9 candidate hub proteins were ultimately identified (Figure 3E), including CTNNB1 (β-catenin), VCAN (versican), POSTN (periostin), SDC1 (syndecan-1), MMP2 (matrix metalloproteinase-2), SERPINC1 (also known as antithrombin III [ATIII]), APOA1 (apolipoprotein A1), PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9), and LIPC (hepatic lipase).

Differential expression of hub proteins between the NS clusters

Based on the PPI network analysis, the expression levels of the 9 candidate hub proteins were further compared across the 2 NS clusters and HCs. The results showed that the expression levels of these hub proteins were significantly higher in cluster 2 than in cluster 1 (Figure 4). Notably, MMP2, SDC1, CTNNB1, and PCSK9 were significantly upregulated in cluster 2, while no significant differences were observed between cluster 1 and HCs. SERPINC1 (ATIII) was significantly higher in cluster 2 than in cluster 1, although the levels in both NS groups were lower than the HCs.

Additionally, coagulation-related proteins PROC (protein C), PROS1 (protein S), and VWF exhibited expression differences between the 2 clusters (Figure 4). Compared with HCs, the levels of PROC and PROS1 were significantly lower in both cluster 1 and cluster 2; however, PROS1 expression was significantly higher in cluster 2 than in cluster 1, while PROC showed no significant difference between the 2 clusters. Moreover, VWF was significantly higher in both cluster 1 and cluster 2 than in HCs, but no significant difference was found between the 2 clusters.

Clinical correlation analysis of the candidate proteins

To further explore the associations between hub proteins and clinical features, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the 9 hub proteins and clinical indicators (Supplementary Figure 3). SERPINC1 was negatively correlated with serum creatinine (r = −0.29). In contrast, VCAN, MMP2, and POSTN were positively correlated with urinary β2-microglobulin (r = 0.40, 0.49, and 0.37, respectively), and POSTN was also positively correlated with urinary α1-microglobulin (r = 0.29).

Subgroup analysis of patients with/without VTE events in cluster 2

In cluster 2, characteristics of the patients with/without cumulative VTE were further compared (Supplementary Table 3): at baseline, patients who developed VTE had significantly higher D-dimer levels than those without VTE (421 vs 209 ng/mL; P = .002). Moreover, markers of tubular injury were significantly increased in the VTE group, including urinary α1-microglobulin (6.2 vs 3.9 mg/dL; P = .045) and the urinary N-acetyl-β-d-glucosaminidase/creatinine ratio (38 vs 27 U/g of creatinine; P = .036). We performed an additional analysis after excluding patients with baseline VTE from cluster 2. As shown in Supplementary Table 4, patients who developed incident VTE during follow-up had higher baseline serum creatinine (P = .046), fibrinogen (P = .020), and urinary α1-microglobulin (P = .010) than those without follow-up VTE, whereas baseline D-dimer showed only a nonsignificant increasing trend (P = .087).

Longitudinal changes of renal function were further compared between patients with and without VTE during the 2-year follow-up period (Supplementary Figure 4): patients with VTE consistently exhibited lower eGFR levels than those without VTE. No significant differences were observed in the longitudinal trajectories of serum albumin or 24-hour urinary protein excretion between patients with and without VTE within cluster 2 (Supplementary Figure 4A,C).

Discussion

In this study, serum proteomics–based analysis revealed marked molecular heterogeneity among patients with NS. By unsupervised clustering, we identified 1 cluster with a certain profile of protein expression and exhibited a potential for developing VTE. Differential protein expression and pathway enrichment analyses further demonstrated that the cluster was characterized by enrichment of ECM remodeling and endothelial-related biological processes, providing a molecular context for the observed differences in the potential of VTE risk. Moreover, clinical correlation analyses demonstrated that several hub proteins identified through PPI network analysis were significantly associated with renal function, especially markers of tubular injury. These findings of our research provide evidence of more detailed mechanisms underlying the increased risk of endothelial damage in patients with NS and may contribute to identification of the patients with a higher risk in developing VTE.

Hypercoagulable state has been considered a common and significant feature of patients with NS, characterized by a decrease of protein C, protein S, and ATIII, predominantly resulted from the loss of low-molecular-weight anticoagulation proteins through the glomerulars [9]. With the comparison of patients with NS and HCs, our study confirmed the hypercoagulable state in patients with NS. After stratifying patients by unsupervised clustering, we found no significant difference in protein C level between the 2 NS subgroups but higher levels of protein S and ATIII in the VTE high–risk phenotype subgroup. Similar evidence has been revealed by a previous study: the levels of tissue-type plasminogen activator and plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 were significantly higher in children with idiopathic NS [10], presenting an inconsistency with the classic hypothesis of losing low-molecular-weight anticoagulant protein in NS. These findings indicated potential mechanisms other than the hypercoagulable state in patients with NS, leading to a higher susceptibility of VTE. Consistent with previous studies [11,20], our results implying endothelial injury, another key element of the Virchow triad, may be an important inducement of VTE in a particular subgroup of patients with NS. By innovatively using untargeted proteomics combined with unsupervised clustering analysis, we described a VTE high–risk phenotype among patients with NS.

Through bioinformatics analysis, we found that the upregulated protein profiles in VTE higher–risk group were primarily enriched in markers related to cellular adherence (VCAN and CTNNB1), ECM damage (MMP2), and glycocalyx injury (SDC1), as well as thrombosis (SERPINC1). The MMP family plays a role in maintaining the function and integrity of the vascular physical barrier. In recent years, multiple studies have confirmed the association of the MMP family with the onset and progression of various venous vascular diseases, such as venous ulcers and lower extremity varicose veins [21]. Mendelian randomization studies have suggested a potential causal relationship between plasma levels of various MMPs and the occurrence of VTE [22,23]. MMP2, also known as a kind of gelatinase, is elevated in various kidney diseases [24,25]. MMP2 have been considered significant related to the development chronic kidney disease and renal fibrosis [26], consistent with our study where VTE high–risk group was marked as elevated MMP2 and lower eGFR. Another protein, SDC1, a member of the heparan sulfate proteoglycan family, is a biomarker of endothelial glycocalyx damage. It participates in the composition and regulation of the ECM and could be cleaved by MMPs [27,28]. Previous studies have shown that elevated SDC1 level was associated with endothelial cell damage and hypercoagulable state in patients with NS [12,29,30] and in patients with infection [31,32] and sepsis [33]. Higher SDC1 has also been reported to be correlated with high-risk pulmonary embolism [34]. Given the above evidence, it is reasonable to deduce that endothelial cell damage may be 1 of the key triggers of VTE in VTE higher–risk group with elevated MMP2 and SDC1, as the hypercoagulable states were comparable in both clusters.

Marked urinary ATIII loss has been proposed as an important mechanism underlying NS hypercoagulopathy. However, controversial results have been reported by studies: some studies have attributed VTE during NS to ATIII deficiency [35]. Others have reported VTE despite normal ATIII levels [36], and some reported the absence of VTE despite ATIII deficiency [37]. In our study, ATIII levels in patients with NS are significantly lower than in HCs, consistent with the principle of protein loss due to damage to the glomerular filtration barrier, but cluster 2 had the highest ATIII. The elevated blood ATIII in this condition might be released by the injured endothelial cells [38]. These findings further indicated that the traditional concept of hypercoagulopathy might play limited role in the mechanisms of VTE in patients with NS, and the connection between hypercoagulopathy, endothelial damage, and other factors of VTE pathogenesis should be considered and further investigated.

Previous studies and guidelines recommended serum albumin as the key indicator for VTE prophylaxis in patients with NS based on the presumed link between hypoalbuminemia and a hypercoagulable state [5]. However, our study provides a different perspective by the result derived from unsupervised clustering of proteomic profiles, with serum albumin not emerging as a core feature for defining molecular subtypes. Intriguingly, the VTE higher–risk subgroup showed elevated serum albumin levels over time than the low-risk subgroup. This finding, coupled with the lack of association with membranous nephropathy, suggested that the specific VTE risk appeared to be driven by broader molecular mechanisms not directly reflected by serum albumin. The thrombotic state in NS is likely driven by a complex interplay of multiple molecules, pathways, and mechanisms. The significance of correlations between some hub proteins (MMP2 and POSTN) and the biomarkers also indicated the relationship between tubular injury and endothelial damage.

Although VTE in NS has been recognized and emphasized for many years, systematic investigations at the molecular level remain limited. Using serum proteomics and an unsupervised clustering approach, we identified molecular subgroups of patients with NS with similar baseline clinical characteristics but distinct risks of VTE, providing new insights into molecular features associated with thrombotic risk. Furthermore, we integrated proteomic data with clinical characteristics and incorporated longitudinal follow-up information on VTE events and renal function trajectories. This enabled a more comprehensive characterization of disease progression and improved the clinical interpretability of the molecular stratification. Our findings provide important clues for future studies aimed at exploring the molecular basis of NS-associated VTE, identifying high-risk populations, and informing potential strategies for risk stratification and precision prevention.

This study has several limitations. First, serum rather than plasma samples were used for proteomic analysis. During serum preparation, certain coagulation-related proteins, such as fibrinogen and its components, may be affected, which could influence the identification of some differentially expressed proteins. Second, differential protein analyses primarily focused on upregulated proteins, while downregulated proteins were not systematically analyzed. In NS, downregulated proteins may partly reflect urinary protein loss rather than true biological suppression. In addition, the sample size and the number of VTE events were relatively limited, which may have reduced statistical power and resulted in some analyses not reaching statistical significance, despite consistent directional trends observed in key clinical and molecular indicators. Moreover, external validation was not available. Therefore, the time-to-event analysis should be considered exploratory, and the observed between-cluster difference in VTE events should be interpreted cautiously. The findings therefore require confirmation in larger, prospective cohorts, as well as further evaluation across different pathological subtypes of NS. Finally, the hub proteins identified through PPI network analysis were defined based on network topology and could not be considered functional drivers. Their specific roles in endothelial function, ECM remodeling, and thrombosis remain to be clarified through further in vitro and in vivo studies.

Conclusion

Serum proteomics analysis identified a group of patients with NS who were potentially at higher risk of developing VTE. ECM and endothelial glycocalyx damage might play important role in VTE development among patients with NS.
