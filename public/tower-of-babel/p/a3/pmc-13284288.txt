Clinical Skill Anxiety and Its Determinants Among Clinical‐Year Health Science Students in Ethiopia: An Institutional‐Based Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Habtie Bantider Wubet, Elelta Mulugeta Ayalew, Temesgen Birlie Asmare, Diriba Teshome, Basazinew Chekol Demilew, Tiruwork Desse Tamir, Metages Hunie Belay, Kaletsidk Dessalegn Mossie, Samuel Fasika Demissie, Negesse Zurbachew Gobezie

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundClinical skill anxiety (CSA) is a common psychological challenge among health science students during their clinical years, potentially impacting clinical competence and patient safety. While previous Ethiopian studies have highlighted high levels of stress and anxiety among students, evidence on context‐specific CSA and its determinants among clinical‐year students remains limited.MethodsThis institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted between 20 August and 25 October 2025. A total of 426 clinical‐year health science students across medicine, nursing, midwifery, and anaesthesia programs at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DTCSH) were invited to participate in a census. The study assessed clinical skill anxiety (CSA) and associated characteristics using an adapted Clinical Skill Anxiety Scale (CSAS). This scale was informed by established instruments (STAI, BAI, and PSS), modified through a literature review and expert consultation, and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in this study population (Cronbach's α = 0.86). Predictors of CSA were identified using multivariable logistic regression (p < 0.05; 95% CI).ResultsThe overall prevalence of CSA among clinical‐year health science students was 64.2% (95% CI: 59.5%–69.0%). Significant determinants included fear of making mistakes (AOR = 4.2; 95% CI: 2.80–6.30), limited instructor feedback (AOR = 3.6; 95% CI: 2.05–6.32), female sex (AOR = 2.2; 95% CI: 1.62–2.98), lack of prior clinical‐skills exposure (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.35–3.26), and high academic stress (AOR = 1.8; 95% CI: 1.25–2.60).ConclusionNearly two‐thirds (64.2%) of clinical‐year students experienced CSA, which was associated with psychological, institutional, and instructional factors. Based on these cross‐sectional findings, we suggest that strengthening mentorship, feedback systems, simulated skill practice, and stress‐reduction strategies may be beneficial. Future research could explore interventions such as maintaining lower student‐to‐instructor ratios, bolstering structured feedback systems, increasing simulation‐based learning opportunities, and integrating resilience training into health science courses. However, causal inferences cannot be drawn from this cross‐sectional design; therefore, intervention studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of these approaches.Patient or Public ContributionNo patients, caregivers, or public members participated in this study; the research focused exclusively on health science students in Ethiopia.

Introduction

Health Science College students experience significantly higher levels of anxiety, a psychological condition characterized by tension, uncertainty, and physical discomfort, due to substantial academic pressure, interpersonal expectations, and fear of failure (Fauzi et al. 2021; Raz et al. 2025). Clinical skill anxiety (CSA), which manifests as fear, tension, or cognitive disarray during patient‐care procedures such as medication administration, wound care, injections, or physical examinations, is a particularly prominent yet understudied issue in health sciences education (Martin and Naziruddin 2020; Jamaludin et al. 2025; Souffrant 2025). Unlike generalized anxiety, CSA is context‐specific and arises when students transition from virtual settings to real interactions with patients.

Several risk factors have been identified for CSA, including female sex, poor clinical learning environments, insufficient supervision, minimal prior skill exposure, fear of making mistakes, inadequate teacher feedback, and high levels of academic stress (Macauley et al. 2018; Ahmad et al. 2022). While educational design can influence these variables, institutions are unable to implement targeted interventions without empirical data.

The effects of CSA extend beyond psychological distress. Elevated anxiety negatively impacts working memory, clinical reasoning, psychomotor function, and learning retention (Al‐Ghareeb et al. 2017; Moran 2016; Malone 2022). Students affected by CSA may skip procedures, hesitate in crucial situations, or make mistakes, which can directly endanger patient safety and undermine their professional credibility. Untreated CSA can lead to burnout, academic attrition, and long‐term avoidance of clinical responsibilities (Bourgeois 2018).

The prevalence of anxiety among health science students during clinical training has been reported to range from 30% to 70% in North America, Europe, and Asia (Tian‐Ci Quek et al. 2019). Specific estimates of CSA among Asian nursing and medical students range from 45% to 68% (Wong et al. 2023). Studies in Africa indicate similarly high rates, attributed to a lack of resources, heavy patient loads, and insufficient monitoring (Nesengani et al. 2025). Eastern Africa has the highest anxiety rates on the continent, exceeding 49% (Aletaha et al. 2025). A systematic review and meta‐analysis conducted in Ethiopia revealed a pooled stress prevalence of 37.64% among students (Bezie et al. 2025). However, most research to date has focused on general psychological stress and anxiety rather than on anxiety specifically related to clinical skill performance. Clinical Skill Anxiety (CSA) is conceptually distinct because it arises during direct patient‐care activities and clinical processes, where anxiety can directly affect psychomotor performance, clinical decision‐making, learning outcomes, and patient safety (Hanfesa et al. 2020), yet there is virtually no data specifically addressing CSA. Additionally, previous Ethiopian research has primarily focused on general mental health outcomes, neglecting the institutional and educational factors that influence anxiety in clinical training settings.

Therefore, by offering the first empirical estimate of CSA among clinical‐year health science students in Ethiopia and identifying modifiable instructional, institutional, and student‐related factors associated with it, this study was carried out to address this significant contextual and educational gap.

Conceptual Framework

The Clinical Skills Anxiety Scale (CSAS) evaluates anxiety in four areas: hospital‐related, school‐related, student‐related, and sociodemographic factors. A mean item score of at least three indicates clinically significant anxiety, while a score below three suggests no significant anxiety. This conceptual framework was developed by three distinguished experts in mental health and medical education after conducting a literature review. Clinical skill anxiety (CSA) is categorized by sociodemographic factors (such as sex and age) (Colbert‐Getz et al. 2013; Henning et al. 2013), student‐related factors (including fear of making mistakes [Kaya Acar et al. 2025], academic stress [Bahashwan and Alhalafi 2024], low confidence [Lam et al. 2016], and limited prior exposure [Tang et al. 2019]), school‐related factors (such as inadequate supervision [Keshavarzi et al. 2022], limited feedback [Forde and OBrien 2022], poor communication, and curriculum overload [Almuways 2025]), and hospital‐related factors (including resource limitations, staff shortages, exposure to critically ill patients, and lack of personal protective equipment [PPE]; Chandio et al. 2024; Ahmed et al. 2023; Shahsavari et al. 2017).

We hypothesized that: (1) fear of making mistakes would have the strongest association with clinical skills assessment (CSA); (2) modifiable instructional factors, such as supervision, feedback, and prior exposure, will show significant independent associations even after controlling for individual‐level characteristics; and (3) the prevalence of CSA among clinical‐year health science students will be substantial (> 50%), consistent with regional estimates of general anxiety.

This conceptual framework guided both the statistical analysis—by organizing variable selection and multivariable modelling methods—and the questionnaire design—by ensuring that all hypothesized domains were represented in the measurement items (Figure 1).

Methods

Study Design, Period, and Setting

An institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from 20 August to 25 October 2025, at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, which has over 250 beds and is located in the South Gondar Zone of the Amhara Region in Northwest Ethiopia. This hospital serves approximately 2.65 million people and offers a wide range of services, including internal medicine, surgery, paediatrics, obstetrics/gynaecology, emergency care, radiography, and laboratory services. DTCSH was selected to assess the prevalence of CSA among clinical‐year students across various disciplines and to evaluate its diverse clinical training services, as it is the largest hospital and teaching facility in the area.

Population and Eligibility

All health science students at Debre Tabor College of Health Sciences constituted the source population. The study population included all clinical‐year students actively participating in clinical rotations at DTCSH during the data collection period, specifically in Medicine, Anaesthesia, Comprehensive Nursing, Paediatric Nursing, Midwifery, and Psychiatric Nursing.

Inclusion Criteria

Clinical‐year students who were present and willing to participate.

Exclusion Criteria

To minimize potential confounding from pre‐existing psychiatric illnesses that could independently affect anxiety levels beyond clinical training experiences, we excluded students who self‐reported clinician‐diagnosed mental disorders and were undergoing active treatment. However, we recognize that this exclusion may have reduced the sample's representativeness and led to an underestimation of the actual prevalence of clinical skill anxiety among all clinical‐year students.

Sample Size and Sampling Technique

Since the entire population of eligible clinical‐year students was manageable (N = 426), a facility‐based census sampling technique was chosen to maximize statistical power and minimize sampling error while including all accessible participants. This approach was more suitable for obtaining comprehensive institutional‐level estimates of CSA across all clinical fields compared to random sampling techniques. The year of study was included as an independent variable in the descriptive analysis and was taken into account during bivariable regression screening.

Given that the study employed a census approach, no formal sample size calculation or proportionate distribution among departments was conducted. Instead, participation was offered to all qualified clinical‐year students from every discipline assigned to the hospital during the study period. Study Variables

Dependent Variable

Clinical Skill Anxiety (CSA) Status.

Independent Variables

The study examined four distinct domains contributing to clinical skills anxiety. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, year of study, and clinical practice area. Student‐related issues encompassed fears of making mistakes, high levels of academic stress, pressure from evaluations, and the demand for excellent performance. School‐related factors primarily referred to educational processes such as feedback, communication, and curriculum issues. In contrast, hospital‐related factors pertained to clinical training conditions, including supervision, patient load, available resources, and the availability of PPE.

Operational Definitions

Academic self‐efficacy: Students' self‐assurance in their capacity to complete clinical and academic learning assignments (Honicke and Broadbent 2016).

Academic stress: The degree to which a student experiences psychological pressure, tension, and perceived overload due to academic demands—such as coursework, tests, clinical obligations, time management, the learning environment, and performance evaluation—is measured using an academic stress measurement tool. For analytical purposes, academic stress was categorized using a mean‐based classification consistent with previous studies on the mental health of Ethiopian students. Additionally, tertile distributions were presented descriptively to improve the interpretation of subgroups (Prabu 2015).

Adequate supervision: involves the frequent presence of clinical instructors, close observation of procedures, and the provision of guidance and constructive criticism during clinical activities (Weallans et al. 2022).

Clinical Skill Anxiety (CSA): A context‐specific form of anxiety marked by fear, tension, apprehension, or cognitive distress that arises during direct patient care procedures and clinical performance situations (Sanson et al. 2018).

Clinical Skill Anxiety Scale (CSAS): The Clinical Skill Anxiety Scale (CSAS) used in this study was adapted from existing instruments (STAI, BAI, and PSS). This adaptation involved a comprehensive process including a literature review, expert consultation, contextual modifications, and pilot testing with students outside the study population (Macauley et al. 2018; Pang et al. 2019; Alwhaibi et al. 2023; Racic et al. 2017). Mental health and medical education experts evaluated the questionnaire for content relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness. While full psychometric validation was not conducted, the adapted scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency for this study population (Cronbach's α = 0.86).

High academic stress: Score above the mean.

Low academic stress: Score ≤ the mean.

Low clinical supervision: Students perceive a lack of supervision and feedback, inadequate procedural instruction, and insufficient teacher presence during clinical practice activities (O'Brien et al. 2019).

Satisfaction with the clinical environment: Based on the questionnaire results, students' perceptions of the clinical learning environment were categorized as low, medium, or high, depending on their views regarding its supportiveness, organization, resource availability, and overall conduciveness to learning (d'Souza et al. 2015).

Data Collection Tools and Procedures

A standardized, pretested, self‐administered questionnaire was developed based on relevant literature and established anxiety assessment instruments to gather data. The questionnaire included the 18‐item Clinical Skill Anxiety Scale (CSAS), the 27‐item Academic Stress Questionnaire for Health Science Students (ASQ‐HSS), and twelve items measuring sociodemographic and academic variables. The CSAS was adapted from the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) through literature review, expert consultation, contextual modification, and pilot testing.

Thirty clinical‐year students from a local teaching hospital, which was not part of the study, completed a pretest one month prior to data collection. To enhance clarity and relevance, repetitive or unclear items were revised, and contextual adjustments were made based on the results.

Under the guidance of the primary investigator, two qualified final‐year pharmacy graduates collected the data. Their training covered study objectives, ethical concerns, confidentiality, informed consent, and the administration of standardized questionnaires. Questionnaires were completed anonymously in neutral classroom settings, away from teachers, to minimize social desirability bias. Completed forms were placed in a closed collection box.

Data Quality Control

To enhance content relevance and cultural appropriateness, the questionnaire was reviewed by professionals in mental health and medical education. In the studied population, the adapted CSAS demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.86). The completeness and consistency of the completed surveys were examined daily. Fortunately, no questionnaires were incomplete or excluded from the analysis. Data entry was conducted using EpiData version 4.6, which includes validation checks. The data was then double‐checked and cleaned before analysis. To minimize reporting and social desirability bias, techniques such as anonymous self‐administration, assurance of confidentiality, and the absence of instructors during data collection were implemented.

Statistical Analysis

EpiData version 4.6 was used for data entry, while SPSS version 27 was employed for analysis. Before the analysis, the normality of continuous data, such as CSAS scores, was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, along with skewness and kurtosis statistics and histograms. Means and standard deviations were calculated to summarize the results, as the data exhibited adequate near‐normality.

For multivariable analysis, candidate variables with p < 0.20 were identified using bivariable logistic regression. Descriptive subgroup comparisons were conducted based on tertile classifications of academic stress levels and CSAS scores (Table 4). Confounders were selected based on bivariable results, prior evidence, theoretical significance, and covariate selection guided by a priori directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) (Figure 3). Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) were reported, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Directed acyclic graph (DAG) showing hypothesized relationship between key determinants and CSA among clinical‐year health science students.

After evaluating multicollinearity (variance inflation factor < 2) and model fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.812), the final model accounted for 38.0% of the variation in CSA (Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.38).

Ethical Considerations

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the DTU College of Health Sciences Ethical Review Committee granted ethical approval (Ref No: CHS/258/2025). All participants were fully informed about the study's goals, methods, risks, benefits, confidentiality safeguards, and their right to withdraw without facing academic consequences. Anonymity was guaranteed through the use of non‐identifiable codes.

Results

Characteristics of Study Participants

A total of 426 clinical‐year students participated, achieving a 100% response rate. The distribution of students by discipline is as follows: 32.6% (n = 139) in medicine, 16.9% (n = 72) in comprehensive nursing, 16.4% (n = 70) in anaesthesia, 15.5% (n = 66) in midwifery, 10.3% (n = 44) in paediatric nursing, and 8.2% (n = 35) in psychiatric nursing (refer Figure 2 above).

Socio‐Demographic and Clinical Profile

The mean age of the participants in this study was 23.3 ± 1.99 years, with 56.6% under the age of 23 and 51.2% identifying as male. The majority were Orthodox Christians (88.0%), followed by Protestants (8.0%) and Muslims (4.0%). Additionally, 55.9% of participants were from rural areas. Most participants were in their second (35.0%) or third (27.0%) year of nursing, with medical students comprising the largest group (32.6%), followed by Comprehensive Nursing (16.9%), Anaesthesia (16.4%), Midwifery (15.5%), Paediatric Nursing (10.3%), and Psychiatry Nursing (8.2%).

Regarding academic and clinical exposure, 57.0% of participants exhibited low academic self‐efficacy, 51.9% reported high academic stress, and 40.0% had prior experience with simulations or OSCEs. The majority practiced three to five key skills (54.2%), studied for two to four hours each day (57.7%), and rated their clinical satisfaction as medium (48.6%) (Table 1).

Student to Instructor Ratios at DTCSH

At DTCSH, 187 clinical instructors supervised 426 clinical‐year health science students from 20 August to 25 October 2025, resulting in an overall student–instructor ratio of approximately 2.8:1. The majority of students (139, 32.6%) and instructors (89, 47.6%) were in the medical field, which had the lowest student‐to‐teacher ratio of 1.6:1. In contrast, psychiatric nursing exhibited the highest ratio at approximately 4.4:1, reflecting the lowest percentage of instructors (8, 4.3%) compared to enrolled students (35, 8.2%). Midwifery and paediatric nursing maintained similar, lower ratios of approximately 2.4:1, while anaesthesia (approximately 3.7:1) and comprehensive nursing (approximately 2.9:1) had moderate student–instructor ratios (Table 3).

The Clinical Skill Anxiety Scale (CSAS) scores displayed an approximately normal distribution, with the majority of students scoring between 45 and 70, and fewer observations at the extremes of the scale (see Figure 4). The distribution showed a slight positive skewness but remained reasonably symmetric for descriptive interpretation. Factors Associated With CSA

To address confounding, variables with a bivariable p‐value less than 0.20 were included in the multivariable logistic regression. Predictor variables, such as academic stress and supervision quality, were coded as binary or categorical. Variables with a p‐value less than 0.05 were considered significantly associated, and the results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals. The multivariable analysis indicated that clinical skills anxiety was significantly correlated with female sex, inadequate supervision, lack of prior clinical skills exposure, limited instructor feedback, fear of making mistakes, and high academic stress (Table 8). Discussion

This study empirically estimates clinical skill anxiety (CSA) among Ethiopian clinical‐year health science students, revealing a significant burden of 64.2% in the context of a resource‐limited teaching hospital. This finding highlights CSA as a considerable yet unrecognized issue that affects both education and patient safety in Ethiopian clinical training settings.

Our finding is in line with findings in Kenya (61%) (Agyapong‐Opoku et al. 2023), Nigeria (66%) (Chukwu et al. 2025), and Uganda (62%) (Ruhangayebare 2018), indicating common structural issues like large patient loads, a lack of simulation labs, and inadequate structured feedback. From the perspective of low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), the reported prevalence aligns with research from similar health systems, which often have high student‐to‐instructor ratios, inadequate feedback mechanisms, and limited simulation capabilities. However, due to methodological heterogeneity—specifically variations in measurement instruments, cut‐off points, and operational definitions of anxiety—direct comparisons of prevalence across studies should be approached with caution (Upadhayay 2017; Nandini et al. 2015).

However, our finding is lower than reports from Addis Ababa University (71%) (Reta et al. 2020) and Gondar University (69%) (Rtbey et al. 2022). The comparatively high prevalence at the local level is a result of structural limitations in clinical education, such as large patient loads, little supervision, and inconsistent procedural practice exposure. Notably, CSA differed between disciplines and was higher among first‐year clinical‐year students, with nursing and anaesthesia students exhibiting relatively higher levels than others (Offiah et al. 2019).

In contrast, our CSA prevalence is significantly higher than Western estimates (USA 45%) (Davis et al. 2024); Canada 50% (Scott 2023). Studies conducted in high‐resource environments typically show lower or moderate levels of anxiety related to clinical conditions. This variation is more likely attributed to systemic factors, such as reduced student‐to‐clinical instructor ratios, standardized competency frameworks, and structured simulation‐based instruction, rather than to ‘cultural’ differences in supervision techniques (Hope and Henderson 2014; Ijaz and Khalid 2020). Consequently, the primary distinctions seem to arise from variations in training infrastructure rather than from psychological traits or educational cultures.

This study identified significant factors associated with clinical skills anxiety (CSA), including fear of making mistakes (AOR = 4.2), limited instructor feedback (AOR = 3.6), inadequate clinical supervision (AOR = 2.5), female sex (AOR = 2.2), lack of prior exposure to clinical skills (AOR = 2.1), and high academic stress (AOR = 1.8).

These factors contribute to a learning environment characterized by low psychological safety and limited opportunities for directed practice. Inadequate supervision and feedback increase uncertainty and restrict opportunities for remedial learning (Kuo et al. 2016), while the fear of making mistakes directly heightens anxiety. Additionally, insufficient early exposure reduces familiarity with skills and increases cognitive load during actual clinical interactions (Fredericks et al. 2021; Ellis 2016).

Fear of making mistakes was the strongest predictor of clinical skills acquisition (AOR = 4.2), emphasizing the critical role of psychological safety in clinical learning environments. This underscores the need for supportive, non‐punitive supervision methods to foster safe skill acquisition, as research indicates that anxiety rises in environments where mistakes are perceived as punitive rather than constructive (Kennett 2024).

The significance of organized instructional support is further emphasized by strong predictors of inadequate supervision (AOR = 2.5) and insufficient feedback (AOR = 3.6). Evidence from LMIC contexts indicates that inadequate feedback mechanisms and a lack of teacher availability contribute to increased anxiety by creating ambiguity in skill execution (Kimari 2023; Ti‐Enkawol 2021). Additionally, high student–teacher ratios likely exacerbate these challenges, making tailored instruction more difficult (Bajwa et al. 2024).

In line with international data, being female was associated with a higher probability of experiencing Clinical Skills Assessment (CSA) (AOR = 2.2). Although previous research indicates that supportive training environments can help reduce gender discrepancies, these differences may stem from variations in stress assessment and performance‐related self‐perception (Kariuki 2022; Dagona 2018; Jha 2022).

Additionally, a lack of prior clinical experience (AOR = 2.1) underscores the importance of early skill familiarization. Supervised exposure and simulation‐based training are crucial protective measures, as students who enter clinical settings without structured prior practice are more likely to experience heightened anxiety (Saad et al. 2023).

Finally, high academic stress (AOR = 1.8) increases perceived threats and sensitivity to errors, which diminishes cognitive capacity during clinical performance and contributes to CSA. These findings support the incorporation of stress‐reduction strategies into health care education (Shetty et al. 2022; Vasli et al. 2021).

Subgroup differences by study year significantly support this interpretation: the steady decline in senior years indicates partial adaptation through repeated practice, while the higher CSA in younger clinical students likely reflects less procedural exposure. Additionally, discipline‐specific variations highlight the importance of exposure intensity and training structure, rather than solely personal vulnerability.

Strong relationships (AORs up to 4.2) were identified by the multivariable model; however, these values should be interpreted with caution due to potential residual confounding. The observed associations may have been influenced by individual psychological characteristics and unmeasured institutional factors, such as ward workload intensity, instructor competence, and rotation patterns. Additionally, self‐reported measures may either underestimate or exaggerate true anxiety levels depending on participant response patterns due to social desirability and recall bias.

In conclusion, this study enhances the literature by shifting the focus from individual‐level explanations of anxiety to a systems‐based understanding of clinical training environments in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs).

Implications and Suggestions for Future Practice and Research

Based on the cross‐sectional findings of this study, we offer the following suggestions for educators and policymakers in resource‐limited settings. However, as this study design cannot establish causal relationships, these suggestions should be viewed as potential directions for future research and quality improvement efforts rather than definitive recommendations.

First, the observed associations suggest that improved monitoring and feedback mechanisms warrant attention. Increasing the frequency and quality of interactions between teachers and students could potentially enhance psychological safety in clinical environments and reduce ambiguity during skill acquisition. This hypothesis, however, requires testing through intervention studies.

Second, providing early and frequent clinical exposure through supervised and simulation‐based practice may be beneficial. For first‐year students, progressively developing skills before engaging in direct patient care might alleviate anxiety and boost procedural confidence, but further research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of such approaches.

Third, health sciences curricula that systematically incorporate stress management and resilience‐building components could potentially help reduce academic stress and its association with clinical performance anxiety. We suggest that educators consider piloting such programs with rigorous evaluation components.

Strengths and Limitations

All eligible students participated in the census‐based study, minimizing sampling error and enhancing internal validity at the institutional level. Measurement reliability was strengthened by using a validated CSA instrument with strong internal consistency.

However, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all Ethiopian health science students, as the study was conducted at a single institution using a non‐probability census approach. Although safeguards such as anonymous responses, the absence of instructors during data collection, and assurances regarding academic consequences were implemented, subtle coercion to participate cannot be entirely ruled out. Additionally, the lack of advanced statistical diagnostics, including factor analysis, invariance testing, and multilevel modelling, presents a methodological limitation.

Selection bias may have arisen from the exclusion of students with clinician‐diagnosed mental illnesses who were receiving active treatment, potentially leading to an underestimation of CSA prevalence. Recall and social desirability biases may have influenced self‐reported data, and the cross‐sectional design limits causal inference.

Conclusion

This study provides the first empirical estimate of clinical skill anxiety among health science students in their clinical years in Ethiopia, revealing a prevalence of 64.2%. The findings identify associations with several modifiable educational factors, including supervision, feedback, academic stress, and prior clinical exposure. While these cross‐sectional associations cannot establish causality, they suggest that strengthening supportive clinical teaching environments and expanding supervised skills training may warrant further investigation as potential strategies to reduce anxiety in similar resource‐limited settings. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to confirm these relationships and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted educational interventions.
