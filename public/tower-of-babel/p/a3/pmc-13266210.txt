Endogenous hydrogen sulfide reprograms mitochondrial respiration and modulates pathogenicity in Ustilago maydis
Authors: Emilio Espinoza-Simón, Nayeli Torres-Ramírez, Margarita Juárez-Montiel, Emmanuel Ríos-Castro, Mariana M. García-Reyes, Damián Ramírez-Robles, Rosario Ortíz-Hernández, Olga Echeverría-Martínez, Lourdes Villa-Tanaca, Francisco Torres-Quiroz

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Respiratory growth with cysteine increases H2S production in U. maydis.•Endogenous H2S promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic remodeling.•H2S shifts respiration toward alternative oxidase-dependent electron transport.•Elevated H2S increases global protein S-sulfenylation and S-persulfidation.•Endogenous H2S modulates disease severity during maize infection.

Introduction

Sulfur metabolism plays a central role in microbial physiology by providing essential metabolites and regulating redox balance and energy metabolism (Park et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). In yeast, sulfur is available in multiple inorganic and organic forms, and its biotransformation is regulated by two interconnected metabolic pathways: the sulfate assimilation pathway, which converts inorganic sulfur compounds into homocysteine and related metabolites, and the transsulfuration pathway, which interconverts organic sulfur compounds (Huang et al., 2017a). Cysteine plays a key role as a precursor for glutathione, proteins and coenzymes, as well as serving roles in the remethylation pathway. However, elevated cysteine can be toxic to yeast cells (Kumar et al., 2006) due to its highly reactive thiol group, which interferes with mitochondrial respiration and iron metabolism (Hughes et al., 2020). Intracellular cysteine homeostasis varies among yeast species. Saccharomyces cerevisiae mitigates cysteine toxicity by sequestering cysteine in vacuoles (Hughes et al., 2020) or by converting it into less reactive sulfur-containing compounds, such as cystathionine, glutathione, and S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine (Deshpande et al., 2017). In Candida albicans, cysteine dioxygenase converts cysteine into sulfite, which is subsequently exported through a sulfite efflux pump (Hennicke et al., 2013). Additionally, growth of S. cerevisiae under cysteine-rich conditions leads to increased hydrogen sulfide (H2S) production (Huang et al., 2017b; Winter and Curtin, 2012).

Hydrogen sulfide is an increasingly recognized gasotransmitter that regulates several physiological processes across animals, microorganisms, and plants, including mitochondrial bioenergetics, cell signaling, and antioxidant and detoxification pathways (Cirino et al., 2023; Módis et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2023). Hydrogen sulfide also functions as a signaling molecule that regulates mitochondrial respiration and redox-dependent protein modifications. H2S interacts with metalloproteins (Mantle and Yang, 2022) and induces redox post-translational modifications of cysteine residues, such as S-persulfidation and S-sulfenylation, which modulate protein structure and activity (Zivanovic et al., 2019), protein folding (Beedle et al., 2016), signal transduction (Heppner et al., 2018), and protein oligomerization (Wang et al., 2023).

In fungi, H2S exhibits concentration-dependent effects. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, low H2S concentrations enhance antioxidant activity during heavy metal exposure (Wu et al., 2024), regulate glucose fermentation and oxygen consumption (Espinoza-Simón et al., 2022), support growth (Gu et al., 2021), extend chronological lifespan (Hine et al., 2015),(Shah et al., 2021) and increase oxidative stress resistance (Li et al., 2019). However, at high concentrations, H2S inhibits mitochondrial oxygen consumption (Huang et al., 2023) and yeast growth (Gu et al., 2021).

Moreover, H2S regulates the ultradian cycle and population synchrony (Sohn and Kuriyama, 2001). In Aspergillus niger and Penicillium italicum, exogenous H2S exposure impairs spore germination and mycelial growth (Fu et al., 2014) and inhibits fungal growth during postharvest fruit storage (Deng et al., 2021). Sueiro-Olivares et al. showed that Aspergillus fumigatus strains with reduced S-persulfidation exhibit decreased virulence and diminished fitness against host immune responses, highlighting the functional relevance of H2S-dependent redox signaling during infection (Sueiro-Olivares et al., 2021). Similarly, in Candida albicans, the H2S-producing enzyme cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS) plays a role in antioxidant defense and pathogenicity and pharmacological inhibition of CBS reduces virulence in a murine candidiasis model (Chang et al., 2022).

Ustilago maydis is a dimorphic pathogenic basidiomycete that infects maize, causing tumors in aerial plant tissues. Following spore germination, haploid yeast-like cells undergo saprophytic growth (Vollmeister et al., 2012) until mating triggers the formation of a pathogenic dikaryon capable of invading plant tissues. Tumor formation typically begins approximately five days after infection, marking the onset of sporogenesis and teliospore production (Ferris and Walbot, 2020). U. maydis is considered a major phytopathogen that causes losses in maize crops worldwide (Lanver et al., 2017); however, in Mexico, it is also valued as a culinary delicacy known as “huitlacoche” (Villagrán et al., 2023).

Pathogenicity of U. maydis depends on multiple factors, including secondary metabolites (Yu et al., 2023), compatible solutes such as trehalose (Cervantes-Chávez et al., 2016), tolerance to oxidative stress (Cuamatzi-Flores et al., 2024), and the coordinated secretion of effector proteins and hydrolytic enzymes that modulate host responses (Moreno-Sánchez et al., 2021; Mueller et al., 2008; Schuster et al., 2024).

Despite increasing evidence that hydrogen sulfide regulates fungal physiology and virulence in several human pathogenic fungi (Amich, 2024), its role in phytopathogenic fungi and its potential impact on mitochondrial metabolism and pathogenic development remain largely unexplored. In particular, whether endogenous H₂S production influences mitochondrial respiratory pathways and redox signaling during fungal infection is unknown.

Here, we investigate how cysteine-driven endogenous H₂S production reshapes mitochondrial physiology, cellular redox homeostasis, and pathogenic behavior in the maize phytopathogen U. maydis. Using an integrative approach combining respiratory physiology, quantitative proteomics, redox analysis, and plant infection assays, we show that increased endogenous H₂S production induces mitochondrial remodeling, shifts respiration toward alternative oxidase–dependent pathways, promotes widespread proteomic reprogramming, and modulates disease severity during maize infection.

Materials and methods

Culture of Ustilago maydis strains

The haploid non-infective strain Ustilago maydis FB2 (Esparza-Perusquía et al., 2023) and the solopathogenic strain SG200 (Rodríguez-Kessler et al., 2012) were used in this study. The SG200 strain carries an active b locus, in which the bW and bE homeodomains interact to form a functional heterodimer that activates the signaling cascade required for the filamentous pathogenic state, enabling host colonization and tumor formation in Zea mays.

Strains were routinely maintained on solid YPD medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, and 2% dextrose) and incubated at 30°C (Juárez et al., 2004). For liquid cultures, cells were grown at 30°C with shaking at 250 rpm.

To determine the optimal conditions for endogenous H2S production, cells were cultured in the following culture media: YPD, YPLac (1% yeast extract, 2% lactate, 1% gelatin peptone, 2% (NH4)2SO4, 0.1% KH2PO4; pH 5.5), synthetic fermentative medium (SD; 0.17% yeast nitrogen base, 2% glucose, 0.5% (NH4)2SO4, 0.1% KH2PO4, Drop Out 1X (in g/L: adenine 0.2, arginine 0.2, asparagine 1.0, glutamic acid 1.0, leucine 0.6, lysine 0.2, methionine 0.2, phenylalanine 0.5, serine 3.75, threonine 2.0, tryptophan 0.4, tyrosine 0.3, valine 1.5, uracil 2.0 and histidine 2.0)), or synthetic respiratory medium (SLac; 0.17% yeast nitrogen base, 2% lactate, 0.5% (NH4)2SO4, 0.1% KH2PO4, Drop Out 1X, pH 5.5). All media were prepared in the presence or absence of 2 mM L-cysteine, with cysteine added after autoclaving.

H2S production

H2S production was assessed using a lead acetate strip assay (Xia et al., 2017). Briefly, FB2 or SG200 cells were grown overnight in YPD at 30°C with shaking. Cultures were then inoculated into SD, SD supplemented with cysteine (SDcys), SLac, or SLac supplemented with cysteine (SLcys), YPD, YPD supplemented with cysteine (YPDcys), YPLac, or YPLac supplemented with cysteine (YPLcys) at an initial OD600 of 0.2 and incubated overnight at 30°C.

Hydrogen sulfide released by the cells reacted with lead acetate strips, resulting in the formation of a dark brown to black precipitate. The intensity of strip darkening was proportional to the amount of H2S produced. Experiments in at least three independent biological replicates were performed.

Yeast cell viability test

Following growth of U. maydis strains under conditions described in Section 2.1, cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in sterile water to an OD600 of 1. Serial dilutions were spotted onto YPD agar plates, incubated at 30°C and monitored after 24 h and 48 h. Six independent biological replicates were performed. (Romero-Aguilar et al., 2022a).

Mitochondrial staining

U. maydis FB2 cells were cultured under the conditions described in Section 2.1. Cells were incubated in YPD, YPDcys, YPLac, YPLcys, SD, SDcys, SLac or SLcys overnight at 30°C. Cells were then stained with 25 nM MitotrackerTM Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MS, USA) for 30 min at 30°C in the dark. Stained cells were mounted on agarose pads (Tanaka et al., 2010) and visualized using a Nikon Eclipse E600 microscope (Nikon Corporation, Japan). Images were acquired with a Nikon DXM1200F digital camera (Nikon Corporation) from three independent biological replicates.

Fluorescence intensity was used as a proxy for mitochondrial biomass (Guerra et al., 2019; Ruiz et al., 2016). Quantification was performed using ImageJ software from at least 200 cells per condition per experiment. Statistical analyses were conducted using Graph Pad Prism 8.

Lipid staining

Cells were cultured as described above, harvested, washed twice with 1X PBS, and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min. Cells were then washed twice with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min.

Neutral lipids were stained with 5 µM of BODIPYTM 493/503 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MS, USA) in PBS for 30 minutes in the dark, as previously described (Romero-Aguilar et al., 2023). After two PBS washes, cells were mounted on agarose pads and visualized using a Nikon Eclipse E600 microscope (Nikon Corporation, Japan). Images were recorded with a Nikon DXM1200F digital camera (Nikon Corporation, Japan). Data were acquired from three independent biological replicates.

Electron microscopy

U. maydis FB2 cells were fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.2 M PIPES buffer (pH 6.8) containing 0.2 M sorbitol, 2 mM MgCl2 and 2 mM CaCl2, overnight at 4°C. Samples were rinsed with water and post-fixed in 2% KMnO4 for 45 min at room temperature. Cells were contrasted with 1% uranyl acetate and dehydrated in a graded ethanol series from 30% to 100%.

Samples were embedded with Durcupan™ ACM resin (Sigma-Aldrich). Ultrathin sections of 60 nm were obtained using a Leica Ultracut UCT ultramicrotome, mounted on formvar-coated copper grids, and contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were examined using a Jeol 1010 transmission electron microscope at 80 kV. Digital images were captured with a Hamamatsu camera (Hamamatsu Photonics K. K., Japan). Observations are representative of three independent biological replicates.

Sample preparation for LC-MS analysis

U. maydis FB2 cells were cultured in SLac or SLcys as described above. Cells were harvested and lysed under liquid nitrogen using glass beads, a mortar and pestle. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. Proteins were precipitated using a CHCl3/MeOH protocol, and pellets were resuspended in 1X PBS. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MS, USA).

Protein samples were processed using the iST® Sample Preparation kit (PreOmics, Munich, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, proteins were enzymatically digested under denaturing conditions for 2 h in a Thermoblock (Eppendorf, Hamburg, GER). Resulting peptides were dried using a Savant DNA120 SpeedVac Concentrator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MS, USA) and then resuspended in “LC-load” buffer (PreOmics, Munich, Germany). Peptide samples were stored at -20°C until LC-MS analysis. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Label-free and mass spectrometry-based quantitative proteomics

Proteomic analysis was performed in a UPLC Nano Acquity M-Class system coupled to a Synapt G2-Si QTOF Mass Spectrometer Synapt G2-Si (Waters Corporation; Milford, MA, USA), according to a modified version of the method described by Ríos-Castro (Ríos-Castro et al., 2020). Briefly, tryptic peptides were separated on an HSS T3 C18 column (Waters, Milford, MA); 75 μm × 150 mm, 100 Å pore size, 1.8 μm particle size; using a mobile phase A, 0.1% formic acid (FA) in H2O, and mobile phase B, 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (ACN) under the following gradient: 0 min 7% B, 121.49 min 40% B, 123.15 to 126.46 min 85% B, 129 to 130 min 7% B, at a flow of 400 nL·min−1 and 45°C in the column compartment.

Spectral data were acquired using a data-independent acquisition (DIA) strategy in High-Definition Multiplexed MS/MS (HDMSE) mode (Full-Scan DIA). Ionization parameters were set to 2.75 kV for the capillary voltage, 30 V for the sampling cone, 30 V in the source offset, and 70°C for the source temperature, 0.5 bar for the nanoflow gas, and 150 L·h−1 for the purge gas flow. Low and high-energy spectra were acquired in positive mode over an m/z range of 50−2000 with a scan time of 500 ms. Fragmentation was achieved using a collision energy ramp from 19 to 55 eV. The system was calibrated using [Glu1]-fibrinopeptide, [M+2H]2+ = 785.84261 with mass accuracy below 1 ppm.

MS-data analysis

Raw MS and MS/MS raw data files were processed using Progenesis QI for Proteomics software v4.2.1 (Geromanos et al., 2011; Li et al., 2009) (Nonlinear Dynamics, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) using a target-decoy strategy against the Ustilago maydis reference proteome (downloaded from UniProt, UP000000561, 6805 protein sequences) (Elias and Gygi, 2010; Käll et al., 2008). Search parameters included trypsin as the proteolytic enzyme with one missed cleavage allowed, carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation as a variable modification. Peptide and fragment mass tolerance were set automatically. Identification criteria required a minimum of two fragment ion matches per peptide, five fragment ion matches per protein, and one unique peptide per protein.

The false discovery rate (FDR) was set to ≤ 1%. Only peptides within ±20 ppm were used for quantification and reversed (decoy) hits were excluded. Proteins were considered differentially expressed if they showed a log2 fold change ± 1 (≥2-fold change) with a p-value ≤ 0.05. Protein abundance ratios were calculated using the Top3 approach (Silva et al., 2006), comparing SLcys versus SLac conditions.

Mass spectrometry proteomics data were deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (Perez-Riverol et al., 2025) repository with dataset identifier PXD060479. Quantitative data were further analyzed using OmicScope (Reis-de-Oliveira et al., 2024) and custom Python 3.11.8 scripts for data preprocessing, functional enrichment analysis (GO and KEGG), and visualization using Matplotlib, Seaborn, and Plotly.

Oxygen consumption assay

Rates of oxygen consumption in U. maydis FB2 cells were measured by high-resolution respirometry using an Oxygraphy-2k system (Oroboros Instruments, Innsbruck, Austria). Cells were cultured as described above, harvested, washed twice with distilled water and resuspended in phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.4). Measurements were performed at 30°C. Basal respiration was recorded followed by sequential additions of 1 mM glucose, 1 mM KCN, and 6 μM of n-Octylgalate (nOG). Oxygen consumption rates were determined from the slope of the oxygen concentration traces using DatLab software (Oroboros Instruments, Innsbruck, Austria). Experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates

S-persulfide and S-sulfenyl detection in U. maydis proteome

Protein S-sulfenylation was analyzed using a dimedone-based labeling approach. U. maydis FB2 cells were cultured as described above, and proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.7, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM neocuproine, 3% Nonidet P-40, 5% SDS, 3% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, and 1X protease inhibitor) supplemented with 100 μM of DAz-2:Cy5. Cells were disrupted under liquid nitrogen using glass beads (425-600 µm), and a mortar and pestle. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 90 min under light-protected conditions. Proteins were separated from debris by centrifugation (5 min, 3,100 rpm, 4°C), precipitated using a methanol-chloroform protocol (4/4/1, sample/methanol/chloroform), washed twice with cold methanol and resuspended in a sample buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.7 and 2% SDS). Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and proteins were resolved by 12% SDS-PAGE. Fluorescence was detected using a Typhoon FLA 9500 scanner with an LPR filter for Cy5 (635 nm).

Protein S-persulfidation was detected as previously described (Zivanovic et al., 2019). Cells were lysed as mentioned above, replacing DAz-2:Cy5 with 15 mM NBF-Cl. After pulverization, samples were incubated at 37°C for 60 min in the dark. Proteins were recovered, resuspended in sample buffer, quantified, and incubated with 100 µM DAz-2:Cy5 for 90 min. Proteins were precipitated, washed, resolved by 12% SDS-PAGE, and fluorescence was detected using an LPB (473 nm) and an LPR (635 nm) filter. Experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

H2O2 production

U. maydis FB2 cells were grown in SD, SDcys, SLac, or SLcys media. Cultures were centrifuged to collect supernatants for extracellular H2O2 quantification, and cell pellets were washed three times with distilled water and resuspended in 1x PBS. To assess H2O2 production associated with cells (Romero-Aguilar et al., 2022b) or extracellular H2O2 accumulated 24 h after culture, supernatants or cell suspensions were transferred to a 96-well plate, and 50 µL of AmplexTM Red reaction mixture (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added. Fluorescence was measured for 3 h at 30°C using an Infinite 200 microplate reader (TECAN, Life Sciences, Mannedorf, Switzerland) at 530 nm excitation and 590 nm emission. H2O2 levels were estimated using a calibration curve. Experiments were performed in six independent biological replicates.

Catalase activity

Catalase activity was measured as previously described (Hadwan, 2018). U. maydis FB2 cells were grown as described above, and cytoplasmic extracts were incubated with 1 mM H2O2 in PBS at 30°C for 5 min. A working solution of 5 mM Co(NO3)2 in 1M NaHCO3 was added, vortexed for 5 s, and incubated in the dark for 5 min. Absorbance was measured at 440 nm, and catalase activity was expressed as U/mL*min. Five independent biological replicates were performed.

Plant infection assay

Plant infection assays were performed using the solopathogenic U. maydis SG200 strain (Kämper et al., 2006). Cells were cultured with or without cysteine, washed and adjusted to an O.D.600 of 1 in sterile water. One-week-old maize seedlings (Zea mays cv. Cacahuazintle), grown in vermiculite filled pots, were inoculated by stem injection under aseptic conditions using 100 µL of cell suspension per plant with a syringe and needle. Mock controls were injected with sterile water (Soberanes-Gutiérrez et al., 2015). Disease symptoms were recorded 12 days post-inoculation (dpi) from three independent experiments, based on the system of Redkar and Doehlemann (Redkar and Doehlemann, 2016): tumor severity was classified as follows: small tumors (<1.5 mm), tumors (2-4 mm), and heavy tumors associated with stunting or bending of stems. We included an anthocyanin category in addition to chlorosis, both scored as 1 in scales based on Kämper and excluded dead plants, based on the observed symptoms (Supp Fig 1). Plants were maintained under greenhouse conditions with a watering regime alternating water and H-E-MX nutrient solution, prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. Experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each including at least twenty technical replicates.

After the infection assay, maize plants and vermiculite were disposed of as infectious biological material, and disinfection of the areas was performed according to institutional and federal guidelines.

Fuzz reaction on charcoal media

Cells were cultured under the conditions described above and inoculated by drop application onto PD-charcoal plates (2.4% Potato Dextrose Broth, 1% charcoal, 2% agar), sealed with Parafilm, incubated at room temperature, and examined after 24 and 48 h (Banuett and Herskowitz, 1989). Hyphal (fuzz) formation was assessed using a Nikon SMZ-1000 Stereo Zoom Trinocular Microscope in three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.02 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-test. Multiple comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SLac supplemented with cysteine induces endogenous H2S production in Ustilago maydis

Previous reports have shown that both low nitrogen concentrations (Song et al., 2020) and high cysteine levels (Huang et al., 2016) promote H2S production in S. cerevisiae. To identify conditions that induce endogenous H2S production in U. maydis, cells were grown in rich and synthetic media under fermentable and non-fermentable conditions, with or without 2 mM cysteine (YPD, YPDCys, YPLac, YPLcys, SD, SDCys, SLac and SLcys). After 24 h of culture, the non-fermentable synthetic medium supplemented with 2 mM cysteine (SLcys) induced the highest H2S production (Fig. 1) without affecting cell viability (Supp Fig 2). To validate the H₂S detection assay, U. maydis cells grown in SLac were treated with one or two pulses of 50 μM NaHS, an exogenous H₂S donor. As expected, NaHS treatment produced a strong H₂S signal (Supp Fig 3). Endogenous H₂S production was specific to cysteine addition, as supplementation with 2 mM methionine or 2 mM glutamine failed to induce H2S release (Supp Fig 4). Thus, respiratory medium supplemented with cysteine was employed as a metabolic strategy to elevate endogenous H2S production. Rather than mimicking environmental cysteine availability, this approach enabled the investigation of the cellular consequences of increased intracellular H2S levels and their impact on Ustilago maydis physiology and pathogenic behavior.

Since acidic media (pH < 3) have been reported to induce filamentation in U. maydis (Ruiz-Herrera et al., 2015), culture pH was measured to exclude pH-dependent morphological or functional effects. After 24 h of growth, SLac and SLcys cultures exhibited identical pH values (4.7), indicating that cysteine-induced H2S production was not associated with medium acidification.

A key determinant of U. maydis pathogenic development is the transition from yeast-like to filamentous growth. To assess whether cysteine supplementation influenced this morphological switch, cells cultured under the conditions described above were spotted onto PD-charcoal and YPD plates. After 48 h of incubation, SLac and SLcys cultures displayed comparable hyphal (fuzz) formation, indicating that cysteine-induced H2S production does not alter filamentous growth under these conditions (Supp Fig 6).

H2S Decreases Cytochrome-dependent Respiration and promotes Alternative Oxidase Activity in U. maydis

Given the pronounced effects of cysteine on mitochondrial morphology and biogenesis under non-fermentable conditions, mitochondrial respiration was evaluated. Oxygen consumption was measured in U. maydis FB2 cells grown in SLac or SLcys medium using high-resolution respirometry. As shown in Fig. 4, cells exhibiting elevated endogenous H2S production displayed significantly higher basal and glucose-dependent oxygen consumption than SLac-grown cells. Upon addition of potassium cyanide (KCN), a cytochrome c oxidase inhibitor, SLac-grown control cells exhibited a marked decrease in oxygen consumption. In contrast, cells grown in SLcys retained most of their respiratory activity in the presence of KCN, indicating activation of a cyanide-resistant respiratory pathway. Importantly, this residual oxygen consumption was strongly inhibited by the alternative oxidase (AOX) inhibitor n-Octyl gallate (nOG) (Romero-Aguilar et al., 2022c), confirming the involvement of AOX-dependent respiration under conditions associated with elevated endogenous H2S production.Fig. 4Elevated endogenous H2S levels in SLcys-grown cells are associated with AOX-dependent respiration in U. maydis. Elevated endogenous H2S levels rewire the proteomic landscape of U. maydis

To determine whether elevated endogenous H2S levels and mitochondrial remodeling were accompanied by global changes in protein abundance, label-free quantitative proteomic analysis was performed comparing SLac control cells and SLcys-grown cells. A total of 1,706 proteins were identified, revealing clear differences in protein abundance patterns between the two conditions and supporting extensive proteome remodeling associated with elevated endogenous H2S levels. Approximately 323 proteins were significantly different in abundance, with 198 proteins showing increased abundance and 125 showing decreased abundance by at least twofold in cells with elevated endogenous H2S levels compared to SLac control (Fig. 5A and Supp Tables 1 and 2). H2S Alters Lipid Distribution in Ustilago maydis

Proteomic analysis revealed that cysteine supplementation increased the abundance of several enzymes involved in lipid metabolism, including long-chain fatty acid–CoA ligase (∼5-fold), farnesyl diphosphate synthase (∼5-fold), 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (∼4.6-fold), acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (∼4.5-fold), and enoyl-CoA hydratase (∼4-fold) (Supp table 1). To assess whether these proteomic changes were accompanied by alterations in cellular lipid organization, intracellular neutral lipids were visualized using BODIPY™ 493/503 staining.

Cells grown in SLac medium displayed numerous lipid droplets distributed throughout the cytoplasm. H2S modulates disease severity during maize infection

To determine whether elevated endogenous H2S levels affected fungal pathogenicity, maize infection assays were performed using the solopathogenic Ustilago maydis strain SG200. Cells were grown in SLac or SLcys medium, washed, and used to inoculate one-week-old maize seedlings. Disease symptoms were evaluated 12 days post-inoculation.

Overall infection efficiency, defined as the percentage of plants showing visible disease symptoms, did not differ significantly between SLac control and SLcys-grown cells (Fig. 10 and Supp Fig 12). Discussion

Sulfur metabolism is a central determinant of microbial physiology and adaptation to environmental conditions. In this study, we demonstrate that cysteine supplementation under respiratory conditions enhances endogenous H₂S production in Ustilago maydis, triggering coordinated metabolic remodeling that includes mitochondrial restructuring, activation of alternative oxidase (AOX)-dependent respiration, extensive proteome reprogramming, altered redox homeostasis, and modulation of disease severity during maize infection.

It is important to note that cysteine supplementation in this study was used as a controlled metabolic intervention to elevate intracellular H₂S levels, rather than to mimic physiological cysteine concentrations in natural environments. Although extracellular cysteine availability in planta is likely limited, intracellular sulfur flux and transsulfuration activity can dynamically regulate endogenous H₂S production (Lyu et al., 2019). Therefore, our findings should be interpreted in the context of H₂S-dependent signaling and metabolic remodeling, rather than as reflecting a direct response to environmental cysteine availability.

Hydrogen sulfide has emerged as a key regulator of diverse physiological processes across biological systems, including plant development, immune response, and microbial pathogenesis (Chang et al., 2022; Cirino et al., 2023; Muñoz-Vargas et al., 2018; Walsh and Giedroc, 2020). This gasotransmitter is primarily generated in the cytosol by enzymes such as sulfite reductase, cystathionine β-synthase, cystathionine γ-lyase, and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase, whereas in mitochondria it is primarily metabolized by sulfide quinone oxidoreductase and cysteine desulfurase.

Here, we investigated the role of endogenous H2S production in U. maydis pathogenesis. Although many studies rely on exogenous H₂S donors (e.g., NaHS, GYY4137) (Shah et al., 2021), such approaches do not necessarily fully recapitulate the endogenous metabolic regulation of intracellular H2S levels. In line with observations in S. cerevisiae, U. maydis grown in a respiratory medium supplemented with cysteine displayed significant H2S production in both FB2 and SG200 strains (Fig. 1 and Supp Fig 5). Notably, H2S production was enhanced under respiratory rather than fermentative conditions, suggesting a functional link between sulfur metabolism and mitochondrial activity.

Under conditions of elevated endogenous H2S, mitochondrial remodeling was observed, characterized by increased mitochondrial abundance and elongation as revealed by fluorescence and electron microscopy. These features are consistent with mitochondrial remodeling under altered metabolic redox conditions. The role of H2S in mitochondrial morphology has been studied in human cells with contrasting results. In human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell lines, H2S induces mitochondrial fragmentation and decreases mitochondrial network formation (Hanna et al., 2024); however, in vascular smooth muscle cells, exogenous H2S donors inhibit mitochondrial fragmentation (Sun et al., 2016) suggesting that hydrogen sulfide exerts concentration-dependent effects on mitochondrial dynamics. At elevated levels, H2S can inhibit cytochrome c oxidase activity (Paul et al., 2021).

In our system, oxygen consumption in SLcys-grown cells was largely resistant to cyanide inhibition and sensitive to n-octyl gallate, consistent with activation of AOX-mediated respiration. Notably, mass spectrometry analysis did not reveal differences in AOX abundance between conditions. This suggests that, rather than inducing AOX expression, H2S may activate the enzyme through post-translational or indirect mechanisms, such as direct persulfidation, as reported for other enzymes (Mustafa et al., 2009), or indirectly through partial inhibition of complex IV of the electron respiratory chain. These observations are compatible with a model in which partial inhibition of the cytochrome pathway is accompanied by a compensatory metabolic shift toward AOX-dependent respiration. Such respiratory flexibility has been described in U. maydis under stress conditions (Juárez et al., 2006).

Inhibition of cytochrome-dependent respiration has been associated with increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in multiple systems. Consistently, SLcys-grown cultures exhibited elevated H2O2 levels and reduced H2O2 removal capacity (Supp Fig 10), supporting altered redox homeostasis under conditions of elevated endogenous H₂S.

Cellular proteomic landscapes are highly responsive to metabolic perturbations. In both S. cerevisiae and U. maydis, nutrient limitation or environmental stress induces coordinated remodeling of mitochondrial, metabolic and proteostasis-related proteins. Glucose or nitrogen starvation induces large-scale remodeling of metabolic, respiratory and proteostasis-related proteins (Campion et al., 2021; Kolkman et al., 2006). In this context, the extensive proteomic changes observed in SLcys-grown cells are consistent with large-scale metabolic reprogramming linking sulfur metabolism, mitochondrial activity, and cellular proteostasis.

Label-free quantitative proteomics revealed broad changes in protein abundance in SLcys-grown cells. Mitochondrial proteins were among the most strongly enriched categories, including enzymes of the TCA cycle and respiratory chain components. These changes are consistent with a metabolic shift toward oxidative metabolism, potentially compensating for reduced cytochrome-dependent activity.

Proteins related to proteasomal degradation (26S proteasome regulatory subunit RPN2, proteasome subunit beta, and proteasome endopeptidase complex), nitrogen metabolism (aspartate aminotransferase, homoisocitrate dehydrogenase, ornithine aminotransferase, threonine dehydratase, and arginine biosynthesis bifunctional protein ArgJ among others) (Supp Table 1 and Supp Table 2) and translation initiation were also differentially abundant, suggesting coordinated adjustments in protein turnover and biosynthetic capacity. Septin family proteins were increased in SLcys-grown cells. Although septins have been implicated in morphogenesis and pathogenicity (Eisermann et al., 2023), the functional consequence of their increased abundance under these conditions remains to be determined. Several antioxidant and metabolic enzymes were less abundant in SLcys-grown cells, including enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase-peroxidase (Supp Table 2). This protein profile is consistent with redistribution of metabolic fluxes and redox buffering capacity under cysteine-supplemented conditions.

Endogenous H2S production was also associated with alterations in lipid metabolism. Increased abundance of beta-oxidation enzymes was accompanied by redistribution of neutral lipids and decreased lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 8). Given the established links between lipid metabolism and pathogenic development in U. maydis, these alterations may represent an additional metabolic layer through which endogenous H₂S signaling influences fungal pathogenic development, although direct causal relationships require further investigation.

Finally, cells exhibiting elevated endogenous H₂S levels showed increased disease severity and enhanced anthocyanin accumulation in infected plants. Anthocyanin production is a marker of plant oxidative and defense responses, suggesting intensified host–pathogen interaction. Exogenous NaHS treatment produced comparable trends, supporting a role for H₂S in the modulation of pathogenic outcome (Supp Fig 13).

The genome of U. maydis encodes several sulfur metabolism enzymes, including cysteine synthase, cystathionine gamma-synthase, homoserine O-acetyltransferase, sulfite reductase, 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase, sulfide quinone oxidoreductase, and sulfate adenylyltransferase (Kämper et al., 2006), indicating that both sulfate assimilation and transsulfuration pathways, including H₂S generating reactions, are conserved in this fungus.

H₂S has been shown to display concentration-dependent effects during fungal infection and host defense mechanisms (Hu et al., 2025). Exogenous NaHS at concentrations above 0.1 mM reduces fruit decay caused by Aspergillus niger and Penicillium italicum by inhibiting spore germination and mycelial growth (Fu et al., 2014) with similar effects reported for Rhizopus nigricans, Mucor roxianus, and Geotrichum candidum (Tang et al., 2014). Conversely, inhibition of endogenous H₂S synthesis reduces fungal virulence, as shown in Candida albicans, where CBS deletion impairs growth, antioxidant response, and hyphal development while increasing beta-glucan exposure (Chang et al., 2022). In plants, H2S is generated by enzymes such as L-cysteine desulfhydrase (DES1), O-acetylserine B (OAS-B), and beta-cyanoalanine synthase (CAS-C1). Its production increases during early infection stages, contributing to antifungal defense responses. These observations highlight the context-dependent and hormetic nature of H₂S signaling in host-pathogen interactions, supporting its potential relevance to the modulation of U. maydis pathogenicity observed in this study.

Together, our results support a model in which cysteine-driven H2S overproduction partially inhibits cytochrome-dependent respiration, promoting a shift toward AOX-dependent electron transport that reshapes redox homeostasis and ultimately modulates fungal pathogenic behavior. This integrative model highlights endogenous H₂S as a metabolic signal that links sulfur metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, redox regulation, and host-pathogen interactions in U. maydis.

Cysteine levels in maize cells are typically maintained in the low micromolar range. However, host infection is associated with metabolic reprogramming and alterations in cellular redox balance, which may affect intracellular cysteine availability (Kopriva and Rennenberg, 2004; Noctor et al., 2012). Under these conditions, U. maydis may experience changes in sulfur fluxes that promote increased endogenous H₂S production. In this context, the ability of U. maydis to grow in the presence of millimolar cysteine concentrations and exhibit increased infectivity highlights its metabolic flexibility and suggests that H₂S-mediated signaling may contribute to fungal adaptation during host colonization. Given that U. maydis is cultivated in Mexico as a food product (“huitlacoche”), these findings may also have implications for strategies aimed at optimizing production.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that controlled elevation of endogenous H₂S levels in Ustilago maydis, achieved through cysteine supplementation under respiratory conditions, induces coordinated changes in mitochondrial function, redox balance, proteome composition, lipid distribution, and disease severity during maize infection.

Our data indicate that increased intracellular H₂S availability promotes a shift toward alternative oxidase-dependent respiration, accompanied by elevated oxidative stress and extensive proteomic remodeling, particularly affecting mitochondrial and metabolic pathways. These findings highlight a close functional relationship between sulfur metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, and cellular redox homeostasis.

Together, these observations support a model in which increased endogenous H₂S levels act as a metabolic signal that integrates sulfur metabolism with mitochondrial physiology, redox regulation, and pathogenic behavior. This integrative perspective suggests that endogenous H₂S production represents an important regulatory layer in fungal adaptation to metabolic and environmental cues.

Although the precise molecular mechanism connecting H₂S signaling with specific virulence determinants remains to be elucidated, our results indicate that modulation of sulfur metabolism and intracellular H₂S levels can influence pathogenic development in Ustilago maydis. Given the importance of this organism both as a phytopathogen and as a fungus of agricultural and biotechnological interest (Corpas and Palma, 2020; Fernández-Ortuño et al., 2008), further investigation of metabolic pathways that regulate H₂S production may provide insights into host-pathogen interactions and potential strategies for modulating fungal infection.
