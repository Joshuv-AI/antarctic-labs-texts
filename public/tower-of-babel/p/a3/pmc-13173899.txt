Using transition design to explore transition from adolescence to adulthood
Authors: Juliet T. Jarrell, Olivia M. Stransky, Jacquelin Rankine, Loreta Matheo, Andrew McCormick, J.D. Tomar Pierson-Brown, Traci M. Kazmerski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost existing research concerning the transition from adolescence to adulthood is narrowly focused on the healthcare setting; few studies have investigated this process holistically while including the viewpoints of young adults and their caregivers. We explored the transition from adolescence to adulthood from the perspectives of multiple constituent groups with a vested interest and expertise in this process.MethodsWe utilized Transition Design, a novel, qualitative methodology. We asked participants to engage in six visual mind-mapping exercises to identify problems associated with the transition to adulthood, and then solve these problems by creating a more sustainable and equitable future model. We collected data from these exercises using the virtual whiteboard platform Miro and applied thematic analysis to code for key themes.ResultsA total of 25 participants completed the exercises (7 young adults, 5 young adult caregivers, 4 social services providers, 9 healthcare providers). Overall, all participants focused on mental health and increasing access to resources. However, each constituent group prioritized a different aspect and level of society in their responses; young adults emphasized large-scale societal themes and structural changes, social services providers emphasized improving access to community resources, caregivers emphasized the role of advocacy, and healthcare providers emphasized the health sector.DiscussionResults highlight the complexity of transition across broad societal domains and the unique perspectives of constituent groups. These results can help to inform future research in this area while improving transition from adolescence to adulthood, both within and outside of the healthcare setting.

Introduction

Within the healthcare setting, although the transition from pediatric to adult medical care is standard practice,1 this shift is not well demarcated. The Society for Adolescent Health and Medicine (SAHM) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) identify the need for a smooth and coordinated transition to adult medical care for adolescents and young adults (AYAs), both with and without chronic health conditions. These guidelines highlight the importance of considering the broader context of AYAs’ lives during this process, as healthcare is just one part of the transition to adulthood.2

Most existing research related to the transition from adolescence to adulthood is narrowly focused on the healthcare setting.3, 4, 5, 6 Few studies have investigated the transition to adulthood in a more holistic sense and included the perspectives of AYAs and their caregivers.7 Most elicit only one perspective (e.g., only caregivers or healthcare providers)8, 9 or focus on a specific subset of people undergoing healthcare transition (e.g., youth with autism spectrum disorder).10, 11, 12 There is still a need for broader studies that conceptualize transition as a multi-layered process both within and outside the healthcare setting, while including the simultaneous perspectives of the different groups involved in the transition process.

Using Transition Design,13 we explored the transition from adolescence to adulthood from the perspectives of multiple key constituent groups simultaneously, including AYAs, caregivers, social services providers, and healthcare providers. We sought to understand how groups who live within and alongside transition to adulthood view this process and their suggested changes to improve this process.

Methods

Recruitment

Participants represented four constituent groups: 18–26-year-old AYAs who were living with or without a chronic illness, caregivers of AYAs who are current parents of one or more AYAs, social services providers, including case managers and social workers who regularly interact with AYAs in the community, and AYA healthcare providers, both ambulatory and inpatient, who regularly treat AYAs. We contacted local AYA healthcare clinics, pediatric specialty clinics, and AYA advocacy organizations to recruit eligible participants. Clinics and advocacy organizations were trained by the research team to ask AYAs within their offices if they would like to participate in a research study and provide a broad overview of our Transition Design methodology. Beyond the age criteria, there were no specific eligibility criteria for AYAs. If recruited participants were interested, a member of the research team called them by phone to explain the details of the research study and verbally consent them. We required participants to speak English so that consent by phone could be completed. We utilized snowballing sampling and encouraged participants to recommend other individuals who may want to participate. The University of Pittsburgh’s Institutional Review Board approved this study (STUDY22080028).

Study design

We adapted the Transition Design approach to conduct this study; this methodology has not previously been used within the young adult population and, more broadly, has not ever been used in the medical research or healthcare space. Transition Design is an interdisciplinary methodology incorporating features of participatory design, qualitative research, and constituent engagement developed to address large-scale societal problems that cannot be solved by fast, one-dimensional solutions (i.e., climate change, income inequality).14 Transition Design assumes that groups who have a vested interest and expertise in social problems, because they are directly affected by these issues, therefore hold the key to creating and implementing the strategies necessary for change.14 For the purpose of this study, transition to adulthood was posed as the large-scale societal problem, and the groups of participants consisted of those directly involved in this societal process, therefore providing unique insight into transition and how to potentially improve the process. Within their constituent group, participants engaged in six mind-mapping exercises, first summarizing how transition affects all layers of society and then solving the “problem” of transition by imaging a future where transition is more equitable, sustainable, and harmonious (See Table 1 for a detailed description of the six mapping exercises and how they relate to transition to adulthood; all tables are visible at the end of the manuscript). Study procedures

Participants attended a 7-hour, in-person workshop in February 2023. The workshop took place in a conference room with four stations each including a large computer screen, a keyboard and mouse, and a microphone for recording. We split participants into the four constituent groups and instructed them to sit at their respective stations. If participants held multiple identities, such as AYA caregiver and healthcare provider, they were instructed to choose the group with which they most identified. Each constituent group completed the six mind-mapping exercises according to the Transition Design methodology as discussed in the previous Study Design section.

For each exercise, participants watched a brief explanatory video and then were guided through the exercise by two trained facilitators. One facilitator prompted participants’ responses while the other documented these responses verbatim within the visual maps using Miro, a virtual whiteboard platform. Each activity lasted 50 min, with five minutes of instructions and 45 min for collecting responses. One ten-minute break followed each activity and participants were given a one-hour lunch break. For the 7-hour workshop, participants received $300. At the conclusion of the workshop, participants completed an electronic demographics survey, which included questions about race/ethnicity, gender, age, level of education, current job/occupation, and years practicing medicine/working in social support fields if applicable (only for participants in the healthcare and social services provider groups). The demographics survey was the same for participants in all constituent groups.

Eight individuals (four physicians, two social services providers, one clinical research coordinator, and one medical student) served as facilitators. Prior to the workshop, the facilitators received a facilitator training guide and participated in a 1.5-hour training in which they became familiar with the Transition Design exercises and the Miro platform.

Data analysis

We sought to understand the similarities and differences in how groups who live within and alongside transition to adulthood view this process and to explore how these groups would implement changes to improve the transition process. To accomplish these goals, we engaged in a qualitative coding process to identify themes and subthemes from the six visual map exercises for each group (young adults, young adult caregivers, social services providers, healthcare providers), and then compared these themes/subthemes across groups. JJ, a cisgender female medical student, reviewed the maps from each constituent group independently, without cross-analyzing data between groups at this stage. She treated each narrative excerpt from each map as a qualitative data point and created a set of overarching codes based on these data points. The six maps yielded 159 narrative excerpts from the AYA group, 156 excerpts from the caregiver group, 220 excerpts from the social services provider group, and 188 excerpts from the healthcare provider group. She developed a set of codes for each constituent group and applied it to all their respective maps. Then, she combined the four sets of independent codes together, and refined all codes based on common themes identified from each group. Using this set of codes as the standard for all the data across groups, she applied this codebook to all excerpts. Thus, the data from the constituent groups and the overarching set of codes were in dialogue with one another, and were used in tandem to qualitatively analyze the data most effectively.

OMS, a cisgender female clinical research coordinator with extensive qualitative coding experience, conducted the same coding process independently. Coders met to discuss discrepancies and congruencies in their analyses and further refined the code set. Finally, using the final set of codes, the two coders extrapolated from this process and identified points of convergence and divergence between groups to better understand areas that must be considered to facilitate future collaborative interventions. We highlighted Transition Design Activities 5 and 6 (the stages in which participants actively generate and apply interventions to the societal problem of transition to adulthood) specifically in this analysis as these provide the most practical insight into how constituent groups would each enact change. The final codebook is provided in the supplementary material (1.1) as well as the completed displays for activities 5 and 6 for each constituent group (1.2, 1.3). Overall, our study design, study procedures, and data analysis methodology seek to uphold the standards of trustworthiness in qualitative research. Regarding credibility, the participants in each constituent group deeply engaged with the six mind-mapping exercises over a 7 h period. The responses from participants were written verbatim, and the responses from participants were recorded for revisitation during the coding process. Additionally, the data collected in this study is transferable to multiple different community contexts, including the healthcare system, social services networks, the legal system, and more. Furthermore, although the methodology of the study and the participant responses are unique, our project establishes dependability based on our transparency with and detailed description of the coding process of the data. Finally, confirmability was established through using multiple coders to analyze the data and produce a cohesive final codebook.

Results

A total of 25 participants representing four constituent groups participated in this study (7 young adults, 5 young adult caregivers, 4 community service providers, and 9 healthcare providers). 21 of these participants completed the electronic demographic questionnaire at the end of the workshop day. For those who completed the questionnaire, 8 self-identified as White, 3 identified as Black or African-American, and 1 identified as Asian. 0 identified as Hispanic. 19 self-identified as women, 1 identified as transmasculine, and 1 identified as a man. Ages of the participants ranged from 17 to 64 years, with a median age of 36 years.

Transition should extend beyond healthcare

All groups agreed that healthy transition should extend beyond the healthcare setting. Healthcare providers specifically noted that there should be “integration of community workers to the healthcare transition team.” Young adults noted the importance of transition resources in schools, emphasizing that we should “educate high school seniors on important life skills” and “funding to make the transition to adulthood better [in schools].” Furthermore, young adult caregivers’ mentioned “not including all aspects of transition in planning [for this process] -- medical, legal, social, financial” as a key area of improvement.

Perspectives on the transition to adulthood -- differences between constituent groups

We identified notable differences between constituent groups, which were driven by a focus and emphasis on their own aspect and even level of society in their responses. First, social services providers specifically focused on the community setting, highlighting the need to develop healthy relationships in the community and expansion of current community resources. For development of healthy relationships, they mentioned “consent and healthy [peer-to-peer] relationships” and “embedded health resource centers in schools (youth are able to come and talk to professionals in schools to give warm referrals to care, healthy relationships and counseling, etc.)” For expansion of current community resources, they included “continuing access to reproductive care BUT expanding access and options,” “public transportation BUT could be better,” “de-stigmatization of mental healthcare BUT need increased access of care,” and more as examples of areas for potential expansion.

Secondly, young adults focused on large-scale themes that encompass broad aspects of society, including community/belonging, societal pressure and expectations, and the divided state of the country. For example, they highlighted “being able to depend more on the people in your community, less isolation and more communication, interactiveness between people.” Additionally, for societal pressure and expectations they mentioned that “[their families] want young adults to do better than them, to be successful.” To emphasize the divided state of the country, they mentioned “divided country, with democrats, republicans, whatever, very divided and hostile,” “use of internet can keep people in echo chambers and willing to attack others very easily,” and “all lives matter vs black lives matter.”

Third, young adult caregivers specifically focused on themes related to their own caregiving; they emphasized inclusion, advocacy, and the complex care/disability needs of adolescents. They prioritized “[the creation of a] permanent advocacy board of parents to leaders, legislators,” “specialized education and support for parents,” “third legal pathway beyond guardianship and power of attorney,” and “inclusion, people with disabilities being part of the world.”

Finally, although all groups noted that transition should extend beyond healthcare, healthcare providers heavily prioritized the healthcare setting, and focused on two specific themes within this setting. They encouraged young adults to be their own healthcare advocates, emphasizing “patient self-efficacy” and “empowerment,” with young adults “advocating for their healthcare needs” and “understanding the disease process.” They also reflected on their feelings of satisfaction and failure with their jobs as healthcare providers who care for young adults, highlighting their fears of “loss of relationship with the patient,” “loss to follow-up,” “unwanted outcomes,” and “angry/unsatisfied patients.”

Discussion

Using the novel Transition Design methodology we collected responses from young adults, young adult caregivers, healthcare providers, and social services providers to identify similarities and differences in how each group would tackle the challenges surrounding transition to adulthood. Our methodology allowed for a unique, ground-breaking analysis of transition to adulthood that considered multiple perspectives simultaneously, therefore yielding a holistic, compare-and-contrast approach to this process. This holistic approach revealed similarities and differences across constituent groups in their approach to analyzing and improving the transition to adulthood -- young adults were focused on large-scale societal themes and identifying structural changes across societal domains, social services providers were focused on improving access to current resources in the community, young adult caregivers were focused on their role in their child’s life as they reached adulthood, and healthcare providers were narrowly focused on the health and wellness domain.

Research and clinical implications

Our results align with prior research that has suggested that young people prioritize overarching societal changes to address issues. For example, many young people report that they are invested in the large-scale issue of climate change, and some of them are choosing to be child-free in the face of this threat.16 A 2022 review paper identified that critical consciousness, defined as the process of analyzing oppressive structural forces in society and developing a response to these forces, can be a protective factor for young adults’ wellbeing.17 The process of critical consciousness is similar to our adaptation of the Transition Design methodology, as the methodology itself focuses on structural forces. More research is clearly needed in this area to further define how young adults see both transition to adulthood and broader problems in our society from a birds-eye view, and how these viewpoints may improve their well-being and sense of identity.

Social services providers prioritized improving current resources that already exist in society, as opposed to large-scale structural changes. They focused heavily on the environmental domain of society in activities 5 and 6, specifically in terms of improving resources for living a healthy lifestyle with basic needs met. There is limited prior research regarding the perspectives of social services providers on transition to adulthood, a further reason why our study is novel. Yet, this type of community-oriented thinking is not entirely new; one study with migrant youth suggested that these type of providers are focused on the importance of community resources to combat loneliness for young people, and improving access to these resources.18

For healthcare providers and young adult caregivers, their respective prioritizations of their own role in healthcare and caregiving is consistent with prior research. Many studies have highlighted healthcare providers’ attempts to improve transition in the healthcare space.19, 20, 21 However, our study is unique in that healthcare providers’ responses focused on the health and wellness domain within the context of other societal domains at play simultaneously. In this holistic context our providers highlighted the importance of an interdisciplinary team of people for a smooth transition from pediatric to adult medical care. Similarly, in past studies young adult caregivers have highlighted their desire to advocate for their child and promote a favorable healthcare transition.22, 23 Our study explores the perspectives of young adult caregivers beyond the healthcare setting, and also underscores their desire for clarity and understanding of the legal components of transition, as well as the social and health components.

In terms of potential clinical implications, as previously discussed throughout this paper our project included an investigation into the healthcare domain of transition to adulthood, but also addressed the broader social components of the transition process. The overall goal of utilizing Transition Design as a methodology was to compile ideas from those invested in the process about how to improve transition both within and outside of the healthcare setting. The majority of current research designed to improve transition from pediatric to adult medical care within the clinic focuses on objective, quantifiable metrics of transition care activities and how these can better serve adolescents.24, 25 Although these studies are crucial to the development of a more streamlined method for the transition process across providers, our study provides an alternative, more holistic perspective which may be useful to healthcare providers in a myriad of ways. For example, healthcare providers may consider adding information for social and community resources to the transition process for patients who may benefit from these services. Additionally, healthcare providers may consider asking patients who their support system is within their personal community during the transition handoff, as our project demonstrates that these social supports can greatly impact transition. In order to legitimize these contributions in the healthcare space, more research about the interleaving of the social aspects of transition with the healthcare aspects is certainly needed.

Limitations of study

Our study design and implementation carry a few key limitations which are important to address in this paper. First, as discussed in the previous paragraph, young adult caregivers were focused specifically on the caregiving setting, but this may have been because many of our young adult caregiver participants were parents of children with complex healthcare needs. We did not specifically capture the percentage of this characteristic in our demographics questionnaire, but a large portion of the young adult caregiver discussions were focused on their children with these complex needs. Future studies should consider more diverse perspectives from young adult caregivers. Additionally, our study yielded a small sample size that was mostly women, specifically White women, and our sample was generated using snowballing sampling as opposed to random sampling. Furthermore, all participants were recruited from one geographic area. Altogether, the recruitment method and characteristics of our participants decrease the generalizability of our results significantly.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study utilized the Transition Design methodology to understand key differences across four constituent groups in their approach around transitioning to adulthood. Participant groups both agreed with and expanded upon prior research in these areas depending on the values of the constituent group, and overall identified a lack of research about transition across broad societal domains. Clinicians within the healthcare system may benefit from applying some of these insights to the transition from pediatric to adulthood medical care. Future research using this holistic methodology is needed with a larger and more diverse sample size to further generate information about how to improve transition to adulthood for all those who are involved with this process.
