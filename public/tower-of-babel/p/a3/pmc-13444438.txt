Synthesizing lumbar computed tomography myelography from plain computed tomography images using a cycle-consistent generative adversarial network
Authors: Ryo Itoga, Terufumi Kokabu, Koji Kato, Yoshinori Hyugaji, Jun Wakasa, Yasushi Yanagibashi, Takahiko Hyakumachi, Yoshihiro Hojo, Ken Nagahama, Masahiro Kanayama, Ryo Fujita, Tomohiro Shimizu, Tsutomu Endo, Takashi Ohnishi, Daisuke Ukeba, Hideki Sudo, Katsuhisa Yamada, Norimasa Iwasaki

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMagnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for diagnosing lumbar canal stenosis (LCS), but it may be contraindicated in patients with implants or claustrophobia. Computed tomographic myelography (CTM) is an alternative imaging modality but requires an invasive procedure. This study aimed to generate synthetic CTM images from plain CT using a modified Cycle GAN and evaluate their diagnostic reliability.MethodsThe training dataset included 111 patients from 3 hospitals, comprising 21,034 plain CT and 21,062 CTM images. The external test dataset included 60 patients from another hospital who underwent plain lumbar CT and MRI, including 20 patients each with LCS, lumbar disc herniation, and no spinal disease. A modified Cycle GAN incorporating a Convolutional Block Attention Module and spectral normalization was trained to translate plain CT into synthetic CTM. Quantitative performance was assessed using Intersection over Union, F1 score, Structural Similarity Index Measure, and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio. Two spine surgeons independently graded stenosis on synthetic CTM and MRI using the Lee staging system. Intrarater reliability, inter-rater reliability, and agreement between synthetic CTM and MRI were evaluated using weighted kappa coefficients.ResultsFor spinal canal enhancement, the modified Cycle GAN achieved a mean Intersection over Union of 0.70 and F1 score of 0.80. Bone structures showed a mean Intersection over Union of 0.89 and F1 score of 0.94. Surrounding tissues demonstrated a Structural Similarity Index Measure of 0.91 and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio of 30 dB. Synthetic CTM showed almost perfect intrarater reliability and substantial inter-rater reliability. Agreement between synthetic CTM and MRI was substantial.ConclusionsSynthetic CTM generated from plain CT demonstrated acceptable quantitative accuracy and clinical reliability for grading LCS. Although spatial overlap was imperfect and nerve-root depiction was not validated, this method may offer a noninvasive diagnostic alternative for patients ineligible for MRI.Keywords: Generative adversarial network, Cycle GAN, CT myelography, Lumbar spine, Lumbar canal stenosis, Plain CT

Introduction

Lumbar canal stenosis (LCS), a common degenerative spinal disease, presents with intermittent claudication from the compression of nerve tissue and blood vessels in the spinal canal [1]. Although MRI is the gold standard for diagnosis [2], some patients have difficulty undergoing MRI because of claustrophobia or implanted devices such as cardiac pacemakers. Computed tomographic myelography (CTM), performed after the administration of a contrast medium into the subarachnoid space, serves as an alternative and is superior to MRI for assessing dynamic factors and is equivalent to MRI for measuring the spinal canal area [[3], [4], [5]]. However, CTM is invasive and relatively contraindicated in patients allergic to contrast medium or with renal insufficiency. In such cases, plain CT is used despite its lower diagnostic accuracy than MRI and CTM [6,7].

Cycle-consistent generative adversarial network (Cycle GAN) is a type of image-to-image translation model [8] that can learn the features of images with similar shapes in 2 domains and cross-convert between them, for example, between horse and zebra images [8]. In the field of spine imaging, Roberts et al. reported an algorithm for translating lumbar MRI images into synthetic lumbar CT images using modified cycle GAN models [9]. Conversely, Gotoh et al. [10] generated synthetic lumbar MRI images from lumbar CT images. To our knowledge, few studies have applied GANs to lumbar spine imaging [[11], [12], [13], [14]], and none have reported the generation of synthetic CTM from plain CT using a cycle GAN.

We hypothesized that the cycle GAN could generate a synthetic CTM sufficient for diagnosing lumbar diseases using plain CT. This study aimed to generate a synthetic CTM from plain CT using a cycle GAN and modified versions, and to validate it with quantitative and qualitative assessments. If successful, this approach may offer a less invasive diagnostic modality for patient's ineligible for MRI and reduce the procedural burden for CTM.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval No.: 023-0038). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective use of images obtained in routine practice.

We included 122 patients who underwent plain lumbar CT or CTM scanning at 3 hospitals between February 2024 and April 2025 (Fig. 1). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of lumbar surgery with instrumentation, (2) infection such as spondylodiscitis, (3) trauma including vertebral fractures in the older adults, and (4) spinal deformity with scoliosis >20° or after a vertebral fracture (Fig. 1). Data from 111 patients were used to train the cycle GAN models (Table 1). Thin-slice axial CT and CTM images between T12 and the sacrum were extracted. The unenhanced CTM images, such as those caudal to severe stenosis, were excluded. The cycle GAN models were trained using 21,034 plain CT images and 21,062 CTM images that were processed as input data.

Overall flow of this study. A total of 42,096 images were used to train the cycle GAN models. Three variants of the cycle GAN were employed: (1) cycle GAN without identity loss, (2) cycle GAN with identity loss, and (3) cycle GAN with modification. CT images from 60 patients, who were not included in the training data, were converted into synthetic CTM images using each model. Synthetic CTM images were evaluated quantitatively and qualitatively. CTM, computed tomographic myelography; Cycle GAN, cycle-consistent generative adversarial network.

To train and evaluate the models, the original Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files were processed as follows: the thin-slice axial images from CT and CT myelography (CTM) were extracted and converted from the original DICOM format (512 × 512 pixels) into Joint Photographic Experts Group (JPEG) files after anonymization. The window level and width were set to 400 and 1,000, respectively. The images were then cropped to include the margins of the lumbar vertebral body, transverse processes, and spinous processes, and then resized (256 × 256 pixels) (Supplementary Fig. A.1). During training, random scaling between 1.0 and 286/256 and random horizontal flipping were applied as data augmentation.

To assess the performance of the trained models, an external test dataset was prepared from 60 additional individuals who underwent plain lumbar CT and MRI at another hospital between June 2024 and March 2025 (Table 1). To avoid overestimating the model’s performance due to class imbalance from an excessive number of normal levels, a single intervertebral level was extracted from each patient’s dataset to evaluate the synthetic CTM images (Fig. 2). The external test cohort consisted of 20 patients each with LCS, lumbar disc herniation (LDH), or without disease (Table 1). The LDH group consisted of subligamentous extrusion and transligamentous extrusion types. In the LCS and LDH groups, we extracted the most stenotic level. For the normal level, we randomly and uniformly selected intervertebral levels across the lumbar spine. The details of the CT scan parameters are shown in Table 2.

Cycle GAN models and modified cycle GAN model (Cycle GANMOD)

The objective of this study was to synthesize CTM-like images from plain CT, rather than simply to segment the spinal canal. Although segmentation-based models can directly delineate the spinal canal, they produce label maps and do not generate images resembling CT myelography with intrathecal contrast enhancement. Therefore, a segmentation-only approach was not aligned with the primary aim of this study. Supervised image-to-image translation methods, such as Pix2Pix, require pixel-wise paired source and target images. However, perfectly paired plain CT and CTM images are difficult to obtain because of differences in patient positioning, field of view, slice location and spinal alignment. For this reason, we selected a Cycle GAN-based approach, which enables unpaired image-to-image translation between the plain CT and CTM domains.

The Cycle GAN framework consisted of 2 generators and 2 discriminators. The first generator translated plain CT images into synthetic CTM images, whereas the second generator translated CTM images back into CT-like images. The CTM-domain discriminator distinguished real CTM images from synthetic CTM images, and the CT-domain discriminator distinguished real CT images from reconstructed CT-like images [8].

We evaluated 3 models to examine the contribution of specific architectural and loss-function modifications. The original Cycle GAN was used as the baseline model. Cycle GANIL incorporated identity loss to test whether preservation of domain-specific image characteristics could reduce unnecessary changes in nontarget anatomical regions. Cycle GANMOD incorporated Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) [15] in the generator to enhance attention to informative canal-related features and spectral normalization [16] in the discriminators to stabilize adversarial training and reduce training instability. CBAM improves the ability of the generator to highlight the lumbar canal areas and preserve the details of other areas during image translation [15]. Spectral normalization helps prevent common training failures, such as mode collapse, and stabilizes the adversarial training process [16]. Details are provided in Appendix A.

Quantitative assessment utilizing objective parameters

For objective quantitative evaluation, ground-truth segmentation of the spinal canal was performed. Due to the inherent difficulty in achieving pixel-perfect alignment between plain CT and real CTM images, quantitative evaluation was performed by marking the spinal canal on CT/MRI fusion images [17], which shared the same field of view for CT and MRI, resulting in “marked spinal canal CT” (mscCT) images (Fig. 3A). These mscCT images were aligned with the synthetic CTM images because the synthetic CTM images were generated using the same CT images as those used for CT/MRI fusion.

Schematic of image preparation for quantitative evaluation. (A) An mscCT image was created by segmenting the spinal canal in the CT/MRI fusion image. (B) The spinal canal areas from both the synthetic CTM and mscCT were binarized and evaluated using overlap-based metrics. (C) The surrounding tissues (excluding the spinal canal) were compared using SSIM and PSNR. (D) Bone areas were similarly binarized and evaluated using overlap-based metrics. mscCT, marked spinal canal CT; IoU, Intersection over Union; SSIM, Structural Similarity Index Measure; PSNR, Peak Signal to Noise Ratio; Cycle GAN, cycle-consistent generative adversarial network.

To assess the consistency of the spinal canal and other areas between the synthetic CTM and mscCT images, the enhanced or marked spinal canal and other areas were separated (Fig. 3B, C). Binarized images of the spinal canal areas and bone edges were obtained by adjusting the brightness of the separated images using image processing software (Ralpha Image Resizer ver.170111, Nilposoft) (Fig. 3D).

Precision, recall, intersection over union (IoU), and F1 score were used as metrics to evaluate the overlap between these binary images. IoU measures the spatial overlap between the generated and reference regions, and the F1 score summarizes the balance between precision and recall. To assess the similarity of areas other than the spinal canal between the synthetic CTM and mscCT images, the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) [18] and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) were calculated. SSIM evaluates the similarity of image structure, contrast, and brightness, whereas PSNR reflects pixel-level image fidelity. Higher values indicate better agreement or greater image similarity for each metric. A detailed description of each parameter is provided in Appendix A.

Qualitative assessment for the spinal canal by the spine specialists

To compare the diagnostic reliability of synthetic CTM with that of MRI, we assessed the intra and inter-rater reliability using weighted kappa coefficients. Two board-certified orthopedic spine surgeons (raters 1 and 2), with 11 and 15 years of clinical experience, respectively, independently graded the stenosis on the synthetic CTM and corresponding MRI images using the Lee staging system [19]. Each observer was evaluated twice in separate sessions, with the order of the intervertebral levels randomized for each session to minimize recall bias.

Intrarater reliability was calculated for each observer to assess the consistency of their judgments under the following 3 conditions: (1) between their 2 separate MRI readings, (2) between their 2 separate synthetic CTM readings, and (3) between their MRI and synthetic CTM readings. Inter-rater reliability was calculated between the 2 observers to assess the agreement for each imaging modality separately.

Reconstruction of synthetic CTM images

For a detailed assessment of the lumbar spine, multiplanar (sagittal and coronal) and 3-dimensional (3D) reconstructions are essential. Accordingly, these reconstructed images were generated from the synthetic CTM data.

We generated reconstructed images using a synthetic CTM with a custom Python script to convert the synthetic CTM images back into Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format, as shown in Appendix A.

Statistical analysis

Weighted kappa coefficients (κ) were interpreted as follows: Poor (<0.00), Slight (0.00–0.20), Fair (0.21–0.40), Moderate (0.41–0.60), Substantial (0.61–0.80), and Almost Perfect (0.81–1.00) [20].

We calculated the required sample size based on the methodology described by Bujang and Baharum [21]. Specifically, to demonstrate that the agreement is superior to the moderate level, we set the null hypothesis at k0=0.41. Based on the previous study by Lee et al. [19], we set the expected target agreement at k1=0.70. With a stricter power of 0.90 and a 2-sided significance level of 0.05, the required sample size for a 4-category scale was calculated to be 49. To ensure a sufficient evaluation of the diagnostic agreement, we included 60 cases in this study.

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 16 (IBM Corp.) was used for data analysis, and statistical significance was defined as p < .05.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that a synthetic CTM was successfully generated for the first-time using cycle GAN. Most synthetic CTM images showed spinal canal enhancement, and a few images showed an unenhanced spinal canal. Accuracy was confirmed using precision, recall, IoU, and F1 scores against mscCT images. Our modified cycle GAN, cycle GANMOD, performed best among the 3 models in enhancing the spinal canal, with mean IoU and F1 scores of 0.70 and 0.80, respectively, suggesting relatively good spinal canal enhancement, given the IoU and F1 score thresholds of 0.5 and 0.7, respectively [22,23]. The performance was consistent across all disease groups.

To our knowledge, no study has assessed the spinal canal area using the quantitative parameters of image conversion using deep learning methods.

Two studies verified whether synthetic MRI could help radiologists or spine surgeons evaluate the spinal canal and its stenoses [11,12]. One study reported that 2 radiologists assessed the similarity of the disc signal, degree of disc protrusion, muscle, fat tissue, facet joint signal, degree of stenosis, thecal sac, bone, and overall image similarity using percentages. The highest similarity features were muscle (mean 87.5%±6.3) and fat tissue (mean 86.3%±12.5), and the lowest similarity features were disc signal (mean 75.5%±10.0) and thecal sac (mean 76.7%±14.9) [11]. Another study reported that synthetic MRI was inferior to original MRI in evaluating the contrast, noise, artifacts, and anatomical structures [12].

In this study, we used a clinically practical grading system (Lee staging system [19]) to assess the degree of stenosis on synthetic CTM images. Compared with previous studies [11,12], our synthetic CTM demonstrated robust clinical performance, which was validated by assessing inter and intrarater reliability. Our study demonstrates that synthetic CTM is a reliable tool for grading spinal stenosis, exhibiting almost perfect intrarater reliability (κ= 0.89–0.94) and substantial inter-rater reliability (κ= 0.71–0.78). Agreement between CTM and MRI was substantial when assessed by the same rater (κ= 0.66–0.72). This level of agreement, while clinically significant, was lower than the intrarater reliability observed for either modality alone. This discrepancy likely reflects the different diagnostic characteristics of the 2 modalities rather than random errors.

For tissues other than the spinal canal, the SSIM and PSNR, used as similarity indicators, were 0.91 and 30, respectively, in cycle GANMOD. Gotoh et al. demonstrated the translation of plain lumbar CT images to MRI images using Pix2Pix, a GAN-based model, reporting SSIM and PSNR values of 0.595 ± 0.045 and 18.41 ± 2.056 at epoch 500 [10]. Hong et al. reported algorithms for lumbar CT-to-MRI translation using an unsupervised generative attentional network with adaptive layer instance normalization (U-GAT-IT), with PSNR and SSIM values of 15.987 ± 11.039 and 0.518 ± 0.042, respectively [12].

Our results showed higher PSNR and SSIM than those reported in other studies, indicating that the synthetic CTM images showed greater similarity to the original plain CT images in the bone, muscle, and vessel areas than the similarity between synthetic and original MRI images. This suggests that the GAN model may offer superior transformation performance for regional features than global image transformation. The structural integrity of tissues, including bone, was supported by high IoU and F1 scores of 0.89 and 0.94, respectively.

A remarkable strength of our study was its ability to reconstruct multiplanar and 3D images from synthetic CTM files, although the image quality was inferior to that of the native image. By developing a script to convert the generated images back into the DICOM format while embedding the original spatial coordinates, we demonstrated their compatibility with standard medical imaging systems. This allows for seamless integration into existing clinical workflows, including Picture Archiving and Communication Systems, surgical navigation systems, and preoperative planning software.

The LDH group had a lower IoU than the other 2 groups, likely due to the simultaneous enhancement of the herniated tissue. One of the causes is the limited number of actual CTM images with herniation during the training of the cycle GAN models. Although the performances of the original cycle GAN and cycle GANIL were evaluated, both models exhibited a tendency similar to that of the cycle GANMOD, showing lower IoU values in the LDH group.

The performance at L5/S1 was lower than that at the other lumbar levels. We attribute this to the distinct anatomical configuration of the L5/S1 level compared with other vertebral levels. Consequently, the proportion of training data representing this morphology was smaller than that for the L1/2/3/4/5 levels, making it more challenging for the model to learn the dural sac enhancement features at this junction. Therefore, the current model should be used with particular caution at L5/S1, and additional training data focused on the lumbosacral junction will be required to improve reliability at this level.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. A particularly important limitation is that the model did not reliably reproduce intradural nerve-root structures. These structures were not validated against real CTM and may represent hallucinated image features generated by the GAN. Such findings should not be used for nerve-root assessment. This limitation is clinically important because hallucinated intradural nerve-root-like structures may lead to diagnostic errors. Therefore, synthetic CTM should be interpreted only as AI-generated images focused on dural sac and central canal assessment at this time.

Second, patients with spinal instrumentation and those with spinal deformity were excluded. The cycle GAN models may not accurately generate synthetic CTM for these patients and would require training with a larger dataset of their images. The model trained on cases with spinal implants would be helpful in clinical situations, where MRI may have artifacts and not provide sufficient images [24].

Third, the external test cohort included 60 patients, with 20 patients in each diagnostic subgroup. Although this sample size exceeded the calculated requirement for the kappa-based agreement analysis, it remains limited for disease-specific validation. In addition, only 2 spine surgeons participated in the qualitative assessment. Future studies should include a larger multicenter cohort, more patients in each disease subgroup, and additional blinded readers to more robustly evaluate inter-reader variability and generalizability.

Fourth, we focused on generating and evaluating the lumbar spine and the lumbosacral junction; therefore, other levels, including the thoracolumbar junction, were excluded. Finally, the evaluation of nerve root and foraminal stenosis was not performed in this study. Although some synthetic CTM images appeared to delineate the nerve roots, verifying their diagnostic accuracy was beyond the scope of this initial investigation, which focused primarily on central canal stenosis. Additionally, establishing a consistent ground truth for foraminal stenosis using real CTM can be challenging because of insufficient contrast filling within the nerve root sheaths.

Despite these limitations, the main clinical relevance of synthetic CTM lies in its ability to clearly delineate the dural sac, which plain CT cannot adequately distinguish from surrounding soft tissues because of its low contrast resolution. Therefore, synthetic CTM has a potential to be a valuable noninvasive surrogate for assessing canal patency in patients who are contraindicated for MRI, filling the gap between plain CT and real CTM. In addition, if CTM-like information could be extracted from plain CT images that have already been obtained, it could potentially reduce the need for intrathecal contrast administration, additional procedures, cost, and procedural burden in selected patients.

Conclusion

We developed and validated a modified cycle GAN algorithm that generates synthetic CTM from plain CT. Although the model is not sufficient for clinical use yet, the synthetic CTM demonstrated acceptable quantitative accuracy and substantial reliability, holding significant potential as a noninvasive diagnostic tool for patients unable to undergo MRI. In selected patients for whom CTM is being considered, this method could reduce the procedural burden for CTM. This potential benefit remains investigational and requires further validation.
