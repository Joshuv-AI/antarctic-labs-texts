Lung Adenocarcinoma Associated With a Horseshoe Intralobar Lung Sequestration
Authors: Shintaro Yokoyama, Hiromi Oku, Yuichiro Ueta, Yusuke Uchida, Toshihiro Hashiguchi, Masaki Kashihara, Yasuhiro Terazaki, Tomonori Chikasue, Kiminori Fujimoto, Masahiro Mitsuoka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHorseshoe intralobar lung sequestration, an extremely rare morphology of the sequestrated lung expanding toward the contralateral thorax, remains insufficiently described. We report a case of an 80-year-old man with lung cancer in the left lower lobe combined with a horseshoe lung sequestration originating from the right lower lobe. The decision of surgical procedure was challenging because bilateral lower lobectomy was considered intolerable for the patient owing to his advanced age and comorbidities. We decided to perform surgery targeting only the lung cancer, because the patient had no history of hemoptysis or pneumonia, resulting in a favorable postoperative course.

Intralobar pulmonary sequestration is a pulmonary sequestration subset and a rare anomaly defined as an aberrant lung tissue devoid of communication with the tracheobronchial tree that receives blood supply from anomalous systemic arteries.1 It shares the same visceral pleura with healthy lung tissue and is generally indicated for surgical resection because of the risks of repeated hemoptysis and pulmonary infection.2

A horseshoe intralobar lung sequestration is a further uncommon form demonstrating a lung parenchyma isthmus across the mediastinum by sequestrated lung protrusion toward the contralateral thorax.3 Although the incidence of this extremely rare entity remains unclear, the medical and research community should be aware of this finding, because it could potentially be confused with a horseshoe lung, a congenital malformation that frequently accompanies scimitar syndrome and hypoplasia of the right lung.4, 5, 6

Herein, we present the surgical case of a patient with lung cancer incidentally diagnosed with a horseshoe intralobar lung sequestration. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient. This study was approved by the Kurume University Hospital Institutional Review Board (approval number 2025-022, approved on June 11, 2025).

An 80-year-old man, undergoing hemodialysis for chronic renal failure without clinical symptoms, including hemoptysis, was diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma in the left lower lobe. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) revealed a solid 4.3-cm mass and a suspicious satellite nodule in the same lobe. Significant lymph node enlargement or metastatic lesions were not noted on systemic contrast-enhanced CT, including the brain. Enhanced magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was waived to avoid nephrogenic systemic fibrosis caused by gadolinium-based contrast agents, given the patient’s reduced kidney function. Transbronchial biopsy using endobronchial ultrasound was also omitted because contrast-enhanced CT did not show any lymph node enlargement, and positron emission tomography/CT did not demonstrate significant uptake of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose in the mediastinal lymph nodes. Thus, the patient was clinically diagnosed with T3 N0 M0 lung adenocarcinoma.

Remarkably, bilateral supradiaphragmatic masses could be observed along the mediastinum, connected by a parenchymatous bridge between the esophagus and descending aorta (Figure 1A). Two aberrant arteries arising from the descending aorta supplied each mass, and the veins drained into the right lower pulmonary vein (Figures 1B–D). Consequently, the patient was diagnosed with intralobar lung sequestration of the right lung expanding toward the contralateral thorax.

(D) Three-dimensional reconstruction clearly demonstrates aberrant arteries and draining veins (red and white arrows, respectively).

Cardiac and pulmonary function was well preserved without any abnormality. Nevertheless, considering the patient’s lack of hemoptysis and history of pneumonia, along with potential intolerance to extensive procedures given his advanced age and comorbidities, we opted for surgery targeting only the lung cancer.

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery using 3 access ports through the left thoracic cavity was performed. During the dissection of adhesions, the sequestrated lung was observed to be entirely covered by the pleura, being anatomically independent from the left lower lobe (Figure 2) and continuous with the left mediastinal pleura, consistent with right-sided intralobar lung sequestration protruding into the left thorax. Left lower lobectomy and mediastinal lymph node dissection were then conventionally performed.

Intraoperative findings. The sequestrated lung accompanied an aberrant artery from the aorta (red arrow) and draining vein (white arrow).

The patient’s postoperative respiratory condition was satisfactory, and oxygen therapy was discontinued on the first postoperative day. Pleuritis developed 1 week later, necessitating chest drain reinsertion. He subsequently recovered well and was discharged on postoperative day 16. The tumor was pathologically diagnosed as a solid adenocarcinoma with a 4.0-cm diameter. No malignancy was detected in the small nodule that had been suspected as a separate tumor preoperatively, or in the lymph nodes, resulting in a final diagnosis of pT2a N0 M0. Tuberculosis developed 4 months later in the residual left upper lobe, but the patient recovered without respiratory failure.

Comment

Intralobar lung sequestration typically lacks anomalous venous drainage, even with extension to a horseshoe variant.5 In contrast, horseshoe lung is a rare congenital malformation often associated with scimitar syndrome, characterized by anomalous drainage into systemic veins.6 Despite their similar morphologies, these entities exhibit distinct developmental mechanisms and should be differentiated.

This case was diagnosed as a horseshoe lung sequestration originating from the right lower lobe, based on the following findings. First, arteries diverging from the aorta were identified. Second, venous drainage flowed exclusively into the right inferior pulmonary vein without anomalous systemic veins. Finally, the pleura of the sequestrated lung was independent from that of the left lower lobe. Although combined left extralobar and right intralobar sequestrations should be discriminated, the overall findings described earlier supported a diagnosis of horseshoe intralobar lung sequestration.

Surgical resection is generally accepted as a treatment of choice for intralobar sequestration, given its tendency to develop uncontrollable hemoptysis and pulmonary infections.2 The respiratory function could be preserved when surgical resection is confined to the sequestrated lung. However, additional lobectomy adjacent to the sequestrated lung is often required based on extensive inflammatory infiltration.7,8

In this case, additional division of aberrant arteries originating from the aorta and partial resection of the horseshoe sequestrated lung on the left side might have been considered; however, the sequestrated lung was very hard. Because the patient had life-threatening, operable lung adenocarcinoma with no history of hemoptysis or pneumonia, surgery was thus limited to the cancer, allowing preservation of respiratory function by avoiding right lower lobectomy. This approach enabled the patient to recover from postoperative pleuritis and tuberculosis without respiratory failure.

Although extremely rare, for patients with concurrent lung cancer and pulmonary sequestration who might not tolerate dual operations, cancer-only resection with sequestrated lung preservation could offer a viable treatment strategy for improved survival.
