Health implications of extreme climate change: Evidence from selected coastal Bangladesh
Authors: Al Jamal Mustafa Shindaini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBangladesh’s coastal communities are considered one of the most climate-vulnerable populations in the world, since they are regularly exposed to natural calamities, tidal waves, and saline incursion, which directly affects the health of the population. The current study examined the health impacts of an extreme weather event and environmental changes in Sarankhola Upazila, Bagerhat District, which is a disaster-prone coastal region of Bangladesh. The aim of the research was to outline key pathways by which climate stressors impact physical and mental health outcomes and to study strategies of community adaptation.MethodsA mixed-methods design was used, combining a quantitative household survey (n=100) with qualitative interviews and focus-group discussions with key informants. Demographic and self-reported data on exposure to climate hazards, disease prevalence, and coping mechanisms were collected. Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 26, while qualitative data were examined through thematic coding techniques.Results72% of the respondents showed increased disease prevalence after cyclones, most of them being waterborne diseases (55%) and dermatological diseases (37%). About 68% of respondents reported the loss of access to safe drinking water, and 64% realized an increase in food insecurity, which could be explained by salinity invasion. There was a high positive correlation between economic vulnerability and housing instability and reported health deterioration (r = 0.71; p <0.01). Qualitative stories described stressfulness, anxiety, and a disproportionate role of women caregivers in the disaster recovery phases.ConclusionsExtreme climatic events significantly increase health risks in Bangladesh along the coastal areas by acting in combination with environmental, social, and economic influences. Increasing the capacity of community-based adaptation, providing reliable access to potable water and health care, and instilling climate resiliency into public health policy are urgent actions needed to protect vulnerable communities in disaster-prone regions.Keywords: Climate change, Coastal Bangladesh, Public health, Salinity intrusion, Vulnerability, Community resilience

Introduction

Climate change creates a significant threat to world health, especially in densely populated low-lying areas. The health hazards associated with climate change are intricate, multifaceted, and intertwined with wider ecological, economic, and social determinants of health [1]. Recent studies reveal that almost 3.6 billion individuals inhabit areas particularly vulnerable to the detrimental effects of climate change [2]. In 2023 the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that from 2030 to 2050, climate change will result in an additional 250,000 fatalities per year, attributable to undernutrition, malaria, diarrheal illnesses, and heat stress alone [3]. According to the global Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT), the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as cyclones and tidal surges have increased globally, placing unprecedented strain on public health infrastructure [4].

For developing nations such as Bangladesh, which has consistently ranked at the forefront of the climate change risk index, the consequences are especially severe. The southern coastal parts of the country, inhabited by approximately 36 million individuals, are particularly susceptible due to their geographical vulnerability and inhabitants' significant reliance on environmental resources [5]. Previous literature emphasizes that seasonal disasters, such as Cyclone ‘Bulbul’ (November 2019) and ‘Amphan’ (2020), not only destroy physical infrastructure but also compromise sanitation systems, leading to spikes in waterborne, respiratory, and dermatological infections [6]. Furthermore, saltwater intrusion in coastal soil and water has been linked to increased hypertension and reproductive health issues, particularly among women and the elderly [7].

Despite these growing threats, studies on climate-health connections remain relatively scarce in the Global South compared to affluent nations. Existing research often treats climate change through narrative reviews, leaving a gap in empirical, mixed-methods data that integrates biological, social, and psychological health dimensions [8]. In Bangladesh, escalating temperatures and rising sea levels compound existing socioeconomic issues like poverty, worsening public health outcomes. Studies indicate that sociocultural norms and limited education often restrict women’s access to emergency healthcare, while recurrent occurrences of diarrhea and pneumonia are frequently reported among malnourished populations in impacted regions [[9], [10], [11]].

This study aims to investigate the escalating urgency and disparate effects of climate-induced health hazards in the deltaic coastal region of Sarankhola Upazila (administrative district). By utilizing a mixed-methods design, it seeks to deliver empirical insights into health consequences to guide community-oriented adaptation measures. We hypothesize that climate variability significantly heightens health vulnerabilities among coastal populations through combined environmental, social, and economic pathways.

Methodology

Study area and design

This study was conducted in Sarankhola Upazila, located in the Bagerhat district of coastal Bangladesh. This region was selected due to its high vulnerability to climate-induced disasters such as cyclones, tidal surges, and salinity intrusion. A mixed-methods research design (concurrent triangulation) was employed to integrate quantitative data with qualitative insights. This approach allows for the validation of statistical patterns through lived experiences, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of the climate-health nexus.

Sampling and participant selection

For the quantitative parts of the study, a simple random sampling technique was used to select households. Based on the local population density, a sample of 100 respondents were interviewed (at a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error). The respondents were primarily heads of households to ensure a reliable overview of family health history. The qualitative participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure diverse and expert perspectives:

Key Informant Interviews (KIIs): Ten (n=10) informants were selected, including local healthcare providers, government health officers, and community leaders who have witnessed long-term climatic shifts in the region.

Focus Group Discussions (FGDs): Five (n=5) FGDs were conducted (3 with men and 2 with women) to capture gendered perspectives on health and disaster recovery. Each group consisted of 6–8 participants.

Data collection procedures

Primary data collection was carried out through face-to-face interviews using a pre-tested semi-structured questionnaire (see Supplementary Materials). The survey captured demographic data (with age recorded in years), exposure levels to climatic hazards, and self-reported health outcomes. To ensure data quality, the surveys were administered during the post-monsoon period when the impacts of climatic stressors are most visible. Qualitative data from FGDs and KIIs were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed for thematic analysis. For the quantitative component, respondents were presented with statements about perceived climate-related risks and asked to indicate their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). Example statements included: “I and my family are at increased risk due to more frequent and intense cyclones,” “Salinity intrusion has worsened our access to safe drinking water,” and “My mental health has been negatively affected by climate-related stressors.” The framing of these statements was designed to capture respondents’ perceptions of changes attributable to climate-related stressors, rather than normal seasonal weather variation. For health outcomes, respondents were asked to identify which diseases or health conditions their household members had experienced following extreme climatic events (e.g., cyclones, flooding, salinity intrusion), selecting from a pre-defined list that included waterborne diseases (e.g., diarrhea, cholera), dermatological infections, respiratory distress, hypertension, and mental health conditions such as anxiety and stress. Respondents could also indicate additional health outcomes not on the list. The full questionnaire, including all statements and response options, is provided in Supplementary Materials. For the qualitative component, FGDs and KIIs were guided by a set of open-ended questions covering the following key topics: (1) perceived changes in climate patterns and their effects on daily life and health; (2) experiences with specific climate-related events (cyclones, flooding, salinity intrusion) and resulting health consequences; (3) gendered dimensions of disaster recovery and access to healthcare; (4) coping strategies employed by households and communities; and (5) perceived gaps in health services and infrastructure during and after extreme events. The full discussion guides for FGDs and KIIs are included in Supplementary Materials.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS (Version 26.0). Descriptive statistics summarized the demographic profile, while cross-tabulations were used for a priori analysis of gender and age-based health vulnerabilities. A 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) was utilized to measure perceived impacts. Qualitative data were analyzed using manual thematic coding, where recurring patterns were identified and triangulated with quantitative results to ensure integration.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the approval from the ethical committee of the Bangladesh University of Professionals (BUP). Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed written/verbal consent was obtained from all participants prior to the interviews. To maintain anonymity, all personal identifiers were redacted during data processing, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without any negative consequences.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study surveyed 100 participants (referred to as household heads) to understand the socio-demographic profile of the coastal population in Sarankhola. The age of respondents, recorded in years, ranged from 20 to 75. The largest proportions were observed in the 20–30 years (28%) and 61–75 years (25%) age brackets. In terms of gender distribution, 70% were male and 30% were female. Educational attainment varied, with 40% having no formal education, 35% completing primary education, and 25% reaching secondary or higher levels. The majority of respondents were involved in climate-dependent livelihoods such as fishing and small-scale agriculture.

Climate change awareness and perceived impacts

Respondents reported high levels of awareness regarding climatic shifts. The perceived impacts were measured using a 5-point Likert scale, where participants rated various stressors from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Health outcomes

Waterborne diseases were the most prevalent health outcome reported by households (55%), followed by dermatological infections (37%) and respiratory distress (28%). A significant majority (72%) of respondents reported a surge in disease symptoms immediately following extreme climatic events like cyclones. Statistical analysis (Chi-square tests) revealed a significant association between the lack of access to safe drinking water and the incidence of diarrheal diseases (χ² = 12.4, p < 0.05). Furthermore, mean scores for perceived health stress were significantly higher among respondents living within 1 km of the coastline (Mean = 4.6, SD = 0.5) compared to those residing further inland (Table 2).

Qualitative integration (FGD and KII)

Qualitative data from five FGDs and ten KIIs corroborated the statistical findings (Table 3). Participants highlighted that disaster recovery phases are particularly stressful for women, who bear the disproportionate burden of collecting fresh water from distant sources. Themes of "persistent fear of future cyclones" and "helplessness due to saline intrusion" emerged as dominant psychological stressors. Local healthcare providers (KIIs) noted a 30% seasonal increase in skin infections and hypertension cases during periods of high salinity, which they linked to both direct exposure and psychological stress. The analysis identified key themes that explain the pathways of health vulnerability.

Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the profound and multifaceted health impacts of climate change on coastal communities in Bangladesh. The reported surge in waterborne and dermatological diseases following extreme climatic events aligns with global patterns where climate-induced disasters disrupt sanitation and safe water access. Consistent with recent literature, dermatologic manifestations such as fungal infections and salinity-induced dermatitis are increasingly prevalent in disaster-prone regions due to prolonged exposure to contaminated and salinated water [12,13].

Our results confirm that the proximity to the coastline significantly correlates with both physical health deterioration and mental health stress. The higher mean stress scores (4.62) among those living within 1 km of the coast suggest that the constant threat of livelihood loss and displacement creates a state of "climatic anxiety." This phenomenon is not unique to Bangladesh; studies undertaken in other Global South environments reveal analogous phenomena, where ecological grief and persistent stress are common among populations dependent on fragile coastal ecosystems [14].

The qualitative data provided a gendered lens into these health impacts, revealing that women face a disproportionate burden during the recovery phase. This supports the findings of previous studies where sociocultural norms and the household role of women as primary water collectors exacerbate their vulnerability to waterborne diseases and physical exhaustion [5,6]. Furthermore, the statistical association between salinity and hypertension observed in our study points toward a growing non-communicable disease crisis in coastal deltas, necessitated by the lack of alternative freshwater sources.

Study limitations

While this study provides valuable empirical insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the survey allows for the identification of associations but does not establish direct causation. Second, the study population had a significant male majority (70%), which may limit the generalizability of the findings regarding gender-specific health nuances. Third, the sample size (n=100) was restricted to one Upazila; therefore, larger multi-regional studies are required to capture the full diversity of coastal health challenges in Bangladesh. Finally, the health data were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias, particularly concerning events that occurred several months prior to the survey.

Conclusion

This research concludes that the coastal population of Sarankhola is ensnared in a compounding cycle of climatic disasters and deteriorating health. By fulfilling the research objective to map climate-health pathways, this study establishes that health vulnerabilities in coastal Bangladesh are not merely biological but are significantly exacerbated by environmental and socioeconomic determinants. The integration of quantitative data and qualitative lived experiences confirms that salinity intrusion and cyclone-induced infrastructure damage are the primary drivers of the observed surge in waterborne and dermatological diseases.

To address these challenges, a multisectoral approach is essential, as noted below:

•For government policy: The findings provide a localized evidence base for the Ministry of Health to integrate "Climate-Sensitive Health Infrastructure" into the national Delta Plan (long-term economic and resiliency strategy). This includes establishing specialized coastal clinics equipped to handle seasonal surges in salinity-linked hypertension and skin infections and investing in climate-resilient sanitation.

Addressing the health impacts of climate change in Bangladesh is no longer a future consideration but an urgent necessity for ensuring climate justice and public health security for those on the front lines of the global environmental crisis.
