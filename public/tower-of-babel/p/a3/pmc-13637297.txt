Mucormycosis caused by Rhizopus oryzae, in a patient with diabetes mellitus: a case report
Authors: A V Kolsanov, A V Lyamin, A V Kozlov, O V Zelyova, D D Ismatullin, O I Pigalova, M O Kochergin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus is a metabolic disease that serves as an independent risk factor for the development of various pathologies, including mucormycosis.Case presentationA 47-year-old male, citizen of the Russian Federation, when seeking medical assistance, presented with the following complaints: pain, swelling, and numbness of the right side of the face; difficulty in nasal breathing through the right nasal passage, with purulent nasal discharge; reduced visual acuity in the right eye; a feeling of shortness of breath; dyspnea at rest; and general weakness. Diabetes mellitus and ketoacidosis were diagnosed for the first time. A comprehensive study was conducted, including radiological imaging, laboratory, and microbiological diagnostics. A clinical diagnosis was made: mucormycosis, rhino-orbito-cerebral form (Rhizopus oryzae). The components of the patient’s treatment included surgical intervention with removal of nonviable tissues and etiotropic therapy. This clinical case ended in a fatal outcome.ConclusionTimely seeking of medical assistance, early diagnosis of the disease, prompt initiation of etiotropic therapy, and complete removal of affected tissues are key to the effectiveness of mucormycosis treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-026-06246-x.Keywords: Clinical case, Mucormycosis, Rhizopus oryzae, Paranasal sinuses, Computed tomography, Diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Mucormycosis is an infectious disease caused by fungi of the class Zygomycetes, order Mucorales. Most often, in humans, this disease is caused by Rhizopus spp., Mucor spp., and Lichtheimia spp. [1].

Rhizopus oryzae—a filamentous fungus that is widespread. There are currently increasing reports of infections in immunocompromised patients as well as in individuals without underlying pathology following a recent COVID-19 infection [2]. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of mucormycosis in Russia was 0.2 per 100,000 population per year [3, 4]. Patients after organ transplantation, individuals with neutropenia, malignant hematologic neoplasms, and uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, including those in a state of ketoacidosis, fall into the risk group. The clinical picture may deteriorate due to corticosteroid use [5].

With each passing year, the spectrum of pathogens causing mucormycosis is expanding, and Rhizopus oryzae—the most common among them––is responsible for about 70% of all cases of the disease [6]. Five main forms of mucormycosis are distinguished, depending on the localization of the pathological process: pulmonary, rhino-orbital, intestinal, cutaneous, and disseminated. [7]. The most common localization of the primary focus in patients is the paranasal sinuses (up to 95% of cases) [8].

Case presentation

A 47-year-old male, citizen of the Russian Federation, was admitted to the otorhinolaryngology department of the Clinics of the Federal State Budgetary Educational Institution of Higher Education of Samara State Medical University (SamGMU) Ministry of Health of Russia on an emergency basis with signs of an exacerbation of chronic sinusitis. During the last 14 days, the patient has experienced the following symptoms: difficulty in nasal breathing, headache and facial pain, an unpleasant odor in the nose, and discharge from the nose.

At the time of admission, he complained of pain in the right side of the face in the region of the maxillary and frontal sinuses. He noted swelling of the soft tissues in the right periorbital area and swelling of the right cheek and nasal bridge. He experienced numbness in the right half of the face and hard palate. He complained of difficulty breathing through the right side of the nose, as well as a purulent discharge from the right nasal cavity. There was reduced mobility of the right eye and decreased visual acuity in the right eye. He noted a feeling of breathlessness, dyspnea at rest that intensifies with minimal physical activity, and marked overall weakness. The patient has considered himself ill for 2 days since the onset of the aforementioned complaints. For an extended period, he used decongestants due to difficulty breathing through his nose. According to relatives, the patient had previously been diagnosed with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and cholelithiasis. Over the course of a year, the patient lost 10 kg, the blood glucose level was consistently above normal, sometimes reaching 30 mmol/l, and no correction was performed.

Clinical manifestations

Upon examination, the overall condition was assessed as moderately severe; the patient was sluggish and gave one-word answers to questions, while consciousness remained clear. The physique was asthenic, the skin is pale-pink, and cyanosis of the skin of the lower extremities was observed. On examination, meningeal signs were negative. Breathing was harsh and diminished, respiratory rate—27/min, SpO2—98%. During rhinoscopy, the mucous membrane of the right nasal cavity was cyanotic with gray-black crusts that were difficult to remove, without accompanying bleeding. In the general nasal passage, the mycelium of fungi is visualized (Figs. 1 and 2). The inferior and middle nasal turbinates on the right are atrophic and covered with crusts, with purulent discharge in the middle nasal passage. The nasal septum is deviated to the left. The mucous membrane of the left half of the nasal cavity is bright pink, the nasal turbinates are of normal size, and there is no pathological discharge. The palpebral fissures are asymmetric due to edema in the right periorbital area, eyelids, exophthalmos, and ptosis on the right; the mobility of the right eye is reduced upward and medially, and on palpation, the eyeball is tense and painful. The mucous membrane of the posterior pharyngeal wall is pink, the palatine tonsils are enlarged and fused with the palatal arches, there is no content in the crypts, the oropharynx is symmetrical, and there is no pathological discharge on the posterior pharyngeal wall. Carious changes in the teeth in the oral cavity. The mucous membrane of the hard palate to the right of the midline is cyanotic, in places yellowish (Fig. 3). On the day of admission, the body temperature was 36.8 °C; on the 3rd day of hospitalization, it increased to 37.4 °C, on the 7th day, it decreased to 36.5 °C. On day 10, the body temperature was 37.0 °C (Table 1).

Diagnostic investigations

For diagnostic search purposes, computed tomography of the brain and paranasal sinuses was performed. In the right maxillary sinus, within the cells of the ethmoidal labyrinth, and in the right sphenoidal sinus, a pathological component with a density of up to 30 Hounsfield units (HU) is identified. The bony structures of the medial wall of the orbit are thinned. The ocular muscles along the medial wall are infiltrated, including a solitary air bubble (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

CT coronary projection, soft tissue window, arrow indicates invasion of the pathological process in the orbital tissue

CT sagittal projection, bone window, arrow indicates destructive changes of the skull base in the region of the ethmoid labyrinth

Also, a computed tomography of the chest was performed, which revealed a recently recovered bilateral multisegmental interstitial pneumonia (a positive PCR test for COVID-19 was received).

Laboratory investigations confirmed leukocytosis, ketoacidosis, and hyperglycemia. Complete blood count: WBCs (white blood cells)—25.71×109, RBCs (red blood cells)—3.64×1012, PLTs (platelets)—394×109, Hb (hemoglobin)—11.5g/dl. Complete urinalysis: ketone bodies—7.4, urine glucose—28. Blood glucose level: 20.5 mmol/L (day 1), 19.8 mmol/L (day 2). Cerebrospinal fluid cell count: 34.1/µL.

Due to the severity of the disease and the high risk of developing and progression of intracranial complications, the decision was made to perform a surgical intervention. Fragments of altered tissues and pathological exudate, obtained intraoperatively, were sent to the bacteriological laboratory for microbiological and microscopic examinations.

At the initial stage, a microscopic examination of a smear prepared from biological material obtained from the secretion of the nasal sinuses was performed using Gram staining. Microscopy was performed at magnifications of 200× and 400×. Fungal elements were detected in the form of fragments of aseptate mycelium characteristic of representatives of the order Mucorales (Figs. 7 and 8), as well as numerous yeast cells.

Gram staining. 200×. In the central part of the specimen, fungal elements are identified as fragments of non-septate mycelium

Gram staining. 200×. In the central part of the specimen, fungal elements are identified as fragments of non-septate mycelium. Numerous yeast cells are observed in the field of view

The next step was the isolation of microorganisms from the clinical material using a culture method. The cultivation of isolates from the nasal sinuses and nasal cavity was performed on commercial universal chromogenic media, blood agar, and Sabouraud agar medium (HiMedia, India) using the “three-point” streak method to detect micromycetes. Cultivation was carried out at a temperature of 37 °C in a dry-air thermostat for 24 h. Petri dishes with a solid Sabouraud nutrient medium were incubated in a thermostat at 28 °C for 2–5 days with daily monitoring of fungal growth.

The growth of the mold on a dense nutrient medium was observed during the first day of cultivation (Fig. 9). Macroscopic morphological characteristics were evaluated visually as well as with the assistance of a stereomicroscope.

Macroscopic appearance: colonies with abundantly developed tall and fluffy aerial mycelia; white, and during sporulation—gray due to the coloring of the sporangia with spores (Figs. 10 and 11).

Petri dish with a colony of Rhizopus oryzae with fluffy mycelium

Image of Rhizopus oryzae, obtained using stereomicroscopy, magnification 10×. Long white sporangiophores with gray spherical sporangia at the ends are identified

A microscopic method was used for further genus identification. We prepared a native preparation for light microscopy with 10% KOH. Microscopy was performed at 200× and 400× magnification.

The microscopic morphological characteristics of the fungus were evaluated: non-septate mycelium, branching at right angles, with round-shaped sporangia located at the ends of the sporangiophores and containing sporangiospores (Figs. 12 and 13).

Microscopic features of fungi of the order Mucorales: broad aseptate mycelium, branching at right angles, with round sporangia located at the ends of the sporangiophores, containing sporangiospores

Microscopic features of fungi of the order Mucorales: broad aseptate mycelium, branching at right angles, with round sporangia located at the ends of the sporangiophores, containing sporangiospores

In addition, it was decided to prepare a micropreparation using the fluorescent dye calcofluor white (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Canada). Fluorescent microscopy was performed at magnifications of 200× and 400×. During fluorescent microscopy using special light filters, the mycelium was illuminated with a bright green light (Figs. 14 and 15).

Luminescent microscopy. Staining—calcofluor white, magnification 200×. The mycelium of the fungus Rhizopus oryzae is highlighted by a bright green light

Luminescent microscopy. Staining—calcofluor white, magnification 200×. The mycelium of the fungus Rhizopus oryzae is highlighted by a bright green light

After 72 h of cultivation on a dense Sabouraud nutrient medium, the appearance of abundant aerial mycelium and sporangia with spores has been noted. For species identification of the cultured isolate, the MALDI ToF mass spectrometry method was used (Figs. 16 and 17) Thus, a microbiological study was conducted using the following methods: culturing the biomaterial on solid nutrient media, assessment of macroscopic and microscopic morphological features, luminescent microscopy, stereomicroscopy, and mass spectrometric analysis. During the study, highly reliable results were obtained, indicating that this species is the mycelial fungus Rhizopus oryzae.

Based on the microbiological, functional, and laboratory investigations performed, a clinical diagnosis was made: primary: mucormycosis, rhino-orbito-cerebral form Rhizopus oryzae (order Mucorales), complicated by orbital phlegmon Complications included unspecified septicemia and systemic inflammatory response syndrome. Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus, newly diagnosed, ketoacidosis.

Treatment administered

The patient underwent a surgical operation in the form of videoendoscopic sinus necrosectomy and orbital decompression.

In the postoperative period, the patient underwent antibacterial therapy with drugs from the tetracycline class, fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, and carbapenems, as well as antifungal therapy: levofloxacin 500 mg IV twice daily, cefoperazone + sulbactam 1g+1g IV three times daily, liposomal amphotericin B 50 mg IV by infusion, meropenem 2g IV by infusion three times daily, tigecycline 50 mg IV by infusion three times daily.

Follow-up visits

Patient D. Specialized medical care was provided in full. A diagnostic evaluation, surgical intervention, and antifungal as well as symptomatic therapy were performed. The clinical case ended with a fatal outcome on the 10th day of hospitalization.

Discussion

Patient D., 47 years old, was admitted to the otorhinolaryngology department on an emergency basis with signs of exacerbation of chronic sinusitis. Based on the reported complaints, patient history, and initial examination, a preliminary diagnosis was made: chronic right-sided hemi-sinusitis, exacerbation. A differential diagnosis was performed and radiological, laboratory, and microbiological studies were carried out. Thus, the diagnosis of mucormycosis, the rhino-orbital form, was confirmed. Patient management involved a surgical procedure aimed at removing necrotic tissues, as well as etiological antifungal therapy. Despite the comprehensive investigation and complete treatment provided, the case ended in a fatal outcome. The patient had not sought medical care for a long time, and no correction of blood glucose levels was performed, which led to an increased risk of developing complications of mucormycosis, such as intraorbital and intracranial invasion. The clinical picture was complicated by the presence of an underlying condition in the form of type I diabetes mellitus, detected for the first time.

Conclusion

Mucormycosis is an extremely life-threatening disease. For its detection, it is important to use radiological and laboratory diagnostic methods. In the process of confirming the diagnosis and selecting the therapy, a pivotal role is attributed to microbiological examination involving the detection of a micromycete in the biomaterial and the assessment of macroscopic and microscopic findings. Equally important in improving the patient’s survival prognosis is timely etiotropic therapy and surgical intervention.
