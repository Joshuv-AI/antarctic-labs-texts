Impact of achieving WHO's 90-70-90 targets on cervical cancer elimination and potential benefits in preventing other HPV-related cancers in China: a modelling study
Authors: Zhuoru Zou, Christopher K Fairley, Jason J Ong, Mingwang Shen, Eric PF Chow, Hanting Liu, Ruyi Xia, Rui Li, Jane Hocking, Guihua Zhuang, Lei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIn the context of the World Health Organization's (WHO) 90-70-90 targets for accelerating cervical cancer elimination, we aimed to assess the impact of achieving these targets and altering intervention factors on cervical cancer elimination in China and their potential benefits from preventing other human papillomavirus (HPV)-related cancers.MethodsWe developed a sexual contact network-Markov model to simulate HPV transmission and the progression of HPV-related cancers (cervical, vaginal, vulvar, penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers). We projected the population impact of achieving 90-70-90 targets by 2030 on the overall HPV-related cancer burden in China during 2024–2100. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to determine ranges for the projected results, and scenario analyses were conducted to quantify the impact of various intervention factors.FindingsMaintaining the status quo would result in 8.72 (range 8.32−9.19) million cervical cancer cases and 944,100 (901,900−988,300) other HPV-related cancer cases from 2024 to 2100. Achieving 90-70-90 targets (with 2vHPV/4vHPV vaccines), scenario 1, would reduce cervical cancer incidence to <4 cases/100,000 women by 2093 (2085−∞), averting 3.57 (2.97−3.98) million cases. Switching to the 9vHPV vaccine from 2025, scenario 2, would advance elimination by 2088 (2078−∞), averting 3.65 (3.07−4.08) million cases. Scenarios 1 or 2 would prevent 168,600 (160,500−176,800) and 173,600 (165,400−184,300) other HPV-related cancers respectively by 2100. Increasing screening frequency from twice-per-lifetime recommendation to 5-yearly or 3-yearly screening moved elimination to 2061 or 2051 respectively. In scenario 1, achieving elimination before 2100 would be challenged if screening or vaccination coverage remains below 60% and 90% or if the time to reach 90% vaccination exceeds 2040 or if screening frequency or test sensitivity are substandard. Switching to the 9vHPV vaccine increased tolerance for these thresholds.InterpretationBased on our prediction model, achieving WHO's 90-70-90 targets in China could eliminate cervical cancer by the century's end and help prevent other HPV-related cancers. Enhanced screening frequency and adoption of the 9vHPV vaccine are critical for accelerating elimination efforts.FundingNational Natural Science Foundation of China (82304206 and 81950410639).Keywords: Cervical cancer, Elimination, Human papillomavirus vaccination, Screening, HPV-related cancer, Model projection

Research in context

Evidence before this study

In China, HPV vaccination pilots for girls are underway in developed regions, with expanding cervical cancer screening. However, limited resources and competing priorities pose challenges to introducing the HPV vaccine into the national immunization programme and achieving high-quality cervical cancer screening in the short term. Assessing the feasibility of cervical cancer elimination and the potential additional benefits of herd protection under various scenarios is crucial for guiding national policy and investment. We searched PubMed for studies published between January 1, 2000, and March 20, 2024, without language restrictions using the search terms (“human papillomavirus” or “HPV”) and (“vaccine” or “vaccination” or “screening”) and (“cervical cancer” or “HPV-related cancers”) and (“elimination” or “impact” or “benefit”) and (“modelling”). We found no studies assessed the impact of altering intervention factors beyond the World Health Organization's (WHO) 90-70-90 targets on elimination feasibility and the additional effects of interventions.

Added value of this study

Using a sexual contact network-Markov model, our study projected the timeframe for cervical cancer elimination in China in the context of achieving WHO's 90-70-90 targets by 2030, as well as herd protection generated by interventions and its effect on preventing other HPV-related cancers. We assessed the impact of various intervention factors and their threshold levels needed for elimination by the century's end. Our findings show that China could achieve cervical cancer elimination by the 2080s or 2090s, depending on the HPV vaccine type used, and prevent approximately one-fifth of other HPV-related cancers between 2024 and 2100. Increasing screening frequency emerged as the most effective factor, while target vaccination coverage and time to reach 90% vaccination might impact the feasibility of elimination.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings suggest that by achieving the WHO's 90-70-90 targets, China may eliminate cervical cancer within this century, with significant benefits for preventing other HPV-related cancers. Increasing screening frequency is crucial in advancing elimination. These results enhance understanding of the population impact of the 90-70-90 targets and provide model-based evidence for policymakers to clarify intervention targets for cervical cancer elimination.

Introduction

In May 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) Director-General initiated a call to eliminate cervical cancer globally, emphasising a renewed political commitment and urging collaboration among all relevant stakeholders. Subsequently, in August 2020, the World Health Assembly endorsed the Global Strategy for cervical cancer elimination, delineating essential measures required for the attainment of this goal. The goal of this is to ensure all nations achieve and sustain cervical cancer incidence below 4/100,000 women. To progress toward cervical cancer elimination by the end of this century, each country must attain the 90-70-90 targets by 2030. This means, that 90% of girls are fully vaccinated with the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine by the age of 15, 70% of women are screened at ages 35 and 45, and 90% of individuals diagnosed with cervical precancerous/cancers receive treatment.1

Cervical cancer remains a significant public health challenge in China. In 2020, China reported 109,741 new cases and 59,060 deaths, accounting for 18.2% and 17.3% of cervical cancer diagnoses and deaths worldwide, respectively.2 Recent developments demonstrate the Chinese government's commitment to enhancing public health policy in cervical cancer prevention. In 2023, China's National Health Commission released the ‘Action Plan for Accelerated Elimination of Cervical Cancer (2023–2030)’ aiming to coordinate intersectoral efforts to achieve the WHO's 90-70-90 targets.3 The integration of the National Cervical Cancer Screening Programme in Rural Areas into the National Basic Public Health Services secured screening funding. Two domestic bivalent HPV vaccines (2vHPV), Cecolin and Walrinvax, gained approval in 2019 and 2022. Meanwhile, the domestic nonavalent HPV vaccine (9vHPV, Cecolin9) finished phase III trials, awaiting approval.4 By April 2023, pilot HPV vaccination programmes in seven of the more developed provinces commenced, providing free vaccination to first-year junior-middle-school girls.5 However, limited resources and competing priorities pose challenges to introducing the HPV vaccine into the national immunization programme and achieving nationwide high-quality cervical cancer screening in the short term. To inform policymakers in setting milestones for interventions to ensure cervical cancer elimination, there is an urgent need for modelling studies to project the timeframe of cervical cancer elimination and to identify the most effective intervention factors and necessary threshold levels.

Three studies projected the timeframe of cervical cancer elimination in China under different assumptions. A global study projected that China could eliminate cervical cancer between 2060 and 2065 by reaching 80−100% coverage of 9vHPV vaccination for 12-year-old girls and 70% coverage of twice-per-lifetime screening by 2020.6 Xia et al. suggested elimination by 2072–2074 with an optimal strategy under the current budget in China, including 95% coverage of 2vHPV vaccination for 12-year-old girls and 33%–90% coverage of screening for women aged 45 years from 2020.7 However, to date, China has not met the optimistic coverage projections for these interventions. Meng et al. found that large-scale HPV vaccination in 2022 and achieving 70% coverage of HPV-based screening in 2030 would be the least costly and most effective compared with delaying HPV vaccination and screening in China.8 No studies have investigated the impact of achieving WHO's 90-70-90 targets on the feasibility of elimination and the impact and thresholds of intervening factors, such as screening frequency, vaccine type, target coverage, time to reach target coverage, test sensitivity, follow-up, treatment, and catch-up vaccinations.

The broader implications of HPV vaccination beyond cervical cancer prevention should also be considered. High-risk HPV (hrHPV) infection can also cause non-cervical cancers, including some anal, oropharyngeal, penile, vaginal, and vulvar cancers.9 Approximately 7192 cancers in men and 4449 non-cervical cancers in women were potentially HPV-attributed in China in 2014, constituting the remaining 10.5% of HPV-attributable new cancer cases.10 As cervical cancer elimination is pursued, HPV intervention programmes' broader impact on other HPV-related cancers cannot be overlooked. In countries with widespread HPV vaccination among girls, observed reductions in HPV types within the HPV vaccines among unvaccinated women and men indicated significant herd protection.11 Reducing circulating HPV in the population will also help prevent other HPV-related cancers in both genders. Quantifying the indirect effects of widespread HPV vaccination in girls on reducing HPV prevalence in men and unvaccinated women, as well as its impact on preventing other HPV-related cancers is crucial to support HPV vaccine inclusion in the National Immunization Programme (NIP). Notably, no modelling studies have explored the incremental impact of widespread HPV vaccination for girls in China.

We conducted a model-based study to project the timeframe of cervical cancer elimination in China within the context of achieving the WHO's 90-70-90 targets by 2030, the herd protection resulting from the HPV vaccination programme, and its impact on the prevention of other HPV-related cancers. We also assessed the impact of altering various factors involved in these interventions.

Methods

Model and assumptions

We developed a hybrid model linking sexual contact network with Markov model to simulate HPV transmission and the progression of six HPV-related cancers (including cervical, vaginal, vulvar, penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers) in infected individuals (Figure S1). This hybrid model linked the age-specific HPV incidence rates for women and men within the sexual contact network to serve as inputs for the Markov model, which in turn modelled the progression of HPV-related cancers.

Sexual contact network

The sexual contact network offers the advantage of assessing the influence of network topology on HPV transmission and capturing the herd protection resulting from HPV vaccination. The sexual contact network was constructed using a randomized network structure, tailored for a sexually active 15−64-year-old population (Appendix pp 2−7). Data on heterosexual behaviour were obtained from the China Health and Family Life Survey, a national probability survey of sexual behaviour among the mainland Chinese population excluding Tibet (Table S1).12 To expedite simulations, we used a population size of 100,000 instead of the actual population size. The model considered population dynamics and simulated the person-to-person HPV transmission on a dynamic evolving network. The hrHPV infection states were categorized into HPV-16/18, HPV-31/33/45/52/58, and other hrHPV types. We assumed the HPV transmission probability per unit time in partnerships relied on HPV type and susceptible individuals’ age (Table S8).13 Natural immunity was not considered because HPV can be recurrently infected, but vaccinated individuals had reduced infection risks against vaccine-targeted types.

Markov model

Markov model traced the progression and regression of hrHPV infections to HPV-related cancers in all women and men aged 15−80 years (life expectancy 80) living in China during 2024–2100 (Appendix pp 7−8). We assumed these cancers occurred independently due to the rare likelihood of multiple cancers in one person. The Markov model for cervical cancer referred to our previous study.14 Similar to cervical cancer, HPV-related cancers of other anogenital sites in women and men are generally considered to be caused by persistent infection with hrHPV, initially presenting as intraepithelial neoplasia in these organs and progressing via increasing degrees of intraepithelial involvement.15 The progression of HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer is also perceived to be similar to the stepwise model of cervical carcinogenesis, although its natural history remains to be confirmed.15 Due to limited longitudinal research and the absence of relevant screening programmes for precancerous lesions of other HPV-related cancers, we regarded these precancerous lesions as hrHPV infection states, reducing uncertainty in the parameters. We assumed the progression and regression probabilities of HPV-related cancers were associated with the duration of infection according to the infected types (Tables S9). We considered age-specific natural background death rates for women and men, and individuals with HPV-related cancer also died of the disease.

Data sources

Through a comprehensive literature review, we obtained initial ranges for probability parameters associated with the natural history of HPV infection and HPV-related cancers, along with relevant real-world epidemiological data (Tables S2, S4–S7). We calibrated the probability parameters for HPV infection, clearance, and disease progression using a likelihood-based approach to maximise the likelihood between model outputs and empirical observations (Appendix pp 8–26).16 Background death rates were obtained from the China Population & Employment Statistics Yearbook, 2022 (Table S3).17 Population projections for 15–80-year-old individuals in 2024 and 15-year-old individuals between 2025 and 2100 in China were derived from publicly accessible UN Population Division Data.18 The probability parameters estimates and ranges were summarized in Tables S8 and S9.

HPV vaccine efficacy was determined based on phase III double-blind randomized controlled trials (Table S10).19,20 Multiple studies underscored its high efficacy in preventing infections and pre-invasive diseases associated with the vaccine-targeted types across various anatomical sites.21 We, therefore, hypothesised similar efficacy against persistent infections of the vaccine-targeted types at non-cervical sites as observed at the cervix. The duration of vaccine protection was assumed to be lifelong.22 Cross-protection effects on non-vaccine types were not considered. The sensitivity of cervical cancer screening tests, compliance with confirmatory diagnosis, treatment rate, and treatment efficacy in the Chinese population were obtained from relevant literature (Table S11).14

Modelled scenarios

In China, the HPV vaccine has not been introduced into the NIP and approximately 36.8% of adult women have undergone cervical cytology testing.23 Therefore, we assumed that the ‘status quo’ was no HPV vaccination and that 36.8% of women aged 30−64 received a single cervical cytology test.

We simulated two intervention scenarios to meet the WHO's 90-70-90 targets. China currently only publicly funds domestic vaccines, making it politically challenging to introduce expensive foreign-produced vaccines into the NIP. Therefore, in scenario 1, we assumed a 2-dose domestic 2vHPV/4vHPV vaccination programme for 13-year-old (first-year junior-middle-school) girls. Considering that the domestic 9vHPV vaccine is expected to be marketed around 2025, in scenario 2, we assumed a switch to a 2-dose domestic 9vHPV vaccination from 2025. For twice-per-lifetime cervical cancer screening in both scenarios, we followed the expert consensus, assuming a primary HPV screening with a triage strategy (Appendix pp 27 and 28). We assumed loop electrosurgical excision procedure for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN)-2/3 and the possibility of hysterectomy for about 20% of patients aged ≥30 years with CIN2/3.24 The intervention coverage rates were assumed to gradually and linearly increase from the current level to reach the target by 2030 and maintain it thereafter.

Statistics

Model projections

Based on the model, we estimated the long-term hrHPV prevalence rates in sexually active women and men aged 15−64, and cervical cancer incidence and the cumulative number of cervical cancer cases averted in women aged 15−80 during 2024–2100 under the two scenarios of achieving the WHO's 90-70-90 targets. The elimination year was defined as the first year in which cervical cancer incidence decreases to <4 cases/100,000 women.1

In addition, we evaluated the impact of declining hrHPV prevalence on the incidence rates of other HPV-related cancers. We projected incidence rates and cumulative numbers of cases averted for other HPV-related cancers in women and men aged 15−80 during 2024–2100 under the same scenarios. Modelling and analyses were completed using MATLAB R2019a software (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Sensitivity analysis

Given parameter uncertainties, we randomly sampled 500 sets of ‘good-fitting’ natural history parameters, combining them with the lower and upper limits of the intervention parameters to ascertain ranges of incidence rates and cumulative number of cases averted for HPV-related cancers. Scenario analyses involved systematically altering each intervention factor (Table S12) in scenarios 1 and 2 to quantify their impact on outcomes, including assessing the combined effects of high-impact intervention factors.

Ethics

The study did not require informed consent or ethical approval as all model parameters were obtained from a comprehensive literature review and publicly available sources.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Impact of achieving WHO's 90-70-90 targets on future HPV prevalence

Assuming a gradual increase of 2vHPV/4vHPV vaccination coverage to 90% by 2030, HPV-16/18 prevalence in women would decrease from 3.3% (range 3.2%−3.5%) in 2024 to 0.3% (0.2%−0.5%) in 2080 and beyond, paralleled by a decline in men from 0.9% (0.8%−0.9%) to 0.2% (0.2%−0.3%) (Fig. 1a and 1c). Replacing 2vHPV/4vHPV with 9vHPV by 2025 made no difference to the projected trajectory of HPV-16/18 prevalence, but reduced non-16/18-hrHPVs prevalence from 2025 onwards in both genders, with projections indicating a 32% (18%–37%) decrease in women (14.4% (13.7%−14.9%) to 9.8% (9.1%−11.8%) from 2024 to 2100) and a 20% (16%–24%) decrease in men (7.4% (7.0%−7.6%) to 5.9% (5.6%−6.2%) from 2024 to 2100) for non-16/18-hrHPVs prevalence (Fig. 1b and 1d).

Impact of achieving WHO's 90-70-90 targets on the timeframe of cervical cancer elimination

Maintaining the status quo would sustain cervical cancer incidence at 17.04 (16.30−18.23) cases/100,000 women, accumulating 8.72 (8.32−9.19) million new cases during 2024–2100 (Table 1, Fig. 2, and Table S13). Scenario 1, involving gradual 2vHPV/4vHPV vaccination up to 90%, alongside 70% twice-per-lifetime cervical cancer screening and 90% treatment for precancerous lesions by 2030, would reduce cervical cancer incidence to <4 cases/100,000 women by 2093 (2085−∞), averting an estimated 3.57 (2.97−3.98) million cases during 2024–2100. Scenario 2, switching to 9vHPV vaccine from 2025, would advance cervical cancer elimination to 2088 (2078−∞), potentially reducing incidence to 3.00 (1.81−4.49) cases/100,000 women by 2100 and averting around 3.65 (3.07−4.08) million cases during 2024–2100.

Impact of achieving WHO's 90-70-90 targets on other HPV-related cancers

In both scenarios 1 and 2, there would also be a significant reduction in incidences of other HPV-related cancers in women and men (Fig. 2). For women, reductions projected from 2024 to 2100 included 56%–78% for vaginal cancer (0.18 to 0.04−0.08 cases/100,000 women), 60%–80% for vulvar cancer (0.10 to 0.02−0.04 cases/100,000 women), 75%–88% for anal cancer (0.24 to 0.03−0.06 cases/100,000 women), and 75%–83% for oropharyngeal cancer (0.12 to 0.02−0.03 cases/100,000 women). Similarly, men could see reductions of 44%–61% for penile cancer (0.36 to 0.14−0.20 cases/100,000 men), 55%–73% for anal cancer (0.33 to 0.09−0.15 cases/100,000 men), and 63%–66% for oropharyngeal cancer (0.32 to 0.11−0.12 cases/100,000 men) during 2024–2100.

The status quo could yield 944,100 (901,900−988,300) new cases of other HPV-related cancers during 2024–2100, with 390,300 (366,700−418,400) in women and 553,800 (535,100−569,900) in men (Table 1, Tables S14−S20). In scenario 1, an estimated 168,600 (160,500−176,800) cases would be averted, with 85,300 (82,500−88,100) in women and 83,300 (78,100−88,600) in men. Scenario 2 would avert 5000 additional cases of these cancers during 2024–2100.

Promoting factors for advancing cervical cancer elimination and reducing HPV-related cancer burden

Figs. 3 and 4 demonstrated the impact of changing each of the intervention factors on elimination year, incidence reduction, and averted cases. In scenario 1, more frequent cervical cancer screening proved most effective for advancing elimination and curbing cervical cancer burden, with 5-yearly screening for women aged 30−64 moving elimination year to 2061 (32 years advancement), reducing incidence to 1.84 cases/100,000 women by 2100, averting 5.19 million cervical cancer cases. Three-yearly screening would achieve elimination by 2051 (42 years advancement), reducing incidence to 1.09 cases/100,000 women by 2100, averting 5.93 million cervical cancer cases. Targeting 100% screening or vaccination (10 years or 6 years advancement), and switching to 9vHPV vaccination from 2025 (5 years advancement) were other top effective interventions (Fig. 3a).

In scenario 2, increased screening frequency emerged as the most impactful for advancing elimination, with 5-yearly or 3-yearly screening achieving elimination by 2061 (27 years advancement) or 2051 (37 years advancement). Targeting 100% screening or vaccination (8 years or 6 years advancement), and implementing catch-up vaccination for unvaccinated women aged 15−26 between 2024 and 2030 (5 years advancement) were other top effective interventions (Fig. 4a). Increasing screening coverage was the second most effective intervention for reducing cervical cancer cases (Figs. 3c and 4c). Improving vaccination coverage was the most effective intervention for reducing HPV-related non-cervical cancer cases, followed by catch-up vaccination (Fig. 3, Fig. 4d).

Limiting factors for achieving cervical cancer elimination

Achieving cervical cancer elimination faced constraints tied to vaccination target coverage rates and the time to reach 90% vaccination (Figs. 3 and 4). Challenges arose if vaccination coverage stayed <90% or reached 90% after 2040 in scenario 1, or if it stayed <80% or reached 90% after 2050 in scenario 2. Some screening-related factors like screening coverage stayed <60% or once-per-lifetime screening, or the reduced sensitivity of screening tests might also hinder the goal under scenario 1 (Fig. 3).

Fig. 5 shows cervical cancer incidence projections by decade for various combinations of HPV vaccine types, screening frequencies, and vaccination and screening coverages. Even at 100% 2vHPV/4vHPV vaccination, raising twice-per-lifetime screening coverage to at least 45% would be required for end-of-century elimination (Fig. 5f). Switching to the 9vHPV vaccine with about 85% coverage could enable achieving the elimination goal while maintaining status quo screening coverage (36.8%) (Fig. 5r).

Discussion

Our study assessed the impact of achieving the WHO's 90-70-90 targets on cervical cancer elimination and other HPV-related cancer burden in China. Our findings suggest that China could achieve cervical cancer elimination by the 2080s or 2090s, depending on the type of HPV vaccine used, if the targets are met on schedule. Widespread HPV vaccination among girls could also prevent about one-fifth of other HPV-related cancer cases in women and men from 2024 to 2100. Increasing cervical cancer screening frequency emerges as the most effective intervention to advance cervical cancer elimination. Target coverage for HPV vaccination and the time to reach 90% vaccination, will impact the feasibility of cervical cancer elimination in China within this century. Non-attainment of some screening-related factors may also impede the achievement of elimination when using 2vHPV/4vHPV vaccines. Switching to the 9vHPV vaccine may increase tolerance to these intervention thresholds.

China is on its way to achieving the WHO's 90-70-90 targets. China's National Health Commission has progressively introduced policies for cervical cancer prevention aligned with the WHO's 90-70-90 targets since 2021.3 Pilot HPV vaccination programmes have been launched in several south-eastern provinces in China since 2022. Other regions are also exploring ways to expand HPV vaccination, including provision of financial incentives and increasing awareness. HPV testing is replacing Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid or Pap smear in China, with over 100 testing-related products being marketed.25,26 In particular, novel HPV DNA rapid testing (careHPV) provides an affordable option suitable for areas with limited medical facilities, enabling HPV-based screening in remote regions.27 Our models accurately reflected this Chinese context, by simulating a gradual increase in HPV vaccination, screening and treatment rates that aligned with the 90-70-90 targets by 2030. Our findings suggested, that regardless of whether using 2vHPV/4vHPV or switching to 9vHPV vaccine, the projected cervical cancer incidence would decline below 4 cases/100,000 women by the 2080s or 2090s. These findings strongly support China's pursuit of the WHO's 90-70-90 targets as an important step for cervical cancer elimination by 2100.

Our study showed scaling up HPV vaccination among girls would produce significant herd protection and reduce the burden of other HPV-related cancers in heterosexual women and men. Simulations based on the sexual contact network demonstrated a decrease in vaccine-targeted HPV prevalence in women and men after HPV vaccination. Countries with universal HPV vaccination have observed similar decreases.11 We projected a 56%–88% decline in other HPV-related cancer incidences in women and a 47%–70% decline in men by 2100. These findings provide new evidence of the additional benefits of scaling up HPV vaccination in China, enriching our comprehension of its population impacts.

Our study demonstrated that increasing cervical cancer screening frequency beyond the WHO's proposed fundamental requirement of twice-per-lifetime screening is crucial to further significantly reduce cervical cancer burden and advance elimination. In particular, implementing 5-yearly screening could achieve elimination by the 2060s, while 3-yearly screening could further advance it to 2050s. Both strategies could prevent a significant number of cervical cancers. Similarly, a previous modelling study found that combining a 95% HPV vaccination among 12-year-old girls with a 65%–90% 5-yearly screening in 2020 could lead to cervical cancer elimination in the 2060s in China.7 These results support our finding for the 5-yearly screening scenarios. Ensuring regular, high-compliance cervical cancer screenings necessitates concerted efforts from the government, corporations, and individuals, involving enhanced policy and financial support at national and local levels, corporate employee benefits, increased medical insurance reimbursement rates, and bolstered health education.

Our study demonstrated that switching to the 9vHPV vaccine could potentially improve the likelihood of achieving cervical cancer elimination goal. We found that if key factors such as target coverages for screening or vaccination, timing for 90% HPV vaccination, screening frequency or test sensitivity do not meet expectations, China may struggle to achieve the elimination goal on schedule. Switching to 9vHPV vaccination may improve tolerance to thresholds for these intervening factors. There exist significant differences in the quality of cervical cancer screening across various geographic and economically diverse regions of China.28 Li et al. found that cytology testing and pathology service delivery capacity in rural China was less than half that of urban areas.29 In regions facing challenges in the quality of cervical cancer screening, we believe a greater emphasis on the adoption of the 9vHPV vaccine is necessary to drive toward the elimination goal. Nevertheless, related health policy decisions should be based on a combination of quantitative data from models, qualitative insights, and practical considerations. In reality, economically developed areas might prioritize expanding 9vHPV vaccination, potentially exacerbating inequalities in HPV-related disease prevention. Hence, when implementing government-sponsored HPV vaccination programmes, it's essential to consider the substantial disparities in the geographical distribution of cervical cancer screening and mortality, efficiently directing limited public resources toward priority groups in resource-scarce areas to facilitate nationwide cervical cancer elimination.

Our study has limitations. First, due to the lack of region-specific data, our analysis was from the overall perspective of China, potentially limiting its applicability to regional policies. Second, the sensitive nature of other HPV-related cancer sites may cause underreporting due to stigma, potentially underestimating HPV vaccination benefits in China. Third, using sexual behaviour data from a 2000 survey due to inadequate updated information or trend assessments, the study did not reflect potential changes in sexual behaviours among the Chinese population over time, thus simulating stable trends in HPV infection and cervical cancer incidence under the status quo. This may underestimate the burden of cervical cancer. Fourth, we overlooked potential biases due to disregarding acquired natural immunity after HPV infection. Fifth, due to insufficient evidence we did not consider the potential HPV type replacement after vaccination in the population. Sixth, we made assumptions about vaccine efficacy. Excluding cross-protective effects on non-vaccine-targeted HPV types and assuming lifetime protection, might under or overestimate vaccine effectiveness. Seventh, we did not assess the impact of gender-neutral HPV vaccination due to the fact that the pilot HPV vaccination programme for girls has just recently been initiated and the lack of momentum and approval for boys' vaccination to date in mainland China. Therefore, this study cannot ascertain whether the potential additional benefits of girls' programmes could replace gender-neutral programmes. Eighth, we did not consider the potential advancements in HPV vaccines and more accurate screening methods that could alter prevention strategies. Lastly, mortality from HPV-related cancers was not estimated due to uncertainty around future cancer treatments, marking a limitation in this study's scope.

In conclusion, our results indicate that by achieving the WHO's 90-70-90 targets by 2030, China is expected to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health threat within this century and will also have significant benefits for the prevention of other HPV-related cancers. Increasing cervical cancer screening frequency is pivotal in advancing elimination. In regions facing screening challenges, adopting the 9vHPV vaccine and achieving high vaccination coverage could aid the goal. This study contributes to promoting cervical cancer elimination and understanding the implications of HPV vaccination in China, offering insights for similar countries in devising HPV-related cancer prevention strategies.
