Associations of physical activity, pain intensity, and quality of life with work ability among Nigerian nurses with mechanical low back pain: a cross-sectional survey

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Sub-Saharan Africa, nursing professionals operate under intense occupational pressure characterized by high patient loads and an acute deficit of ergonomic support. These systemic constraints amplify the impact of mechanical low back pain (LBP) on work ability, the functional equilibrium between a clinician’s health status and their professional obligations. Guided by the biopsychosocial model, this study investigated the associations of physical activity (PA), pain intensity (PI), and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) with work ability among nurses experiencing mechanical LBP within a Nigerian tertiary clinical setting.MethodsA hospital-based, cross-sectional descriptive survey was conducted among 188 clinical nurses at a federal referral hospital in Gombe, Nigeria. Eligible participants were nurses who had experienced a diagnosis of mechanical LBP persisting for at least three months. Validated instruments were deployed: the Work Ability Index (WAI) captured the dependent variable, while the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), and the Physical Component Summary (PCS) of the RAND-36 Health Survey quantified the independent variables. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson’s product-moment correlation, and multiple linear regression analysis (alpha = 0.05).ResultsBivariate analysis demonstrated significant positive correlations between work ability and both PA (r = 0.435, p = 0.002) and HRQoL (r = 0.388, p = 0.001), alongside a significant inverse correlation with PI (r = -0.312, p = 0.001). The multiple linear regression model was statistically significant, F(3, 184) = 12.525, p = 0.001, accounting for 21.5% of the total variance (R2 = 0.215, Adjusted R2 = 0.198). Notably, physical activity emerged as the most robust independent indicator in the model (beta = 0.412, t = 3.14, p = 0.002), displaying a substantially greater association with functional work ability than the subjective intensity of clinical pain symptoms.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that physical activity, subjective pain intensity, and health-related quality of life are concurrent factors significantly associated with the work ability of Nigerian clinical nurses managing mechanical low back pain. The empirical findings indicate that higher physical activity levels and lower pain intensities are both independently linked to optimal work ability scores within this cohort. Consequently, occupational health strategies in resource-constrained Nigerian medical environments should consider a comprehensive approach that couples traditional pain management with structured, low-cost institutional physical activity and exercise rehabilitation programs to support the functional capacity of the healthcare workforce.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) remains the leading global contributor to years lived with disability, placing an extraordinary operational and economic strain on healthcare delivery systems worldwide [1]. Within the clinical environment, nursing professionals exhibit a pronounced, disproportionate vulnerability to mechanical LBP due to the hazardous physical demands embedded in daily clinical practice. These occupational stressors typically involve manual patient handling, repetitive or sudden trunk flexion, sustained static postures, and prolonged standing during extended shifts [2]. While historical clinical research has focused heavily on isolated structural symptom reduction or anatomical curation, a more critical priority for healthcare institutions is evaluating and preserving work ability. Work ability represents the functional equilibrium between a worker’s holistic health resources and the specific physical and psychological demands of their occupational role [3]. When this equilibrium balances poorly, it results in increased absenteeism, institutional presenteeism, and early retirement, creating an operational crisis for health systems.

Within Sub-Saharan Africa, and specifically within the Nigerian clinical landscape, the structural impact of mechanical LBP on the nursing workforce is severely compounded by unique systemic constraints. Chronic institutional understaffing, driven by economic limitations and accelerated by the massive emigration of healthcare professionals to high-income countries—popularly termed the “brain drain”—has resulted in extreme patient-to-nurse ratios across domestic hospitals [4]. Unlike clinical environments in Western nations, Nigerian public tertiary referral hospitals are acutely under-resourced and generally lack basic assistive technologies, such as mechanical patient hoists, slide sheets, or adjustable ergonomic transfer systems. Consequently, frontline clinicians must rely almost exclusively on manual physical force to transfer, lift, and reposition completely dependent patients [5]. This continuous kinetic strain places the work ability of these professionals under constant threat. Epidemiological data from West African cohorts show that musculoskeletal disorders affect up to 78% of working nurses, with the lumbar region being the most frequent site of impairment [2]. Despite the severity of this issue, the precise behavioral and individual variables that sustain or compromise work ability within these resource-limited spaces remain insufficiently characterized in contemporary literature.

To fully understand these dynamics, it is necessary to move beyond traditional biomedical approaches, which have historically failed to explain the wide variance in how individual clinicians function despite chronic pain. The traditional biomedical framework operates on a linear assumption that clinical disability is directly proportional to structural tissue damage or anatomical pathology [6]. However, this model cannot account for why two nurses with identical radiographic profiles of lumbar disc degeneration can report completely divergent levels of work capacity and occupational performance. To resolve these limitations, this study utilizes the multidimensional biopsychosocial framework, which posits that a worker’s functional status and pain experience are complex products of dynamic interactions between biological stressors, behavioral choices, and socio-environmental realities [6]. To capture this multidimensionality in a measurable clinical model, three distinct independent variables were selected to examine their relationship with work ability: pain intensity, health-related quality of life, and physical activity. Pain intensity represents the primary biological stressor that directly limits the mechanical execution of high-load physical tasks [7]. Health-related quality of life serves as a holistic indicator of the broader psychological and social impact of navigating a chronic musculoskeletal condition [8]. Physical activity operates as a highly modifiable, proactive behavioral factor capable of acting as a physiological buffer against functional decline [9, 10].

The justification for this investigation extends beyond its localized geographic context. In a systemic environment largely devoid of institutional ergonomic support or structural modifications, it is unclear which of these biopsychosocial domains most strongly correlates with a nurse’s capacity to remain actively in the workforce. By identifying the relative strength of these associations, hospital administrators and rehabilitation specialists can shift from reactive, expensive sick-leave protocols toward targeted, risk-specific wellness strategies. Identifying physical activity as a dominant correlate of work capacity as suggested by its effectiveness in improving functional output among high-stress industrial and clinical cohorts globally [9] would provide an evidence-based foundation for low-cost, movement-based interventions within Nigerian health facilities. This study sought to investigate the associations of physical activity, pain intensity, and health-related quality of life with the work ability of clinical nurses managing mechanical LBP within a major tertiary hospital in Gombe, North-Eastern Nigeria.

Methods

Study design, setting, and ethical clearance

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Bayero University Kano (Reference: AKTH/MAC/SUB/12A/P-3/VI/3264) and the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Federal Teaching Hospital, Gombe, Nigeria (Reference: NHREC/25/04/2024) provided formal ethical approval for this study. Following regulatory authorization, a hospital-based cross-sectional descriptive survey was employed to examine the associations of physical activity, pain intensity, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) with the work ability of nursing professionals experiencing mechanical low back pain (LBP).

The investigation was conducted from April to October 2024 at a prominent tertiary referral hospital in Gombe, North-Eastern Nigeria. This facility was selected due to its high patient volume and its representative cohort of nursing professionals who routinely serve a highly diverse regional population.

Participant recruitment and selection pathway

The target population was strictly limited to professional clinical nurses actively engaged in direct bedside care roles. To ensure methodological transparency, a multi-stage screening and recruitment protocol was enforced. Eligible participants were initially identified by auditing institutional nursing duty rosters across major shifts. Prospective candidates were approached by the research team during scheduled shift handovers or low-workload break windows.

Mechanical low back pain was clinically operationalized as pain, discomfort, or structural muscle tension localized between the 12th rib and the inferior gluteal folds, with or without somatic lower-limb radiation, where symptoms were clearly modified or provoked by physical movement, loading, or distinct postures. To ensure diagnostic homogeneity, an experienced clinical physical therapist verified each case using objective physical screening, evaluating lumbar active range of motion restriction and ruling out neurological deficits through straight-leg raise testing.

Inclusion required a confirmed clinical history of mechanical low back pain persisting for a minimum of three months, and active deployment in an inpatient or outpatient bedside care capacity. Nurses were excluded if the back pain was secondary to suspected or confirmed systemic conditions—such as spinal malignancies, infectious discitis, or inflammatory arthropathies or if they presented with active pregnancy or a history of invasive spinal surgery.

A power analysis performed using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9) for a simultaneous multiple linear regression design with three independent variables, an assumed medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), a critical significance alpha level (alpha = 0.05), and a target statistical power (1 - beta = 0.95) indicated a minimum sample requirement of 119 participants. Our final analyzed cohort of 188 clinical nurses safely exceeded this statistical threshold, providing sufficient power to detect significant predictors.

Sampling and data collection procedure

Prospective participants were sourced from various clinical departments of the hospital, including Internal Medicine, General Surgery, Pediatrics, and Obstetrics and Gynecology, using a purposive sampling method. This approach ensured that the sample accurately reflected the varying physical demands, patient transfer volumes, and lifting postures characteristic of different nursing specialties within the tertiary center. However, it is explicitly acknowledged that this non-probability sampling approach may introduce localized selection bias and limit the broader generalizability of the findings to secondary or private medical institutions.

Prospective candidates were thoroughly briefed on the study’s clinical objectives, and written informed consent was obtained from all participating volunteers prior to data collection. All experimental procedures involving human participants were conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Biodata and sociodemographic information of the participants were recorded. All questionnaires were administered in a standardized layout during neutral periods in clinical shifts to minimize workplace interference. A trained research assistant remained physically present during these sessions to provide technical clarification on scale items, ensuring data completeness and protecting the integrity of the self-reported data without introducing response bias.

Statistical analysis

Data processing was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 25.0). Descriptive parameters were expressed as means and standard deviations for continuous data, and as absolute frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The internal consistency of the psychometric scales within this specific sample was verified using Cronbach’s alpha (alpha), demonstrating highly acceptable values across all scales: alpha = 0.82 for the WAI, alpha = 0.79 for the IPAQ-SF, and alpha = 0.84 for the SF-36 PCS. Bivariate associations between the independent variables and work ability were evaluated using Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficients (r).

A multivariable linear regression model using the simultaneous entry method was constructed to compute the collective variance explained (R2) and isolate individual standardized regression coefficients (beta). Statistical significance was maintained at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The findings of this cross-sectional survey indicate clear associative and correlational relationships between physical activity, subjective pain intensity, health-related quality of life, and the work ability of clinical nurses experiencing mechanical low back pain (LBP) in Gombe, North-Eastern Nigeria. Rather than merely describing baseline scores, the simultaneous regression model demonstrates that these variables represent an integrated network of behavioral and clinical factors associated with an individual’s perceived capacity to manage occupational demands. A primary finding of this investigation is the robust independent association between physical activity and work ability scores. This statistical significance indicates that within this cohort of clinical nurses, the reported level of routine movement and exercise engagement shares a stronger relationship with sustained work capacity than the subjective severity of their musculoskeletal pain symptoms [4, 12]. This finding coincides with the work of Gordon and Bloxham [9], who observed that active behavioral movement profiles are linked to higher functional capacity over time than passive symptom relief or rest protocols.

Physiologically, routine physical activity is often associated with underlying core muscular endurance, lumbar joint stability, and cardiovascular capacity required to execute heavy manual nursing tasks such as lifting dependent patients and enduring prolonged static standing especially within clinical settings characterized by minimal mechanical or ergonomic assistance [2, 5]. Therefore, the data suggest that a nurse’s localized functional capacity is heavily related to active movement behaviors, which characterize individuals who avoid the deconditioning effects typically associated with mechanical spinal disorders.

A critical interpretation of the multivariable model explains why health-related quality of life and pain intensity experienced an attenuation in their independent predictive values when evaluated simultaneously alongside physical activity. This change is explained by the high bivariate correlation observed between physical activity and the physical component of quality of life, indicating a substantial amount of shared variance. Within this nursing cohort, this overlap reflects a clear biopsychosocial pathway. Elevated pain intensity from mechanical strain typically acts as a biological trigger for kinesiophobia the fear of movement or re-injury [6, 12]. This fear response is associated with a voluntary reduction in physical activity, which frequently co-occurs with musculoskeletal deconditioning, reduced overall physical well-being (HRQoL), and ultimately diminishes the individual’s self-reported work ability [7, 8, 14]. Because all three variables maintained independent statistical significance within the final model, our results indicate that interventions addressing occupational low back pain may be sub-optimal if they rely exclusively on passive medical means (such as analgesic prescriptions or enforced rest) without considering accompanying physical activity patterns when evaluating indicators of career longevity.

The multiple linear regression model accounted for 21.5% of the total variance in work ability. While a substantial portion (78.5%) of the variance remains unexplained, explaining over one-fifth of the total variance is practically and clinically significant for a complex behavioral and occupational outcome. This level of explained variance is consistent with international occupational health studies investigating work ability among healthcare cohorts, where single-model predictors rarely capture a dominant majority of variance due to the multi-factorial nature of workplace performance [3, 11]. The remaining unexplained variance underscores that a nurse’s perception of their work capacity is also guided by external organizational and structural factors not measured in this study. In public referral hospitals, these likely include administrative nurse-to-patient ratios, the frequency of overnight shift rotations, variations in baseline physical fitness, and localized psychosocial workplace hazards such as low job control or insufficient administrative support [4, 13]. Consequently, our empirical findings should be viewed as an evaluation of vital individual behavioral and clinical factors, within a wider framework of structural and institutional workplace determinants.

The primary contribution of this study to occupational health knowledge lies in demonstrating that physical activity emerged as the strongest modifiable factor associated with work ability within a developing health system operating with severe structural understaffing and an absolute deficit of mechanical lifting equipment. However, several methodological limitations constrain the external validity and interpretation of these findings. First, due to the cross-sectional design of the survey, it is impossible to infer clinical causality or establish directional timelines among the variables. The observed temporal simultaneity means it cannot be definitively determined whether higher routine physical activity levels support better work ability, or if clinical nurses who maintain higher baseline work capacity are naturally more capable of remaining physically active. Second, the utilization of non-probability purposive sampling within a single public tertiary referral facility limits the generalizability of these findings. The unique occupational constraints and extreme patient loads experienced by clinical nurses in Gombe may not accurately reflect the work environments, ergonomic support structures, or staff experiences found in secondary or private healthcare facilities across Nigeria. Third, the study relied entirely on self-report questionnaires to quantify physical activity, pain, and quality of life metrics, which introduces an inherent vulnerability to recall bias and social desirability bias. Fourth, as a limitation inherent to the rapid screening required for this cross-sectional survey, while an experienced physical therapist utilized the straight-leg raise test to screen out lower lumbar radiculopathy, a comprehensive neurological examination triad (including explicit myotomal, dermatomal, reflex, and upper lumbar dural testing) was not conducted. Consequently, the potential inclusion of participants with subtle or upper lumbar neurological impairments cannot be entirely discounted, which represents a limitation in our diagnostic screening protocol. “Finally, because all data points were collected simultaneously via a single standardized survey instrument, there is an inherent risk of common method variance inflating the baseline bivariate relationships.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that physical activity, pain intensity, and health-related quality of life are all significant factors associated with the work ability of clinical nurses managing mechanical low back pain in Gombe, Nigeria. Crucially, the data reveal that physical activity stands as the strongest independent correlate of work ability within this nursing cohort, demonstrating a more substantial relationship with institutional function than the subjective intensity of clinical pain symptoms. Based on these findings, policy makers and hospital administrators in North-East Nigeria should consider a strategic paradigm shift away from passive, reactive pain relief protocols toward structured, low-cost institutional physical activity and exercise rehabilitation initiatives, as these behavioral factors share a vital connection with the work ability of the healthcare workforce.
