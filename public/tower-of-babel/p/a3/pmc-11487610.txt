Metal–Ligand Covalency in the Valence Excited States of Metal Dithiolenes Revealed by S 1s3p Resonant Inelastic X-ray Scattering
Authors: Christopher
B Larsen, Kathryn Ledbetter, Daniel R Nascimento, Elisa Biasin, Muhammad Qureshi, Stanisław
H Nowak, Dimosthenis Sokaras, Niranjan Govind, Amy A Cordones
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetallo dithiolene complexes with biological and catalytic relevance are well-known for having strong metal–ligand covalency, which dictates their valence electronic structures. We present the resonant sulfur Kβ (1s3p) X-ray emission spectroscopy (XES) for a series of Ni and Cu bis(dithiolene) complexes to reveal the ligand sulfur contributions to both the occupied and unoccupied valence orbitals. While S K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy played a critical role in identifying the covalency of the unoccupied orbitals of metal dithiolenes, the present focus on XES explores the occupied density of states. For a series of [Cu(mnt)2]n− and [Ni(mnt)2]n− anions and dianions, a comparison of the nonresonant and resonant S Kβ XES spectra highlights the dramatic improvement in spectral resolution and corresponding ability to differentiate subtle changes in occupied electronic structure across the series. Furthermore, the use of resonant inelastic X-ray scattering (RIXS) probes the valence excited states and the core–valence couplings of the complexes. By employing a theoretical approach based on time-dependent density functional theory to interpret the RIXS spectra, we reveal how metal–ligand covalency influences the excited state energies and covalencies. We identify the low energy excited states as having the same symmetry as the nominal “ligand field” or “d–d” states that typically dominate the photophysics of 3d metal complexes but with significant metal–ligand charge transfer character dictated by their covalency. These results suggest that strong metal–ligand covalency can be used to influence the charge-transfer photochemistry of first row transition metal complexes.

Introduction

Metal sulfur coordination complexes are biologically and synthetically relevant, often playing a central role in the reactivity of bioenzymes and therefore widely mimicked for applications in redox chemistry and catalysis.1−9 Due to the high covalency of the metal–sulfur bonding, the nature of the sulfur bearing ligands can strongly influence the electronic properties and redox activity of the metal active site. For example, metal dithiolene complexes (with particular emphasis on metal-bis(dithiolenes) in the present work) have been widely investigated for their strongly covalent electronic structures and the known redox noninnocence of the dithiolene ligand.10−12 The sulfur oxidation state of dithiolenes can vary between the neutral dithione and dianionic dithiolate. Taken together with their highly covalent metal bonding interactions, this can make the formal assignment of metal and ligand redox states ambiguous. The many applications of metal dithiolenes in chemistry and catalysis have made use of this cooperative reactivity of metal and dithiolene ligand.13,14 Thus, element-specific characterization methods are highly important to understand the degree of metal–ligand covalency and its effect on electronic structure for this class of metal–sulfur complexes.

Wide ranging electronic spectroscopies have been applied to characterize and quantify the metal–ligand covalency of metal-bis(dithiolenes).10,15−17 Of particular emphasis, S K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS, Figure 1a) was central to quantifying the S 3p character in their unoccupied frontier molecular orbitals.18−23 This is possible due to the presence of pre-edge absorption features in the S K-edge XAS spectra, which are attributed to unoccupied valence orbitals of mixed S 3p and metal d character and have intensities proportional to the S 3p character in the corresponding orbital.24 These studies led to the identification of “inverted bonding” in Ni- and Cu-bis(dithiolenes), where the mixing of ligand orbitals that are destabilized relative to the metal d orbitals results in frontier orbitals with more sulfur than metal character.18 However, XAS is limited to the access of unoccupied molecular orbitals that participate in the electron accepting reactions of the complexes. There have been limited investigations focused on the covalency of the occupied valence orbitals and how it may influence their electron donating reactions.

Schematic depiction of (a) S K-edge XAS, and nonresonant Kα and Kβ XES; and (b) resonance inelastic X-ray scattering (RIXS).

S K-edge X-ray emission spectroscopy (XES, Figure 1a) instead accesses the occupied orbitals by probing either valence-to-core (S 3p-to-1s, Kβ) or core-to-core (S 2p-to-1s, Kα) transitions. While nonresonant S Kα and Kβ XES (as shown in Figure 1a) exhibit indirect sensitivity to changes in the S atom oxidation state, the spectra are dominated by broad overlapping features and they are largely found to be less sensitive to subtle changes in valence electronic structure and covalency than XAS.25−31 1s–3p resonant inelastic X-ray scattering (RIXS) combines the XAS and XES methods, and therefore probes both the occupied and unoccupied frontier molecular orbitals and the core–valence excited state couplings. The K-edge absorption process creates a S 1s core-hole that is filled by transitions from the occupied S 3p-containing valence orbitals. Additionally, the RIXS processes leaves the system in a valence, rather than core, excited state (Figure 1b), significantly decreasing the lifetime broadening and resulting in sharpened spectral features, when compared to nonresonant XES.

The sensitivity of RIXS to metal–ligand orbital covalency is derived from the intensities of the absorption and/or scattering transitions, which reflect the delocalization/overlap of the valence orbitals with the localized core hole.32,33 Thus, to identify ligand contributions to the occupied valence orbitals, one would preferably measure the ligand atom K-edge RIXS spectra. A few examples of this in the soft X-ray regime (largely for ligand N atoms) have been reported, often combining the ligand K-edge with metal L-edge RIXS to gain a comprehensive view of valence electronic structure and metal–ligand bonding interactions.34−39 For coordination complexes with a normal bonding description (valence antibonding orbitals with primarily metal d character), ligand atom K-edge RIXS primarily probes higher energy valence excited states with ligand-centered character (ligand π–π*), while metal L-edge RIXS primarily probes lower energy excited states with metal-centered (“d–d” or “ligand field”) character.32 Weak transitions corresponding to metal-to-ligand or ligand-to-metal charge transfer may only be observed if there is sufficient mixing of metal and ligand character in the unoccupied orbitals. However, for the inverted bonding description of metal bis(dithiolenes) (antibonding valence orbitals with primarily ligand character), one could imagine that their strong metal–ligand covalency results in high sensitivity not only to the ligand-centered, but also charge transfer excitations, and even the ligand field excitations previously only observed at the metal L-edges. Given this amplified sensitivity to metal–ligand covalency, S K-edge RIXS is an obvious extension of the previous electronic structure studies of metal bis(dithiolenes) to establish a similar understanding of occupied valence electronic structure and the nature of their lowest lying optically dark valence excited states. However, due to a small number of emission spectrometers designed to operate in the 2–5 keV range,40,41 RIXS studies for absorption edges in the tender X-ray regime have been scarce.42−47

Here we report a systematic S Kβ RIXS study of metal bis(dithiolenes), including [Ni(mnt)2]−, [Ni(mnt)2]2–, [Cu(mnt)2]−, and [Cu(mnt)2]2– (where “mnt” is the prototypical maleonitriledithiolate ligand), as shown in Figure 2. This series of complexes was selected to probe the effects of one-electron changes in the valence occupation (formally d7, d8, and d9 occupations) and to compare isoelectronic metals ([Ni(mnt)2]2– and [Cu(mnt)2]−). Direct comparisons can be drawn with the early S K-edge XAS studies on the same series.20 We report the S K-edge XAS with an improved resolution of the pre-edge absorption features due to high energy resolution fluorescence detection (HERFD), and the nonresonant Kα and Kβ XES spectra, which are demonstrated to provide limited sensitivity to the valence electronic structure due to the broad features observed. Most importantly, we report the S Kβ valence RIXS spectra for the full series of complexes. We combine our results with a recently developed theoretical approach to valence RIXS simulations using time-dependent density functional theory48 to investigate the assignment of RIXS features and dependence on changes in electronic structure across the series of complexes. Well-resolved RIXS features corresponding to scattering from occupied valence orbitals with mixed metal 3d and S 3p character are observed, reflecting the strong metal–ligand covalency of the occupied electronic valence structure. These measurements additionally characterize the charge transfer character and the energies of the lowest energy, optically dark, valence excited states of the complexes. This work presents S Kβ RIXS as a newly explored, powerful method capable of assessing the metal–ligand covalency of the occupied electronic structure, analogous to S K-edge XAS for the unoccupied states. Systematic changes in ligand field splitting and metal–ligand covalency are observed as a function of metal and one-electron oxidation changes, highlighting the impact of this method for driving rational design of transition metal complexes with highly covalent S-containing ligands.

Results and Discussion

S K-Edge X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy

Normalized S K-edge XAS spectra, recorded in both total fluorescence yield (TFY, solid black traces) and high energy resolution fluorescence detection based on S Kα XES (HERFD, dashed black traces) are presented for the [Ni(mnt)2]−,2– and [Cu(mnt)2]−,2– complexes in Figure 3a. The TFY spectra reproduce those previously reported by Sarangi et al. for the same set of complexes.20 The spectra and assignments of the absorption features are reviewed again here due to their relevance for the resonant excitation emission measurements that follow. For each complex, the XAS features highlighted in orange and red are consistent with those observed in the free ligand (mnt2–), whereas those in green and blue are unique to the metal complexes.20 The assignments of each absorption feature to specific unoccupied/partially occupied orbitals shown in Figure 3 are made on the basis of TD-DFT calculations (Tables S11A–S14A) and are generally consistent with the assignments reported previously for these and other related metal bis(dithiolenes).18−21,23 The orange and red absorption features attributed to ligand-centered excitations are assigned here as transitions into dominantly ligand character orbitals with S–C π* (4au and 5b3g) and σ* (21a1g and 22a1g) symmetries, respectively. The lower energy blue and green pre-edge absorption features probe transitions into the unoccupied frontier orbitals with mixed S 3p and Cu/Ni 3d character. The green pre-edge peak present for all complexes is attributed to excitation into the 15b1g orbital (Cu/Ni 3dxy + S 3p character). The blue peak present only for [Ni(mnt)2]− is attributed to excitation into the 5b2g orbital (Ni 3dxz + S/C/N np character), which is only partially unoccupied in this complex. For both pre-edge features, the absorption intensity is proportional to the S 3p contributions to the frontier orbitals and is thus highly sensitive to metal–ligand covalency. Our experimental and DFT results are consistent with the previous findings20 of nearly equal metal 3d and S 3p character in the 15b1g orbital and dominant ligand character in the 5b2g orbital (Tables S1 and S2).

For the most part, these key excitations can be clearly resolved in the TFY XAS spectra. However, for [Ni(mnt)2]2–, there is strong overlap between the orange and green features. Previous work recognized that the asymmetry of the apparent lowest energy TFY XAS feature of [Ni(mnt)2]2– arises from overlap of two close-lying features, and resolved them using spectral fitting.20 Using the HERFD technique by integrating the S Kα emission over a narrow energy window (∼0.5 eV), we are able to record higher resolution XAS spectra (Figure 3a, dotted black traces) and experimentally distinguish the features that overlap when attempting conventional XAS.

Non-Resonant S Kα and Kβ X-ray Emission Spectroscopy

Nonresonant S K-edge XES has been explored extensively in both inorganic25−27 and organic26,28−30 S-containing compounds to assess its sensitivity to S oxidation state and bonding. The nonresonant S Kα and Kβ XES spectra of the [Ni(mnt)2]2–,– and [Cu(mnt)2]2–,– complexes are shown in Figure 4. The Kα spectrum reflects the 2p-to-1s transitions of S and is characterized by two large peaks due to the spin–orbit splitting of the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 final states. The shape of the nonresonant Kα spectra of all four measured complexes (Figure 4, black traces) are invariant across the series. Generally, the shape of the Kα spectrum is insensitive to the chemical environment of S, as it probes transitions between core levels that are not involved in bonding. However, monotonic shifts in the Kα spectrum are ubiquitously observed for S atoms of varied oxidation state, reflecting the changes in electronic screening of nuclear charge on the core levels.25,26,28 For the complexes measured here, the Kα1 peaks are all centered between 2307.9 and 2308.0 eV, consistent with sulfide compounds reported previously.25,26,28 A small shift of <0.1 eV distinguishes [Ni(mnt)2]− from all other complexes, consistent with it having the lowest valence electron count. The small magnitude of the redshift upon reduction of [Ni(mnt)2]− is consistent with the expectation for an incomplete change in S valence state (previous work indicates a shift of <0.25 eV per 1 electron change in S valence).25 Similarly, the lack of significant further energetic shift upon reduction of [Cu(mnt)2]− is consistent with the higher metal character in the electron accepting orbital of the Cu anion.20

Nonresonant S Kα (left, black) and Kβ (right, blue) X-ray emission spectra (Ei = 2950.0 eV) for investigated complexes.

The valence-to-core Kβ emission spectrum probes transitions between the valence 3p containing orbitals and the core 1s orbitals, and thus is inherently sensitive to the valence electronic structure of S. The shape of the Kβ spectrum has been found to vary sufficiently to distinguish some classes of organosulfur compounds (i.e., aryl vs aliphatic sulfides/thiols vs thioketones; sulfides vs sulfoxides vs sulfones), but with limited variability between similar classes (i.e., thiols vs sulfides vs disulfides), and insufficient sensitivity to more subtle changes in valence electronic structure (i.e., substituent group changes) within a given class.26,28−30 The Kβ spectra of all four complexes reported here (Figure 4, blue traces) are similar in shape to those of the sulfides published previously.26,30 The character of the highest energy Kβ emission features of both aryl and aliphatic sulfides was previously noted to have primarily S and C p-character, while the lower energy features increased in s-character, on the basis of ab initio ground state calculations.26 While the highest energy emission features (i.e., the shoulder just below 2470 eV) should be attributed to the occupied frontier orbitals, it is clear that the broadness of those features will not allow one to distinguish small changes in valence electron occupation or covalency. Thus, overall, we find that the nonresonant XES spectra cannot elucidate the detailed electronic structure changes of the occupied frontier orbitals of the [Ni(mnt)2]2–,– and [Cu(mnt)2]2–,– series.

S 1s3p Resonant Inelastic X-ray Scattering

To overcome the issue of spectral broadening in the nonresonant valence-to-core S Kβ emission spectra, we investigated resonant valence-to-core S Kβ emission spectroscopy, also referred to as S 1s3p RIXS. The RIXS process is illustrated in Figure 1b. By tuning the incident X-ray energy (Ei) to that of an XAS transition, an intermediate state with a core hole and valence electron is formed. The S 1s core hole is then repopulated by emission from an occupied S 3p orbital (corresponding to scattering of a photon with energy Ee). This results in a final state with valence excitation character and energy (Ei – Ee). In RIXS, spectral broadening is determined by the lifetime of the final valence excited state, as opposed to that of the core excited state (as is the case for nonresonant XES), resulting in substantially improved spectral resolution.

The S Kβ nonresonant XES and S 1s3p RIXS spectra of [Cu(mnt)2]2– are presented together in Figure 5, and for the other complexes in Figures S4–S6. Immediately apparent is that the RIXS spectra (color traces) exhibit substantially narrower line widths, resulting in sharper spectral features relative to the nonresonant spectrum (black trace). This is particularly notable on the high energy side of the nonresonant spectrum, where features corresponding to the highest occupied valence orbitals become clearly defined in the resonant spectra.

Nonresonant and resonant S Kβ X-ray emission spectra for [Cu(mnt)2]2–. Asterisks denote the elastic scattering peak of the incident photon energy. Parentheses denote key acceptor orbitals in resonant excitations. Colors correspond to excitations as depicted in Figure 3.

Excitation into the frontier orbitals of mixed metal–ligand character (i.e., 15b1g orbital, green trace) yields additional features, indicating excitation-dependent scattering, compared to excitation to ligand centered orbitals (4au and 5b3g in orange; 21ag and 22ag orbitals in red). Within the D2h point group, the valence orbitals with mixed metal–ligand character must possess gerade parity (as all of the d orbitals have gerade parity, Table S3). In RIXS, the Raman selection rules dictate that the final (valence excited) state conserves the parity of the initial (ground) state (Table S5). The outcome is that upon excitation into mixed metal–ligand character orbitals (i.e., 15b1g, 5b2g), the scattering occupied valence orbitals also possess mixed metal–ligand character (Tables S6–S8).

Peak Assignments in the 15b1g and 5b2g Excitation RIXS Spectra

The 15b1g and 5b2g excitation RIXS spectra for all complexes are shown in Figure 6A, plotted on an energy transfer (Ei–Es) axis. For the reasons described above, the symmetries of the valence excited states probed in the 15b1g or 5b2g excitation RIXS spectra match those of the ligand field (or “d–d”) excitations typical of noncovalent metal complexes and often measured using metal L-edge RIXS. Because of the large degree of S 3p hybridization with the metal d orbitals in the Cu and Ni bis(dithiolene) complexes, these ligand field symmetry excitations become apparent in the ligand S atom RIXS as well. To our knowledge, this has not been experimentally observed previously.

The 15b1g excitation RIXS spectra for all complexes are characterized by a well-separated low energy peak attributed to scattering off the 5b2g occupied orbital. The energies of the 5b2g–15b1g excited state are 1.38 eV for [Ni(mnt)2]2–, 1.15 eV for [Ni(mnt)2]−, 1.02 eV for [Cu(mnt)2]2–, and 0.74 eV for [Cu(mnt)2]−. These energies represent the splitting of the HOMO–LUMO (or HOMO–SOMO in the case of [Cu(mnt)2]2–) frontier orbitals. While this state shares the symmetry of the more traditional metal-centered dxz–dxy ligand field state, the considerable metal–ligand covalency complicates this assignment. The 5b2g HOMO is the π-antibonding combination of the dxz and bis(dithiolene) b2g ligand group orbitals, while the 15b1g LUMO/SOMO is the σ-antibonding combination of the dxy and bis(dithiolene) b1g ligand group orbitals. Thus, the energetic splitting between them indicates a change in the relative strength of π and σ metal–ligand interactions. We observe two key trends in the energies of the 5b2g–15b1g final state, to be assessed in more detail below: (1) the energy of this state is smaller for both Cu complexes compared to both Ni complexes, and (2) the energy of this state is smaller for the anionic complex of a given metal compared to its dianion.

First, the metal dependence of the 5b2g–15b1g energy is most clearly made by comparison of isoelectronic [Ni(mnt)2]2– and [Cu(mnt)2]−, which sees the splitting decrease from 1.38 to 0.74 eV as Ni is replaced with Cu. Given that these two complexes are isoelectronic, the primary relevant difference is the stabilization of the Cu d orbitals due to its larger effective nuclear charge compared to Ni.19,49 Within the inverted bonding picture, this stabilization implies less covalent metal–ligand interactions. Indeed, the 15b1g orbital of [Cu(mnt)2]− was found to have more ligand S character than that of [Ni(mnt)2]2–, consistent with decreased metal–ligand interaction.20 Thus, stabilization of both the π* and σ* valence orbitals is expected for the Cu compound. However, because the overlap integral for σ interactions is larger than for π interactions,50 it is expected that an equal decrease of dxy and dxz results in a larger stabilization of the σ* 15b1g orbital compared to the π* 5b2g orbital, resulting in the lower final state energy observed. The smaller decrease of the 5b2g–15b1g state energy by ca. 0.3 eV upon one-electron oxidation of both the Cu and Ni dianions has similar origin. Previous studies of these and related complexes showed that the more oxidized complexes have less covalent metal–ligand interactions.17,20 Thus, there is a larger splitting between the metal dxy/dxz and bis(dithiolene) b2g/b1g ligand group orbitals for the oxidized complexes, and so the σ* 15b1g orbital is expected to stabilize more than the π* 5b2g orbital for the same reasons described above.

The second peak of the 15b1g excitation RIXS spectra of each complex can also be assigned, as they are generally attributed to one (or two) intense transitions. The assignment of this feature is metal-dependent. For the Cu complexes, the dominant transition underlying the second RIXS peak is attributed to a 4b3g–15b1g excitation. For the Ni complexes it is attributed to a 4b2g–15b1g excitation (with contributions from a 20ag–5b3g transition in the case of [Ni(mnt)2]2–). This difference is explained by considering the DFT calculated differences in the S 3p character of the 4b2g and 4b3g donor orbitals for the Cu versus Ni complexes. As noted above, less covalent metal–ligand interactions are observed for Cu, due to the stabilization of the Cu d orbitals relative to Ni. The 4b2g donor orbital is the π-bonding combination of the metal 3dxz and ligand b2g orbital (5b2g is the corresponding antibonding combination). In the inverted bonding picture, the less covalent Cu-ligand interactions result in less S character in the bonding 4b2g orbital. Thus, the 4b2g–15b1g final states will have less intensity for the Cu complexes than for the Ni complexes. Alternatively, the 4b3g donor orbital is the antibonding combination of the metal dyz and ligand b3g orbitals. The less covalent Cu-ligand interactions, in this case, result in more S character in the antibonding 4b3g orbital and thus the Cu complexes have stronger intensity RIXS features for the 4b3g–15b1g final state.

The two lowest energy features of the 5b2g excitation RIXS spectrum of [Ni(mnt)2]− are assigned to the 20ag–5b2g and 4b2g–5b2g final states. The symmetry of the former matches that of the more traditional metal-centered dz2–dxz ligand field state, while the latter is the splitting of the bonding and antibonding dxz ± ligand b2g orbitals. It is plausible that the lowest energy peak in the measured spectrum gained some intensity due to the overlapping contributions of the 15b1g excitation. The higher energy peaks in both the 15b1g and 5b2g excitation RIXS spectra are generally broader and more challenging to assign to a specific transition. The orbitals involved in the final states for the largest calculated transitions in this region are indicated in Figure 6A but are not discussed further here.

Finally, we draw the analogy again between the final states measured in the 15b1g and 5b2g excitation RIXS spectra and the ligand field excited states. Although the ligand field states are electric dipole forbidden, weak visible absorption transitions can be observed if they do not overlap with stronger symmetry-allowed transitions. In comparing the 5b2g–15b1g RIXS final state energies to the lowest energy features of the ligand field absorption spectra reported previously we observe a remarkable match: The lowest ligand field transitions were reported at 1.45 eV for [Ni(mnt)2]2–, 1.03 eV for [Ni(mnt)2]−, 1.03 eV for [Cu(mnt)2]2–, and 0.79 eV for [Cu(mnt)2]−.51−53 While these features were initially interpreted to indicate metal-centered d–d transitions, the strength of the corresponding S K-edge RIXS features is evidence for the strong ligand S character in both the occupied and unoccupied valence orbitals. While the equivalence of the measured energies highlights the complementarity of the optical and X-ray methods, the inherent elemental specificity of RIXS reveals additional information about the valence excited state charge distribution that cannot be accessed by optical methods. This is assessed in detail in the following section.

Charge Transfer Character in the “Ligand Field” Excited States of Metal Bis(dithiolenes)

As noted above, the observation of the ligand field excited states in the S K-edge RIXS spectra of the Ni- and Cu-bis(dithiolenes) is indicative of the significant S 3p contributions to those states. Rather than having pure d-d excitation character, the large degree of metal–ligand covalency in the frontier orbitals confers some ligand character onto the excited states. Depending on the relative metal and ligand contributions to each of the donor and acceptor orbitals involved in the valence excitations, the ligand field states can take on ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT), metal-to-ligand charge transfer (MLCT), or intraligand (IL) character. This character can be assessed from the DFT and TD-DFT calculation results by considering the change in relative contributions of metal and ligand character in the donor and acceptor orbitals involved in each transition in the RIXS spectrum.

Considering the 15b1g excitation RIXS spectra for all complexes, the acceptor 15b1g orbital is nearly equally split between metal and ligand character (41–47% metal) for all complexes except [Cu(mnt)2]−, which has significantly more ligand character (27% Cu). Thus, when the donor orbital is dominantly metal in character, the excitation takes on partial MLCT (more appropriately metal-to-mixed metal and ligand charge transfer) character. Alternatively, when the donor orbital is dominantly ligand in character, the excitation takes on partial LMCT (more appropriately ligand-to-mixed metal and ligand charge transfer) character. The donor and acceptor orbital contributions are delineated in Table S15 for the calculated transitions that comprise the first two peaks in the RIXS spectrum. The charge transfer character in excited states probed by the S RIXS spectra are also indicated by the color coding in the calculated RIXS transitions shown in Figure 6A. Cyan sticks indicate significant LMCT character (>10% increase in metal character in acceptor orbital relative to donor orbital), orange transitions indicate significant MLCT character (>10% increase in ligand character in acceptor orbital relative to donor orbital), and gray transition indicate excited state that do not involve CT character (or have very small oscillator strength <0.1 or do not fall within the first 5.7 eV, which were not analyzed).

For all complexes measured here, the lowest energy 5b2g–15b1g ligand field excited state has partial LMCT character. The 5b2g donor orbital is dominantly ligand in character (79–95% ligand character), resulting in significant 22–36% increase in metal character upon excitation (orbital character in Table S15 and Figure 6B). While the symmetry of this state is consistent with a dxz–dxy excitation, the large ligand contributions in the 5b2g donor orbital indicate that excitation results in significant hole density on the ligand, making LMCT a better description of the excited state character.

The second peak in the 15b1g excitation RIXS spectra differ in charge transfer character for the two metals, having partial LMCT character for both [Cu(mnt)2]−,2– complexes and partial MLCT character for both [Ni(mnt)2]−,2– complexes (see Figure). For the Cu complexes, the 4b3g donor orbital is primarily ligand S 3p character, and thus the valence excitation results in a 17–28% increase in metal character (orbital character in Table S15 and Figure 6B). For the Ni complexes, the 4b2g donor orbital is primarily Ni 3d character. Thus, the valence excitation results in a 16–24% decrease in metal character (orbital character in Table S15 and Figure 6B).

In the 5b2g excitation RIXS spectrum of [Ni(mnt)2]− the two lowest energy RIXS transitions are found to have MLCT character (Figure 6). The donor orbitals are 87% (20ag) – 60% (4b2g) metal in character (dz2 and dxz, respectively), while the 5b2g acceptor orbital is predominately ligand in character (73%). This results in 60% (peak 1) to 33% (peak 2) MLCT character upon excitation. The more intense transitions contributing to main peak in the 5b2g excitation RIXS spectrum do not involve metal–ligand charge transfer.

The above analysis of charge transfer character in the “ligand field” excited states of the [Cu(mnt)2]−,2– and [Ni(mnt)2]−,2– complexes is derived from the calculations of RIXS transitions and orbital compositions, without direct experimental input. In principle, the experimental RIXS data (i.e., absolute intensity) should also reveal the strength of ligand S contributions to each of the measured valence excited final states. As described by Jay et al.,32 the RIXS transition intensity described using the Kramers–Heisenberg formula54,55 is sensitive to metal–ligand covalency through the RIXS matrix elements (oscillator strengths) describing dipole transitions between the initial/final and intermediate states. By expanding the valence orbitals involved in these states to a LCAO description, Jay et al. suggests that the S K-edge RIXS intensity should be proportional to the product of the squares of S 3p contributions in the donor and acceptor orbitals of the final state. Thus, for the RIXS spectra reported here, the well-resolved peak attributed to the 5b2g–15b1g excited state should lend itself to a quantitative comparison of the excited state covalency across the series of [Cu(mnt)2]−,2– and [Ni(mnt)2]−,2– complexes. The experimental challenges to overcome for such quantitative comparisons of absolute intensity relate to measuring and/or accounting for differences in sample conditions, such as S concentration and matrix composition/thickness, sample geometry (incident/takeoff angle and thickness/shape), accurate mass absorption coefficients for the incident and the fluorescence/scattered photons, incident X-ray flux, reproducible response of the X-ray spectrometers, stability of the sample environment (i.e., partial pressure of He atmosphere), etc. In the absence of careful systematic considerations of these parameters one cannot directly compare absolute intensities of the RIXS features between different samples. In subsequent work, we aim to address these challenges with the goal of forming a quantitative analysis framework to assess excited state covalency from the experimentally detected RIXS intensities, analogous to the framework that exists for extracting covalency from S K-edge XAS pre-edge peak intensities.

Conclusions

We report the first to our knowledge resonant S K-edge emission spectroscopy for S-containing metal complexes, interpreted using theoretical calculations based on TD-DFT methods. A series of [Cu(mnt)2]−,2– and [Ni(mnt)2]−,2– complexes were selected to emphasize the sensitivity of the S RIXS spectra to metal–ligand covalency. The S K-edge XAS of this series is consistent with previous reports, however, the improved resolution achieved here using HERFD-XAS better resolves the overlapping absorption features. While the nonresonant S Kα and Kβ emission spectra are shown to be largely insensitive to the changes in electronic configuration and covalency across the series of measured complexes, the resonant Kβ emission spectra are found to exhibit pronounced complex-dependent spectral differences. The observation of sharp spectral features in the S 1s3p RIXS spectra, not observed in the nonresonant XES, enable a much higher degree of sensitivity to changes in electronic structure and covalency. The observed RIXS features were compared to TD-DFT simulated spectra to assign the underlying valence excited final states.

Importantly, the resonant excitation into unoccupied orbitals with mixed metal–ligand character give rise to final states with “ligand-field” or “d–d” excitation symmetry. Their strong intensity in the S RIXS spectrum indicate significant ligand contributions to the donor/acceptor orbitals, resulting in considerable charge transfer character. The dithiolene contributions to the lowest energy ligand field excited states are assessed based on the calculated orbital contributions for each RIXS transition. The lowest energy ligand field states are found to have partial LMCT or MLCT character, dependent on the differences in metal–ligand covalency in the donor orbitals involved in the transitions.

The observation of significant charge transfer character in the nominal “ligand field” excited states and the ability to capture those changes have important implications for the photochemistry of metal dithiolenes. The ligand field states dominate the photophysics of first row transition metal complexes. The “d–d” excitation character that is typical of these states often does not enable the intramolecular charge separation that underlies the photoredox chemistry driven by second and third row metal complexes. However, we demonstrate herein that strong metal–ligand covalency can confer charge transfer character onto these states. This suggests that metal–ligand covalency could be utilized to tune and explore the photoredox activity of highly covalent first row transition metal complexes.

Methods

Samples

The synthesis of [(C2H5)4N]2[Cu(mnt)2] and [(C2H5)4N]2[Ni(mnt)2] complexes followed the procedure of reference (56). The synthesis of [(C2H5)4N][Cu(mnt)2] and [(C2H5)4N]2[Ni(mnt)2] followed the procedure of reference (57), with a modification for [(C2H5)4N][Cu(mnt)2] which instead used AgPF6 as an oxidant in dichloromethane.

S K-Edge XAS Measurements

S K-edge XAS spectra were collected at beamline 4–3 at SSRL. Thin powder samples were prepared by diluting the [Cu(mnt)2]−,2– and [Ni(mnt)2]−,2– complexes using sucrose (∼10% by mass), grinding the mixture to a powder, and pressing the powder on carbon tape. The powder samples were mounted in a liquid He cryostream during measurement. XAS was measured by scanning the incident X-ray energy and detecting total S fluorescence using a silicon drift detector. The XAS spectra were normalized by the incident X-ray flux (∼1012 ph/s) measured before the sample. The Si(111) double-crystal monochromator resolution was ∼0.25 eV, and the energy was calibrated to the first thiosulfate (Na2S2O3 5H2O) peak at 2472.02 eV. Radiation damage was assessed by monitoring for spectral changes upon successive scans at one sample position, and no damage was observed. The reported spectra are the average of two scans measured per sample. A baseline (constant, set to the minimum value) was subtracted and the data was normalized to a linear fit between 2550 and 2670 eV.

S K-Edge XES, HERFD, and RIXS Measurements

All S K-edge X-ray emission spectroscopy measurements were performed at beamline 6–2a at SSRL. A liquid nitrogen cooled Si(111) monochromator provided tunable monochromatic X-rays in the vicinity of the S K-edge with an energy resolution of ∼0.25 eV. The monochromator energy was calibrated by setting the S K-edge whiteline of KSO4 at 2482.6 eV. The X-rays were focused to a spot size of 400 × 250 μm2 (v × h, full width at half-maximum). The emission spectra were recorded using a high energy resolution Johannson-type X-ray spectrometer designed for the tender X-ray regime40 using a cylindrically bent Si(111) analyzer crystal (resolution ∼0.32 eV at the S Kβ emission energy) and a CCD camera detector, all enclosed in a vacuum chamber. The X-ray emission spectrometer calibration for the S Kβ region was performed using elastic scattering scans across the energy range of interest. The sulfur Kα regime was calibrated by setting the Kα1 and Kα2 peaks of Na2S2O4 to 2309.12 and 2307.90 eV, respectively.25 All nonresonant XES measurements were collected using an incident energy of 2950 eV. The RIXS scans were collected at the incident energies indicated in the text. The HERFD data were collected by scanning the incident energy from 2460 to 2490 eV with a minimum step size of 0.2 eV, and detecting the S Kα1 emission intensity at its maximum. The spectrometer CCD images were analyzed as described in reference.40

The sample chamber was He-purged and mounted either pellets containing powder samples of the metal complexes investigated herein or a liquid jet. The liquid jet was a 250 μm diameter round jet created by flowing the sample solutions through a Kapton capillary. The sample solutions were recirculated using an HPLC pump. Powder samples were prepared as 7 mm diameter pellets using 10 mg sucrose (for pellet binding) and 40 mg of the [Cu(mnt)2]−,2– or [Ni(mnt)2]−,2– complexes. Solution samples of [Cu(mnt)2]2– and [Ni(mnt)2]2– were measured at 50 mM concentration in acetonitrile solutions (the [Cu(mnt)2]− and [Ni(mnt)2]− complexes were found to be unstable in solution and were thus only measured in powder form). The X-ray emission spectra were acquired for 15–20 min (Kα solutions samples), 1–2 min (Kα solid samples), 23–46 min (Kβ solutions samples), or 3.5–5.8 min (Kβ solid samples).

Electronic Structure Calculations and S K-Edge XAS and RIXS Simulations

All ground state DFT and excited state TD-DFT calculations were performed with the NWChem computational chemistry package.58,59 The TD-DFT calculations were performed employing the Tamm-Dancoff Approximation.60 The B3LYP61,62 exchange-correlation was used for all calculations. For the basis sets, the ligand atoms were described with the 6–311+G* basis,63−66 while the metal centers were described with the Sapporo-TZP-2012+Diffuse basis67 set. To generate the manifold of S 1s excited states, a restricted excitation window68−70 from −90.0 to −85.0 Eh was chosen and a total of 200 roots were computed for each complex, while the manifold of valence excited states employed no window restriction and was composed of 200 roots. All calculated XAS spectra plotted in Figure 3 were shifted arbitrarily to better align with the measured spectra (shifts: [Cu(mnt)2]2– 57.7 eV, [Cu(mnt)2]− 57.5 eV, [Ni(mnt)2]2– 57.5 eV, [Ni(mnt)2]− 57.4 eV). Finally, RIXS calculations were performed following the protocol published earlier.48 In order to minimize the effects of elastic scattering, the angle between the polarization of the incoming photon and the propagation direction of the outgoing photon, θ, was set to zero. Population analysis (Table S15) was performed using Multiwfn program using the Hirshfeld population analysis method.71
