Dispensing of Antiemetics During Pregnancy in Aotearoa New Zealand, 2005–2020
Authors: Sarah Donald, Katrina Sharples, Dave Barson, Karyn Maclennan, Leanne Te Karu, Diana Phone, Hedwig van Asten, Michael Stitely, Lianne Parkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAntiemetic use during pregnancy is rising in high‐income countries. Increasing use of antiemetics has also been documented in Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ); however, the specific antiemetics dispensed have not been reported.AimTo describe patterns of antiemetic dispensing during pregnancy in NZ, 2005–2020.Materials and MethodsCommunity‐based antiemetic dispensings were linked with 1.37 million pregnancies in the New Zealand Pregnancy Cohort. The proportions of pregnancies in which there was at least one dispensing of any and of specific antiemetics were calculated for each trimester and calendar year. Maternal characteristics and the sequence in which unique antiemetic regimens were introduced were described for pregnancies with first trimester exposure.ResultsFirst trimester antiemetic exposure increased from 4.1% to 19.7% of pregnancies between 2005 and 2019. Although metoclopramide was the most commonly dispensed antiemetic during Trimester 1 in all years (increasing from 3.1% to 12.4%), there was a substantial increase in ondansetron dispensing (0.1% to 9.1%). Ondansetron users were more likely than metoclopramide users to be European/Other and have a hospital admission for hyperemesis gravidarum. Metoclopramide and ondansetron monotherapy were first‐line regimens in 70.0% and 1.9% of pregnancies with Trimester 1 exposure between 2005 and 2010 versus 46.5% and 26.5% between 2016 and 2020.ConclusionsOndansetron dispensing increased substantially over time. Further exploration of the context of ondansetron prescribing in NZ is warranted, as is further research into potential adverse outcomes.

Introduction

Nausea and vomiting in pregnancy (NVP) affects approximately 70% of pregnancies [1]. Management guidelines generally recommend dietary modifications and non‐pharmacological interventions if symptoms are mild, but if symptoms persist or are more severe, pharmacological therapies are appropriate [2, 3].

Choice of antiemetic will be guided by local guidelines, but also by perceived benefit, cost, and patient preference. For example, ondansetron and metoclopramide may both be effective at reducing moderate symptoms [4, 5], but metoclopramide users may be more likely to experience drowsiness, dry mouth, or mood disorders, whereas ondansetron may lead to constipation [4, 6]. Side effects may prompt a pregnant individual to cease antiemetic treatment [6], therefore, treatment choice is important. Further consideration should also be given to any potential impact on the developing fetus.

Prescribed antiemetic use during pregnancy has increased substantially over recent decades in several high‐income countries, including Norway [7], Canada [8, 9], and the United States (US) [9, 10], with a more modest increase in the United Kingdom (UK) [9]. The most commonly used antiemetics are doxylamine‐pyridoxine in Canada [8, 9]; metoclopramide in Norway [7], France [11], and Australia [12]; and prochlorperazine in the UK [9, 13]. In the US, ondansetron has become the leading antiemetic used in pregnancy; by 2014, between 10.1% [9] and 22.2% [14] of privately insured pregnant women were dispensed ondansetron. First trimester exposure also increased among publicly insured women, from 0.01% in 2000 to 12% in 2013 [15].

In Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ), a national study found that the proportion of pregnancies in which an antinausea or vertigo agent was dispensed rose from 6.7% in the period 2005–2008 to 15.6% during 2012–2015 [16]. However, there have been no detailed investigations of antiemetic use during pregnancy.

Aim

The aim of the present study was to describe antiemetic dispensing patterns during pregnancy in NZ between 2005 and 2020. Specifically, the study sought to describe (i) the dispensing of prescription antiemetics during each trimester by calendar year, (ii) first trimester antiemetic dispensing by maternal characteristics and region, and (iii) the sequence in which antiemetic regimens were dispensed during the first trimester, as well as their proportionate use.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort

This study was undertaken using the New Zealand Pregnancy Cohort (NZPC), a national cohort constructed from pregnancy‐related data in four national health collections: the National Maternity Collection (live and stillbirths), the National Minimum Dataset (pregnancy‐related hospitalisations), the Mortality Collection (stillbirths and maternal deaths), and the Laboratory Claims Collection (antenatal blood tests) [17]. Initially containing pregnancies from 2005 to 2015, the NZPC has been updated to include pregnancies to the end of 2020. The cohort includes most pregnancies ending in delivery, and many other pregnancies that did not progress to a delivery [17].

Pregnancies with a last menstrual period (LMP) between 1 January 2005 and 12 March 2020 were eligible (to ensure all pregnancies had concluded by 31 December 2020). Pregnancies to overseas visitors were excluded as information on healthcare utilisation during pregnancy may have been incomplete.

Dispensing Data

All government‐funded medicines listed as “antinausea and vertigo agents” in the community pharmaceutical schedule (excluding antiemetics solely indicated for use during chemotherapy) and antinausea drugs recommended in NVP guidelines were identified (Data S1). Dispensings of these medicines from community pharmacies (recorded in the Pharmaceutical Collection [PHARMS]) were linked to individuals in the study cohort using an encrypted unique identifier (National Health Index). Exposure was defined as having at least one dispensing during the period of interest. As this study focused on treatment of NVP, dispensings of some antiemetics during pregnancy were excluded if there was a history of a relevant pre‐existing condition (Meniere's disease, migraine, or certain mental health conditions) and the medicine had also been used in the year prior to conception (Data S2).

Trimester Definitions

We defined the start of the pregnancy and Trimester 1 as the date of conception (estimated as 14 days after the LMP). Start dates for Trimester 2 and Trimester 3 were 77 days post‐conception (13 weeks gestation) and 182 days post‐conception (28 weeks gestation), respectively.

Covariates

Maternal characteristics included age at LMP, prioritised ethnicity [18], deprivation quintile (using the New Zealand Index of Deprivation [NZDep] [19]), urban–rural geographic classification (using the Geographic Classification for Health [GCH] [20]), body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), smoking status in early pregnancy, parity, and health region of residence (District Health Board [DHB]).

Analyses

For each trimester, the number and proportion of pregnancies during which at least one antiemetic was dispensed were calculated by year of LMP, for any antiemetic and for the individual antiemetics. Products with the same active ingredient (e.g., cyclizine hydrochloride and cyclizine lactate) were combined for analyses. Changes over time were evaluated by estimating prevalence ratios (and 95% confidence intervals) using a log‐linear regression model with Poisson errors, an independence working correlation and robust standard errors within a generalised estimating equation framework [21, 22] to allow for clustering by individual mother (referred to below as relative risk regression).

Multiple imputation with chained equations was undertaken to estimate values for missing maternal characteristics (See details in Data S3). Pregnancies without a recorded ethnicity were excluded from the multiple imputation and subsequent analyses by maternal characteristics, as imputation of ethnicity would be inconsistent with the self‐identified approach to recording ethnicity in NZ [18].

Using relative risk regression, the numbers and proportions of pregnancies with at least one dispensing of any antiemetic in Trimester 1 were described by maternal characteristics. To explore whether the antiemetic dispensed differed according to maternal characteristics, pregnancies exposed in Trimester 1 were first categorised into three mutually exclusive groups – (1) ondansetron‐exposed (irrespective of any other antiemetics dispensed), (2) metoclopramide‐exposed (without ondansetron, and irrespective of other antiemetics), and (3) exposed to other antiemetic(s) only. Multinomial regression with adjustment for clustering by mother was then undertaken to compare the odds of dispensing in the ondansetron‐exposed and other antiemetic‐exposed groups to the metoclopramide‐exposed group by maternal characteristics.

Finally, the Trimester 1 antiemetic dispensings for each pregnancy were examined according to therapeutic regimens—for example, if metoclopramide alone was dispensed on a given date, this regimen was classified as “metoclopramide monotherapy”, while if metoclopramide and ondansetron were both dispensed on the same day, this was classified as “metoclopramide + ondansetron”. Sunburst plots were then drawn to illustrate the proportion of pregnancies in which each regimen was dispensed as first‐line therapy, and the sequence in which any other regimen was subsequently introduced in Trimester 1, during three periods: 2005–2010, 2011–2015, and 2016–2020.

Analyses were undertaken using Stata MP v 17.0. The Northern B Health and Disability Ethics Committee approved the study (21/NTB/235; 2021 AM 10896).

Results

Study Cohort

The study cohort included 1,371,537 pregnancies of 646,056 individuals. Characteristics of these pregnancies are shown in Data S4.

Antiemetic Dispensing by Trimester and Year of LMP

Over the study period, at least one antiemetic was dispensed during 200,249 (14.6%) pregnancies, and in Trimester 1 during 161,655 (11.8%) pregnancies. Dispensing of an antiemetic during Trimester 1 increased from 4.1% of pregnancies in 2005 to 19.7% in 2019 (the last full year of data) (relative risk [RR] 4.83 [95% CI 4.66–4.99]) (Figure 1; Data S5). The proportions of pregnancies with at least one dispensing in Trimester 2 and Trimester 3 were lower, but similarly increased 5‐fold between 2005 and 2019. By 2019, an antiemetic dispensing occurred during Trimester 2 and Trimester 3 in 10.0% and 4.0% of pregnancies, respectively.

Proportion of pregnancies with at least one dispensing of (A) any antiemetic by trimester, and (B) the most commonly dispensed antiemetics during Trimester 1. (Note that individual antiemetics with very low use are not depicted in Figure 1B, and that data for 2020 only included pregnancies with an LMP up to 12 March).

In all years, metoclopramide was the most commonly dispensed antiemetic in Trimester 1, with exposure rising from 3.1% of pregnancies in 2005 to 12.4% in 2019 (RR 4.05 [3.89–4.22]) (Data S6). The proportion of pregnancies with a Trimester 1 ondansetron dispensing increased from 0.1% to 9.1% by 2019 (RR 89.83 [73.24–110.18]), becoming the second most dispensed antiemetic in Trimester 1 from 2011 onwards. In the earlier study years, metoclopramide was the most dispensed antiemetic in Trimesters 2 and 3; however, ondansetron surpassed metoclopramide in more recent years.

Antiemetic Dispensing in Trimester 1 by Maternal Characteristics

A very small number of pregnancies (n = 1,279, 0.1%) were excluded from the multiple imputation and subsequent analyses by maternal characteristics as they were missing information on ethnicity. Of these excluded pregnancies, 62 had been dispensed an antiemetic during Trimester 1.

Any Antiemetic vs. no Antiemetic

Relative to the 20–29 years group, a dispensing of any antiemetic during Trimester 1 was less likely during pregnancies of older individuals (30–39 years: adjusted RR [aRR] 0.78 [0.77–0.78]; 40–49 years: aRR 0.57 [0.56–0.59]) (Table 1). Antiemetic dispensing was also less likely during pregnancies of Māori women (aRR 0.88 [0.86–0.89]), and more likely during pregnancies of Middle Eastern/Latin American/African (MELAA) women (aRR 1.11 [1.08–1.13]), relative to those of European/Other women. Dispensing occurred less frequently with increasing rurality, and was less common during pregnancies of smokers versus non‐smokers (aRR 0.77 [0.76–0.78]), and more common during pregnancies of non‐nulliparous versus nulliparous women (aRR 1.22 [1.21–1.23]). The strongest association was with a Trimester 1 hospital admission for hyperemesis gravidarum (aRR 7.10 [7.03–7.18]). There was no clear association between increasing deprivation quintile or BMI category and antiemetic dispensing. Dispensing patterns by health board region are presented in Data S7.

Dispensing of Specific Antiemetics

Among Trimester 1‐exposed pregnancies, 31.3% were in the ondansetron group (dispensed ondansetron ± other antiemetics), 56.0% in the metoclopramide group (dispensed metoclopramide ± other non‐ondansetron antiemetics), and 12.7% in the other antiemetics group (dispensed non‐metoclopramide, non‐ondansetron antiemetics). Those in the ondansetron group were much more likely than those in the metoclopramide group to have been admitted for hyperemesis gravidarum (adjusted odds ratio 9.20 [8.70–9.72]) and less likely to be of Māori, Pacific, or Asian ethnicity (Table 2). Those in the other antiemetics group were more likely than those in the metoclopramide group to be aged 30 + years (relative to those aged 20–29 years) and live in more rural areas, and less likely to have been admitted for hyperemesis gravidarum. Because dispensing patterns changed over time (Data S8), an analysis restricted to 2016–2020 was undertaken; the findings were similar to those for the whole study period (Data S9).

Antiemetic Regimens and Dispensing Sequence in Trimester 1

In the earliest study years (2005–2010), metoclopramide monotherapy was the initial antiemetic regimen in 70.0% of Trimester 1‐exposed pregnancies, while 22.0% were first dispensed an antiemetic or combination of antiemetics other than metoclopramide or ondansetron (Figure 2; Data S10). Ondansetron monotherapy was the first‐line dispensing in 1.9% of pregnancies, with another 0.9% dispensed ondansetron in combination with another antiemetic(s). Ondansetron (alone or in combination) was dispensed as second‐line therapy in an additional 2.1%.

Sequence of antiemetic regimens dispensed during Trimester 1 over three time periods (2005–2010, 2011–2015, 2016–2020). The inner ring represents the first antiemetic regimen dispensed and the outer ring represents a subsequent dispensing of a different regimen.

In the later study years (2016–2020), initial metoclopramide monotherapy occurred in 46.5%, ondansetron monotherapy was seen in 26.5%, and a further 8.7% were dispensed ondansetron as combination first‐line therapy.

As only 5.3% of exposed pregnancies progressed to a third treatment regimen in Trimester 1, these regimens were not formally examined.

Discussion

Dispensing of an antiemetic during pregnancy in NZ increased 5‐fold between 2005 and 2020. In all years, metoclopramide was the most frequently dispensed antiemetic during Trimester 1, but the proportion of pregnancies exposed to ondansetron increased substantially—from 0.1% to almost 10%. In the most recent study years, ondansetron was included in the first‐line dispensing for one in three (35.2%) pregnancies exposed in Trimester 1.

The major strength of this study is its use of a national pregnancy cohort to both establish a baseline and to document changes in antiemetic prescribing practice over time in NZ. It is possible, however, that antiemetic exposure could have been higher than reported for two reasons. First, the PHARMS database does not record medicines purchased over‐the‐counter (OTC), and pyridoxine (vitamin B6), prochlorperazine, promethazine, and cyclizine were available from pharmacies without a prescription (as well as on prescription) during the study period. The treatment regimens may be inaccurate if OTC antiemetics, including doxylamine (which is not funded in New Zealand), were purchased before an antiemetic was dispensed. However, in New Zealand, many prescription medicines are heavily subsidised by the government, including the antiemetics included in this study. Most patients only pay a $5 co‐payment at the time of dispensing; therefore, prescription antiemetics are likely cheaper than those purchased OTC, which may limit OTC use. Although there is a cost associated with attending a general practice, maternity care is fully funded, and there should be little financial barrier to accessing a prescription from a midwife.

The second reason for potentially underestimating antiemetic use is that PHARMS does not include medicines administered in inpatient and emergency department/clinic settings. Although the numbers presenting with NVP/hyperemesis gravidarum who were not dispensed an antiemetic in the community before or after their visit are likely to be very small, it is possible that they may have been initially treated with something other than what they were later prescribed, or were provided with tablets directly from their health practitioner (practitioner supply order [PSO]).

Conversely, it is also possible that antiemetic exposure for some women was over‐estimated in this study, as they may not have taken their dispensed course of antiemetic treatment.

This study has provided a detailed description of antiemetic dispensing patterns over a recent 15‐year period; however, the reasons behind the substantial increases in use have not been explored. Increases are unlikely to be related to increased symptom severity, as hospital admissions for hyperemesis gravidarum in the cohort did not increase over the same period; between 1.4% and 1.7% of pregnancies were associated with a hyperemesis gravidarum admission each year between 2005 and 2020. Some studies suggest that NVP has been undertreated, due to health professionals' reluctance to prescribe medications to pregnant patients [23], or because of patients' own concerns about safety [24].

Previous studies have found that NVP is less common during pregnancies of older individuals [25, 26, 27], nulliparous individuals [26, 27], and smokers [25, 27], with little association with income [25] or pre‐pregnancy weight [25]. Use of antiemetics in our cohort was consistent with these findings. Although Pacific women in NZ have twice the rate of hospitalisation for hyperemesis gravidarum than the overall population [28], this study found similar antiemetic exposure during pregnancies of Pacific and European/Other women after adjustment for other maternal characteristics. Whether this reflects under‐prescribing for the former group or over‐prescribing for the latter is uncertain. Differences in NVP and hyperemesis gravidarum have not been documented between Māori and women of European/Other ethnicity; reasons for lower antiemetic use during pregnancies of Māori women cannot be determined from this study but could include systemic barriers in healthcare, patient choice, or preference for Rongoā Māori (traditional healing). Lower use of antiemetics with increasing rurality could indicate healthcare access barriers for those individuals or higher PSO supply.

Despite not being indicated for use in NVP, the use of ondansetron increased substantially, similar to changes documented in the US [9, 10, 14, 15]. Over the study period, several local NVP management guidelines were available. The 2011 Best Practice Advocacy Centre New Zealand guidelines recommended metoclopramide as the preferred first‐line pharmacotherapy, advising that ondansetron be considered only in those with hyperemesis gravidarum due to limited safety data [2]. The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners' guidelines published in 2016 suggested that after trying pyridoxine‐doxylamine, those with moderate–severe NVP or hyperemesis gravidarum be treated with diphenhydramine, moving to ondansetron only if symptoms persisted or dehydration was present, or after metoclopramide or prochlorperazine were trialled with no effect [29]. Recommendations from the Society of Obstetric Medicine of Australia and New Zealand (SOMANZ) from 2019 were slightly different, recommending ondansetron as one option for first‐line pharmacotherapy in those with moderate NVP (Pregnancy Unique Quantification of Emesis [PUQE‐24] score 7–12), and the best option for those with severe NVP or hyperemesis gravidarum (PUQE‐24 score ≥ 13) [3]. However, these guidelines were only released in the last year of the study and would not have guided prescribing practice for most of that period. Widened access to funded ondansetron in mid‐2011 [30] and its availability as an orodispersible tablet likely contributed to its increasing use. Guidelines recommend that metoclopramide not be used for more than 5 days due to the risk of dystonic reactions [31], which may be another reason for the increasing preference for ondansetron.

Due to the increasing exposure to ondansetron in pregnancy in NZ, any potential safety concerns should be considered. Ondansetron crosses the placenta [32], and several studies have suggested that first trimester use may be associated with a small increased risk of specific birth defects (e.g., orofacial clefts [15, 33], cardiac defects [34, 35]. Increased risk of malformations has not been found in all studies [36, 37], and a recent meta‐analysis found that ondansetron was not significantly associated with orofacial defects (OR 1.22 [95% CI 1.00–1.49], I2 = 0%) or cardiac malformations (OR 1.17 0.97–1.40), I2 = 66% [38]). It is important to note that although ondansetron does not appear to be a major teratogen, the 95% confidence intervals have not ruled out small increases in risk. For a medicine with rising use in the first trimester of pregnancy locally, further evidence would be helpful to clarify this potential issue.

Conclusion

Antiemetic dispensing during pregnancy rose substantially over the study period, largely due to increased dispensing of metoclopramide and ondansetron. Use of ondansetron as a first‐line agent also increased over time. Although research into the pregnancy‐related safety of ondansetron has received more focus in recent years, questions remain about its fetal impact. Because of this uncertainty, it is important to explore the contexts in which dispensing of ondansetron is occurring during pregnancy in NZ, as well as undertake further research into the safety profile of ondansetron.
