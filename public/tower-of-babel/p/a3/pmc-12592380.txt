Exploring Cadaba fruticosa bioactive compounds for oral cancer an in silico and in vitro approach
Authors: Silambarasan Tamil Selvan, A. Venkateshkumar, Manikandan Alagumuthu, Elumalai Perumal, Devi Muthusamy, Mohmed Isaqali Karobari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates the efficacy of phytotherapy in treating oral cancer. This study evaluated the phytocompounds extracted from Cadaba fruticosa for anticancer potential using GC–MS analysis. A total of 10 bioactive phytocompounds were identified, of which 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester emerged as a highly potent candidate. A pharmacokinetic analysis was performed using SwissADME, and the selected compound did not adhere to Lipinski’s rule of five, indicating no toxicity. A network pharmacology and molecular docking study was conducted to investigate the interaction between these compounds and the target proteins of oral cancer. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database provided 10 hub genes associated with critical pathways: EGFR, MAPK3, PPARG, MTOR, KRAS, MDM2, ERBB2, IRS1, HRAS, and JAK2. Molecular docking studies confirmed strong binding interactions between 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester and the oral cancer protein SMAD, which had binding energy of -7.9 kcal/mol and interactions with the amino acids TYR280 (2.89 Å), ARG285 (3.04 Å), THR413 (3.21 Å), ARG415 (2.98 Å) and SER276 (2.09 Å), TYR280 (3.74 Å), THR289 (4.09 Å), ARG285 (4.31 Å), ARG415 (3.63 Å), ARG410 (3.54 Å), HIS441 (5.07 Å), PRO360 (4.45 Å), ARG410 (4.46 Å), ARG410 (4.93 Å) and ALA278 (4.34 Å). A molecular dynamics simulation was performed over 200 ns and showed the complex stable, with RMSD values ranging from 0.2 to 0.8 nm. Apoptotic gene expression analysis of KB-1 oral cancer cell lines showed downregulation of Bcl-2 and upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes BAD, BAX, and caspase-3 at an IC50 of 38.60 µg/ml. In a density functional theory (DFT) analysis, 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester was found to have HOMO and LUMO energy levels ranging from − 0.8100 to 0.2701, highlighting its electronic properties. The RDG scattered plot topological analysis indicated significant Van der Waals interactions and steric repulsions. Accordingly, the present study concludes that 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester is a promising new drug for treating oral cancer.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, with the burden expected to triple by 2030 despite advances in treatment. The prevalence of oral cancer (OC), a subtype of head and neck cancers, has increased worldwide, particularly in India, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh, where up to 25% of new cases are related to OC1–3. Approximately 95% of oral cancer cases are squamous cell carcinomas, which result from epithelial dysplasia. The 5-year survival rate for this disease is approximately 50% despite advances in surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. The consumption of tobacco smoke and alcohol, both key risk factors, synergistically contribute to carcinogenesis4,5. Tobacco smoke contains over 4,000 carcinogens, including methoxymethylfurfural, nicotine, methanol, and arsenic. Alcohol increases these toxins’ penetration into cells through cell membranes6,7. OC is on the rise in Asia, and it accounts for 50%–70% of cancer-related deaths in India alone, mostly among men. Among the various anatomical sites of the oral cavity, the lips, tongue, gums, and palate frequently exhibit cancerous growths, emphasizing the urgency for prevention and early detection methods8–10.

The phytochemopreventive properties of phytochemicals are due to their modulation of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, which inhibit the initiation and progression of cancer11. Among their many benefits are their effect on inflammation, cancer, and migration12. They are found in dietary sources such as fruits, vegetables, and spices13–15. They are potent antioxidants, anti-migratory, anti-proliferative, and anti-invasive15. The actions of phytochemicals in induced apoptosis and modulated cancer-related signalling pathways have shown promise as potential treatments for cancer patients resistant to cisplatin and those with oral cancer16–18. A plant-derived compound is cost-effective compared to conventional cancer treatment and reduces the financial burden on developing nations. Moreover, they protect normal cells throughout treatment, minimizing the side effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy19–21.

The phytochemical profile of Cadaba fruticosa reveals alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds that contribute to its therapeutic potential22. In addition to anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer properties, C. fruticosa is a promising phytochemical and pharmacological candidate. A docking study of C. fruticose bioactive compounds revealed their affinity for key cancer-related proteins, including SMAD, which is implicated in cancer progression. These interactions’ stability and electronic properties have been further elucidated by computational methods such as molecular dynamics simulations and density functional theory (DFT) analyses, providing valuable insights into their therapeutic potential23,24.

This current study investigated the computational and experimental approaches to validate C. fruticosa anticancer efficacy. The plant inhibited cancer cell proliferation and induced apoptosis in various in vitro tests, including MTT cell viability assays, morphological analyses, and real-time PCR. In addition to Molecular Docking and dynamics simulations, DFT analysis confirmed stable interaction between bioactive compounds and cancer targets, supporting the plant potential as a targeted cancer treatment. The findings of this study demonstrate that C. fruticosa may provide a natural source of bioactive compounds that could be used to develop therapeutics that are more effective against oral cancer and provide an alternative to existing therapies. This study assessed the oral cancer activity, physical and chemical properties of 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester was conducted in the first study involving a plant compound from the C. fruticosa, 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester. The 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester is a novel drug molecule that treats oral cancer and can be used in conjunction with existing drugs.

Materials and methods

Collection of plant samples

The Cadaba fruticosa plant sample was collected from the Kolli Hills, Namakkal, Tamil Nadu, India. The plant taxonomical characteristics was identified from Department of Botany, Periyar University and the plant was submitted (voucher no. 23732). The experiment carried out complied with relevant guidelines and legislation. The collected plant root sample was air dried at room temperature for 10 days, and the dried root sample was ground into a powder form using a mixer grinder. The powder was collected and stored at 4 °C for further studies.

Extraction of bioactive metabolites

The bioactive metabolites were extracted from Cadaba fruticosa plant root powder using the soxhlet extraction method, and the 100 g powder sample was transferred to a small filter paper bag. The sample bag was transferred to the soxhlet extraction column, and the column was fixed on the round bottom flask. 350 ml of ethyl acetate was added to the soxhlet apparatus, and the heating mantel was fixed at 80 °C for 6 h. After extraction, the sample was dried under a rotatory evaporator at 80 °C for 4 h. The crude extract was collected and stored at 4 °C for further studies25.

Analysis of phytochemical

The extracted bioactive metabolites profile was analyzed using GCMS and identified the bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, quinones, terpenoids, steroids, anthraquinones, glycosides, tannins and phenols. 1 µL of extract sample was injected in the capillary column into the GCMS mobile phase. The helium gas was injected into the capillary column at a flow rate of 70 µL per minute, maintaining a pressure of 7.0 Psi at a temperature of 30 ˚C. The analysis was performed using a GCMS-QP2020 NX instrument with GCMSsolution™ software by Shimadzu, Japan. During the analysis, compound peaks were detected within a scan range of 50–20000 mz⁻¹ at an electron energy of 150 eV. These peaks ' retention times (Rt) were then compared with the NIST Wiley library. The identification of compounds was carried out through a comprehensive mass spectrum analysis26,27.

Analysis of Pharmacokinetic properties

Drug-likeness properties were analyzed before a molecule could transform into a drug. The drug-like properties were absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion using the ADME profile. The ADME profile performed the physicochemical properties viz., water-solubility, lipophilicity, gastrointestinal absorption, and blood-brain barrier permeability. The compounds adhering to Lipinski’s rule of five criteria, allowing for a maximum of one violation, were deemed optimal candidates for progression in drug development. This comprehensive analysis used the SwissADME web server23.

Toxicological properties prediction

Assessing the safety profiles of the selected compounds was a critical precursor to their progression into clinical trials. The toxicity profile prediction identifies fewer toxicity compounds and their efficiency, accuracy, and accessibility. The identified compounds’ toxicity profile and potential adverse effects were determined using the ADMETSAR online tool23,24.

Analysis of network pharmacology

Identify the target genes (oral cancer)

The Venn diagram was created using the VENNY2.1 website to visually display the common targets shared by oral cancer-related targets and the projected targets of 3,5 dichlorobenzyl propyl ester compound (3,5 DPE) and Pimelic Acid28.

Target prediction oral cancer

The Swiss target prediction database was used to predict the potential targets of 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester and Pimelic Acid28. The canonical SMILES were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and submitted to the Swiss target prediction website. The species homo sapiens were selected to establish a uniform identification system for gene symbols. The STITCH platform was used for the configuration of specific homo sapiens target genes. The accurate target genes and prediction of target compound genes were used. A minimum required interaction score of combined score ≥ 0.4 was used to choose the target genes during the STITCH prediction process. Simultaneously predicted the targets of oral cancer information were analyzed using the online database GeneCards (GeneCards: https://www.genecards.org/). Oral cancer and squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) were utilized to collect genes, amalgamate all the parameters, eliminate redundant information, and obtain a comprehensive inventory of pertinent targets28–30.

Intersecting targets for oral cancer

The Venn diagram was created using the VENNY2.1 website to visually display the common targets shared by oral cancer-related targets and the projected targets of 3,5 dichlorobenzyl propyl ester compound30.

PPI network analysis

The target genes were selected using a minimum required interaction score of combined score ≥ 0.4 for the derived compound-gene interactions from the STRING prediction results and STRING prediction process. The resulting interaction network was constructed, visualized and analyzed using the Cytoscape version. 3.10.2. The nodes in the network represent drugs and genes, whereas the edges indicate the interactions between substances and genes. The primary active components and key genes of one drug molecule with potential efficacy against oral cancer were selected by evaluating the degree value of genes determined by analyzing the topological structure of the network. The degree value of genes showed the number of edges that connect them in a network. The CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape was discovered to hub genes and is considered a significant target. The higher the degree value of compounds and genes, the more significant they are for the beneficial effects of compounds on oral cancer30–32.

Pathways and interactive enrichment analysis

The gene ontology enrichment analysis was performed by the ShinyGO v0.80 database and selected the homo sapiens mode to conduct a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the intersecting 89 genes identified from the venny diagram. The identified core target genes are screening criteria with FDR in KEGG and GO analysis. The study was conducted using the web-based search tool. The KEGG enrichment pathway analysis results were utilized to deduce the molecular mechanisms of the drug in oral cancer. The Bubble chart depicting paths was generated using the ShinyGO v0.80 software. GO analysis is employed to examine biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) to identify the probable molecular mechanism pathways of the drug for oral cancer using the enrichment analysis database31–33.

Analysis of computational studies

Preparation of compound structure

The structures of identified natural phytochemical compounds were synthesized, and the Structure Data Files (SDF) were obtained from the PubChem database. Subsequently, the molecular structures of all phytochemicals were prepared and optimized for docking procedures using UCSF Chimera 1.15 software. The preparation of ligands involved a stepwise approach, incorporating commands such as adding hydrogens and calculating charge to zero from the structure editing command section. The Gasteiger logarithm was selected as the charge calculation method. Finally, the prepared ligand structures were saved in SDF format for further analyses and docking studies23,24.

Analysis and validation of ligand

The phytochemicals identified in Cadaba fruticosa underwent bioactivity assessment for drug-likeness properties using the Molinspiration Cheminformatics Software24. The molinspiration predicts molecular properties and bioactivity by calculating activity scores for drug targets, including the TGF Beta protein. The bioactivity scores of molecules indicate their potential activity levels, categorizing them as active, moderately active, or inactive.

Analysis of in Silico and visualization

The molecular docking studies between oral cancer proteins and ligands were performed using AutoDock and AutoDock Vina software (version 1.5.6). The chosen bioactive compounds were docked against two oral cancer protein SMAD (1KHX). The grid box analysis was employed to define target binding site parameters, with coordinates set at X: 113.450000, Y: 39.014000, Z: -7.767000 for SMAD protein (1KHX). The centre-oriented grid box facilitated the exploration of flexible ligand binding to oral cancer proteins. Docking results were expressed as binding affinities (kcal/mol), denoted by negative values. The top ten docking models, each accompanied by a binding affinity score, were provided for scrutiny. The binding affinity data were saved in CSV format, and the docked models were stored in PDBQT format. The output docked models were utilized in the BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer version 2020 software to visualize and analyze the ligand-protein complex interactions. The receptor-ligand interaction segment of the software was employed to predict 2D and 3D structures of the protein-ligand complexes, highlighting interacting bonds, bond categories, and bonding distances23.

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations

Molecular Dynamic (MD) simulations of the docked complex of 1KHX-3,5DPE were performed for 200 ns to ascertain the stability of the interaction between the two target molecules. MD simulation is a computer simulation technique that looks at how atoms and molecules, actually move34–36. MD simulation of the selected 1KHX-3,5DPE complex was carried out using the GROMOS96 (53a6) force field for protein dynamics34. All simulations were conducted on a high-performance computing cluster with CUDA-accelerated GPUs for enhanced performance35–40. The system was solvated in a triclinic box with a 10 Å padding from the solute and filled with explicit water molecules. Counterions (Na, Cl) were added to neutralize the system and maintain a 150 mM NaCl ionic strength. Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force was below 1000 kJ/mol/nm to remove steric clashes and optimize the system geometry. The system was equilibrated for 100 ps under a constant Number of particles (N), Volume (V), and Temperature (T) using the V-rescale thermostat at 300 K. The system was equilibrated for 1 ns under a constant Number of particles (N), Pressure (P), and Temperature (T) using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat at 1 bar pressure. A 200 ns production run was conducted using a time step of 2 fs. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) were applied in all directions. The LINCS algorithm was used to constrain hydrogen bonds, and the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was employed for long-range electrostatics. A cutoff of 1.0 nm was used for van der Waals and short-range electrostatic interactions. Snapshots were saved every 10 ps for analysis. The trajectory was analyzed using GROMACS built-in tools and VMD/PyMOL for visualization. Properties such as Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) and Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) for structural stability and Secondary Structure Analysis using super-imposing tactics. The native protein structure was overlapped to unveil which region of the docked protein has an impact/conformational change due to the ligand binding40–42.

Analysis of in-vitro oral cancer studies

Cell line maintenance

The oral cancer cell lines (KB-1) were obtained from the NCCS in Pune. The cells were grown in T25 culture flasks containing DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO243.

Cell viability (MTT) assay

The cell viability of 3, 5 DPE treated with oral cancer (KB-1) cells was assessed by MTT assay. The assay is based on metabolically active cells reducing soluble yellow tetrazolium salt to insoluble purple formazan crystals. KB-1 cells were plated in 96 well plates at a concentration of 5 × 103 cells/well 24 h after plating, and cells were washed twice with 100 µl of serum-free medium and starved by incubating the cells in serum-free medium for 3 hours at 37 °C. After starvation, cells were treated with different concentrations of 3, 5 DPE for 24 and 48 h. At the end of treatment, the medium from control and 3, 5 DPE -treated cells were discarded, and 100 µl of MTT containing DMEM (0.5 mg/ml) was added to each well. The cells were then incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in the CO2 incubator. The MTT-containing medium was discarded, and the cells were washed with 1x PBS. Then, the formazan crystals formed were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (100 µl) and incubated in the dark for an hour. Then, the intensity of the colour developed was assayed using a Micro ELISA plate reader at 570 nm. The number of viable cells was expressed as a percentage of control cells cultured in a serum-free medium43,44. Cell viability in the control medium without any treatment was 100%. Morphology study

Based on the MTT assay, we selected the optimal doses of 3.5 DPE (IC-50: 38.60 µg/ml) for further studies. Analysis of cell morphology changes by a phase contrast microscope. 2 × 105 cells were seeded in 6 well plates and treated with 3.5 DPE for KB-1 cells for 24 h. At the end of the incubation period, the medium was removed, and cells were washed once with a phosphate buffer saline (PBS pH 7.4). The plates were observed under a phase contrast microscope45.

Determination of mode of cell death by acridine orange (AO)/ethidium bromide (EtBr) dual staining

As described previously, the effects of 3.5 DPE in KB-1 (1 mg/ml) cell death were also determined by AO/EtBr dual staining. The cells were treated with 3.5 DPE for 24 h and then harvested and washed with ice-cold PBS. The pellets were resuspended in 5 µl of acridine orange (1 mg/mL) and 5 µl of EtBr (1 mg/mL). The stained cells apoptotic changes were then observed using a fluorescence microscope43–45.

Real-time PCR

The gene expression of apoptosis molecules was analyzed using real-time PCR. The total RNA was isolated using the standardized protocol of Trizol Reagent (Sigma). 2 µg of RNA is used for cDNA synthesis using reverse transcription using a PrimeScript, 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (TakaRa, Japan). The targeted genes were amplified using specific primers (Table 1). The PCR reaction was performed with GoTaq- qPCR Master Mix (Promega), which contains SYBR green dye and all the PCR components. Real-time time PCR was performed in a CFX96 PCR system (Biorad). The results were analyzed using the comparative CT method, and the 2-CT method was used for fold change calculation, as described by Schmittgen and Livak46.

Physical and chemical properties of 3,5 DPE compound

Analysis of density functional theory (DFT)

The crystal structure of the 3,5 DPE complex was optimized with B3LYP level utilizing DFT/6-31G(d, p) basis set in the Gaussian 09 W program package, and the findings are envisaged with GaussView 647–52. The TD-DFT approach was used to simulate the electronic and FMO characteristics of DCBP53–60. In addition, the MEP and Mulliken distribution of charges was performed using the same basis set.

Analysis of compound topology

The topological insights comprising ELF, NCI, LOL, and RDG were simulated using Multiwfn software and Atomistica online molecular modelling platform. The results were emphasized using the VMD program61–64.

Statistical analysis

All data obtained were analyzed by One way ANOVA flowed by Students-t-test using SPSS, represented as mean ± SD for triplicates. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.0545.

Result and discussion

Morphological identification

The identified plant morphology characteristics include a rigid, unarmed, wiry structure, with simple leaves forming a straggling shrub and either solitary or racemed flowers. The sepals, numbering four, are unequal and arranged in two whorls, with the outer two valvate and petals, either four or two, having claws and are hypogynous. The plant features a large, coloured disk that encircles the gynophore, with a tubular stalk expanding trumpetwise at the top or spathulate and stamens, numbering 4–6, are unilaterally inserted on the slender gynophore. The seeds are globose, with a horny testa and cotyledons arranged convolutely. The selected plant was identified as a Cadaba fruticosa plant. As reported by Guddeti65, Cadaba fruticosa has a rigid, unarmed structure, solitary flowers with two valves and four petals. In their study, Shashikanth et al.66 found that the flowers of the wiry shrubs are unarmed and straggling, with two whorls, outer 2 valvate, outer 4 Petalsor, hypogynous, hypogynous placentas, globose, testa horny, and convolute cotyledons identified the morphological characteristics from Cadaba fruticosa.

Extraction and analysis of metabolites

The Cadaba fruticosa plant root extract was subjected to GCMS analysis. In the extract, 10 bioactive metabolites were identified, viz., dimethyl sulfoxonium formylmethylide, 2-chloro-6-trichloromethylpyridine, n-hexadecanoic acid, pimelic acid, 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester, 1,3,7-trihydroxy-9 H-xanthen-9-one, 1,3-dihydroxy-4-methyl, 2,6-Dichlorophenylthioacid, 5,5,10,10-tetrachlorotricyclo decane and 4-oxatricyclo decane-3,5-dione, 8-chloro-9-(phenylthio) respectively (Fig. 1; Table 2). The Cadaba fruticosa plant secondary metabolites, which were extracted with methanol, were investigated by Sekar et al.26 and 15 bioactive compounds were identified, including cadabicine, quercetin, cadabicine triacetate, isoquercetin, thiazolidine, 1,2 benzene dicarboxylic acid mono-2 ethyl ester, 9,12 octadecatrienoic acid, benzene 1 methylterdecayl, n-hexadectrinoic acid, hexadecanoic acid ethyl ester, strchidine, vanillic acid, oleic acid, and phytol.

Analysis of pharmacokinetic properties

The Cadaba fruticosa plant-extracted bioactive compounds were evaluated for pharmacokinetic properties using ADMET and pKCSM tools (Table 3; Fig. 2a, b). The ADMET analysis employs a framework of established criteria that encompasses the evaluation of compounds’ bioavailability, solubility, gastrointestinal absorption (GIA), blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, molecular weight (MW), molar refractivity (MR), topological polar surface area (TPSA), hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), hydrogen bond donors (HBD), Cytochrome P450 (CYP) inhibition, and toxicity. The progression of a drug through the body was determined using ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination, and toxicity)67,68. Totally 10 bioactive metabolites were analyzed the pharmacokinetic properties viz., dimethyl sulfoxonium formylmethylide, 2-chloro-6-trichloromethylpyridine, n-hexadecanoic acid, pimelic acid, 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester, 1,3,7-trihydroxy-9 H-xanthen-9-one, 1,3-dihydroxy-4-methyl, 2,6-Dichlorophenylthioacid, 5,5,10,10-tetrachlorotricyclo decane and 4-oxatricyclo decane-3,5-dione, 8-chloro-9-(phenylthio). The pharmacokinetic properties of the identified compounds are described in Table 3. The selected compound 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester toxicity parameters were analyzed and observed absorption (water solubility − 4.437 log mol/L, CaCO2 permeability- 0.87 log Papp in 10− 6 cm/s, intestinal absorption (human)- 88.591%, skin permeability- -2.803 log Kp, P-glycoprotein substrate, P-glycoprotein I inhibitor and a non-P- glycoprotein II inhibitor), distribution (VDss (human)- 0.66 log L/kg, fraction unbound (human)-0 Fu, BBB permeability- -1.343 log BB, CNS permeability- -2.382 log PS), metabolism (non-CYP2D6substrate, CYP3A4 substrate, non CYP1A2 inhibitor, non CYP2C19 inhibitor, non CYP2C9 inhibitor, non CYP2D6 inhibitor and CYP3A4 inhibitor), excretion (total clearance- 0.504 log ml/min/kg and non Renal OCT2 substrate), toxicity (non-AMES toxicity, Max. Tolerated dose (human) - -0.72 mg/ kg/day, non-hERG I inhibitor, non-hERG II inhibitor, oral rat acute toxicity (LD50)- 2.415 mol/kg, oral rat chronic toxicity (LOAEL)- 0.7 log mg/kgbw/day, T.Pyriformis toxicity- 0.38 log µg/L) respectively. An egg-shaped organization plot reveals that the compound in albumin (the white region) represents high intestinal absorption, and its presence in albumin indicates good intestinal absorption (Fig. 2a-b). Based on the above-observed results, the 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester compound has sufficient pharmaceutical potential (Table 3)69.

Network mapping

The Swiss-target prediction online tool was used to identify the active targets, specifically 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester and Pimelic acid and collected the relevant genes associated with oral cancer from the GeneCards database. The Swiss-target prediction program identified 108 specific targets for 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester and Pimelic acid. Additionally, GeneCards offered information on 10,224 genes associated with oral cancer and squamous cell carcinoma. The targets of 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester and Pimelic acid and oral cancer were combined in a Venn diagram. The 89 genes overlapping in all two categories were identified as possible targets for 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester and Pimelic acid and anti-oral cancer treatment. Gene‑ontology function and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

The components of 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester targets were analyzed for functional annotation and enrichment analysis. This study identified significant biological roles linked to these components. The targets associated with the 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester components were involved in various biological processes (BP), particularly those related to nitrogen compounds, phosphorylation, and signal transduction. This was determined through gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. Targeted cellular components (CC) include the receptor complex, the plasma membrane region, the membrane microdomain, the plasma membrane protein complex, and other membranes. In addition, the targets’ molecular functions (MF) encompassed binding to phosphatase, identical protein, purine nucleotide, anion, and various other activities. By applying a false discovery rate (FDR) criterion of 0.05, we discovered a link between 18 gene ontology (GO) terms and the top 20 genes. Specifically, 18 terms were connected with molecular function (MF) (Fig. 4a), 18 terms were associated with cellular components (CC) (Fig. 4b), and 72 terms were associated with biological processes (BP) (Fig. 4c). Nicotine and other substances stimulate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), which activates CYP1A1, an enzyme that enhances the metabolism of substances that may lead to cancer3. The figure highlights the activation of many pathways, including Wnt, PI3K-Akt, MAPK, mTOR, Jak-STAT, Hedgehog, Notch, and TGF-β, and it depicts the relationship between receptors and ligands in cell biology. The SMAD proteins play a pivotal role in cancer progression and may serve as therapeutic targets, a view that is strongly supported by our network pharmacology analysis. Oral cancer is often associated with dysregulated SMAD proteins, which can contribute significantly to disease progression, metastasis development, and resistance to therapy73,74. Oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCC) frequently exhibit mutations, deletions, or aberrant expression of SMAD proteins, with SMAD4 mutations being found in 18% of samples. SMAD protein expression changes are associated with poor prognosis, according to meta-analyses that have shown low SMAD4 expression is associated with decreased overall survival74,75. A promising treatment approach may be to target SMAD-dependent signaling, with preclinical studies showing that inhibiting SMAD2/3 phosphorylation or restoring SMAD4 activity suppress oral cancer growth and metastasis. In present analysis identified multiple hub genes and SMAD protein was selected because they have well-established roles in oral cancer biology, are frequently exhibited in clinical samples, and have potential as therapeutic agents. Molecular docking determined that the binding energies of the bioactive compounds ranged from − 4.2 kcal/mol to -7.9 kcal/mol, as shown in Table 4. A 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester compound with a binding energy of -7.9 kcal/mol was selected for further investigation based on its binding affinity to the amino acid residues in the active site of the SMAD protein (Fig. 6). The BIOVIA Discovery Studio was used to visualize the interactions between the compounds (ligands) and SMAD protein. The 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester compound interacted more with SMAD and formed several key interactions. Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations

MD simulations were performed on the docked complex of 1KHX-3,5DPE to verify its stability concerning RMSD, RMSF and molecular interactions during a 200 ns duration (Fig. 7a-c). The stability of the protein-ligand combination was simulated to determine the backbone atoms’ RMSD and RMSF. The RMSD profiles (Fig. 7a) of the backbone atoms provide insights into the overall conformational stability of the systems. The apo form of the protein (1KHX, red trace) displayed RMSD fluctuations stabilizing around ~ 0.3–0.4 nm after ~ 20 ns, suggesting equilibrium dynamics. In contrast, the 1KHX + 3,5DPE complex (blue trace) showed a slightly higher deviation (~ 0.35–0.45 nm) but retained a stable trajectory throughout the simulation. This marginal increase in RMSD upon 3,5DPE binding suggests moderate conformational adaptation without significant destabilization. At the end, after the completion of the 200 ns simulation run, every protein-ligand complex achieved its stable conformations.

Fig. 7Analysis of MD simulations (a) RMSD, (b) RMSF and (c) overlapping approach between native protein (undocked) & docked complex.

Analysis of MD simulations (a) RMSD, (b) RMSF and (c) overlapping approach between native protein (undocked) & docked complex.

According to the computed RMSF, there are no considerable variations in the secondary structure of the protein at any location along the protein (Fig. 7b). The RMSF plot depicts residue-wise flexibility, highlighting local mobility. Both systems showed similar fluctuation patterns, with minor peaks corresponding to loop and terminal regions. Notably, a slight increase in fluctuation was observed in residues 5–15 and 100–120 in the complex compared to the apo form, possibly indicating local rearrangements induced by 3,5DPE binding. However, the majority of the residues showed RMSF values < 0.2 nm, reflecting a structurally rigid backbone. The amino acid residues in the active site were ultimately stable with a 0.4 nm variation. No abnormal spikes or large fluctuations were seen, and localized flexibility near the binding region was expected and informative. The overlapped structure of the native protein (not used for docking) and docked protein-ligand complex depicted here (Fig. 7c) reveals that no significant deviation happened due to the ligand’s binding to the target protein76,77.

Hydrogen bonds play a critical role in stabilizing biomolecular structures and mediating intermolecular interactions during molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Fig. 7d). The time evolution of the number of hydrogen bonds formed within the system was monitored over a 200 ns simulation period (Fig. 7d). The plot demonstrates that the number of hydrogen bonds fluctuates consistently within a stable range throughout the trajectory, indicating that the system maintains structural stability during the course of the simulation. On average, the number of hydrogen bonds remains between ~ 210 and ~ 235, with occasional spikes up to ~ 240 and dips toward ~ 205. Such fluctuations are typical in dynamic systems due to conformational flexibility, solvent rearrangement, and transient bond formation and breakage. Importantly, no significant drift or trend toward either an increase or decrease in hydrogen bonding was observed across the 200 ns simulation. This suggests that the structural integrity of the system was preserved, and the interactions between molecules were sustained throughout the simulation. The relatively high number of hydrogen bonds further highlights the role of strong intermolecular interactions in maintaining the stability of the simulated complex. Overall, the hydrogen bond profile supports the conclusion that the system reached equilibrium and remained dynamically stable under the simulation conditions. To ensure the stability, we performed three independent 100 ns replicates for the top ligand–protein complexes to ensure reproducibility. The stability of the trajectories was assessed by calculating RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, and hydrogen bond occupancy, followed by averaging across replicates. Statistical analysis of these parameters was carried out using ANOVA, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Analysis of anticancer activity

Cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects on oral cancer cells

The present investigation, 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester was evaluated for its in vivo cytotoxicity and pro-apoptotic activities against the KB-1 oral cancer cell line. The compound was treated with various concentrations of the cell line (3.75–120 µg/mL) for 24 h. In Fig. 8a, the amount of cell viability decreased dose-dependently, with the treated cells demonstrating significantly reduced sustainability compared to the control group. In the current study, the IC50 value was defined as the concentration required to inhibit 50% of cell growth (IC50- 38.60 µg/ml). For further analysis, this dosage was selected (Fig. 8a). Similarly, the curcumin and catechin polyphenols from curcuma longa and green tea have been reported to treat squamous cell carcinoma by Saleh et al.78. Chandra et al.79 investigated the cytotoxic activity of Centella asiatica plant extract at 82 mg/mL and 69 mg/mL of inhibitory concentration (IC50) when compared to Withanaia somnifera plant extract at 69 mg/mL for A-549 cells (lung epithelial carcinoma). As reported by Farooq Khan et al.80, the plant root extract of Moricandia sinaica (Boiss.) was tested against MCF7, MDA-MB-231 (breast cancer cells), HUVEC (human umbilical vein endothelial cells) and HepG2 (liver carcinoma) cells with an IC50 concentration of 114.32, 148.93 and 143.98 mg/mL, respectively. Ficus deltoidea plant extract treatment was reported to be effective in treating oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) at high doses by Al-Koshab et al.81.

Morphological changes in oral cancer cells

The oral cancer cells treated with 38.60 µg/ml of 3,5-Dichlorobenzyl propyl ester for 24 h exhibited significant morphological changes, and compared to untreated controls, the treated cells exhibited reduced cell counts and visible cellular atrophy (Fig. 8b). Additional observations included the detachment of dying cells from the plate surface and a loss of contact between neighbouring cells, indicative of apoptosis. The morphological changes were observed in the cell lines viz., the control cells display normal morphology and 3,5-Dichlorobenzyl propyl ester treated cells were showed shrinkage, membrane blebbing, and floating cells, indicating apoptosis. Images were obtained using phase contrast inverted microscope.

Detection of apoptotic cells using EtBr staining

In a study of apoptotic cells exposed to 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester at 38.60 µg/mL for 24 h, ethidium bromide (EtBr) staining was used to evaluate the nuclear morphology of the cells. An EtBr staining was applied for 20 min to cells, and they were observed under a fluorescent microscope. Apoptosis cells were membrane-compromised, with orange-stained and yellow-stained cells corresponding to the early and late stages (Fig. 8c). The results of this study confirm that 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester induces apoptosis in oral cancer cells KB-1.

Apoptotic gene expression analysis

The apoptotic effects of 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester in oral cancer cells and the expression levels of key apoptosis-related markers were evaluated. These markers include pro-apoptotic genes BAD and BAX, the executioner protease caspase-3, and the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2. The analysis revealed a marked alteration in the expression profile of these genes upon treatment with 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester, indicating its potent role in modulating the apoptotic pathway. Significant downregulation of Bcl-2 was observed, which is known to play a critical role in inhibiting apoptosis by stabilizing mitochondrial integrity and preventing the release of pro-apoptotic factors (Fig. 8d). The reduced expression of Bcl-2 suggests a loss of anti-apoptotic signaling, favoring cell death processes in oral cancer cells. Conversely, pro-apoptotic genes BAD, BAX and caspase-3 were significantly upregulated. Both of these proteins are key regulators of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. These findings suggest that 3,5-dichloro benzyl propyl ester mediates its anticancer effects by targeting the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Conclusion

This study evaluated the anticancer potential of phytochemicals derived from Cadaba fruticosa, with a particular focus on 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester. Among the ten bioactive compounds screened, this compound emerged as a promising lead candidate for the treatment of oral cancer. It exhibited favorable drug-likeness profiles according to Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge rules, with acceptable toxicity compared to the control drug. Network pharmacology analysis revealed strong interactions with key oncogenic targets including EGFR, MAPK3, MTOR, KRAS, and JAK2, implicating its role in modulating crucial cancer-related pathways such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK, mTOR, Wnt, and TGF-β. Molecular docking confirmed a high binding affinity with the SMAD protein (-7.90 kcal/mol), supported by molecular dynamics simulations that demonstrated complex stability over 200 ns. In vitro cytotoxicity assays against KB-1 oral cancer cells showed a significant IC₅₀ of 38.60 µg/mL, markedly lower than that of the control drug (360 mg/mL), highlighting its potent anticancer activity. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) analysis revealed favorable electronic properties, including high electron donor/acceptor capacity and HOMO–LUMO energies of 6.1810 eV and 1.8016 eV, respectively. This study is the first to report the DFT-based evaluation of 3,5-dichlorobenzyl propyl ester, establishing its stability and potential reactivity. Overall, the findings underscore the therapeutic potential of this natural compound for oral cancer treatment. Future work involving structural optimization and mechanistic studies could further enhance its clinical applicability and efficacy.
