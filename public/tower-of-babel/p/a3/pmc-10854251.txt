Introduction

Despite significant advancements in pediatric dentistry, especially regarding children's oral health, tooth decay remains the predominant childhood ailment. The management of caries in children necessitates a meticulous selection of restorative materials, with a focus on ensuring that the restoration preserves function until the natural exfoliation of the tooth.1,2

Simultaneously, the landscape of restorative dentistry has undergone changes, with adhesive dentistry steadily gaining prominence. Modern operative dentistry emphasizes minimizing tooth tissue removal and utilizing adhesive restorative materials with therapeutic effects on demineralized dentin.2 Glass ionomer cement (GIC) aligns well with these requirements, having revolutionized the restorative aspects of pediatric dentistry.3

Despite the advancements, conventional GICs still exhibit several drawbacks, such as the need for final polishing after a 24-hour period, slow development of ultimate properties, limited working time, susceptibility to moisture dehydration leading to microcracks, and lower cohesive strength compared to resin cements.4 In response to these challenges, modifications have been introduced in the form of restorative materials for primary teeth, namely Ketac Universal (3M), GC 9 EXTRA (GC), and Cention N (Ivoclar).

The objective of this study was to assess the clinical and radiographic performance of three restorative materials—Ketac Universal, GC 9 EXTRA, and Cention N—using modified United States Public Health Service (USPHS) Ryge criteria and bitewing radiographs. The null hypothesis suggested that, in the study conditions, there would be no noticeable difference in the clinical performance of the three materials based on the assessed criteria.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Criteria

This randomized clinical trial compared three restorative materials in primary molars at the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Karnavati School of Dentistry, following the acquisition of written informed consent. Prior to the study's initiation, the study protocol, procedures, and informed consent were thoroughly explained to patients and their guardians. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (KSDEC/17-18/apr/37), and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria encompassed patients aged 3–8 years, requiring new class I restorations in primary molars with normal appearance, occlusion, periodontal status, oral hygiene, likelihood of recall availability, and absence of radiographic pulpal involvement or periapical pathology or furcal issues.

Exclusion criteria involved patients with behavioral issues, medical complications, poor oral hygiene, pulp-involved caries, partially erupted teeth, absence of adjacent or antagonist teeth, special needs, bruxism or traumatic malocclusion, radiographic bone loss or increased tooth mobility, and those not providing consent.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was determined using the formula—with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and 80% power, a sample size of 75 was calculated to detect an effect size (W) of 0.40 using a 2 degrees of freedom (DF) Chi-squared test. The formula used was N = CHISQUARE/W2, where W = 0.40 and CHISQUARE = 12 (DF = 2). Considering a 20% dropout rate, the final sample size for each group was 25, resulting in a total study sample size of 75.

The principal investigator conducted examinations on all participants. A total of 75 primary molars from 43 patients aged 3–8 years and meeting the specified criteria were chosen for restoration after obtaining informed consent. Intraoral photographs of carious teeth were captured with a 1.5× magnification Canon 1500D digital single-lens reflex (DSLR) camera (Fig. 1). Bitewing radiographs were taken using standard techniques with size 0 (22 × 35 mm) or size 1 (24 × 40 mm) sensor films (E-speed films, Kodak, Eastman) and Rinn XCP Film holders (Dentsply, XCP film holding system) with the same digital imaging system (Port-X II EZX-60). The process was carried out by a single operator with standardized exposure parameters (60 kV, 2 Ma, 0.2 seconds).

The sample size was further divided into 25 teeth in each group by computerized randomization method. The three restorative groups included are as follows: Group I—Ketac Universal (3M), group 2—GC 9 EXTRA (GC), and group III—Cention N (Ivoclar).

Cavity Preparation

The extent of the lesion determined the cavity preparation for all three restorative materials. Local anesthesia (2% lignocaine hydrochloride with adrenaline 1:80,000) was administered to patients experiencing pain or sensitivity to ensure comfort during restorative procedures. Rubber dam isolation was employed, and cavities were prepared using diamond burs (330 SS white) at high speed with water cooling. Hand instruments and slow-speed tungsten carbide burs were utilized for caries removal. Cavity preparation followed the principles of minimally invasive dentistry, steering clear of affecting one or more cusps. All gingival margins encompassed sound enamel without beveling on the cavity walls. When dealing with deep cavities, a TheraCal LC liner cured with light was applied. Following the manufacturer's instructions, all cavities were restored with either Ketac Universal, GC 9 EXTRA, or Cention N. The entire restoration process was performed by a single operator.

Restoration Placement

Ketac Universal (3M)

Ketac Universal is one of the commercially available Glass Ionomer Restorative materials, which is composed of oxide glasses with enhanced properties and designed to save steps for a faster procedure, so it can be used for treating children. According to manufacturer's instruction, the dosed material was mixed on a mixing pad and bulk fill was directly placed into the cavity by hand instrument. The restoration was completed during the same visit, employing standardized procedures that began with a course, followed by a medium, and then a fine diamond abrasive bur, concluding after 6–8 minutes at the final cement set.

GC 9 EXTRA (GC)

GC 9 EXTRA is strontium-based glass ionomer restorative with extra fluoride-releasing properties and extra translucency. Following the manufacturer's instructions, the dentin and enamel of cavities underwent conditioning with 20% polyacrylic acid (GC conditioner) for 10 seconds, followed by washing and brief drying. Subsequently, GC 9 EXTRA powder and liquid were combined on a mixing pad in a ratio of 1 level scoop of powder to 1 drop of liquid. The mixture was then placed into the cavity using hand instruments, followed by the contouring of the restoration. Final finishing was done after 6 minutes from the start of the mix, and the patient was instructed not to apply pressure for 1 hour.

Cention N (Ivovclar)

Cention N is a recently introduced alkasite with properties combining those of glass ionomer and amalgam. It is composed of dimethacrylates, glass, iso-fillers, calcium fluorosilicate glass, barium glass, calcium-barium-aluminum fluorosilicate, and ytterbium trifluoride. This composition combines the benefits of both amalgam and GICs, functioning as a self-curing powder/liquid restorative with the option for additional light curing.

Adhering to the manufacturer's guidelines, the procedure includes smoothing enamel margins with diamond burs. Following this, the cavity is etched with phosphoric acid for 15 seconds (3M ESPE, St Paul, Minnesota, United States), rinsed for 15 seconds to remove residual acid, and dried with oil-free air for 10 seconds, maintaining moisture to prevent dentin desiccation. The subsequent steps involve applying Tetric N-Bond Universal (Ivoclar) in two consecutive coats, gently drying for 5 seconds, and curing for 10 seconds.

The subsequent step entails mixing one scoop of powder and one drop of liquid on a mixing pad until homogenous consistency is achieved. This mixture is then applied into the cavity, followed by condensation of the material and removal of occlusal excess. The process is concluded with light curing (intensity ≥ 500 mW/cm²) for 40 seconds. After polymerization, excess material is removed using suitable tungsten carbide finishers/fine-grit diamonds. Occlusion and articulation are assessed, and adjustments are performed as needed to avoid premature contacts or unintended articulation paths on the restoration's surface.

Oral Hygiene Instructions

Children were advised not to chew hard or sticky foods or drink staining products such as tea, coffee, and cold beverages on recently placed restoration for 24 hours. The patient was instructed to brush twice daily with fluoridated toothpaste and informed to report if any symptom occurred in the treated tooth.

Failure Criteria and Dropout

The restorations were deemed unsuccessful if replacement was required due to issues such as loss of retention, material fracture, or the occurrence of secondary caries.

Evaluation and Follow-up

Two independent examiners, unaware of the filling composition, conducted blind evaluations of the restorations to ensure unbiased judgment based on the modified USPHS criteria. These examiners underwent training and regular calibration to achieve an 85% agreement with established standards. The evaluation involved the use of mirrors and probes, with any discrepancies in opinions resolved by a third experienced evaluator. Restorations were assessed using the modified USPHS criteria at baseline, 6 months, and 1 year, supported by intraoral color photographs and bitewing radiographs taken during these periods. Recorded values included A (alpha), B (bravo), and C (Charlie) to signify different outcomes.

Criteria for marginal discoloration were categorized as absent/present, while radiographic criteria assessed material homogeneity, classified as follows:

Good—no voids present, complete adaptation to vertical wall and floor.

Good—no voids present, complete adaptation to vertical wall and floor.

Poor—voids present, incomplete adaptation to vertical wall and floor, indicating material homogeneity issues.

Poor—voids present, incomplete adaptation to vertical wall and floor, indicating material homogeneity issues.

The statistical analysis utilized IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 20 for Windows, employing the Chi-squared test for categorical data and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for quantitative data. Significance levels were set at p < 0.05 for all statistical analyses, determining the statistical significance of the results.

Results and Observation

A total of 75 class I restorations were examined in 45 patients aged 3–8 years, distributed as 25 Ketac Universal, 25 GC 9 EXTRA, and 25 Cention N. Evaluations were conducted at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months using modified USPHS criteria and bitewing radiographs.

At baseline, all 75 restorations (100%) were available for recall. However, at 6 months, the number decreased to 69 restorations (92%) for evaluation, comprising 24 Ketac Universal, 23 GC 9 EXTRA, and 22 Cention N restorations. By the end of 1 year, 63 restorations (84%) were available for assessment, with 22 Ketac Universal, 21 GC 9 EXTRA, and 20 Cention N restorations.

Dropouts at the study's conclusion were attributed to patients who did not attend follow-up appointments and half of the participants relocated.

Radiographic Evaluation

Homogeneity of Material

At baseline, all three restorative groups exhibited good homogeneity with no voids and proper adaptation on walls. At 6 months, 21 Ketac Universal (87.5%), 21 GC 9 EXTRA (91.3%), and 21 Cention N (95.5%) restorations were evaluated as good, while three Ketac Universal (12.5%), two GC 9 EXTRA (8.7%), and one Cention N (4.5%) were rated as poor. At 12 months, 18 Ketac Universal (81.8%), 19 GC 9 EXTRA (90.5%), and 19 Cention N (95%) restorations were assessed as good, with three Ketac Universal (18.2%), two GC 9 EXTRA (9.5%), and one Cention N (5%) were considered poor. No significant differences were observed among the groups at 12 months (p > 0.05).

Marginal Integrity

No changes in marginal integrity were noted at baseline for all three restorations. At 6 months, three Ketac Universal (12.5%), two GC 9 EXTRA (8.7%), and one Cention N (4.5%) restorations showed compromised integrity. At 12 months, 4 Ketac Universal (18.2%), 2 GC 9 EXTRA (9.5%), and 1 Cention N (5%) exhibited compromised integrity. No significant differences were observed among the groups at 12 months (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In recent years, advancements in esthetic dentistry have witnessed significant progress, resulting in the creation of several enhanced restorative materials. Presently, the primary focus on these materials revolves around their durability and the maintenance of marginal sealing integrity.5 The fundamental aim of restorative materials is to stimulate the functional, biological, and esthetic attributes of a healthy tooth structure.6

Numerous alternatives exist for the restoration of primary teeth, including conventional GIC, resin-modified GIC, composite resins, and polyacid-modified composites.4,7 Although traditional GICs present enhanced features like adhesion, rapid setting, ion exchange, and high levels of compressive, flexural, and tensile strength, along with surface hardness, abrasion resistance, and fluoride release, they do come with certain limitations.4 These include the requirement for final polishing after 24 hours, a short working time, gradual development of ultimate properties, susceptibility to moisture-induced dehydration resulting in microcracks, and lower cohesive strength when compared to resin cements.

Cention N, a newly introduced filling material, combines the advantages of both amalgams and GICs. It is a foundational, resin-based, self-curing powder/liquid restorative with the option for additional light curing. This radiopaque material releases fluoride, hydroxide ions, and calcium and can be recharged by exposure to fluoride solutions and gels. Being a dual-cured substance, it can function as a full-volume (bulk) replacement material. The choice of optional light curing involves the use of blue light within the wavelength range of approximately 400–500 nm.8,9 The USPHS criteria for the clinical evaluation of dental restorations were formulated by Cvar and Ryge in 1971. This evaluation framework has been widely adopted for assessing the clinical performance of restorations over the long term. It stands as the predominant and widely accepted criteria, facilitating comparisons across various studies conducted at different observation periods.4,7,10,17 This validity for comparative purposes has been acknowledged by researcher such as Daou et al.10

Our study investigated the clinical and radiographic comparison of three restorative materials in the age group of 3–8 years in 43 patients in 75 primary molars using modified USPHS criteria and bitewing radiographs at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. For all three restorative materials, cavity preparation was done according to minimal invasive procedure as pulp horns in primary teeth are high and retentive preparation may lead to more enamel loss, whereas in Cention N type of restoration, adhesive (Tetric N-bond) was applied in case of minimal invasive preparation, according to manufacturer's instructions. The results of our study showed that Cention N proved to have higher alpha scores and clinically better properties compared to GC 9 EXTRA and Ketac Universal restorative material.

A higher incidence of color mismatch cases was noted in the Ketac Universal group at 12 months, attributed to an increased opacity of Ketac Universal, rendering the restoration excessively opaque. This phenomenon may be attributed to the size of glass particles in the Ketac Universal powder, falling within the range of 7.2 μm.18 Exceeding the wavelength of light (350–750 nm), this size leads to light scattering by the particles in the Ketac Universal powder, resulting in opaque materials.11 Similarly, more Bravo scores were observed in GC 9 EXTRA at 12 months, potentially owing to the size of glass particles in the GC 9 EXTRA powder, ranging from 7.13 μm (7130 nm) to 13.43 μm (13430 nm), contributing to its opacity.19

Cention N features particle sizes ranging from 0.1 to 35 μm (nanoclusters), well below the wavelength of light. This makes them indiscernible by the refractive index. When exposed to light, it seamlessly traverses through the material, ensuring high translucency. Additionally, the nanoparticles selectively scatter blue light, contributing to an opalescent effect in the restoration.12

Marginal discoloration and marginal integrity are indicative of microleakage. GICs establish a chemical bond with tooth structure by exchanging ions between the tooth and the restoration, leading to the formation of a calcium-polyacrylate bond.20 Due to their high powder-to-liquid (p/l) ratio and reduced glass particle size, both GC 9 EXTRA and Ketac Universal exhibit high viscosity. The observed microleakage behavior is likely linked to this increased viscosity, which hampers effective wetting of the tooth surface and impedes the formation of a strong seal between the interfaces of the tooth and the restoration.21

These cements require meticulous technique due to their sensitivity, with isolation from moisture being a critical aspect during the initial 30 minutes post placement. Exposure to water during the setting phase can lead to the leaching out of matrix-forming ions, potentially disrupting the interface between the tooth and restoration. Excessive dehydration can lead to a surface that is chalky, cracked, or crazy, contributing to notable marginal leakage and subsequent discoloration at the margins.22

Cention N showcases a dense polymer network and thorough polymerization across the entire depth of the restoration. It includes a patented Isofiller filler designed to act as a shrinkage stress reliever, effectively minimizing the forces responsible for low volumetric shrinkage and, consequently, reducing microleakage to a minimum.23 With a low elastic modulus of 10 GPa, Cention N's shrinkage stress reliever actively mitigates polymerization shrinkage and microleakage, promoting improved marginal integrity and reduced marginal discoloration at the 12-month mark.22 Furthermore, a study by Mazumdar et al. in 2019 emphasized that Cention N exhibited minimal microleakage when compared to GIC and amalgam in an in vitro setting.24

At the 12-month assessment, Ketac Universal GIC exhibited a significantly higher incidence of marginal integrity issues and reduced marginal adaptation when compared to GC 9 EXTRA and Cention N. This may contribute to the notable increase in secondary caries observed with Ketac Universal at the 12-month mark, as evidenced by the evaluation of one case of secondary caries. Notably, existing studies have indicated that the application of a high-filler GIC for the restoration of primary molars resulted in a survival rate of 92% for class I cavities and 66% for class II cavities at 2 years, with no instances of failure attributed to recurrent caries. In contrast, our study findings deviate from these previously published results.25

The alterations in anatomical form suggested that the assessed materials experienced minimal impact from wear. However, a notable limitation of our study lies in the subjective nature of the wear evaluation. It depended on the examiner's judgment to determine whether any changes in anatomical form had occurred over time. Consequently, caution is warranted when interpreting these findings. The qualitative nature of our method lacks the precision offered by alternative wear analysis methods, like the indirect cast comparison method, which provides more accurate quantification.10

The current investigation utilized bitewing radiographs for radiographic assessment, revealing a higher score of poor outcomes regarding material homogeneity and marginal integrity in Ketac Universal when compared to GC 9 Extra and Cention N. This disparity could be attributed to microleakage occurring between the tooth and glass ionomer due to smear layer dissolution. The existence of the smear layer during cavity preparation hinders the bonding of restorative material to dental hard tissues. Moisture contamination, in turn, can contribute to material loss at the margins, leading to the formation of micro gaps. In contrast, Cention N involves the application of adhesive to modify the smear layer, ensuring adequate wetting of the tooth surface to facilitate adhesion. Additionally, Isofiller in Cention N serves as a shrinkage stress reliever, minimizing shrinkage forces and reducing gap formation.

Comparisons with previous studies provide additional context. Welbury13 conducted a clinical trial involving compomer and GIC, concluding that Dyract compomer outperformed ChemFil Superior GIC over a 42-month follow-up period.

Dedania et al. evaluated the clinical performance of amalgam and Cention N, finding equal performance after 1 year and highlighting the superior esthetics of Cention N compared to amalgam.12 Dodiya et al. compared Cention-N with Nanohybrid composite resin, concluding that Cention-N was as effective as Tetric-N-Ceram for marginal integrity and gross fracture up to six months.14 Kaur et al. reported significantly higher compressive strength for Cention N compared to GC 9 Extra in an in vitro study.26 Gupta et al. emphasized Cention-N's heightened fluoride ion release and alkalizing potential in acidic pH when compared to GIC. This was attributed to the alkaline glass fillers releasing hydroxyl and calcium ions.27

Restorations having deep cavities were lined by recently introduced light cured resin-modified calcium silicate TheraCal LC as it exhibits a shorter setting time, superior mechanical properties and bonds physicochemical to dentin, and faster apatite-formation compared to calcium hydroxide-based materials.28

Despite these findings, the present study acknowledges certain limitations. It did not address the caries risk assessment of participating children, potentially impacting restoration survival. Additionally, a longer follow-up period and a larger sample size could enhance the understanding of each restorative material's performance. Within the study's constraints, the conclusion suggests that in adhesively designed occlusal cavities in primary molars, Cention N surpasses Ketac Universal and GC 9 Extra in terms of anatomic form, cavosurface discolorations, marginal integrity, recurrent caries, maintenance of interproximal contact, surface texture, and overall failure, leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis.

Conclusion

The study yields the following conclusions:

All three restorative materials, Ketac Universal, GC 9 EXTRA, and Cention N, demonstrated acceptable clinical performance as assessed by modified USPHS criteria and bitewing radiographs. However, Ketac Universal exhibited a higher number of unsatisfactory scores.
