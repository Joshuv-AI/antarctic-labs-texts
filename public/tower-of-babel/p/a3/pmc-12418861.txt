Digital speech assessments and machine learning for differentiation of neurodegenerative diseases
Authors: Kyurim Kang, Adonay S. Nunes, Ilkay Yildiz Potter, Ram Kinker Mishra, Andrew Geronimo, Jamie L. Adams, Catherine Isroff, Jesse E. Wang, Ashkan Vaziri, Anne-Marie Wills, Alexander Pantelyat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Key speech features such as Total Voiced Time differentiated clinical conditions.•Elastic Net models reached 77% balanced accuracy across the 5 diagnostic classes.•Machine learning can extract key speech features in neurodegenerative diseases.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases, including PD, PSP, HD, and ALS, present significant challenges for both patients and healthcare providers due to their complexity and heterogeneity, with distinct motor, speech, and cognitive symptoms [1]. Speech analysis has gained attention as a tool for detecting and monitoring neurodegenerative diseases. Temporal and spectral acoustic features of speech provide valuable insights into disease-specific patterns, aiding diagnosis and revealing underlying pathophysiology, potentially paving the way for novel treatment strategies [2]. Early phonation changes can identify patients prior to diagnosis with PD [3], and speech features also help distinguish PSP from PD, which share clinical symptoms but differ in distinct speech characteristics [4]. While PD is typically associated with hypokinetic dysarthria, PSP manifests as mixed dysarthria with specific prosodic and articulatory deficits [4]. Changes in speech clarity, articulation, and prosodic features manifest early in HD, offering promising avenues for both diagnosis and disease monitoring [5]. Similarly, prior research has highlighted speech as a potential digital biomarker for monitoring disease progression in ALS [6].

Traditional clinical assessments can be limited by inter-observer variability and lower sensitivity, whereas digital measures can provide objective, quantitative, remote, and continuous assessment [7]. The increasing use of ML in speech feature analysis is contributing to the evolving diagnostic process for neurological diseases, including PD [4], PSP [4], HD [8], and ALS [9]. However, few studies have employed these features to differentiate multiple neurodegenerative diseases, leaving an important gap in the field.

To address this, this study used digital speech assessments and ML to classify PSP, PD, HD, ALS, and CTR. Including multiple populations improves model performance and broadens understanding of speech biomarkers across neurodegenerative diseases.

Methodology

Participants

Participants were enrolled in four similarly designed observational cohort studies, using the same digital tablet for voice-recording. The study protocols were reviewed and approved by the Mass General Brigham Human Research Committee, Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board, the University of Rochester Institutional Review Board, and Penn State University Institutional Review Board. All procedures followed the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 1983.

During baseline visit, demographics, cognitive assessments and clinical assessments were collected, as well as digital speech assessments. This study focused on the Rainbow Passage speech assessment, previously shown to capture disease progression and identification in PSP, PD [4] and HD [8]. Some studies have performed longitudinal data collection with participants returning for visits every few months; however, this manuscript focuses solely on a cross-sectional baseline dataset.

Participants with probable PSP [10] or PD [11] diagnosed according to established criteria were included, contingent on having a caregiver available to support all study-related procedures. ALS participants met the definition of definite, probable, probable laboratory-supported, or possible ALS by revised El Escorial research criteria [12]. For HD participants, the status was confirmed clinically by a movement disorders specialist, with eligibility based on either a self-reported first-degree relative with HD or a genetic test showing a CAG expansion greater than 36 in the Huntington gene [13]. Control group participants were age-matched healthy individuals with no evidence of neurological disorders. Clinical severity was assessed using disease-specific rating scales: the Progressive Supranuclear Palsy Rating Scale (PSPRS) for PSP, the Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS-UPDRS III) for PD, the ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised (ALSFRS-R) for ALS, and the Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale (UHDRS) for HD.

Data collection

Motor, speech, and cognitive function in participants' homes were measured using BioDigit Home (BioSensics LLC, Newton, MA, USA), which employs digital assessments and wearable sensors to provide real-time, remote data collection. BioDigit Home provides visual and audio instructions via a tablet to participants, along with reminders, ensuring consistent and timely data acquisition.

Participants were instructed to read aloud the full passage (The Rainbow Passage) at their natural pace and volume and in a quiet environment, with minimal external noise interference. If a participant made a mistake while reading, they were instructed to continue from the point where the error occurred, rather than backtracking.

Data analysis

Speech data were processed and analyzed using BioDigit Speech (BioSensics LLC, Newton, MA, USA). BioDigit Speech employs automated speech recognition (ASR) to transcribe speech, providing timestamps for each word. The pre-processed audio was then analyzed for phonatory, articulatory, prosody, and intelligibility features, which were relevant to the passage reading task.

Speech features were extracted to assess various aspects of speech production during the passage reading task, including pause per second, total signal time, articulatory rate, total voiced time, total pause time, speech-to-pause ratio, mean pause length, mean pitch, as well as the ratio of extra and missing words. Furthermore, two dynamic time warping (DTW) metrics were used: similarity DTW, which indicated the similarity between the original and transcribed readings, and intelligibility DTW, which evaluated the accuracy of transcriptions from two different speech recognition models. The compared models were the normal sized one and a tiny model more prone to error if speech is not clear. Detailed information on the speech features is available in Kang et al. [4].

Machine learning

An Elastic Net model was initially employed to classify speech features from PSP, PD, HD, ALS, and CTR groups. This model was trained on the speech features extracted from the rainbow passage along with age, aiming to distinguish between the five groups. However, upon analyzing the performance of this initial model, it was found that distinguishing between PSP and PD was particularly challenging. To address this issue, a four-class model was trained on speech features along with age to classify combined PSP-PD, HD, ALS, and CTR groups. A specialized model based on Support Vector Machine was then used to further classify the PSP-PD group into PSP and PD based on speech features along with age. A leave-one-subject-out cross-validation strategy was applied to all models, ensuring that the training data was robust and that the models' ability to generalize was properly evaluated. Model performance was assessed with balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and Area Under the Curve (AUC one vs all others). Furthermore, the model coefficients were calculated to assess the contribution of each feature in predicting the target outcomes for each population.

Results

A total of 92 individuals from four different sites were included in this study, comprising 21 PSP (mean age = 67.6 years, SD = 5.70; 9 females, 42.8 %; mean years since diagnosis = 0.99, SD = 0.66), 21 PD (mean age = 70.9 years, SD = 6.00; 6 females, 28.5 %; mean years since diagnosis = 8.78, SD = 4.73, 18 HD (mean age = 51.90 years, SD = 11.00; 8 females, 44.4 %; mean years since diagnosis = 6.40, SD = 6.65), 15 ALS (mean age = 69.00 years, SD = 7.50; 4 females, 26.7 %; mean years since onset symptoms = 2.63, SD = 1.98), and 16 control participants (mean age = 58.90 years, SD = 12.20; 11 females, 68.5 %). All individuals with PSP were clinically classified as having the Richardson’s Syndrome (PSP-RS) subtype based on established diagnostic criteria [10]. First, the five-class model achieved a balanced accuracy of 77 % over 5 diagnostic classes (group-specific sensitivities of 76 % for PSP, 67 % for PD, 83 % for HD, 73 % for ALS, and 88 % for CTR), demonstrating a particular challenge to differentiate between PSP and PD. Instead, the four-class model was able to accurately differentiate between diseases with a balanced accuracy of 83 %, and group-specific sensitivities of 83 % for PSP-PD, 83 % for HD, 73 % for ALS, and 94 % for CTR (Fig. 1A). Next, for differentiating between PSP and PD, the specialized classification model demonstrated a balanced accuracy of 85 %, with sensitivity values of 88 % for PSP and 82 % for PD (Fig. 1A). To account for the dependency between the two models, the joint probability of correct classification was calculated as the product of the two models’ classification probability. Fig. 1C indicates a clear distinction between groups. Group means and standard deviations for all speech features are provided in the Supplementary Material. Fig. 1D displays the model coefficients for speech analysis across different clinical conditions, including PSP-PD, HD, ALS, CTR, and PD vs. PSP. For the combined PSP-PD group, the model coefficients indicated that positive values for Total Voiced Time, Similarity DTW, Total Signal Time, Mean Pause Length, Total Signal Time and Speech to Pause Ratio suggest that these features are more indicative of the combined PSP-PD population. In the case of HD, positive coefficients were observed for Ratio Extra Words and Ratio Missing Words. For ALS, a strong positive coefficient for Pauses per second was identified, along with additional positive coefficients for Total Signal Time, Total Pause Time, and Articulatory Rate. In the CTR group, positive coefficients were seen for Intelligibility DTW, Similarity DTW, Articulatory Rate and Pitch. When comparing PD and PSP, positive coefficients were observed particularly for Articulatory Rate, Similarity DTW and Pauses per Second. Discussion

The classification models in this study highlight the promising potential of leveraging ML techniques. Elastic Net models achieved a balanced accuracy of 77 % over 5 diagnostic classes (group-specific sensitivities of 76 % for PSP, 67 % for PD, 83 % for HD, 73 % for ALS, and 88 % for CTR) and 83 % over 4 diagnostic classes (group-specific sensitivities of 83 % for PSP-PD, 83 % for HD, 73 % for ALS, and 94 % for CTR). The four-class ML model sensitivity values reflected its robust ability to identify and classify distinct groups. These findings support the model's capacity to generalize effectively across PD, PSP, HD, and ALS while maintaining strong classification performance for CTR. The high AUC values further underscore the distinctiveness of speech feature profiles among PSP-PD, HD, ALS, and CTR groups. Our results align with previous studies, which have shown that speech-based biomarkers can play a crucial role in distinguishing between conditions such as PD and PSP [4], as well as HD and prodromal HD [8].

The specialized PSP-PD classification model achieved a balanced accuracy of 85 %. It demonstrated fairly high sensitivity for both PSP (88 %) and PD (82 %), addressing the limitation of the four-class model in distinguishing between these two closely related conditions. This improvement suggests that the specialized model was able to capture the nuanced differences in speech features between PSP and PD. The ability to fine-tune the model for challenging subgroup distinctions suggests a significant step forward in clinical diagnostics, where differentiating between PSP and PD is often a critical challenge due to overlapping symptoms. This approach ensured that the combined system remains robust and reliable, providing an integrated framework for accurate classification. Such a probabilistic methodology emphasizes the model's practical applicability in clinical settings, where multimodal or layered decision-making processes may be required.

Our results suggest that ML-based speech analysis, combined with digital health tools, can provide valuable diagnostic support for clinicians – e.g., PSP-PD speech profiles with specific patterns such as positive contributions from features like Total Voiced Time, and distinct profiles for HD and ALS. These findings can guide targeted interventions and monitoring.

A key limitation is the small sample size, which may impact the generalizability of the results. As a cross-sectional, longitudinal study is needed to evaluate diagnostic sensitivity and track progression in speech changes. Further, the algorithm will need to be tested in earlier stage patients to determine whether it can accurately distinguish patients whose diagnoses may not yet be clinically obvious. Future research should investigate the applicability of this approach for early detection and differential diagnosis in prodromal or preclinical phases. Additionally, this study used only one speech task (passage reading), which may limit feature capture. Future work could include varied tasks, incorporating longitudinal data to assess disease progression, and validating the models across larger, more diverse populations.

Further exploration of the joint classification approach could optimize decision-making frameworks, paving the way for seamless integration of ML tools into routine clinical practice. Multimodal data fusion and advanced analytical techniques could enhance diagnostic accuracy, reduce uncertainty in clinical assessments, and support personalized treatment.

All participants with PSP in this study met criteria for the Richardson’s Syndrome subtype (PSP-RS). While this enhances diagnostic consistency, it limits generalizability to other PSP variants (e.g., PSP-Parkinsonism, PSP-Speech/Language variant, PSP-Corticobasal syndrome, PSP-Primary Gait Freezing), which may present with different speech-language features and progression patterns. Although our study focused on PSP-RS, which is generally less challenging to diagnose, we recognize that PSP–Parkinsonism (PSP-P) in particular presents substantial diagnostic obstacles due to its overlap with PD and multiple system atrophy-parkinsonism (MSA-P)— especially in early stages [14]. PSP-P has been difficult to differentiate from these entities using fluid biomarkers or imaging methods such as MRI and SPECT/PET [14]. Future studies using digital speech assessments and machine learning will need to include PSP-P to evaluate whether this approach can reliably differentiate PSP-P from PD, PSP-RS and MSA-P. Overall, well-characterized PSP subtype representation is necessary to assess the broader applicability of the findings reported here.

Notably, despite the small sample size, we achieved 77 % and 83 % classification accuracy for five-class and four-class diagnostic models, respectively, using a single cross-sectional assessment, with durations ranging from 30.88 to 60.02 s. This level of performance is comparable to that of expert diagnosticians, who require detailed neurological evaluations and often longitudinal follow up to ascertain the clinical diagnosis. We thus highlight the potential of digital health technologies and ML to revolutionize the diagnosis and management of neurodegenerative diseases, offering a scalable, non-invasive, and efficient solution to a critical healthcare challenge.
