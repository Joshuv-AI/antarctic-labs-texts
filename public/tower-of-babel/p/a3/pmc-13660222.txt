Missed diagnosis of cardiac myxoma and the subsequent complications despite therapeutic anticoagulation: a case report
Authors: Somaye Mohammadi, Mohammad Reza Eftekhari, Amin Eskanday Torbaqan, Hamidreza Soleimani, Amir Nasrollahizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMyxomas are the most prevalent primary cardiac tumors, primarily found in the left atrium. Symptoms include mitral valve obstruction and systemic embolization, leading to complications like ischemic stroke. Accurate diagnosis through cardiac evaluation is crucial to prevent delays in treatment and recurrence of symptoms.Case presentationA 53-year-old male patient with a history of an acute stroke three months prior and an incomplete workup presented with right-sided hemiplegia and dysarthria. A diagnosis of acute stroke was made, and the patient was managed with mechanical thrombectomy, retrieving a large clot in the Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) territory. Cardiac workup revealed a large mobile mass in the left atrium, and a diagnosis of myxoma was made. The tumor was removed successfully via thoracotomy. The patient remained asymptomatic without any residual mass.ConclusionWhile cardiac myxomas are primarily benign tumors, they can occasionally lead to catastrophic complications. A comprehensive cardiac evaluation, including Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE), is essential in stroke cases where a cardioembolic source is suspected to identify the underlying pathology. Our case highlights how omission of TTE during the index admission resulted in recurrent stroke despite anticoagulation and underscores the preventable nature of such complications through routine cardiac imaging in suspected cardioembolic events.

Introduction

Myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumors [1], arising in more than 80% of cases from the left atrium [2]. Symptoms commonly associated with left atrial myxomas include those resembling mitral valve obstruction, systemic embolization, and constitutional symptoms [1, 3].

The most common serious complication of systemic embolization of left atrial myxomas is cardioembolic ischemic stroke, resulting from embolization of blood clots or fragmentation of the tumor [4, 5]. Although atrial myxomas account for only 0.5% of cardioembolic ischemic strokes, they should be considered in young patients and those without traditional atherosclerotic risk factors [6].

Identification of the cardiac origin of ischemic stroke might impact the overall therapeutic strategy [7], and a cardiac evaluation, including baseline electrocardiogram for all patients and transthoracic echocardiography in selected patients, has been endorsed by guidelines [8, 9]. This step is essential for achieving an accurate diagnosis; however, it may be overlooked, resulting in delays in diagnosis, inadequate treatment, and potentially serious consequences, such as the recurrence of debilitating symptoms.

Here, we present a case of a 53-year-old patient who first experienced neurologic symptoms three months prior to his current admission. Following a recurrence of these symptoms and subsequent evaluations, a large hypermobile mass in the left atrium was identified.

Case presentation

History and physical examination

A 53-year-old male patient presented to our center with a sudden onset of dysarthria and hemiplegia affecting the right upper and lower limbs, occurring two hours prior to his arrival at the emergency room. Upon reviewing the patient’s medical records, it was noted that he experienced similar symptoms three months prior and was admitted to another facility for five days, during which he showed relative improvement. The discharge summary indicated normal findings on electrocardiogram (ECG), brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) (early MRI without diffusion-weighted imaging, which can yield false-negative results in the hyperacute phase), and bilateral Color Doppler ultrasonography of carotid and vertebral arteries. He was discharged with a prescription for Atorvastatin 40 mg daily and Apixaban 5 mg twice daily. The initiation of this direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) was driven empirically by the treating clinicians’ high clinical suspicion of an underlying cardioembolic stroke source, given the sudden onset of symptoms and the absence of significant large-vessel carotid disease, despite their failure to perform an echocardiogram or document a transient atrial arrhythmia to objectively justify its use. Further follow-up visits had been scheduled, but the patient did not attend them. He complained of exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, easy fatigability, and palpitations in the past month. He reported a history of cigarette smoking and oral opium use. His family history was unremarkable.

Upon arrival, his vital signs were stable and included a regular pulse rate of 84 bpm, a blood pressure of 140/85mmHg, a respiratory rate of 19 per minute, axillary temperature of 37.2˚C, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. The patient experienced significant communication difficulties due to dysarthria. Muscle strength assessments revealed scores of 0, 2, 5, and 5 for the right upper limb, right lower limb, left upper limb, and left lower limb, respectively, on a scale of 0 to 5 (where 0 indicates no muscle contraction and 5 signifies the ability to move the joint through its full range of motion against both gravity and examiner-applied resistance). The patient exhibited difficulty with the finger-to-nose test on the right side. Cranial nerve examination results were normal. An ECG demonstrated a normal sinus rhythm without any conduction abnormalities (Fig. 1). The blood glucose level, measured using a finger-stick test, was 127 mg/dL.

Diagnostic evaluations and therapeutic procedures

The patient was immediately transferred to the neurology catheterization laboratory. Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA) demonstrated total occlusion of both the M1 and M2 branches of the Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA), with no perfusion observed in the distal vascular beds (Fig. 2). Thrombus aspiration using a micro-catheter resulted in the removal of a blood clot measuring 3 cm in length. Following the thrombectomy, DSA indicated normal perfusion in the aforementioned vascular beds. The patient subsequently showed improvement in muscle strength and dysarthria. The retrieved 3 cm clot was consistent with either thrombus or tumor fragment, both of which are common in myxoma-related embolism. Grossly, it appeared as a dark red, elongated, friable clot. Microscopic analysis revealed a dense network of fibrin and entangled erythrocytes interspersed with small fragments of an acellular, pale-staining myxoid matrix consistent with elements of a cardiac tumor, confirming a combined embolic mechanism of secondary thrombus and tumor fragment, which explained the excellent response to mechanical thrombectomy. He was then transferred to the stroke unit for further care. His laboratory test results are shown in Table 1. Notably, the patient had mild anemia (hemoglobin 8.9 g/dL), a finding sometimes associated with myxoma-related constitutional symptoms.

Brain MRI of the patient, utilizing T1 and T2 sequences and Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) protocol, revealed multiple areas of restricted diffusion in the left basal ganglia, as well as in the left frontal and temporal lobes, suggestive of acute infarction (Fig. 3). Given the involvement of multiple vascular territories, a cardiac evaluation was recommended to assess for a possible embolic source.

A Transthoracic Echocardiogram (TTE) was subsequently performed, which revealed normal left ventricular size and function, with an ejection fraction of 55%, while demonstrating a highly mobile, large left atrial mass. To achieve superior anatomical characterization prior to surgical intervention, a Transesophageal Echocardiogram (TEE) was promptly executed. The TEE confirmed the presence of a large, hypermobile mass in the left atrium, attached via a stalk to the interatrial septum (IAS) and dynamically protruding through the mitral valve into the left ventricle during diastole, measuring 50 × 24 mm (Fig. 4).

A consultation was requested with the cardiothoracic surgery service. The patient was transferred to the operating room, where a lobulated, polypoid, soft gelatinous mass was identified in the left atrium and successfully removed via median sternotomy and left atriotomy (Fig. 5). The remainder of his hospital stay was unremarkable, with notable improvements in motor function on the right side and enhanced speech abilities. He was discharged on the ninth day of his hospitalization. Further follow-up visits were scheduled.

Follow-up

The pathology report of the removed mass confirmed the diagnosis of left atrial myxoma. Grossly, it appeared as a dark red, elongated, friable clot, with areas of focal hemorrhage, consistent with the intraoperative appearance. Microscopic analysis revealed a dense network of fibrin and entangled erythrocytes interspersed with small fragments of an acellular, pale-staining myxoid matrix consistent with elements of a cardiac tumor, confirming a combined embolic mechanism of secondary thrombus and tumor fragment. the tumor was composed of scattered stellate and polygonal myxoma cells (lepidic cells) embedded within an abundant myxoid stroma rich in proteoglycans. The cells were arranged singly or in small clusters and cords, without evidence of cytological atypia, necrosis, or mitotic activity. These histopathological features are characteristic of benign cardiac myxoma and are consistent with established diagnostic criteria. At three, six, and twelve months post-admission, the patient reported no symptoms. He was undergoing physical rehabilitation, demonstrating significant improvements in motor function, indicating a stable postoperative recovery period. A TTE performed six months after the operation revealed no residual mass in the left atrium.

Discussion

We presented a 53-year-old male patient who initially developed signs and symptoms of acute stroke three months prior to his presentation at our center. Despite initiation of therapeutic-dose Apixaban after the index event, the patient experienced recurrent stroke, illustrating the limitations of empiric anticoagulation without identification of the underlying cardiac source. Due to an incomplete diagnostic evaluation that completely omitted routine guideline-mandated baseline cardiac imaging during his index admission, the clear cardiac source of his brain ischemia went undetected. This case vividly demonstrates that checking for an embolic source via echocardiography should be pursued in all stroke cases without clear atherosclerotic etiologies, regardless of whether a normal sinus rhythm is captured on an initial ECG. This case underscores the importance of a holistic approach to acute stroke patients, emphasizing that comprehensive evaluations can prevent diagnostic delays and facilitate timely treatment, thereby reducing the risk of further recurrences and debilitation.

While myxomas are primarily benign tumors, they can be associated with embolic events. In a series of 130 patients with atrial myxoma described by Wen et al. [10] 22 initially presented with neurological symptoms, with the MCA territory being the most commonly affected area. However, myxomas are relatively rare as a cause of cardioembolic stroke. But similar to our case, there have been reports of recurrent ischemic attacks associated with a myxoma [11]. Recent evidence indicates that high tumor mobility (OR ~ 3–4) and irregular/friable surface are the dominant risk factors for embolism; tumor size is not significantly associated, and female sex appears protective [12, 13]. Our patient’s 5 cm highly mobile myxoma illustrates the critical role of mobility in embolic risk.

Embolic events in this setting arise via two principal mechanisms: [1] direct fragmentation and systemic embolization of myxomatous tumor material, and [2] thrombus formation on the irregular, friable surface of the myxoma, which subsequently dislodges [14]. Neither mechanism is reliably suppressed by anticoagulant agents. Tumor emboli, composed of myxomatous tissue, are biologically distinct from fibrin-platelet thrombi and are entirely refractory to anticoagulation. Surface thrombus formation in myxoma reflects an active, tumor-driven prothrombotic microenvironment, driven by cytokine secretion, endothelial disruption, and disturbed flow, that differs fundamentally from the flow-dependent thrombus formation observed in the left atrial appendage in atrial fibrillation, where anticoagulation is highly efficacious [15, 16]. This distinction has been demonstrated in case series and reviews documenting recurrent stroke in patients with myxoma despite anticoagulation therapy, and it underscores that surgical resection is the only definitive treatment to eliminate the embolic source [17–19]. Our patient’s experience, a recurrent, more severe stroke after three months of therapeutic-dose apixaban, is a compelling clinical illustration of this mechanism.

Large myxomas can protrude into the left ventricle, potentially causing “functional” mitral valve obstruction. As a result, patients may present with signs and symptoms indicative of valvular heart disease, including exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, and palpitations (which may occur due to atrial fibrillation (AF)). Additionally, patients with myxoma may exhibit constitutional symptoms like weight loss, fever, fatigue, and anemia on laboratory tests. It is suggested that the production of cytokines and growth factors by myxoma cells may contribute to these symptoms [15, 20]. Our patient exhibited both sets of symptoms.

Although the “tumor plop” is regarded as the auscultatory hallmark of atrial myxomas, it is observed in only 15% of patients [21]. TTE is a simple method for diagnosing atrial myxomas, boasting a sensitivity of 95%, making it the preferred diagnostic tool [22]. Characteristic imaging findings include an echogenic, polypoid, or papillary mobile mass located within the atrial cavity and attached to the IAS [23].

When TTE is equivocal or atypical features are present, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or cardiac MRI/CT should be pursued promptly, as these modalities improve the detection of friable or sessile myxomas that continue to be missed on initial imaging [24].

Cardiac evaluation following an acute stroke may influence secondary prevention strategies and, by identifying potential sources of embolization, could have therapeutic implications [25, 26], thus making TTE a highly relevant modality in stroke patients [27]. While screening for AF is now common practice, the role of routine imaging evaluation, including TTE, is less well-established [8, 9, 28–33] (Table 2). What most guidelines agree on is that in patients suspected of having a stroke of cardiac origin, TTE is crucial. Embolic strokes frequently present with sudden-onset symptoms and may show rapid recovery following mechanical or pharmacological thrombectomy. It is essential to consider cardiac sources even in patients who do not exhibit traditional risk factors for large vessel atherosclerosis [34]. Consequently, these patients require comprehensive cardiac evaluations, which should include a detailed medical history, physical examination, ECG, 24-hour monitoring, and TTE. As demonstrated in our case, the absence of a comprehensive cardiac work-up can result in missed diagnoses and potentially catastrophic consequences. Therefore, especially in cases where a cardioembolic source of brain ischemia is suspected, routine TTE, and if needed, bubble contrast and transesophageal echocardiography, may provide timely diagnoses that can significantly influence the therapeutic process.

Conclusion

While atrial myxomas are predominantly benign cardiac tumors, they can lead to significant complications, like acute stroke caused by the embolization of clots or tumor fragments. A thorough cardiac evaluation, including TTE, is essential for identifying the source of emboli in cases where the origin of ischemic stroke remains unclear; this assessment can have critical therapeutic implications. Surgical removal of the myxoma is a safe procedure that frequently results in the complete resolution of symptoms associated with the tumor. Our case underscores the necessity of a comprehensive approach when managing patients presenting with acute ischemic stroke.
