Effect of a structured educational intervention delivered through a mobile application on glycated haemoglobin and self-efficacy in adolescents with type 1 diabetes mellitus: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Huma Rubab, Faisal Aziz, Raisa Gul, Erika S Froelicher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesType 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is a chronic illness affecting children and adolescents worldwide. Mobile health apps (MHAs) are increasingly used to deliver structured educational interventions (SEI) aimed at reducing glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), enhancing self-efficacy and self-management in adolescents with T1DM. This review and meta-analysis assessed the effectiveness of SEI via MHAs on HbA1c and self-efficacy in adolescents with T1DM.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesPubMed, Pak Medinet, Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Wiley Online Library and host database Offshore Vessel Inspection Database (OVID) were searched from 1 January 2013 to 30 December 2023.Eligibility criteriaRandomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies (QES) conducted in adolescents aged 10–19 years with T1DM that evaluated the effectiveness of SEIs delivered through mobile app on HbA1c and self-efficacy were included.Data extraction and synthesisTwo independent reviewers screened the abstracts and articles by assuring standardised methods. Quality of the studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tools for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and QESs. The meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model.ResultsEight studies met the inclusion criteria, with 555 adolescents aged 10–19 years. The study duration ranged from 7 to 48 weeks. Meta-analysis showed no significant difference in HbA1c at 3 months between the MHAs and control groups (Hedge’s g: −0.03, 95% CI: −0.37 to 0.31, p=0.86). Study heterogeneity was moderate (I2=45%) with no publication bias (Begg’s test p>0.99). Self-efficacy outcomes were inconclusive, with only one RCT reporting improvement in the intervention group and two QES studies showing mixed results.ConclusionsSEI via MHAs did not significantly affect HbA1c levels in adolescents with T1DM compared with those in the controls. We recommend conduction of well-designed RCTs with larger sample sizes, standardised measurements and tailored educational components to better determine the effectiveness of SEI via MHAs in adolescents.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024537942.Keywords: Systematic Review, Adolescent, General diabetes, Digital Technology, Mobile Applications

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is a chronic illness affecting children and adolescents worldwide. It is estimated to affect 1.52 million individuals under the age of 20 years worldwide,1 and its incidence has risen from 7.78 per 100 000 population in 1990 to 11.07 per 100 000 in 2019.2 In Pakistan, 7927 individuals aged 20 years or younger have been diagnosed with T1DM1; however, this number is most likely lower than that in European countries because of the underreporting of cases.3

T1DM is a challenging illness to manage because it requires adherence to strict self-management regimes involving frequent glucose monitoring, insulin administration and balancing it with physical activity, diet control, prevention and management of acute complications. Its management becomes more challenging in adolescents (10–19 years), which is a transitional period from childhood to adulthood. During this phase, significant hormonal and physiological changes occur, which can increase insulin resistance and deteriorate glucose metabolism.4 In addition, eating habits and physical activity patterns are transformed as well as socialisation and independence levels increase during this phase, which makes it difficult for adolescents to embrace the responsibility of self-managing their diabetes.5 In addition, research suggests that adolescents lack sufficient knowledge and skills to effectively manage their illness.6 These challenges can result in inadequate glycaemic control as evidenced by higher glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels in adolescents than in children and adults with T1DM.7 Hence, it is imperative to enhance adolescents’ self-efficacy to improve their glycaemic control and prevent diabetes-related complications.8

Evidence shows that structured educational interventions (SEI) can significantly improve healthy lifestyle practices among adolescents with T1DM and enhance their self-efficacy.9 10 Additionally, diabetes-related education has been shown to reduce the frequency of hypoglycaemic episodes and improve glycaemic control.11

An increase in the use of smartphones by adolescents offers an excellent medium for improving health literacy in various health problems, including diabetes. In fact, various mobile health applications (apps) have been developed and are being used to improve health literacy and promote self-management practices of patients with diabetes in various age groups, including adolescents.11,15 Some experimental studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of SEI delivered via mobile health apps in improving self-efficacy and modestly reducing HbA1c in adolescents with T1DM.16,19

Although some individual educational interventional studies have shown the effectiveness of mobile health apps in improving diabetes self-efficacy and glycaemic control, most of the existing reviews have been conducted on adults,20,23 have not stratified their results by children and adolescent populations,24 25 or included people with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes.26 A summary of previous systematic reviews on the effect of mobile health apps on glycaemic outcomes is presented in table 1. Given the escalating global prevalence of T1DM in adolescents, the effectiveness of mobile health educational apps on self-efficacy and glycaemic control, and the lack of synthesised evidence in adolescents with T1DM, a systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of mobile health apps on self-efficacy and glycaemic control in adolescents with T1DM.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were used to report this systematic review.27 28 This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, registration no. CRD42024537942) before conducting the literature search.

Research framework

The participants, intervention, comparison and outcome (PICO) criteria were used as a framework to identify and select studies for this systematic review.29 According to the PICO criteria, the participants were adolescents with T1DM (age: 10–19 years). The intervention was defined as SEI on diabetes, which included basic knowledge of T1DM, physical activity, healthy dietary behaviours, glucose monitoring, insulin administration techniques, monitoring for complications and healthy coping styles delivered through a mobile app. Additionally, mobile apps that allowed the recording of glucose values and carbohydrate consumption and sought feedback on insulin dosage adjustments were included. The control group included individuals who received only routine or usual diabetes care.

The primary outcome was defined as the mean change in HbA1c following the intervention compared with the control group. The secondary outcome was self-efficacy, which was defined as adolescents’ own assessment of their performance on certain behaviours30 and was measured using the Diabetic Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES), a validated and reliable tool for measuring self-efficacy in managing diabetes.31 The DMSES comprises 20 questions that measure responses on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (cannot at all) to 10 (completely capable) to assess self-efficacy.

Data sources

The literature search was conducted using four databases: PubMed, Pak Medinet, Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Wiley Online Library and host databases Offshore Vessel Inspection Database (OVID). The search was limited to studies published in English between 1 January 2013 and 30 December 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Only randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies (QESs) which are defined as intervention studies that provide evidence of interventional effects in a real setting, were included. However, QES do not require random allocation, making them prone to selection bias and confounding.32 Studies conducted on adolescents aged 10–19 years with T1DM were considered in this systematic review. Observational studies, case reports, case studies, qualitative studies and review articles were excluded. Studies targeting healthcare providers and evaluating mobile health apps in hospital settings were excluded from the systematic review. Studies focusing on type 2 diabetes and involving patients with chronic diseases other than type 1 diabetes were excluded. Furthermore, studies on insulin pump therapy were excluded.

Patient and public involvement

As this is a systematic review and meta-analysis, it did not involve the patients directly.

Search strategy

To conduct a comprehensive literature search and identify eligible studies, specific medical subject heading (MeSH) terms were developed according to the PICO framework. A list of the MeSH terms is provided in online supplemental file 1. These MeSH terms were used to extract relevant articles from each database using the recommended search strategy. The initial search, without applying specific inclusion criteria, yielded 26 755 records in PubMed, 0 in Pak Medinet, 229 in CINAHL, 10 in OVID and 2 in Wiley. After applying specific inclusion criteria for language, publication year, age of participants and study design, 232 abstracts were retrieved from PubMed, 229 from CINAHL, 10 from OVID and 2 from Wiley databases. In addition to the selected database, 100 records were manually searched using Google Scholar, of which only one article met the inclusion criteria. Mendeley was used to identify and remove duplicate articles. After a thorough independent screening of abstracts by two researchers (HR and BS), two articles were selected from PubMed, OVID and Wiley each; one from CINAHL; one from Google Scholar; and none from Pak Medinet. Eight studies were included in this systematic review. The details of the search process are presented in the PRISMA-2020 diagram (figure 1).

Data extraction and quality assessment

The components of data extraction were agreed on by all authors, and one author (HR) conducted the data extraction. The study characteristics included the authors, year of publication, country where the study was conducted, study design, SEI duration, sample size, age and sex of the participants. The intervention data included the name of the mobile app, educational content of the app, and whether the apps recorded information on glucose levels, insulin administration and physical activity. Primary (HbA1c) and secondary (self-efficacy) outcome data were presented as mean and SD.

The quality of the studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal checklist (JBI) for RCTs.33 The JBI checklist consists of 11 items that assess RCT quality based on randomisation, concealment of treatment groups, blinding, reliable measurement of outcomes and statistical analysis methods. Responses to each question were categorised as ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’. All elements were reported as low risk, except for concealment of treatment groups (50% high risk), blinding to assignment (25% high risk), blinding (25% high risk) and follow-up (50% high risk). See online supplemental figure 2 for further details.

The JBI 10-item critical appraisal checklist was used for the QESs (Critical Appraisal Tool, 2017). Responses to each item were recorded as ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’. All elements were at low risk except multiple outcome (25% high risk) and follow-up (25% high risk), as illustrated in online supplemental figure 3.

Data synthesis

Information was extracted on the author, country, study design, geographical setting, participant age group, intervention duration, app name, educational content of the app and follow-up duration. In studies comparing more than two interventions, we extracted the control and intervention data using a stand-alone mobile application. We extracted the mean and SD values of the primary and secondary outcomes from the studies. We held regular consensus meetings to discuss and adjudicate any discrepancies. We arbitrated any discrepancies by inviting a third reviewer.

The meta-analysis of the primary outcome absolute HbA1c was conducted in Stata V.18.0 using a random-effects model at 3-month follow-up because it was consistently reported by the RCTs and a minimum of 3 months are needed for HbA1c to provide accurate changes over the past 3 months.34 Only three of the four RCTs were included in the meta-analysis because one study did not assess HbA1c as an outcome. The standardised mean difference and 95% CI in HbA1c at 3 months between the mobile health app and control groups were estimated using the Hedge’s g method. Publication bias was assessed in terms of meta-bias using the funnel plot and the Begg’s test.

Results

Overview of the studies

The studies included in this review were conducted in the following continents: two studies from Asia19 35 and six from Europe1618 36,39 and were published in English between 2013 and 2023. Of the eight studies included in this systematic review, four were RCTs and four were QESs. The sample sizes of the studies ranged from 25 to 150, comprising a pooled sample of 555 adolescents. The participants’ age ranged from 10 to 18 years, and the study duration varied from 7 to 48 weeks. The risk of bias ranged from low in five studies16 18 19 37 38 to high in one study36 (table 2).

Educational content of mobile app

Common educational components of mobile apps included diet, exercise, medication and regular feedback to assist patients in managing their HbA1C and self-efficacy. Three RCTs did not include education on insulin adjustment and dosage calculations.16 36 37 None of the RCTs reported the delivery of educational content related to medications. Furthermore, telephone calls were not used for follow-up of any issues regarding app functioning or reinforcing the app use in the RCTs. These studies reported seven distinct components of various mobile apps. The primary component, blood glucose monitoring, was reported in all studies, except one.36 Telephone calls were used as a follow-up method in three QES.18 19 35 Multimedia messages (educational content delivered through pictorial, audio or video clips) were used as a channel of communication in two QES.18 35 However, reminders and motivational messages (text messages to reinforce adherence to self-care practices and maintain motivation) were reported in all studies except one study,38 and feedback was reported in all studies except one study.19 Further details regarding the components of the apps in the RCT and QES are presented in online supplemental table 3.

The primary outcome, HbA1c was measured in three RCTs,16 37 39 while self-efficacy was only reported in one RCT.36 In two RCTs,16 39 the SEI intervention was conducted for 12 months. Meta-analysis

Figure 2 shows that the standardised pooled mean difference in HbA1c at 3 months between the mobile health app and the control group was −0.03 (95% CI: −0.37 to 0.31, p=0.86), indicating no statistically significant difference between the groups. In addition, heterogeneity between studies was moderate, as indicated by a value of I2=45%. A funnel plot online supplemental figure 5 suggested no evidence of publication bias (Begg’s test, p>0.999). Only three studies were included in the meta-analysis, limiting the statistical power.

Discussion

This review assessed the impact of SEI on HbA1c levels (primary outcome) and self-efficacy (secondary outcome) using mobile health apps. The included studies were conducted in Asia and Europe. The duration of the intervention varied across the studies. However, the DMSES was employed to assess self-efficacy in three studies.35 36 38

The QESs included in this review showed an improvement in HbA1c levels at 3 and 6 months. However, our meta-analysis of RCTs revealed no statistically significant effects of SEIs via mobile apps on absolute HbA1c −0.03 (95% CI: −0.37 to 0.31, p=0.86) at the 3-month follow-up rather than change score. The baseline HbA1c was similar in both the intervention and control groups (ranging from 0% to 0.2%). Hence, the risk of bias due to differences in baseline characteristics was minimal or absent. However, we acknowledge that the utilisation of follow-up is considered a methodological limitation, and for a more robust synthesis, future studies should report the adjusted mean difference. A previous meta-analysis of five studies reported the effect of using mobile apps versus a control group with a pooled effect size of −0.36; 95% CI (−0.81 to 0.81). This null effect could be due to the small sample size and quality of the studies included in the meta-analysis, as the studies were heterogeneous.24 In contrast, another meta-analysis reported that mobile health apps have a small-to-moderate effect on HbA1c levels in patients with T1DM (MD: −0.37; 95% CI: −0.53 to –0.22, p<0.001).21 This result could be attributed to the varying age group, which was a mix of adolescents and adults. A mobile app-based study among individuals aged 14–22 years with type 1 diabetes did not improve HbA1c.40 Furthermore, another meta-analysis reported a mean HbA1c reduction of –0.27% (95% CI: –0.76 to +1.31; p<0.001) at 6 months depicting modest but statistically significant reduction in HbA1c among adolescents at 6 months. This discrepancy could be because of the mode of delivery through mobile health apps, web-based programmes and e-health tools.41 While our SR focused on the mobile apps,

This review highlights the various components of education delivered via mobile apps, including blood glucose, diet, insulin and exercise. Insulin and exercise were reported by all QESs, whereas, only one RCT reported insulin, and none of the RCTs reported exercise. Insulin dosage was adjusted according to diet and exercise; therefore, this variation in educational content could be the reason for the non-significant results of the app on HbA1c. Systematic review findings suggest a moderate level of reduction in HbA1c as well as insulin per day in adolescents.42 Education is essential for diet, that is, carbohydrate counting and exercise to adjust the dosage of insulin in patients with T1DM.43 However, a study emphasised that frequent reminders were important to use educational material.44

The effect of self-efficacy was assessed in only one RCT and two quasi-studies reporting improvement in self-efficacy by one RCT and one quasi-study, whereas one quasi-study did not report a difference.38 Given the scarcity of studies on self-efficacy, we suggest additional experimental studies with adequate sample sizes, standardised measurements and consistent follow-up periods to generate more robust evidence. Moreover, the educational component delivered through mobile health apps should be standardised by including essential educational components, such as insulin dosage, carbohydrate counting and exercise. Along with standardisation, it is essential to ensure usability via user-centred design, which helps promote the acceptance of mobile health apps.15

The level of certainty was assessed using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation.45 For HbA1c outcome evidence, the RCTs suggest low certainty, while evidence from QES suggests very low certainty, due to the risk of bias, inconsistency and imprecision. For self-efficacy outcome evidence, the RCT suggests very low certainty due to a very serious risk of bias and imprecision, while the QESs suggests very low certainty due to evidence of mixed results. The details are presented in online supplemental file 1.

Limitations

Only three RCT studies were included in the meta-analysis. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was not performed due to the small number of studies, as removing any single study could render the pooled estimate unreliable. Very low certainty of evidence was observed due to the small number of available publications and heterogeneity within these articles. Our analysis was confined to papers written in English, and the exclusion of non-experimental studies represents an additional limitation. This could lead to a limited comprehensiveness of the evidence. The variation in sample sizes across studies may have introduced potential bias in the results of the meta-analysis. The meta-analysis used the absolute HbA1c rather than change from baseline score, which could introduce baseline difference bias, although the baseline HbA1c in our studies was similar in the control and intervention groups. Limited data was available on self-efficacy outcome, due to which meta-analysis was not performed. Furthermore, the limited number of studies and small sample size led to limited statistical power to observe meaningful effects especially on self-efficacy outcomes.

Additionally, heterogeneity in intervention components and duration across studies could potentially influence the outcomes. Furthermore, there was a lack of long-term follow-up data in most studies.

Strengths

The reviewers adhered to the rigorous methodology by following PRISMA 2020 guidelines and preregistration of the review protocol. Furthermore, a comprehensive account of the search strategy was provided to delineate the specifics of each software program used.

Additionally, systematic reviews targeting specifically focusing on adolescents with T1DM were conducted. Reviewers used JBI critical appraisal checklist for RCTs and QESs to assess quality and risk of bias.

Conclusions

Mobile health apps offer a promising alternative for SEI in adolescents with T1DM. However, their effectiveness on HbA1c levels and self-efficacy remains unclear. Few studies have evaluated the self-efficacy of app-based SEI. These studies did not demonstrate the significant effectiveness of mobile app interventions in improving HbA1c levels; however, self-efficacy improved. Several limitations might explain the lack of an effect on HbA1c, including small sample sizes, variations in intervention duration and mobile app features, and differences in the measurement methods. Consequently, there is a critical need for conducting high-quality research, that is, RCTs with special consideration towards larger sample sizes and longer follow-up to determine effectiveness. Standardisation of educational components in mobile apps may be beneficial for future interventions. Tailoring of mobile app interventions may be necessary to address the unique needs of adolescents with type 1 diabetes. Furthermore, integration of insulin dosage adjustment, carbohydrate counting and exercise components in mobile apps should be considered in future interventions. While the current evidence does not support the widespread implementation of mobile app interventions for reducing HbA1c in adolescents with type 1 diabetes, this review underscores the importance of continued innovation and evaluation in this field. As technology evolves and our understanding of adolescent diabetes management improves, mobile apps may yet prove to be valuable tools in supporting this vulnerable population.
