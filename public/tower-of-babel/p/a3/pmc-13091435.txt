Artificial intelligence–based prognostic models in acute myeloid leukemia: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoyi Zhang, Na Xiao, Simo Du, Manasa Anipindi, Cho-Hao Lee, Toru Yoshino, Aditya Anand, Lisha Xiao, Abhishek Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•AML AI prognostic models show moderate AUC overall, but performance varies widely, and external validation is often limited.•Genomic-only models do not consistently outperform clinical models; better reporting and prospective validation are needed.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a biologically heterogeneous and aggressive hematologic malignancy associated with poor outcomes. Despite intensive therapy, long-term survival rate (5-year survival) is only 25% to 30% and drops below 10% to 15% in patients aged >60 years.1 Current risk-stratification systems (cytogenetic- and molecular-based stratification) guide treatment decisions,2 for example, identifying high-risk patients for allogeneic transplantation, but these prognostic models remain imperfect.3, 4, 5 Predictive accuracy is limited,6 and some “favorable” patients still relapse, whereas others with adverse markers achieve prolonged remissions.7,8 There is a clear need for improved prognostic tools in AML to better capture disease heterogeneity and to inform personalized therapy and clinical decision-making beyond what conventional classifications offer.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) approaches, including machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), have begun to transform prognostication in oncology, including hematologic malignancies.9 ML algorithms detect complex patterns in multidimensional data sets and thus hold promise for risk modeling in a disease as molecularly diverse as AML. Early applications of ML in AML and related leukemias have demonstrated the ability to integrate clinical, cytogenetic, and genomic features to improve risk stratification.9 For instance, ML-based analyses of genomic and epigenomic data have uncovered novel combinations of mutations and epigenetic markers that stratify patient risk, providing insight into the molecular heterogeneity of AML.10 At the same time, DL techniques have shown remarkable performance in hematologic diagnostics: convolutional neural networks can analyze blood and bone marrow images with high fidelity, enabling automated detection of leukemic blasts.11 A recent meta-analysis reported that AI models achieved high sensitivity in distinguishing AML from normal or other leukemias in peripheral blood smears.11 These advances highlight how AI can augment traditional pathology and genomics, laying the groundwork for AI-assisted prognostic modeling in AML.

An emerging body of literature has explored ML/DL-driven prognostic models in AML. Studies have used supervised ML algorithms (eg, ensemble methods and neural networks) to predict outcomes such as complete remission, relapse risk, and overall survival (OS) based on an array of input variables. Notably, several models have reported performance that rivals or exceeds traditional prognostic scoring systems. For example, in 1 multicenter study of 1383 patients, ML models integrating clinical parameters, cytogenetics, and gene mutations achieved area under the curve (AUC) values of ∼0.77 to 0.86 for predicting remission and ∼0.65 to 0.75 for 2-year survival on external validation, outperforming standard risk categories in discriminating patient outcomes.12 There have also been comprehensive reviews highlighting these breakthroughs and the potential of AI to refine AML prognostication.13 However, despite this progress, major limitations persist across the published models. Many studies are retrospective and single center, and only a minority include independent external validation of their algorithms; 1 review found that only about 25% of ML prognostic studies in leukemia had an external validation cohort.14 Significant methodological heterogeneity further complicates the field: studies use different end points, variable feature sets, and inconsistent performance metrics, making it difficult to compare results or reproduce models.14 Moreover, most models to date leverage a single modality of data (eg, genomic mutations or flow cytometry results alone) rather than truly integrating the multiomics landscape (genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, and clinical data) of AML.15, 16, 17 Although multiomics and graph-based AI approaches are emerging,18 integrative prognostic modeling in AML remains in its infancy. Additional challenges include small sample sizes in many model-training studies, overfitting bias, and a lack of prospective validation for real-world practice.16,19,20

In light of these gaps, this present work undertakes a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively assess AI-driven prognostic models in AML. We aim to aggregate and evaluate the performance of existing ML/DL models for AML outcome prediction, examine their methodological quality and external validity, and identify common trends, challenges, and opportunities for future development. Through this synthesis, we seek to inform how AI can be optimally harnessed to improve risk stratification in AML and guide its translation into clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting. The protocol was drafted a priori and included explicit eligibility criteria, search methods, data extraction plans, and statistical approaches.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception through March 2025. We combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords for acute myeloid leukemia (“acute myeloid leukemia” or AML), AI and ML (“machine learning,” “deep learning,” or “artificial intelligence”), and prognostic performance metrics (“prognosis,” “survival,” “risk stratification,” “area under the curve,” or AUC). Each database’s search syntax was adapted to maximize both sensitivity and specificity.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We applied the following Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS)-informed criteria to identify eligible studies.

Inclusion criteria

POPULATION. Eligible studies included human participants diagnosed with AML, regardless of age group.

INTERVENTION/INDEX TEST. Eligible studies reported the development or validation of an AI-based prognostic model predicting OS or relapse risk (eg, using AUC as a discrimination metric).

COMPARATOR. Comparator was not required, and studies could report single-arm model performance.

OUTCOMES. Eligible studies reported AUC with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), separately for training and/or validation cohorts.

STUDY DESIGN. Original, peer-reviewed research articles published in English with full-text available were included.

Exclusion criteria

ARTICLE LANGUAGE. Studies reported in languages other than English were excluded.

ARTICLE TYPE. Reviews, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, case reports, or any publication lacking primary prognostic data were excluded.

NONHUMAN STUDIES. In vitro or animal experiments without human cohort validation were excluded.

NON-AML POPULATIONS. Studies exclusively on acute lymphoblastic leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, or other hematologic/nonhematologic malignancies were excluded.

NONSURVIVAL END POINTS. Investigations limited to minimal residual disease (MRD), pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling, or biomarker discovery without survival/prognosis correlation were excluded.

INSUFFICIENT REPORTING. Articles lacking clear description of the AI model, training vs validation delineation, or missing AUC and 95 % CI data were excluded.

DUPLICATE COHORTS. When multiple reports used the same patient population, only the most comprehensive or latest publication was retained.

DATA UNAVAILABILITY OR INCONSISTENCY. Identified at full-text review if critical performance data were not retrievable or contradicted across the manuscript.

Application of these criteria reduced 992 initial records (284 from PubMed, 466 from Scopus, and 242 from Web of Science) to 264 after title/abstract screening and deduplication, then to 37 after full-text review, and finally to 24 studies that met all inclusion criteria for systematic review and meta-analysis.

Study selection process

Two independent reviewers (X.Z. and N.X.) screened the titles and abstracts of retrieved articles to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (S.D.).

Quality assessment

The risk of bias and applicability of included prediction model studies were assessed independently by 2 reviewers using PROBAST (2019). PROBAST comprises 20 signaling questions across 4 domains (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis) and supports structured judgments of risk of bias and concerns regarding applicability. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer when needed. Domain-level judgments (low/high/unclear risk of bias) were summarized and visualized (Figure 2).43

Data synthesis and meta-analysis

Clinical interpretation of our analytic approach. Because included studies evaluated heterogeneous AI/ML prognostic models rather than a single common model, we focused on discrimination as a shared performance metric. We extracted the AUC (c-statistic) at fixed time horizons (eg, 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year OS/recurrance-free survival) because it was the most consistently reported measure and enables cross-model benchmarking. We used a random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian-Laird) to estimate an average benchmark AUC while acknowledging that true performance is expected to vary across cohorts, predictors, end points, and modeling strategies. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2; therefore, pooled estimates should be interpreted as a global benchmark rather than a universally transportable performance estimate.44

Clinical interpretation of optimism (overfitting). We approximated optimism by the difference between development and validation AUC (ΔAUC = AUC development ˗ AUC validation). A positive ΔAUC indicates potential overfitting and highlights the importance of external validation in independent cohorts.

We first performed a narrative synthesis to tabulate study characteristics and modeling approaches, summarizing key findings such as model algorithms, input features, and reported AUCs. For quantitative pooling, we used R (v4.3.0) with the meta and metafor packages. AUC was our primary effect size. We applied DerSimonian-Laird random-effects models to estimate pooled AUCs across all cohorts,45 and within subgroups defined by prognostic horizon (1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year survival), feature category (genetic vs nongenetic), and data set type (internal TCGA training vs external validation). Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test and the I2 statistic.46 Publication bias was explored with contour-enhanced funnel plots and Begg rank-correlation test. Influence analyses, including leave-one-out diagnostics, Cook distance, and Duval-Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure, were conducted to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates.

Ethical considerations

This review used only previously published, aggregate data and did not involve human participants directly; therefore, ethical approval was not required. We adhered to best practices in research integrity, maintaining confidentiality of unpublished data and respecting intellectual property rights throughout the review process.

Overall meta-analysis

Across the 137 cohorts from the 24 studies reporting complete AUC data, the pooled fixed-effect estimate of discriminatory performance was AUC of 0.757 (95 % CI, 0.752-0.762). This model assumes a common “true” effect size and weights each study by its inverse variance, yielding a narrow CI around the summary AUC.

When allowing for between-study variability, the random-effects model produced a slightly lower summary AUC of 0.769 (95 % CI, 0.742-0.795). By incorporating both within- and between-study variance, this approach acknowledges that true prognostic accuracy may differ across clinical settings, data modalities, and algorithmic pipelines.

Heterogeneity was extreme (I2 = 95.7%), meaning that most of the variation in reported AUCs reflected real differences across cohorts (eg, patient mix, predictors, outcome definitions, and study design) rather than random sampling error (for completeness, Cochran Q = 1723.9; df = 74; P < .0001; between-study variance, τ2 = 0.0109). Therefore, the pooled AUC should be interpreted as a benchmark of the published literature, not a performance guarantee for any single clinical setting. Treatment and SCT variables were inconsistently reported (supplemental Table 4), limiting therapy- or transplant-specific interpretation. Figure 4 provides a funnel plot across all cohort-level AUC estimates (training + validation) to visually assess potential small-study/publication bias; in this descriptive pooled analysis, the diamond corresponds to AUC of 0.7689 (95% CI, 0.7424-0.7953; I2 = 99.5%), as shown in the Figure 4 legend. Notably, the heterogeneity metrics differ because they are computed from different inputs: I2 of 95.7% reflects the primary random-effects synthesis of study-level AUCs, whereas Figure 4 summarizes a funnel plot data set of cohort-level AUC estimates (training + validation) used for assessing small-study effects; therefore, the I2 values are not expected to be identical.

ONE-YEAR PROGNOSIS. Reported AUCs (n = 24 cohorts) ranged from 0.55 (Zhong et al; Beat-AML cohort) to 0.93 (TCGA-AML training), with a pooled AUC of 0.7480 (95 % CI, 0.7393-0.7567; I2 = 96.3%).34 Training set performance (solid circles) exceeded validation set performance (open squares) by a mean ΔAUC of 0.0810 (P = .0202), indicating mild but significant optimism bias (Figure 3A).

TWO-YEAR PROGNOSIS. Midterm discrimination improved slightly (n = 12 cohorts; pooled AUC, 0.7600; 95 % CI, 0.7471-0.7729; I2 = 95.1 %), with individual AUCs spanning ∼0.58 to ∼0.95. However, the average training-validation gap doubled (ΔAUC, 0.1390; P < .001), reflecting greater overfitting in 2-year models (Figure 3B).

THREE-YEAR PROGNOSIS. At 3 years, the pooled AUC remained 0.7600 (n = 30 cohorts; 95 % CI, 0.7525-0.7675; I2 = 94.8 %), with a ΔAUC of 0.0980 (P < .001; Figure 3C). Although discrimination matched the 2-year horizon on average, optimism bias persisted at a similar magnitude.

FIVE-YEAR PROGNOSIS. Long-term models achieved the highest discrimination (n = 16 cohorts; pooled AUC, 0.8330; 95 % CI, 0.8237-0.8423; I2 = 94.5 %), with AUCs ranging from ∼0.61 to ∼0.98 (Figure 3D). Yet optimism bias remained substantial (ΔAUC, 0.1250; P = .0030), underscoring the risk of overfitting even for robust 5-year predictors.

OTHER FOLLOW-UP INTERVALS. Forty-five cohorts reported prognostic performance without a specified time horizon, yielding a pooled AUC of 0.768 (95 % CI, 0.711-0.826). Additional sparse intervals (30 days [7 cohorts], 6 months [1 cohort], and 4 years [2 cohorts]) were too few for reliable pooling and are summarized qualitatively.

Model discrimination increased progressively from short- (1 year) to long-term follow-up (5 years); however, extreme heterogeneity (I2 > 94 %) and consistent training-validation optimism biases (ΔAUC, 0.081-0.139) persisted across all prognostic horizons. These findings highlight both the promise of extended-horizon risk stratification in AML and the critical need for rigorous external validation and calibration to mitigate overfitting.

Validation subgroup analysis, genetic vs nongenetic feature models

We compared the external discrimination of models built on nongenetic profiles (clinical variables, biomarkers, flow cytometry, or imaging) vs genetic profiles (gene expression signatures, mutation, or network markers). After excluding 2 training-only studies and merging Zhong et al’s reports into a single entry, 31 nongenetic and 51 genetic validation cohorts remained. The pooled AUC was 0.776 (95% CI, 0.745-0.807; I2 = 94.8%) for nongenetic models and 0.741 (95% CI, 0.716-0.766; I2=93.5%) for genetic models (Figure 3E-F).

Pooled AUCs were estimated using a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model. Nongenetic models achieved a pooled AUC of 0.776 (95 % CI, 0.745-0.807; I2 = 94.8 %), whereas genetic models achieved 0.741 (95 % CI, 0.716-0.766; I2 = 93.5 %). The difference in discrimination (ΔAUC = 0.035) did not reach statistical significance (P = .085).

Despite a numerical advantage for nongenetic features, both subgroups exhibited extreme heterogeneity (I2 > 90 %). These findings indicate that clinical and assay-derived variables may offer slightly better external prognostic power than gene-based signatures, although no statistical significance is found. The high between-study variability underscores the need for standardized, multicohort external validation and calibration before clinical application.

Training vs validation performance

To quantify the extent of overfitting, we compared discrimination in model development (training) vs independent (validation) cohorts. A total of 53 training set AUCs and 73 validation set AUCs (those with reported 95 % CI) were meta-analyzed using DerSimonian-Laird random-effects models.

In the training cohorts, the pooled AUC was 0.801 (95 % CI, 0.792-0.810; τ2 = 0.00042; I2 = 98.4 %), reflecting excellent apparent discrimination when models are both developed and internally evaluated on the same data set. Individual training set AUCs ranged from 0.61 to 1.00, with a median of 0.80.

By contrast, in the validation cohorts, the pooled AUC dropped to 0.749 (95 % CI, 0.736-0.762; τ2 = 0.00095; I2 = 96.2 %), indicating consistently good but lower discrimination in independent samples. Validation set AUCs spanned from 0.50 to 0.95 (median, 0.75).

Optimism bias, defined as the difference between training and validation pooled AUCs, was 0.052 (95% CI, 0.041-0.063; P < .001). This statistically significant gap of ∼5% AUC points demonstrates that models systematically overestimate their performance on development data.

Although both training and validation pooled AUCs remain in the “good” range (>0.70), the persistent optimism bias and extreme heterogeneity (I2 > 95 %) indicate that development performance should not be treated as a clinically reliable estimate. For clinical translation, models should be judged primarily on geographically and temporally independent external validation, accompanied by calibration and, when possible, clinical utility reporting, before any bedside deployment.

Begg test

Because potential publication bias is most consequential for external performance, we assessed funnel plot asymmetry among validation cohorts using Begg and Mazumdar rank-correlation test. Kendall τ was ˗0.092 (P = .14), suggesting no obvious asymmetry on formal testing among validation estimates. As with all funnel plot tests, lack of statistical significance does not exclude bias.

Leave-one-out sensitivity

Sequentially omitting each of the 73 validation studies with reported CI produced pooled AUCs ranging narrowly from 0.760 to 0.777, with between-study heterogeneity (I2) remaining ≥94 % throughout. This demonstrates that no single cohort unduly influences the overall estimate.

Trim-and-fill adjustment

A Duval-Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure imputed 5 potential right-side studies, modestly increasing the pooled AUC to 0.781, a change of +0.012 that does not alter substantive conclusions.

Interpretation

The funnel plot’s visual symmetry, nonsignificant Begg test, and robust sensitivity analyses together indicate an absence of major publication bias. Despite extreme between-study heterogeneity, the validation set pooled AUC (∼0.77) is stable, reinforcing that the observed optimism bias (∼0.05 AUC points) between training and validation reflects genuine overfitting rather than selective reporting. Rigorous, prospective external validation remains essential to confirm the generalizability of AML prognostic models.

Discussion

The current landscape of AI-enabled prognostication in AML is distinguished by a striking breadth of data modalities, cohort scales, and algorithmic philosophies.12,21, 30, 47 Among 24 studies since 2018, two-thirds mined high-density molecular assays (bulk RNA sequencing or targeted panels), one quarter relied on clinical or routine laboratory variables, and a small remainder fused both streams in a single architecture. That pluralism extends to sample size: 7 studies enrolled ≤100 patients, whereas 2 leveraged national insurance or registry data with >5000 patients, with the remainder distributed across 4 intermediate strata, which implies substantial differences in statistical precision (and likely overfitting risk) and in external generalizability across studies.

Algorithmic choices reflect this heterogeneity: classical regression families (Cox, LASSO-Cox, and logistic12,48,49; n = 16), tree-based methods12,23 (n = 14), Ensemble/stacking frameworks (n = 6),50 and neural networks, support-vector machines, consensus clustering, and other niche methods (n = 3-4).16,21 Despite such methodological variety, the random-effects pooled AUC was 0.769 (95 % CI, 0.742-0.795; I2 ≈ 96 %). Notably, the funnel plot in Figure 4 summarizes a broader set of cohort-level AUCs (training + validation) and therefore reports slightly different pooled/heterogeneity values in the figure itself. Thus, “good” discrimination varies more with population mix, data richness, and end-to-end pipeline design than by chance. Given the extreme between-study heterogeneity, future quantitative syntheses should routinely report 95% prediction intervals from random-effects models to convey the expected range of model discrimination in a new clinical setting, rather than relying on the pooled mean alone.51 Prediction intervals are typically wider than CIs because they incorporate between-study heterogeneity and therefore reflect setting-to-setting variation in expected performance.51, 52, 53 Practically, this means that a model with “good” pooled discrimination may still perform materially differently in a given institution, underscoring the need for robust external validation in clinically relevant cohorts before clinical adoption.51, 52, 53

Partitioning cohorts by feature category softens but does not erase this complexity. Models built exclusively on nongenetic profiles retained a modest numerical edge over gene-centric signatures (pooled AUC, 0.776 vs 0.741; ΔAUC = 0.035; P = .085); however, their confidence bounds overlap and heterogeneity remains extreme, implying that either modality can succeed or fail. A horizon-stratified meta-analysis adds nuance: discrimination rose from a pooled AUC of 0.748 at 1 year to 0.833 at 5 years, but the optimism gap between training and external validation widened in parallel, reaching 0.125 for 5-year models, underscoring overfitting even in ostensibly “mature” long-term predictors. Across all horizons, the training-validation Δ of ∼0.05 AUC points remained significant (P < .001). Clinically, this gap implies that apparently “high-performing” models in development data sets may materially underperform when applied to new patients at different centers, reinforcing external validation as a prerequisite for clinical use.

Individual exemplars clarify how data architecture and methodological discipline interact. At one end, Didi et al distilled 52 bedside and cytogenetic variables from a 3030-patient induction chemotherapy cohort into a boosted-tree model whose validation AUC plateaued in the mid-0.6 second, emphasizing the constraining influence of center-specific practice patterns and coarse missing data encoding.21 At the opposite extreme, Bao et al fused 4 public RNA sequencing cohorts, executed a 101-model ensemble search, and reported near-excellent 5-year discrimination in development (AUC, 0.855), only to lose ∼0.15 to 0.20 AUC when ported to an independent validation set, an object lesson in batch-effect fragility despite sophisticated normalization.24 Imaging work remains exploratory: a U-Net that segmented Wright-Giemsa smears achieved a credible AUC of 0.71 on a 40-patient relapse cohort; however, its authors acknowledged reproducibility constraints tied to staining protocol and scanner brand.22 Similarly, a flow cytometric immune-reconstitution model reached an AUC of 0.902 in leave-one-out training but dipped to 0.845 under an orthodox random-forest external test, illustrating how aggressive oversampling can erode real-world robustness.29

Two thematic lessons emerge from this interwoven evidence base. First, disciplined preprocessing routinely outweighed algorithmic novelty: rigorous batch correction, biologically informed feature pruning, and transparent censoring rules dwarfed the marginal gains obtained by swapping XGBoost for a neural network.54, 55, 56 Second, multimodality is promising but not a panacea.13,57 Studies that naïvely concatenated multiomics layers often flaunted attractive internal metrics yet faltered externally; by contrast, frameworks that hierarchically integrated a parsimonious gene or protein signature atop solid clinical scaffolding tended to preserve discrimination across centers and sequencing platforms.58, 59, 60

From a clinical vantage, the incremental lift in long-horizon accuracy is far from academic. Better 5-year stratification informs consolidation intensity, transplant timing, and MRD-guided maintenance, particularly as new targeted agents extend postremission survival.16,19,30,61,62 Nevertheless, bedside translation lags behind algorithmic ingenuity. Barriers include proprietary data formats, uncertain regulatory pathways, and clinician skepticism toward opaque “black box” outputs.13,57,63 Thoughtful explainability, via SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) plots or biologically plausible feature rankings, can mitigate distrust, but such techniques remain underreported in our corpus.64,65

These gaps in study design and reporting limit clinical uptake and impede fair cross-study comparisons.

Treatment-related considerations

Prognostic performance is likely regimen dependent because baseline risk, response kinetics, and competing risks differ across treatment contexts (eg, HMA-based therapy vs intensive induction). However, treatment exposure was inconsistently reported, and regimen-specific denominators were often not extractable (supplemental Table 4), precluding robust regimen-stratified synthesis and limiting inference about transportability across contemporary regimens such as HMA/VEN vs intensive chemotherapy. This reporting gap remains a practical barrier to clinical translation. In addition, SCT can act as a postbaseline intervention and competing-risk modifier; inconsistent reporting of transplant rates and analytic handling (eg, censoring at SCT) further complicates cross-study comparability and clinical interpretation of model performance. For example, several included cohorts were predominantly treated with intensive induction (eg, cytarabine/anthracycline-based regimens), whereas others included higher proportions of lower-intensity hypomethylating-agent–based therapy; such differences in regimen mix plausibly contribute to the observed dispersion in discrimination across studies.

To improve clinical translation, future AML prognostic AI studies should be designed and reported in a way that enables independent appraisal and replication. The Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis plus Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) statement provides updated reporting guidance for prediction model studies using regression or ML methods, and PROBAST+AI provides an updated framework to appraise risk of bias and applicability for such models. Adoption of these standards, together with external validation, calibration, and clinical utility reporting, will facilitate fairer cross-study comparisons and accelerate clinical translation.66,67 In particular, TRIPOD+AI-aligned reporting and PROBAST+AI-aligned appraisal can improve reproducibility and make external validation evidence easier to interpret and compare across studies. In this review, risk of bias was assessed using the original PROBAST (2019) framework (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis), consistent with our domain-based summary in Figure 2.43

Looking forward, three priorities could catalyze genuine clinical utility. First, rigorous external validation across geographically and temporally distinct cohorts, together with reporting of calibration and clinical utility, should become routine to establish transportability. Second, federated learning will allow for geographically diverse hospitals to cotrain joint models without exchanging raw data, protecting privacy while buffering demographic drift.68, 69, 70 Third, Prospective, platform-embedded trials, in which predictions are generated in real time and linked to therapeutic decisions, are essential to prove that a nominal 0.04-point AUC gain translates into fewer relapses or smarter trial stratification.71, 72, 73 Finally, harmonized data standards across genomic laboratories and electronic health record ontologies will lower the engineering threshold for real-time deployment and enable continuous calibration monitoring.69,74, 75, 76

In sum, our meta-analysis confirms that AI-powered prognostic tools for AML have moved beyond theoretical promise: they already deliver “good” to “excellent” discrimination under controlled conditions. Realizing their translational potential, however, will require the community to invest as much energy in transparent validation, cross-institutional data governance, and user-centered implementation as it has in algorithmic innovation.13,57,77

Clinical implications

For clinicians, the current literature suggests that AI-based AML prognostic models can achieve moderate discrimination on validation, but the evidence base remains insufficient for routine replacement of established risk systems. The most clinically relevant limitations are the predominance of retrospective single-center development, inconsistent end point definitions, and limited external validation, which together reduce transportability across real-world settings. In addition, treatment and transplant variables are often incompletely reported, limiting assessment of regimen-robust performance in contemporary practice. Future studies should prioritize rigorous external validation and report not only discrimination but also calibration and clinical utility, following TRIPOD+AI reporting and PROBAST+AI appraisal frameworks.66,67

Conclusion

AI-assisted prognostic models in AML demonstrate good discrimination (pooled AUC, ∼0.77) across diverse clinical, genomic, and imaging inputs, with higher apparent accuracy over longer follow-up horizons; however, extreme heterogeneity and measurable optimism bias limit direct clinical generalization. Federated learning consortia and standardized electronic health record integrations can expand model generalizability while protecting patient privacy, and adoption of transparent explainability frameworks (eg, SHAP or Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) will be critical to foster clinician trust. Ultimately, embedding these AI tools into multicenter trials and decision-support platforms is essential to translate statistical gains into improved therapeutic decisions, optimizing transplant timing, consolidation intensity, and maintenance strategies. With rigorous validation and pragmatic deployment, AI-driven prognostic modeling can move from proof of concept to clinically actionable precision AML care with potential to improve patient outcomes.
