An Update and Overview of the Ocular and Extraocular Microbiome and Its Impact on Ophthalmic Care
Authors: Konstantinos Benekos, Andreas Katsanos, Panagiotis Laspas, Georgios D. Panos, Iordanis Vagiakis, Fotios S. Fousekis, Robert Luca, Beryl Zhou, Charilaos Kostoulas, Ioannis Georgiou, Konstantinos H. Katsanos, Dimitra Skondra, Anastasios G. Konstas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The microbiome has been described as the last human “organ” and is currently the topic of great research interest worldwide. The application of culture-independent methods, like 16S ribosomal next-generation sequencing, has offered researchers the opportunity to identify bacterial populations that were impossible to detect previously using conventional culture methods. Further standardization of these new approaches to characterizing the microbiome is desirable. The present review discusses the mounting evidence suggesting that alterations in the microbiome and microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids in the gut, mouth, and ocular surface, may play a key role in the pathogenesis of ocular pathologies such as ocular surface disease, glaucoma, uveitis, age-related macular degeneration, and diabetic retinopathy. Clarifying the probable role of the microbiome in ocular diseases would not only offer valuable insights into pathogenesis but could also enable the development of novel therapeutic approaches. As yet, microbial-based therapeutic applications in ophthalmology are limited. Nevertheless, recently emerging strategies utilizing probiotics and prebiotics, or even fecal transplantation to regulate microbiome composition, offer promising research avenues for developing future innovative therapies for ocular diseases. Further studies employing standardized methodological protocols are needed to ensure the reproducibility of results and to eventually unlock the precise links between the microbiome and the eye.

Introduction

The human organism comprises a complex structure that hosts trillions of different microorganisms in a unique combination. One of the most significant achievements in the field of medicine in the twenty-first century has been the growing understanding of human microbiota, which comprise the bacteria, eukaryotes, and viruses that reside within and outside the human body [1]. The advent of multi-omics and molecular technologies such as next-generation sequencing (NGS) and bioinformatics has proven a crucial contribution, offering the opportunity not only to detect these microbes more efficiently than the standard culture-dependent approaches but also to understand their role and their associated metabolic pathways.

To date, the vast majority of studies examining the microbial composition in the human body in relation to ophthalmic health have focused on the gastrointestinal microbiome. Such studies have suggested that imbalances in the gut microbiome are linked to various retinal diseases like age-related macular degeneration [2, 3] and diabetic retinopathy [4], as well as to other ocular disorders such as primary open-angle glaucoma, dry eye disease, and uveitis [5]. Cumulative evidence suggests that several microorganisms can maintain or alter the immune and metabolic equilibrium in multiple organs. More recently, the microbial constitution at other body sites, such as the eyes, has become a topic of considerable interest. Although until recently the intraocular environment was thought to be sterile, there is now evidence to the contrary [6]. In addition, there is also a well-established commensal microbial population on the ocular surface [7].

Microorganisms can influence and modulate several key host processes, including nutrient metabolism, the immune system, and defense against pathogens [8]. Alterations in the microbial composition have been associated with various diseases [9]; however, these correlations do not automatically imply causal relationships. The aim of this review is to explore and discuss critically existing evidence regarding the role of both ocular and extraocular microbial populations in ophthalmic health and disease.

Methods

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed between August and December 2025, across PubMed, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library, using combinations of free text keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms including “microbiome”, “gut microbiome”, “ophthalmic microbiome”, “ocular surface microbiome”, “microbiome sequencing”, “16S rRNA sequencing”, “gut-retina axis”, “glaucoma”, “macular degeneration”, "uveitis”, “diabetic retinopathy”. Different combinations of these terms were used to ensure all relevant studies have been captured. The search strategy focused on studies published until December 2025, which were included in the present review. Moreover, we included studies in any language if an English, French or German abstract or translation was obtainable.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible studies were those that evaluated the relation of the microbiome with ocular diseases in humans or animal models. In addition, we included published studies that evaluated the methodological approaches, fundamental principles, and definitions in microbiome research. Studies without accessible full-text manuscripts or abstracts were excluded from our analyses.

Analyzing the Microbiome

Basic Definitions

The term microbiome refers to the microbes and their genetic material that are associated with humans [1]. It is important to differentiate this term from microbiota, which specifically refers to the collection of microorganisms present in a particular environment; despite this, these two terms are frequently used interchangeably. Although the term microbiome encompasses bacteria, eukaryotes, and viruses that reside inside and outside the human body, it is mainly used to refer to bacteria.

Taxonomy

Linnaeus was the first to introduce the fundamental principles of modern biological taxonomy, the systematic arrangement of plants, animals, and microorganisms into hierarchical classes, along with nomenclature rules for naming these taxonomic groups (Fig. 1) [10]. Ever since, these principles have remained the cornerstone of biological classification. Overview of Sequencing Technologies (16S rRNA Sequencing)

Two types of methods can be used in microbiome studies for identifying microorganisms. Culture-dependent methods were the first used and have been the backbone of microbiology studies ever since the development of the first solid culture medium by Robert Koch [18]; however, nowadays, these are now regarded as outdated when it comes to characterizing the human microbiota. In 1982, Xu et al. revealed the existence of microorganisms that could not be cultured but were viable [19]. Three years later, Colwell introduced the term “viable but not culturable” (VBNC) to describe these organisms [20], and several factors have been identified as promoters of VBNC cell formations. A range of environmental factors, such as lack of basic nutrients, extreme temperatures, limited oxygen, high salinity, fluctuations in pH, exposure to UV radiation, ultrasound, osmotic pressure, and heavy metal presence, have been proposed as the main culprits responsible for this inability to culture certain microorganisms.

Studies based on culturing methods may fail to fully reveal the true diversity of organisms in an ecosystem. In order to overcome this obstacle, new culture-independent methods have been developed, aiming to improve the detection of difficult-to-culture and VBNC cells. The next-generation sequencing method (NGS) targeting bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) has been widely accepted and used in microbiology research in recent decades. As the name suggests, this gene encodes the 16S ribosomal RNA, a vital component found in the 30S subunit of prokaryotic ribosomes. Its length is estimated to be around 1500 base pairs. Remarkably, it contains nine hypervariable regions (V1–V9) positioned between highly conserved regions that are quite similar across all bacterial species. This high degree of preservation in these regions is a consequence of the crucial role that 16S rRNA plays as a fundamental building block in prokaryotic cells. In contrast, the variable regions exhibit significant sequence diversity among different bacterial types, making them a valuable tool for prokaryotic identification. Using 16S rRNA NGS, it becomes feasible to differentiate between bacteria at the taxonomic genus level, but the current capacity of this method falls short of achieving such differentiation at the species level [21].

Challenges and Limitations in Microbiome Studies

Although there is a lot of enthusiasm regarding the new NGS methods that allow more accurate characterization of the human microbiome, these techniques come with some limitations [22]. The main challenge arises from the application of different sampling, data extraction, bioinformatics, and statistical analysis methods across various studies, which hinders the direct comparison of their results [23]. In fact, it has been shown that each bioinformatic and statistical approach generates unique microbiome profiles, which may render direct comparisons challenging [24].

Another limitation in microbiome research is the biological heterogeneity and inter-individual variability. What constitutes a ‘healthy microbiome’ remains unclear because host genetics, along with age, gender, diet, and geographical location, can lead to wide variations in its composition [25]. Thus, the concept of a core and variable microbiome has been suggested [26]. More specifically, it has been proposed that certain microbial species could be present constantly in a specific habitat, while others might be transient or variable, and their presence might be influenced by factors such as the surrounding environment, lifestyle, and physiological variations. Further understanding of the composition and function of both core and variable microbiomes could offer new insights into the pathophysiology of diseases whose etiology is still unclear.

The Extraocular Microbiome and The Gut–Eye Axis

The Gut Microbiota

The human intestine is a nutrient-rich environment that hosts up to 1014 microbes [35]. Microorganisms are unevenly spread throughout the digestive system, and the colon contains the greatest variety and quantity of them due to the sluggish fluid flow and the pH level that tends to be neutral or mildly acidic in the large intestine. Progressing along the gastrointestinal tract, anerobic bacteria increasingly dominate the population of those microorganisms [36].

In the large intestine, the predominant phyla are Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, accounting for approximately 65% and 23% of organisms, respectively [37]. Within these phyla, the Clostridium genus makes up about 95% of the Firmicute populations, while major genera such as Bacteroides and Prevotella make up the majority of the Bacteroidetes populations [25].

The Gut–Eye Axis

The purported existence of a gut–eye axis, in which gut bacteria and their products might impact immunity in distant areas, such as the eye, may be of particular interest and clinical significance [4, 38]. Nevertheless, such findings must be interpreted with caution. Given that most available evidence derives from animal models, small cohort microbiome profiling studies, or indirect systemic correlations, the specific role of the microbiome in retinal or optic nerve pathology remains unproven.

The Role of Animal Studies in the Gut Eye Axis

Except for observational studies in humans [39], animal models, and particularly mouse models, offered researchers new insights into how the dysbiosis of the gut microbiome contributes to ocular diseases, proposing associations and possible mechanisms of the gut–retinal axis [40]. Non-human models, such as germ-free and human microbiota-associated mice, offer a more controllable and reproducible environment to study possible metabolic or immunological pathways that connect the gut microbiome and ocular health [41]. However, to ensure that these models accurately reflect human conditions, strict and standard methodological procedures should be adopted [42]. By refining these protocols, researchers can more accurately model the complex interactions of the gut–eye axis, ultimately paving the way for reliable, targeted microbial therapies in human ocular conditions.

Mechanisms of the Gut–Eye Axis

Emerging evidence indicates that the gastrointestinal microbiome plays an important regulatory role in retinal immune homeostasis, complement activity, and angiogenic signaling [43]. Disruptions in microbial composition and function have been implicated in age-related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma, uveitis, and other retinal disorders. Rather than acting solely through local mechanisms, dysbiosis promotes systemic low-grade inflammation, increases retinal susceptibility to oxidative stress, and alters the concentration of microbiome-derived metabolites [40, 44, 45].

A healthy gut supports intestinal barrier integrity and limits translocation of pro-inflammatory bacterial products into systemic circulation [46]. Aging, western-style diets, and chronic inflammatory states weaken epithelial tight junctions, resulting in increased intestinal permeability and enabling the dissemination of bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS). These circulating pathogen-associated molecular patterns have been shown to activate innate immune pathways through toll-like receptor signaling and downstream nuclear factor kappa-B pathways, driving the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and establishing a state of metabolic endotoxemia [47].

In the context of retinal disease, it has been proposed that circulating LPS and cytokines contribute to microglial priming and macrophage activation within the highly vascularized choroid [48, 49]. Experimental studies in mice demonstrate that the elevated systemic LPS exacerbates choroidal neovascularization (CNV) via enhancement of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) mRNA expression [50, 51]. Consistent with these findings, microbiome profiling studies in patients with AMD have identified enrichment of LPS-producing taxa, including Negativicutes and Escherichia–Shigella.

Diet-induced dysbiosis is thought to further amplify these potentially adverse processes. High-fat and high-glycemic diets promote the expansion of Gram-negative and pro-inflammatory taxa, including members of the Clostridia class, Proteobacteria, and Negativicutes [44, 52, 53]. These shifts correlate with elevated circulating inflammatory mediators and worsened retinal pathology in animal models. Fecal transplantation studies demonstrate that transfer of microbiota from aged mice into young recipients accelerated microglial infiltration and complement activation, whereas transplantation from young donors to aged mice partially reversed age-associated inflammatory changes [54]. In laser-induced CNV models, germ-free mice lacking microbiota develop significantly smaller lesions and exhibit reduced microglial infiltration compared with normally colonized controls, providing direct evidence that gut microbes modulate angiogenic susceptibility and innate immune activation [55]. These data support a model in which dysbiosis primes a systemic inflammatory state that lowers the threshold for retinal immune activation.

Beyond immune activation, microbial-derived metabolites represent a major hypothesized mechanistic link between the gut and the eye [56]. Certain short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) exert broad immunoregulatory effects by promoting regulatory T cell differentiation, suppressing Th1 and Th17 polarization, and limiting effector T cell trafficking from the intestine [57–59]. In advanced AMD, reductions in SCFA-producing taxa and lower circulating SCFA levels suggest that the loss of this protective anti-inflammatory signaling may potentially contribute to disease progression [60]. Bile acids (BAs) represent a second major class of metabolites that are first synthesized in the liver and then converted to secondary BAs by gut microbes. Tauroursodeoxycholic acid and ursodeoxycholic acid have demonstrated protective effects in multiple retinal degeneration models, including preservation of photoreceptors, attenuation of oxidative stress, and inhibition of pathological angiogenesis [61, 62]. In addition, local ocular bile acid synthesis via CYP27A1 and CYP46A1 contributes to cholesterol clearance and retinal homeostasis [63]. Disruption of these pathways in animal models produces AMD-like phenotypes, further implicating bile acid metabolism as a regulator of retinal integrity and lipid handling [64].

Complement dysregulation and activated microglia further sustain this inflammatory milieu through the secretion of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and VEGF [50, 65, 66]. Increased systemic bacterial antigens are proposed to sequester complement factor H, a key inhibitor of excessive complement activation, away from retinal tissues [67, 68]. This promotes local complement over-activation and deposition of C3 fragments in the subretinal space [69–71]. Together, these processes establish a feed-forward loop in which gut-derived immune priming enhances retinal susceptibility to local stressors and accelerates progression from early degeneration to advanced ocular pathologies.

Ocular Surface Microbiome

Definition and Composition

The term ocular surface microbiome (OSM) describes the group of microorganisms, mainly bacteria, that colonize the surface of the eye. In contrast to the gastrointestinal tract lumen, only a small number of microbes colonize the healthy conjunctiva, the margins of the eyelids, and the tear lake [72]. Originating from the skin, these bacteria inhabit the surface of the eye immediately after birth, and it has been proposed that this community remains relatively stable throughout a person’s life unless it is disturbed by factors like antibiotic treatment, surgery, infections, or use of contact lenses [73]. However, whether a core microbiome resides on the ocular surface really exists has yet to be determined.

Functions of the Ocular Microbiome

The role of the ocular microbiome is understood to maintain and support ocular surface health and homeostasis. The host immune system, in cooperation with the commensal ocular surface microflora, acts synergistically against external harmful pathogens [79]. More specifically, a study performed on a mouse model demonstrated that Corynebacterium mastitidis, which belongs in the Actinobacteria phylum (one of the dominant ocular surface phyla), can elicit a commensal-specific interleukin-17 response from γδ T cells. This immune response eventually leads to the recruitment and activation of neutrophils, which secrete antimicrobials into the tears effective against pathogens such as Candida albicans and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [80].

Emerging Links Between Microbiome and Ocular Diseases

Glaucoma

Glaucoma, often described as the “silent thief of sight”, is one of the primary causes of permanent vision loss worldwide [81, 82]. It is defined as an optic neuropathy characterized by the loss of retinal ganglion cells and their axons, leading to cupping of the optic disc and permanent vision loss. Several known risk factors that have been extensively described are age, family history, exfoliative syndrome, and raised intraocular pressure (IOP) [83–86]. Because the latter is the only modifiable risk factor of the disease, IOP reduction is currently the main goal of pharmacological or surgical treatment for glaucoma [87]. Nonetheless, the true causative mechanism of this disorder has yet to be clarified. Similar to other neurodegenerative disorders, local inflammation has been identified in the optic nerve of patients with glaucoma [88–93]. It has also been argued that open-angle glaucoma may potentially have an autoimmune basis [94, 95].

Numerous scientific reports have highlighted the crucial role of the microbiota and microbiome in human health, illness, and body homeostasis, while a recent murine study supports the view that the microbiome may influence the inflammation and neurodegeneration process that takes place in patients with glaucoma [96]. It should be stressed, however, that this process remains to be proven in humans.

Changes in the Gut Microbiome in Glaucoma

Various criteria can be used to define gut microbial dysregulation, including metrics like the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (F/B) phyla ratio or indices that describe the diversity of an ecosystem. In their animal study, Zhang et al. [97] used rats that were divided into glaucomatous and control groups, and found that the Shannon index was significantly decreased within the glaucomatous group. This suggests that the microbial communities in rats with glaucoma were less varied compared to the control group. In the same study, at the phylum level, the glaucoma group had a significantly higher F/B ratio than the control. Gut microbiome alterations have also been found in patients with open-angle glaucoma [98, 99]. Gram-Positive Versus Gram-Negative Microbiota

There is emerging evidence that the presence of Gram-positive bacteria in the gut, particularly Bifidobacterium, may play a protective role in the survival of retinal ganglion cells and the regeneration of the optic nerve in a murine model [100]. In a preclinical animal model, the alterations in intestinal flora caused by Bifidobacterium have impacted the control of retinal microglia and the activation of Müller cells. This, in turn, is associated with changes in inflammatory cytokines and neurotrophic factors that are secreted in the retina [100].

The Oral Microbiome and Glaucoma

It has been suggested that the dysbiosis of the oral microbiome may be associated with several neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease, as it can promote systemic inflammation, which in turn can influence the progression of these diseases [101]. With regard to glaucoma, Astafurov et al. [102] were among the first who showed that, in patients with glaucoma, the oral microbiome is different compared to healthy controls. More precisely, measuring the amount of 16S rRNA in mouthwash specimens, they suggested that the bacterial load in samples from patients with glaucoma was greater than in those of healthy individuals. However, this study had major limitations as the control group differed significantly from the glaucoma group regarding age, gender, and diabetes status, raising the possibility that the observed difference in the buccal microbiome may be due to these confounding factors. This was not the case in a more recent case–control study [103], in which the variations between the two groups were less prominent. In this study, the levels of Streptococci were elevated in patients with glaucoma, while the number of teeth, serving as an indicator of oral health, along with the fluctuations in oral bacteria levels, could be linked to glaucoma. In a similar manner, Yoon et al. [104] suggested that the buccal microbiome of the elderly with glaucoma differs significantly compared to those without glaucoma, revealing a substantial reduction in the presence of Lactococcus in the oral microbiome of the patients with glaucoma but with enrichment of Faecalibacterium.

Another study [105] has highlighted the association between periodontitis and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), showing that there is an increased risk of glaucoma among individuals with periodontitis than in those without. The exact mechanism that bridges these two clinical entities is not yet fully understood; however, it has been speculated that the oral microbiome linked to periodontitis could potentially trigger immune reactions and contribute to the neurodegeneration associated with glaucoma. It has been suggested that nitric oxide (NO) production triggered by these microbes might be the culprit of this neurodegeneration [105].

Last, but not least, it has been shown that long-term glaucoma medication usage can change the buccal microbiome by itself. More specifically, the oral microbiome of the patients who used topical prostaglandin analogs exhibited notable enrichments of specific taxa, such as Rikenella and Stenotrophomonas [106].

The Role of Short-Chain Fatty Acids

Within the anaerobic conditions of the large intestine, undigested carbohydrates are predominantly fermented by microbes, producing SCFAs that can be used topically as energy sources by the host or can be absorbed systemically [107]. Interestingly, it has been suggested that these molecules, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, can act as a “double-edged sword”, exerting either inflammatory [98], anti-inflammatory, or even neuroprotective effects [108].

It has been shown that several SCFAs in both blood and fecal samples are increased in patients with glaucoma [98]. More specifically, the analysis of serum samples from patients with glaucoma has revealed increased levels of propionate, isovalerate, and caproate, while, in their fecal samples, acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, valerate, isovalerate, and caproate were significantly enriched [98]. In a mouse animal model [98], the supplementation with a SCFA cocktail containing acetate, propionate, and butyrate, along with the induction of acute ocular hypertension up to 45 mmHg, had a significant effect on retinal microglia. The administration of SCFAs combined with high intraocular pressure was associated with the over-expression of inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in the retina and the activation of microglial cells. However, this was not the case in mice that were treated with antibiotics instead of SCFAs. In the latter, exactly the opposite was observed, as the expression of M1 microglial cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, was reduced, whereas M2 microglial cytokines, IL-10, increased. Notably, M1 and M2 microglia and their associated cytokines exert opposite effects in the central nervous system. M1 microglia activation and their cytokine release lead to neurological damage, whereas M2 activation and the release of their anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, promote neuron survival [109]. However, the above observations are in contrast with those of Vergroesen et al. [110], who reported a decrease in butyrate-producing gut bacteria in patients with glaucoma, suggesting a potential link between gut dysbiosis and lower butyrate levels in glaucoma.

On the grounds of these observations, it seems that the microbiome may play a major role in SCFAs synthesis and could conceivably be involved in the survival of retinal ganglion cells via the modulation of retinal microglia activation and the subsequent neuro-inflammatory response [98, 108, 110]. The disruption of the SCFA balance may be correlated with inflammation and retinal diseases; however, it is not yet clear which SCFAs are protective and whether their up- or downregulation is beneficial in humans.

The Role of Retinal MicroRNAs

The microRNAs (miRNAs) are a group of non-coding small RNAs, approximately 19–23 nucleotides long, which are evolutionarily conserved and involved in the post-transcriptional control of gene expression [111]. Wang et al. have shown that miRNAs play a fundamental role in triggering and activating retinal microglial cells and the subsequent inflammatory response within a rat model, whereby an acute rise of intraocular pressure was induced [112]. A recent study on mice identified that antibiotic treatment could downregulate 26 different types of miRNAs in neuroretinal tissues, including miR-122-5p, whereas 8 others were over-expressed. These miRNAs that were expressed at different levels after antibiotics are associated with pathways such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-Akt signaling, axon guidance, and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling [98]. Regarding the miR-122-5p, its injection in the eye after inducing high intraocular pressure increased the retinal inflammatory reaction due to the over-expression of TNF-α and IL-1β, while the injection of its antagonist, which, in fact, is an anti-miR-122-5p molecule, reduced this inflammatory response. In addition, the treatment of mice with SCFAs instead of antibiotics increased the level of miR-122-5p. Furthermore, the upregulation of miR-122-5p was also observed after the transplantation of fecal material from patients with glaucoma into mice that were previously treated with antibiotics in order to eliminate their own microbiota.

Consequently, these results demonstrate that gut microbiota may be strongly related to the miRNAs of the retina, which in turn play an important role in neuroprotection against high intraocular pressure [98]. The Role of Trimethylamine

A recent pilot study revealed that trimethylamine (TMA), a metabolite generated by gut bacteria, can be detected in the aqueous humor along with betaine and trimethylamine N-oxide, which are components of the same metabolic pathway [114]. It was revealed that TMA levels may be significantly higher in the aqueous humor of those with glaucoma compared to controls. Given the previous studies indicating that TMA might have a toxic effect on smooth muscles [115], it has been proposed that TMA could potentially have an adverse impact on the smooth muscle cells within the trabecular meshwork, thus hindering the outflow of the aqueous humor from the anterior chamber of the eye.

The Role of Helicobacter pylori

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a Gram-negative bacterium characterized by its curved spiral shape that commonly colonizes the gastric mucosa. Although H. pylori infection is one of the most prevalent infections worldwide and can be detected in a substantial portion of the population [116], a recent meta-analysis suggested a decreasing trend in its prevalence [117]. It is associated with a wide spectrum of gastroduodenal diseases such as ulcers, gastritis, gastric cancer, and lymphoma, but also with extra-digestive diseases [118]. From the ophthalmology perspective, Kountouras et al. [119] were among the first who highlighted the correlation between H. pylori infection and open-angle glaucoma (OAG). Several studies have reported a statistically significant link between H. pylori infection and OAG [120–123], but others have reported no significant association [124, 125]. These contradictory data have been analyzed by two meta-analyses [126, 127]. Both of these papers suggest a positive relationship between POAG or normal tension glaucoma and the presence of H. pylori, but not in those with exfoliative glaucoma. Regarding the pathophysiologic mechanism linking H. pylori and glaucoma, it is believed that this infection unsettles the homeostatic balance of gastrointestinal microbiota and may trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines and nitric oxide synthase (NOS), theoretically leading to the production of nitric oxide (NO) and the formation of reactive nitrogen species like peroxynitrite, contributing to cellular damage and eventually to what is known as nitrosative stress [128]. Other mechanisms that have been proposed are the cross-reactivity of H. pylori IgG antibodies with ocular tissues [122] or even the colonization of this microorganism within the eye [120].

The Role of Nitric Oxide

The nature of the balance between overproduction, underproduction, and normal levels of NO is crucial for the eyes, as NO can be both beneficial and detrimental depending on its concentration. Indeed, the list of possible beneficial effects of NO on the eye is long. In the case of glaucoma, it plays a significant role in facilitating the efficient outflow of aqueous humor from the anterior chamber of the eye as it regulates the volume of cells in the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal [129]. Typically, a small amount of NO is produced by two enzymes, namely eNOS and nNOS, which in turn activate guanylyl cyclase to produce cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) [130]. cGMP initiates multiple physiological reactions such as blood vessel dilation, ocular blood flow increase, and intraocular pressure reduction.

On the other hand, insufficient levels of NO due to various factors, such as endothelial damage or genetic deficiencies in eNOS, can cause contraction of blood vessels and resistance in the aqueous humor outflow [131].

In cases when homeostasis is challenged, a third type of NO synthetase called iNOS generates a significant amount of NO. These high levels of NO can be helpful against microbial infections due to the antimicrobial effects of NO as a part of the human innate immune system [132]. However, excessive NO can be transformed into compounds like NO2−, nitrite, peroxynitrite, and free radicals, which can interact with various molecules such as thiols, iron–sulfur centers within enzymes, cytochrome oxidase, and glycolytic enzymes. This cascade may potentially lead to changes in cellular functions, DNA damage, neurotoxic effects, degeneration of the optic nerve, and glaucoma [133]. As previously highlighted, H. pylori initiates an overproduction of NO, and compelling evidence suggests that this NO overproduction can also be induced by other microorganisms, potentially resulting in neurotoxic effects [134].

Microbiome and ocular surface disease

Uveitis

Uveitis is a significant cause of vision impairment and blindness worldwide [146]. Non-infectious uveitis (NIU) is presumed to be immune-mediated. and at least some cases of NIU are indeed associated with very specific systemic autoimmune diseases, such as ankylosing spondylitis [147], inflammatory bowel disease [148], psoriasis [149], sarcoidosis [150], Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease [151], and Behçet’s disease [152]. As a consequence, modulation of the immune system is the therapeutic cornerstone of NIU [153]. Despite the availability of therapeutic agents, complete control of the disease is not always possible. Research efforts have focused on the mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of uveitis in an attempt to gain insight into the pathophysiology of these conditions and ultimately develop novel therapeutic approaches.

Recent evidence demonstrates a link between gut microbiome dysbiosis and uveitis, especially NIU [154]. For instance, bacterial profiles in the gut microbiome of patients with uveitis demonstrated far less diversity than in healthy controls [155]. Furthermore, controls appear to have higher populations of symbiotic bacteria that are known for their anti-inflammatory properties, while patients with uveitis seem to harbor mostly bacteria associated with proinflammatory conditions [156].

Several recent studies propose four possible mechanisms by which the gut microbiome can affect the development and course of NIU. Firstly, through antigenic mimicry [157], T-cells that are originally generated against gut microbial peptides can become autoreactive and attack self-antigens, some of which are presumably located in ocular tissues. A second proposed mechanism is related to the loss of intestinal–immune system homeostasis [158]: imbalances in the microbiome ecosystem may result in maladaptive immune regulation, which can include, on the one hand, an increase of pro-inflammatory Th-17 and IL-17A + T-cells, and, on the other hand, a decrease in anti-inflammatory Treg cell production, ultimately facilitating inflammation. A third possible mechanism is the depletion of microbial metabolites that appear to have a protective role against autoimmunity [159]: SCFAs, which are byproducts of fiber digestion by the gut microbiome, and seem to promote the production of Treg cells, while limiting the migration of Th17 cells. Lastly, the loss of intestinal barrier integrity due to dysbiosis-related inflammation can lead to increased intestinal permeability for harmful microbes and their byproducts [160]. These agents can reach the eye through the bloodstream, promote the migration of immune cells in the eye, and cause uveitis.

Although the establishment of a direct causal link between NIU and gut microbiome dysbiosis remains elusive, its potential implication could be enormous both for diagnosis as well as the therapeutic and dietary approaches that could be pursued in NIU. For example, if microbiome profiles for specific subtypes of uveitis could be characterized, screening would perhaps become possible in order to assess the potential risk of developing uveitis [161]. Regarding potential therapies, interventions that can modify the gut microbiome could positively affect the course of NIU. The ingestion of live microorganisms that may provide health benefits when administered in adequate amounts (i.e., probiotics), the adoption of diets rich in nutrients not fully digested by the human body, but degraded and fermented by intestinal microbes (i.e., prebiotics), antibiotics, dietary modifications such as ketogenic diets or caloric restriction, as well as fecal microbiota transplantation, have all been proposed as therapeutic strategies for the modification of the gut microbiome, in order to control autoimmune diseases in general and NIU in particular [39, 162–164].

Nonetheless, as most of the currently available evidence comes from observational studies or animal models, well-designed controlled research in humans is needed to decipher the interplay between genetic factors, diet, and lifestyle on the role of microbes in NIU.

Age-Related Macular Degeneration

Evidence from experimental models and human studies suggests that the gut microbiome may modulate pathways relevant to AMD [44]. In obese mice, gut microbiome dysregulation increased systemic inflammation and primed the retina, so that choroidal neovascularization became exaggerated, which implies the existence of gut–retina cross-talk potentially influencing angiogenic responses [52]. Human case–control data have reported compositional shifts in AMD, with enrichment of Proteobacteria and depletion of SCFA-associated taxa such as Blautia and Anaerostipes, changes that inevitably alter intestinal metabolic functions [165]. Germ-free and antibiotic-treated mouse studies show that removing or reshaping the gut flora reprograms retinal pigment epithelium/choroid transcriptional networks reduces experimental neovascularization, thus linking microbial status to key pathophysiological pathways in AMD, such as complement, lipids, and angiogenic cascades [55, 60]. Contemporary reviews integrate these findings to frame a gut–retina axis in which diet, microbial metabolites, and immune tone converge on chorioretinal tissues [166].

Several microbial-derived metabolites plausibly connect gut dysbiosis to retinal phenotypes. Tryptophan catabolites (indoles) produced by commensals activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a pathway with barrier-protective and anti-inflammatory effects; profiling in humans and mice shows that these ligands predict AhR activity [167]. In the retina, AhR-active tryptophan metabolites dampen microglial NF-κB/ERK signaling and are neuroprotective in ischemia/reperfusion injury, supporting a metabolite-to-retina mechanism [168]. Conversely, trimethylamine-N-oxide generated from dietary choline/carnitine by gut microbes and hepatic oxidation, has been causally implicated in vascular inflammation and atherogenesis, and can induce trained innate immunity and endothelial activation, processes with potential relevance to choroidal vasculopathy and para-inflammatory states in AMD [169, 170].

Appropriate modification of diet to enhance the microbiome provides a potential upstream lever to diminish AMD risk. Adherence to a Mediterranean-style dietary pattern is associated with a lower incidence of advanced AMD and slower progression in observational cohorts, consistent with anti-inflammatory and lipid-modulating effects that could be microbiome-mediated [171, 172]. Whether targeted modulation of gut communities (e.g., via fiber-rich diets or defined probiotics) can alter AMD trajectories remains unproven, but it is biologically plausible given the immunoregulatory role of SCFAs and the diet–metabolite–host triad observed in many diseases.

Genetic epidemiology provides complementary data. To date, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses have provided mixed results: one study found no robust causal effect of broad microbial taxa on overall AMD risk [173], whereas more recent work using mediation MR has suggested that specific bacterial families may influence stage-specific AMD via circulating metabolites (for example, a valine-mediated pathway linking Oxalobacteraceae to early AMD) [174]. Collectively, these data indicate that the microbiome is unlikely to be a singular driver of AMD, but that it may modulate risk through diet-dependent metabolites, immune training, and lipid/complement signaling. Priorities now include longitudinal phenotyping, interventional trials that read out chorioretinal endpoints, and multi-omic integration to pinpoint causal taxa–metabolite–host axes.

Diabetic Retinopathy

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a common microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM) affecting up to one-third of patients with DM and is one of the leading causes of vision loss in adults [175, 176]. With the increasing prevalence of DM worldwide, it is expected that, by 2045, there will be 160.5 million cases of DR globally [177]. In DR, chronic hyperglycemia induces a low-grade pro-inflammatory environment characterized by activation of pathways such as the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) transcription factor, which results in increased expression of inflammatory cytokines and excessive generation of reactive oxygen species. These pathological processes contribute to endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular permeability, which instigate retinal ischemia and the subsequent over-expression of VEGF [178–181].

It is now known that these chronic inflammatory processes are influenced by the gut–retina axis through gut microbial dysbiosis, which in individuals with type 2 diabetes is characterized by the reduction of populations of butyrate-producing bacteria and the corresponding increasing numbers of opportunistic pathogens. [176, 182, 183] This effect is mediated by the systemic release of bacterial metabolites and endotoxins resulting from an imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory microbial species, which compromises intestinal barrier integrity, thus eliciting systemic inflammation [176, 183]. This concept is further supported by metabolomic evidence demonstrating lower plasma levels of SCFAs, along with the presence of maleate, N,N,N-trimethyl-5-aminovalerate, and N-(2-furoyl) glycine, which are strongly associated with an increased risk of diabetic retinopathy [184, 185]. In a comparative study, Das and colleagues [186] employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing and demonstrated an increase of pro-inflammatory bacterial populations including Methanobrevibacter and a decrease of beneficial, anti-inflammatory bacteria such as Roseburia, Lachnospira, Coprococcus, Phascolarctobacterium, and Anaerostipes in patients with DR. Similarly, another study [187] examined the microbiome of patients with DR utilizing 16S rRNA gene sequencing to demonstrate microbiome dysbiosis with an increase of Bacteroides, Megamonas, Ruminococcus torques group, Lachnoclostridium, and Alistipes population and decreased levels of Blautia, Eubacterium, Collinsella, Dorea, Romboutsia, Anaerostipes, and Fusicatenibacter. Microbiome dysbiosis has been further confirmed by Zhou and colleagues [188], who observed enrichment of Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Lachnospira, and Romboutsia in patients with DR, alongside a depletion of certain anti-inflammatory bacteria. This microbial shift in patients with type 2 diabetes was accompanied by a reduction in specific fecal metabolites, including carnosine, succinate, nicotinic acid, and niacinamide, implying a disruption of metabolic pathways adversely impacting mitochondrial function, inflammatory regulation, and antioxidant defenses [39, 188].

A recent metagenomic shotgun-sequencing study that compared patients with type 2 diabetes with and without DR, documented that, in subjects with type 2 diabetes, Actinobacteria were decreased and Bacteroidetes increased, while the beneficial anti-inflammatory Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species were decreased [189]. Another investigation demonstrated that lower levels of Butyricicoccus and Ruminococcus torques group were individually associated with an increased risk of DR in diabetics, further corroborating the evidence that gut microbial alterations may play a role in the pathogenesis of DR [190]. Indeed, recent findings by Zhou and colleagues [191] have provided important causal evidence linking gut microbiota alterations to the progression of DR through a Mendelian randomization study, identifying 11 causal taxa during the progression of DR. Further, focusing on patients with proliferative DR, Ye and colleagues [192] have highlighted the profound degree of gut microbiome dysbiosis compared with patients with diabetes without retinopathy, as seen by the decrease in the population of 22 bacterial families and enrichment of only 2. In addition, metabolomic profiling revealed significant alterations in fecal metabolites, which could distinguish patients with proliferative DR from those with diabetes without retinopathy with a high degree of accuracy (AUC = 0.943) [192].

Of interest, an Indian study [193] focusing on mycobiome (fungal) dysbiosis also demonstrated significant alterations in patients with DR, with 12 of 18 fungal genera showing decreased abundance in diabetics with and without DR and 6 genera reduced exclusively in patients with evidence of DR, suggesting a potential role of fungal imbalance in the pathogenesis of chronic retinal inflammation.

The variability in gut microbial profiles associated with the development or progression of DR may be attributed to differences in the populations examined, including geographic, dietary, and lifestyle factors that shape the intestinal microbiome. A recent meta-analysis of major case–control studies investigating gut microbiota in DR has delineated this heterogeneity, reporting a marked increase in the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and a decline in Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria, specifically in patients with DR compared with those without [194]. The established microbiome dysbiosis in DR provides an opportunity for the development of future microbial biomarkers. In the same vein, two studies have reported that the increased ratio of Bacteroidetes to Firmicutes species could serve as a potential biomarker for the progression to DR [195, 196].

The dysregulation of the gut microbiome in DR can potentially be modulated with the targeted use of probiotics and dietary interventions. In experimental mouse models, the administration of recombinant Lactobacillus rhamnosus as a probiotic has been shown to reduce retinal damage and signs of inflammation in diabetic eyes [197, 198]. Similarly, dietary interventions such as intermittent fasting, which induce favorable restructuring of the gut microbiome, have been associated with a decrease in DR severity [199]. However, in the only human study to date, daily intake of Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium bifidum, Bifidobacterium longum, and Bifidobacterium lactis for 12 weeks led to only modest improvements in the metabolic parameters examined, including reduced insulin resistance, but showed no significant effects on clinical features of DR [200]. It should be noted, however, that this study was limited by the short duration of the intervention and did not include the recombinant Lactobacillus rhamnosus strain employed in animal studies. Moreover, in human populations, adherence to a diet beneficial to gut microbial health, e.g., including high intake of whole grains and vegetables and reduced consumption of processed meats, is associated with lower DR severity scores [201]. Ideally, future research should aim to clarify the mechanisms linking gut dysbiosis and retinal pathology, identify specific microbial or metabolite biomarkers predictive of DR progression, and evaluate the long-term efficacy of microbiota-targeted therapies in large, controlled clinical trials.

Clinical Implications for Ophthalmic Care

As the human microbiome plays an important role in maintaining overall health, its imbalance may lead to various diseases. While much attention has been given to the gut microbiome, recent research suggests that the microbiome found in extra-intestinal locations, such as the ocular surface, could also affect health and contribute to diseases. Probiotics, prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) have been investigated for their therapeutic roles in gastrointestinal, metabolic, rheumatological, and neurological disorders [202–205]. Although their applications in ophthalmology remain limited, an understanding of their systemic effects and mechanisms of action provides insights into potential future applications for ocular diseases.

Effect of Topical Treatments on the Ocular Surface Microbiome

All topical eye drops containing preservatives that can potentially alter the ocular surface microbiome, as can topical antibiotics. Benzalkonium chloride (BAK), which is commonly used as a preservative in a wide range of ophthalmic drops, acts against microbes, disrupting their cell membranes and reducing the microbial load [206]. It has thus been speculated that chronic exposure to BAK can change the composition of the ocular surface microbiota. This notion is supported by a 16S rRNA next-generation sequencing study [207] showing that the ocular surface microbiome of patients treated with preserved glaucoma eye drops exhibits a diverse range of Gram-negative bacteria, which is notably different from the predominantly Gram-positive microbes found in the eyes of healthy controls. A peculiar finding of this study was that alterations in the ocular surface and eyelid margin microbiota were observed in both the treated and untreated eyes of patients with unilateral treated glaucoma. The explanation for these findings is unclear. Notably, an increase in the abundance of Gram-negative microbes after long-term use of preserved antiglaucoma drops has also been reported in culture-based studies [208].

In line with the above observations, the recent study by Borroni et al. reported significant differences in microbial diversity, composition, and differential abundance between patients with POAG treated with topical drops and healthy individuals [209]. However, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution because the participants in each study group differed significantly in basic characteristics such as age and sex [34, 210].

On the other hand, there is evidence that points in the opposite direction. A study by Priluck et al. has shown that the regular use of preserved eyedrops did not induce changes in the lid margin microbiome, as assessed through both conventional culture methods and 16S rRNA analysis [211]. This lack of effect was consistent across measures of microbiome diversity, quantified by the Shannon index and the prevalence of common bacteria among participants. Similarly, Lim et al. [106] found that the eyelid microbiome was not significantly different between patients with OAG who were treated with preserved prostaglandin drops and untreated controls. However, it should be noted that both these studies are limited because they examined the periocular, rather than the ocular, surface microbiome. It is possible that the eyelid margin might be exposed to eye drops for only a short period and consequently not exhibit measurable changes on the eyelid microbiome. Finally, it could be that the eyelid microbiome is inherently more stable and resistant to external factors such as the instillation of preserved drops.

Probiotics and Prebiotics

In recent years, probiotics and prebiotics have attracted attention due to their beneficial effects on the intestinal microbiome. Probiotics are live microorganisms that may provide health benefits when administered in adequate amounts. Prebiotics are nutrients not fully digested by the human body, but degraded and fermented by intestinal microbes. Probiotics are essential for maintaining a balanced immune response, affecting both adaptive and innate defenses. In particular, probiotics influence immune responses by modulating dendritic cells, macrophages, and T- and B-lymphocytes, primarily through the activation of toll-like receptors [212, 213]. In addition, probiotics appear to contribute to the integrity of the intestinal epithelium and intestinal barrier [214]. In particular, recent evidence has demonstrated that probiotics may contribute to the restoration of the damaged epithelial barrier, the blockade of cytokine-induced apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells, the production of cytoprotective substances and antibacterial compounds, and the regulation of immune function of the intestinal epithelium, including cytokine production [215]. The benefits of probiotics have been documented in a number of disorders, both in animal models and in clinical studies, including ocular diseases. The anti-inflammatory effects of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum CRL759 cell-free supernatant in ocular inflammation were investigated, both in vitro on human retinal ARPE-19 cells and in vivo in mice with endotoxin-induced inflammation. The results demonstrated that the supernatant reduced inflammatory markers such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, as well as clinical signs of eye inflammation [216]. Another study investigated the immunomodulatory effects of two probiotics, Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) and E. coli O83:K24:H31 (EcO), in a model of experimental autoimmune uveitis. The results showed that EcN, but not EcO, successfully prevented experimental autoimmune uveitis, but only when administered as a live organism before or at disease onset, leading to a reduced IRBP-specific T-cell response and an anti-inflammatory gut environment. These findings suggest that probiotics like EcN may regulate the gut–eye axis, offering potential therapeutic strategies for inflammatory eye diseases [217].

Even though probiotics and their use offer various health benefits, concerns have been raised about their direct use as drops on the ocular surface regarding their safety and shelf life. Thus, aiming to reduce the risk of an eye infection, paraprobiotics, which are nonviable bacterial cells or bacterial components, have been developed as an alternative [218]. In a recent study, the bacterial lysate of Lactobacillus sakei proBio-65 has been shown to enhance the endogenous anti-oxidant capacity of human conjunctival cells in vitro by increasing glutathione levels and upregulating key anti-oxidant enzymes, while simultaneously reducing TNF-α expression [219]. In line with this approach of using non-viable bacteria and bacterial components, the postbiotic POF-759, which is a cell-free supernatant derived from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum CRL 759 in a phosphate buffer, was administered as eye drops in a study involving mice with endotoxin-induced uveitis. The results showed a significant reduction in ocular inflammation, cytokine levels, and protein concentrations in the aqueous humor. Additionally, the treatment appeared to decrease cellular infiltration in both the anterior and posterior chambers [220].

Regarding clinical studies, a pilot study on chalazion treatment compared conservative therapy alone to conservative therapy plus probiotics in 20 adults. The probiotics used in the study comprised a mixture of Streptococcus thermophilus ST10 (DSM 25246), Lactococcus lactis LLC02 (DSM 29536), and Lactobacillus delbrueckii (DSM 16606) once a day for 3 months. While both approaches were effective for small chalazia, the addition of probiotics significantly shortened resolution time (21.00 days vs. 38.50 days, P = 0.039). Medium and large chalazia required invasive treatment, and no complications or recurrences were observed in either group [221]. In addition, a case report described a patient with a 3-year history of acute anterior uveitis, who responded well to a combination treatment of dietary probiotic supplementation and steroids, suggesting that probiotics may reduce the recurrence of acute anterior uveitis [222]. Furthermore, a symbiotic supplement containing seven probiotic strains and fructo-oligosaccharides (i.e., containing both pro- and prebiotics) was administered twice daily for 7 months to a patient with anterior uveitis associated with Behçet’s syndrome. This treatment was followed by improved symptoms and significantly reduced serum inflammatory markers [223]. Furthermore, a randomized clinical trial demonstrated that probiotics and prebiotics may be effective in the management of dry eye disease, improving ocular surface disease indices and maintaining tear stability [224]. Despite the present data being limited, prebiotics and probiotics appear to be able to contribute to the treatment of ocular disorders, especially those of an immunological etiology.

The Role of Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

FMT is an emerging therapeutic approach that has gained interest in recent years. FMT involves infusing fecal microbiota from healthy donors into the gastrointestinal tract of individuals with various disorders in order to restore a balanced intestinal flora. FMT has been successfully used to treat relapsing Clostridium difficile infection as well as inducing remission in patients with ulcerative colitis [225, 226]. Recent years have seen a growing interest in the use of FMT for treating non-gastrointestinal diseases, including autoimmune conditions like rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis [202].

From a pathogenetic perspective, FMT could be applied in the treatment of ophthalmological disorders through modification of the gut–eye axis. In a study, the impact of FMT on retinal aging and ocular inflammation was investigated by exchanging gut microbiota between young and aged mice. The results showed that transferring aged microbiota to young mice accelerated retinal inflammation and loss of key functional proteins, whereas young microbiota reversed these effects in aged mice. These findings suggest that modulating the gut microbiome could be a novel therapeutic approach to prevent inflammatory retinal degeneration associated with aging [54]. FMT was safely performed in an open-label, nonrandomized clinical trial involving 10 individuals with symptoms and signs of dry eye, who met the criteria for Sjögren’s syndrome or had positive early markers for Sjögren’s. The results showed that individuals with immune-mediated dry eye exhibited certain bacterial profiles similar to those of the donor for up to 3 months after the FMT. Additionally, half of the patients reported an improvement in dry eye symptoms [227]. In conclusion, FMT appears to be a promising treatment option for ocular disorders. However, more clinical data and randomized clinical trials are required in order to determine its efficacy and its possible place in the treatment algorithms.

Limitations

Although the present narrative review provides a comprehensive overview of the microbiome and its associations with ocular diseases, it lacks the systematic approach and quality assessment of the included studies typical of a systematic review. Our goal was to provide a broad synthesis of current evidence and concepts, serving as a foundation for future research and microbiome-targeted therapeutic research.

Importantly, there are significant limitations with existing evidence. It should be emphasized that most published data rely on animal models. Despite the similarities, animals are different from humans in anatomy, genetics, and physiology, and thus cannot perfectly mirror human conditions and pathologies [228]. Moreover, available human studies are small observational trials that generally lack methodological standardization, which makes them susceptible to biases [23, 24]. In light of the aforementioned, caution is required in the interpretation of available evidence. Although existing research suggests strong associations between ocular diseases, the microbiome, and its metabolites, only controlled studies with standardized methodology can establish (if any) a causal association in the future.

Conclusions

When taken collectively, the expanding body of the evidence reviewed supports the concept that the microbiome may be a regulator of ocular homeostasis and disease through both local ocular surface interactions and systemic gut–eye axis signaling. With recent advances in germ-free animal models and animals with well-characterized microbial composition (i.e., gnotobiotic models), culture-independent sequencing, and multi-omics technologies, alterations in microbial composition, diversity, and metabolic output have been shown to be consistently associated with a broad spectrum of ophthalmic diseases, including glaucoma, ocular surface disease, uveitis, age-related macular degeneration, and diabetic retinopathy. Moreover, dysbiosis within the gut microbiome appears to be the heart of such pathophysiologic perturbation, with priming of retinal and optic nerve tissues for immune activation, complement dysregulation, angiogenesis, and neurodegeneration being promoted by a state of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, increased intestinal permeability, and altered microbial metabolite profiles. Concurrently, increases in susceptibility to inflammation and infection have been associated with alterations of the ocular surface microbiome, which may disrupt epithelial barrier integrity and local immune surveillance. Metabolites that are derived from the microbiome, such as short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, tryptophan catabolites, and trimethylamine-related compounds, emerge as key mechanistic intermediates linking microbial ecosystems to ocular immune and metabolic pathways, thus accentuating the importance of functional, rather than purely taxonomic, microbial characterization. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that, despite some promising evidence and growing enthusiasm, the gut–eye axis remains as yet largely unproven. Whether the microbiome plays a definitive causative role in human ocular diseases cannot yet be addressed due to the fact that current data rely on animal models, small cohort microbiome profiling studies, and indirect systemic correlations.

Beyond these limitations, translation to clinical ophthalmology remains somewhat limited due to several methodological factors, such as inter-individual variability, low-biomass sampling challenges, and the lack of standardized analytical frameworks in microbiome studies. To distinguish causal microbial signatures from disease-associated epiphenomena, addressing these limitations through harmonized and integrative methodologies, longitudinal human studies will be essential. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of microbiome–host interactions may enable the development of innovative, microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies, ranging from dietary modulation and prebiotics, probiotics, to postbiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation, that complement existing treatments and advance precision medicine approaches in ophthalmic care. These therapies offer the potential for both adjunctive treatment in refractory cases to the current standard of care and a relatively economic, novel approach in the form of diet modifications and microbiome/metabolite supplementation.
