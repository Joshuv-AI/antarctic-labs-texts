Carnosic acid-loaded stimuli-responsive hydrogel promotes wound healing through PGP-1 mediated immune remodeling
Authors: Renshuai Zhang, Rufei Wang, Yixiao Wang, Guojun Pan, Xia Zhao, Qiuyu Gong, Miao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent inflammation is a major barrier to effective wound healing. The local immune microenvironment, closely associated with the inflammatory state, exerts central regulatory control throughout repair. Pyroglutamyl aminopeptidase 1 (PGP-1) is implicated in inflammation-related conditions, but its role in wound inflammation and regeneration remains unclear. Here, carnosic acid (CA) was identified as a potent PGP-1 inhibitor that reduced the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and promoted M2 macrophage polarization, thereby demonstrating substantial potential to remodel the immune microenvironment. Based on this, CA was employed as a bioactive structural building block, together with 4-aminophenylboronic acid (APBA), to construct a stimuli-responsive hydrogel (CAP hydrogel). The hydrogel not only exhibited self-healing ability and cytocompatibility (cell viability ~95%), but also achieved 82.48% of pH/ROS-responsive CA release, with accelerated release under acidic and ROS-rich conditions that mimic inflammatory wounds. This targeted responsiveness is expected to enhance on-demand local CA availability during the early inflammatory phase, thereby strengthening PGP-1 inhibition and immune microenvironment remodeling. In the deep burn wound model, the CAP hydrogel significantly accelerated wound closure and improved angiogenesis after 20 days of treatment. In addition, it promoted favorable remodeling of the local immune microenvironment by downregulating PGP-1 and related inflammatory signaling pathways. Overall, this study provides a feasible strategy for promoting wound healing through PGP-1 inhibition and immune microenvironment modulation. Introduction

Wound healing is a dynamic physiological process encompassing hemostasis, inflammation, cell proliferation, extracellular matrix deposition, and tissue remodeling [1, 2]. During wound healing, an appropriate inflammatory response contributes positively to tissue repair; however, excessive inflammation disrupts the balance of the local immune microenvironment and delays closure [3, 4]. The immune inflammatory microenvironment at the wound site, characterized by elevated inflammatory cytokines, activation of inflammation-related signaling pathways, and the accompanying reactive oxygen species (ROS) and local acidification (lower pH), directly governs the inflammatory response and ultimately influences wound healing outcomes [5]. Modulation of the wound immune microenvironment has been validated as an effective strategy to promote healing [6]. Macrophages are key regulators of wound inflammation and repair, typically exhibiting a pro-inflammatory phenotype at early stages and shifting toward a pro-healing phenotype as healing progresses [7, 8]. Dysregulated macrophage responses (e.g., prolonged pro-inflammatory activation) can disrupt the local immune microenvironment and delay wound closure [9, 10]. Therefore, restoring a balanced immune microenvironment is a promising strategy for refractory wounds.

In recent years, research on novel inflammatory cytokines has opened new avenues for wound therapy [11–13]. Pyroglutamyl aminopeptidase 1 (PGP-1), a cysteine peptidase widely distributed and highly conserved across mammalian tissues, has emerged as a key cytokine in various inflammatory conditions [14]. Upregulation of PGP-1 has been observed in both cellular and animal models of inflammation, as well as in patient samples, where its activity correlates with the severity and persistence of the inflammatory response [15–18]. Mechanistically, suppression of PGP-1 expression or activity has been shown to attenuate pro-inflammatory cytokine release and downregulate classical inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby reshaping the local immune microenvironment toward resolution [19]. Despite these advances, most studies to date have focused on systemic or tumor-related inflammation, and the specific role of PGP-1 in wound-associated inflammation and tissue repair remains largely unexplored. Bridging this knowledge gap holds promise for the development of innovative molecular therapies aimed at improving wound healing outcomes. However, to date, only a limited number of PGP-1 inhibitors have been reported, including mechanism-based covalent inhibitors and several natural inhibitors with micromolar activity, while their in vivo anti-inflammatory validation remains scarce [14]. Carnosic acid (CA), a small molecule derived from Rosmarinus species, has been identified as an efficient inhibitor of PGP-120. In addition, CA exhibits potent antioxidant and antibacterial activities, making it highly suitable for wound-healing applications [21, 22]. However, to date, the immunoregulatory mechanisms that link CA to wound microenvironment remodeling remain incompletely defined. Notably, although CA exhibits relatively high systemic bioavailability, its plasma protein binding exceeds 60%, which markedly restricts tissue distribution and target engagement [23]. As a result, oral or injection administration is not optimal. Accordingly, local delivery (for example, dermal application) is preferred to maximize on-site exposure and therapeutic effect.

Hydrogels have emerged as leading wound-dressing candidates and constitute an ideal platform for localized drug delivery due to their outstanding hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, and unique three-dimensional porous structure [24–26]. Recent advances have focused on engineering multifunctional or smart hydrogels that integrate bioactive ingredients, enabling them to respond dynamically to changes in the wound microenvironment and deliver therapeutic agents on demand [27–29]. For example, curcumin and some drugs have been loaded into the hydrogel matrix to promote re-epithelization and granulation tissue formation in wound site [30]. Despite these advancements, most current hydrogels are formulated by encapsulating bioactive agents for sustained release, whereas the strategy of utilizing the therapeutic agent itself as a structural component (building block) of the hydrogel network has been comparatively underexplored [31]. Constructing the hydrogel framework with such therapeutic building block offers clear advantages: as the bioactive agent is released, the hydrogel matrix gradually disintegrates, which facilitates painless removal of the dressing and minimizes secondary injury to the newly formed tissue during dressing changes. Notably, the integration of PGP-1 inhibitor (CA) into hydrogel systems remains largely uninvestigated, and their application as structural building block in smart hydrogel fabrication has yet to be reported. Incorporating CA directly into the hydrogel network not only enables a comprehensive evaluation of their therapeutic potential in wound healing but also establishes a new paradigm for the molecular design of smart hydrogel dressings.

In this study, we first confirmed the inhibitory activity of CA against PGP-1 and systematically investigated the PGP-1-mediated anti-inflammatory effects of CA. The regulatory impact of CA on macrophage polarization and its suppression of key inflammatory signaling pathways were clearly demonstrated. Building upon these mechanistic insights, CA was employed as a bioactive structural building block, in combination with 4-aminophenylboronic acid (APBA), to construct a dynamic, stimuli-responsive hydrogel (CAP hydrogel) via covalent crosslinking. The resultant CAP hydrogel was comprehensively characterized for its physicochemical properties, biocompatibility, and multifunctional biological activities. Utilizing the hydrogel as a wound dressing platform, the therapeutic potential of CA in promoting wound healing was further evaluated in vivo (Fig. 1). Overall, this work not only elucidates the mechanisms by which PGP-1 inhibition and immune microenvironment modulation accelerate wound repair, but also establishes a novel strategy for integrating PGP-1 inhibitors into smart hydrogel systems, providing a foundation for future translational applications in regenerative medicine.

Results and discussion

The PGP-1 inhibitory and anti-inflammatory activity of CA

The inhibition effect of CA on PGP-1 was assayed according to the previous reported method [18]. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of CA on PGP-1 was determined to be 2.30 ± 0.2 µM, which indicates that CA was a highly active molecule among known PGP-1 inhibitors and was consistent with the reported research results (Fig. 2a) [20]. Furthermore, inhibition kinetic analyses with different concentrations of both the substrate and CA were conducted to determine the inhibitory mechanism and the Ki values. CA was a competitive inhibitor against PGP-1 mediated hydrolysis, evidenced by the Lineweaver-Burk plots (Fig. 2b). The Ki value of CA was determined as 0.98 µM (Figure S1). Notably, this low-micromolar potency is comparable to the classic competitive PGP-1 inhibitor benarthin (Ki = 1.2 µM), and is markedly stronger than small substrate-analog inhibitors such as 2-pyrrolidone (Ki = 50 µM) or N-ethylmaleimide (Ki = 30 µM) reported for PGP-132,33. Collectively, these comparisons place CA among the more potent small-molecule PGP-1 inhibitors reported to date. Molecular docking analyses further demonstrated that CA could effectively occupy the active site of PGP-1, and Arg75 and Ser38 were involved in the hydrogen bonding with CA (Fig. 2c). After identifying CA as a potent PGP-1 inhibitor, its safety and anti-inflammatory effects were evaluated in RAW 264.7 cells. According to CCK-8 assays, RAW 264.7 cell viability remained > 90% even at 10 µM CA (Figure S2), which we considered non-cytotoxic under our experimental conditions. Therefore, 10 µM was chosen as the maximum working concentration in subsequent in vitro assays. Cytokine analysis showed decreased concentrations of pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NO, which demonstrated the anti-inflammatory activity of CA (Fig. 2d). To further clarify the role of PGP-1 in the anti-inflammatory effect of CA, the PGP-1 knockdown cell model was constructed through transfecting with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs. Table S1) [32, 33]. The mRNA expression levels and western blot of PGP-1 were used to confirm the knockdown results (Fig. 2e). Notably, the concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NO remained unchanged in siRNA-transfected cells after LPS stimulation, which indicated that the anti-inflammatory activity of CA was mediated via PGP-1 (Fig. 2f and g). Taken together, CA acts as a potent PGP-1 inhibitor and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine production in a PGP-1 dependent manner.

As mentioned earlier, PGP-1 has been reported to be highly linked to inflammation and was increasingly recognized as a new inflammatory cytokine [18]. However, the mechanisms by which PGP-1 inhibition attenuates inflammation remain unclear. Accordingly, in this study we employed CA as a model compound to elucidate how PGP-1 inhibition exerts its anti-inflammatory effects. As shown in Fig. 2h, LPS stimulation markedly increased PGP-1 expression, whereas treatment with the PGP-1 inhibitor CA significantly reduced PGP-1 levels, mirroring the trend observed for the canonical pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α, further supporting the close association between PGP-1 and inflammation. The JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT pathways are classical inflammatory signaling cascades implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of numerous inflammation-related diseases [34, 35]. Previous reports have shown that CA broadly suppresses inflammatory activation of macrophages by inhibiting canonical signaling axes, such as NF-κB and MAPKs [36]. Accordingly, this study further investigated whether JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT pathways are involved in the PGP-1 mediated anti-inflammatory effects. Notably, CA suppressed the phosphorylation of JAK, STAT3, PI3K, and AKT, indicating that inhibition of the JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways are involved in the PGP-1 mediated anti-inflammatory effects. In addition, our data extend the mechanistic framework by identifying PGP-1 inhibition as an upstream regulatory event that links CA exposure to suppression of JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT signaling, which is particularly relevant to immune microenvironment remodeling in early-stage wound repair.

The JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways have been shown to be closely associated with macrophage reprogramming, which plays a pivotal role in the progression of inflammation [37, 38]. Macrophages are highly plastic immune cells capable of differentiating into two principal phenotypes: the pro-inflammatory M1 type and the anti-inflammatory M2 type [39]. In the context of wound healing, macrophages at the wound site are predominantly polarized toward the M1 phenotype during the early stages, which aggravates local inflammation through the secretion of various pro-inflammatory cytokines [40]. Consequently, promoting the transition from the M1 to the M2 phenotype is widely recognized as a promising strategy to accelerate wound healing and tissue repair [1]. To investigate the potential of CA in modulating macrophage polarization, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the expression of iNOS (a marker for M1 macrophages) and CD206 (a marker for M2 macrophages) [41]. As expected, LPS stimulation increased iNOS staining while reducing CD206 positivity, indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Treatment with CA resulted in a noticeable reduction in iNOS expression and a concomitant increase in CD206 positivity (Fig. 2i and S3), suggesting an effective repolarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. To corroborate these observations, we quantified the mRNA levels of several macrophage phenotype-associated markers, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (characteristic of M1 macrophages), as well as CD206, Arg-1, and IL-10 (characteristic of M2 macrophages), using real-time PCR (RT-PCR). As expected, CA significantly decreased the expression of M1 markers, while increased the M2 markers (Figure S4). In addition, to provide precise quantitative evidence for macrophage polarization, flow cytometry was performed to quantify M1/M2 subsets using CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) in RAW 264.7 cells. LPS stimulation markedly increased the proportion of CD86-positive cells while reducing CD206-positive cells, whereas CA treatment significantly decreased the CD86-positive population and partially restored the CD206-positive population (Figure S5). Data of flow cytometry corroborate the immunofluorescence and RT-PCR results, supporting that CA promotes an M1-to-M2 phenotypic shift. Collectively, CA not only modulated key inflammatory pathways but also regulated macrophage polarization via PGP-1 inhibition, thereby contributing to immune microenvironment remodeling and providing mechanistic support for its role in promoting wound healing.

Preparation and characterization of CAP hydrogel

Considering the anti-inflammatory effects of CA, it was first used as a bioactive structural building block, in combination with APBA, to form CA-APBA complex. Subsequently, CA-APBA was applied to construct a stimuli-responsive CAP hydrogel via covalent crosslinking (Fig. 3a). The APBA in CA-APBA copolymerized with acrylamide (AM) via free radical crosslinking. The pre-assembled CA-APBA offered tissue-adhesive and self-healing capability through dynamic borate ester bond. To verify the successful formation of the CA-APBA complex, Alizarin Red S (ARS) was employed as a fluorescent probe [42]. The fluorescence intensity exhibited a linear increase with rising concentrations of APBA (Figure S6a). When the molar ratio of CA to APBA exceeded 1:2, the fluorescence intensity plateaued, indicating no further increase (Figure S6b). Consequently, a CA/APBA ratio of 1:2 was selected for subsequent complex preparation. Further characterization of the CA-APBA was performed using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, with a physical mixture of CA and APBA serving as the control. As presented in Fig. 3b, the stretching vibration bands at 1663 cm⁻¹ and 1439 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the carbonyl groups of CA, disappeared in the spectrum of the CA-APBA. Moreover, a distinct peak at 1072 cm⁻¹, attributed to the vibration of the newly formed phenyl-boron ring, was observed in the complex, further confirming successful complexation of CA-APBA.

CAP hydrogels incorporating varying concentrations of the CA-APBA were subsequently prepared (Fig. 3a), and the gelation time was evaluated using the tube inversion method. As the concentration of the CA-APBA increased from 1 mM to 6 mM, the gelation time was prolonged from 38 s to 86 s (Figure S7). This phenomenon may be ascribed to the higher abundance of phenol groups at elevated CA-APBA content, which could interfere with the polymerization process [43]. The frequency sweep analysis of CAP hydrogels with different CA-APBA contents is presented in Fig. 3c. Notably, the hydrogel without CA-APBA exhibited the highest storage modulus, and the mechanical strength decreased progressively as the concentration of the CA-APBA increased. In particular, the CAP hydrogel containing 6 mM CA-APBA displayed the lowest storage modulus, falling below 1000 Pa, which may render it unsuitable for application to human skin [44]. As shown in Figure S8, the cylindrical CAP hydrogel demonstrated the ability to bend and twist into various shapes and subsequently recover its original form, indicating excellent flexibility for potential application at joint sites. Additionally, the CAP hydrogel exhibited a porous three-dimensional network structure (Fig. 3e), which underpins its favorable air permeability and water retention properties. It was also observed that the average pore size increased with rising CA-APBA content (Figure S9).

ROS levels are elevated and the physiological environment becomes acidic in chronic wounds due to persistent inflammation [45]. Based on the dynamic boronic ester bonds, the CAP hydrogel exhibited responsive behavior under different pH and redox conditions. The in vitro release profile of CA from CAP hydrogel was evaluated using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. To simulate physiological and inflammatory conditions, the hydrogels were immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4, pH 5.0, and 10 mM H2O2, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3d, the 4 mM CAP hydrogel displayed comparable CA release at pH 7.4 and pH 5.0 during the first 24 h. After 24 h, release was significantly accelerated under acidic conditions, reaching 82.48% over 120 h at pH 5.0 versus 70.61% at pH 7.4. Moreover, oxidative stimulation (10 mM H₂O₂) further enhanced CA release, with 84.8% released over 120 h (Figure S10). This behavior is consistent with the pH/ROS sensitivity of the boronate ester and boroxine bonds incorporated in the hydrogel network. The CAP hydrogel showed faster degradation rate at pH 5.0 compared to pH 7.4, with only 34.6 ± 5.9% residue at 14 days (Figure S11). Furthermore, the cumulative release of CA increased as the initial concentration of CA-APBA in the hydrogel was elevated (Figure S12). The sustained release profile of CA ensures the hydrogel’s capacity to provide prolonged therapeutic activity. Overall, the CAP hydrogel possesses tunable physicochemical properties and pH/ROS-responsive, sustained release capabilities, providing a robust foundation for its application as a multifunctional wound dressing.

The water absorption capacity of CAP hydrogel was further evaluated by the swelling degree [46]. As depicted in Figure S13, all hydrogels reached equilibrium swelling after 100 h. Notably, the swelling degree increased in accordance with the rising content of the CA-APBA complex. At pH 7.4, the CAP hydrogel containing 0 mM CA-APBA (i.e., the PAM hydrogel) exhibited a swelling degree of 524% at 100 h, whereas the hydrogel with 6 mM CA-APBA reached a swelling degree of 948%. This trend is consistent with the release profiles and likely arises from the dynamic, reversible boronate ester and boroxine bonds formed within the CA-APBA complex. The interaction between these dynamic bonds leads to greater intermolecular spacing and volume, thereby promoting water uptake[47]. In addition, the CAP hydrogel demonstrated pronounced pH-responsive swelling behavior. Specifically, the swelling rate in a pH 5.0 environment was higher than that observed at pH 7.4. This effect can be ascribed to the pH-sensitive nature of boronate ester and boroxine bonds, which facilitate increased swelling under acidic conditions.

For the tensile strength test, CAP hydrogels containing 1–6 mM CA-APBA were able to stretch to 10–15 times their original length, which was substantially greater than that of the PAM hydrogel (without CA-APBA), which could only be stretched up to five times its initial length (Figure S14). Incorporation of CA-APBA markedly enhances the tensile strength and flexibility of the CAP hydrogel. This improvement is primarily attributed to the covalent crosslinking between AM and APBA, as well as the introduction of dynamic borate bonds within the CA-APBA, which together impart increased elasticity to the hydrogel network. Based on the combined findings from rheological and tensile extension tests, a concentration of 4 mM CA-APBA was selected for the preparation of CAP hydrogels in subsequent biological experiments. Furthermore, the CAP hydrogel demonstrated excellent adhesive properties on the surface of human skin. As illustrated in (Fig. 3f), the hydrogel was able to adhere firmly and conform dynamically to the contours of the finger, accommodating finger movements without detachment. Additionally, the hydrogel could be easily removed from the skin without leaving any residue. The CAP hydrogel also exhibited strong adhesion to a variety of substrates, including glass, plastic, metal, and porcine skin (Figure S15). Importantly, robust self-adhesive interactions were observed between the hydrogel and human tissue, which is particularly advantageous for biomedical applications.

The self-healing performance was further validated by a cut-and-paste experiment. When the CAP hydrogel was cut in half and rejoined (Fig. 3g), it adhered together and no visible flaws at the junction, furthermore its stretchability maintained after healing. However, distinct flaws were visible at the interface under microscopic observation in PAM hydrogel (without CA-APBA) (Figure S16). Rheological analysis was conducted to assess the self-healing capability of the CAP hydrogel containing 4 mM CA-APBA. As shown in (Fig. 3h), when subjected to a high strain of 5000%, the storage modulus (G′) decreased markedly and fell below the loss modulus (G″), indicating disruption of the hydrogel network. Remarkably, upon returning to a low strain (1%), the hydrogel was able to recover to nearly its original G′ value (approximately 1000 Pa) within 5 min. Repeated cycles of alternating high and low strain (seven cycles) resulted in no significant changes in the moduli, demonstrating the robust self-healing ability of the CAP hydrogel. The self-healing property of the CAP hydrogel is primarily attributed to the dynamic borate ester crosslinking between CA and APBA (Fig. 3i). When the fractured hydrogel pieces are brought into contact, the hydroxyl groups on CA rapidly re-crosslink with the boronic acid groups on APBA to reform borate ester bonds, thus facilitating self-repair of the network. This intrinsic self-healing capability enables the hydrogel to withstand joint movements or localized mechanical stress in practical applications, allowing the wound dressing to recover rapidly and minimizing the adverse effects of shear forces during body movement.

Collectively, the CAP hydrogel possesses 10 times tensile strength compare to original length, self-healing capability, and tissue-adhesive properties. Such features enable the hydrogel dressing to conform fully to the wound site, withstand diverse deformations and mechanical disruptions, and maintain integrity during use. Importantly, these attributes may reduce the risk of secondary infection by preserving continuous wound coverage, even in the event of hydrogel breakage during application.

Biocompatibility, antioxidant and antibacterial efficiencies of CAP hydrogel

To further assess cytocompatibility, L929 fibroblast cells were cultured in the presence of CAP hydrogels. Live/dead staining using Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) was conducted on L929 cells at day 1 and 3, with the resulting fluorescence images presented in (Fig. 4a). All groups exhibited robust green fluorescence and spindle-shaped healthy cell morphology, while red fluorescence, indicating cell death, was not detected. Cell viability remained above 95% after 24 h co-culture with no significant differences observed among the different hydrogel formulations (Fig. 4b), confirming the favorable cytocompatibility of the material. In addition, enhanced cell proliferation was also apparent in the hydrogel-treated groups (Fig. 4c). Cell migration is a key factor in tissue regeneration. A scratch assay was performed to assess cell migration. At 20 h, the CAP group showed a migration rate of 22.34%, which was higher than that of the PAM hydrogel group (16.31%) and the blank group (12.40%) (Figure S17). This enhanced migratory response may be associated with CA incorporation and is favorable for tissue regeneration. Taken together, the CAP hydrogels exhibit outstanding biocompatibility, supporting cell viability, growth, and proliferation, thereby demonstrating great potential as wound dressings.

ROS are commonly present at wound sites and can induce oxidative stress, leading to DNA damage and impaired wound healing [48]. To evaluate the intracellular ROS scavenging capability of CAP hydrogel, L929 cells were treated with the hydrogel in the presence of excessive ROS, and DCFH-DA was used as a fluorescent probe to monitor ROS levels. As shown in Fig. 4d, strong fluorescence was observed in cells treated with ROSup, whereas diminished fluorescence of only 30% fluorescence intensity was observed in the blank and CAP hydrogel groups (Figure S18), indicating reduced ROS accumulation. Since higher fluorescence intensity reflects greater ROS production, these results demonstrate that the CAP hydrogel possesses efficient ROS removal properties, which is important for wound healing. Moreover, CA is well-known for its strong antioxidant activity, which may contribute to neutralize free radicals, thereby facilitating wound healing and protecting tissues from oxidative damage. To further assess the antioxidant performance of the CAP hydrogel, its scavenging efficiency against DPPH and ABTS radicals was measured. As illustrated in (Fig. 4e and f), CAP hydrogels exhibited pronounced free radical scavenging rates, with antioxidant effects comparable to those of vitamin C.

Bacterial infection is a common complication during wound healing and can significantly delay tissue repair [49]. Therefore, wound dressings endowed with antibacterial properties are highly desirable in clinical applications [50]. The antibacterial activity of CA and CAP hydrogel was assessed using the inhibition zone assay. Following 12 h of incubation, the CAP hydrogel exhibited pronounced bactericidal effects against Staphylococcus aureus, as evidenced by the clear inhibition zones, whereas the PAM hydrogel displayed no antibacterial activity (Fig. 4g). Additionally, CA demonstrated concentration-dependent inhibitory effects on Staphylococcus aureus (Fig. 4h). In addition to Staphylococcus aureus, a common wound pathogen, Clostridium perfringens was included because deep burn wounds can present hypoxic and necrotic microenvironments that favor anaerobic bacterial growth, and Clostridium perfringens is a clinically relevant anaerobe implicated in severe soft-tissue infections [51]. Moreover, CA exhibited a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) against Staphylococcus aureus and Clostridium perfringens that was only slightly higher than that of ciprofloxacin, which served as the positive control (Table S2). The zone-of-inhibition and MIC results highlight the robust antibacterial capability of the CAP hydrogel, demonstrating strong in vitro antibacterial activity and suggesting potential relevance for reducing bacterial contamination in wound care. The hemocompatibility of the CAP hydrogel wound dressings was evaluated using a standard hemolysis assay. As shown in Fig. 4i, there was no evidence of red blood cell rupture after exposure to the hydrogel, and the hemolysis ratios for all hydrogel samples were considerably lower than the accepted threshold of 3% [52]. In contrast, the positive control group treated with Triton X-100 displayed a distinct red coloration, indicative of pronounced hemolysis. The hemolysis assay indicates that the CAP hydrogels are hemocompatible. Furthermore, as demonstrated in the earlier sections of this study, CA possesses potent anti-inflammatory activity and the ability to promote macrophage reprogramming. Together, above features support the potential of CAP hydrogel as a multifunctional wound dressing.

Wound healing assessment of CAP hydrogel in vivo

To further substantiate the therapeutic efficacy of CAP hydrogel, a deep burn wound model was employed for in vivo evaluation. Deep burn wounds are characterized by extensive tissue damage accompanied by pronounced oxidative stress and sustained inflammation, which represent major barriers to effective healing [53]. Given that our CAP hydrogel is designed to provide local, stimuli-responsive release of CA and to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, we prioritized a deep burn wound model as an initial and clinically relevant setting to evaluate its regenerative and immunomodulatory efficacy. Circular dorsal wounds were generated and subsequently assigned to three groups: treatment with CAP hydrogel dressing, coverage with Tegaderm film (a commercially available wound dressing used as a positive control), and no dressing (untreated control). A schematic overview of the experimental procedure is presented in Fig. 5a. Throughout the study, both the CAP hydrogel and Tegaderm dressings were replaced every five days. This schedule was guided by the in vitro release kinetics, where CA release gradually increased and approached a plateau at ~120 h (Fig. 3d). Thus, a 5-day interval was used as a reproducible and practical replacement frequency to support sustained on-site delivery while minimizing frequent disturbance to the wound bed. Representative macroscopic images of the wounds from each group at designated time points are displayed in (Fig. 5b). Macroscopically, the wounds treated with CAP hydrogel exhibited a markedly accelerated healing process compared to those in both the untreated control and Tegaderm film groups. At day 20, the control group still displayed extensive scabbing, while unhealed areas remained evident in the Tegaderm film group despite partial wound reduction. In contrast, wounds in the CAP hydrogel group showed substantially reduced wound area and superior closure. Quantitative analysis of wound closure rates revealed that, by day 20, animals treated with CAP hydrogel achieved significantly higher healing rates (93%) relative to both controls (49%) and the Tegaderm-treated group (71%) (Fig. 5c).

Wound healing is a highly coordinated and dynamic biological process, encompassing multiple overlapping phases such as hemostasis, inflammation, cellular migration, proliferation, extracellular matrix deposition, and tissue remodeling [1]. To investigate the histological impact of CAP hydrogel dressings on wound repair, regenerated skin tissues were harvested on day 20 post-injury and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as well as Masson’s trichrome staining (MTS). The histological findings closely paralleled the macroscopic wound healing outcomes. As illustrated in Fig. 5d, persistent scab formation was evident in both the Tegaderm film and control groups after 20 days, indicating delayed tissue repair. In contrast, no residual scab was observed in wounds treated with the CAP hydrogel. Moreover, the control samples exhibited abundant dead inflammatory cells encapsulated within the scab (Fig. 5d, white dotted circle), suggesting ongoing inflammation in progressing of healing. In comparison, both the CAP hydrogel and commercial dressing groups demonstrated reduced inflammation, fostering an environment more conducive to tissue regeneration. Tissue sections from the CAP hydrogel group also revealed a more organized skin architecture relative to the other groups, with clear identification of newly formed blood vessels, sebaceous glands, and hair follicles (Fig. 5d). Additionally, the formation of granulation tissue at the wound site was also markedly enhanced in the CAP hydrogel group, with granulation thickness approximately 203 μm greater than that observed in the commercial film dressing group (Fig. 5e). Moreover, MTS staining was utilized to assess new collagen deposition within the wound area (Fig. 5f). Both the CAP hydrogel and commercial dressing groups showed substantially greater collagen content than the control after 20 days (Fig. 5g). Notably, the CAP hydrogel group exhibited the most substantial collagen deposition, indicating that the functional hydrogel accelerated wound repair by promoting collagen formation.

Angiogenesis is a pivotal process in wound healing, as newly formed blood vessels supply essential nutrients to regenerating cells and support the development of granulation tissue [54]. In the present study, neovascularization within the wound bed was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining for CD31 [55]. As shown in Fig. 5h, minimal fluorescence was detected in the control group, whereas wounds treated with CAP hydrogel exhibited a pronounced increase in CD31-positive fluorescence, indicating increased CD31-positive endothelial cell enrichment and a pro-angiogenic microenvironment. This observation was consistent with the presence of microvessel-like structures in H&E sections (Fig. 5d). Quantitative analysis presented in Fig. 5i revealed that the mean fluorescence intensity of CD31 in the CAP hydrogel group was approximately 1.6 times higher than that in the control group and commercial dressing group. Collectively, these in vivo macroscopic and histological findings underscore the beneficial effects of CAP hydrogel dressings on wound repair, particularly by promoting angiogenesis and thereby accelerating the overall healing process.

CAP hydrogel modulated inflammation microenvironment in vivo

It is widely recognized that the inflammatory microenvironment plays a critical role in orchestrating wound healing and tissue regeneration [56, 57]. To elucidate the mechanisms by which the CAP hydrogel dressing modulates inflammation and facilitates tissue repair, wound tissues were collected three days post-injury for analysis. The concentrations of key cytokines were quantified using ELISA assays (Fig. 6a and b). Compared to the untreated control group, wounds treated with CAP hydrogel exhibited marked reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, along with a notable increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines including IL-4 and IL-10, which indicate that CAP hydrogel effectively attenuates the local inflammatory response. Notably, the anti-inflammatory efficacy of CAP hydrogel surpasses that of the commercial dressing. Furthermore, the decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines and the concomitant increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines suggest that the CAP hydrogel may also promote macrophage polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype within wound tissues. Moreover, as an established immunosuppressive factor, TGF-β1 is closely associated with macrophage M2 polarization and plays a critical role in endothelial cell migration and angiogenesis [58]. As shown in Fig. 6c, treatment with the CAP hydrogel markedly upregulated TGF-β1 expression, reaching levels significantly higher than those in the untreated control and commercial dressing groups. M2 macrophages secrete reparative growth factors, such as VEGF-A, to support neovascularization. Consistent with this, VEGF-A protein levels were significantly higher in the CAP hydrogel group than in the Tegaderm and untreated control groups, indicating CAP-mediated enhancement of angiogenesis in the regenerating skin of burn wounds (Fig. 6d), which is also consistent with the previously observed elevation of CD31 expression (Fig. 5h and i). Collectively, enhanced neovascularization induced by the CAP hydrogel dressing promoted tissue regeneration, resulting in faster and more organized healing of chronic wounds. Mechanistically, the therapeutic benefits of the CAP hydrogel may be associated with local CA delivery together with inflammation microenvironment modulation, which is consistent with our in vitro PGP-1 inhibition and macrophage polarization results.

Our in vitro data have established that CA can modulate macrophage polarization (Fig. 2i). To further substantiate the capacity of CAP hydrogel to induce macrophage phenotypic transformation in vivo, immunofluorescence staining for F4/80 and CD163 was performed on wound tissue sections. As illustrated in Fig. 6e and f, compared with the untreated group, the CAP hydrogel group showed reduced overall F4/80-positive macrophage signal, consistent with attenuated inflammation. Meanwhile, an increased CD163 signal was observed in the CAP group (Fig. 6e), which, together with the changes in inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6a and b), supports that CAP treatment favors a pro-healing macrophage phenotype during wound repair. Although the Tegaderm film group also showed the presence of M2 macrophages, as indicated by red fluorescence, both the fluorescence intensity and relative positive area were considerably lower compared to those observed in the CAP hydrogel group (Fig. 6g and S19). Taken together, CAP hydrogel dressing effectively promotes the polarization of macrophages toward a pro-healing M2 phenotype in vivo, thereby contributing to the accelerated wound healing process.

Extensive research has established that PGP-1 serves as a key inflammatory marker, exhibiting elevated expression levels under inflammatory conditions [14]. In the present study, Western blot analysis revealed that PGP-1 was highly expressed in the early-stage wound tissues, thereby validating its involvement in acute wound inflammation (Fig. 6h and S20). Treatment with the CAP hydrogel resulted in a marked decrease in PGP-1 expression compared to the untreated control, with the reduction being more pronounced than that observed in the commercial dressing group. It is well documented that suppression of PGP-1 is associated with diminished secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators [18]. Our findings further demonstrate that the anti-inflammatory effects of CA are, at least in part, mediated through PGP-1 inhibition, as evidenced in CA-treated cell models (Fig. 2e and f). Additionally, the JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, two well-known regulator of inflammatory responses, were also examined. CAP hydrogel treatment substantially lowered the levels of phosphorylated JAK, STAT3, PI3K, and AKT, indicating effective suppression of these pathways. Overall, relative to the other groups, the CAP hydrogel increased IL-4, IL-10, VEGF-A, and TGF-β1, while decreasing TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. It also promoted macrophage polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype and, at early time points, effectively suppressed JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT inflammatory signaling pathways, changes that collectively favor subsequent wound closure (Fig. 6i).

The systemic toxicity of the CAP hydrogels in vivo was further evaluated. After treatment, morphologies of the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were evaluated by H&E staining (Figure S21). All the tissues appeared normal, and no detectable signs of damage or inflammatory lesion were observed, indicating that the nanocomposite hydrogel had no systemic toxic effect.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates for the first time that inhibiting PGP-1 contributes to improved wound healing. Furthermore, CA as a natural PGP-1 inhibitor, was shown to downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines, suppress relevant inflammatory signaling pathways, and drive macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype through PGP-1 inhibition. Moreover, a CA-based hydrogel (CAP hydrogel) was developed that exhibits excellent mechanical strength, self-healing capacity, tissue adhesion, and enables sustained pH and ROS responsive release of CA. In a deep burn wound model, accelerated wound closure, enhanced collagen deposition and angiogenesis, and improved tissue regeneration were observed with CAP hydrogel treatment compared to control group. Mechanistically, PGP-1 and inflammatory signaling were downregulated, and macrophage reprogramming to the M2 phenotype was facilitated by the hydrogel. Although residual acrylamide monomer was effectively eliminated from CAP hydrogel through rigorous cross-linking and sequential solvent extraction protocols, the inherent toxicity concerns associated with acrylamide-based hydrogels remain non-negligible for biomedical applications. Consequently, future research should prioritize developing reduction strategies for acrylamide content in biomedical hydrogels or exploring alternative monomer systems with enhanced biosafety profiles. Overall, the integration of PGP-1 inhibition and hydrogel provides an effective strategy to promote wound healing through remodeling of the inflammatory immune microenvironment, with potential applicability to other challenging wounds.

Experimental section

Inhibition activity of CA toward PGP-1 PGP-1 (0.05 U/L) was preincubated with various concentrations of CA at 37 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, the substrate for PGP-1 (L-pyroglutamic acid 2-naphthylamide) was introduced to achieve a final concentration of 2.5 µM, and the reaction mixture was further incubated at 37 °C for an additional 40 min. Absorbance measurements were obtained using a TU-1900 spectrophotometer, and enzyme activity was quantified based on the corresponding standard curve. The IC50 values for CA against PGP-1 were determined to assess its inhibitory potency.

Western Blots Assays: All western blot analyses were carried out as follows: Protein samples were prepared and quantified using the BCA assay, with 5–10 µg of protein loaded per lane for each experiment. Protein transfer onto membranes was conducted at a constant current of 250 mA, with transfer durations adjusted according to the molecular weight of the target proteins. Membranes were subsequently blocked for approximately 15–25 min. Visualization of the blots was accomplished using the Amersham ImageQuant™ 800 system (Cytiva).

Molecular Docking: The crystal structure of PGP-1 in complex with pyroglutamic acid (PDB ID: 5Z48) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and processed using the Protein Preparation Wizard in the Schrödinger software package. Ligand structures were generated with the LigPrep module. Hydrogen atoms were added and bond orders were adjusted for the protein residues, followed by energy minimization with the OPLS3 force field to eliminate steric clashes. A grid box measuring 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å was defined around the protein’s active site. Docking simulations were performed using Schrödinger’s Ligand Docking protocol, and PyMOL was used for visualization and analysis of the docking results.

Cytokines testing and RT-PCR Cytokine levels in cell culture supernatants were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. For quantitative RT-PCR analysis, RAW264.7 cells and siRNA-transfected cells (5 × 104 cells/mL) were exposed to LPS (1.0 µg/mL) for 24 h. Total RNA was then extracted with an RNA isolation kit and reverse transcribed into cDNA. RT-PCR was performed using GAPDH as the internal reference gene. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Relative mRNA expression was quantified using the 2-ΔΔCT method.

Polarization of macrophages experiments in vitro RAW 264.7 macrophages were plated in 24-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) in the absence or presence of CA (10 µM) for 8 h. For flow cytometry analysis, cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) and analyzed on a Fusion flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). In parallel, macrophage polarization was also assessed by immunofluorescence/confocal imaging. After treatment, cells were immunostained with iNOS (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) (4 °C overnight), and fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Preparation and Characterization of CA-APBA Complex: 120 mg (0.36 mmol) CA in 5 mL methanol and 138 mg APBA (0.82 mmol) in 5 mL methanol was mixed under vigorous stirring, for 5 min and rest for 15 min to drive complex formation. The ultimate molar ratio of CA to APBA was 1:2. To confirm the formation of CA-APBA complex, ARS was chosen as the fluorescent indicator. ARS will exhibit fluorescence intensity when binding to APBA. 50 µL 0.5 mM ARS was incubated with different concentrations of APBA. The fluorescence intensity of the mixtures at 595 nm was measured using the fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-6000, Shimadzu, Japan) with Ex = 468 nm and Em = 595 nm. The binding efficiency between CA and APBA was further confirmed by ARS fluorescence assay, CA and APBA of different molar ratio (1:5 − 1:0) were mixed with ARS solution and the absorption spectrogram scanned by the fluorescence spectrophotometer. Characterization of the CA-APBA complex was performed using FT-IR spectroscopy.

Preparation of CAP hydrogel The CAP hydrogel was prepared by the one-pot radical copolymerization method [59]. Briefly, CA-APBA complex with different concentrations was dispersed in AM solution. TEMED (0.3 wt % of the AM monomers), APS (1.8 wt % of the AM monomers), and BIS (0.18 wt % of the AM monomers) was added to initiate the polymerization. The gel time was determined by the bottle inversion method [60]. Then the hydrogels were purified by immersing them in PBS and 75% ethanol solution and swelled to the equilibrium state [61].

Characterization of CAP Hydrogel: SEM: To observe the microstructures of CAP hydrogel, the freeze-dried CAP hydrogels were cut into pieces to spray gold for 90 s, and the surface morphology was observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM, JEOL, Japan) at acceleration voltage of 20.0 kV. To test the tensile strength, the hydrogel was prepared in a dumbbell type mold with the width of 1.5 mm and a thickness of 1.3 mm. the hydrogel was fixed on the clamps of universal material testing machine (5300, Instron, America) and the gap between the clamps was 17 mm with loading rate of 50 mm/min to simulate average human skin expansion rate during joint movement [62]. Rheological test: The dynamic viscoelasticity of the hydrogel were analyzed with a rheometer (MCR702, Anton Paar, Austria). The hydrogel was prepared with a width of 20 mm and a thickness of 1 mm and placed on the lower parallel plate of the rheometer at 37 °C. The frequency sweep range was set as 0.1–100 rad/s under 1% strain to assess structural stability under shear oscillation. Continuous step strain test was used to confirm the rheological self-healing behavior of the hydrogel. First, the value of the critical strain was determined by the strain amplitude sweep (1%‒7000%), and the network collapsed when the strain was 5000%. Next, the hydrogel was treated with a high strain (γ = 5000%, frequency = 1 Hz) for 300 s, followed by a low strain (γ = 10%, frequency = 1 Hz) for 300 s. The storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’) were recorded and investigated for 4 cycles. Swelling: The swelling performance of CAP hydrogel was performed as follows [63]: the hydrogel was soaked in 0.01 M PBS (pH = 7.4 and pH = 5.0), and the swelling degree was tested at different time point until swelling equilibrium had been reached. Swelling degree (%) = (Wm - W0)/W0*100%, where W0 is the initial weight of the hydrogel and Wm is the weight of the swelled sample measured at different time points. Drug release: Briefly, 1 mL hydrogel was immersed in a 10 mL release solution (0.01 M PBS, pH = 7.4 and pH = 5.0) and incubated in a shaker at the speed of 100 rpm at 37 °C. The released CA were quantified at different time point by UV-Vis spectrometer (UV-1900, Shimadzu, Japan).

In Vitro Cytotoxicity and Biocompatibility: Hemocompatibility of the CAP hydrogel was performed by hemolysis test [5]. Briefly, red blood cells were collected from anticoagulated rat whole blood by centrifugation (3000 rpm for 10 min), washed 3 times with 0.9% NaCl buffer until the supernatant was clear, and then diluted to final concentration (5%, v/v). 500 µL of the cell suspension and 500 µL of the 0.9% NaCl were gently added to the prepared CAP hydrogel and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with the shaking speed of 100 rpm. The 0.1% Triton X-100 and 0.9% NaCl containing cells were positive and negative controls, respectively. After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm was measured by a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA). The following formula is used to count the hemolysis ratio: Hemolysis ratio (%) = (ODsample - ODnegative)/(ODpositive - ODnegatvie) × 100%.

The cell viability of CAP hydrogel was tested by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8). Briefly, L929 cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 3000 cells/well and incubated for 12 h for cell adhere. The freeze-dried CAP hydrogel (200 mg) was sterilized by UV for 24 h and then immersed in 20 mL DMEM for 24 h and filtered with a 0.22 μm micro filter before use. Hydrogel extracts of different concentrations were added and incubated for 1, 2, and 3 day. At set time point, the culture medium was removed and 100 µL fresh culture medium containing 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well (be careful not to generate bubbles), and the culture plate was incubated in the incubator for 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (CMax Plus, Molecular Devices, America).

Live/dead cell assay was performed to further evaluate the biocompatibility of CAP hydrogel. The L929 cells were seeded into confocal laser dishes at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. After cell adherence, CAP hydrogel extracts were incubated with the cells for 1 and 3 day. Calcein-AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Dalian Bergolin Biotechnology Co., Ltd) was applied to determine the live/dead staining. Then, the wells were observed by laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM, A1R HD25, Nikon, Japan). Untreated cells and PAM hydrogel extracts treated cells were served as controls.

Antibacterial activity The antibacterial activity of hydrogel against Staphylococcus aureus and Clostridium perfringens was tested by inhibition zone method. The bacterial suspension was spread evenly onto a fresh Luria-Bertani medium agar plate (clostridial medium for Clostridium perfringens) and incubated with CAP hydrogel (diameter of 20 mm) and CA paper discs (diameter of 6 mm) at 37 °C for 24 h and the inhibition zone was observed.

Antioxidant Test: For DPPH assay, First, 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazine (DPPH) assay was used to evaluate the free radical scavenging ability of the hydrogels [64]. Briefly, 10 µL fresh hydrogel was ground and mixed with the freshly prepared 0.1 mM DPPH ethanol solution and incubated for 30 min in the dark, the absorption of OD517 nm was recorded. The degradation of DPPH was calculated based on the following equation: Scavenging% = (ODBlank - ODHydrogel)/ODblank, OD Blank and OD Hydrogel are the absorption intensity of the blank and the hydrogel at 517 nm, respectively.

For ABTS assay, 2,2’-Azinobis-(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulphonate) (ABTS) radicals were firstly prepared by mixing 2 mM ABTS with 2.47 mM K2S2O8 in PBS and reacted in dark for 4 h at room temperature [65]. The obtained ABTS solution was diluted with deionized water 20 times with PBS to an appropriate concentration, 3 mL diluent was mixed thoroughly with hydrogel ground and incubated in the dark for 30 min. The diluent ABTS radical solution was defined as blank. the UV-vis absorption at 734 nm was recorded to calculate the ABTS radical scavenging ratio. The degradation of ABTS was calculated based on the following equation: Scavenging% = (ODBlank - ODHydrogel)/ODblank.

The intracellular ROS level was investigated using Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit based on the fluorescent dye DCFH-DA. L929cells (5*104 cells/well) were seeded in a Confocal dish and incubated for 12 h. cells were first treated with 1 mL DCFH-DA (10 µM) in serum-free medium and incubated for 30 min in the dark, remove the DCFH-DA and washed for 3 times with serum-free medium. The cells were disposed with CAP hydrogel extracts and Rosup (50 µg/mL) (serum-free medium) and incubated for 30 min. Cells with only Rosup treatment were used as positive control and cells with no treatment were used as negative control. Cell images were recorded with CLSM.

In Vivo Wound Healing Experiments: Age-matched C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, 20–25 g, male) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Company (Beijing, China). All animal protocols in this study were approved by the Ethical Committee of Qingdao University (QDU-AEC-2025749). C57BL/6J mice were acclimatized for 1 week before any treatment. Mice were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital. Deep burn wound model was created using a constant temperature and pressure scald apparatus (1.5 cm) on the shaved back. Immediately after the scald, 5 mL of Ringer’s lactate solution was intraperitoneally injected to prevent shock. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 5): CAP hydrogel dressing, Tegaderm film, or no dressing. Dressings were replaced every 5 days. This interval was chosen based on the in vitro release profile, in which CA release from the CAP hydrogel approached a plateau at ~120 h (Fig. 3d), to maintain sustained on-site delivery over the treatment course. Wounds were photographed on days 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20, and wound area was quantified using ImageJ.

Histology and immunohistochemistry On day 20, regenerated skin tissues were excised and processed for histological analysis. Tissue samples were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm and subjected to H&E as well as MTS. All stained sections were examined and imaged using a DMi8 microscope (Leica, Germany). In addition, skin wound tissues collected on day 20 were subjected to immunofluorescence staining of CD31 to assess neovascularization. Separately, skin wound tissues were harvested three days after surgery for immunofluorescence staining of F4/80 and CD163 to evaluate macrophage phenotype.

Statistical analysis Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two-tailed Student’s t test. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. For analyses involving two factors, two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was applied. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance was denoted as ns, not significant; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.005.
