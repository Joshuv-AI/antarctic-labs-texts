A standardized rehabilitation protocol following two-stage surgery for nonunion of lateral humeral condyle fracture with cubitus valgus in children: functional and radiographic outcomes
Authors: Fei Su, Yuze Dang, Min Li, Jining Qu, Bo Wang, Xiaowei Wang, Qingda Lu, Yishan Ma, Yating Yang, Qiang Jie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTreating nonunion of the lateral humeral condyle with cubitus valgus deformity in children is challenging. While various surgical strategies exist, the role of a structured, standardized rehabilitation protocol in achieving optimal functional outcomes remains underexplored. This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the clinical and functional results of a two-stage surgical strategy (primary in situ fixation with bone grafting followed by secondary corrective osteotomy) coupled with a standardized, closely monitored rehabilitation protocol for pediatric lateral humeral condyle nonunion with cubitus valgus.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on nine pediatric patients with humeral lateral condyle nonunion combined with cubitus valgus deformity who were treated at Honghui Hospital, Xi’an Jiaotong University, from January 2016 to March 2020. All underwent the two-stage surgery. In stage I, we performed debridement of the nonunion site, autologous iliac bone grafting, and in situ fixation using cannulated compression screws. Once bone union was achieved and elbow joint function had recovered, we performed corrective supracondylar medial closing wedge osteotomy to address the cubitus valgus deformity in stage II. The key focus of this study is the detailed, phase-specific rehabilitation protocol implemented after each stage: immobilization followed by graduated active-assisted and active range-of-motion exercises post-Stage I, and progressive resistance training post-Stage II. Elbow range of motion (ROM), carrying angle, and Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS) were recorded and compared between the preoperative period and the final follow-up to evaluate treatment outcomes.ResultsAll patients were followed up postoperatively, with a mean duration of 45.5 months (range: 30–66 months). The mean time to union after the first-stage surgery for nonunion of lateral condyle fractures was 81.3 days (range: 55–120 days). All osteotomy sites in the second-stage corrective surgery achieved union, with an mean healing time of 51.1 days (range: 45–60 days). The elbow ROM improved significantly from a preoperative mean of 128.0° ± 4.6° to 138.5° ± 5.4° at the final follow-up (P < 0.001). The mean carrying angle of the unaffected side was 5.5° (range: 3°–8°). On the affected side, the carrying angle significantly improved from 31.8° ± 4.7° (range: 25°–42°) preoperatively to 4.0° ± 2.2° (range: 1°–8°) at final follow-up (P < 0.05). The MEPS improved from a preoperative mean of 58.5 ± 6.5 (4 fair, 5 poor) to 96.2 ± 3.4 (7 excellent, 2 good) at final follow-up (P < 0.05), indicating significant functional recovery. No avascular necrosis occurred. Transient hardware irritation occurred in one patient (11.1%).ConclusionThe two-stage surgical approach, when integrated with a standardized, phased rehabilitation protocol, is an effective strategy for managing pediatric lateral humeral condyle nonunion with cubitus valgus, yielding significant functional improvements and reproducible outcomes.

Background

Lateral humeral condyle fracture are common elbow fractures in children [1, 2], second only in incidence to supracondylar fractures of the humerus. As intra-articular fractures, improper early treatment often leads to nonunion or malunion, resulting in elbow pain, limited mobility, and joint instability. Progressive cubitus valgus deformity and delayed ulnar nerve palsy are the most severe complications of nonunion in children [3]. Treating nonunion of the lateral humeral condyle with cubitus valgus deformity is particularly challenging, and there remains ongoing controversy regarding the optimal treatment strategy. Some scholars believe that surgical intervention may reduce elbow range of motion (ROM) or lead to serious complications such as avascular necrosis (AVN) of the lateral condyle [4, 5]. However, other studies have reported good outcomes with surgical intervention, suggesting that success largely depends on appropriate surgical indications and continuous improvement in surgical techniques [5–9]. Currently, there is no consensus on whether a single-stage or staged surgical approach should be used. Previous literature reports various surgical strategies, each with its own advantages, disadvantages, and technical challenges. While surgical technique is paramount, postoperative rehabilitation is crucial for optimizing functional recovery. However, detailed descriptions of rehabilitation protocols are often lacking in the literature, making it difficult to compare outcomes and establish best practices. This study retrospectively analyzes nine pediatric patients treated with a consistent two-stage surgical approach. The primary novel aspect of this report is the detailed description and outcome analysis of a standardized, closely supervised rehabilitation protocol applied after each surgical stage. We hypothesize that this structured rehabilitation is instrumental in achieving the favorable functional outcomes observed, complementing the surgical principles of in situ fixation and staged correction.

Materials and methods

General information

Inclusion criteria: (1) clear history of trauma; (2) nonunion and cubitus valgus deformity following conservative treatment; (3) cubitus valgus deformity ≥ 20°; (4) open distal humeral epiphyseal plate; (5) follow-up > 12 months.

Exclusion criteria: (1) cubitus valgus deformity not caused by trauma; (2) single-stage surgery; (3) concurrent elbow infection; (4) underlying conditions such as osteogenesis imperfecta or other metabolic bone diseases; (5) incomplete radiographic data.

According to the above criteria, a total of nine pediatric cases of lateral humeral condyle nonunion combined with cubitus valgus were treated in our hospital between January 2016 and March 2020 (Table 1). Among them, there were 6 males and 3 females, aged 6 to 14 years, with an average age of 10 years. The left elbow was affected in 6 cases, and the right elbow in 3 cases. The interval from injury to surgery ranged from 2 to 6 years, with an average of 3.67 years.

Preoperative clinical characteristics of the children

Causes of injury included bicycle falls (3 cases), stair falls (3 cases), and sports-related elbow injuries (3 cases). All 9 patients had isolated lateral condyle fractures of the humerus, with no associated injuries.

Clinical presentation and preoperative evaluation

In all nine patients, a visible prominence was observed on the lateral side of the elbow, accompanied by varying degrees of cubitus valgus deformity, with carrying angles ranging from 25° to 42°. Among them, 6 cases experienced significant pain during elbow flexion and extension. The mean ROM of the affected elbow was 128.0° ± 4.6°, with three cases exhibiting severe limitations in elbow mobility. Five patients avoided using the affected elbow due to joint instability. Two cases presented with intermittent numbness in the ring and little fingers. Preoperative assessments included elbow appearance, presence of pain, elbow range of motion, carrying angles of both sides, joint stability, and ulnar nerve symptoms. The Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS) was used to evaluate clinical outcomes [4]. According to the MEPS, 90–100 points are considered excellent, 75–89 points as good, 60–74 points as fair and 0–59 points as poor results.

Preoperative imaging

Imaging examinations included anteroposterior and lateral X-rays of the affected elbow, which showed displacement or rotation of the avulsed lateral condyle fragment of the humerus in an outward and downward direction. Preoperative routine imaging also included full-length anteroposterior and lateral X-rays of both upper limbs in maximal extension and supination. On the affected side, these revealed an increased carrying angle, indicating a valgus deformity of the elbow. Carrying angles were measured on full-extension AP radiographs, correcting for flexion contractures by aligning the humeral and ulnar shafts. In some patients, partial dislocation of the humeroradial joint was observed due to displacement of the lateral condyle of the humerus. Preoperative 3D CT scans confirmed complete lack of bony union at the fracture site in 6 cases, and ectopic pseudo-bony union in 3 cases. Meanwhile, 3D CT quantified pseudoarthrosis volume and guided graft sizing.

Surgical techniques

Under general anesthesia, patients were positioned supine with the affected arm abducted on a side table. A pneumatic tourniquet was applied.

In Stage I, we performed debridement of the nonunion site, autologous iliac bone grafting, and in-situ compression fixation with cannulated compression screws.

A lateral approach to the elbow was used to exposed the nonunion site between the lateral condyle of the humerus and the proximal metaphysis. Fibrous tissue that impeded bone healing was carefully removed, and the bone ends were freshened to create bleeding surfaces without damaging the articular cartilage. Two Kirschner wires (K-wires) were used for temporary fixation, maximizing contact between the bone fragments and ensuring a full range of elbow motion. An autologous iliac bone graft was harvested from the ipsilateral side and trimmed to fit the gap. A wedge-shaped bone graft was then inserted between the metaphysis and the lateral condyle fragment. One or two cannulated screws were used to apply compression and fix the fragment to the distal humeral metaphysis. Additional K-wires were used if necessary to achieve rigid fixation. The elbow range of motion and the stability of the fracture site were checked, the wound was irrigated, and closure was done in layers.

In Stage II, supracondylar medial closing wedge osteotomy of the humerus was performed. In cases with ulnar nerve palsy, anterior transposition of the ulnar nerve was done simultaneously.

A medial approach to the elbow was made. Under C-arm fluoroscopic guidance, a medial closing wedge osteotomy was performed 0.5 cm above the olecranon fossa. Care was taken to ensure sufficient contact between the osteotomy surfaces. The medial cortex of the distal humerus was removed to promote osteotomy healing. After osteotomy, fixation was done using K-wires or a plate. The position of the K-wires or plate and the correction of the deformity were checked. Fluoroscopy was used again to confirm the accuracy of internal fixation and the correction of the valgus deformity.

Postoperative rehabilitation protocol

A standardized, phased rehabilitation protocol was strictly followed for all patients:

Stage I (Following In Situ Fixation): Postoperatively, a long-arm plaster splint was applied to the upper limb to immobilize the elbow at approximately 90° of flexion with the forearm in a neutral position for 4 to 6 weeks. At 6 weeks after surgery, an X-ray was taken to confirm callus formation. Upon radiographic union (typically at 6–8 weeks), the cast is removed and active-assisted elbow flexion and extension exercises are initiated, to be performed by parents under professional guidance with an emphasis on a gentle, gradual recovery of motion. The goal is to achieve a functional arc of motion (approximately 30°–130°) before Stage II surgery can be considered.

Stage II (Following Corrective Osteotomy): The post-operative protocol consists of long-arm cast immobilization for the initial 4 weeks, followed by supervised, progressive resistance and strengthening exercises with a physiotherapist from weeks 5 to 8 to regain full range of motion and improve muscle strength, transitioning to continued home-based exercises with periodic clinical reviews beyond week 8.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Patients underwent scheduled postoperative evaluations at 4, 8, and 12 weeks, then at 3, 6, and 12 months, and biennially until skeletal maturity. At each visit, we systematically documented the carrying angle, range of motion (ROM), MEPS and any procedure-related complications. Two independent orthopedic physicians, performed all carrying angle measurements with excellent interobserver reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.91). Standardized anteroposterior and lateral radiographs were obtained to assess healing progression. Figure 1 illustrates a representative clinical case.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 23.0 software was used to process the data and measurement data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was first used to assess the normality of the continuous data. Range of elbow motion, carrying angle, and MEPS scores were found to be normally distributed with homogeneity of variance, and were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (± s). Paired t-tests were used to compare these measurements between the preoperative and final follow-up time points. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 9 children underwent surgery and were followed up postoperatively, with a mean follow-up duration of 45.5 months (range: 30–66 months). After the first-stage surgery, the mean time to healing of the lateral humeral condyle nonunion was 81.3 days (range: 55–120 days). Following the second-stage surgery, no delayed unions or nonunions occurred at osteotomy sites, with a mean healing time of 51.1 days (range: 45–60 days). The adherence to the rehabilitation protocol was excellent, with all patients progressing through the prescribed exercise regimens as scheduled. All hardware was electively removed at a mean of 5.2 ± 1.1 months postoperatively following confirmed radiographic union. One patients (11.1%) reported transient hardware irritation, resolved after screw removal. The ROM of elbow improved significantly from 128.0° ± 4.6° preoperatively to 138.5° ± 5.4° at final follow-up (t = 36.365, P < 0.001). The mean carrying angle on the unaffected side was 5.5° (range: 3°–8°). On the affected side, the carrying angle decreased from 31.8° ± 4.7° preoperatively (range: 25°–42°) to 4.0° ± 2.2° at final follow-up (range: 1°–8°), showing a statistically significant difference (t = 31.926, P < 0.001). Discussion

Lateral humeral condyle fracture in children account for 16.9% of distal humeral fractures and are classified as Salter-Harris Type IV epiphyseal injuries, meaning they are intra-articular fractures. High-energy trauma can disrupt the blood supply to the lateral condyle of the humerus, and the infiltration of synovial fluid into the joint cavity can interfere with callus formation at the fracture site. In addition, traction from the extensor tendons can lead to displacement of the fracture fragment, further impairing healing. These factors are considered the intrinsic causes of nonunion. According to Flynn’s criteria, a lateral condyle fracture that fails to heal within 12 weeks is considered a nonunion. In cases of nonunion, the lateral condyle shifts proximally relative to the medial condyle, resulting in asymmetry of the articular surface of the distal humerus. This leads to incongruity of the humeroradial joint, which can cause instability and pain during elbow flexion and extension [11], as well as delayed ulnar nerve palsy [3].

The treatment of lateral condyle nonunion remains controversial, with surgical intervention carrying risks such as AVN, reduced ROM, and recurrent nonunion [5, 12, 13]. Attempting anatomical reduction of an old lateral condyle fracture during surgery inevitably disrupts the blood supply to the bone fragment, potentially leading to AVN, which can further compromise elbow function postoperatively. As a result, surgery-related complications have limited the widespread adoption of surgical interventions in such cases. Nevertheless, for patients experiencing significant pain or instability, surgical intervention can offer substantial clinical benefits in terms of joint stabilization and symptom relief.

Several studies have noted that prolonged nonunion significantly increases the technical difficulty of fracture fragment reduction. The surgical procedure itself substantially elevates the risk of complications, ultimately compromising treatment outcomes [4, 8]. For chronic lateral humeral condyle fractures with more than 3 weeks’ duration, Jakob et al. even do not recommend surgical reduction [4]. Shimada et al. [6] reported the outcomes of 16 cases with nonunion of lateral humeral condyle fractures, with an 11-year follow-up. Among them, 15 cases achieved excellent or good results, while 1 case had poor outcome due to evident avascular necrosis of the bone fragment. The authors emphasized that bone grafting serves as a fundamental step to fill bone defects, correct articular incongruity, and promote fracture healing. During surgery, it is crucial to preserve the blood supply to the lateral condyle in order to prevent osteonecrosis. Recent literature has demonstrated satisfactory outcomes in treating nonunion of lateral humeral condyle fractures in children [5, 8, 14]. These studies highlight the importance of strict patient selection and meticulous protection of the blood supply to the fracture fragments during surgery, which significantly reduces the incidence of AVN.

To minimize iatrogenic disruption of the blood supply, several authors have recommended in situ fixation of the bone fragment [15], while others suggest non-anatomical reduction of the fragment [8], or selecting patients with minimal displacement for treatment [9]. According to Flynn’s criteria, the surgical indications for old humeral lateral condyle fracture nonunion include: ① a large fracture fragment involving the metaphysis; ② displacement less than 1 cm from the joint surface; ③ an open lateral condylar epiphysis with preserved blood supply. During surgery, it is essential to remove the fibrous tissue between the fracture fragments and use a small curette to create a fresh, bleeding surface to promote healing. It is critical to avoid extensive soft tissue dissection, especially posterior soft tissues, which should not be disturbed, as anatomical studies have shown that the blood supply to the lateral condyle originates from this area [16, 17]. Based on this, our surgical approach prioritized functional stability over anatomical perfection, focusing on fracture union first and correcting cosmetic deformities second. This stepwise strategy created a favorable biomechanical environment to facilitate fracture healing while minimizing risks of further deformity or neurological complications. While staged surgeries increased total hospitalization compared to single-stage reports [8], the lower AVN rate (0% vs. 12%) justifies this approach [6].

In this series, one to two compression screws should be used for strong internal fixation, taking care to avoid inserting screws into the olecranon fossa, which can lead to postoperative limitation of motion. Cannulated screws provide stability and compression at the fracture site, thereby promoting fracture healing and allowing for early functional exercise [18, 19]. In contrast, Kirschner wires do not provide compression and only serve as auxiliary fixation. Using K-wires alone may result in delayed union, and delayed removal of the K-wires can lead to pin tract infections, which in severe cases may cause loss of reduction and fixation [20].

Progressive cubitus valgus may exacerbate elbow instability and contribute to delayed-onset ulnar nerve palsy. Successful union of the fracture site reduces valgus progression and may facilitate ulnar nerve recovery. It should be emphasized that bone grafting at the fracture site is essential. It not only serves as a bridge between the fragments, maintaining joint integrity and increasing the healing rate, but also helps to avoid disruption of the blood supply, thus preventing AVN. Non-vascularized iliac grafts healed via creeping substitution, avoiding AVN by preserving the lateral condyle’s posterior blood supply [16]. Postoperative plaster immobilization is necessary. In old lateral condyle fractures, contracture of the extensor tendons and distal displacement of the bone fragment after reduction can worsen the contracture. Therefore, the elbow should be immobilized in less than 90 degrees of flexion, which is more conducive to postoperative functional exercises. In this study, no AVN was observed on X-ray during follow-up, suggesting the surgical technique successfully preserved blood supply.

In this study, we used the classic medial closing wedge supracondylar osteotomy of the humerus to correct the cubitus valgus deformity. The long-term lateral condylar nonunion had created significant valgus torque due to lateral joint instability and dense fibrous tissue formation [12]. Therefore, we performed a medial wedge osteotomy in the second stage, which further stabilized the elbow joint and minimized the risk of loss of correction. After the osteotomy, the distal humerus was pushed laterally, which avoided lateral deviation of the humeral mechanical axis. This ensured better alignment between the proximal and distal osteotomy segments, thereby increasing mechanical stability and the healing rate of the osteotomy.

Our surgical approach prioritized fracture union and stability, deferring angular correction to a second stage. However, we posit that the consistently favorable outcomes in this series are not solely attributable to the surgical technique but are significantly enhanced by the implementation of a standardized rehabilitation protocol. While previous studies have discussed surgical strategies for similar conditions [21], our work provides a detailed emphasis on the postoperative rehabilitation process. The protocol was designed to protect the healing construct initially (immobilization) and then proactively restore function (graduated exercises). The 10° mean improvement in ROM exceeds the minimal clinically important difference for pediatric elbow function [10], and the absence of significant postoperative stiffness underscores the protocol’s effectiveness. This focus on systematic rehabilitation addresses a gap in the literature and contributes a reproducible model for patient management.

The low complication rate, particularly the 0% incidence of AVN, supports the safety of our combined surgical and rehabilitative approach. The staged strategy, though increasing overall treatment duration, may reduce the biological insult at each stage, potentially contributing to this safety profile.

Conclusions

The two-stage surgical strategy for pediatric lateral humeral condyle nonunion with cubitus valgus, when supported by a standardized, closely monitored rehabilitation protocol, proves to be a reliable and effective treatment, resulting in significant radiographic correction, excellent functional recovery, and a low complication rate. Future studies should prospectively evaluate the specific contribution of rehabilitation protocols to overall outcomes in similar complex pediatric elbow conditions.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective design, small sample size, and relatively short follow-up period. The absence of a control group limits the ability to generalize the outcomes. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up are necessary to validate the efficacy and safety of the proposed two-stage approach.
