Investigating the causal relationship between inflammatory bowel disease and simple appendicitis using Mendelian randomization
Authors: Yawei Zhang, Lichao Yang, Lianwen Yuan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The relationship between simple appendicitis and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is not clear. In this study, we approach the issue from a genetic perspective, using Mendelian randomization (MR) tools to explore the potential causal connection between the two. We used GWAS data from 12,882 IBD patients (21,770 controls), 5956 crohn’s disease (CD) patients (14,927 controls), 6968 ulcerative colitis (UC) patients (20,464 controls), and 4604 simple appendicitis patients (481,880 controls). These statistical data were derived from a large-scale whole-genome association study of individuals with European ancestry. The primary analytical method for inferring the causal relationship between the conditions involved the use of the Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW) method as the main approach for bidirectional MR analysis. The MR analysis results predicted IBD was associated with a lower risk of simple appendicitis (OR: 0.947 (0.911, 0.984), p = 0.005). The results for CD (OR: 0.948 (0.916, 0.981), p = 0.002) and UC (OR: 0.954 (0.917, 0.992), p = 0.020) are consistent with this finding. In the reverse MR analysis, there is no significant association between simple appendicitis and the occurrence of IBD (p > 0.05), and the same holds true for CD and UC (p > 0.05). Our MR study results suggest a potential negative causal effect of IBD on the occurrence of simple appendicitis. Conversely, there does not appear to be a significant causal relationship between simple appendicitis and the risk of developing IBD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74572-5.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a recurrent and incurable inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract, primarily encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC)1. In recent years, with the continuous improvement of people’s living standards, the global incidence of IBD has been on the rise, posing a serious threat to the health and healthcare systems of people worldwide, especially in developing countries2,3.

The appendix is often considered a vestigial organ, but evidence suggests a connection between the occurrence of appendicitis and the onset of IBD, particularly in UC4. Epidemiological studies indicate that appendectomy can reduce the risk of developing UC and even decrease the risk of flare-ups in UC patients5. However, research also suggests that in individuals with or without a family susceptibility to inflammatory bowel disease, appendicitis and mesenteric lymphadenitis in childhood or adolescence are significantly associated with a reduced risk of adult-onset ulcerative colitis, and appendectomy itself does not prevent ulcerative colitis6,7. Additionally, a nationwide cohort study from Sweden suggests a reduced risk of adult IBD (UC and CD) associated with appendectomy performed in childhood8, although appendiceal involvement in CD is uncommon, even in cases of severe ileocecal inflammation9. Due to the complex mechanisms underlying intestinal diseases and the presence of various confounding factors, the association between IBD occurrence and simple appendicitis is not clear.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a recently developed research approach that uses genetic variations identified in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer whether an exposure factor has a causal impact on the outcome10. Several GWAS have identified numerous loci closely related to IBD, providing the possibility to apply MR analysis to investigate the genetic association between IBD and simple appendicitis11,12. This study downloaded data on IBD and simple appendicitis from GWAS databases and conducted MR analysis to further explore the potential causal relationship between the two from a genetic perspective.

Method

Study design

This study was designed as illustrated in the diagram (Fig. 1). The MR analysis investigated the causal relationship between IBD and simple appendicitis. In the forward MR analysis, the exposure factors were the three diseases: IBD, CD, and UC. In the reverse MR analysis, the exposure factor became simple appendicitis. All original studies obtained ethical approval and participant consent, and this study did not require additional ethical approval. This study adheres to the guidelines for transparent reporting of Mendelian Randomization (MR) studies. Specifically, we followed the recommendations outlined in the STROBE-MR (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization) guidelines to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of our MR analysis.

Data sources for IBD

We chose the Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) conducted by the International IBD Genetics Consortium (IIBDGC)11 as the focal point for examining the causal relationship with simple appendicitis. The study involved individuals of European descent. The exposure cohort comprised participants in the comprehensive whole-genome association study, encompassing male and female IBD patients (12,882 cases; 21,770 controls), 5956 CD patients (14,927 controls), and 6968 UC patients (20,464 controls)11. When constructing instrumental variables, we consulted recent publications13, systematically excluding common confounding factors such as smoking, drinking, etc. The eliminated SNPs and their associated traits are elaborated in Supplementary File 1. To mitigate weak instrumental variable bias, we evaluated the impact of SNP-exposure associations’ strength for each SNP using the F-statistic (F = beta/se)13,14.

Data sources for appendicitis

We chose the UK Biobank study as the exposure cohort (https://pan.ukbb.broadinstitute.org/) because it contains a substantial amount of GWAS data on simple appendicitis. In the ieu-b-4967 dataset, the study participants are of European descent and include 4604 cases of both male and female patients with simple appendicitis (481,880 controls). When constructing instrumental variables, a p-value < 5e-06 was chosen as the SNP selection criterion to ensure obtaining a greater number of SNPs for subsequent analysis.

Final selection of IVs

After excluding SNPs associated with confounding factors, we retained SNPs that were robustly associated with IBD and not with confounders. These final SNPs were used as instrumental variables in our Mendelian Randomization analysis. By systematically excluding SNPs associated with common confounding factors, we aimed to ensure that our IVs met the assumptions of relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction, thereby strengthening the validity of our causal inferences. We have provided a detailed list of the SNPs used as instrumental variables, including their associations with both the exposure and potential confounding factors, in the supplementary material. This includes information on the statistical significance of each association.

Assessment of subject overlap

In conducting our Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis, it is crucial to consider the potential for subject overlap between the GWAS datasets used for exposure (Inflammatory Bowel Disease, IBD) and outcome (simple appendicitis). Subject overlap can introduce bias and affect the validity of causal inferences. We determined that there is no explicit indication of overlapping subjects between the IIBDGC and the appendicitis GWAS datasets based on the consortium documentation and cohort descriptions. However, we acknowledge that a small degree of overlap may be possible but is likely to be minimal given the large sample sizes and the distinct recruitment strategies typically employed by these consortia. The potential for minimal subject overlap is unlikely to significantly bias our results, as the primary sources of bias in MR studies are weak instrument bias and horizontal pleiotropy. We have taken steps to mitigate these biases through the use of robust instrumental variables and sensitivity analyses, such as MR-PRESSO and other pleiotropy-adjustment methods.

MR analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the “TwoSampleMR” and “MRPRESSO” packages in R language (version 4.2.0). The code used for the analysis can be found in Supplementary File 2. The Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW) method was employed as the primary estimation method to assess the causal relationship between exposure and outcome. Additionally, Weighted Median (WM), MR-Egger, Weighted Mode, and Simple Mode methods were used for MR analysis. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). To assess the robustness of our study results, Cochran’s Q statistic was used to evaluate heterogeneity for individual SNPs. When significant heterogeneity was present in MR results, correction was performed using the IVW random-effects model15.

The MR-PRESSO method, addressing pleiotropy and outliers, was employed to assess and adjust for horizontal pleiotropy by detecting and removing outliers16. In the presence of significant pleiotropy, the MR-PRESSO method was used to identify and correct for outlier SNPs, providing adjusted OR and CI. A p-value < 0.05 indicated significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy between SNPs. Additionally, leave-one-out (LOO) analysis was conducted as a sensitivity analysis, systematically excluding each SNP to examine the potential impact of individual SNPs on MR estimates17.

Results

Genetic instruments and strength

In this study, we computed the F statistics for each instrument, resulting in 167, 154, and 111 SNPs selected as instruments for IBD, CD, and UC, respectively. The genetic instruments used demonstrated considerable accuracy. Detailed information about the SNPs serving as instruments for IBD, CD, and UC is provided in Supplementary File 1. When simple appendicitis became the exposure factor, a total of 10 SNPs were identified as instruments for simple appendicitis.

Discovery results of IBD to appendicitis risk

According to the IVW analysis results, in the European population, Our analysis indicates a causal relationship between the occurrence of simple appendicitis and IBD (Table 2). The occurrence of simple appendicitis is negatively correlated with patients with IBD, CD, and UC (Figs. 2 and 3). Additionally, in CD, the results from the Weighted Median (WM) method also support this conclusion. To ensure the reliability of the results, Cochran’s Q test was conducted to examine whether SNPs in IBD, CD, and UC exhibit heterogeneity. The results show a uniform distribution of points in the graph (Fig. 4), indicating no significant heterogeneity. The IVW method yielded p-values greater than 0.05 for both heterogeneity and multiplicity tests, suggesting the absence of horizontal pleiotropy in the IVs (Table 2).

Discovery results of appendicitis to IBD risk

To further investigate the causal relationship between simple appendicitis and IBD, we conducted a reverse MR analysis. Simple appendicitis was set as the exposure factor, and IBD and its two subtypes were set as outcome factors. We employed five methods, including IVW, for the MR analysis. The results revealed that the presence of simple appendicitis did not show a definite association with the occurrence of IBD, CD, and UC (Fig. 7). Additionally, the results from the IVW method suggested the potential presence of heterogeneity among the SNPs in the study (Table 2).

Discussion

Over the years, the association between simple appendicitis and IBD has been a subject of controversy8,18. This study is the first to use two-sample Mendelian Randomization with GWAS summary statistics to study the causal relationships between IBD and appendicitis. Although numerous real-world studies have identified the impact of simple appendicitis or appendectomy on IBD development, our research introduces new insights, particularly for clinicians managing IBD, including surgeons.

In this study, we identified SNPs from large-scale GWAS data in the general population, specifically selected for accurately identifying IBD (including CD and UC) and simple appendicitis. Through MR analysis, we linked the two conditions to uncover their potential relationship. The results indicate, at the genetic level, a reduced likelihood of simple appendicitis occurring in patients with IBD, CD, and UC, while no significant association was observed between simple appendicitis patients and the occurrence of IBD. This finding contrasts with results from real-world studies5.

The appendix is considered an immunological organ related to mucosal immune function and intestinal microbiota stability5. The development of IBD is closely associated with disruptions in intestinal immune regulation and imbalances in intestinal microbial ecology1. Researchers have thus been intrigued by the potential connection between the two. In experimental mouse models, removing the inflamed appendix can prevent colitis18. Observational studies in humans have linked appendectomy to the prevention of ulcerative colitis6,19. The risk of CD increases shortly after appendectomy, potentially due to misdiagnosing CD as appendicitis20. Appendectomy-related increases in CD risk occur only in the short period before a CD diagnosis, possibly reflecting diagnostic bias, suggesting that past appendectomy may not influence the characteristics or course of CD21.

From a biological mechanism perspective, IBD is characterized by chronic inflammation and immune system dysregulation, and this altered immune response could potentially protect against acute appendicitis by preventing the kind of bacterial infection or obstruction that typically triggers appendicitis22. The appendix is involved in immune function, and in IBD patients, the immune activity in the appendix may be altered in a way that reduces the risk of developing appendicitis23. Many IBD patients are treated with immunosuppressive or anti-inflammatory medications. These treatments might also reduce the risk of appendicitis by controlling inflammation and preventing the immune responses that could lead to an appendicitis episode25.

In clinical practice, some CD patients often present with acute appendicitis as the initial symptom26, but in these cases, the appendicitis is usually due to Crohn’s disease involving the appendix rather than simple appendicitis27. National observational studies have shown that appendicitis is significantly associated with a reduced risk of adult-onset ulcerative colitis6, and other studies have found that the history of appendectomy in UC patients is lower than in the general population28. This suggests that the genetic risk of simple appendicitis in UC patients is relatively low. Currently, most studies focus on the risk of developing IBD in patients with appendicitis, but there are no prospective data showing the odds of acute appendicitis in patients after being diagnosed with IBD. This may be related to the pathogenesis of IBD, where chronic inflammation in the gut can influence clinicians’ judgment regarding simple appendicitis.

To date, numerous mechanistic, epidemiological, and intervention studies have explored this association, but the generated data are often ambiguous and sometimes contradictory. Therefore, the exact nature of the relationship between these two conditions remains unknown4,5. The clinical course of IBD is unpredictable, with manifestations including recurrent abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, malnutrition, and the risk of cancer. It involves multiple factors such as genetics, dietary habits, environmental factors, psychological factors, immune system dysregulation, and dysbiosis of the intestinal microbiota29. Because the occurrence of IBD is multifactorial, inferring the exact connection between simple appendicitis and IBD from a genetic perspective, as done in this study, is insufficient and has limitations. Therefore, more basic and prospective research is needed to further confirm these findings. However, our study provides valuable insights for clinicians managing IBD, especially surgeons. Most IBD patients undergo at least one surgical procedure in their lifetime30, and our research found a negative causal effect between the occurrence of CD and simple appendicitis. Therefore, in emergency surgery for simple appendicitis, surgeons not only need to evaluate intestinal lesions but also should give more consideration to whether to remove the appendix. Additionally, for UC patients, caution is advised when considering appendectomy. A recent study has indicated a reverse causation between appendicitis or appendectomy and IBD (particularly UC)31, which is similar to our conclusions. This provides additional data to clarify the relationship between IBD and simple appendicitis.

Our study has certain limitations. Firstly, our research was conducted only in a European population, and the GWAS data used are not the most recent, which may present some lag. Additionally, our study conclusions have not been validated in real-world data and need to be confirmed in subsequent prospective studies.

In conclusion, our MR study reveals a potential negative causal effect between genetically determined IBD and simple appendicitis. However, these findings should be viewed as preliminary and require further validation through additional research.
