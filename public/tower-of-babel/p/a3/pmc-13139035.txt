Hypoxia-induced XBP1s-MYDGF axis suppresses ferroptosis through UBQLN1-mediated stabilization of LCN2 in gastric cancer
Authors: Qi Zhou, Hongyan Qi, Yonghui Zou, Zhenqi Yue, Jinheng Gan, Hesong Xu, Luojun Fan, Hua Wan, Lin Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solid tumors such as gastric cancers exploit hypoxia-induced adaptive mechanisms to evade cell death. Here, we identify a hypoxia-triggered signaling axis in which the spliced form of XBP1s transcriptionally activates MYDGF. We demonstrate that MYDGF competitively binds the ubiquitin adaptor protein UBQLN1 at the STI1-4 domain, thereby blocking UBQLN1-mediated recognition and endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD) of LCN2. Stabilized LCN2 sequesters redox-active iron and inhibits iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, thereby suppressing ferroptosis. Hypoxia promotes the splicing of XBP1s, which directly binds to the MYDGF promoter, increasing its expression. Genetic disruption of this axis sensitizes gastric cancer cells to ferroptosis inducers both in vitro and in vivo. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism of hypoxia-induced ferroptosis resistance and suggest that the XBP1–MYDGF–UBQLN1–LCN2 pathway is a therapeutic target for hypoxic tumors.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most prevalent malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Its aggressive progression is fueled by high molecular heterogeneity and a hypoxic tumor microenvironment (TME), which drives therapeutic resistance and metastasis [2]. Hypoxia, a hallmark of solid tumors, reshapes cellular metabolism, promotes angiogenesis, and suppresses antitumor immunity, creating a permissive niche for malignant evolution [3]. The hypoxic microenvironment participates in the regulation of malignant phenotypes in GC through multiple mechanisms. Hypoxia upregulates HIF-1α to promote the transcription of the acetyltransferase NAT10, which increases the expression of SEPT9 via mRNA modification (ac4C), thereby stabilizing the nuclear localization of HIF-1α and forming a positive feedback loop that drives glycolysis dependency and enhances hypoxia tolerance in GC cells [4]. Additionally, hypoxia-induced HIF-1α/lncRNA-PMAN expression suppresses ferroptosis by promoting the cytoplasmic translocation of ELAVL1 during the peritoneal dissemination of GC [5]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the survival mechanisms of GC cells under hypoxic conditions is crucial.

The importance of hypoxia in regulating ferroptosis has been well-documented in various studies [6, 7]. Notably, hypoxia plays a dual regulatory role in ferroptosis, which is capable of either promoting or suppressing this cell death pathway depending on the cellular context and adaptive response.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of programmed cell death, has emerged as a research hotspot because of its regulatory mechanisms and therapeutic potential. The importance of hypoxia in regulating ferroptosis has been well-documented in various studies [8, 9]. Notably, hypoxia plays a dual regulatory role in ferroptosis, which is capable of either promoting or suppressing this cell death pathway depending on the cellular context and adaptive response. On the one hand, hypoxia can increase ferroptosis susceptibility by upregulating BAP1 expression [10]. On the other hand, prolonged hypoxic adaptation triggers multiple protective mechanisms [6, 7]. However, the dynamic regulatory network of ferroptosis in GC remains incompletely understood and warrants further investigation. Elucidating the mechanisms of ferroptosis resistance in gastric cancer cells within hypoxic microenvironments is pivotal for overcoming barriers to the clinical translation of ferroptosis-inducing therapies.

Myeloid-derived growth factor (MYDGF/C19orf10) was initially identified as a protein secreted by bone marrow-derived monocytes and has attracted considerable attention because of its paradoxical roles in tissue repair and tumor progression [11]. In nonneoplastic diseases, MYDGF has protective effects [12–14]. However, in hepatocellular carcinoma and bladder cancer, MYDGF promotes tumor growth by activating the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways [15]. Intriguingly, hypoxia can significantly induce MYDGF expression, suggesting its potential role in hypoxia adaptation [16]. Despite these advances, the function of MYDGF in gastric cancer, particularly its relationship with ferroptosis, remains unexplored.

LCN2 possesses a highly specific iron-binding capacity, demonstrating a particularly strong affinity for bacterial-derived catechol-type siderophores [17, 18]. Through its lysosome-localized iron homeostasis regulatory network, this molecule can sequester iron-siderophore complexes, thereby maintaining the intracellular iron metabolic balance [19]. Although LCN2 primarily binds catechol compounds (which can chelate Fe³⁺ and subsequently facilitate its conversion to Fe²⁺ to promote ferroptosis), the binding of LCN2 to these siderophores paradoxically inhibits this process [18].

Here, we reveal a hypoxia-driven axis centered on the expression of XBP1s, a key effector of the unfolded protein response (UPR), which transcriptionally activates MYDGF to suppress ferroptosis in gastric cancer. Through integrated single-cell transcriptomics, biochemical assays, and in vivo models, we demonstrate that MYDGF competitively binds UBQLN1, disrupting the endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD)-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of LCN2. Stabilized LCN2 sequesters redox-active iron. Hypoxia amplifies this pathway by inducing the splicing of XBP1s, which directly binds to the MYDGF promoter to increase its expression. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism of hypoxia adaptation in GC with direct therapeutic implications: targeting the XBP1s–MYDGF–LCN2 axis may sensitize hypoxic, therapy-resistant tumor regions to ferroptosis inducers, potentially overcoming a major barrier in GC treatment. These insights help overcome critical barriers to the clinical application of ferroptosis inducers, potentially advancing the treatment paradigm for advanced cancers characterized by hypoxic microenvironments.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA-seq acquisition and processing

To comprehensively analyze the gastric cancer continuum, we integrated two key single-cell RNA-seq datasets from GEO: GSE183904 (gastric cancer) [20] and GSE134520 (precancerous lesions) [21]. Quality control was performed separately on each dataset using Seurat to filter cells (retaining those expressing 300–6000 genes and excluding those with mitochondrial RNA ratios >20%) and genes (expressed in ≥3 cells). Doublets were removed with DoubletFinder [22]. Datasets were then integrated by cell type, and batch effects were corrected using harmony [23]. Marker genes for each subcluster were identified via FindAllMarkers (min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25). Cell types were annotated into nine major classes (Supplementary Table 1) and eight epithelial subtypes (Supplementary Table 2) based on established markers.

Cell culture, hypoxia treatment and transfection

Human GC cell lines AGS and HGC27 (Shanghai Institute of Cell Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences) were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Solarbio) supplemented with 10% FBS (Zeta Life) at 37 °C/5% CO₂. For the hypoxia experiments, the cells were incubated with 1% O₂ for 24 h. All lentiviruses, plasmids, and siRNAs used in this study were purchased from General Biology Co., Ltd. Transfection procedures for lentiviral vectors and plasmids were strictly performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. All shRNA and siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3. All cell lines were recently authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit prior to experiments.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were extracted from gastric cancer cells using ice-cold RIPA buffer (NCM Biotech) supplemented with 1× protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (TargetMol Chemicals, Inc.). Protein concentrations were quantified via a BCA assay (Jiangsu Cowin Biotech). Proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE and electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (Yeasen Biotechnology). The membranes were then blocked in Rapid Blocking Buffer (NCM Biotech) for half an hour and then incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After being washed 3 times with TBST (10 min each time), the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then washed again with TBST three times, after which enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) was performed using an ECL kit (Yeasen Biotechnology). β-actin served as the loading control.

All primary antibodies used for Western blotting were as follows: anti-MYDGF (1:1000, R&D Systems, catalog#MAB1104), anti-β-actin (1:10,000, Proteintech, catalog#66009-1-Ig), anti-LCN2 (1:1000, Abcam, catalog#ab125075), anti-ubiquitin (1:10,000, Santa Cruz, catalog#sc-8017), anti-P62 (1:5000, Proteintech, catalog# 66184-1-Ig), anti-LC3 (1:4000, Proteintech, catalog#81004-1-RR), anti-UBQLN1 (1:1000, CST, catalog#14526), anti-UBQLN2 (1:1000, CST, catalog#85509), anti-Myc (1:1000, Abcam, catalog#ab314108), anti-XBP1s (1:1000, Abcam, catalog# ab220783), anti-Flag (1:3000, Abcam, catalog#ab205606), anti-HA (1:1000, Abcam, catalog#ab314237) and anti-HIF1α (1:1000, Abcam, catalog# ab179483).

EdU assay and cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay

Gastric cancer cells were seeded in 96-well plates at optimized densities (EdU: 2×10⁴/well; CCK-8: 3×10³/well). Cells were pulsed with 50 μM EdU reagent (UElandy) diluted in prewarmed serum-free medium for 2 h, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (30 min), glycine quenching (5 min), and permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 (30 min). Nonspecific sites were blocked with 3% BSA (1 h). Click-iT reaction cocktail containing Alexa Fluor 488-azide was applied for 30 min in the dark, and the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 for 15 min prior to fluorescence imaging.

At designated intervals (6, 24, 48, and 96 h), 10% CCK-8 reagent (APExBIO) was directly added to the existing medium without replacement. After a 2-h incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cell viability was normalized to the 6-h baseline OD values.

Lipid ROS detection

BODIPY 581/591 C11 sensor was prepared as a 10 mM stock solution in DMSO and stored at −20 °C in the dark. Cells seeded in 6-well plates (4×10⁵ cells/well), washed with PBS after adherence, and then incubated with 5 μM probe solution in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. Following incubation, the cells were detached using trypsin, resuspended in PBS, and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. Lipid peroxidation levels were quantified by measuring oxidized-state signals via the FITC channel and comparing the MFI between groups using FlowJo software.

MDA assay

After trypsinization and counting, 1×10⁷ cells were pelleted and washed with PBS. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µl of Antioxidant PBS, followed by the addition of 100 µl of lysis buffer and vortexing for 30 s. After a 5-min incubation at room temperature, 300 µl of freshly prepared working solution was added. The mixture was vortexed, incubated at 95 °C for 15 min with intermittent shaking, and then cooled on ice. After centrifugation at 10,000×g for 10 min, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm. The MDA concentration was determined using a standard curve and normalized to the total protein content quantified by a BCA assay.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis

Following drug treatments, the cells were immersion-fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (buffered in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate, pH 7.2) for 24 h at 4 °C. The samples were then postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in the same buffer for 2 h at room temperature. After fixation, the samples underwent stepwise dehydration in graded ethanol solutions (50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%), followed by infiltration and embedding in Spurr’s epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) were counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and then examined under a transmission electron microscope (TEM) to analyze ultrastructural alterations.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cellular samples using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher). The expression of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), a constitutively expressed housekeeping gene, served as the endogenous control for data normalization. The 2^-ΔΔCt method was employed to calculate the relative expression levels of the target genes. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 8.0.2, with intergroup differences assessed by two-tailed Student’s t test (statistical significance threshold: p < 0.05).

Fe2+ detection

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (10,000 cells/well, triplicates) and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After adhering, the cells were exposed to normoxic or hypoxic conditions for 24 h. The supernatant was then discarded, and the cells were washed three times with prewarmed HBSS. The cells were then incubated with 1 μmol/L FerroOrange working solution for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescence imaging was performed immediately after incubation without further washing, using filters optimized for FerroOrange detection.

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

The cells were lysed using precooled IP lysis buffer supplemented with 1× protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the cell-containing supernatant. Protein A/G magnetic beads were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C for 2 h. After magnetic separation, the supernatant was discarded, and the beads were collected. The beads were then incubated with the cell lysate supernatant overnight at 4 °C under gentle agitation. The immunoprecipitated complex was pelleted, resuspended in 2× SDS sample buffer, and denatured by boiling at 95–100 °C for 10 min. The samples were analyzed by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence staining assay

Following passaging, the cells were resuspended and seeded onto sterile coverslips in 12-well plates at 1000–5000 cells/well. After 24 h of culture (37 °C, 5% CO₂), the cells were washed with cold PBS (3×3 min), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (500 μL, 15 min), and washed again with PBS. Permeabilization was performed using 0.5% Triton X-100 (500 μL, 10 min), followed by washing with PBS. Nonspecific sites were blocked with 2% BSA (500 μL, 37 °C, 30 min). Primary antibody (diluted in 1% BSA per the manufacturer’s protocol) was applied overnight at 4 °C, with PBS as a control. After the cells were washed with PBS, they were incubated with species-matched fluorescent secondary antibodies (2 h, dark). Coverslips were then washed, mounted with DAPI-containing anti-fade medium (50 μL), and imaged by confocal microscopy.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP‒qPCR)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed on gastric cancer cell lysates. After crosslinking with 1% formaldehyde (quenched with 0.125 M glycine), the chromatin was sonicated to generate 200–1000 bp fragments. The lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with anti-XBP1s antibody or control IgG, after which the input DNA was used for normalization. Antibody complexes were captured with Protein A magnetic beads, washed with salt buffers, and eluted. The crosslinks were reversed (65 °C, ≥6 h in 0.2 M NaCl), followed by RNase A and proteinase K digestion. Purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR with target-specific primers. XBP1s binding enrichment was quantified by the ΔΔCt method relative to that of the Input and the IgG control. The primer sequences for the four potential binding sites in the MYDGF promoter are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Cells were cotransfected with XBP1s overexpression plasmid, pRL-TK control plasmid, and wild-type/mutant MYDGF promoter reporter plasmids. After 48 h, the cells were lysed, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. A 20 μL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 100 μL of luciferase assay reagent in a black 96-well plate and incubated for 2 min in the dark before luminescence measurement. Renilla luciferase activity was detected using a freshly prepared Substrate II/Buffer II (1:50) mixture with a 1000 ms integration time.

Subcutaneous tumor xenograft experiment

BALB/c-Nu nude mice (male, 6-8 weeks old) were randomly divided into four groups (NC, OE-MYDGF, sh-LCN2, and OE-MYDGF+sh-LCN2; n = 6/group) and injected subcutaneously with HGC27 stable cells. One week later, half of the mice in each group received intraperitoneal imidazole ketone erastin (IKE) (40 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks), a modified erastin derivative specifically engineered for improved pharmacokinetics and in vivo stability, making it ideal for animal studies [24]. Tumor volumes were measured weekly, and the tumors were harvested and weighed on Day 21.

The sample size for animal experiments (n = 6 per group) was determined based on previous studies in similar models and power analysis using G*Power software to ensure adequate power (80%) to detect a pre-specified effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.5) at a significance level of α = 0.05. Animals were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a random number generator to ensure unbiased allocation. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent researcher not involved in the experiment.

Statistical analysis

In this study, statistical analysis and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2. For normally distributed data, the t test was applied; otherwise, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA. All independent experiments were repeated three times to ensure statistical significance at P < 0.05. The following symbols were used to denote statistical significance: ns, not significant; P < 0.05; *, P < 0.01; **, P < 0.001; ***, P < 0.0001.

Results

MYDGF is highly expressed in gastric cancer cells

Our objective was to investigate genes that are differentially expressed between tumor cells and normal cells at the cellular level. Therefore, to avoid confounding effects from other gastric cancer subtypes, such as diffuse-type gastric carcinoma, on our conclusions, we exclusively selected samples of intestinal-type gastric carcinoma. In the GSE183904 dataset, a total of 13 samples were included (Supplementary Table 5). For the GSE134520 dataset, we obtained 3 nonatrophic gastritis samples, 3 atrophic gastritis samples, 2 samples with mild intestinal metaplasia, 4 with severe intestinal metaplasia, and 1 with early gastric cancer (Supplementary Table 6). After the two single-cell datasets were merged, we categorized the epithelial cells into multiple clusters (Fig. 1A and B). Subsequent GSEA of normal epithelial cells and malignant epithelial cells revealed that the gastric acid secretion signaling pathway was significantly enriched in normal epithelial cells, which is consistent with the physiological function of the gastric epithelium (Fig. 1C). Through cellular-level deconvolution of tumor tissues, we sought to identify genes that were specifically overexpressed in tumor cells. To this end, we applied the “FindAllMarkers” function to pinpoint genes highly expressed in malignant epithelial cells. Analysis of the top 20 upregulated genes revealed that most were associated with biological processes, including cellular metabolism, ion transport, and signal transduction, which is consistent with the hypermetabolic nature of tumor cells. Among the remaining genes, MYDGF expression was uniquely elevated in malignant epithelium (Fig. 1D). We subsequently stratified all malignant epithelial cells into MYDGF-high and MYDGF-low groups using an expression threshold of MYDGF = 2. GSEA v4.3.2 demonstrated that MYDGF-high cells were predominantly enriched in pathways related to protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, proteasomal degradation, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis (Fig. 1E), suggesting potential functional links between MYDGF and these processes.

Building on our single-cell RNA-seq findings, we expanded our analysis to validate MYDGF expression in bulk tumor datasets. As shown in Fig. 1F–H, MYDGF expression was significantly upregulated in gastric cancer tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues across three independent cohorts (TCGA-STAD, GSE66229, and GSE184336). We found that MYDGF expression was significantly associated with patient prognosis in the GSE84437 dataset (Supplementary Fig. 1); however, no significant difference in prognosis was observed between high and low MYDGF expression groups in the TCGA-STAD and GSE66229 datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1).

MYDGF inhibits ferroptosis in gastric cancer cells under hypoxia

As shown above, MYDGF may play an important role in GC. First, we hypothesized that MYDGF might affect the proliferation of GC cells; therefore, we knocked down MYDGF in two human gastric cancer cell lines (AGS and HGC27). Both CCK-8 and EdU assays revealed that MYDGF knockdown did not significantly affect cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 2). Because previous studies have reported that hypoxia can upregulate MYDGF expression [16], we speculated that MYDGF expression might be associated with hypoxia. We confirmed that hypoxia indeed increased MYDGF expression in AGS and HGC27 cells (Fig. 2A). After 24 h of hypoxic culture, we observed a significant decrease in GC cell viability (Fig. 2B and C), which was attributed to increased cell death under hypoxic conditions. Because hypoxia can induce various forms of programmed cell death, we sought to determine the specific death pathways involved in hypoxia- and MYDGF knockdown-mediated cell death. To this end, we treated cells with a ferroptosis inhibitor (DFO), an apoptosis inhibitor (Z-VAD-FMK), and a necroptosis inhibitor (necrosulfonamide). MYDGF knockdown promoted cell death under hypoxia; however, this effect was partially reversed by a ferroptosis inhibitor, with apoptosis inhibitor also showing partial protective effects (Fig. 2D and E). Similarly, in hypoxic cells without MYDGF knockdown, all three inhibitors (ferroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis inhibitors) partially inhibited hypoxia-induced cell death (Fig. 2D and E). These results indicate that both hypoxia and MYDGF knockdown promote ferroptosis and apoptosis in GC cells, although MYDGF appears to play a more prominent role in suppressing hypoxia-induced ferroptosis; thus, we focused our subsequent investigations on ferroptosis. After 24 h of treatment, we observed that hypoxia significantly increased intracellular lipid ROS and MDA levels (Fig. 2F–I). Under normoxic conditions, MYDGF knockdown had minimal effects on these markers (Fig. 2F–I). However, under hypoxia, MYDGF knockdown further increased lipid ROS and MDA levels, and these effects were reversed by treatment with DFO or ferrostatin-1 (Fig. 2F–I). The results of transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of cells with MYDGF knockdown under hypoxic conditions further support this conclusion (Fig. 2J). These findings confirm that MYDGF inhibits hypoxia-induced ferroptosis in GC cells.

MYDGF suppresses ferroptosis in GC cells by inhibiting the ubiquitination-dependent degradation of LCN2

Our studies revealed that MYDGF inhibits hypoxia-induced ferroptosis in gastric cancer (GC) cells. Bioinformatics analysis of protein interaction databases (HuRI and IntAct) revealed LCN2 as the only ferroptosis-associated binding partner of MYDGF (Supplementary Table 7). Hypoxia decreased LCN2 protein but not mRNA levels, suggesting posttranscriptional regulation. MYDGF knockdown reduced LCN2 protein expression, whereas its overexpression increased LCN2 protein expression under hypoxia (Fig. 3A, B), without affecting its mRNA expression (Fig. 3C, D). All the experiments described below were conducted under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂). Under these conditions, LCN2 overexpression reversed the MYDGF knockdown-induced increases in lipid ROS and MDA levels (Fig. 3E–J), whereas LCN2 knockdown reversed the MYDGF-mediated suppression of ferroptosis markers. Our studies revealed that hypoxia promotes LCN2 degradation via the proteasomal pathway, as treatment with MG132 (5 μM) but not treatment with chloroquine (CQ, 10 μM) restored LCN2 levels (Fig. 4A). Hypoxia also significantly increased LCN2 ubiquitination in cells pretreated with MG132 to inhibit proteasomal degradation (Fig. 4B). MYDGF stabilizes LCN2 by inhibiting its proteasomal degradation. This conclusion is supported by two key observations: First, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 abolished the reduction in LCN2 expression induced by MYDGF knockdown (Fig. 4C). Second, and consistent with this mechanism, MYDGF overexpression did not further increase LCN2 levels when proteasomal activity was blocked by MG132 (Fig. 4D), as the maximal stabilization by the inhibitor masked any additional effect of MYDGF. IKE is a potent, metabolically stable ferroptosis inducer that is particularly suitable for in vivo animal studies; therefore, we selected IKE for our in vivo experiments [24]. In vivo, MYDGF overexpression attenuated IKE-induced tumor suppression in xenografts, whereas LCN2 knockdown enhanced the effects of IKE (Fig. 4G, H). The protective effect of MYDGF was abolished by concurrent LCN2 knockdown (Fig. 4G, H). Tissue analysis revealed that MYDGF reduced IKE-induced MDA accumulation, and this effect was reversed by LCN2 knockdown (Fig. 4I). These results validate the role of the MYDGF–LCN2 axis in the suppression of ferroptosis in GC.

MYDGF stabilizes LCN2 by inhibiting its endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD)

Building on previous evidence that MYDGF stabilizes LCN2 by suppressing its ubiquitination-dependent degradation, we sought to elucidate the specific degradation pathway involved. Our single-cell transcriptome analysis shown in Fig. 1E revealed that MYDGF-high malignant epithelial cells were significantly enriched in pathways related to “protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum” and “ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis” (Fig. 1E), suggesting a potential functional link between MYDGF and ER-associated protein homeostasis. Given that hypoxia triggers ER stress—which activates ERAD—we hypothesized that hypoxia degrades LCN2 via ERAD, a process potentially modulated by MYDGF. Using the ERAD inhibitor eeyarestatin I, we confirmed that hypoxia-induced LCN2 degradation occurs primarily through ERAD (Fig. 5A). Confocal microscopy revealed LCN2-ER colocalization (Fig. 5B). We next asked whether the regulatory effect of MYDGF on LCN2 stability is dependent on a functional ERAD pathway. To test this hypothesis, we inhibited ERAD using eeyarestatin I. Strikingly, eeyarestatin I treatment abrogated the changes in LCN2 protein levels induced by either MYDGF knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 5C and D). Under conditions of ERAD inhibition, LCN2 degradation was already maximally blocked, and LCN2 levels reached a plateau. Consequently, neither depleting nor overexpressing MYDGF could further alter LCN2 abundance. These results indicate that the ability of MYDGF to modulate LCN2 stability is strictly dependent on the activity of the ERAD pathway, which is consistent with our model that MYDGF functions by competitively interfering with the ERAD machinery. Surprisingly, although eeyarestatin I blocked LCN2 degradation, it did not alter LCN2 ubiquitination levels when cells were cotreated with MG132 (Fig. 5E). We attribute this to the mechanism of eeyarestatin I: it inhibits the translocation of ubiquitinated proteins from the ER to the cytosol without affecting ubiquitination itself. In the absence of MG132, eeyarestatin I significantly increased ubiquitinated LCN2 accumulation (Fig. 5F), confirming that LCN2 undergoes ERAD-mediated ubiquitination for degradation. LCN2 degradation by UBQLN1 depends on ERAD

Our prior findings that MYDGF knockdown promotes LCN2 ubiquitination-dependent degradation without altering its ubiquitination levels prompted us to investigate the underlying mechanism. We hypothesized that [1] MYDGF acts as a competitive binding partner for E3 ubiquitin ligases or [2] affects ubiquitin‒proteasome adaptor proteins. Systematic analysis of LCN2-interacting proteins in the HuRI and IntAct databases revealed that UBQLN1 and UBQLN2 facilitate the degradation of ubiquitinated proteins by binding them to the proteasome [25], with both also showing binding potential to MYDGF. Under both hypoxic and normoxic conditions, only UBQLN1 knockdown significantly increased LCN2 protein levels (Fig. 5G). This established role of UBQLN1 in promoting LCN2 degradation led us to examine whether this process involves ERAD and proteasomal pathways. We next investigated whether UBQLN1 degrades LCN2 via the ERAD-proteasome pathway. The accumulation of LCN2 resulting from UBQLN1 knockdown was blocked by cotreatment with either the ERAD inhibitor eeyarestatin I or the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 5H). These results demonstrate that UBQLN1-mediated degradation of LCN2 is dependent on the activity of both the ERAD machinery and the proteasome. Consistent with the function of UBQLN1 as a ubiquitin‒proteasome adaptor, its knockdown did not alter LCN2 ubiquitination levels (Fig. 5I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that UBQLN1 specifically facilitates ERAD-dependent proteasomal degradation of LCN2 without affecting its ubiquitination status.

MYDGF inhibits LCN2 degradation by competitively binding to UBQLN1

Although prior evidence has established that UBQLN1-mediated degradation of LCN2 depends on ERAD and the proteasome, whether MYDGF stabilizes LCN2 through UBQLN1 remains unclear. To address this, we first knocked down UBQLN1 and subsequently knocked down MYDGF, which abolished the reduction in LCN2 typically induced by MYDGF depletion alone (Fig. 6A). Conversely, overexpressing MYDGF failed to increase LCN2 levels when UBQLN1 was knocked down (Fig. 6B). These results demonstrate that the MYDGF-mediated inhibition of LCN2 degradation is strictly dependent on UBQLN1. Bioinformatics analyses predicted interactions between UBQLN1 and both MYDGF and LCN2; therefore, we validated these interactions experimentally: coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) confirmed binding between MYDGF and UBQLN1 (Fig. 6D) and between LCN2 and UBQLN1 (Fig. 6E). The results of exogenous coimmunoprecipitation experiments further supported the interaction between UBQLN1 and MYDGF and between the UBQLN1 and LCN2 proteins (Fig. 6F, G). Immunofluorescence further demonstrated the subcellular colocalization of MYDGF with UBQLN1 and LCN2 with UBQLN1 (Fig. 6C). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that MYDGF competitively binds UBQLN1 to block its degradation of LCN2. To test this hypothesis, we subjected two equal cell populations (control cells vs. cells with MYDGF knockdown or overexpression) to hypoxia. MYDGF knockdown increased the amount of LCN2 bound to UBQLN1, whereas MYDGF overexpression reduced the amount of UBQLN1-bound LCN2 (Fig. 6H, I). UBQLN1 regulates MYDGF-LCN2 competitive binding via STI1-4 and UBA Domains and specifically recognizes K48-linked ubiquitination on LCN2

To elucidate the mechanism by which MYDGF competitively binds to UBQLN1 and inhibits LCN2 degradation, we examined the domain architecture of UBQLN1 based on the UniProt database and generated a series of truncation mutants corresponding to its six key structural domains: ΔUBL, ΔSTI1-1, ΔSTI1-2, ΔSTI1-3, ΔSTI1-4, and ΔUBA (Fig. 6J). Each mutant was cotransfected with full-length MYDGF and LCN2 into HEK293T cells. When the STI1-4 domain was deleted, the anti-UBQLN1 antibody failed to pull down MYDGF, indicating that the binding of MYDGF to UBQLN1 depends on the STI1-4 domain (Fig. 6L). In contrast, deletion of the UBA domain abolished the interaction between UBQLN1 and LCN2, suggesting that LCN2 binding requires the UBA domain (Fig. 6K). The ubiquitin‒proteasome degradation pathway involves seven major types of ubiquitin chains (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63); therefore, it remains unclear which type of ubiquitin chain linked to LCN2 is recognized by UBQLN1. To investigate this, we transfected gastric cancer cells with Myc-tagged ubiquitin mutants (K6R, K11R, K27R, K29R, K33R, K48R, and K63R). To enhance UBQLN1–LCN2 binding, MYDGF was knocked down in all the groups under hypoxic culture conditions. Using an anti-UBQLN1 antibody for coimmunoprecipitation, we specifically detected ubiquitinated LCN2 bound to UBQLN1. Because the transfected ubiquitin plasmids were tagged and carried mutations at specific lysine residues, changes in ubiquitination levels could be assessed via tag-specific antibodies. The results revealed that transfection with the K48R ubiquitin mutant led to a significant decrease in the ubiquitin signal (Fig. 6M), indicating that UBQLN1 specifically binds to LCN2 conjugated with K48-linked ubiquitin chains.

To determine whether XBP1s directly activates MYDGF transcription, we identified four potential binding sites in the MYDGF promoter using JASPAR (Fig. 7F). ChIP‒PCR revealed specific binding of XBP1s only to the BS2 site (Fig. 7G). Luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that mutation of BS2 abolished XBP1s-mediated promoter activation (Fig. 7H), confirming direct transcriptional regulation.

Rescue experiments revealed that XBP1s regulates LCN2 through MYDGF: XBP1s knockdown reduced LCN2 expression, which was reversed by MYDGF overexpression (Fig. 7I), whereas the upregulation of LCN2 expression induced by XBP1s expression was abolished by MYDGF knockdown (Fig. 7J). Finally, XBP1s knockdown promoted ferroptosis, as shown by increased lipid ROS and MDA levels, effects that were reversed by MYDGF or LCN2 overexpression (Fig. 7K–N). Together, these results demonstrate that hypoxia inhibits ferroptosis in gastric cancer via the XBP1s–MYDGF–LCN2 axis.

Discussion

Through the integration of multiomics data and functional experiments, this study systematically reveals a novel molecular mechanism through which GC cells inhibit ferroptosis and promote survival under hypoxic conditions via the XBP1s–MYDGF–UBQLN1–LCN2 signaling axis (Fig. 7O). This discovery not only deepens the understanding of the hypoxia adaptation mechanism in GC but also provides an important theoretical basis for combination therapeutic strategies targeting tumor metabolism and cell death pathways.

First, through single-cell transcriptome analysis, we found that MYDGF is highly expressed specifically in malignant epithelial cells. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies indicating that the expression of MYDGF, a bone marrow-derived secreted protein, is upregulated in various tumors, such as hepatocellular carcinoma, bladder cancer, and lung adenocarcinoma [15, 16, 26]. However, unlike the previously reported role of MYDGF in promoting tumor proliferation via activation of the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, this study reveals that MYDGF primarily promotes cell survival in GC by inhibiting ferroptosis, demonstrating its context-dependent functionality. This difference may stem from the specific microenvironments of different tumor types, suggesting that MYDGF may exert its effects through distinct molecular mechanisms in various cancers. The heterogeneity in the prognostic value of MYDGF may reflect the complex pathophysiological landscape of gastric cancer. As our study demonstrates, the functional role of MYDGF in suppressing ferroptosis is highly dependent on the hypoxic tumor microenvironment. Consequently, in tumors with milder hypoxia or those possessing alternative compensatory survival pathways, MYDGF expression levels alone may be insufficient to serve as an independent prognostic indicator. This finding underscores that the biological significance of MYDGF lies more in its defined role in mediating hypoxia-induced ferroptosis resistance than in its utility as a universal prognostic biomarker. Future studies stratifying patients based on the extent of tumor hypoxia or the activation status of the XBP1s-MYDGF-LCN2 axis will be crucial for precisely defining the patient subsets that would benefit from therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway.

Notably, the high expression of MYDGF was significantly correlated with pathways such as endoplasmic reticulum protein processing and ubiquitination degradation. These findings prompted us to further investigate the role of MYDGF in regulating protein stability. Through a series of experiments, we demonstrated that MYDGF competitively binds to the STI1-4 domain of UBQLN1, thereby blocking UBQLN1-mediated recognition of K48-linked polyubiquitin chains on LCN2 via its UBA domain, which inhibits the degradation of LCN2 through the ERAD. UBQLN1 is a key adaptor protein in the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, and its function has been reported in various tumors [27–30]. Previous research by Kurlawala et al. highlighted the importance of the STI and UBA domains of UBQLN1 for substrate recognition and the regulation of proteasomal stability [25]. This study is the first to identify the critical role of UBQLN1 in regulating ferroptosis in GC and to elucidate the molecular mechanism through which UBQLN1 mediates LCN2 degradation through specific recognition of K48-linked ubiquitin chains. Furthermore, the regulatory effect of MYDGF on LCN2 was completely abolished upon ERAD inhibition (Fig. 5C, D), indicating that MYDGF does not function as a broad-spectrum ERAD suppressor but acts specifically within this pathway as a competitive inhibitor of UBQLN1-mediated substrate recognition.

The iron-binding protein LCN2 plays a significant role in ferroptosis. Previous studies have shown that LCN2 chelates intracellular free iron, alleviates lipid peroxidation, and consequently inhibits ferroptosis. However, the regulatory mechanism of LCN2 in tumors remains unclear. This study revealed that hypoxia induces the formation of the XBP1s spliceosome, which directly binds to the BS2 site (TCCACGTGTCC, 1949–1959) in the MYDGF promoter region, transcriptionally upregulating MYDGF expression. XBP1s, a key transcription factor in endoplasmic reticulum stress, has been widely reported to play a role in helping tumors adapt to stressful environments. Notably, we revealed a hypoxia-specific regulatory mechanism: hypoxia promotes LCN2 ubiquitination within the endoplasmic reticulum, resulting in its proteasomal degradation, whereas MYDGF specifically inhibits this ubiquitination process. Under normoxia, LCN2 undergoes minimal ubiquitination, explaining why LCN2 is stabilized only by MYDGF under hypoxic conditions. Hypoxia predominantly drives LCN2 degradation through ERAD activation; however, concurrent XBP1s-mediated MYDGF induction establishes a critical buffering mechanism that partially preserves LCN2 protein levels and is sufficient to maintain iron homeostasis and ferroptosis resistance. For instance, Yang et al. found that hypoxia enhances glycolytic dependency and hypoxia tolerance in GC through a HIF-1α/NAT10/SEPT9 positive feedback loop [4]. Lin et al. reported that hypoxia inhibits ferroptosis via the HIF-1α/lncRNA–PMAN axis [31]. This study extends the function of XBP1s to the regulation of ferroptosis, enriching the theoretical framework of the cross-talk between endoplasmic reticulum stress and ferroptosis.

From a translational medicine perspective, this study has significant clinical implications. First, the plasma levels of the secreted proteins MYDGF and LCN2 could serve as biomarkers for predicting the response to ferroptosis inducers. Second, inhibitors targeting the XBP1s–MYDGF–LCN2 pathway may enhance the efficacy of existing GC treatments. Particularly in advanced GC with abundant hypoxic microenvironments, combining ferroptosis inducers with MYDGF or XBP1 inhibitors holds promise for overcoming resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapy. Recent research has indicated the great potential of ferroptosis inducers in reversing chemotherapy resistance in patients with GC. This study provides a theoretical basis for such combination strategies.

Furthermore, the finding that UBQLN1 degrades LCN2 via the ERAD pathway expands the understanding of the role of the protein degradation system in ferroptosis. ERAD, a crucial mechanism for endoplasmic reticulum quality control, is associated with various diseases when dysfunctional [32–35]. This study is the first to link ERAD with ferroptosis, offering new insights for antitumor therapies targeting the protein degradation system.

This study has several limitations. First, all the mechanistic experiments were primarily based on cell lines and mouse models; the activation of this pathway and its correlation with prognosis and treatment response have not been extensively validated in clinical samples. Second, whether MYDGF also regulates LCN2 stability through other molecular chaperones or E3 ubiquitin ligases requires further exploration. Additionally, the universality of this pathway in other cancer types remains to be verified. Finally, this study focused primarily on tumor cell-autonomous mechanisms; future work should further investigate the role of this pathway in tumor microenvironment cells, such as macrophages and fibroblasts.

Despite these limitations, this study systematically elucidates the molecular mechanism through which GC cells inhibit ferroptosis under hypoxic conditions through the XBP1s–MYDGF–UBQLN1–LCN2 axis, providing a significant theoretical foundation for the development of new therapeutic strategies for GC. Inhibitors targeting this pathway may become new treatment options, especially for hypoxia-associated therapy-resistant GC.

Conclusion

In summary, this study is the first to reveal the critical role of the XBP1–MYDGF–UBQLN1–LCN2 signaling axis in hypoxia adaptation in gastric cancer and elucidate its molecular mechanism in inhibiting ferroptosis. This discovery not only deepens the understanding of cell death regulation in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment of gastric cancer but also provides an important theoretical foundation for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis. Inhibitors targeting this pathway may represent a new treatment option for gastric cancer, demonstrating significant translational value—particularly in overcoming hypoxia-related therapy resistance.
