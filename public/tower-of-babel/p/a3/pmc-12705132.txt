Resilience and physical functioning trajectories in people aging with disability: concordance and determinants over seven years
Authors: Seeun Park, Ivan Molton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThis study sought to estimate trajectories of psychological resilience and physical functioning in adults aging with disability, to examine the alignment of those trajectories, and to explore determinants of trajectory membership over a 7-year span.Research Design and MethodsData were drawn from a longitudinal survey of 772 adults diagnosed with multiple sclerosis, muscular dystrophy, post-polio syndrome, or spinal cord injury, diagnosed before age 55 and with at least 10 years since diagnosis. Resilience and physical functioning were measured using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale and the PROMIS Physical Function Scale. Growth mixture modeling, contingency analysis, and multinomial logistic regression were performed.ResultsParticipants averaged 55.9 years of age (SD = 10.4) and were 20.9 years since diagnosis. Four distinct trajectories emerged for both resilience (i.e., high-improving, mid-improving, low-stable, very low-stable) and physical functioning (i.e., high-stable, mid-stable, low-stable, very low-declining). Favorable resilience classes were associated with better physical function trajectories, while no individuals with high-improving resilience were classified in the very low-declining physical function class. Approximately one-third of participants concurrently exhibited low resilience and suboptimal physical functioning. Psychological factors predicted resilience trajectories, whereas health-related factors predicted physical functioning trajectories. Self-efficacy was the only determinant significantly associated with both trajectories.Discussion and ImplicationsResilience and physical functioning trajectories exhibited significant concordance, suggesting a complex interdependence rather than isolated change processes. The interdependence of these trajectories supports tailored rehabilitation interventions that simultaneously target psychological factors (resilience and self-efficacy) as well as physical function in order to promote successful aging among individuals with compounded age- and disability-related vulnerabilities.

Findings of this study support the use of tailored rehabilitation interventions that simultaneously address emotional well-being and physical health, with intensity guided by regular screening of resilience and physical function to account for individual differences. Self-efficacy was identified as a shared determinant, indicating its potential as a cross-cutting intervention target.

Background and objectives

The experience of long-term or early-acquired disability can exert a profound influence on an individual’s life trajectory by shaping access to educational, professional, fiscal, and social opportunities and outcomes (Putnam & Stark, 2025). The degree to which disability influences these trajectories varies considerably depending on a number of timing factors (e.g., age at onset, duration of disability) and the socio-structural context in which the disability occurs. For the largest proportion of the aging population, functional limitations first emerge in later adulthood related to physiological decline, chronic health conditions, or acute medical events (Molton et al., 2025; Verbrugge et al., 2016), a phenomenon referred to as “aging into disability” (Verbrugge & Yang, 2002). In comparison, a relatively smaller population is aging with long-term impairments associated with congenital conditions, chronic illnesses, or traumatic injuries that emerged from early life through mid-adulthood and are commonly described as “aging with disability” (Verbrugge et al., 2017).

People aging with disability represent a demographically understudied and structurally marginalized subpopulation (Yoshizaki-Gibbons, 2021). Unlike those aging into disability, they navigate the cumulative consequences of early-onset impairments that intersect with age-related decline in later adulthood. As such, they encounter a dual challenge of maintaining adaptation across both disability- and aging-related domains (Yeung & Breheny, 2022) within systems primarily designed for able-bodied individuals or those who are now aging into disability later in life. In this context, the dominant frameworks of successful aging, which have traditionally focused on the absence of disease or disability tend to obscure the lived realities of people aging with disability and fall short in accounting for that convergence. In response, scholars have called for more inclusive frameworks that recognize diverse aging experiences, and subsequent research has found that resilience and maintenance of physical functioning, along with autonomy/independence and social connectedness, are core components of successful aging as identified by people aging with disability (Khamzina & Rogers, 2025; Molton & Yorkston, 2017; Villar et al., 2025). Within this multidimensional constellation, resilience and physical functioning consistently emerge as centeral themes.

Although the concept of resilience has been defined and conceptualized in various ways, current consensus focuses on the process and outcome of effective adaptation to adversity, particularly through psychological and behavioral flexibility in response to stressors. Resilience is widely recognized as a key psychosocial mechanism promoting well-being (American Psychological Association, 2025; Trică et al., 2024). Among people aging with disability, the experience of impairments during critical developmental stages often interferes with anticipated life roles and transitions, and resilience provides an essential capacity for addressing these challenges. In this population, greater resilience has been known to be associated with reduced pain, lower stress, decreased anxiety and depression, and higher quality of life (Novak & Lev-Ari, 2023; Ovaska-Stafford et al., 2021). It is also associated with better employment outcomes and greater satisfaction with social roles, suggesting potential to enhance community integration and economic stability in this population (Battalio et al., 2020; Ordway et al., 2020). In parallel, physical functioning is another fundamental pillar of successful aging. Physical function does not represent measured impairment or inability but rather a perception of one’s capacity for physical activity and ability to engage in household and community tasks, given one’s health. For people with disabilities, this is deeply contextualized and contingent on both bodily and environmental affordances (Molton & Yorkston, 2017; Putnam & Stark, 2025).

Both resilience and physical functioning have been recognized as dynamic and evolving constructs that shape aging trajectories which leads to growing longitudinal research among people aging with disability. Edwards et al. (2017) observed upward trends in resilience over a 1-year period and found that these changes were positively associated with physical function. Similarly, Bonanno et al. (2012) identified distinct trajectories of resilience, including stable and delayed patterns and noted that individuals demonstrating stable trajectories exhibited greater levels of fighting spirit. Notably, Geard et al. (2018) emphasized that people with physical disabilities leverage fighting spirit (a construct adjacent to resilience) as a vital resource for sustaining and improving physical function. Collectively, these studies substantiate the robust linkage between resilience and physical functioning. Nevertheless, little is known about how the trajectories of these variables may align or change over time. Most prior studies have relied on fixed-interval score comparisons to assess change in resilience and have analyzed physical functioning as a static outcome, rather than modeling both as dynamic and longitudinal constructs (Edwards et al., 2017; Silverman et al., 2015). Moreover, although a few studies have examined resilience trajectories, their focus has often been restricted to a single diagnostic group or relied on proxy indicators of resilience, such as the absence of psychopathology (Bonanno et al., 2012; Quale & Schanke, 2010).

Although resilience and physical functioning are conceptually distinct, they represent mutually important constructs of lived experience, reflecting how individuals maintain physical capacity and adapt psychologically to ongoing functional limitations and health-related challenges as they grow older with disability. Understanding their temporal alignment may provide insight into whether they are shaped by overlapping influences and contribute, alone or in tandem, to the broader trajectory of successful aging with disability. The concordance or discordance between these trajectories, along with an examination of their predictors, can identify subgroups experiencing dual strengths or dual vulnerabilities across psychological and physical constructs, patterns that remain obscured when examined separately.

The present study therefore sought to estimate and describe longitudinal trajectories of psychological resilience and physical functioning, evaluate the extent to which these trajectories were concordant or divergent, and examine the role of individual-level and contextual factors (e.g., pain, self-efficacy, and social support) in predicting trajectory membership. For the purposes of these analyses, we rely on the World Health Organization (WHO) framework for healthy aging, as described in the 2015 Report on Ageing and Health (World Health Organization [WHO], 2015). In this framework, healthy aging is defined as the “process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables wellbeing in older age.” Functional ability is what “enables people to be and do what they have reason to value,” specifically within five domains (meeting basic needs; ability to learn, grow and make decisions; being mobile; building and maintaining relationships; and contributing to society). As defined by WHO, functional ability also represents an interaction of personal capacities (cognition, mobility, sensory function, psychology, vitality) and the physical, built and social environment in which someone lives (WHO, 2015). Our analysis focuses on several capacities, primarily within mobility and psychology domains, but is not intended to diminish the importance of the social and built environment in healthy aging and disability.

Research design and methods

Participants and procedures

This analysis is based on data from a national longitudinal survey conducted in the United States by the University of Washington (2008–2018), aimed at advancing knowledge of successful aging among community-dwelling adults with long-term disabilities. The dataset includes multidimensional measures encompassing physical and cognitive function, health-related behaviors, social determinants, and quality of life. Although data collection spanned seven waves, the present study focused exclusively on four time points across those waves (T1–T4), corresponding to Waves 1, 5, 6, and 7, respectively, which were collected between July 2009 and May 2017. The approximate timeframes for each wave were November 2009 (T1, Wave 1), February 2015 (T2, Wave 5), January 2016 (T3, Wave 6), and December 2016 (T4, Wave 7).

The inclusion criteria for the larger survey were as follows: (a) at least 18 years of age, (b) the ability to read and understand English, and (c) self-report of a physician’s diagnosis of multiple sclerosis (MS), muscular dystrophy (MD), post-polio syndrome (PPS), or spinal cord injury (SCI). These four medical conditions were selected to include a variety of trajectories of disability, in terms of onset (acute vs. gradual) progression (static vs. deteriorating) and typical first emergence in the life course. Despite these differences, these conditions also share many characteristics (e.g., chronicity and functional impact), making them suitable for combination to investigate common aging-related challenges and adaptation processes. For the present analysis, data were limited to participants who identified, (d) onset of disability before the age of 55, and (e) having had the diagnosed condition for at least 10 years.

Participants were recruited through research registries, advertisements, and prior cohorts. Surveys were administered by mail and online. All participants in the present study completed a brief screen of cognitive function prior to enrollment (the 6-item Cognitive Screener, Callahan et al., 2002), supporting ability to comprehend study procedures and complete self-report. Informed consent was obtained, and all procedures were approved by the Human Subjects Division at the University of Washington (No. 00000836).

Given the study population, accessibility was considered at all phases of enrollment and data collection. Study participants were asked for their preferred format for completing study questionnaires, which could include an online survey, a physical paper survey mailed to them, or via one-on-one telephone interview with study staff. Participants with visual impairment were provided with an option to use a form accessible to Job Access With Speech screen reader technology, or were offered the opportunity to complete surveys by interview. Participants with fine motor difficulties preventing physical access to the survey (either in paper form or electronically) were encouraged to enlist support from a care partner, or to complete study questionnaires via individual interview.

Measures

Resilience

Resilience was measured using the 10-item short form of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10), a unidimensional scale assessing the ability to cope with challenges such as change, health issues, and emotional distress (Campbell‐Sills & Stein, 2007; Connor & Davidson, 2003). Example items include “Under pressure, I stay focused and think clearly” and “I am not easily discouraged by failure.” Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = Not true at all to 4 = True nearly all the time), yielding total scores from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The CD-RISC-10 has shown strong reliability and validity, including among people with physical disabilities (Miller et al., 2021; Terrill et al., 2016). In this study, internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.92–0.93).

Physical function

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Short Form (v1.0) Physical Function for Samples with Mobility Aid Users was used to measure physical functioning (Rose et al., 2008). This measure is intended for adult populations, including those who use mobility aids (e.g., wheelchairs), but it is not limited to them. It includes a screening question on one’s ability to stand and walk, as well as items such as “Are you able to wash and dry your body?” and “Are you able to stand upright briefly without support?” Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (unable to do) to 5 (without any difficulty), with higher scores reflecting better functioning. Scores were standardized as t scores (M = 50, SD = 10). It has demonstrated strong reliability and validity among people with physical disabilities (Hung et al., 2014; Miller et al., 2016). In this study, internal consistency was excellent across waves (α = 0.91–0.94).

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the depression domain from the PROMIS-29 Profile (v2.1), which includes four items assessing negative emotions experienced in the past seven days (HealthMeasures, 2021). Participants rated items such as “I felt worthless,” “I felt helpless,” and “I felt hopeless” on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always). This measure has demonstrated strong validity in diverse clinical populations (Cook et al., 2016) and exhibited high internal consistency in this study (α = 0.89–0.91).

Fatigue

Fatigue was assessed using the PROMIS Short Form v1.0 Fatigue 4a, which comprises 4 items assessing fatigue severity and its impact on daily activities over the past week (Lai et al., 2011). Sample items include “I feel fatigued” and “I have trouble starting things because I am tired,” rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 5 = very much). Internal consistency was acceptable at T1 (α = 0.85) and excellent at T2 through T4 (α = 0.95).

Pain intensity

Pain intensity was measured using the Numeric Rating Scale, where participants rated their average pain in the past week on a scale of 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable).

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was assessed using the six-item short form of the University of Washington Self-Efficacy Scale (UW-SES), developed to evaluate disability management self-efficacy among people with chronic health conditions (Amtmann et al., 2012). Items such as “Can you keep the physical discomfort related to your health condition or disability from interfering with the things you want to do?” and “Can you figure out effective solutions to issues that come up related to your health condition or disability?” were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 5 = completely), with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The UW-SES has proved valid in populations with physical disabilities (The University of Washington Center on Outcomes Research in Rehabilitation, 2018). In this study, internal consistency was good (α = 0.87–0.93).

Sleep disturbance

Sleep disturbance was measured was assessed using the PROMIS Short Form (v1.0) Sleep disturbance scale, a four-item measure evaluating sleep quality, difficulty, and restoration (Reeve & Tereso, 2016). Items such as “My sleep was refreshing” and “I had difficulty falling asleep” were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores (range: 4–20) indicating greater disturbance. This measure has demonstrated strong validity and reliability across various physical disabilities (Fogelberg et al., 2015), and internal consistency in this study was high across waves (α = 0.85–0.87).

Perceived social support

Social support was measured using the 12-item Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), which assessed support from family, friends, and significant others across a broad spectrum, including emotional support, availability of confidants, and assistance with decision making (Zimet et al., 1990). Sample items include “My family really tries to help me” and “There is a special person with whom I can share my joys and sorrows.” Participants rated items on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = very strongly disagree to 7 = very strongly agree), with higher total scores (range: 12–84) reflecting greater perceived support. The MSPSS has been validated in populations with chronic conditions and physical disabilities (De Maria et al., 2018; Iwanaga et al., 2021). Internal ­consistency in this study was excellent across all waves (α = 0.94–0.95).

Sociodemographic and disability-related factors

Sociodemographic and disability-related factors included age, categorized as young adults (19–44 years), middle-aged adults (45–64 years), and older adults (>65 years); sex (male vs. female); race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White vs. all other race/ethnicity groups); educational attainment [less than high school and high school/technical school (combined), some college, college graduate, professional/graduate school]; marital status (married/partnered vs. not); and income, categorized into tertiles (low, middle, high). Included disability-related variables were duration of disability and diagnosis (MD, SCI, PPS, or MS).

Statistical analysis

Trajectories of resilience and physical functioning over a 7-year period were first examined using latent growth curve modeling (LGCM). To capture temporal patterns of change, three baseline models were estimated for each construct: an intercept-only model, a linear change model, and a quadratic change model. Then, growth mixture modeling (GMM) was conducted to classify individuals into distinct latent trajectory classes for resilience and physical functioning. GMM enables the detection of unobserved heterogeneity in longitudinal patterns by simultaneously modeling within-class growth trajectories and between-class variability (Jung & Wickrama, 2008). Posterior probabilities were used to assign class membership, and model fit was evaluated across one- to five-class solutions. Model selection was guided by multiple fit indices, such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted Bayesian information criterion.

Subsequently, a contingency analysis was conducted to explore the joint distribution of resilience and physical functioning trajectories. Cross-tabulations of latent class membership for the two constructs were generated, and a chi-square test of independence was used to assess the degree of association between them. Standardized and adjusted residuals, based on Haberman’s method, were calculated to detect statistically meaningful deviations from independence and to identify combinations of resilience and physical functioning classes that were over- or under-represented.

Finally, multinomial logistic regression analyses were performed separately for resilience and physical functioning classes to examine determinants of trajectory class membership. Predictors included sociodemographic variables (e.g., age groups, sex, educational attainment, and race/ethnicity), psychological factors (e.g., depression, self-efficacy), physical symptom variables (e.g., pain and fatigue), and disability-specific characteristics (e.g., type and duration of disability). All statistical analyses were performed in R (Version 4.4.1) by using the OpenMx and nnet packages (Neale et al., 2016; Venables & Ripley, 2013).

Trajectories of resilience and physical functioning

To identify the best-fitting trajectories of resilience and physical functioning over time, LGCM was conducted. Among the three models tested, the linear growth model showed the best fit for both constructs, as indicated by the lowest AIC and BIC values (Supplementary Table 1). This model was selected for subsequent analyses, reflecting a consistent linear pattern of change without evidence of curvature. For descriptive purposes, trajectories were labelled using a common convention that includes the intercept (e.g., high, mid, low) and trajectory shape (e.g., improving, stable, declining), without use of additional interpretive labels.

For resilience, a four-class solution best captured the longitudinal trajectories of resilience (Supplementary Table 2). The four latent trajectories are illustrated in Figure 1, and key parameters for each class are summarized in Table 2. These trajectories were (1) High-improving (5.18%), the smallest group, characterized by the highest baseline resilience and a gradual increase over time; (2) Mid-improving (18.13%), showing relatively high initial resilience and the most pronounced upward trend; (3) Low-stable (62.56%), marked by low baseline resilience with little change across waves; and (4) Very low-stable (14.12%), characterized by the lowest baseline resilience with no statistically significant change over time.

For physical functioning, as with resilience, a four-class model was identified as the best-fitting and most parsimonious solution (Supplementary Table 2). The four distinct latent classes demonstrated clear differences in baseline levels and exhibited negative slopes but mostly non-significant, with only one class showing a statistically significant decline (Figure 1; Table 2). These classes include (1) High-stable (24.35%), characterized by the highest intercept and the greatest variance in both intercept and slope, indicating high within-class heterogeneity; (2) Mid-stable (33.81%), defined by a moderate intercept and statistically non-significant slope; (3) Low-stable (39.38%), the largest group, marked by a lower intercept and statistically non-significant slope; and (4) Very low-declining (2.46%), the smallest group, characterized by severely limited function at baseline and a significant decline over time.

Concordance in classification of resilience and physical function

Individuals in high- or mid-improving resilience classes were primarily classified into high- or mid-stable physical functioning trajectories (15.5%), with no high-improving individuals in the very low-declining class (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, 34.1% of participants belonged to both low- or very low-stable resilience trajectories and low-stable or very low-declining physical functioning trajectories. Adjusted residuals further substantiated this pattern, indicating significant concordance between resilience and physical functioning classifications (Table 3). The high-improving resilience class exhibited significant over-representation in the high-stable physical function class (adjusted residual = 2.45, p < .05). Similarly, the Mid-improving resilience class was significantly over-represented in the high-stable class (adjusted residual = 4.11, p < .001) but markedly under-represented in the low-stable class (adjusted residual = −2.89). Conversely, individuals in resilience classes with relatively lower baseline levels (i.e., low-stable and very low-stable) were significantly underrepresented in the high-stable physical functioning class (adjusted residuals = –2.53 and –2.06, respectively).

Determinants of longitudinal trajectories of resilience and physical functioning

Several baseline psychological and demographic factors were associated with resilience trajectory membership (Table 4). Most notably, greater self-efficacy consistently differentiated individuals in more favorable trajectories, particularly those classified within high- or mid-improving classes, from their counterparts exhibiting lower or stable resilience profiles (Est = −0.35, SE = 0.04, p < .001). Likewise, reduced depressive symptoms and lower levels of sleep disturbance were associated with a greater probability of inclusion in better resilience classes. Depression showed a graded effect, as estimated log‑odds increased from 0.14 in the mid‑improving class to 0.18 in the low‑stable class and 0.26 in the very low‑stable class relative to the high‑improving reference class, which may reflect an incremental association between baseline depression and poorer resilience trajectories. In terms of demographic or individual level variables, educational attainment also exhibited a pronounced gradient effect. Individuals with higher levels of educational attainment demonstrated progressively diminished odds of being classified into the low-stable or very low-stable resilience trajectories (Est. = −13.74 and −14.83, respectively). Individuals identifying as non-white were significantly more likely to be classified in the low-stable or very low-stable classes than were white participants (Est. = 18.19 and 19.15, respectively), which highlights potential racial disparities in resilience pathways.

In terms of physical functioning trajectory membership, disease and disability variables appeared to be the most influential (Supplementary Table 4). Longer disability duration was associated with increased odds of classification into the low-stable or very low-declining classes relative to the high-stable class (Est. = 0.09 and 0.10, respectively). Similarly, greater pain was linked to a higher likelihood of membership in the low-stable class (Est. = 0.30, SE = 0.07, p < .001). Psychological and demographic factors had a more limited influence on physical function trajectory membership. Greater self-efficacy significantly predicted lower odds of being classified in the low-stable or very low-declining classes (Est. = −0.14 and −0.19, respectively). Higher income was also linked to decreased odds of classification into suboptimal physical functioning trajectories (Est. = −0.86, SE = 0.39, p < .05). In terms of social support, elevated levels significantly predicted increased odds of being classified in the low-stable (Est. = 0.29, SE = 0.12, p < .05) or very low-declining classes (Est. = 0.77, SE = 0.28, p < .01) compared to the high-stable class.

Discussion and implications

The nature of long-term disability requires near-constant adaptation to changing circumstances over time. It could be said that a need for flexibility and resilience is apparent from the moment impairments emerge, which may be instantaneous (e.g., following traumatic SCI), or spread over decades (e.g., in relapsing/remitting MS). As an individual with a disability ages, health-related impairments may progress, halt, improve, or worsen, sometimes week-to-week or month-to-month. These shifts all occur in the context of interactions with broader social and environmental conditions. Such conditions include physical barriers, community accessibility, caregiving availability, and disability-related benefits (Park & Molton, 2025). These structural and interpersonal resources play an important role in influencing adaptation and health over time. Physiologic realities of accelerated and premature aging then intersect with previous limitations, creating additional pressure to adapt, often in the context of an aging social support network. Before one even considers the dynamic and fluid reality of disability as a social construct, it can be understood that living with long-term disability is an exercise in resilience, an observation supported by various qualitative studies in this population (Khamzina & Rogers, 2025; Molton & Yorkston, 2017). Based on these observations in the literature, the purpose of this study was to identify trajectories of resilience and physical function over a 7-year span, to examine the degree to which those trajectories were interwoven, and to evaluate potential baseline variables predicting trajectory membership in a heterogenous group of people aging with physical disability.

Resilience levels among people aging with disability in this study exhibited a mild upward trend over the 7-year period, averaging 28.82 (SD = 6.33), which corresponds to an average of 72.05 out of 100 on the CD-RISC-25 scale. These levels remain below the US general population benchmarks reported by Connor and Davidson (2003), where the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles were 73, 82, and 90, respectively, indicating that people aging with disability in this context fall beneath the 25th percentile. Comparisons with other groups experiencing disability further highlight relatively low resilience. Marwitz et al. (2018) reported a mean CD-RISC-25 score of 76.8 (SD = 17.2) at three months post-injury among people with traumatic brain injury, and Miller et al. (2021) noted a mean of 33.3 (SD = 5.8) among those with lower-limb amputation. The findings suggest that levels of resilience among people aging with disability may be relatively lower than those of groups with similar health conditions and the general adult population.

Regarding longitudinal trajectory, four classes were identified in each construct, with 47.8% of participants classified into low-stable class for resilience and mid- or low-stable physical functioning classes. This indicates a general trend of stability rather than dramatic change. However, approximately one-quarter of participants demonstrated upward trajectories of resilience, which were characterized by either a high baseline with a steady upward trend or a moderate baseline with the steepest rate of increase. This supports previous observations that resilience is a malleable capacity and therefore an appropriate target for intervention (Edwards et al., 2017). Taken together, the combination of low average levels of resilience and, nevertheless, the presence of a substantial subset of individuals who exhibited meaningful psychological improvement suggests that community-dwelling people aging with disability constitute both a high-priority and promising population for resilience-promoting interventions.

Perhaps the most important finding here is the significant degree of concordance between resilience and physical functioning over time. Favorable resilience classes (i.e., high- and mid-improving classes) were substantially aligned with favorable trajectories of physical function. Surprisingly, none of the individuals with High-improving resilience class also belonged to the very low-declining physical functioning class, which suggests that consistently higher resilience is closely linked to the maintenance of physical capacity and may serve as protective mechanism, one that is rarely observed in conjunction with substantial and sustained functional deterioration. Previous studies have found that people reporting high resilience also tend to have lower psychological distress, and healthier lifestyle behaviors (e.g., exercise and diet). Resilience may also indirectly influence physical functioning through greater physical activity in people with disabilities (Gromisch et al., 2021, 2025; Ploughman et al., 2020). It is therefore plausible that greater resilience serves as a catalyst for managing ongoing health-related stressors, attenuating negative affectivity, and engaging in more physical activity, which contributes to the preservation of physical function. At the same time, the reverse pathway cannot be ruled out, as higher levels of physical function may also facilitate a higher initial level of resilience or promote subsequent enhancement of resilience. Although the present study cannot determine temporal precedence or causality, it clarifies that resilience and physical function are significantly concordant over the years. This concordance sheds light on a new dimension of understanding by suggesting that these two constructs are better conceptualized as interdependent components of the broader health process in people aging with disability.

These data also suggest that an improving slope of resilience over time may be more protective than simply having a high baseline level, in terms of physical function trajectories. Individuals in the mid-improving resilience class who exhibited the steepest upward slope in resilience were the most significantly overrepresented in the high-stable physical functioning class. The process of active building or regaining resilience may accurately capture how people aging with disability successfully surmount the dual challenges of disability and aging and adapt to life transitions, which offers a more dynamic and meaningful indicator of one’s capacity to maintain physical functioning across time than a single-point assessment. Conversely, individuals in lower resilience classes were significantly underrepresented in the high-stable functional class, suggesting a decoupling between low resilience and optimal physical functioning. This result supports the gatekeeping role of resilience, which, without sufficient psychological capital, may leave individuals less equipped to meet the multifaceted challenges that undermine physical function.

Collectively, we were able to identify several typologies or groups of participants within our sample based on these concordances. One group was typified by high or improving resilience, paired with high-stable physical function, reflecting a buffering effect and a low-risk profile. Two groups were typified by a combination of low resilience and low, stable or declining physical function, signifying compounded psychological and physical vulnerability and a need of support. Interestingly, one group was typified by improving resilience despite significant deterioration in physical function, providing evidence that some individuals may improve in psychological outlook in the presence of worsening physical health and ability.

These typologies may have clinical implications, as they underscore the need for stratified interventions based on individual profiles. The combination of resilience and physical function could influence, for example, the approach to the intensity and nature of rehabilitative care. Individuals at greatest risk (e.g., those with both low resilience and poor function) may be prioritized for multidisciplinary rehabilitation that includes structured psychological support, whereas those with high resilience but declining function may require primarily physical and/or occupational therapies to maintain function. Although speculative, profile analyses such as this one may be useful in identifying groups at risk for negative outcomes (such as losses in community participation) associated with low resilience and worsening physical function.

This study also looked at baseline determinants of trajectory class membership. In resilience trajectories, the most significant predictors of trajectory class membership were baseline depression and baseline sleep disturbance. Higher baseline depression consistently increased the likelihood of assignment to suboptimal resilience trajectories across all reference contrasts. This pattern aligns with previous cross-sectional research revealing an inverse relationship between depression and resilience in people aging with disability (Phutane et al., 2024; Terrill et al., 2016). This study extends the literature by demonstrating that baseline depression not only co-occurs with lower resilience but also significantly predicts divergent long-term trajectories, which implies a temporal sequence in which early mood disturbances may be associated with subsequent trajectories of psychological resilience.

The relationship between sleep and resilience is understudied but has recently garnered growing attention (Guida et al., 2023). Healthy sleep affords essential physiological restoration and repair; thus, it bolsters an individual’s capacity to resist and adapt to stressors and fostering protective pathways (Guida et al., 2023; Mander et al., 2017). Our data supported this relationship, as greater reported sleep disturbance was significantly associated with increased odds of belonging to the low-stable class relative to the high-improving resilience classes. Particularly, sleep disturbance did not differentiate membership among other resilience classes, suggesting that sleep disturbance may act as a trajectory-specific role rather than a universal determinant of resilience.

In the case of physical function, disease-related and demographic factors tended to more closely predict trajectory membership than psychological factors. One of these was duration of disability, which was associated with poorer trajectories of physical function. Importantly, while longer disability duration was a significant predictor, chronological age was not. This stands in contrast to the well-established finding that increasing age is a predictor of functional decline trajectory in the general population (Deeg et al., 2024; Dombrowsky, 2023). It may be that in our sample, disability duration more accurately reflects the accumulated physiological burden and progression of an underlying condition than chronological age per se. Furthermore, this pronounced influence of disability duration offers empirical support for the concept of premature aging in this population, which highlights the importance of early and periodic interventions (e.g., regular mobility evaluations, preventive screenings for secondary conditions, and tailored rehabilitation services).

Only one baseline factor predicted class membership in both resilience and physical function trajectories, and this was self-efficacy. Our questionnaire specifically addressed disease management self-efficacy, which was found to significantly predict both favorable resilience and physical functioning trajectories, suggesting a broad protective role across domains. Individuals who feel generally efficacious may experience greater confidence in control over their health and daily lives, which in turn buffers against stressors and negative affect and promotes effective coping skills, which are a core component of resilience. Simultaneously, greater self-efficacy can be a foundational psychological motivator for engaging in health-promoting behaviors, such as adhering to rehabilitation despite physical discomfort, which ultimately contributes to the maintenance of physical functioning. Supporting this interpretation, previous research found that improvements in self-efficacy partially mediated effects of a health coaching intervention on social participation outcomes among people aging with disability (Molton et al., 2025). Self-efficacy not only serves as a psychological resource but may also drive tangible behavioral changes.

This study has several strengths worth noting. The sample included participants with a broad range of physical function and abilities, including individuals with more significant or severe functional impairments (e.g., in the Very low-declining trajectory group). Populations experiencing more severe impairments, especially those associated with early-onset disability, remain underrepresented in aging research. By including this harder-to-reach subgroup over the 7-year period, this study provides valuable insights into the full continuum and diversity of aging experiences across varying levels of impairment. Moreover, this study extends the scope of resilience research beyond acute or subacute stages. Whereas previous literature tends to explore immediate or short-term responses following disability onset (Bonanno et al., 2012; Marwitz et al., 2018), this study explores long-term resilience trajectories among community-dwelling people aging with disability. By employing GMM, our analysis advanced the field by moving beyond average group trajectories to uncover unobserved heterogeneity and identify subgroup-specific patterns. This approach provides a more refined understanding of how resilience and physical function trajectories interrelated within distinct subgroups.

Despite its contributions, this research has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, although the four-wave design spanned 7 years, the relatively longer interval between the T1 and T2 may reduce temporal sensitivity. Additionally, while the sample was diverse in terms of diagnosis, physical function, and income, it was nearly homogeneous in terms of race/ethnicity and education. The vast majority of participants were white and tended to have college degrees, which significantly limits the generalizability of the findings. Efforts for more diverse samples of people aging with disability are needed to improve representation, generalizability, and accuracy of results. Also, this study primarily focused on resilience and physical functioning and therefore did not encompass other important dimensions of successful aging, such as social functioning. In addition, several potential determinants of our outcomes, including environmental factors (e.g., housing conditions, neighborhood), diagnosis-specific information (e.g., type of MS or MD and levels of SCI), health-related information (e.g., multimorbidity), and disability-related information (e.g., use of assistive devices), were not considered. Future research should estimate trajectories across additional dimensions and incorporate a wider range of predictors to more fully capture how structural and social contexts shape aging trajectories among people aging with disability.

Collectively, the heterogeneous dynamics observed within both domains, together with significant over‑ and under‑representation across several pairings, highlight the concomitant variability and complexity of these trajectories. These findings underscore the value of trajectory‑based classifications for revealing cross‑domain disparities that may be obscured by apparent stability in a single domain and support the need for refined risk‑stratified approaches to support successful aging in people with disability.
