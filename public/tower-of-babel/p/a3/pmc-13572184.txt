Comparative evaluation of different chilling methods on carcass and meat quality traits of pigeons
Authors: Shraboni Jhora Badhan, Most. Mustary Mahbub, Shuoshuo Lv, Jannatul Fardous Juthi, Md. Siam Ul Bari, Md. Mushfiquer Rahman Raihan, Hasan Mohammad Murshed, Shuangshuang Guo, Zhengfan Zhang, Md. Abul Kalam Azad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effect of different chilling methods on carcass traits, physicochemical properties, and sensory attributes of indigenous pigeon (Columba livia domestica) carcasses. Two hundred pigeon’s carcass was assigned to four treatment groups: no chilling (control), iced water chilling (30 min), and conventional chilling (3 h and 24 h) at 4°C. After the birds were slaughtered, their carcass was assigned into the treatment groups. Then carcass rate and dressing rate were calculated for all treatment groups. The breast muscle was used to measure physicochemical properties, including pH, color, water-holding capacity, cooking loss, shear force, and proximate analysis. Among the different chilling methods compared, the highest carcass rate was observed in the 30 min iced water chilling (42.73%), while the lowest rate was found in the 24 h chilling (35.78%). The dressing rate and retail cuts were both significantly decreased (P < 0.05) after 24 h of chilling compared to the others. In 3 h, conventional chilling moisture content was significantly reduced, but protein, fat, and ash content were increased to a major extent. The conventional chilling showed the highest (P < 0.05) water-holding capacity and the lowest pH, cooking loss, and shear force value. A lower L* (lightness) value and a higher a* (redness) value was observed (P < 0.05) at 3 h of conventional chilling. In sensory evaluations, the 3 h chilled carcasses were scored higher in juiciness, flavor, and overall acceptability. The short-term conventional chilling method is the most effective one for improving the physicochemical and sensory attributes of pigeon meat.

Introduction

Globally poultry meat production is expected to rise by 2% to reach 109.6 million tons by 2026 (USDA, 2025). Asia is the largest poultry meat-producing region in the world, which shared 38.6% (54.8 million tons) of global white meat production in 2024 (Learmonth, 2025). Among all the poultry species, chickens, along with turkeys, ducks, and geese, hold a significant role in global meat production (Kober et al., 2024). The continued popularity of commonly consumed poultry species has led to more scientific and commercial interest in finding niche poultry meat sources including quail, turkey, ostrich, waterfowls, guinea fowls and pigeon (Mnisi et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2019)

Among these, pigeon meat is becoming a prominent type of niche meat in certain geographic regions (Bostami et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025). Pigeons are represented as a promising poultry species due to their low production cost and rapid growth rates (Sule et al., 2025). Pigeon meat is commonly marketed as squab and is valued for its high-quality protein, beneficial fatty acid composition, and unique sensory attributes (Becker, 2008; Sales and Janssens, 2003).

Proper post slaughter processing is very crucial to get maximum nutritional benefits and keep the quality of the meat. Among the various post-slaughter processing, carcass chilling is the first stage, and it has a direct effect on the physicochemical qualities and nutritional value of the meat.

The poultry industry widely practices two main methods, conventional air chilling and water immersion chilling (James et al., 2006). The method of water immersion chilling is commonly practiced due to its effectiveness in chilling quickly; however, it also leads to increased water absorption, resulting in greater drip and cooking losses (Huezo et al., 2007b). Conventional chilling, which is more prevalent in Europe and Canada, prevents water absorption; however, it may result in carcass dehydration and surface discoloration (James et al., 2007). As chicken dominates the poultry industry in terms of both production and consumption, various chilling methods have been studied primarily in chickens. But there is relatively limited research available on the effects of different chilling methods on pigeon carcasses all over the world (Keshri et al., 1989). Moreover, pigeon carcasses respond differently to chilling compared to chickens (Guo et al., 2020; Shapiro and Domyan, 2013) as they are biologically different. In order to support a sustainable pigeon production sector, advancements in research on the quality of pigeon meat have been published in recent years (Wang et al., 2025). Most of these advancements have concentrated on the pre-slaughter stage, emphasizing feeding techniques, management procedures, and live bird handling (Lan et al., 2025). However, there have been limited scientific studies on post-slaughter processing practices, particularly chilling methods. These major information gaps were the reason for the current study.

Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to compare the effects of different chilling methods iced water immersion (30 min), conventional chilling (3 h and 24 h) on the characteristics of pigeon carcasses. In addition, it aimed to determine the most effective chilling method to enhance meat quality and meet the growing consumer demand, while also establishing baseline dataset for future research.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This experiment was carried out in accordance with Animal Welfare and Experimentation Ethics Committee (AWEEC) No: AWEEC/BAU/2025(2)/67(a).

Experimental design

A total of 200 indigenous male pigeons (Columba livia domestica) were purchased from the local market and transported to the Meat Research Unit under the Department of Animal Science at Bangladesh Agricultural University. The feed and water were withdrawn for twelve hours before slaughter. The pigeons were identified and live weight (g) were recorded immediately before slaughter. The pigeons were exsanguinated according to humane method. After slaughter, the blood, feathers, head, feet, and giblets were removed. Every carcass was treated as an individual experimental unit and was randomly allocated to one of the four treatments (n = 50). Each treatment included 10 replicates, with 5 carcasses assigned to each replicate. The four treatments were no chilling (control), iced water chilling (30 min at 4°C), 3 h conventional chilling (4°C) and 24 h conventional chilling (4°C). After that, carcass weight, breast weight, leg weight, wing weight, and back weight were recorded. Carcass rate (%), dressing rate (%) were calculated according to Park et al. (2021).Carcassrate(%)=Carcassweight(g)Liveweight(g)×100Dressingrate(%)=Allretailscutweight(g)Carcassweight(g)×100

Proximate composition

The proximate components of meat samples were determined in accordance with the AOAC official method 932.06 (AOAC, 2005). The moisture content was determined using a micro-oven at 105°C for 24 h, while ash content was assessed using a muffle furnace at 550°C for 5 h. The crude protein content of all samples was determined using the Kjeldahl technique, whereas the crude fiber content was measured by digesting samples in 1.25% H₂SO₄ and then 1.25% NaOH. The Soxhlet device was used to determine the crude fat in all samples.

pH

After the end of the treatment for each group the pH measurement was performed by the direct method using a pre-calibrated portable pH meter (HI98163, HANNA Instruments, Australia). The tip of the above pH meter was inserted into the sample until a stable reading was obtained. The pH reading was recorded from three different regions of each sample, and then the mean value was calculated (Islam et al., 2022).

Color

The color of the outer surface of the breast samples was measured using instrumental color measurements in the CIE L*, a*, b* system, which was carried out using a NR20XE precision colorimeter (Shenzhen, China). As a reference a white standard plate (CIE L*: 97.83; CIE a*: –0.43; CIE b*: 1.98) was used (Park et al., 2021).

Water holding capacity

The water-holding capacity of the sample was measured in terms of dry matter content using a modified centrifugation method, as described by Szmanko et al. (2021). Briefly, about 1 g of breast muscle was placed into 2 ml Eppendorf tubes and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. After centrifugation, the tubes were decanted and reweighed to obtain the post-centrifugation weight. The samples were then dried in a hot air oven at 105°C for 24 hours until a constant weight was achieved to determine dry matter content. Water-holding capacity was calculated as the remaining moisture in the meat sample on the basis of the original sample.

The water holding capacity was calculated using the following formula:WHC(%)=Weightaftercentrifugation−DryweightInitialweight×100

Cooking loss

Approximately 10 g of breast muscle was placed in polyethylene bags and then put into a hot water bath (Farberware Bronx, NY) at 40°C and cooked until the samples reached a core temperature of 71°C. Then the Samples were removed from the water bath and reweighed the sample after 10 minutes of surface drying (Mustari et al., 2025). The results were expressed as the cooking loss percentages using the following formula:Cookingloss(%)=(weightbeforecooking−weightaftercooking)weightbeforecooking×100

Shear force

The samples used for cooking loss were collected and cut into 1.27-cm cores which later were placed perpendicular to the muscle fiber orientation on a Warner-Bratzler shear machine (G-R Elec. Mfg. Co, Manhattan, KS) to determine the mechanical force (N) required to shear the muscle fibers. An average shear force was calculated and recorded for each sample (Mustari et al., 2025).

Sensory evaluation

Sensory attributes were evaluated according to the 9-point hedonic liking scale described by Sheng et al. (2025). The samples were collected from each treatment group, taken from the breast muscle of the pigeon. The samples were cleaned and boiled in a water bath until the inside temperature reached 71°C (no spices were used) at the Meat Research Unit under the Department of Animal Science. The boiled samples were placed on a plate and presented to fifty trained panelists for assessment. Participants were instructed to chew the boiled meat samples associated with each treatment (control, iced water, 3 h, and 24 h conventional chilling, designated as 764, 321, 389, and 703) and evaluate them for color, tenderness, juiciness, flavor, and overall acceptability using the hedonic scale ranging from 1 (dislike extremely) to 9 (like extremely). Panelists were given toothpicks, and a water bottle was served to each individual for cleaning their mouths after tasting each sample, ensuring that there was no residual effect of flavor between the treatment samples.

Statistical analysis

The dataset was recorded in Microsoft Excel 2021 and analyzed with Minitab statistical software (version 23.0; Minitab LLC, State College, PA, USA) for Windows through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by using the Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test at a 5% probability level (α = 0.05).

Carcass rate also responded in a similar manner. Due to higher moisture absorption in iced-water chilling, carcasses exhibited the highest carcass weight as well as carcass rate. Proximate composition

The different chilling methods affect the proximate composition (%) of the pigeon carcass (Table 3). Moisture content was reduced, but protein, fat, and ash content were increased to a major extent (P < 0.05) in the case of 3 h conventional chilling. This result indicated that the proximate component had become more concentrated when the carcass lost moisture during chilling (Skarovsky and Sams, 1999). Conventionally chilled carcasses with a 3 h chilling time had lower moisture content, whereas carcasses with a 24 h chilling time had higher moisture content. This finding aligned with previous research in which Afolayan et al. (2023) found a link between the duration of the chilling period and the decrease in moisture content; additionally, Dutra et al. (2025) found that extended chilling periods resulted in more moisture retention in the carcass. Physicochemical properties

The effects of chilling type had a significant effect on pH (P < 0.05). As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the iced water chilling treatment significantly increased pH. This result indicated that iced water chilling slowed the rate of post-mortem glycolysis by rapidly decreasing muscle temperature, thereby limiting lactic acid accumulation, which consequently elevated pH values. This finding is consistent with Jeong et al. (2011), who reported that water-chilled chicken breasts exhibited significantly higher pH values compared to those subjected to air chilling and evaporative air chilling. In contrast, conventional chilling maintained a lower pH level than the control group, and the lowest pH level (P < 0.05) was observed in the 24 h conventional chilling. This finding indicated that the prolonged conventional chilling induced glycolytic activity, resulting in increased lactic acid production. Similarly, Huezo et al. (2007a) found that air-chilled broiler breast muscles had lower pH values compared to water-chilled ones, due to the rapid onset of rigor mortis.Fig. 1Physicochemical properties: A. pH, B. A significant reduction in cooking loss percentage (Fig. 1B) was observed by different chilling methods. Among the chilling methods, the highest reduction was shown by 0.5 h of iced water chilling. The higher cooking losses observed in immersion chilling may be attributed to tissue swelling and excessive moisture absorption during the chilling process, which is subsequently released upon cooking. Similar results have been reported in broilers, where conventional chilling generally results in lower cooking loss compared to water immersion (Huezo et al., 2007b; Nauman et al., 2020). However, Perumalla et al. (2011) found no significant difference in cooking loss in marinated breast fillets between the air and water immersion chilling systems.

The water-holding capacity is a critical quality attribute for meat and meat products. It directly influences product appearance, production efficiency, and consumption aspects such as juiciness (Zhuang et al., 2008). Our study showed that WHC (Figure 1C) was significantly increased by conventional chilling and showed a reduction (P < 0.05) by half an hour of iced water chilling compared to the control value. The decrease in water-holding capacity observed with iced water chilling could be associated with rapid temperature declines, which may harm muscle fibers and diminish the proteins' ability to retain water. On the other hand, conventional chilling facilitates a slower decrease in temperature, preserving protein integrity and minimizing drip loss. These findings align with previous data indicating that chilling procedures affect meat water retention and texture. Research on chickens has demonstrated that air chilling (AC) did not influence water holding capacity (WHC) in comparison to immersion chilling (IC). Moreover, air chilling (AC) did not substantially affect cook yield, drip loss, bound water, or water uptake in comparison to immersion chilling (IC) or no chilling, suggesting that air chilling might successfully preserve water-holding capacity (WHC) without adversely influencing tenderness (Carroll and Alvarado, 2008; Huezo et al., 2007a; Perumalla et al., 2011). Collectively, these findings substantiate the assertion that conventional, slower chilling techniques more effectively preserve meat quality characteristics compared to short-term iced water chilling.

The Warner-Bratzler device is the most widely used method for measuring meat texture or softness; the more tender the meat, the lower the shear force (Chang et al., 2023; Nauman et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 1D, short-term chilling or no chilling had no role in changing shear force values. However, half an hour of iced water chilling dramatically increased (P < 0.05) the toughness of pigeon meat. It has been reported that increased shear force value in iced water-chilled carcasses was probably caused by cold shortening due to rapid temperature reduction in muscle (Dunn et al., 1995; Marsh and Leet, 1966). On the other hand, an improvement in tenderness was shown by 24 h conventional chilling. This finding is consistent with Thielke et al. (2005), who reported that shear force values in broiler breast fillets stored at temperatures between 2 and 4°C decreased significantly after 6 h of aging, due to proteolytic breakdown of myofibrillar proteins.

Instrumental color

Changes in CIE Lightness (L*), Redness (a*), Yellowness (b*), chroma (c*), and hue values (h*) throughout the study are shown in Table 4. Iced chilling significantly increased the L* value and decreased the a* value compared to the control value. 24 h conventional chilling significantly increased (P < 0.05) both the L* value and the b* value compared to the control. 3 h of conventional chilling and meat without chilling did not induce any remarkable changes in the CIE (L* a* b*) values. 24 h chilling showed discoloration in the color profile. This result may have been caused by iced water chilling, which increased L* values and decreased a* values by enhancing light scattering and diluting myoglobin through water absorption and surface moisture. In contrast, 24 h conventional chilling increased L* and b* values, possibly due to myoglobin oxidation to metmyoglobin and lipid oxidation, resulting in paler, yellower meat. Pigment and structural changes were minimal after three hours of conventional chilling or no chilling. Comparable findings have been expressed in poultry research. Huezo et al. (2007b) found that immersion-chilled breast fillets exhibited significantly elevated L* values, indicating a lighter look, while displaying reduced a* and b* values compared to air-chilled carcasses. Zhuang et al. (2008) similarly indicated that immersion chilling elevated lightness, whereas air chilling increased redness and yellowness. The color variation corresponds to moisture evaporation and surface desiccation: air-chilled carcasses have a darker and more translucent appearance due to less surface moisture, while immersion chilling enhances light reflection, resulting in lighter yet less red and yellow flesh (Huezo et al., 2007a). The present study indicated that the elevated lightness associated with iced water chilling and the increased redness and yellowness observed under conventional chilling align with these mechanisms. The chilling method and duration significantly affected the color of pigeon meat; immersion chilling produced lighter, paler flesh, but conventional chilling preserved greater redness and yellowness values; however, prolonged storage led to discoloration. These results aligned with the findings of Chang et al. (2023), Huezo et al. (2007a), Huezo et al. (2007b), Nauman et al. (2020), and Zhuang et al. Summary and conclusions

The iced water chilling caused an increase in carcass weight, simultaneously impacting negatively on tenderness and color profile. The 3 h conventional chilling method significantly improves pigeon meat quality by increasing water holding capacity, reducing cooking loss, and enhancing tenderness and color profiles, while the 24 h conventional chilling process negatively impacted both the color profile and dressing percentage. All over the 3 h conventional chilling method shows to have positive effects on the carcass characteristics as well as the physicochemical and sensory attributes of pigeon meat.
