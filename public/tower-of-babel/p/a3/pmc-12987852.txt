Association and predictive value of C-reactive protein, fibrinogen and periodontal indices in periodontitis severity : a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ziyue Yang, Jingci Zhu, Shuo Sun, Tongtong Hu, Qin Yu, Can Xiao, Lifang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo assess associations between systemic inflammatory markers, periodontal parameters, and their combined ability to identify severe periodontitis.Materials and methodsA total of 215 patients with Stage I-IV periodontitis were included. Periodontal parameters (PLI, GI, BI, PPD, and CAL) were recorded, and fasting blood samples were collected to measure serum CRP and plasma fibrinogen. Associations between inflammatory markers and periodontal parameters were analyzed using correlation and trend analyses. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate the combined predictive performance of CRP, fibrinogen, PPD, and CAL in distinguishing Stage IV from Stage III periodontitis.ResultsAmong 215 patients across four periodontitis stages, age differed significantly by stage (P < 0.05), whereas gender, hypertension, and diabetes did not. PPD and CAL were strongly associated with periodontitis staging (P < 0.0001), while PI, GI, and BI showed no significant stage-related differences. Systemic inflammatory markers CRP and fibrinogen were significantly associated with periodontitis stage overall (P < 0.05). CRP correlated with PPD, CAL, and fibrinogen (P < 0.05), whereas fibrinogen was associated only with CRP. A composite model integrating CRP, fibrinogen, PPD, and CAL demonstrated good discriminatory ability for severe periodontitis (AUC = 0.885). In multivariable analysis, CRP, PPD, and CAL remained independently associated with Stage IV disease.ConclusionSystemic inflammatory markers, particularly CRP, provide complementary information to established periodontal parameters. A composite model integrating CRP with PPD and CAL effectively identified severe periodontitis.Clinical relevanceAssessment of systemic inflammatory burden, especially CRP, may enhance risk stratification and support more individualized monitoring of patients with advanced periodontitis when used alongside routine clinical measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00784-026-06775-1.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease initiated by dysbiosis of the subgingival plaque microbiota, which triggers a dysregulated host immune-inflammatory response under the influence of genetic, environmental and behavioral risk factors, ultimately leading to gingival bleeding, periodontal pocket formation, and alveolar bone resorption [1]. Globally, the prevalence of periodontitis ranges from approximately 4% to 76% in developed countries and from 50% to 90% in developing countries, with severe forms affecting about 3%-18% and 8%-46% of the population, respectively [2, 3]. Beyond its local manifestations, periodontitis is increasingly recognized as a condition with systemic relevance, as persistent periodontal infection and inflammation may contribute to systemic immune activation and an elevated inflammatory burden [4]. Contemporary evidence supports periodontitis as a multifactorial disease driven by a dysbiotic subgingival biofilm rather than specific pathogens alone, with periodontal tissue destruction largely mediated by an exaggerated or dysregulated host immune response [5]. Systemic and behavioral risk factors, including aging and hypertension, further modulate host-microbiome interactions and contribute to disease progression and severity [6–8].

The concept of periodontal medicine, first proposed by Offenbacher in 1996 [9], established periodontitis as a condition with important systemic implications. Since then, extensive evidence has demonstrated associations between periodontitis and a wide range of systemic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and adverse pregnancy outcomes [10]. These associations are now widely accepted and are thought to be mediated by chronic systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and shared risk factors. Despite this well-documented systemic relevances, however, less attention has been directed toward whether commonly used systemic inflammatory markers can reflect or predict the severity and progression of periodontal disease itself, which remains an important clinical challenge.

Accumulating evidence indicates that periodontitis is closely linked to systemic inflammatory burden. Mainas et al. reported that periodontitis is associated with elevated systemic inflammatory markers and may contribute to the onset and progression of systemic complications [11]. Among these markers, C-reactive protein (CRP) has received particular attention. CRP is an acute-phase protein synthesized primarily by hepatocytes in response to inflammatory stimuli and is widely used in clinical practice to monitor both acute and chronic inflammatory conditions [12, 13]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that patients with moderate to severe periodontitis exhibit elevated circulating CRP levels compared with healthy individuals [14–16]. These findings suggest a bidirectional relationship in which periodontal inflammation contributes to systemic inflammation, while elevated CRP may, in turn, exacerbate periodontal tissue destruction through inflammatory pathways. Moreover, increased CRP levels in patients with periodontitis have been associated with a higher risk of systemic diseases, underscoring the importance of periodontal health in maintaining overall systemic well-being [17].

In addition to CRP, growing evidence highlights an important immunomodulatory role of fibrinogen (Fg) and its polymerized form, fibrin, at the oral mucosal barrier. Fibrinogen is a liver-derived plasma glycoprotein and the precursor of fibrin in the coagulation cascade. Beyond its role in hemostasis, fibrinogen actively participates in systemic inflammation by promoting platelet aggregation, endothelial and smooth muscle cell proliferation, and increasing blood viscosity, thereby contributing to vascular dysfunction and inflammatory responses [18].

Notably, Silva et al. demonstrated that commensal oral microbiota promote localized fibrin deposition in the oral mucosa, which regulates neutrophil effector functions and supports mucosal immune homeostasis [19]. These findings indicate that fibrin functions as an active regulator of innate immunity rather than a passive byproduct of inflammation. Given the central role of neutrophil-driven inflammation in periodontal tissue destruction, dysregulation of fibrinogen-fibrin dynamics may contribute to disease severity and progression. Consistent with this concept, Wu et al. reported significantly elevated serum fibrinogen levels in patients with severe periodontitis, suggesting a link between periodontal inflammation and systemic prothrombotic states [20]. Moreover, elevated fibrinogen levels have been associated with atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease [21], further supporting its relevance as a systemic inflammatory marker. Collectively, these findings provide a biological rationale for investigating systemic fibrinogen as a potential indicator of inflammatory burden and disease severity in periodontitis, and suggest that targeting fibrin–neutrophil interactions may represent a novel therapeutic strategy [19].

Although numerous studies have reported associations between CRP, Fg, and periodontitis, evidence specifically addressing their value in assessing periodontal disease severity remains limited. Most previous investigations have focused on the presence or absence of periodontitis or on its associations with systemic diseases, whereas the ability of these systemic inflammatory markers to reflect the extent of periodontal tissue destruction has not been clearly established. In addition, limited attention has been paid to the integration of systemic inflammatory markers with established clinical periodontal parameters in routine clinical assessment. Therefore, the present study aims to examine the relationships between CRP, Fg, pocket probing depth (PPD), and clinical attachment loss (CAL), and to evaluate their combined value in assessing the severity of periodontitis. By integrating systemic inflammatory indicators with key clinical measures, this study seeks to provide a clinically relevant approach for periodontal risk assessment and identification of patients with advanced disease.

Materials and methods

Study population and ethics statement

A total of 215 patients treated at the Department of Stomatology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, between January 2022 and August 2023 were included in this clinic-based cross-sectional study. Periodontitis was diagnosed and staged according to the classification system jointly developed by the European Federation of Periodontology and the American Academy of Periodontology [22], with patients categorized into Stage I, II, III, or IV based on these criteria.

This study was conducted in compliance with the revised Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Approval Number. 2023378). All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of Stage I, II, III, or IV periodontitis based on clinical and radiographic examinations according to the classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases; (2) male or female participants aged 18–60 years; and (3) body mass index (BMI) ≤ 40 kg/m². Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of smoking; (2) presence of severe cardiovascular, hepatic, or renal diseases, malignant tumors, or autoimmune disorders; (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) use of antibiotics, immunosuppressive agents, or anti-inflammatory drugs within the preceding three months, due to their potential effects on periodontal status and systemic inflammatory markers.

Blood sample collection and laboratory analysis

Fasting venous blood was drawn from each participant in the morning. For plasma, 5 mL was collected in citrate tubes and centrifuged (3000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) within 30 min. For serum, an additional 5 mL was collected in serum tubes and processed identically. All plasma and serum aliquots were stored at -80 °C until analysis. All procedures were conducted by the Department of Laboratory Medicine following standard protocols.

Assessment of clinical and systemic variables

Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, and systemic medical history, were obtained from medical records, structured questionnaires, and face-to-face interviews. Hypertension and diabetes were defined based on documented medical history and/or current pharmacological treatment and were coded as dichotomous variables (present = 1, absent = 0) for statistical analyses.

Clinical periodontal examinations were performed by a single certified periodontist (L.Z.) in accordance with the 2018 EFP/AAP classification guidelines, using standardized protocols and a Williams periodontal probe. PPD and CAL were recorded to the nearest millimeter at six sites per tooth: mesiobuccal, mid-buccal, distobuccal, mesiopalatal, mid-palatal, and distopalatal. The plaque index (PLI), gingival index (GI), and bleeding index (BI) are semi-quantitative ordinal indices, with higher scores indicating greater plaque accumulation, gingival inflammation, or bleeding tendency, respectively. For each participant, index scores were calculated as the mean value across all examined tooth surfaces, as described in previous studies [23–25].

Sample size justification

Sample size was justified based on detecting differences in periodontal parameters across three disease stages using a one-way ANOVA (two-sided and 90% power). Effect-size inputs were anchored to published stage-specific PPD values (mean ± SD: 4.10 ± 0.47, 4.86 ± 0.82, and 6.03 ± 0.76 mm for Stages II-IV, respectively) [26]. Approximately 42 patients were required when using PPD (the smallest between-stage separation) as the conservative driver of sample size. The achieved enrollment of 215 (210 at stages II-IV)participants therefore exceeded the required sample size, providing adequate power for PPD and, consequently for CAL which exhibits larger stage-to-stage differences in the same reference data.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 25.0. For continuous variables, either the rank sum test or the independent-samples t-test was applied, depending on the distribution of the data and the assumption of homogeneity of variances. Categorical variables, including periodontitis stage (I-IV), grade, and demographic characteristics (e.g.,age, hypertension, diabetes), were analyzed using either Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, based on sample size and expected frequencies in contingency tables.

To examine the relationships between inflammatory markers (CRP and Fg and clinical periodontal indices, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted. Furthermore, to assess the predictive value of CRP, Fg, PPD and CAL in distinguishing between advanced stages of disease, a multivariate binary logistic regression model was constructed using data from patients with Stage III and IV periodontitis. In this model, the outcome variable was coded as 1 for Stage IV and 0 for Stage III. Model performance was evaluated through five-fold cross-validation and by fitting the model on the entire dataset. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall clinical features of periodontitis

A total of 215 patients (52.6% male, 47.4% female; mean age: 42.54 ± 12.13 years) were included in the study. The majority were classified as Stage III (63.3%), followed by Stage IV (25.1%), Stage II (9.3%), and Stage I (2.3%). Among all the patients, 7.9% had hypertension and 4.7% had diabetes mellitus .The average CRP concentration was 1.86 ± 0.82 mg/L, and the average fibrinogen level was 2.52 ± 0.47 g/L. Clinical findings showed that the majority of participants presented with moderate to high PLI, with 30.2% scoring level 1, 64.2% level 2, and 5.6% level 3. GI was predominantly moderate (71.2%), with 14.0% and 14.9% exhibiting mild and severe inflammation, respectively. The most frequently observed BI score was level 4 (81.9%), with fewer cases observed at levels 2, 3, and 5. The mean PPD was 6.28 ± 1.95 mm, and the mean CAL was 7.41 ± 2.26 mm (Table S1).

General patient characteristics in relation to periodontitis stages and grades

The general characteristics of patients revealed no statistically significant differences in gender, hypertension, or diabetes mellitus between groups classified as stage I, II, III and IV periodontitis (P > 0.05); however, a significant difference was observed in age (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Further analysis focusing on patients with severe periodontitis (stage III and IV) also demonstrated a statistically significant difference in age (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were found in gender, hypertension, or diabetes (P > 0.05) (Table 2). Additionally, in a subgroup analysis of patients with severe periodontitis (stage III C and IV C), a significant association with hypertension was identified (P < 0.05), whereas age, gender, and diabetes showed no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Correlation of clinical and inflammatory parameters with periodontitis severity

An analysis of clinical periodontal indices (PLI, GI, BI, PPD, and CAL) and inflammatory markers (CRP and Fg) indicated that PLI, GI, and BI were not significantly associated with the four stages of periodontitis (P > 0.05, Table 1). In contrast, both PPD and CAL exhibited strong correlations with periodontitis staging (P < 0.0001, Table 1). Notably, the inflammatory markers CRP and Fg also demonstrated significant associations with periodontitis stages (P < 0.05, Table 1).

Correlational findings between CRP, Fg and clinical periodontal parameters

Based on the above findings, further correlation analyses were conducted to evaluate the relationships between CRP, Fg, and clinical periodontal parameters. In the overall patient cohort, CRP levels were positively correlated with PPD (P = 0.0129), CAL (P = 0.0015), and Fg (P < 0.0001). Although Fg expression was significantly associated with CRP levels (P < 0.0001), no statistically significant correlations were found between Fg and either PPD or CAL (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Correlation analysis of CRP and Fg with clinical parameters of all periodontitis

In patients with severe periodontitis (including Stage III, Stage IV, and their corresponding Grade C classifications), CRP remained significantly correlated with PPD (P = 0.0391/0.0124), CAL (P = 0.0057/0.0077), and Fg (P < 0.0001/0.0017) (Table 4). However, consistent with findings in the overall cohort, Fg showed no statistically significant correlation with PPD or CAL in any subgroup (P > 0.05), despite its continued strong association with CRP (P < 0.001). Moreover, no statistically significant correlations were observed between either CRP or Fg and PLI, GI, or BI at any stage of periodontitis (P > 0.05) (Table 4).

Additionally, across all patients and within subgroups of those with severe periodontitis, the correlation between CRP and Fg was stronger than the correlations between CRP and either PPD or CAL. Among the clinical parameters, the correlation between CRP and CAL was consistently higher than that between CRP and PPD (Table 4).

Predictive value of CRP, Fg, PPD, and CAL for severe periodontitis

The above results demonstrated strong correlations between CRP, Fg, PPD, CAL, and the stages of periodontitis. To further assess their potential for screening severe cases, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted on these indicators in the severe periodontitis group. A predictive model was constructed, yielding a cross-validated area under the curve (AUC) of 0.87, indicating robust performance across different training/testing splits and suggesting good generalizability to unseen data. When the model was applied to the entire dataset, the AUC increased to 0.885, reflecting enhanced predictive accuracy with the inclusion of all available data points (Figure S1).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis further revealed that elevated levels of CRP, PPD, and CAL were significantly associated with increased odds of being classified as Stage IV compared to Stage III. Specifically, each one-unit increase in CRP (mg/L) nearly doubled the odds of Stage IV classification (odds ratio [OR]: 1.97, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.16–3.52, P = 0.016). PPD (mm) also showed a significant association (OR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.06–2.09, P = 0.024), as did CAL (mm) (OR: 1.98, 95% CI: 1.40–2.94, P < 0.001). Although higher Fg (g/L) levels were associated with increased odds of Stage IV (OR: 2.19), this association did not reach statistical significance after adjusting for CRP, PPD, and CAL (95% CI: 0.86–5.91, P = 0.107) (Table S2).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the relationships between systemic inflammatory markers and clinical periodontal parameters across different stages and grades of periodontitis. Consistent with the contemporary staging framework, PPD and CAL increased progressively with disease severity and showed the strongest associations with periodontitis staging, underscoring their central role as indicators of cumulative periodontal tissue destruction. Among systemic inflammatory markers, both CRP and Fg were significantly associated with advanced disease stages, and CRP demonstrated consistent correlations with PPD, CAL, and Fg levels. Notably, a composite predictive model integrating CRP, Fg, PPD, and CAL showed strong performance in identifying severe periodontitis, supporting the clinical value of combining systemic inflammatory markers with key periodontal measures.

Age was significantly associated with periodontitis severity, with patients in Stages III and IV tending to be older. This observation is consistent with previous epidemiological studies identifying age as an important risk indicator for periodontitis progression [27, 28]. Rather than representing age as an independent biological driver, this association is most plausibly explained by the cumulative effect of long-term exposure to periodontal inflammation and tissue destruction. As periodontitis is a chronic condition characterized by repeated microbial challenge and host response, prolonged disease duration increases the likelihood of greater attachment loss and alveolar bone resorption [29]. In this context, age likely functions as a surrogate marker of cumulative periodontal inflammatory burden rather than a direct mechanistic cause of disease progression.

Hypertension was associated with the most severe disease categories, whereas no significant association was observed in mild or moderate stages. This pattern suggests that advanced periodontal inflammation may contribute to systemic vascular dysregulation, consistent with prior reports describing links between periodontal disease and cardiovascular conditions [30, 31]. By contrast, diabetes mellitus was not significantly associated with periodontitis severity in this cohort, which may be partly attributable to the restricted age range of the study population [32, 33].

Among clinical periodontal indices, PLI, GI and BI did not demonstrate significant associations with disease stage or severity. This finding likely reflects the biological characteristics of these indices rather than measurement limitations. PLI, GI, and BI primarily capture current or short-term inflammatory activity, which may fluctuate in response to recent oral hygiene behaviors, probing conditions, and local vascular responses [34–36]. In advanced stages of periodontitis, inflammatory activity may reach a plateau despite ongoing tissue destruction, thereby limiting the ability of these indices to discriminate disease severity. In contrast, PPD and CAL represent cumulative and largely irreversible periodontal tissue breakdown, making them more closely aligned with staging definitions and severity assessment [37].

Although both CRP and Fg are acute-phase proteins synthesized by the liver in response to systemic inflammation, they exhibited distinct association patterns with clinical periodontal parameters in this study. CRP showed consistent correlations with PPD and CAL, whereas Fg was more strongly associated with CRP itself but not directly with measures of periodontal tissue destruction. This differential pattern does not suggest fundamentally different inflammatory pathways, but rather reflects differences in biological kinetics, regulation, and functional roles. CRP is a highly sensitive and rapidly responsive marker that closely mirrors current inflammatory activity and cytokine signaling, particularly interleukin-6-mediated responses, and may therefore better reflect ongoing periodontal inflammatory damage [38, 39]. In contrast, Fg, with a longer plasma half-life and additional involvement in coagulation and vascular pathways, may preferentially reflect a broader systemic inflammatory and prothrombotic milieu, which complements rather than replaces local measures of periodontal tissue destruction [40–42].

In the present study, the multivariable model was explicitly designed to evaluate predictive performance rather than to construct a fully adjusted etiological model. Within this predictive framework, PPD and CAL were included as established clinical reference parameters intrinsic to periodontitis staging, allowing assessment of the incremental value of systemic inflammatory markers rather than redefining the etiological role of these periodontal measures.

Notably, the apparent similarity in mean CRP and Fg levels between Stage I and Stage IV should not be interpreted as a lack of association with disease severity. The relatively small number of Stage I cases, together with substantial interindividual variability in systemic inflammatory status, may obscure stage-wise differences when mean values alone are compared. Accordingly, disease severity was not inferred from isolated comparisons of group means but was evaluated using correlation analyses and multivariable modeling. Within this analytical framework, CRP retained independent predictive value alongside PPD and CAL in distinguishing Stage IV from Stage III disease, suggesting that systemic inflammatory burden conveys clinically relevant information beyond local measures of periodontal tissue destruction alone.

Consistent with this predictive objective, although age and hypertension were associated with disease stage in univariate analyses, their inclusion did not meaningfully improve model discrimination and was therefore omitted during model selection to achieve a more parsimonious and clinically applicable model. This strategy aligns with the study aim of developing a screening-oriented predictive tool, rather than a comprehensive causal or etiological model.

From a clinical perspective, the proposed integrative model may assist clinicians in identifying patients with severe periodontitis who also exhibit an elevated systemic inflammatory profile. Routine measurement of CRP or Fg is not required for standard periodontal diagnosis; however, these markers may provide complementary information in selected patients, particularly those with advanced disease, suspected rapid progression, or relevant systemic comorbidities. Such information may support individualized risk stratification, closer clinical monitoring, and interdisciplinary management.

Recent studies have consistently demonstrated that active matrix metalloproteinases, particularly aMMP-8 and aMMP-9, are sensitive local biomarkers of periodontal tissue destruction. aMMP-8, the active form of neutrophil collagenase, closely reflects ongoing collagen degradation and current disease activity, whereas aMMP-9 is primarily associated with inflammatory cell infiltration and extracellular matrix breakdown [43, 44]. Clinical and translational evidence further indicates that oral-fluid–based aMMP-8/9 measurements provide valuable information on site-specific disease activity and treatment response [45–47]. In contrast, the present study focused on systemic inflammatory burden, as reflected by circulating CRP and fibrinogen levels, which integrate host inflammatory responses beyond the periodontal tissues and may capture broader inflammatory phenotypes associated with severe disease. Accordingly, this methodological focus reflects the specific scope of the present study rather than an inherent conceptual limitation. Importantly, the accumulating evidence supporting aMMP-8/9 highlights their complementary role alongside systemic inflammatory markers [48–50]. Integrating local proteolytic biomarkers with systemic indicators and established clinical parameters may therefore further enhance periodontal risk stratification and provide a more comprehensive assessment of disease activity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits assessment of temporal changes in systemic inflammatory markers. Second, consistent with the systemic focus of this study, local proteolytic biomarkers directly involved in periodontal tissue destruction, such as active matrix metalloproteinases, were beyond the scope of the present analysis. Third, although formal intra-examiner calibration was not performed, all periodontal examinations were conducted by a single experienced periodontist using standardized protocols and the same periodontal probe, which helped minimize measurement variability. Finally, as this was a single-center study, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Despite these limitations, the findings provide meaningful evidence supporting the combined use of systemic inflammatory markers and established periodontal parameters for assessing the severity of periodontitis.

Conclusion

This clinic-based cross-sectional study shows that systemic inflammatory markers, particularly CRP, are associated with periodontitis severity and provide information complementary to established periodontal parameters. A composite model integrating CRP with PPD and CAL demonstrated good discriminatory performance for advanced disease. These findings suggest that systemic inflammatory markers may serve as adjunctive tools to clinical periodontal assessment for risk stratification and identification of severe periodontitis, rather than replacing standard diagnostic or therapeutic decision-making.
