Markers of immune activation and immunotherapy responsiveness are increased in 3D Pancreatic cancer organoids when primed with photodynamic- and chemo-therapy

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is among the most aggressive cancers, currently ranking as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States and seventh worldwide [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Its incidence continues to rise, with projections indicating PDAC may become the second leading cause of cancer mortality in the U.S by 2040 [[1], [2], [3]].

Despite advances in early detection and treatment, outcomes remain poor. Only 10–15 % of patients are eligible for surgical resection at diagnosis, while approximately 30–35 % have locally advanced, unresectable tumors, and nearly 50 % present with distant metastases[[1], [2], [3]]. As a result, the overall 5-year survival rate is just 12 %[1].

For unresectable or metastatic PDAC, systemic chemotherapy is the standard of care. The FOLFIRINOX regimen, a combination of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin, significantly improves survival compared to gemcitabine monotherapy, increasing median overall survival from 6.8 to 11.1 months [[1], [2], [3]]. However, FOLFIRINOX is associated with substantial toxicity and frequent adverse events, limiting its use to patients with good performance status [2,5]. Gemcitabine-based alternatives are better tolerated but only offer modest improvements, highlighting the urgent need for more effective, less toxic therapies.

Immunotherapy represents a major advance in cancer treatment, offering a promising option for several malignancies, including lung cancer, melanoma, and renal cell carcinoma, through immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [[6], [7], [8]]. Among ICIs, monoclonal antibodies targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), or its ligands (PDL-1), have shown remarkable success. By blocking inhibitory signaling pathways, these therapies revive exhausted T cells, enhance effector function, and promote durable antitumor immunity [[6], [7], [8]].

Despite these advances, PDAC remains highly resistant to checkpoint blockade [9,10]. This resistance stems from its poor immunogenicity and highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by a dense desmoplastic stroma, extracellular matrix proteins (ECM), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), immunosuppressive populations, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) [[10], [11], [12]]. These factors not only promote tumor progression and metastasis but also limit vascularization, drug delivery, and effector T-cell infiltration, further contributing to therapeutic resistance and poor prognosis [[10], [11], [12], [13]].

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these barriers. PDT uses a photosensitizer (PS) activated by light of a specific wavelength in the presence of oxygen, generating reactive molecular species that induce oxidative stress and direct cancer cell death [[14], [15], [16]]. Beyond its cytotoxic effects, PDT modulates the TME by disrupting stromal barriers, permeabilizing vasculature, and facilitating immune cell infiltration, a process known as photodynamic priming (PDP). PDP sensitizes the residual tumor cells and their microenvironment to subsequent treatments by modulating cellular pathways associated with drug resistance [14,[17], [18], [19]].

Additionally, PDT has been translated into the clinic for PDAC. A Phase I/II trial of verteporfin PDT in patients with locally advanced pancreatic cancer demonstrated safety, feasibility, and early signals of efficacy (NCT03033225) [20]. More recently, Hanada and colleagues reported the feasibility of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided verteporfin PDT in pancreatic cancer, further supporting the translational potential of this approach [21]. These findings reinforce the rationale for developing PDT-based multimodal strategies to remodel the PDAC microenvironment and improve responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

Preclinical studies indicate that PDT/PDP can act synergistically with irinotecan, a topoisomerase I inhibitor and component of FOLFIRINOX, by enhancing drug uptake and therapeutic response in PDAC models [19,22]. Combining PDT with immune checkpoint inhibitors has also been shown to prolong survival in immunocompetent PDAC models. However, the doses of the chemotherapeutic agent (IRI) and immunotherapy used in this study were high, limiting their translational potential as a combination [23].

PDT/PDP can convert immunologically “cold” tumors, such as PDAC, into “hot” tumors and induce the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby triggering immunogenic cell death (ICD) and promoting antitumor immunity [17,24].

Building on this evidence, we hypothesized that sensitizing the tumors with PDP could potentiate the effects of irinotecan and anti-PD-1 therapy by improving drug penetration while enhancing immune activation (Fig. 1) [17,24,25].

For this, we employed three-dimensional (3D) mouse-derived immune organoids co-cultured with autologous peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). This platform preserves the cellular architecture, stromal complexity, and immune interactions of the original tumor, offering a physiologically relevant and translational system to evaluate combinatorial chemo-immunotherapy strategies [26,27].

Materials and methods

Animals and tumor implantation

All animal procedures were conducted at Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, protocol 2019N000044), Harvard Medical School, Boston, USA. Female C57BL/6 J mice (7–8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were obtained from Jackson Laboratories and housed under controlled conditions (30–70 % relative humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle at 21°C) with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). For tumor implantation, anesthetized mice received pre-operative Ethiqa, and a left flank incision was made to exteriorize the pancreas. A suspension of 5 × 10⁴ KPC (FC1245) cells in 25 µL cold culture medium mixed with 25 µL cold Matrigel (1:1) was injected into the pancreas. The organ was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the incision was aseptically closed.

Generation of mouse-derived immune organoids (MDOs)

One week after tumor implantation, when the tumor size reached 30–50 mm3, animals were euthanized, and tumors were excised and cut into 1–2 mm fragments. Samples were digested with collagenase II (5 mg/mL, Gibco) and dispase (2mg/ml, Sigma) in mouse splitting medium [Advanced DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with Glutamax (1x, Invitrogen), HEPES (1x, Invitrogen), and penicillin/streptomycin (1x, Invitrogen)], for 2 h. Cells were resuspended in Matrigel domes and plated in 3D cultures using mouse feeding medium (mouse splitting medium supplemented with A83–01 (0.5 μM, Tocris), B27 (1x, Invitrogen), mouse epidermal growth factor (mEGF, 50 ng/ml, Peprotech), human fibroblast growth factor 10 (hFGF10, 100 ng/ml, Prepotech), human Gastrin (10 nM, Sigma), N-acetyl-L-cysteine (1 mM, Sigma), mouse Noggin conditioned medium (100 ng/ml) or recombinant protein (0.1 μg/mL, Peprotech), epidermal growth factor (EGF, 50 ng/ml, Peprotech), human fibroblast growth factor 10 (hFGF10, 100 ng/ml, Prepotech), and Nicotinamide (10 mM, Sigma)].

MDOs were validated by H&E and CK-19 staining and compared with the original tissue. Autologous peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using SepMate™ PBMC isolation tubes (Stem cell Technologies). MDO was then co-cultured with the isolated PBMCs (at a 1:1 effector-to-target ratio) for subsequent experiments (Fig. 2A and B). Prior to co-culture, PBMCs were pre-activated with anti-CD28 (2 μg/mL, clone CD28.2, eBioscience) and anti-CD3 (2 μg/mL, clone OKT3, eBioscience) in the presence of IL-2 (150 U/mL) for 3 days to stimulate T-cell expansion (Fig. 2A–B).

Schematic illustration of the experimental workflow. (A) Orthotopic PDAC tumors were established by implanting 5 × 10⁴ KPC cells into the pancreas of C57BL/6 J mice. One week post-implantation, tumors were harvested, and mouse-derived immune organoids (MDOs) were generated in Matrigel domes. Autologous PBMCs were isolated and expanded before co-culture. (B) MDOs were co-cultured with autologous PBMCs and subjected to photodynamic priming (PDP), followed by treatment with nal-irinotecan and/or anti-PD-1 to evaluate combination efficacy. The strategy models how PDP can enhance drug penetration and immune activation, ultimately leading to tumor cell death. Created with BioRender.com.

Dose-response analysis of 3D MDO PDAC models to nal-irinotecan and anti-PD-1 therapy

MDOs derived from two animals were dissociated with Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (1 mL), dispase (2 mg/mL), and DNase I (5 μL of 10 mg/mL) for 10 min, followed by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min) and single-cell preparation using TrypLE (37°C, 10 min). Cells (5000/well) were seeded into 96-well Ultra-Low Adherent (ULA) plates and treated with varying concentrations of irinotecan nanoliposomal formulation (nal-IRI) (0–800 μM) or anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (InVivoMab anti–mouse PD-1 [CD279], clone RMP1–14; Bio X Cell) (0–125 μg/mL) for 72 h. Viability was assessed with 3D CellTiter-Glo (Promega). Results were normalized and plotted as a percentage of live cells defined as relative viability.

Photodynamic therapy

Dissociated MDOs were seeded in 96-well Ultra-Low Adherent (ULA) plates and incubated with Visudyne (0.25 μM) for 90 min, followed by illumination with a 690 nm diode laser (150 mW/cm², 0–50 J/cm²). After 72 h, cell viability was measured using 3D CellTiter-Glo. Data were normalized to represent the percentage of viable cells.

Photodynamic priming and combination therapy

Photodynamic priming (PDP), a sublethal photodynamic therapy (PDT) dose, was calculated using a nonlinear regression curve to determine the inhibitory concentration required to reduce organoid viability by 30 % (IC₃₀). Based on this analysis, a priming light dose of 2 J/cm² was selected. MDOs were irradiated at an irradiance of 150 mW/cm² to deliver the total light dose of 2 J/cm². MDOs were then exposed to an irradiance of 150 mW/cm², delivering a total light dose of 2 J/cm2. Following PDP, MDOs were treated with nal-irinotecan (0–200 μM) and/or anti-PD-1 (0–60 μg/mL) for 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using the 3D Cell Titer-Glo assay, as described previously. For triple-combination therapy, sublethal doses of nal-irinotecan (4 μM) and anti-PD-1 (3 μg/mL) were used. We also performed live/dead (LIVE/DEAD™ Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit, Invitrogen) 72 h after treatment. Images were acquired using the Operetta imaging system and subsequently processed with ImageJ software.

Intracellular uptake of nal-irinotecan and SN-38 formation after PDP

To quantify intracellular nal-IRI accumulation and its active metabolite (SN-38), 2.5 × 10⁴ MDOs were seeded in 24-well ULA plates and incubated for 24 hours before PDP. Groups included: (1) untreated control, (2) nal-irinotecan alone (4 μM), and (3) PDP + nal-irinotecan (4 μM, 2 J/cm², 690 nm, 150 mW/cm²). Twenty-four hours after treatment, cells were washed with PBS, and uptake of irinotecan (Ex/Em: 355/460 nm) and SN-38 (Ex/Em: 355/538 nm) was quantified using flow cytometry.

Evaluation of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)

MDOs (2.5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were treated with PDP or PDP + nal-irinotecan, and levels of calreticulin, HMGB1, Hsp60, and Hsp70 were measured at 24, 48, and 72 h. Cells were stained with anti-Calreticulin-PE (Cell Signaling), anti-HMGB1-PE (BioLegend), anti-Hsp60-FITC (StressMarq), and anti-Hsp70-APC (Miltenyi Biotec). Flow cytometry results were normalized and expressed as percentages.

PD-1 expression in T cells following combination therapy

To determine whether PDP and/or the combination strategy stimulates PD-1 expression on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, PBMCs were co-cultured with MDOs for 3 days following treatment, at a 1:1 effector-to-target ratio, as previously described (Fig. 2). Surface markers used for analysis included anti-CD3-Vioblue, anti-CD8-FITC, anti-CD4-PE-Vio770, anti-PD1 PerCP-Vio700, and anti-CD45-Viogreen (all from Miltenyi Biotech). Flow cytometry was performed using the BD FACSAria™ II (BD Biosciences), and data was analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10, BD).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error (SE) of the mean. When appropriate, statistical analysis was performed using One-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey post-test or the unpaired t-test assessed by GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Results

MDOs derived from PDAC tumors exhibit resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy but respond to photodynamic therapy

Orthotopic implantation of KPC tumor cells into the pancreas of mice was performed, and tumors with matched blood samples were collected 8 days later for organoid generation (Fig. 2).

Histological analysis with H&E confirmed that the 3D organoid structures closely recapitulated the architecture of the original tumor tissue. H&E staining revealed that the original tissue displayed irregular glandular structures with dense epithelial organization and well-defined luminal spaces, characteristic of ductal morphology. The MDOs exhibited a spherical cystic architecture, characterized by a clear central lumen surrounded by a single layer of epithelial cells. The lumen exhibited a hollow appearance, resulting from apical polarization of epithelial cells, a characteristic feature of ductal lumen formation during organoid maturation [28,29]. Immunofluorescence staining for pan-cytokeratin confirmed that the epithelial phenotype was preserved, with strong cytokeratin expression outlining the outer cellular layer. Immunostaining for Pan-cytokeratin, a key marker for identifying epithelial cells and adenocarcinomas, was also performed to validate the epithelial origin of the organoids (Fig. 3A). MDO growth was monitored over time, and experimental treatments were initiated after one week in culture (Fig. 3B).

(C–D) Dose-response analysis of MDOs from two animals exposed to increasing concentrations of nal-IRI (0–800 μM) for 72 h. (E–F) Dose–response analysis of MDOs treated with anti-PD-1 antibody (0–125 μg/mL) for 72 h. Treatment with either nanoliposomal irinotecan (nal-IRI) or anti-PD-1 monotherapy resulted in minimal antitumor activity, with both mouse-derived organoids (MDOs) displaying resistance across all tested concentrations. As shown in Fig. 3C–D, nal-IRI treatment over a broad dose range did not produce a statistically significant reduction in organoid viability in either MDO line. Although the highest concentration tested (800 µM) resulted in a modest 12.1 % decrease in viability, this effect was not statistically significant compared with untreated controls (p > 0.05), indicating a lack of meaningful cytotoxic response.

A similar lack of efficacy was observed following immune checkpoint blockade. Treatment with anti-PD-1 alone did not significantly reduce organoid viability across all tested concentrations (Fig. 3E–F). Moreover, co-culture of MDOs with autologous peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in the absence of additional priming failed to induce tumor cell killing, further confirming the intrinsically immune-resistant phenotype of these PDAC organoids.

In contrast, photodynamic therapy (PDT) induced a pronounced and statistically significant reduction in MDO viability in a fluence-dependent manner (Fig. 3G). Even at relatively low light doses, PDT elicited robust tumor cell killing, with 3 J/cm² reducing viability by approximately 59 % (p < 0.0001). Both MDOs lines exhibited comparable sensitivity to PDT, yielding a consistent IC₃₀ value of 2 J/cm². This sublethal dose was therefore selected as the photodynamic priming (PDP) condition for subsequent combination studies.

These results demonstrate that PDAC organoids are intrinsically resistant to chemotherapy and immunotherapy monotherapies, while remaining highly sensitive to PDT. Importantly, these findings establish PDP as an effective strategy to overcome baseline therapeutic resistance and provide a rationale for its use in subsequent combination treatment studies.

Photodynamic priming enhances chemosensitivity and immunotherapy efficacy in PDAC organoids

Based on the dose-response data presented in Fig. 4, the IC30 for PDT was determined to be 2 J/cm2, which was selected as the sublethal or priming dose. MDOs were primed with PDP and subsequently exposed to nal-IRI (0–200 μM) or anti-PD-1 (0–60 μg/mL). Notably, following PDP, previously treatment-resistant MDOs became responsive to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy, indicating that PDP sensitizes resistant tumor cells to further therapeutic interventions (Fig. 4A and B).

(A) Dose-response analysis of MDOs treated with nal-IRI (4–200 μM) following PDP at 2 J/cm2. PDP significantly reduced organoid viability in combination with increasing nal-irinotecan concentrations compared to PDP alone. (B) MDOs treated with PDP and co-cultured with PBMCs exhibited further reductions in viability when combined with anti-PD-1 (7.5–60 μg/mL), showing a dose-dependent effect. (C) Comparison of combination regimens revealed that PDP + nal-IRI (4 μM) + PBMCs + anti-PD-1 (3.0 μg/ml) achieved the strongest reduction in viability. (D) Representative live/dead staining images of MDOs confirm enhanced cytotoxicity with PDP-based combinations. Green: live cells; red: dead cells. Scale bar = 250 μm. Data are shown as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-test. Different letters above the bars indicate statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05). Asterisks denote levels of significance: **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Following PDP, nal-IRI exhibited a clear dose-dependent cytotoxicity. Low concentrations (4–12 μM) reduced cell viability by approximately 25 %, while 25 μM induced ∼67 % tumor cell death, and the highest dose (200 μM) eliminated nearly all cells (∼98 %) (Fig. 4A). Similarly, resistance to immune checkpoint blockade was overcome by PDP. In co-culture with PBMCs alone, the cytotoxicity was limited to 16.9 %. However, after PDP, even low doses of anti-PD-1 markedly increased tumor cell killing, with 3 μg/mL achieving ∼80 % death and 7.5 μg/mL approaching complete eradication (∼98 %) (Fig. 4B).

To determine if PDP could improve combination therapy, we administered subtherapeutic doses of nal-IRI (4 μM) and anti-PD-1 (3 μg/mL) after PDP treatment. When PBMCs were co-cultured with untreated MDOs, there was no notable cell killing, indicating that immune cells could not infiltrate or recognize the tumor cells. However, treating organoids with PDP before co-culturing with PBMCs reduced cell viability by approximately 66 %, demonstrating that PDP stimulates immune cells to recognize and attack tumor cells (Fig. 4C). Adding nal-IRI further increased cell killing to nearly 95 %. Remarkably, combining PDP, nal-IRI, and anti-PD-1 eradicated tumor cells completely at these low drug concentrations. In contrast, nal-IRI and anti-PD-1 without PDP did not cause significant cytotoxicity, highlighting PDP's essential role in sensitizing PDAC organoids to therapy (Fig. 4C).

Together, these results demonstrate that PDP not only augments the cytotoxicity of nal-IRI and anti-PD-1 individually but also enables synergy when all three modalities are combined. By integrating agents with distinct and non-overlapping mechanisms of action, this multimodal strategy has the potential to overcome therapeutic resistance and minimize systemic toxicity.

PDP increases intracellular uptake of irinotecan and its hydrolysis to SN-38

To elucidate the mechanism of PDP-based enhancement in nal-IRI activity, we explored the intracellular uptake of nal-IRI and its active metabolite, SN-38. Twenty-four hours after irinotecan incubation with PDAC 3D organoids, a 10 % increase in drug uptake was observed (Control vs. nal-IRI, p = 0.0007). In contrast, PDP significantly increased nal-IRI accumulation, resulting in a 2-fold increase in the uptake relative to nal-IRI alone (nal-IRI vs. PDP + nal-IRI, p < 0.0001) and doubling compared with untreated controls (control vs. PDP + nal-IRI, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5A).

The effect of PDP was even more pronounced for SN-38. Without priming, nal-IRI led to only a slight ∼1.4-fold increase in SN-38 accumulation compared to controls (p < 0.0001). However, PDP elevated SN-38 uptake by approximately 2-fold compared with nal-IRI alone (p < 0.0001) and by 3-fold relative to untreated controls (p < 0.0001). These results demonstrate that PDP not only enhances irinotecan uptake but also promotes its metabolic conversion to SN-38 and its cellular retention, thereby enhancing drug potency at the cellular level (Fig. 5B) [22].

PDP and nal-irinotecan induce the release of DAMPs

To evaluate how PDP combined with nal-IRI enhances anti-PD1 efficacy, we examined the expression of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), markers of immunogenic cell death (ICD). DAMP release was measured at 24, 48, and 72 hours after treatment with PDP and nal-IRI, either alone or together. Calreticulin (CRT), an early ICD marker, did not change with nal-IRI alone at 24 hours, increased by 47.4 % at 48 hours, then dropped to 27 % at 72 hours. In contrast, PDP alone led to a sustained CRT increase: 30 % at 24 hours, 68.4 % at 48 hours, and remained high at 72 hours. Remarkably, the combination of PDP and nal-IRI at sublethal doses produced a strong, time-dependent rise in CRT: 70 % at 24 hours, 98.9 % at 48 hours, and peaking at 143 % at 72 hours versus untreated controls. Combination therapy achieved significantly higher CRT levels than either treatment alone at all time points (Fig. 6A).

MDOs were treated with PDP (2 J/cm2), nal-IRI (4 μM), or the combination (PDP + nal-IRI), and DAMPs expression was assessed at 24, 48, and 72 h. Data represent mean ± SD. We also measured HSP70 levels. Nal-IRI alone, like with HSP60, did not cause significant changes. PDP alone and combined with nal-IRI significantly increased HSP70 by 52.2 % and 67.3 %, respectively, at 24 hours. The combination led to sustained HSP70 upregulation: 78.6 % at 48 hours and 92.2 % at 72 hours (Fig. 6B).

For HSP60, nal-IRI, and PDP alone, only minor, non-significant increases were observed (4 % and 20 %, respectively). However, the combination strongly elevated HSP60: 38.2 % at 24 hours and 67.2 % at 72 hours; PDP alone reached a significant 59 % increase only at 72 hours. Nal-IRI alone did not cause significant changes at any point (Fig. 6C).

No significant differences in HMGB1 release were observed at 24 hours; however, by 48 hours, all treatments increased HMGB1 levels: nal-IRI by 60 %, PDP by 94.1 %, and PDP + nal-IRI by 53.8 % compared to the control. These levels remained stable at 72 hours (Fig. 6D). Overall, PDP alone strongly induces ICD signaling, and its combination with low-dose nal-IRI further increases DAMP release, thereby enhancing the immunostimulatory potential of therapy in PDAC organoids

PDP enhances PD-1 expression in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells

To study the effect of PDP/nal-IRI on the immune cell population, we analyzed the CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations for their expression of PD-1. Our results show that after 72 hours, treatment with nal-IRI significantly increased the population of CD4⁺ T cells, whereas PDP alone had no observable effect (Fig. 7A). None of the treatments led to a significant increase in CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 7B). However, all treatment groups exhibited a significant upregulation of PD-1 expression on both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells. Notably, PD-1 expression was elevated across all groups in the CD4⁺ population, while in the CD8⁺ population, a significant increase was observed only in the nal-IRI–treated group (Fig. 7C and 7D). The basal level of PD-1 exposure in our study can be due to the immunosuppressive microenvironment in these MDOs, while the increase in PD-1 expression after treatment can be due to tumor antigen exposure. Importantly, the induction of PD-1 indicates a potential adaptive resistance mechanism, further supporting the basis for combining PDP and nal-IRI with PD-1 checkpoint blockade.

Discussion

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is among the malignancies in which both chemotherapy and immunotherapy have shown limited efficacy, largely due to its dense desmoplastic stroma and immunosuppressive TME [1,10,11]. Furthermore, PDAC is largely unresponsive to immunotherapy, with clinical response rates of approximately 1-2 % in patients [30]. Our findings demonstrate that PDP can function in a dual capacity, serving both as a chemosensitizer that enhances drug penetration and as an immunological adjuvant that promotes antitumor immunity.

The use of 3D tumor organoids offers significant advantages, as they closely recapitulate the molecular, histological, and phenotypic characteristics of the original tumors [26,27]. In this study, we employed organoids derived from the well-established KPC (Kras^G12D/+; Trp53^R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre) mouse model, which is widely recognized for mimicking the progression and biology of human PDAC [26]. The KPC cell line has key driver mutations in KRAS and TP53, the most prevalent genetic alterations in human PDAC, and reproduces the characteristics of this cancer, including therapeutic resistance [26]. The tumor organoid model retains the multicellular and self-organizing capacity of tumors, making it superior to homotypic or heterotypic spheroid models developed from established cancer cell lines [24,31,32].

To evaluate immune interactions, we co-cultured these organoids with autologous peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). This setup enabled us to assess T-cell activity in the same donor, thereby providing a clinically relevant immune environment. Collectively, KPC-derived organoids combined with autologous PBMCs offer a highly translational preclinical platform for testing drug responses and immunotherapy strategies in a personalized treatment setting [26,27].

Prior in vivo work has demonstrated that PDT/PDP can functionally remodel the dense PDAC stroma, improving therapeutic access and efficacy. Obaid et al. developed EGFR-targeted photoactivable multi-inhibitor liposomes that, upon light activation in orthotopic pancreatic tumors, substantially inhibited tumor growth and doubled overall survival at a fraction of the clinical dose of nanoliposomal irinotecan; this was associated with a dramatic reduction in stromal and collagen density and alleviation of desmoplasia [33]. More recently, Bhandari et al. reported that PD-L1–targeted photoactivable liposomes (iTPALs) not only induce immunogenic cell death but also reduce collagen density and tumor fibroblast content and prolong survival in PDAC models [34].

In our study, PDAC organoids that were intrinsically resistant to chemotherapy and immunotherapy alone became susceptible following PDP, reflecting that the combined effects of direct tumor cell killing and stromal permeabilization can overcome therapeutic resistance.

This finding is particularly important, as the emergence of acquired resistance to chemotherapy after prolonged treatment remains a major obstacle in the clinical management of PDAC. Moreover, this combination may enhance the tolerability of these agents, which are often discontinued due to off-target toxicities.

Our o results reveal increased intracellular accumulation of nal-IRI and its active metabolite, SN-38, following PDP, along with enhanced immune-mediated tumor cell killing. SN-38 is up to 1000 times more potent than nal-IRI, and although its conversion from nal-IRI typically occurs via carboxylesterase (CES) in the liver, some tumor cells also express CES, enabling local activation. This finding aligns with recent work demonstrating CES-mediated intratumoral conversion of nal-IRI [32].

Poor intratumoral drug penetration has long been recognized as a major contributor to therapeutic resistance, largely attributed to the dense stromal barrier in vivo. Although our organoid system does not fully recapitulate this stromal complexity, the observed increase in intracellular nal-IRI and SN-38 following PDP suggests that this approach can facilitate drug access to tumor cells. These effects may, in part, mirror in vivo mechanisms in which PDP enhances vascular permeability and modulates physical barriers, as demonstrated in prior preclinical studies [19,22].

In addition, PDP has been shown to downregulate the ATP-binding cassette G2 (ABCG2) efflux transporter, resulting in approximately a 50 % increase in intracellular levels of irinotecan and SN-38, which persists for at least 24 hours. This is particularly important, as sustained intratumoral concentrations of these agents are essential for strong antitumor activity [22].

The synergism between PDP and nal-IRI has previously been shown to reduce metastasis and improve survival (1-year disease-free) in orthotopic xenograft models of pancreatic cancer by downregulating chemoresistance markers, such as CD44 and CXCR4 [19]. Our findings demonstrate that the synergy between chemotherapy and PDT extends beyond direct cytotoxicity to induce immunogenic cell death (ICD). By amplifying the exposure and release of DAMPs, PDP reprogrammed tumor cells into a source of antigens and danger signals that could elicit the innate and adaptive immune systems.

Although nal-IRI and other chemotherapies have been reported to trigger ICD, their effects are typically modest in PDAC. In contrast, and in line with our previous observations, PDP enhanced this response by increasing the exposure and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT), HSP60, HSP70, and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) [17,24].

CRT and HSPs are critical for dendritic cell (DC) maturation and subsequent T-cell priming [35,36]. CRT is a well-established “eat-me” signal and an early marker of ICD, and in our study, the combination of PDP and nal-IRI increased CRT surface exposure by 143 %. During ICD, CRT can translocate from the endoplasmic reticulum to the plasma membrane, where it promotes DC-mediated phagocytosis of dying tumor cells [37,38]. This process is essential for initiating an adaptive immune response, hence enabling cross-presentation to CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells and activation of CD4⁺ helper T cells [37,38].

We also observed increased expression of HSPs, with HSP60 rising by 67.3 % and HSP70 by 92.2 %. HSPs are molecular chaperones with a dual role in immunity, capable of either promoting antitumor immune activation or facilitating the differentiation of regulatory T cells, depending on the context [39,40]. When associated with immunogenic cell death, extracellular HSPs can engage dendritic cells and macrophages through Toll-like receptors (TLRs), thereby stimulating pro-inflammatory signaling and adaptive immune responses [39].

Beyond the induction of ICD markers, growing evidence supports a functional link between photodynamic priming (PDP)–induced tumor cell stress and adaptive immune activation. In advanced 3D heterocellular pancreatic cancer models, De Silva et al. demonstrated that PDP using triple-receptor–targeted nanoconjugates induces immunogenic cell death and directly triggers T-cell–mediated immune responses, providing functional validation that PDP-induced danger signaling extends beyond marker expression alone [24]. Complementing these in vitro findings, PDP with red light has been shown to elicit adaptive immune responses and antitumor immunity in pancreatic cancer mouse models, further establishing PDP as a driver of immune engagement in vivo [17].

Clinical and preclinical evidence demonstrate that therapies capable of inducing ICD can enhance and extend the effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy by promoting durable antitumor immunity [36]. Additionally, some reports have shown that combining targeted agents with chemotherapy can trigger ICD and strengthen adaptive immunity. The EGFR-specific antibody cetuximab, when combined with FOLFIRI, induces ICD that facilitates DC-mediated phagocytosis of dying tumor cells and the development of protective CD8⁺ T-cell memory [41].

Similarly, PDP significantly amplified nal-IRI–induced ICD, suggesting that the benefit of PDP-based combinations may result from increased ICD rather than direct cytotoxicity alone. This is especially important in PDAC, a cancer typically considered immunologically “cold” and resistant to immune checkpoint therapies.

While dendritic cell maturation was not directly assessed in the present organoid–immune co-culture system, the enhanced immune-mediated tumor killing observed following PDP is consistent with these established functional ICD pathways. When autologous PBMCs were co-cultured with untreated organoids, the organoids remained largely viable, reflecting poor T-cell cytotoxicity and the immunologically “cold” environment typical of PDAC. In contrast, adding PBMCs after photodynamic priming (PDP) reduced organoid viability by almost 70 %, indicating that PDP increases tumor immunogenicity and substantially enhances T-cell recognition and killing in PDAC, which is typically limited in this disease.

PDP combined with low-dose nal-IRI increased tumor cell killing compared with either treatment alone. Importantly, adding anti-PD-1 to this combination resulted in the complete eradication of PDAC organoids, even at sublethal drug doses. This effect was accompanied by increased PD-1 expression on CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, indicating engagement of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis [42]. In this context, PD-1 upregulation serves as evidence that the organoid–immune co-culture model captures adaptive immune checkpoint regulation and is suitable for evaluating anti-PD-1 therapy. Consistent with clinical observations, sensitivity to PD-1 blockade emerges when adaptive checkpoint pathways are engaged [42,43]. Thus, our data support a model in which PDP converts PDAC from a checkpoint-refractory disease into one that can be sensitive to anti-PD-1 therapy.

Clinically, this approach is attractive because it integrates FDA-approved agents (verteporfin and nal-IRI) and is supported by early-phase clinical studies demonstrating that PDT is feasible and safe in PDAC [20,21]. By achieving efficacy at sublethal doses of nal-IRI and anti-PD-1, PDP may also mitigate systemic toxicities and costs, thereby addressing key challenges to implementing combination regimens in the clinic.

Nevertheless, our work was conducted in 3D organoid-immune co-culture systems, which, while physiologically relevant, cannot fully capture the complexity of immune trafficking, metastasis, and systemic toxicity. Future in vivo studies are necessary to validate these findings and optimize treatment.

Conclusion

In this work, we confirm that photodynamic priming (PDP) helps reduce the need for high concentrations of irinotecan [19,22,44], while simultaneously converting an immune-silent tumor into an immune-responsive one (Fig. 4). We postulate that this is an impactful finding if it can be repeated in vivo, and appears to be caused by several factors, including: (i) PDP-induced enhancement of intratumoral concentrations of both nal-IRI and its active metabolite SN-38, (ii) increased infiltration of immune effector cells, and (iii) amplification of immunogenic cell death (ICD) through elevated release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), ultimately resulting in increased responsiveness to anti–PD-1 therapy. The findings merit further in vivo examination and will help guide clinical treatments which are ongoing to get improved outcomes. The implications could be that PDP has the potential to make immune checkpoint inhibitors effective in a larger population of PDAC patients and not only those with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H).
