Detection of dengue virus serotypes in Aedes larvae: a multi-location study from Bhubaneswar, India, suggestive of vertical transmission
Authors: Aditi Pal, Swatismita Priyadarsini, Jyotsnamayee Sabat, Ira Praharaj, Arun Kumar Pradhan, Mahesh Chandra Sahu, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue virus (DENV), primarily spread by Aedes mosquitoes, remains a significant public health concern in India. This study focused on Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India aiming to detect DENV serotypes in Aedes larvae to gain early insights into viral circulation. A total of 8,257 larvae were collected from 60 locations, pooled (n = 390) and screened for dengue using real-time-PCR (pan-dengue assay). Serotyping was carried out using serotype-specific RT-PCR targeting DENV-1 to DENV-4. Overall, 60 pools tested positive for DENV. DENV-1 emerged as the dominant serotype across both winter and rainy seasons. DENV-2 appeared only during the rainy season, often in co-infections with DENV-1 in areas such as Bhagabatipur, Katani, and Dashpur. Higher viral loads were detected in Dashpur, BDA Colony, and Acharya Vihar. Persistent detection near locations like Kalinga Hospital and evidence of vertical (transovarial) transmission further emphasize the ongoing risk. The emergence of adult Aedes from larvae confirmed vector species identity. These findings underscore the value of larval molecular surveillance in identifying transmission hotspots and timing interventions. Integrating such data with public health efforts can significantly enhance dengue outbreak prediction and vector control in urban settings.

Introduction

Dengue fever, caused by the dengue virus (DENV), represents one of the most prevalent and rapidly spreading arboviral infections worldwide, exerting a significant burden on global public health [1–3]. The disease is endemic in over 100 countries and poses a substantial threat in tropical and subtropical regions particularly in Southeast Asia, the Indian subcontinent, Latin America, and parts of Africa [4, 5]. In India, dengue remains a major public health concern, with a significant number of cases reported over the past two decades [6, 7]. Factors such as rapid urbanization, population growth, changing climate patterns, and inefficient implementation of vector control strategies have contributed to the escalating incidence and geographic expansion of dengue in both urban and peri-urban settings [8, 9]. DENV belongs to the Flaviviridae family and is primarily transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Aedes mosquitoes, especially Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. These vectors are well-adapted to urban ecosystems, breeding predominantly in artificial containers such as flowerpots, discarded tires, and water storage vessels commonly found in and around households. DENV exists as four antigenically distinct serotypes, DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4. Infection with one serotype confers lifelong immunity to that specific serotype, but not to the others [10, 11]. Sequential infections with different serotypes have been linked to severe clinical outcomes such as dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS), which are often attributed to a phenomenon known as antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE). Therefore, understanding serotype distribution and dynamics is crucial to public health decision-making and outbreak preparedness [12, 13]. Traditionally, dengue surveillance has focused on the detection of symptomatic clinical cases and monitoring of adult mosquito populations. While these approaches are essential, they are often reactive rather than predictive. By the time human cases are reported, transmission may already be widespread, and valuable time for intervention may have been lost. Moreover, adult mosquito surveillance tends to underestimate early viral circulation, especially in asymptomatic or inter-epidemic phases. In contrast, entomological surveillance focusing on immature mosquito stages, such as larvae and pupae, offers a proactive method for early detection of circulating viruses in the environment before human infections become evident. Larval-based surveillance for DENV is gaining traction as a valuable early warning tool [14, 15]. Detecting viral RNA in mosquito larvae not only indicates the presence of the virus in the mosquito but also confirms vertical transmission, where infected female mosquitoes pass on the virus to their offspring. This route of transmission allows the virus to persist in mosquito populations across seasons and in the absence of active human-to-mosquito transmission [15, 16]. Consequently, the presence of viral RNA in larvae provides insights into silent transmission cycles and highlights the importance of incorporating larval virological data into broader dengue surveillance frameworks. The city of Bhubaneswar has emerged as a hotspot for dengue transmission, owing to its rapid urbanization, changing land use, seasonal monsoon rains, and expanding population density. Despite ongoing vector control and public health initiatives, the city continues to experience recurrent outbreaks of dengue. These outbreaks are often characterized by varied serotype circulation and regional differences in vector distribution. Mapping the spatial distribution of DENV serotypes in mosquito vectors, especially during their immature stages, could help identify transmission hotspots, improve outbreak forecasting, and guide targeted vector control interventions.

Materials and methods

Study area

Bhubaneswar is situated in the eastern part of Odisha and experiences a tropical climate, characterized by hot and humid summers, a monsoon season with heavy rainfall, and mild winters. The city is densely populated and undergoes rapid urbanization, with a mix of residential, commercial, and industrial areas.

Study duration

The study was conducted in Bhubaneswar during October 2023–October 2025 (2 year). Extensive field surveys, mosquito larvae sampling, colony maintenance, morphological identification of the emerged adults and molecular detection formed the major study.

Mosquito collection, morphological identification, and colony maintenance

Aedes larvae collection was performed in the morning by using dippers, containers, bowls, and droppers from a total of 60 urban and rural locations, including BDA near RMRC, Chandrasekharpur, Khandagiri, Lumbini Vihar, and others (Fig. 1).

Sampling sites were selected based on high mosquito density and human activity. Larvae collected from mud pots, discarded tires, and discarded containers like plastic cups, coconut shells, desert air coolers, plastic glass, water drums, buckets, etc. from both indoor and outdoor areas (Fig. 2). The collections were brought to the laboratory, identified based on their morphological characteristics [17, 18] and scored to determine which species may be responsible for dengue transmission in the respective locations. The larvae were reared in an insectary maintained at 27 ± 1 °C and 70–90% relative humidity under 14:10 light and dark cycles at Regional Medical Research Centre (RMRC), Bhubaneswar.

Larval indices

Larval indices including the House Index (HI), Container Index (CI), and Breteau Index (BI) were calculated to assess the level of Aedes density in the surveyed areas. The HI was defined as the percentage of houses positive for Aedes larvae or pupae, the CI as the percentage of water-holding containers breeding-positive, and the BI as the number of positive containers per 100 houses inspected. The indices were computed following standard guidelines of the World Health Organization and were used to evaluate dengue transmission risk in the study area [19].

HI = (Number of houses positive for larvae or pupae/Total number of houses inspected) X 100

CI = (Number of positive containers / Total number of containers inspected) X 100

BI = (Number of positive containers/No. of houses inspected) X 100

Viral RNA extraction

Third instar larvae were pooled (n = 20) and placed in a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube. To prevent RNA degradation, 50 µl of RNA Later (Invitrogen) was added. The mosquito larvae were then triturated thoroughly using a micropestle, added 100 µl of PBS and mixed thoroughly to ensure proper homogenization. The homogenate was centrifuged at 5000G for 15 min, collected supernatant and RNA was extracted using MB615M HiMedia viral RNA extraction kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions. To the supernatant (50 µl), 530 µl of lysis solution (C1), 5 µl of Proteinase K, 5 µl of Carrier RNA, and 30 µl of magnetic beads were added. The mixture was vortexed for 15 sec and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Then, 300 µl of ethanol was added, mixed gently by pipetting, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. For the binding step, the tube was placed on a magnetic separator for 1 min, and the supernatant was removed by pipetting. For the first wash, 500 µl of diluted wash solution was added, and the tube was inverted for 3 min. The tube was placed on the magnetic separator again, and the supernatant was discarded. The second wash was performed in the same manner using another 500 µl of diluted wash solution. After washing, the sample tube was dried by placing it on a heating block at 65 °C for 10 min. Then, 50 µl of elution buffer was added to the sample and vortexed briefly. The tube was again placed on the heating block at 65 °C for 10 min. Finally, the sample was placed on a magnetic separator for 1 min, and the supernatant, containing pure RNA, was transferred to a new collection tube. The extracted RNA was stored at − 80 °C.

RT-PCR for dengue detection and serotyping

The PAN Dengue Real-Time RT-PCR was performed by first preparing the master mix for a single reaction using the components: 10.0 µl of 2X One Step Master Mix, 0.5 µl of enzyme, 1.8 µl of PAN Dengue PP mix (containing 0.4 µl forward primer, 0.6 µl reverse primer, and 0.8 µl probe), and 1.5 µl of Actin PP mix (with 0.5 µl each of forward primer, reverse primer, and probe). Nuclease-free water was added to adjust the final master mix volume to 15 µl. The reaction mixture was gently vortexed and briefly spin to collect the contents at the bottom of the tube before addition of the RNA template. Subsequently, 15 µl of the master mix was dispensed into each well according to the plate layout. For the no-template control (NTC), 5 µl of nuclease-free water was added instead of RNA. Each well was sealed with an optical sealer to prevent cross-contamination, and the plate was covered with aluminum foil before being transferred to the pre-PCR or nucleic acid extraction room. Inside the biosafety cabinet, 5 µl of the extracted RNA sample was added to each well, following the layout precisely. The thermal cycling conditions used for PAN Dengue RT-PCR were as follows: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 17 min, polymerase activation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. During setup, the appropriate fluorophores were selected for individual targets in each well, and the sample names were assigned based on the layout. The program was run, and the data were saved in a designated folder. For Dengue serotyping RT-PCR, which was conducted on PAN Dengue-positive samples, the master mix was prepared separately for two reactions, briefly mentioned as 20 µL RT-PCR master mix containing 2x buffer, enzyme mix (Agpath One-Step RT-PCR mix with Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) [20, 21], primers, probes, and nuclease-free water. For DENV1, DENV2, and DENV3, the mix contained 12.5 µl of One Step Master Mix (2X), 0.5 µl of enzyme, 4.5 µl of primer-probe mix (0.5 µl each of forward primer, reverse primer, and probe for each serotype), and 2.5 µl of nuclease-free water, totaling 20 µl of master mix. For DENV4, the reaction included 12.5 µl of One Step Master Mix, 0.5 µl of the enzyme, 3.0 µl of primer-probe mix, and 4.0 µl of nuclease-free water. To each reaction mix, 5.0 µl of RNA sample was added to make the total reaction volume to 25 µl. The cycling protocol used for serotyping began with reverse transcription at 50 °C for 30 min, followed by polymerase activation at 95 °C for 2 min, and 40 amplification cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Negative and positive controls were included as previously described. After sealing the plate, it was inserted into the real-time PCR machine. Targets and samples were assigned in the software, the PCR run was initiated, and the resulting data were saved in a specific folder for analysis.

Results

Based on entomological surveys conducted across 60 locations in Bhubaneswar, a total of 150 households and 500 water-holding containers were inspected for Aedes breeding. Among these, 18 houses and 30 containers were found positive for Aedes larvae. The calculated larval indices were as follows: House Index (HI) = 12%, Container Index (CI) = 6%, and Breteau Index (BI) = 20%. According to World Health Organization thresholds, these values indicate a moderate to high risk of dengue transmission. Higher indices were recorded in densely populated localities such as Jaydev Vihar, Acharya Vihar, and Dashpur, which also showed frequent detection of DENV-1 and DENV-2. The results highlight that persistent larval breeding in urban residential zones contributes significantly to sustained viral circulation and increased transmission potential in Bhubaneswar.

The results of the study, based on Table 1, reveal significant insights into the distribution of DENV serotypes and their corresponding Ct values across various locations during the winter and rainy seasons. DENV-1 was identified as the predominant serotype in all locations during the winter season and in most sites during the rainy season. In contrast, DENV-2 appeared exclusively during the rainy season and was detected in a limited number of areas, indicating a seasonal variation in serotype distribution. Notably, co-circulation of DENV-1 and DENV-2 was observed in several locations during the rainy season, including Analapatna, Andharua, Bhagabatipur, Dashpur, Pitagadia, Katani, Mendhasala, Rathasahi, Poparanga, Bhashanra, and Kanti (Sana). This co-detection suggests the possibility of mixed infections and highlights regions with increased risk for hyperendemic transmission.

Analysis of the Ct values further supports these observations. During the winter season, Ct values for DENV-1 ranged between 21 and 24, indicating moderate levels of viral RNA. The rainy season samples exhibited a broader range of Ct values, from 20 to 25, with lower Ct values observed in locations such as BDA Colony (Chandrasekharpur), Dashpur, Rathasahi, Acharya Vihar, Munduli, and Bhimatangi, reflecting higher viral loads in these regions. Conversely, higher Ct values (25) recorded in Bapuji Nagar, Unit I, Unit IX, and other sites having only DENV-1 suggest comparatively lower viral loads (Fig. 3).

The Jaydev Vihar, sampled during both seasons, consistently tested positive for DENV-1 with a Ct value of 23, pointing to a persistent presence of the virus. Additionally, locations like Katani and Bhagabatipur showed detection of both DENV-1 and co-circulating serotypes, implying they may serve as focal points for dengue virus transmission and serotype interaction. Overall, the findings underscore the dominance of DENV-1 across seasons, the emergence of DENV-2 during the rainy season, and the potential for concurrent infections in certain hotspots, emphasizing the need for sustained surveillance and vector control measures in these high-risk areas. Surveillance reports documented multiple Aedes species across urban and peri-urban habitats, revealing significant spatial and seasonal variations in species composition and dengue serotypes.

Discussion

The current study presents a significant insight into the detection and spatial distribution of DENV serotypes in Aedes mosquito larvae collected from multiple locations within Bhubaneswar. The findings reveal co-circulation of multiple DENV serotypes in larval stages of Aedes mosquitoes, providing critical evidence of vertical transmission and the potential for early-stage dengue virus dissemination within endemic regions. This observation aligns with prior research indicating the ability of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus to transmit DENV vertically, thereby maintaining the virus in mosquito populations even in inter-epidemic periods. A study conducted in Arroyo Naranjo, Havana, by Gutiérrez-Bugallo et al. detected DENV in Ae. aegypti larvae [22–24]. They found evidence of vertical transmission for all four DENV serotypes, with varying minimum infection rates. The study highlighted the role of vertical transmission in maintaining DENV circulation during inter-epidemic periods, emphasizing the need for incorporating viral monitoring in pre-adult stages as a strategy for dengue surveillance and control. Similarly, in Sinaloa, Mexico, Avendaño and colleagues in 2021 [25] demonstrated vertical transmission of DENV-4 in Ae. aegypti. They detected DENV-4 RNA in 41.66% of larval samples, indicating a high minimum infection rate of 22.32%. Genetic sequencing showed 98% identity with circulating human strains, suggesting uninterrupted local transmission cycles supported by larval infection. Their work confirmed that, Ae. aegypti serves not only as a vector but also as a reservoir host through vertical transmission. The study stated the significance of larval screening in areas where DENV-4 is re-emerging or persists between epidemics [26, 27]. A study conducted in Anapoima, Colombia, focusing on transovarial DENV transmission showed 54.7% of households had Ae. aegypti breeding, Virological investigation revealed the presence of DENV RNA, particularly DENV-1, in several larval and pupal pools [28, 29]. Some pools exhibited co-circulation of multiple serotypes, suggesting complex intra-vector viral dynamics. This work underlined the epidemiological importance of monitoring immature mosquito stages in high-risk rural and peri-urban environments and called for using larval viral detection as an early indicator of outbreak potential in the region.

In Tarlac City, Philippines, Balingit et al. [30] found that 2.8% of 359 individually tested female mosquitoes carried DENV RNA, with serotypes DENV-1, DENV-2, and DENV-4. Phylogenetic analysis revealed the concurrent circulation of multiple genotypes, indicating a diverse viral pool within mosquito populations. Although primarily focused on adults, the findings highlighted the need for comprehensive mosquito-based surveillance, which could be enhanced by integrating larval testing to trace vertical transmission and predict outbreak patterns in densely populated urban areas. Various studies examined the vertical transmission of DENV among field-collected Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus in Peshawar, Pakistan [31]. Their data showed that 17.64% of larval pools tested positive for DENV, predominantly serotypes DENV-2 and DENV-3. The detection of DENV in larvae, even during non-epidemic periods, suggested persistent virus circulation through transovarial routes. The study advocated for larval-based surveillance as a complement to adult mosquito and clinical case monitoring, especially in endemic regions with recurring dengue outbreaks. This approach could help in designing preemptive vector control strategies [32, 33].

Detection of DENV serotypes in larvae, specifically DENV-1 and DENV-2, across diverse wards in Bhubaneswar highlights the wide-scale persistence of the dengue virus in mosquitoes. The presence of viral RNA in the immature stages of Aedes suggests an ongoing, silent viral transmission cycle, which can serve as a precursor to human outbreaks. These results are particularly important for vector surveillance and public health planning, as they suggest that routine larval monitoring could act as an early warning system, especially during pre-monsoon and monsoon seasons when mosquito breeding is prolific. Interestingly, the highest concentration of positive samples was identified in areas with high population density and frequent water stagnation, which are conducive to mosquito breeding [34]. This indicates a correlation between urban infrastructure and vector habitat proliferation. The data support the necessity for targeted vector control measures, emphasizing environmental sanitation and the management of water storage practices in dengue-prone zones. Moreover, circulation of multiple serotypes simultaneously raises concern over the increased risk of severe dengue infections such as DHF and DSS, particularly in individuals experiencing secondary infections with different serotype [35, 36]. The epidemiological implication is profound, as it necessitates robust surveillance not just of human cases, but also of vector populations, including their larval stages. Molecular diagnostics like RT-PCR have proven invaluable in detecting DENV in mosquitoes, underscoring their utility in arboviral surveillance. The study reinforces the value of integrating molecular tools with entomological surveys to better understand the dynamics of dengue transmission. Despite its strengths, this study has limitations, including a relatively small sampling window and a limited sample size per ward. Seasonal variation in dengue incidence could also affect larval positivity rates. Future longitudinal studies incorporating climate variables, human seroprevalence data, and expanded spatial sampling would enhance understanding of virus-vector-host interactions [37–39].

Several studies conducted globally have emphasized the significance of larval-based dengue surveillance. In countries like Cuba, Colombia, Mexico, and the Philippines, detection of DENV RNA in larvae has provided critical evidence of vertical transmission and its role in maintaining endemicity during non-epidemic periods. However, such surveillance efforts are relatively limited in India, and there is a paucity of large-scale studies focusing on the presence of DENV in larvae across urban landscapes. This knowledge gap needs urgent attention to complement existing surveillance systems and reduce the burden of dengue [40, 41].

A unique aspect of this study is the use of molecular techniques directly on larval samples. This approach not only confirms transovarial transmission but also enables high-resolution mapping of virus presence at a micro-geographic scale. Such precision can help public health authorities prioritize neighborhoods for intervention based on real-time entomological data rather than waiting for human cases to accumulate. Furthermore, serotype-specific data from larval surveillance can be used to understand the complexity of transmission dynamics, especially in the context of concurrent circulation or replacement of dominant serotypes. For instance, the emergence of DENV-2 in locations already dominated by DENV-1 could increase the risk of secondary infections and severe disease outcomes [42, 43]. In summary, this study emphasizes the value of integrating larval-based molecular surveillance into the current dengue control infrastructure. Detecting viral RNA in Aedes larvae from 60 urban places of Bhubaneswar, highlights the presence of active virus transmission even before the emergence of clinical cases. The findings aim to support an evidence-based framework for early intervention, risk stratification, and sustainable vector management. Given the growing public health threat of dengue in India, such proactive and innovative approaches are indispensable for achieving timely, location-specific, and impactful dengue control outcomes [44, 45].

The novelty of this study lies in its larval-based detection of DENV, which complements existing epidemiological data by offering a more immediate understanding of virus ecology within vector populations. Furthermore, the identification of co-infections in larval pools provides preliminary evidence of complex serotype interactions at the vector level, which may have implications for disease transmission dynamics and vaccine development. Ultimately, this work contributes to a more refined and spatially resolved model of dengue risk in Bhubaneswar, underscoring the importance of integrating entomological and virological data in urban public health planning [46, 47].

Conclusion

This multi-location study in Bhubaneswar provides critical evidence of dengue virus (DENV) circulation within Aedes mosquito populations, revealing the detection of DENV-1 and DENV-2 in larval stages. The presence of viral RNA in immature mosquitoes confirms vertical transmission, indicating that virus propagation can occur before human infections arise. This finding emphasizes the need for early entomological surveillance, as larval infection may precede clinical outbreaks. The detection of multiple serotypes across diverse ecological zones signifies a complex, sustained viral ecology rather than seasonal transmission alone. Integrating molecular diagnostics such as RT-PCR with traditional vector monitoring offers a powerful approach for early detection. Regular screening of larvae could serve as an early warning system, prompting timely interventions like targeted vector control and public awareness campaigns. High larval positivity in densely populated, waterlogged areas underscores the importance of improved sanitation and urban planning. Moreover, the coexistence of multiple serotypes heightens the risk of severe dengue through antibody-dependent enhancement. Overall, this study supports incorporating larval-based DENV detection into routine surveillance programs, advocating for integrated vector management, community engagement, and intersectoral collaboration to mitigate dengue risks and ensure proactive control in endemic urban environments.
