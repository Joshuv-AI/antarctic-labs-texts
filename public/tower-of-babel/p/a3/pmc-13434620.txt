Project delivery methods regulation and performance of construction projects in Uganda
Authors: Wanjusi Febiano, Deepa Krishnan, Nnadi Ezekiel, Rotimi Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda, focusing on the effect of Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) and the moderating effect of regulatory frameworks. Guided by the Resource-Based View (RBV), the research investigates three questions: (1) How does the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda compare? (2) What is the effect of PDMs on the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda? (3) How does the regulatory framework moderate the effect of PDMs on the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda? A mixed-methods design was adopted, combining quantitative data collected through structured questionnaires from 170 construction professionals and qualitative insights from key informant interviews with regulatory and professional bodies. Secondary data from project reports and government audits complemented the primary data. Quantitative analysis involved descriptive statistics to compare sectoral performance, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to assess the effects of PDMs, and Hayes’ PROCESS macro for moderation analysis. Qualitative data were thematically analyzed to contextualize quantitative findings. Results indicate that private projects outperform public projects in cost control and time efficiency, while public projects excel in quality, safety, and risk mitigation. PDMs significantly influence project performance in both sectors, with regulatory frameworks amplifying these effects in public projects but not in private projects. The study contributes theoretically by extending RBV to include regulatory moderation, empirically by providing context-specific insights from Uganda, and practically by informing sector-sensitive PDM selection and regulatory oversight. These findings offer policymakers and practitioners guidance for optimizing construction project performance through aligned delivery methods and governance strategies.Keywords: Project delivery methods, Construction project performance, Regulatory framework, Public vs private projects, Resource-based view, UgandaSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The construction industry remains one of the most critical sectors in any economy, contributing substantially to infrastructure development, employment, and economic growth1. In Uganda, like many developing countries, construction projects play a pivotal role in social and economic development by providing essential infrastructure such as roads, hospitals, schools, and housing2. Despite its importance, the sector is often characterized by challenges including cost overruns, time delays, quality compromises, and safety concerns3. These challenges affect both public and private construction projects and are influenced by various factors such as the choice of project delivery methods, regulatory frameworks, and stakeholder engagement. Project delivery methods (PDMs) constitute a fundamental determinant of project success as they define the contractual relationship, project organization, and the flow of information and responsibilities among parties involved4. Globally, traditional delivery methods like Design-Bid-Build (DBB) have dominated for decades due to their simplicity and well-understood legal frameworks. However, alternative approaches such as Design-Build (DB), Construction Management (CM), Private Public Partnerships (PPP), and Design-Build-Operate-Transfer (DBOT) have gained prominence due to their potential to address limitations of traditional methods, particularly in complex and large-scale projects5. The effectiveness of these delivery methods varies widely depending on project context, including sector (public vs. private), complexity, and regulatory environments6. In Uganda, the public construction sector is typically characterized by bureaucratic processes, stringent regulatory oversight, and involvement of multiple stakeholders, including government agencies, consultants, and contractors7. These factors often result in rigid procurement procedures and slower decision-making, which contribute to delays and cost overruns8. Conversely, private construction projects tend to be more flexible, driven by client-specific objectives, market competition, and a greater focus on cost efficiency and time reduction. This dichotomy manifests in varying performance outcomes across key metrics such as cost control, schedule adherence, quality assurance, safety, and risk mitigation9.

Performance measurement in construction projects is a multifaceted concept encompassing both quantitative and qualitative metrics. Key performance indicators (KPIs) typically include cost control, timely completion, compliance with quality standards, safety management, and effective risk mitigation10. In Uganda, empirical evidence underscores persistent challenges in the execution of public sector projects, particularly related to cost control, timelines, and safety outcomes. Public sector projects frequently exhibit significant cost overruns and inefficient budget performance. Parliamentary oversight of the Auditor General’s findings for the 2023–2024 financial year revealed that taxpayers are paying up to three times more for roads of similar length and quality, with inflated contracts and weak supervision driving excessive costs relative to average estimates provided by the Ministry of Works and Transport Africa11–13. Similarly, government audit and parliamentary data show that large proportions of loan and grant funding for infrastructure projects remain under-utilised or partly absorbed, indicating inefficiencies that often translate into cost escalations and reduced value for money11. Delays are another prominent issue in public projects. Recent government performance reporting demonstrates that about 83% of externally funded initiatives reviewed were behind schedule, with a number stagnating despite substantial funding releases. These delays have been linked to procurement inefficiencies, land acquisition challenges, and slow clearances, all of which impede the timely delivery of infrastructure investments14. Detailed audits of major road infrastructure projects, such as the Busega–Mpigi (or Mpigi) Expressway, have also documented prolonged schedules and rising costs, with the Auditor General’s report noting that the project’s cost more than doubled from its original allocation and its completion deadline was extended by several years due to inadequate planning, funding shortfalls, and payment arrears13,15.

Weak governance and regulatory frameworks further exacerbate performance challenges. For example, delayed payments and accumulating arrears in the road construction sector, including billions in unpaid liabilities, have been reported by parliamentary committees, highlighting poor budgeting and financial management processes that disrupt project execution and compromise safety and quality outcomes16. Moreover, limited compliance monitoring and enforcement of building standards remain ongoing concerns. Although the National Building Review Board (NBRB) exists to oversee building control, safety, and quality standards under the regulatory framework established by the Building Control Act and related codes, practical compliance and inspection capacity remain areas for development in the broader construction sector17. Collectively, these reports illustrate a pattern in which public construction performance in Uganda suffers from cost overruns, extended timelines, governance gaps, and quality/safety risks, pointing to the need for stronger project planning, enhanced financial controls, and improved regulatory enforcement to bolster project outcomes. However, empirical studies on the comparative, and effect of PDMs on project performance for public and private construction projects is lacking. In addition, while the research shows that the regulatory framework exerts a significant moderating influence on public construction project performance by establishing standards, enforcing compliance, and shaping stakeholder behavior18,19, its impact on the performance of private construction projects is lacking. This study therefore addressed these conceptual and contextual gaps. The primary aim of this study is to examine the influence of Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) on the performance of construction projects in Uganda, with particular emphasis on differences between public and private sector projects and the moderating role of regulatory frameworks. Further the study seeks to ccompare the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda across key performance indicators, including cost control, time reduction, quality assurance, safety, and risk mitigation. Also to assess the effect of Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) on the performance of construction projects in Uganda, considering both public and private sector contexts. And finally, to examine the moderating role of the regulatory framework on the relationship between Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) and the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda. In light of these research gaps and aim the following research questions were answered: (1). How does the performance of Public and Private Construction Projects in Uganda compare? (2). What is the effect of PDMs on the Performance of Public and Private Construction Projects in Uganda? (3). How does the regulatory framework moderate the effect of PDMs and Performance of Public and Public Construction Project in Uganda?

Literature review

Theoretical framework

This study is guided by the Resource-Based View (RBV), a widely applied strategic management theory that explains how organizations achieve competitive advantage and superior performance by effectively leveraging resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable (VRIN)20,21. RBV emphasizes that sustained organizational success depends not merely on the possession of resources but on their strategic deployment to create unique capabilities that competitors cannot easily replicate. In the context of the construction industry, project delivery methods (PDMs) including Design-Bid-Build, Construction Management, and Design-Build-Operate-Transfer, can be conceptualized as strategic organizational capabilities. These methods embody managerial expertise, technical skills, coordination mechanisms, and risk management practices, all of which collectively shape critical project performance outcomes such as cost control, timely completion, quality assurance, safety, and risk mitigation22. By framing PDMs as key organizational resources, RBV provides a robust lens for understanding how construction firms can leverage internal capabilities to achieve superior project performance. Recent empirical investigations have broadened the scope of the resource-based view (RBV) within the domains of construction and infrastructure delivery. Drawing upon the findings of Loosmore & Cheung (2023) and Osei-Kyei and Chan (2024), it is evident that the amalgamated expertise encompassing contractual, technological, and governance resources enables project-based organizations to attain competitive advantages through their intrinsic capabilities23,24. This competitive edge positions project delivery models (PDMs) as widely recognized as dynamic capabilities that facilitate organizations in reallocating resources to address the multifaceted challenges and uncertainties inherent in projects, particularly in the context of large-scale infrastructure initiatives.

The RBV further allows for the integration of external resources that interact with internal capabilities to influence outcomes. In this study, regulatory frameworks are conceptualized as external resources that can either enhance or constrain the value derived from PDMs. Public sector projects often benefit from strict regulatory oversight, which enforces accountability, ensures compliance, mitigates risks, and promotes quality outcomes. In contrast, private sector projects generally rely more on flexible internal resources and client-driven decision-making, where the effectiveness of PDMs is influenced more by organizational capacity and adaptability than by formal regulation25,26. The results from latest literature studies reveals the evidence that public sector projects are frequently executed under institutional frameworks that prioritize transparency, accountability, and compliance; conversely, private sector projects tend to be motivated by performance maturity, cost efficiency, and innovation27,28. These distinctions markedly diminish the connections between internal and external resources, thereby highlighting the imperative for sector-specific investigations into the efficacy of project delivery methodologies. Incorporating regulation as an external resource allows the study to examine how internal and external resources interact, reflecting the RBV principle that both types of resources are critical for achieving strategic outcomes. Contemporary critiques assert that institutional pressures and governance practices that substantially affect the execution of public infrastructure projects inadequately account for the resource foundation24. This inadequacy is particularly pronounced in developing nations, where regulatory enforcement, political dynamics, and institutional capacities are likely to exert considerable influence on project outcomes. Despite its explanatory power, RBV has several limitations in construction project research.

First, the sector-specific applicability of RBV remains limited, as most studies have examined manufacturing or service organizations, leaving the public–private construction context underexplored. Construction projects involve complex stakeholder interactions, regulatory oversight, and dynamic environments that differ significantly from other sectors29. Second, while RBV emphasizes the strategic value of internal resources, it provides limited guidance on the moderation effect of external factors, such as regulation, policy, or industry standards, in influencing performance outcomes20. Finally, RBV assumes relatively stable resources; however, construction projects are inherently temporary and context-dependent, requiring dynamic adaptation and real-time deployment of resources to meet project-specific challenges30. Despite these theoretical gaps, the RBV remains highly relevant to this study. It offers a clear framework for examining how internal resources (PDMs and organizational capabilities) and external resources (regulation) jointly influence construction project performance, particularly in Uganda’s evolving construction sector. By extending RBV to include regulation as a moderating resource, the study addresses both internal and external determinants of project success, providing nuanced insights into sector-specific dynamics. This approach highlights how the strategic deployment of internal capabilities, aligned with regulatory conditions, can optimize outcomes in both public and private construction projects, thereby contributing to both theory and practice in project management. The theory therefore, provided a foundation for addressing these gaps by factoring in the influence of resources through the cost element and the moderation effect of the regulatory framework regarding the context of public and private construction projects.

Empirical review

The selection of an appropriate project-delivery method (PDM) is pivotal in determining the success of construction projects. Project Delivery Methods influence various performance metrics—including cost, time, quality, safety, and risk mitigation. Recent global assessments from 2023–2026 affirm that integrated and collaborative delivery methodologies consistently surpass traditional approaches in intricate infrastructure contexts, especially within scenarios characterized by elevated complexity and fragmented stakeholder engagement28,31. These analyses imply that the efficacy of project management practices (PDMs) is contingent upon the degree of governance and the robustness of institutional capacity. This review synthesizes recent empirical studies to elucidate the impact of prevalent construction PDMs: Design-Bid-Build (DBB), Design-Build (DB), Construction Management (CM/CM at Risk), Public–Private Partnerships (PPP or P3), and Design-Build-Operate-Transfer (DBOT). Additionally, it examines the moderating role of regulatory framework on these relationships, highlighting research gaps and advancements in the field32,33.

The DBB remains the traditional baseline PDM, in which design is completed before contractors bid to build34. Empirical reviews show that DBB is especially vulnerable to cost growth and schedule slippage when design errors or incomplete specifications surface during construction35. The sequential nature of DBB, separate contracts for design and for construction means there is limited early contractor input and less integration of constructability, often resulting in change orders, rework and disputes36. As a result, the cost and schedule-growth distributions for DBB tend to be larger than more integrated alternatives34. In the DB model, design and construction responsibility are consolidated under a single contract. Multiple meta-analyses and industry surveys consistently show that DB is associated with faster delivery and fewer claims when compared with DBB. For example, Sullivan et al., in 2017, found that across 4,623 projects, the DB exhibited average cost growth of approximately 2.8%, compared with 5.1% for DBB, and schedule growth of about 10.7% vs. 18.4% respectively37. A recent industry reports likewise found that DB projects averaged 3.8% less cost growth than DBB and 1.7% less schedule growth38. However, cost outcomes are mixed: one major study noted that while DB dominated in time savings, cost and quality advantages were less statistically consistent39. Generally, DB seems to deliver better schedule performance and somewhat improved cost control when properly applied37. Construction management models especially CM at Risk seek to bring contractor (or construction manager input into the pre-construction phase without fully transferring design risk to the contractor. Studies show that CM approaches can improve cost-predictability and constructability when the CM is engaged early and incentives are aligned32,40. For example, CM at Risk may limit change-order volatility because the CM shares greater price and risk responsibility41. But the performance gains depend heavily on clear scopes, transparent fee structures, and competent owner oversight; otherwise, complexity may increase and benefits fail to materialize40.

In PPPs, private-sector partners assume responsibility for financing, construction and often operation/maintenance—transferring lifecycle risk away from the public owner. Recent comparative studies of highways and other infrastructure indicate PPPs can deliver tighter cost control and schedule performance than traditional procurement, especially when projects are structured with aligned incentives and enforceable long-term performance metrics. For instance, a 2024 study by Parekh of U.S. highway projects found P3 cost growth averaged 2.12% versus 8.95% for DB and 7.27% for DBB; schedule growth averaged 0.59% for P3, compared with 37.94% for DB and 31.39% for DBB38. That said, PPP success is highly conditional on contract design (risk allocation, payment mechanisms), competitive procurement, and the government’s capability to manage contingent liabilities and uphold transparency31,42. The DBOT (and related concession models) extend DB by adding an operational period before transferring back to the public owner. The rationale is that by bundling design, build and operate phases, private partners internalize lifecycle and performance risks, thereby promoting durable design choices and aligning incentives for long-term performance. The practical contract-analysis literature and model-based studies suggest DBOT can improve whole-life performance and spread capital costs over time. However, these benefits only emerge when enforceable performance standards exist, the financial structure is credible, and regulatory certainty about tariffs or availability payments is present; otherwise, operators face unsustainable revenue risk and public partners face fiscal exposure43.

Across all PDMs, the strongest, most consistent finding in recent literature is that regulatory and institutional context fundamentally shape whether a delivery method can realize its theoretical advantages in the context of public construction projects. Furthermore, recent research investigations have identified a notable disparity in regulatory performance between the public and private sectors. Public projects frequently depend on laissez-faire regulatory compliance and oversight mechanisms, whereas private projects demonstrate a greater focus on internal governance frameworks, contract enforcement, and market regulation27,28. This divergence elucidates the variation in the influence of performance-enhancing project management mechanisms (PDMs) across different sectors. Key regulatory levers include procurement rules (competitive vs negotiated awards), contract-enforcement capacity, transparency/disclosure requirements, dispute-resolution institutions, and the public-sector owner’s capability to specify technical scope and manage complex contracts. Comparative studies of PPP/DBOT outcomes show that transparency, clear allocation of contingent liabilities, and independent oversight significantly improve value-for-money; conversely, weak procurement governance or politicides approvals correlate with poor cost/schedule outcomes regardless of PDM choice27,31. In short, PDMs do not operate in a vacuum legal, financial and regulatory systems determine which risks truly shift and which become hidden public liabilities.

Literature however, highlights several persistent gaps in research on Project Delivery Methods (PDMs). Most comparative studies have predominantly focused on cost and schedule performance, while overlooking other equally critical dimensions such as safety, quality, innovation, and lifecycle performance, including operation and maintenance outcomes37,44. In addition, the moderation impact of the regulatory framework on PDM and the performance of private construction projects is not highlighted by literature. Furthermore, existing evidence is heavily concentrated in developed economies, particularly the United States and Europe, which limits the generalizability of findings to emerging contexts such as Uganda, where institutional capacities, regulatory maturity, and governance mechanisms differ significantly28,31. Methodologically, existing studies adopted quantitative studies often neglecting rigorous qualitative techniques that control for project complexity, size, and sectoral differences. This study addressed these gaps by adopting a comprehensive and context-specific approach within the Ugandan construction sector, incorporating in the safety, risk mitigation, and quality aspects in performance measurement unlike prior research that mainly emphasized cost and schedule outcomes. The current study also regarded the moderating influence of the regulatory framework on the relationship between PDM and the performance of private construction projects unlike previous studies which emphasised regulation for public construction projects. Methodologically, the study employed a mixed-method design, integrating quantitative with qualitative perspectives from stakeholders such as civil engineers, project managers, contractors, and consultants to provide more rigorous findings that captured projects complexity, size, and sectoral disparities. In addition to these gaps, recent studies highlight a critical lack of comparative analysis between public and private sector construction projects in developing countries, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa24,28. Furthermore, there is limited empirical evidence examining how regulatory frameworks differentially influence project delivery effectiveness across sectors. While substantial research has examined regulatory impacts in public sector projects, the role of regulation in shaping private sector construction performance remains underexplored, especially in contexts where informal governance and client-driven controls dominate. This study therefore contributes to the literature by extending RBV into a dual-sector (public–private) comparative framework, integrating both internal capabilities (PDMs) and external institutional factors (regulation), and applying this framework within a developing country context.

Hypothesis development and conceptual model

Grounded in the Resource-Based View (RBV), this study conceptualizes Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) as strategic organizational capabilities that influence construction project performance. RBV posits that organizations achieve superior performance through the effective deployment of valuable and well-coordinated resources20. In construction projects, PDMs determine how resources, responsibilities, risks, and information flows are structured, thereby directly affecting project outcomes. Different PDMs offer varying levels of integration, collaboration, and risk allocation, which in turn influence key performance indicators such as cost control, time efficiency, quality assurance, safety, and risk mitigation. Empirical studies suggest that integrated delivery approaches such as Construction Management (CM) and Design-Build (DB) enhance coordination and reduce inefficiencies compared to traditional methods31,37. Given that PDMs shape project organization and execution processes, their selection is expected to significantly influence project performance outcomes across both public and private sectors.

Hypothesis 1:

Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) have a significant effect on the performance of construction projects in Uganda.

The effectiveness of PDMs is context-dependent and varies between public and private sector projects. Public projects are characterized by regulatory oversight, accountability requirements, and complex stakeholder environments, whereas private projects emphasize efficiency, flexibility, and profitability27,28.

Hypothesis 2:

The effect of Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) on project performance differs significantly between public and private construction projects.

RBV further recognizes that external institutional factors can influence how internal resources translate into performance outcomes. In this study, the regulatory framework is conceptualized as a moderating variable that strengthens or weakens the effectiveness of PDMs. Strong regulatory environments enhance accountability, enforce standards, and improve coordination, particularly in public sector projects.

Hypothesis 3:

The regulatory framework significantly moderates the relationship between Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) and project performance.

Methodology

This study adopted a mixed methods design to examine the impact of project delivery methods and the moderating role of regulation on the performance. The mixed methods design allowed for the systematic collection and statistical analysis of standardized data from a broad range of industry professionals through the quantitative approach, while at the same time enabling more rigorously detailed analysis through a qualitative approach to generate more informed findings45. The target population for this study constituted of 296 registered construction projects of which 129 (43.6%) were public and 167(56.4) were private construction projects. These projects were represented by construction professionals, including civil engineers, quantity surveyors, project managers, contractors, consultants, and site supervisors as units of inquiry. Equal representation from both sectors was deliberately chosen to ensure that the findings reflect the perspectives of professionals across different organizational contexts, thereby enhancing generalizability.

To capture quantitative, qualitative data and supporting statistical evidence from secondary sources, data were collected using a structured questionnaire, Key Informant (KI) interviews and document review respectively. The questionnaire was structured using a Five Point Likert Scale ranging from 1-Strongly Disagree-5-Strongly Agree and this was issued to construction professionals, including civil engineers, quantity surveyors, project managers, contractors, consultants, and site supervisors who provided data that was analysed quantitatively. Key informant interview were conducted with members of regulatory and professional bodies including Uganda Building Review Board (UBRB), Uganda Institution of Professional Engineers (UIPE) and the Uganda Association of Consulting Engineers (UACE). These KIs provided data that was analysed qualitatively. Supporting secondary data that provided actual performance indicators such as cost overruns, time of project completion (planned versus actual), measured defect rates, quality audit results, recorded accident and safety statistics and documented risk events and claims was obtained through a review of relevant documents.

Secondary data on project performance, including cost overruns, schedule deviations, defect rates, quality audits, safety incidents, and risk events, were obtained through a review of relevant documents. Key sources included the Cost Uganda Independent Review Reports, which provide detailed information on project costs, timelines, and quality outcomes, and academic studies from Makerere University, which document cost and time overruns, risk events, and management practices in both public and private projects47,48. Additional evidence came from government audit and parliamentary reports, such as those from the Office of the Auditor General and the Parliamentary Public Accounts Committee, which highlight expenditure variances, contract management challenges, and performance inefficiencies in public construction projects16. Industry regulatory and professional documents, including compliance reports from the Uganda Building Review Board and professional guidelines from the Uganda Association of Consulting Engineers, provided metrics on safety, quality, and defect management. Together, these sources offered a combination of quantitative and qualitative evidence that supported the analysis of performance differences between public and private construction projects in Uganda. The validity of the study constructs was ensured through factor analysis (with a minimum threshold of 0.4), while reliability was confirmed with Cronbach’s Alpha scores exceeding 0.70 for all variables49. To address the first research question; How does the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda compare? the study utilized descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, to summarize and compare performance indicators across sectors. Descriptive analysis is appropriate for establishing patterns and differences in central tendencies and dispersions within performance measures such as cost control, time efficiency, quality assurance, safety, and risk mitigation50. These statistical summaries provided an empirical baseline for understanding sectoral differences before advancing to inferential analyses.

The second research question: What is the effect of Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) on the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda? was examined using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) in SmartPLS. SEM is a multivariate analytical technique that allows simultaneous estimation of measurement and structural models and is especially useful for testing complex relationships among latent constructs49. The use of SmartPLS enabled the estimation of direct effects of various PDMs (e.g., Design-Bid-Build, Design-Build, Construction Management, Public–Private Partnership, Design-Build-Operate-Transfer) on project performance while accounting for measurement error51. This method is well suited for studies with theoretical models involving latent variables and small- to moderate-sized samples.

For the third research question: How does the regulatory framework moderate the effect of PDMs on the performance of public and private construction projects in Uganda? Hayes’ PROCESS Macro 4.2 for SPSS 29.0 was employed to test moderating effects. Moderation analysis investigates whether the relationship between an independent variable and an outcome changes at different levels of a third variable52. The regulatory framework, operationalized through indicators such as policy clarity, compliance monitoring, and enforcement consistency, was tested for interaction effects with PDMs on project performance. PROCESS Macro offers bootstrapping and conditional effect estimation, enhancing the robustness of moderation tests52.

Across all research questions, qualitative data from document analysis and content analysis complemented quantitative findings. Secondary sources, including project reports, government policy documents, and industry guidelines, were thematically analysed to contextualize and deepen interpretation of statistical results45. This triangulated, mixed-methods approach enriched the study’s conclusions by corroborating quantitative patterns with qualitative insights, yielding more informed and contextualized deductions about performance dynamics in Uganda’s construction sector53.

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the relevant institutional review board, ensuring that all research activities adhered to established ethical standards for human subject research. In addition, the study complied fully with Uganda’s Data Protection and Privacy Act (2019), which governs the collection, storage, processing, and sharing of personal and sensitive information. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, their rights, and the voluntary nature of their participation, and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Measures were taken to guarantee confidentiality and anonymity, including secure handling of responses, restricted access to identifiable data, and the use of aggregated reporting to prevent the disclosure of individual identities. These steps ensured that the study not only met ethical research standards but also aligned with national legal requirements for protecting the privacy and data rights of all respondents in Uganda.

The quantitative findings are further supported by qualitative insights from key stakeholders in Uganda’s construction sector. According to the Uganda Building Review Board (UBRB), strict enforcement of building regulations and compliance monitoring ensures that public projects meet safety and quality benchmarks. Secondary data from sector reports and studies further corroborate these patterns. Research indicates that public projects often experience delays and cost overruns due to bureaucratic procedures, frequent design changes, and procurement challenges, whereas private projects benefit from market-driven efficiency and leaner management systems36,48. These combined insights suggest a trade-off between efficiency and regulatory compliance: private projects excel in cost and time performance, while public projects prioritize quality, safety, and risk mitigation. Overall, the findings underscore the distinct operational and governance characteristics of public and private construction projects in Uganda, highlighting the influence of regulatory oversight, professional standards, and sector-specific management practices on project performance outcomes.

Structured methods such as CM and DBOT facilitate clear allocation of responsibilities, transparency, and rigorous oversight, which are critical in public projects where accountability, regulatory compliance, and public value are emphasized28,43. This is consistent with the high-performance scores observed in Table 4, where public projects achieved mean scores of 4.529 in quality assurance (SD = 0.584), 4.517 in safety (SD = 0.473), and 4.426 in risk mitigation (SD = 0.551). CM allows early contractor involvement in planning and design, reducing risks of cost overruns and schedule slippage, supporting Hale et al.’s (2009) conclusion that collaborative delivery improves constructability and minimizes conflicts in complex projects. Similarly, DBOT aligns long-term operational incentives with public-sector objectives, enhancing lifecycle performance and sustainability38.

Private-sector projects, in contrast, prioritize speed, innovation, and flexibility. Table 4 shows that CM (β = 0.481, t = 5.402, p < 0.001) and DB (β = 0.298, t = 2.563, p = 0.032) significantly improve performance, while DBOT (β = 0.108, t = 1.046, p = 0.749) does not have a statistically significant effect. This aligns with Nikou Goftar et al. (2014), who observed that flexible, integrative approaches are particularly advantageous in projects with higher uncertainty and market-driven objectives34.

The validity of these results is further supported by the CFA fit indices in Table 3, which confirm that the measurement model is robust and reliable. Key indices: χ2/df = 2.15, CFI = 0.942, TLI = 0.931, RMSEA = 0.061, SRMR = 0.049, all fall within acceptable thresholds, indicating that the constructs measuring PDM effectiveness, regulatory influence, and project performance are valid and reliable. This ensures confidence in the observed sector-specific effects of PDMs on project performance. Qualitative evidence from key stakeholders reinforces these findings. The Uganda Building Review Board (UBRB) emphasizes regulatory compliance and building safety, explaining the superior performance of public-sector projects in quality and risk mitigation. The Uganda Institution of Professional Engineers (UIPE) and Uganda Association of Consulting Engineers (UACE) highlight the importance of professional oversight, structured collaboration, and adherence to technical standards, which are reflected in the high-performance scores of public projects. Private-sector projects benefit from fewer bureaucratic constraints, enabling faster delivery and better cost and schedule performance54.

Overall, these results underscore the importance of aligning PDM selection with sectoral context and project objectives. Public projects in Uganda benefit from methods that strengthen governance, structured collaboration, and regulatory compliance, whereas private projects thrive under approaches that enhance agility and rapid decision-making. This reinforces the consensus in the literature that PDM selection should be strategically tailored to project characteristics, stakeholder priorities, and sector-specific requirements, rather than adopting a “one-size-fits-all” approach28,31,37.

In the context of Uganda’s public construction sector, where projects aim to deliver equitable benefits to society, this underscores the importance of not only selecting appropriate PDMs but also maintaining strict compliance with regulatory standards. Discussion

The findings of this study provide important insights into how Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) influence construction project performance in different institutional contexts. The results demonstrate that PDM effectiveness is not universal but varies significantly between public and private sector projects, reinforcing the argument that project delivery strategies must be context-specific.

The superior performance of private construction projects in cost control and time reduction aligns with recent studies emphasizing the efficiency-driven nature of private sector operations. Private projects typically operate under competitive market conditions, which incentivize streamlined decision-making, reduced bureaucratic delays, and efficient resource utilization. Similar findings have been reported in recent studies, which highlight that flexible and integrated delivery approaches improve speed and cost efficiency in market-oriented construction environments.

Conversely, the strong performance of public projects in quality assurance, safety, and risk mitigation reflects the influence of regulatory frameworks and institutional oversight. This finding is consistent with contemporary research indicating that structured and collaborative delivery methods, such as Construction Management and Public–Private Partnerships, enhance accountability, transparency, and compliance with standards in public infrastructure projects. Public sector projects are often subject to strict regulatory requirements, which, while potentially slowing delivery, improve adherence to technical specifications and long-term sustainability goals.

The significant positive impact of Construction Management (CM) and Design-Build-Operate-Transfer (DBOT) on public project performance further supports the growing body of literature advocating for collaborative and integrated delivery approaches. Recent studies emphasize that project delivery methods directly influence key performance indicators such as cost, quality, and dispute reduction, and that no single method is universally optimal. This aligns with the findings of this study, where traditional methods such as Design-Bid-Build (DBB) showed comparatively weaker or even negative effects in certain contexts.

The moderating role of regulation observed in public projects highlights the critical importance of institutional frameworks in shaping project outcomes. Strong regulatory environments enhance the effectiveness of PDMs by ensuring that project processes are properly implemented and monitored. This supports existing literature suggesting that governance structures and enforcement mechanisms are key determinants of construction project success, particularly in developing countries where institutional capacity varies.

In contrast, the absence of a significant moderating effect of regulation in private projects suggests that performance in this sector is driven more by internal management practices and client priorities than by external regulatory pressures. This finding reinforces the view that private construction projects prioritize flexibility, speed, and profitability over strict regulatory compliance, as also observed in recent construction management studies.

Overall, the findings of this study contribute to the ongoing debate on the suitability of different project delivery methods by demonstrating that sectoral context plays a critical role in determining effectiveness. The results confirm that there is no “one-size-fits-all” approach to project delivery, and that selecting appropriate methods requires careful consideration of governance structures, stakeholder expectations, and project objectives.

Practical implications

The findings of this study provide important implications for policymakers, construction practitioners, and industry stakeholders in Uganda and similar developing economies.

First, the results highlight the importance of aligning Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) with sector-specific objectives. Public sector agencies should prioritize structured and collaborative approaches such as Construction Management (CM) and Design-Build-Operate-Transfer (DBOT), which enhance regulatory compliance, accountability, and long-term project sustainability. These methods are particularly suitable for infrastructure projects where public value, safety, and quality are critical.

Second, private sector developers can improve performance by adopting flexible and integrated delivery methods, such as Design-Build (DB), which support faster decision-making, innovation, and cost efficiency. The findings suggest that minimizing bureaucratic constraints and enhancing internal coordination mechanisms can significantly improve project outcomes.

Third, the study underscores the critical role of regulatory frameworks in enhancing project performance, particularly in public construction. Strengthening regulatory enforcement, improving procurement transparency, and enhancing institutional capacity can significantly improve the effectiveness of PDMs and reduce risks associated with cost overruns and delays.

Finally, policymakers should focus on developing adaptive regulatory systems that balance control with flexibility. While strict regulations improve public project outcomes, overly rigid frameworks may hinder efficiency in private sector projects. Therefore, a differentiated regulatory approach tailored to sector-specific needs is recommended.

Conclusion

The findings of this study reveal distinct performance patterns between public and private construction projects in Uganda, reflecting differences in operational priorities, governance, and regulatory oversight. Private projects consistently outperform public projects in cost control (Mean = 4.067 vs. 2.252) and time reduction (Mean = 4.004 vs. 2.284), highlighting the efficiency and speed advantages of market-driven and client-focused project management. These outcomes suggest that private developers benefit from streamlined decision-making processes, flexible resource allocation, and fewer bureaucratic constraints, which enable adherence to budgets and schedules.

Conversely, public projects demonstrate superior performance in quality assurance (Mean = 4.529), safety (Mean = 4.517), and risk mitigation (Mean = 4.426), indicating a strong emphasis on compliance, technical standards, and long-term risk management. The high scores in these areas reflect the impact of professional oversight and regulatory enforcement, as corroborated by qualitative insights from the Uganda Building Review Board (UBRB), Uganda Institution of Professional Engineers (UIPE), and Uganda Association of Consulting Engineers (UACE). These institutions emphasize regulatory compliance, professional ethics, and structured supervision, which collectively enhance quality and safety outcomes in public-sector projects.

Analysis of the effect of Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) further underscores the sector-specific nature of project performance. In public projects, structured and collaborative methods such as Construction Management (CM) and Design-Build-Operate-Transfer (DBOT) exhibited the strongest positive effects (CM: β = 0.529, p < 0.001; DBOT: β = 0.387, p < 0.001), while traditional Design-Bid (DB) methods were less effective (β = −0.135, p = 0.014). For private projects, flexible methods like CM (β = 0.481, p < 0.001) and DB (β = 0.298, p = 0.032) significantly influenced performance, whereas DBOT had no significant effect (β = 0.108, p = 0.749). This highlights that PDM effectiveness is contingent on project context, with public projects benefiting more from structured collaboration and private projects favoring flexible approaches that enhance speed and cost efficiency.

The study also examined the moderating role of regulation. Regulatory frameworks significantly strengthened the effect of PDMs on public project performance (interaction β = 0.535, p = 0.0037), indicating that adherence to rules, standards, and compliance mechanisms amplifies the benefits of structured delivery methods. In contrast, regulation did not significantly moderate the PDM–performance relationship in private projects (interaction β = 0.140, p = 0.4525), reflecting the more client-driven and market-oriented nature of private construction, where regulatory compliance is less central to project execution.

Overall, these findings indicate a trade-off between efficiency and regulatory compliance: private projects excel in cost and time performance, while public projects prioritize quality, safety, and risk mitigation. The results underscore the importance of sector-specific project management strategies, highlighting that delivery methods and regulatory frameworks must be aligned with project objectives and operational context to achieve optimal performance.

Limitations of the study

Despite these contributions, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study is based on a relatively limited sample size (N = 85 for each sector), which may affect the statistical generalizability of the findings. Second, the data relies partly on self-reported perceptions from industry stakeholders, which may introduce response bias or subjectivity. Third, the study adopts a cross-sectional research design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between project delivery methods, regulatory frameworks, and performance outcomes. Additionally, the research is geographically confined to Uganda, and therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other countries with different institutional, regulatory, and economic environments. The variability in regulatory enforcement and construction industry practices across regions may influence the applicability of the results.

Recommendations

The findings of this study highlight the need for sector-specific strategies in the management of construction projects in Uganda. For public-sector projects, it is recommended that structured and collaborative Project Delivery Methods (PDMs), such as Construction Management (CM) and Design-Build-Operate-Transfer (DBOT), be prioritized. These methods enhance quality, safety, and risk mitigation by facilitating clear allocation of responsibilities, promoting professional oversight, and ensuring adherence to regulatory standards. Strengthening regulatory enforcement and compliance mechanisms further amplifies the benefits of these delivery methods, maximizing public value and lifecycle performance in infrastructure projects.

For private-sector projects, emphasis should be placed on flexible and client-oriented delivery methods, particularly CM and Design-Build (DB), which improve efficiency in cost control and time management. Private developers should continue leveraging agile decision-making, resource flexibility, and streamlined processes to maintain competitive advantage while ensuring that minimum quality and safety standards are still observed. Regulatory oversight, while less critical for performance in this sector, should focus on enforcement of essential safety and technical standards to prevent project failures and reputational risks.

Across both sectors, project managers should adopt a strategic approach to PDM selection, aligning delivery methods with project objectives, operational context, and stakeholder priorities. Continuous monitoring of performance indicators—cost, time, quality, safety, and risk—should guide adjustments in project management practices. Additionally, professional institutions such as the Uganda Institution of Professional Engineers (UIPE), the Uganda Association of Consulting Engineers (UACE), and the Uganda Building Review Board (UBRB) should maintain their advisory and oversight roles, providing guidance, audits, and enforcement to ensure that technical standards and ethical practices are consistently upheld.

Implementing these recommendations will enable public projects to balance regulatory compliance with operational efficiency, while private projects can sustain speed and cost-effectiveness without compromising essential quality and safety requirements. Ultimately, aligning PDM selection, regulatory oversight, and sector-specific priorities will optimize construction project performance in Uganda.

Contribution to knowledge

This study makes significant contributions to theory, practice, methodology, and policy in construction project management. Theoretically, it extends the Resource-Based View (RBV) to the construction sector by conceptualizing Project Delivery Methods (PDMs) as strategic organizational resources and regulatory frameworks as external moderators. This approach highlights how the interaction between internal capabilities and external resources shapes project performance, addressing RBV’s traditional limitation regarding external factors in temporary and complex projects.

Empirically, the study provides new insights from Uganda, an under-researched context, showing that private projects excel in cost and time efficiency, while public projects prioritize quality, safety, and risk mitigation. It also demonstrates that regulatory frameworks strengthen the impact of PDMs on public projects but not on private projects, filling a gap in literature that has largely focused on developed countries and limited performance dimensions.

Methodologically, the study adopts a mixed-methods design, combining SEM, moderation analysis, qualitative interviews, and document review to capture sector-specific dynamics and project complexity. Practically, it offers actionable guidance for selecting PDMs and aligning regulatory oversight with sector priorities, enabling public projects to maximize compliance and quality, and private projects to enhance efficiency without compromising safety.

From a policy perspective, the findings provide evidence to guide government agencies, regulators, and professional bodies in Uganda on the importance of enforcing regulatory standards in public projects while promoting flexible, efficient approaches in the private sector. Policies that strengthen professional oversight, compliance monitoring, and risk management in public projects can enhance public value and infrastructure quality, while targeted guidance for private developers ensures efficiency without compromising minimum safety and quality standards. Overall, the study advances RBV theory, provides robust empirical evidence in a developing-country context, and informs context-sensitive project management strategies and policies.
