ABSTRACT
PURPOSECervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer mortality in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The International Gynecologic Cancer Society (IGCS) Global Gynecologic Oncology Fellowship aims to build human capacity to address the burden of cervical cancer in LMICs. This study assesses resource constraints experienced at fellowship sites with regard to management of cervical cancer.METHODSFrom September to December 2020, one fellow from each of the 12 existing IGCS fellowship programs participated in a survey that assessed capacity for cervical cancer management, including access to care, diagnostics and treatment, cancer surveillance, and palliative care. Descriptive statistics were used for analysis.RESULTSPatients at IGCS sites experienced significant delays to care, especially for chemotherapy and radiation therapy. Less than half of the sites had a gynecology-trained pathologist, and only 58% of sites had access to a magnetic resonance imaging machine, though with many delays in obtaining imaging reads. For treatment, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is not commonly used. Access to radiation therapy is poor, with 58% of sites reporting wait times of 5-8 weeks or more. The radiation machine downtime ranges from 1 to 3 months per year, creating gaps where no patients can access this treatment. Palliative care is practiced by variable members of the health care team although hospice services are rare.CONCLUSIONThis study demonstrates significant resource constraints experienced by gynecologic oncology providers in various LMICs when managing cervical cancer. This includes delays to diagnosis, poor access to chemoradiation services, and need for palliative care. Despite these limitations, the IGCS Global Gynecologic Oncology Fellowships have built workforce capacity to manage cervical cancer, serving as local champions to address this disease.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with over 90% of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 In 2018, the WHO made a global call for the elimination of cervical cancer.2 Efforts to eradicate cervical cancer have focused on prevention with human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination as well as screening and treatment of precancerous lesions. However, less attention has been placed on management of invasive cervical cancer.

Several strategies have been used to address cervical cancer care in LMICs, including increasing educational capacity and provision of resources.3 The International Gynecologic Cancer Society (IGCS) has focused its efforts on the development of structured fellowship training programs in gynecologic oncology (GYO), with the goal of cultivating a new generation of health care workers with increased capacity to screen for, diagnose, and treat cervical cancer.4 The IGCS Global GYO Fellowships work in collaboration with local expert physicians at each training site in addition to regular in-country visits from international mentors at established GYO programs in high-resource settings. These fellowships also leverage advances in technology for consistent virtual tumor board meetings to continually assess case volume and learning progress for their fellows.5

Since the inception of the IGCS fellowship program with five pilot sites in 2017, there are now 22 sites across 18 countries, with a total of 52 graduated fellows and another 38 currently in training.4 Currently, there are 40 international mentors and 53 local mentors throughout these programs. Over 11,000 surgeries have been performed in country as a part of the IGCS program, including 1,600 radical hysterectomies.4 The process of establishing these fellowships brought new insight to the general challenges and resource constraints limiting management of invasive cervical cancer in these settings.

To better understand both shared and unique circumstances affecting cancer care delivery at each of the IGCS training sites, a comprehensive survey was developed by IGCS in collaboration with experts from the National Cancer Institute, the CDC, and the Society of Gynecologic Oncologists. The aim of this survey was to establish baseline data at each of these fellowship sites with regard to general access to cancer care, in-country availability of diagnostic and treatment modalities, capacity for cancer surveillance, and provision of palliative care. The results could inform resource provision and allocation, increase training and expertise, and provide targeted long-term mentorship to improve cervical cancer outcomes.

METHODS

From September to December 2020, we invited each of the existing 12 IGCS fellowship programs (Bahamas, Jamaica, Fiji, Guatemala, Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, Mozambique, Zambia, Nepal, Qatar, and Vietnam) to participate in the survey. Each site received the survey link, instructions, and consent by e-mail via SurveyMonkey. Sites were allowed to work collaboratively with existing fellows and mentors to complete the survey with results that were representative of their collective experience. The survey consisted of 66 questions related to cervical cancer management, including access to care, available diagnostics (imaging and pathology), available treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy), capacity for cancer surveillance, and palliative care. Descriptive statistics were used to assess the frequency of each variable given the small sample size.

The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) determined that this protocol met criteria for exemption from IRB review. All study participants provided informed consent. In accordance with the journal's guidelines, we will provide our data for independent analysis by a selected team by the Editorial Team for the purposes of additional data analysis or for the reproducibility of this study in other centers if such is requested.

RESULTS

The survey was completed by one fellow from each of the 12 sites with a 100% response rate. The survey revealed consistent resource constraints across all IGCS sites, including significant wait times for cervical cancer care, difficult access to pathology and imaging, insufficient availability of operating rooms and radiation therapy, limited palliative care, and inconsistent health surveillance.

The results demonstrated challenges with access to cervical cancer care across the IGCS sites. While 11 of the 12 sites reported an average time of 2 weeks or less between cervical cancer diagnosis and GYO appointment, six of the 12 sites reported average wait times of >5 weeks from diagnosis to surgery and >8 weeks from diagnosis to chemoradiation (Table 1). Data were not obtained regarding length of time between the onset of symptoms and presentation to care nor wait times between presentation to care and diagnosis.

With regard to pathology and imaging services to obtain a diagnosis, more than half of the sites (58.3%, n = 7) reported not having an in-country pathologist with specialized training in gynecologic pathology. When sending pathology to diagnose cervical cancer, nine (75%) sites reported that they are able to obtain results within 3 weeks. Similarly, when sending surgical pathology, eight (66.7%) sites reported that they can obtain results within 3 weeks. Of the 12 sites, seven (58.3%) use standard pathology checklists or synoptic reporting for interpretation of their pathology specimens, and approximately half (57.1%) of those sites use these checklists for both the loop electrosurgical excision procedure and hysterectomy specimens. Most of the IGCS sites (83.3%, n = 10) have access to telepathology consults, including another tertiary hospital in the region, remote sites, and international sites. Most commonly, these telepathology consults are through IGCS international mentors.

For diagnostics, access to imaging is particularly challenging. While 11 of the 12 sites have access to computed tomography (CT) scans, only seven have access to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and only three have access to positron emission tomography-CT. Despite having access, written comments from fellows at each of the sites suggest that there are significant cost, functional, and time barriers to obtaining imaging. Once patients obtain imaging, GYO providers most often access images via paper records or physical films brought by the patient. Fellows at four sites noted that patients bring disks to upload images, and only two sites have access to electronic records. Regarding both pathology and imaging, sites do have access to out-of-region pathology and radiology services, and four of the 12 sites noted that patients waited at least 3-4 weeks for these outside reads (Table 2).

All 12 IGCS sites have access to surgical care. Chemotherapy for metastatic cervical cancer is offered at 11 sites, and chemoradiation for locally advanced cervical cancer is offered at 10 sites. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is less commonly used for cervical cancer management, with two (16.7%) sites never using neoadjuvant chemotherapy and four (33.3%) sites rarely using it. For surgical management, tumor boards are used at 75% (n = 9) of sites to help with decision making and quality control. This is consistent with the Project ECHO tumor boards that have been implemented at most IGCS sites as a part of the Global GYO Fellowship Program. Surgical care itself is delayed for several reasons at these sites. Most common reasons cited were the lack of operating room availability (10 sites), including being postponed for more emergent cases, limited availability of blood products (six sites), and lack of anesthesia staff or anesthetic agents (four sites). Other reasons included patient delays, surgeon availability, limited ventilators and OR equipment, and limited electricity or power.

External beam radiation services are available in country for some IGCS sites. Half of the sites (n = 6) have a linear accelerator, whereas 33.3% (n = 4) of sites only have access to cobalt-based machines. Two (16.7%) sites do not have access to radiation therapy in country. Brachytherapy is available at 75% (n = 9) of sites, whereas interstitial implants specifically are only available at 33.3% (n = 4) of sites. Despite the presence of radiation machines in country, access is limited, leading to significant delays. Of the 12 IGCS sites, seven (58.3%) noted that patients have to wait more than 5 weeks on average to access external beam radiation, sometimes waiting more than 8 weeks. For brachytherapy, two (16.6%) sites also noted patient wait times of >5 weeks, and three sites described not having access to brachytherapy (Table 3).

The difficulty in accessing radiation therapy is exacerbated by machine downtime at the IGCS sites. Of the sites that offer radiation, 50% only experience a downtime of <30 days per year, whereas 30% have a downtime of 1-2 months per year, and one site has a downtime of 2-3 months per year. One site specifically noted minimal downtime for their cobalt machine (<30 days) but significant downtime for their linear accelerator (3-4 months). Cisplatin, which is frequently used as a radio-sensitizing chemotherapy agent during cervical cancer treatment, is also not always available at the IGCS sites. Four (33.3%) sites noted frequent inability to access cisplatin based on in-country supply. To improve access to radiation services, 58.3% (n = 7) of sites offer housing for patients coming from far distances.

At most IGCS sites, palliative care is provided by GYO providers alone (n = 6) or in collaboration with specialized palliative care providers (n = 6). However, some sites have other providers to administer palliative care, including medical oncologists (n = 2), radiation oncologists (n = 1), general physicians (n = 2), and nurses (n = 3). At 50% of the sites, home hospice services are available to some extent.

All IGCS sites reported some level of surveillance for patients after their initial therapy, typically through a hospital cancer registry. Eight (66.7%) sites are able to capture patient outcome information, including the presence or absence of recurrent disease. Most sites reported that they follow patients for up to 5 years, and two sites follow patients for up to 7-10 years.

DISCUSSION

The WHO initiative to eliminate cervical cancer by 2030 delineates three pillars of action: vaccination, screening, and treatment and palliative care.2,6 This presents major economic and logistical challenges as cervical cancer still remains a significant health challenge in the majority of LMICs.7 Most IGCS training sites are in countries with limited resources for HPV vaccination, inconsistent screening programs, and significant resource constraints for treatment. As we scale up cervical cancer screening, many patients will be identified with invasive disease. To successfully attain all three of these pillars, there must be an increase in the number of skilled medical personnel who can treat cervical cancer.6

The IGCS Global GYO Fellowship Program represents a model for scaling up and strengthening services for cervical cancer management by leveraging international collaborations and telementoring platforms to educate and train health care workers in LMICs.4,5 This survey serves as an initial starting point in understanding the landscape for management of invasive cervical cancer within the IGCS fellowship programs with the goal of facilitating improved support to scale up cancer care in these regions. Overall, the survey results suggest that there are limited and variable resources for the provision of cervical cancer care in LMICs.

Awaiting diagnostic results is a significant source of delay in LMICs. At the IGCS sites, <50% have access to an in-country gynecologic pathologist. Imaging, while available, is often not ideal. Ultrasound is most frequently available followed by CT; however, MRI, an important imaging modality for cervical cancer staging, is rarely available. The survey noted long wait times for review of radiologic studies once obtained. This delay in diagnosis often translates to a delay in seeing an appropriate provider and ultimately obtaining treatment. However, even once a diagnosis has been obtained, wait times to access subspecialty care are long, evidenced by the low number of GYO providers at the IGCS sites.

For decision making, IGCS fellowship sites often have associated virtual tumor boards with international mentors; however, because of extremely high clinical volumes, only select difficult cases can be reviewed.8 In addition, patient outcome data are variably collected, so it is challenging to perform necessary research to understand appropriate interventions for management of cervical cancer in each respective area.

Unfortunately, the survey results outline that delays to provision of surgical care are frequent, an ongoing issue within the field of global surgery.9 Broadly, this is due to the limited number of hospitals covering large geographic areas and the limited number of surgical providers within these hospitals. However, even once patients access hospitals that have surgeons, there are a limited number of physical operating rooms, anesthesia staff, blood products, and equipment to perform surgery.

While surgical care and chemotherapy are more frequently available, access to radiation services poses one of the greatest challenges for LMICs.10 Many patients who require radiation as a part of their treatment plan never receive it because of limited availability. Both external beam radiation therapy and brachytherapy, necessary components of definitive treatment for locally advanced cervical cancer, are often delayed by more than a month at approximately 50% of IGCS sites.11 Even when radiation is available, several sites are still using cobalt machines, which are unable to provide precise image-guided treatment and carry higher local toxicity for patients. Wait times in areas with access to radiation are also challenging, often longer than 2 months and as high as 18 months at some IGCS sites, because of either lack of availability or machine downtime throughout the year, which can last for 30 days or more at a time. Palliative care is also an area that can be strengthened.12 There is limited availability of in-home hospice services and variable palliative care training for different types of providers. More focus is needed on palliative care services, an area of ongoing research.12

One strength of this study is that it provides insight into the resource constraints faced by gynecologic oncologists managing invasive cervical cancer in LMICs. Given that the results are from 12 different countries, this study addresses needs across a variety of settings, in contrast to a single site. However, one of the limitations is the small sample size, which may lead to a less accurate representation of available resources. In addition, most IGCS fellowships are within major cities, so their experience of patient care may not reflect that of patients in more rural settings within that country. The survey was also completed as some of the IGCS fellowships were only just being set up, possibly leading to under-reporting of resource limitations.

The results of this study provide unique insight into the experience of gynecologic oncologists in low-resource settings as they navigate significant resource constraints to treat patients with invasive cervical cancer. It details where patients experience delays to care, which resources are in highest demand, and the need for increased capacity to treat patients. While a significant proportion of cervical cancer literature is focused on HPV vaccination and screening for precancerous lesions, our study contributes to the important data on management of invasive cervical cancer, which will continue to be a major part of cervical cancer efforts globally.

Next steps include further refining this survey instrument and dissemination to a larger sample size that includes newer IGCS fellowships and non-IGCS sites. A mixed-methods approach with a qualitative component could also add an important perspective to understanding the challenges in managing invasive cervical cancer in low-resource settings. Ultimately, a prospective needs assessment to actively understand patient access to care, delays to care, diagnostic capabilities, and availability of resources would be an important contribution to future work in cervical cancer treatment.

Although there are significant global health inequities in the management of cervical cancer, there are several areas where impactful progress can be made, including improving diagnostic capabilities, increasing access to chemoradiation services, and capacity-building of the GYO workforce. Significant investment in oncology infrastructure is necessary to achieve these goals. Despite limited resources, the IGCS global fellowships continue to train gynecologic oncologists in LMICs who are facilitating improvement in resource utilization and access to care. While this is only a starting point, this study demonstrates that ongoing support of these fellowship programs is needed, particularly with their potential capacity to build a workforce that both prevents and manages cervical cancer.
