The association between dual RAAS inhibition and risk of acute kidney injury and hyperkalemia in patients with diabetic kidney disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Reid Whitlock, Silvia J Leon, Hazel Manacsa, Nicole Askin, Claudio Rigatto, Samuel T Fatoba, Youssef M K Farag, Navdeep Tangri
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesDual renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade involves dual therapy with a combination of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis), angiotensin-receptor blockers (ARBs), direct renin inhibitors (DRIs), or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs). It is hypothesized that dual RAAS blockade would result in a more complete inhibition of the RAAS cascade. However, large clinical trials on dual RAAS inhibition have shown increased risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) and hyperkalemia without additional benefit on mortality, cardiovascular events, or chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression compared to RAAS inhibitor monotherapy in patients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD). The development of newer, more selective non-steroidal MRAs as cardiorenal protective therapies has created a new opportunity for dual RAAS inhibition. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the risks of AKI and hyperkalemia with dual RAAS blockade in patients with DKD.Design, setting, participants, and measurementsThis is a systematic review and meta-analysis of the randomized controlled trials (RCT) published from 1 January 2006 to 30 May 2022. The study population included adult patients with DKD receiving dual RAAS blockade. A total of 31 RCTs and 33 048 patients were included in the systematic review. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random effects.ResultsThere were 208 AKI events in 2690 patients on ACEi + ARB versus 170 in 4264 patients with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 1.48, 95% CI: 1.23–1.39). There were 304 hyperkalemia events in 2818 patients on ACEi + ARB versus 208 in 4396 patients with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 1.97, 95% CI: 1.32–2.94). A steroidal MRA + ACEi or ARB had a 5-fold higher risk of hyperkalemia with 28 events in 245 at risk in dual therapy versus five events in 248 at risk in monotherapy (pooled RR 5.42 95% CI: 2.15–13.67).ConclusionDual therapy with RAASi is associated with an increased risk of AKI and hyperkalemia compared to RAASi monotherapy. Conversely, dual therapy with RAAS inhibitors and non-steroidal MRAs have no additional risk of AKI but a similar risk of hyperkalemia, which is lower than dual therapy with RAAS inhibitors and steroidal MRAs.Keywords: acute kidney injury, ACEi, ARB, diabetic kidney disease, dual RAAS inhibitors

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD), and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) contributes nearly half of all cases of kidney failure requiring replacement therapy [1]. Glycemic control, blood pressure control and the single-agent renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade are cornerstones of DKD treatment [2]. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) and angiotensin-receptor blockers (ARBs) have been shown to reduce the progression of kidney disease in several trials [2].

It is plausible that dual inhibition of the RAAS with combination of an ACEI, ARB, MRA, or direct renin inhibitor (DRI) can achieve a more complete inhibition of the RAAS and thus confer greater cardiovascular and kidney protection. However, large clinical trials that combined ACEis and ARBs (RAS inhibitors) have shown increased risk of adverse events such as hyperkalemia and acute kidney injury (AKI) and absence of any additional benefit on mortality, or CKD progression [3–6]. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) have also been considered for dual blockade. A Cochrane review of 44 studies of patients with CKD observed that although adding MRAs (mostly steroidal MRAs) to ACEi or ARBs reduced albuminuria by 51% they were also associated with a 2-fold increase in AKI and hyperkalemia compared to ACEi or ARB monotherapy [7].

Non-steroidal MRAs are a novel, selective, MRA that block the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) in a more selective manner, causing conformational alterations in the MR through a bulky side chain [8]. In the FIDELIO-DKD, the incidence of hyperkalemia-related adverse events was two-fold higher in the finerenone-treated patients than in the placebo-treated patients (18.3% and 9.0%, respectively) [9]. The development of newer, more selective non-steroidal MRAs as cardiorenal protective therapies has created a new opportunity for dual RAAS inhibition therapies.

We conducted a systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of dual RAS blockade and MRA/RAS inhibitor combination for patients with DKD and a meta-analysis of its association with AKI or hyperkalemia as compared with RAAS inhibitor monotherapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted our systematic review using methodological approaches from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [10] and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) criteria [11]. A technical panel of experts from the nephrology field formulated the review questions, reviewed the search strategies and review methods, and provided input throughout the review process. All references were managed in Mendeley. This protocol has been registered online (CRD42022330670).

Populations, interventions, comparators, outcome measures, settings, and study designs

We included studies in the systematic review that met the following criteria: RCTs with a follow-up period >30 days, a population of adults (aged 18+) with diabetes and CKD, defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 or albuminuria, which included both micro and macro albuminuria (>30 mg/day) or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) >30 mg/g, an outcome of AKI or hyperkalemia, and a comparison of dual therapy vs. monotherapy (with or without placebo). Hyperkalemia and AKI captured are investigator defined adverse events as part of the clinical trial conduct. Dual therapy was defined as any combination of two of the following classes of medication: ACEi, ARB, DRI, steroidal MRA, or non-steroidal MRA. Monotherapy was defined as only one of those classes. Observational studies, cross-over trials, animal studies, and gray literature were all excluded.

Search strategy for identification of studies

We searched MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE(Ovid), Cochrane CENTRAL (Ovid), CINAHL with Full Text (EBSCO), Global Health (Ovid), LILACS (BIREME), and Scopus (Elsevier) from 1 January 2006 to 30 May 2022 for relevant citations of trials and congress abstracts published in English. We began our search in 2006 to provide an update on a previous systematic review [12]. Starting from this point reflected both a possible change in clinical practice with respect to prescribing both ACEis and ARBs due to the previous review and also reflected a time when newer RAAS blockade therapy (DRIs, non-steroidal MRAs) became available [13]. The searches incorporated controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for concepts that included diabetic nephropathy, CKD, chronic renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, dual therapy, combined therapy, dual blockade, renin–angiotensin system, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, angiotensin-receptor blocker, MRAs, and DRI including individual drugs. The full search strategy can be seen in Supplementary Appendix 1. We performed reference management in Covidence.

Study selection

We used a two-stage process for study screening and selection using standardized and piloted screening forms. Two reviewers (S.J.L. and H.M.) independently screened the search results. The full text of citations classified as include or unclear were reviewed independently with reference to the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1). Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through consensus by discussion with a third reviewer (N.T.), as required.

Data abstraction and management

Two reviewers (S.J.L. and H.M.) independently extracted data, using standardized and piloted data extraction forms, from included trials. We extracted article information (author, title, journal, year), study information (trial registration number, location(s), single-center vs. multi-center, study duration), demographics of the enrolled participants (age, sex), baseline laboratory values (hemoglobin A1C, serum potassium, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR)), details of intervention and comparator (drug class, drug name, dosage), and relevant outcomes (case definition, number of events). Data management was performed using Covidence.

Assessment of methodological quality and potential risk of bias

We assessed internal validity using the Cochrane Collaboration's Risk of Bias tool [10]. This tool consists of seven domains and assesses five specific biases. The two reviewers assigned a rating of high risk of bias, low risk of bias, or unclear risk of bias in each domain for all studies. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Measures of treatment effect

We analyzed data from the included studies using Review Manager (RevMan v.5.4, the Cochrane Collaboration). We conducted a formal meta-analysis for both AKI and hyperkalemia using the DerSimonian and Laird method. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Mantel–Haenszel random effects models where all events and subjects were pooled together. Statistical heterogeneity was explored and quantified using the I2 statistic for inconsistency and we conducted the Chi-squared test to assess the statistical significance of heterogeneity using a two-sided α of 0.05 [14].

We stratified comparisons by classes of drug. We used an intention-to-treat approach where the sample size reported for both the intervention and comparator group was the number of patients randomized to each treatment. Where not possible, we used the number of patients who completed the study in each group as the sample size. In a sensitivity analysis, we only included studies who scored a low risk of bias in all domains for each comparison.

Risk of bias

Of the 31 studies included in the systematic review, 13 were rated as a low risk of bias in all domains, five were rated as a low risk of bias in six of seven domains, five were rated as a low risk of bias in five of seven domains, and the remaining eight were rated as a low risk of bias in a maximum three of seven domains (Fig. 2a,b). Risk of bias was mainly due to blinding of participants and personnel procedures as well as allocation concealment in the included trials.

Outcomes

Acute kidney injury (AKI)

A total of 21 studies assessed AKI events, 16 reported at least one AKI. There was a total of 1691 AKIs reported in 30 925 patients. AKI definitions per trial can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

A total of 12 studies compared ACEi + ARB vs. ACEi or ARB and assessed AKI, of which eight reported at least one AKI event. Overall, 208 of 2690 patients had an AKI in the ACEi + ARB group compared to 170 AKIs in 4264 patients in the monotherapy group. The meta-analysis showed that ACEi + ARB dual therapy was associated with higher risk of an AKI compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 1.48, 95% CI: 1.23–1.79) (Fig. 3a). Heterogeneity was not significant (I2 = 0%, P = 0.54).

(a) ACEi + ARB vs. ACEi or ARB for AKI. (b) Non-steroidal MRA + ACEi or ARB vs. ACEi or ARB for AKI.

A sensitivity analysis in three studies with a low risk of bias in all domains also found that ACEi + ARB dual therapy was associated with higher risk of an AKI compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 1.78, 95% CI: 1.02–3.11) (Supplementary Fig. S1).

A total of six studies, all with a low risk bias in all domains, compared non-steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB vs. ACEi or ARB and assessed AKI, all of which reported at least one AKI event. In these trials, there were 266 AKI events in 7837 patients reported in the dual non-steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB group compared to 243 AKIs in 6895 patients in the monotherapy group. Non-steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB dual therapy was not associated with higher risk of an AKI compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 0.97, 95% CI: 0.81–1.16) (Fig. 3b). Heterogeneity was not significant (I2 = 0%, P = 0.60).

One study, all with a low risk bias in all domains, compared DRI + ACEi/ARB vs. ACEi or ARB which assessed AKI and had events in both the dual therapy and monotherapy group [5]. This study did not show a statistically significant association between dual therapy and AKI as compared to monotherapy (pooled RR 1.13, 95% CI: 0.99–1.29). Similarly, there was one study, with a low risk bias in all domains, that compared steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB vs. ACEi or ARB which assessed AKI and had events in both the dual therapy and monotherapy group [31]. This study did not show a statistically significant association between dual therapy and AKI compared to monotherapy (pooled RR 1.30, 95% CI: 0.69–2.44).

Hyperkalemia

All 31 studies assessed hyperkalemia, of which 22 reported at least one hyperkalemia event. There was a total of 4950 hyperkalemia events reported in 33 048 patients. Definitions of hyperkalemia included 13 studies that defined hyperkalemia as a serum potassium of >5.5 mmol/l or mEq/l, two studies that used a threshold of >5.6 mmol/l, six studies that used a threshold of >6.0 mmol/l, two studies that used MedDRA preferred terms, and others where the term was undefined (Supplementary Table S2).

A subgroup of 15 studies compared ACEi + ARB vs. ACEi or ARB and assessed hyperkalemia, of which nine reported at least one hyperkalemia event. There were 304 of 2818 patients (10.8%) who had hyperkalemia in the dual therapy group compared with 208 of 4396 patients (4.7%) in the monotherapy group. ACEi + ARB dual therapy was associated with higher risk of hyperkalemia compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 1.97, 95% CI: 1.32–2.94) (Fig. 4a). Heterogeneity was statistically significant (I2 = 67%, P = 0.002).

(a) ACEi + ARB vs. ACEi or ARB for hyperkalemia. (b) Non-steroidal MRA + ACEi or ARB vs. ACEi or ARB for hyperkalemia. (c) Steroidal MRA + ACEi or ARB vs. ACEi or ARB for hyperkalemia. (d)DRI + ACEi or ARB vs. ACEi or ARB for hyperkalemia.

A sensitivity analysis in three studies with a low risk of bias in all domains also found that ACEi + ARB dual therapy was associated with higher risk of hyperkalemia compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 2.47, 95% CI: 1.60–3.79) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Six trials compared 7837 patients using non-steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB vs. 6895 patients using ACEi or ARB and assessed hyperkalemia, of which five reported at least one hyperkalemia event (953 events in the dual blockade group and 454 in the monotherapy group). The risk of hyperkalemia was higher in non-steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB dual therapy risk compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 2.05, 95% CI: 1.84–2.28) (Fig. 4b). Heterogeneity was not significant (I2 = 0%, P = 0.70).

Among the included studies, six studies, all with a low risk of bias in all domains, six assessed hyperkalemia with a comparison of 245 patients on a steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB regimen vs. 248 using an ACEi or ARB. Of the six studies, four reported at least one hyperkalemia event (28 events in the dual blockade group and five in the monotherapy group). Analysis showed that steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB dual therapy was associated with higher risk of hyperkalemia compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 5.42, 95% CI: 2.15–13.67) (Fig. 4c). Heterogeneity was not significant (I2 = 0%, P = 0.83). Only two of these studies scored a low risk of bias in all domains [18, 31]. In both these studies, the dual therapy group had seven times more hyperkalemia events (21/136 vs. 3/136).

Four studies, all with a low risk of bias in all domains, compared DRI + ACEi/ARB vs. ACEi or ARB reported at least one hyperkalemia event. Here, 1718 hyperkalemia events were reported in the dual arm versus 1280 in the monotherapy arm. DRI + ACEi/ARB dual therapy was associated with higher risk of hyperkalemia compared with ACEi or ARB monotherapy (pooled RR 1.35, 95% CI: 1.27–1.43) (Fig. 4d). The results were heavily weighted by one study [5]. Heterogeneity was not significant (I2 = 0%, P = 0.54).

Finally, two studies compared steroidal MRA + ACEi/ARB vs. steroidal MRA. However, the number of events were insufficient for meaningful meta-analysis [19, 29]. Only one patient in the spironolactone-losartan group had a potassium level >5.5 mEq/l in the Makhlough et al. trial [29]. In El Mokadem et al. trial, two patients in the monotherapy group and two patients in the dual blockade group reported a hyperkalemia event [19].

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs of adults with DKD, dual RAS blockade with an ACEi and ARB was associated with a 50% increase in the risk of an AKI compared to ACEi or ARB monotherapy. In contrast, the addition of an MRA to an ACEi or ARB was not associated with an increase in AKI compared to ACEi or ARB monotherapy. All comparisons among different drug classes for hyperkalemia showed that dual therapy was associated with a roughly 2-fold increase in the risk of hyperkalemia compared to monotherapy, which increased to more than 5-fold when a steroidal MRA was added to an ACEi or ARB. Taken together, these results suggest dual RAS blockade using ACEi and ARB therapy is associated with an increased risk of AKI and hyperkalemia compared to RAS inhibitor monotherapy, and should continue to remain contraindicated. Our findings also suggest adding a non-steroidal MRA to a RAS inhibitor may not be associated with an increased risk of AKI compared to RAS inhibitor monotherapy, and that the increase in risk of hyperkalemia versus RAS inhibitor monotherapy may be smaller when a non-steroidal MRA is added to a RAS inhibitor compared to when a steroidal MRA is added to a RAS inhibitor.

The benefits of RAAS inhibitors therapy to slow CKD progression have been clearly demonstrated. It had been suggested and hypothesized that a more complete blockade using a combination of ACEis and ARBs could further decrease proteinuria and improve outcomes in patients with DKD [44]. However, large clinical trials have failed to find a benefit, and confirmed harm from AKI and hyperkalemia. Newer therapeutic options such as DRIs or non-steroidal MRAs have become available but conclusive evidence on the risks of AKI and hyperkalemia with combination of these drugs has been lacking.

To our knowledge, this systematic review represents the latest assessment of dual RAAS inhibitor therapy in patients with DKD and also includes analyses with both steroidal and non-steroidal MRAs. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2006 that compared dual therapy with ACEi + ARB to monotherapy with an ACEi in patients with CKD and proteinuria found beneficial effects of dual therapy on decreasing proteinuria [12]. Similar to our findings, the review found that dual therapy caused an increase in serum potassium and a decrease in eGFR. They did not meta-analyze hyperkalemia or AKI and only reported when patients had to drop out of the study for those reasons. A previous analysis published in 2010 compared dual therapy with ACEi + ARB to monotherapy with an ACEi in a patient population with heart failure found that combination therapy was associated with a reduction in heart failure hospitalizations [45]. The study meta-analyzed the outcomes of worsening kidney function and hyperkalemia. Their findings were similar to our study. Dual therapy was associated with an increased risk of worsening kidney function (RR 1.91, 95% CI: 1.40–2.60) and a trend toward an increased risk of hyperkalemia (RR 1.95, 95% CI: 0.85–4.48). In a meta-analysis of the dual blockade of RAAS in DKD, ACEis plus ARBs did not significantly increase serum potassium compared with ACEis alone (WMD 0.15 mmol/l; 95% CI − 0.02 to 0.33) or ARBs alone (WMD 0.18 mmol/l; 95% CI − 0.20 to 0.56). Nor did they significantly increase the risk of hyperkalemia (Odds Ratio: 1.57; 95%CI: 0.99, 2.50). MRAs plus ACEi/ARB significantly increased the risk of hyperkalemia (Odds Ratio: 2.94; 95%Ci: 1.42–6.05). This meta-analysis did not include newer therapies for DKD like non-steroidal MRAs [46].

The findings of dual therapy increasing the risk of hyperkalemia are not surprising. RAAS inhibition decreases aldosterone production which reduces the secretion of aldosterone [47, 48]. Potassium excretion is regulated by serum aldosterone; therefore, low levels of aldosterone cause an increase in serum potassium levels that may develop into hyperkalemia. Patients with DKD are even more prone to hyperkalemia since their RAAS activity is already suppressed because of an impaired conversion of pro-renin to renin or suppression of plasma renin activity [49, 50]. Furthermore, RAAS inhibitors also increase resistance to aldosterone, which necessitates even higher levels be produced to regulate potassium in the blood [47]. Some believe MRAs act as another class of RAAS inhibitors as they are aldosterone antagonists and block the interaction between aldosterone and the MR. Serum aldosterone levels typically rise with the addition of an MRA; however, the ability to bind/activate the MR is reduced [51].

Although RAAS inhibition has been shown to preserve kidney function [52], and dual RAAS inhibition improves proteinuria reduction [12, 53], there appears to be a point at which further RAAS blockade may have deleterious effects on the kidney. Angiotensin blockade decreases the ability to respond to volume depletion [21]. Volume depletion in turn reduces kidney perfusion which decreases eGFR. For these reasons, current clinical guidelines for DKD, recommend not treating with any combination of ACEi/ARB [54]. It is important to note that studies that compared non-steroidal MRA + ACEi or ARB to ACE or ARB monotherapy do not demonstrate an increased risk of AKI, suggesting that dual blockade with ACE/ARB and MRA is not the same as dual blockade with ACEi and ARB.

Non-steroidal MRAs, when added to ACEi or ARB, have similar effects on reducing proteinuria to ACEi/ARB combinations and they appear to confer this benefit without the increase in AKI risk [7]. This result was mostly driven by two recent trials (FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO DKD) with the novel selective non-steroidal MRA finerenone [9, 43]. The addition of finerenone increases protection against cardiorenal events without an increased risk in AKI and a similar increased risk of hyperkalemia compared with dual RAS blockade from a combination of ACEi and ARB. It is important to note that there were substantial differences in the risk of hyperkalemia with the use of non-steroidal vs steroidal MRAs. Although no head-to-head trials were conducted, against a monotherapy comparator, steroidal MRAs had a nearly 5-fold higher risk of hyperkalemia compared to a 2-fold higher risk with non-steroidal MRAs. These differences are likely due to the molecular structure, selectivity, and mechanism of action of these agents, and will be valuable to be explored in real world data as non-steroidal MRAs become integrated in clinical practice. Included studies with esaxerenone were phase II trials with subgroups that received high doses of the medication (5 mg/day) and thus it may be that the higher dosage of esaxerenone may have contributed to higher rates of hyperkalemia. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that the risks of hyperkalemia and its related cardiac/other hospitalization events may not be as high with non-steroidal MRA use as seen with steroidal MRA spironolactone [55]. It is important to note that finerenone has been studied in patients with heart failure. The ARTS-HF phase II study, randomized patients with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LEF ≤ 40%) to receive finerenone or eplerenone for 90 days. The incidence of hyperkalemia (≥5.6 mEq/l) at any time during the study periods was similar among groups [56]. Further research, including head-to-head comparisons between non-steroidal and steroidal MRAs in the DKD population, is warranted.

The main strength of our study was that in addition to ACEi and ARBs, we also included other RAAS inhibitors or drugs similar to RAAS inhibitors such as DRIs and MRAs. This allowed us to choose from a larger number of studies and allowed us to conduct subgroup analyses among different medication class comparisons. Additionally, our search strategy was widely inclusive and incorporated several databases.

Our review does have some limitations. First, different types of medication have different dosing levels and dose levels were not reported in all studies, therefore this precluded us exploring a dose–response relationship. However, many studies indicated that they used maximal dosing, but this could have limited that number of studies that we could assess using meta-analysis. Second, the study durations were heterogeneous, therefore a meta-analysis of hazard ratios would have been ideal to ensure the hazards were proportional over time but as studies did not report hazard ratios, we could not pool them together. However, the signal toward dual therapy showing a higher risk of AKI and hyperkalemia appeared to be consistent regardless of study duration. Finally, the case definitions of the outcomes were not fully aligned among the studies and sometimes were not reported together because they were obtained from adverse event tables and were not the primary outcomes, leading to some adverse reaction indices that could not be accurately statistically analyzed. The inconsistency in the outcome definitions, study duration, and severity of CKD and albuminuria across studies may have contributed to the statistical heterogeneity of our results, although there was no statistical heterogeneity found in our AKI analysis. The definitions of hyperkalemia were not consistent across studies, which may be a source of heterogeneity.

In conclusion, dual therapy with ACEi and ARBs is associated with an increased risk of AKI and hyperkalemia compared to RAS inhibitor monotherapy. By contrast, combination therapy with non-steroidal MRAs and RAS inhibitors were not associated with an additional risk of AKI, but a similar risk of hyperkalemia, which is lower than what we found with dual therapy with ACEi/ARBs and steroidal MRAs. These findings highlight that there may be important differences in the risks and benefits when implementing dual RAAS combination therapies for the treatment of DKD.
