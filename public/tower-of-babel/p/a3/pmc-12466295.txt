Regulation mechanism of lotus root juice quality: From the perspective of interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols
Authors: Peng-peng Sun, Chong-yang Yu, Cheng Liu, Xu Zhang, Yuan-yuan Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLotus root juice contains a large amount of colloidal substances including polysaccharides, which leads to low juice yield and difficulty in clarifying during the juice extraction process. This paper was aimed to explore regulation mechanism of lotus root juice quality from the perspective of interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and poly-phenols by a series of spectroscopic methods. Results indicated that the uniformity of chelating agent soluble polysaccharide (CSP) was high, with the minimum molecular weight of 7.17 × 104 Da. UV, fluorescence spectrum and infrared spectrum showed that there was non-covalent interaction between cell wall polysaccharide and polyphenol through hydrogen bond and hydrophobic interaction. And the specific type of cell wall polysaccharide and metal ions as well as pH values could all influence these interactions. Meanwhile, the interaction could significantly improve antioxidant activity of cell wall polysaccharide (p < 0.05). Finally, the color and stability of simulated lotus root juice were optimal at pH 7 and a ratio of 1:3 for polysaccharides and polyphenols. Our results could provide theoretical basis for deep processing of lotus root products.Keywords: lotus root, Cell wall polysaccharides, Polyphenols, Interaction, Different pH values, Metal ions

Introduction

Lotus root (Nelumbo nucifera G.) belongs to Nymphaeaceae and is widely cultivated in China now, mostly in the middle and lower Yangtze River basins (Gao et al., 2017). Lotus root is not only rich in nutrients but also has multiple medicinal benefits, such as antioxidant, antidiabetic and immunomodulatory activity. (Hu et al., 2019; Yi et al., 2016). The bioactive substances in lotus roots are mainly polysaccharides and polyphenols. Jiang et al. (2011) indicated that lotus root polysaccharide is a kind of acidic heteropolysaccharide. Yi et al. (2019) showed that lotus root polysaccharides had antioxidant properties. Moreover, studies have shown that lotus root contains abundant phenolic compounds, including catechins, gallic acid, ferulic acid, chlorogenic acid, and resveratrol (Yi et al., 2016). At present, the processed products of lotus root are diverse, mainly focusing on pickled lotus root, canned lotus root, lotus root juice, lotus root powder, crispy lotus root slices and lotus root wine.

Biomacromolecules including polysaccharides and proteins are essential components of raw material in many processed foods and play a key role in imparting structure and stability to food. They can interact with polyphenols, forming complexes with reversible or irreversible properties and high biological activities (Le Bourvellec & Renard, 2012). Increasing studies have confirmed that the interactions between polysaccharides and polyphenols are closely related to the digestion, metabolism, and bioavailability of polyphenols in the body. And it also affects the physicochemical properties and biological activities of polysaccharides and polyphenols (Liu et al., 2018; Zou et al., 2024). Moreover, their interactions are closely related to the processing qualities of fruits and vegetables, such as color and flavor, which help improve their application in food industry (Xue et al., 2024).

For fruits and vegetables, cell walls are mainly composed of polysaccharides, including pectin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, and they are in different bonding states, especially pectin. It is reported that pectin and cellulose in cell walls can affect the appearance (such as turbidity) and mouthfeel (such as grittiness) of processed products like beverages, negatively impacting their sensory quality (Ribeiro et al., 2010). Moreover, polyphenols are an important family of plant secondary metabolites present in fruits and vegetables and have various biological actions. The interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols can also lead to the formation of reversible or irreversible binary complexes, thereby affecting sensory properties and nutrition of products (Fu et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2022). Lotus root contains a large amount of colloidal substances such as starch and polysaccharides, which often leads to issues during juice extraction, including high viscosity of the slurry after crushing, low juice yield, and difficulty in clarifying the product. Additionally, cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols in lotus root can interact during the juicing process, affecting the quality of final lotus root juice. Many researchers have tried to improve juice quality by degrading cell wall polysaccharides and adding antioxidants. Recently, there are also many studies reporting that plasma modification treatment and pulsed electric field treatment of fruits could enhance the overall quality of fruit juice (Han et al., 2025; Mundanat et al., 2025; Weilack et al., 2024). But few reports are concentrated on the interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols to regulate the quality of lotus root juice.

Therefore, it was hypothesized that the interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols will have a positive impact on regulating the quality of lotus root juice. In this study, cell wall polysaccharides in different bonding states were extracted and characterized, respectively. Subsequently, interactions between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols were investigated, as well as the effects of ions with different valence states and pH values on these interactions. Finally, simulating lotus root juice was prepared and its quality was preliminarily evaluated. Our study would lay a theoretical foundation for revealing the molecular mechanism by which the interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols in lotus roots regulates the quality of lotus root juice.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Lotus roots were purchased from the local market in Jingzhou, Hubei. And they were consisted of 4 to 5 segments, each about 20 cm in length with a diameter of 10 cm. After peeling, they were cut into small cubes and immediately stored at −80 °C for further use. Freeze-dried lotus root was firstly extracted with 60 % ethanol at 60 °C for 1 h, then the extracts were volatilized and lyophilized to obtain crude polyphenols of lotus root. Crude polyphenols (4 g) were fully dissolved in 200 mL of distilled water, filtered by 0.22 μm polyethersulfone filter membrane and subsequently passed into AB-8 macroporous adsorption resin chromatographic column (Φ1.6 cm × 40 cm). At the end, 70 % ethanol solution was used to elute, and the eluate was collected in 10 mL brown tubes. Every 5 mL eluate was in each tube and a total of 20 tubes were used. The eluate with the highest polyphenol content, which was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu method (Li et al., 2019), were collected and lyophilized to obtain purified lotus root polyphenol (LP). After purification, the purity of LP (30.03 % ± 0.39 %) was approximately 8.2 times that of crude lotus root polyphenol (3.66 % ± 0.04 %).

Monosaccharide standards (L-rhamnose, D-glucuronic acid, D-galacturonic acid, D-mannose, d-glucose, D-galactose, and L-arabinose, with purity of 98 %) were purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). KBr with spectral purity was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HPLC-grade acetonitrile was purchased from Merk Company (Darmstadt, Germany), and other reagents were all analytical-grade.

Extraction of cell wall polysaccharides

The extraction of cell wall polysaccharides from lotus root was slightly modified according to Zhou et al. (2024). Take the frozen lotus root in a sealed plastic bag from the −80 °C refrigerator and thaw at 25 °C for 4 h. The post-thawed lotus root cubes (100 g) were pulped with anhydrous ethanol (300 mL) at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:3 (g/mL). Then it was boiled for 20 min followed by centrifugation (10,000 r/min, 10 min). Subsequently, the residue was soaked in a mixed solution consisting of chloroform and methanol (200 mL, 1:1, V: V) at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 (g/mL) and placed at 4 °C overnight. After being washed twice with acetone (200 mL), 90 % DMSO solution (200 mL) was used to remove starch. Finally, the residue was washed twice with 80 % ethanol and dried at 60 °C to obtain cell wall material (CWM).

CWM (1 g) was stirred with deionized water (60 mL) at 4 °C for 12 h and centrifuged at 10000 r/min for 20 min. The precipitate was washed with distilled water (30 mL) and centrifuged again. The supernatants were merged, then dialyzed (molecular cut-off 100 kDa) for 72 h and freeze-dried to obtain water-soluble polysaccharide (WSP). The precipitate above was then extracted with 50 mmol/L EDTA-Na2 solution containing 20 mM sodium borohydride (60 mL) at 4 °C for 12 h. After centrifugation (10,000 r/min, 10 min), the precipitate was washed with distilled water (30 mL) and centrifuged again. The supernatants were merged, and the pH value was adjusted to 7 using sodium acetate. Finally, it was dialyzed at 4 °C for 72 h and freeze-dried to obtain chelating agent soluble polysaccharide (CSP). The extraction procedure of sodium carbonate-soluble pectin (SSP) was exactly the same except 50 mmol/L Na2CO3 solution (60 mL) instead of EDTA-Na2 solution. Contents of total sugar, reduced sugar, protein and uronic acid were determined according to reported references (Dong et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2020). Note: The content of polysaccharides is equal to the total sugar content minus the reducing sugar content.

Molecular weight distribution of cell wall polysaccharide

Using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Aglient 1200, Agilent Technologies Inc., USA), the molecular weight distribution of cell wall polysaccharide (2 mg/mL) was determined. The chromatographic column was TSK-GEL G4000PWXL (7.8 mm × 300 mm); the temperature of column and refractive index detector (RID) was 30 °C and 35 °C, respectively; the mobile phase was ultrapure water; the flow rate was 0.6 mL/min. The standardized dextran samples, namely T-10, T-40, T-70, T-110, T-500, and T-2000, were utilized to establish the precise calibration curve (Ren et al., 2020).

Monosaccharide compositions of cell wall polysaccharide

The monosaccharide composition of cell wall polysaccharides was determined according to the method of Yu et al. (2022). 10 mg of WSP, CSP, and SSP were hydrolyzed by 4 mL of trifluoroacetic acid solution (2 mol/L) at 110 °C for 4 h, then methanol solution was added three times to remove residual trifluoroacetic acid by rotary evaporation. Monosaccharide standards (mannose (Man), rhamnose (Rha), glucuronic acid (GluA), galacturonic acid (GalA), glucose (Glu), galactose (Gal) and arabinose (Ara)) and hydrolysates were analyzed through PMP pre-column derivatization. The hydrolysate was filtered with a 0.22 μm membrane and injected to HPLC equipment (Waters e2695, Waters, USA). The chromatographic column was Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). Mobile phase A was acetonitrile and 0.05 mol/L KH2PO4-NaOH (pH 6.9) with a volume ratio of 40:60, and mobile phase B was the same solutions with a volume ratio of 15: 85. Gradient elution (0–10 min, 2 %–6 % mobile phase A; 10–22 min, 6 %–12 % mobile phase A; 22–26 min, 12 %–9 % mobile phase A; 26–40 min, 9 %–2 % mobile phase A; 40–42 min, 2 % mobile phase A); Column temperature was 30 °C; flow rate was 1 mL/min; detection wavelength was 245 nm; injection volume was 10 μL.

Interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols in lotus root

Ultraviolet spectra

Different volumes of cell wall polysaccharides solutions (0.05 mg/mL, dissolved in deionized water) and 1 mL of 0.1 mg/mL LP solution (dissolved in deionized water) were mixed together to make the final polysaccharides concentrations being 0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, 0.045 mg/mL, respectively. Then they were stirred with a magnetic stirrer for 30 min at room temperature and immediately scanned in the range of 190–400 nm using a UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan).

In addition, the effects of metal ions with different valence states (Na+, Ca2+, Fe3+) and different pH values (pH = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8) on the interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols in lotus root were also explored with UV spectra. To be specific, different volumes of metal ions solutions (0.5 mg/mL NaCl, 0.5 mg/mL CaCl2, 0.0005 mg/mL FeCl3), 4 mL of 0.05 mg/mL cell wall polysaccharides solution and 1 mL of 0.1 mg/mL LP solution were mixed together to make the final Na+ and Ca2+ concentrations being 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25 mg/mL and Fe3+ concentrations being 0, 5 × 10−5, 1 × 10−4, 1.5 × 10−4, 2 × 10−4, 2.5 × 10−4 mg/mL, respectively. The subsequent operations were the same as above. 1.5 mL of 0.2 M acetic acid‑sodium acetate buffer solutions with different pH values (pH = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8), 1 mL of 0.2 mg/mL cell wall polysaccharides solution (prepared with 0.2 M different pH values sodium acetate solution) and 2.5 mL of 0.02 mg/mL LP solution (the same solvent as polysaccharides solutions) were mixed. The subsequent operation methods were the same as above.

Fluorescence spectrum

Different volumes of cell wall polysaccharide solutions (2 mg/mL, prepared with 0.2 M pH 4.8 sodium acetate solution) and 200 μL of 1 mg/mL polyphenol solution (the same solvent as polysaccharides solutions) were added together to make the final polysaccharides concentrations being 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6 mg/mL, respectively. Then they were stirred with a magnetic stirrer for 30 min at room temperature and immediately scanned with an F-7000 FL fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan) at an excitation wavelength of 340 nm between 370 and 550 nm.

Moreover, the effects of metal ions (Na+, Ca2+, Fe3+) and pH values (pH = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8) on the interaction between lotus root cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenol were also explored by fluorescence spectra. Specifically, different volumes of metal ions solutions (0.5 mg/mL NaCl, 0.5 mg/mL CaCl2, 0.0005 mg/mL FeCl3, prepared with 0.2 M pH 4.8 sodium acetate solution), 0.2 mL of 2 mg/mL cell wall polysaccharides solution and 0.2 mL of 1 mg/mL LP solution (prepared with 0.2 M pH 4.8 sodium acetate solution) were mixed together to make the final Na+ and Ca2+ concentrations being 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3 mg/mL and Fe3+ concentrations being 0, 5 × 10−5, 1 × 10−4, 1.5 × 10−4, 2 × 10−4, 2.5 × 10−4, 3 × 10−4 mg/mL, respectively. The subsequent operations were the same as above. 0.8 mL of 0.2 M acetic acid‑sodium acetate buffer solutions of different pH values (pH = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8), 0.4 mL of 1 mg/mL cell wall polysaccharides solution (prepared with 0.2 M different pH values sodium acetate solution) and 0.8 mL of 1 mg/mL LP solution (the same solvent as polysaccharides solutions) were mixed. The subsequent operation methods were the same as above.

Infrared spectrum

5 mL of different concentrations of lotus root cell wall polysaccharide solution (2 mg/mL, 1 mg/mL, and 0.5 mg/mL) and 5 mL of 1 mg/mL polyphenol solution were added together and stirred for 30 min in the dark with a magnetic stirrer, then freeze-drying to prepare polysaccharide-polyphenol complexes with different ratios (termed as WSP/CSP/SSP: LP = 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, respectively). Subsequently, complexes in different ratios (1 mg) and dry KBr (150 mg) were thoroughly ground and pressed into a thin disk. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Nicolet iS20, USA) was used for scanning from 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1.

Quality evaluation of simulated lotus root juice

Equal amounts of three cell wall polysaccharides from lotus root were mixed together and dissolved in 0.2 M acetic acid‑sodium acetate buffer with different pH levels (pH 3, 5, and 7). Then they were mixed with LP solutions (the same solvent as polysaccharides solutions) at different concentration ratios (MCP: LP = 1:3, 2:3, 1:1, 3:2, 3:1 respectively and MCP represented mixed cell wall polysaccharide) to simulate lotus root juice. Their L*, a*, and b* values were measured by a color difference meter (CR400), and ∆E value was calculated according to formula (3). The absorbance of simulated lotus root juice before centrifugation (A0) and after centrifugation (As) (4200 ×g, 15 min) were measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600) at 625 nm and its stability coefficient (CS) was determined referring to formula (4).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by SPSS 27.0 and examined by analysis of variance (ANOVA). Significance analysis was detected with Duncan's multiple range tests at a level of 5 %, with all measurements taken in triplicate. The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Origin 2021 was used for data visualization.

Results and discussion

Basic component analysis of cell wall polysaccharides

As shown in Table 1, the basic component of three kinds of cell wall polysaccharides from lotus root were different. To be specific, their total sugar contents were all above 19 %, with WSP being the highest (50.37 %), followed by SSP (26.98 %) and CSP (19.98 %). Correspondingly, WSP had the highest content of polysaccharides and reducing sugars, accounting for 44.73 % and 7.51 %, respectively. The content of uronic acid in WSP, CSP, and SSP was 11.31 %, 7.94 %, and 15.02 %, respectively. Besides, WSP had the highest protein content (7.51 %), while others were all below 1.5 %. The differences in the chemical composition of three kinds of cell wall polysaccharides were mainly due to the different extraction methods and the complexity as well as diversity of cell wall structure. Different extraction conditions also could effectively separate pectic polysaccharides with different chemical properties (Koh et al., 2018; Reichembach et al., 2024).

Structural characterization of cell wall polysaccharides

Molecular weight distribution

The molecular weight distribution of cell wall polysaccharides from lotus root was shown in Fig. 1. The chromatograms of WSP and SSP were asymmetric, non-unique, and WSP mainly consisted of four peaks, with the first peak appearing at 9.11 min. CSP only showed a single symmetrical peak at 12.92 min, indicating its high purity and uniformity. Based on the regression equation of dextran standard (LgMw=−0.35t+9.3765,R2=0.98), the molecular weight of CSP was estimated to be 7.17 × 104 Da. SSP exhibited six chromatographic peaks, with the first peak at 8.37 min. According to the relationship between retention time and molecular weight, it could be concluded that the molecular weight of CSP was the lowest and the molecular weight of SSP was the highest. SSP contained the most abundant side chains than WSP and CSP, and had a higher proportion of RG-I regions, resulting in a higher molecular weight than other two kinds of cell wall polysaccharides. This phenomenon was also consistent with previous research results (Yao et al., 2023).

Distribution of polysaccharide molecular weight in lotus root cell walls (A: WSP; B: CSP; C: SSP).

Monosaccharide composition

As summarized in Table 1, WSP, CSP, and SSP all contained a large amount of glucose, and CSP was only consisted of glucose. In detail, WSP was composed of mannose (Man), rhamnose (Rha), glucuronic acid (GluA), glucose (Glc), galactose (Gal), and arabinose (Ara), with a molar ratio of 0.10: 0.23: 0.28: 97.67: 1.17: 0.54; while SSP contained Man, Rha, GluA, GalA, Glc, and Ara, with a molar ratio of 0.02: 0.04: 0.05: 0.12: 99.63: 0.13. These results differed from the proportions reported by Song et al. (2025). The variations in the extraction methods of different polysaccharides and the differences in raw materials might lead to this disparity. Additionally, both WSP and SSP contained Man, Rha, GluA, Glc, and Ara, but their proportions in WSP were all higher than those in SSP. This difference might lead to variations in their biological activity, and Yi et al. (2018) also confirmed through the fingerprint-activity relationship model that the monosaccharide compositions of polysaccharide was closely related to its bioactivity.

Study on interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols

Ultraviolet spectroscopy analysis

The effects of interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols on UV spectra were shown in Fig. 2A-C. As the polysaccharide concentration increased, the maximum absorption wavelength of the solution slightly shifted blue. This phenomenon indicated that the spatial structure of polyphenols changed after interacting with cell wall polysaccharides, possibly due to the reaction between the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of polyphenols and polysaccharides (Wang et al., 2019). Furthermore, the blue shift phenomenon in CSP was the most significant than that of WSP and SSP, which suggested the interaction force between CSP and polyphenols was the strongest. This difference might be related to the degree of methylation of pectin and the ionization strength of carboxyl groups (Le Bourvellec et al., 2009; Le Bourvellec et al., 2012).

The effects of interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols on UV spectra (A-C: different concentrations of lotus cell wall polysaccharides interacted with LP; D-L: Na+, Ca2+, and Fe3+ were added respectively; M-O: in different pH environments).

The effect of metal ions (Na+, Ca2+, Fe3+) on UV spectra were shown in Fig. 2D-L. When adding Na+ and Ca2+, the intensity of maximum absorption peak increased with the increased concentration of metal ion. However, when Fe3+ (below 1.5 × 10−4 mg/mL) was added, its maximum absorption peak intensity decreased compared to the control group and it increased at higher Fe3+ concentration. The interaction between polyphenols and polysaccharides increased with the enhancement of ionic strength, indicating the presence of hydrophobic interactions (Renard et al., 2017). In addition, the addition of Na+ and Ca2+ caused blue shift phenomenon, but as their concentrations increased, red shift phenomenon occurred. This result indicated that the spatial structure of polyphenols might change after interaction with polysaccharides (Wang et al., 2019). When different concentration of Fe3+ was added, a red shift phenomenon occurred, which might be because Fe3+ was easily hydrolyzed in an aqueous solution, altering the pH value of system.

The effect of pH values on UV spectra were depicted in Fig. 2M-O. The maximum absorbance of the composite solution appeared at pH 7 for WSP and CSP, while for SSP the absorbance was the highest at pH 5. Besides, all three kinds of cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenol composite solutions had the lowest absorption at pH 3. This phenomenon could be because when the pH value dropped, electrostatic repulsion increased, weakening the electrostatic contact. It was believed that electrostatic contact had an important role in polyphenol and polysaccharide interaction. Moreover, redshift or blueshift phenomenon also occurred at different pH values, which might be due to the inconsistent polarity of composite solutions at different pH values caused by buffer solution as solvent. On the other hand, this phenomenon also suggested that the spatial structure of polyphenols changed after interacting with cell wall polysaccharides (Wang et al., 2019).

Fluorescence quenching analysis

The effects of interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols on fluorescence spectra were shown in Fig. 3A-C. As the concentrations of WSP, CSP and SSP increased, the fluorescence intensity gradually decreased, which was so-called fluorescence quenching; however, the peak's shape and location remained unchanged. This phenomenon indicated that the interactions between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols might create non-fluorescent complexes (Zhu, 2018). Furthermore, SSP demonstrated the strongest fluorescence quenching impact than WSP and CSP. This difference might be related to the structure of polysaccharides, which was consistent with the research result of Zhou et al. (2024).

The effects of interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols on fluorescence spectra (A-C: different concentrations of lotus cell wall polysaccharides interacted with LP; D-L: Na+, Ca2+, and Fe3+ were added respectively; M-O: in different pH environments).

The effect of metal ions (Na+, Ca2+, Fe3+) on fluorescence spectra were displayed in Fig. 3D-L. The fluorescence intensity decreased with increasing concentrations of Na+ and Ca2+, which might owe to the weakened hydrogen bond interaction between polysaccharides and polyphenols (Zhang et al., 2017), but there was no obvious trend or pattern. Interestingly, as the concentration of Fe3+ increased, the fluorescence intensity dropped regularly. No matter which kind of metal ions (Na+, Ca2+, and Fe3+) was added, a slight red shift occurred as the concentration of metal ions increased, which declared that metal ions might cause structural changes in polyphenols, reducing the hydrophobicity of aromatic ring in polyphenol structure (Jakobek & Matić, 2019).

The effect of pH values on fluorescence spectra were shown in Fig. 3M-O and it seems to have little to do with the specific kinds of cell wall polysaccharides. The maximum fluorescence intensity decreased correspondingly as pH values dropped from 7 to 3 (from neutral to acidic environments), and there was redshift or blueshift phenomenon, possibly due to the influence of varying pH on the charge distribution and molecular conformation of the complexes. When pH value was 8, three kinds of cell wall polysaccharide-polyphenol complexes showed comparatively equal fluorescence intensities, which were all significantly lower compared to pH 7. This result could be due to increased anions in the system, which might affect the structure of polysaccharides and polyphenols.

Antioxidant activity analysis

As shown in Fig. 5A-C, both polyphenols and cell wall polysaccharides from lotus root had capacity to scavenge hydroxyl radicals in the order of LP > CSP > SSP > WSP. When the concentrations of WSP, CSP and SSP increased, their scavenging ability gradually increased. This might be because polysaccharides could chelate metal ions, forming complexes with Fe2+ that prevent it from reacting with H2O2, thereby inhibiting the formation of hydroxyl radicals (Gao et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2013). In addition, when WSP, CSP and SSP combined with LP at different ratios, their scavenging ability of hydroxyl radical were significantly higher than that of single cell wall polysaccharide (p < 0.05). This phenomenon indicated the interaction between cell wall polysaccharide and polyphenols may greatly improve polysaccharide's antioxidant activity. Previous researchers have reported that non-covalent attachment of epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) to dendrobium officinale polysaccharide significantly improved its hydroxyl radicals scavenging activity, which was consistent with our results (Peng et al., 2025). Specifically, when the ratio of WSP and LP was 1:1, the hydroxyl radical scavenging ability was maximized (28.54 %); while at the ratio of 2:1 for CSP/SSP and LP, the strongest scavenging ability of hydroxyl radical (45.78 % and 37.56 %, respectively) appeared. It was worth noting that when the ratio of CSP and LP was 2:1, the scavenging rate was even higher than that of single LP.

Determination of Hydroxyl radical scavenging rate, DPPH scavenging rate, and Total reducing power of Polysaccharide-Polyphenol complexes at different ratios (Note: Different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05), which is detected with Duncan's multiple range tests at a level of 5 %.)

Fig. 5D-F exhibited that both polyphenols and cell wall polysaccharides from lotus root could scavenge DPPH radical in the order of LP > SSP > WSP > CSP. With increased concentrations of WSP and CSP, their scavenging ability of DPPH radical gradually increased, while three concentrations of SSP showed no significant difference (p > 0.05). When WSP, CSP and SSP combined with LP at different ratios, their scavenging ability of DPPH radical were significantly higher than that of single cell wall polysaccharide (p < 0.05). This phenomenon suggested that the interaction between cell wall polysaccharide and polyphenols could enhance the ability of polysaccharides to scavenge DPPH radical, which was possibly because the presence of phenolic hydroxyl groups increased antioxidant capacity of the complex (Tian et al., 2024). Liu et al. also obtained similar results that the non-covalent binding of polyphenols could effectively enhance the DPPH radical scavenging activity of lotus root polysaccharides (Liu et al., 2023). When the ratio of WSP/CSP/SSP and LP was 1:2, the DPPH radical scavenging ability in all of them (except VC and LP) was the strongest (75.03 %, 79.86 % and 61.49 %, respectively).

Total reducing power can also be used to reflect the antioxidant ability of compounds, and the larger the absorbance value was, the stronger the total reducing power was. As displayed in Fig. 5G-I, the total reducing power of LP was the highest, and those of WSP, CSP and SSP with different concentrations were extremely weak. However, when WSP, CSP and SSP combined with LP at different ratios, their total reducing power were significantly higher than that of single cell wall polysaccharide (p < 0.05). These results were consistent with the results of Peng et al., and this phenomenon was attributed to the strong reducing power of polyphenols itself, which led to an increase in the reducing power of complexes (Peng et al., 2025). Specifically, when the ratio of WSP/SSP and LP were 1:1, the total reducing power was the highest in all complex, while it reached the maximum at the ratio of CSP and LP being 2:1.

Effect of interactions on the quality of simulated lotus root juice

The effect of different ratios between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenol as well as pH values on the color difference and stability of simulated lotus root juice were shown in Fig. 6. Under different pH environments, the simulated lotus juice prepared with the same ratio showed significant differences in L values. This might be because different pH values caused polysaccharides to undergo polymerization reactions, forming larger molecules and altering the optical properties of solution. The change of overall color difference ∆E value and b value were relatively close. Additionally, the stability of simulated lotus root juice was the best at pH 7, while it significantly decreased at pH 3 and 5. This phenomenon was possibly because acidic conditions affected the charge state and structure of cell wall polysaccharides as well as polyphenols from lotus root, resulting in a decrease in the electrostatic interaction force between them (Zhao et al., 2026), which reduced their solubility and consequently lowered the stability of the simulated lotus root juice. Overall, when pH value was 7 and the ratio of MCP: LP was 1:3, the color and stability of simulated lotus root juice were optimal, which could enhance the quality of lotus root juice.

Conclusion

In this study, three kinds of cell wall polysaccharides (WSP, CSP, and SSP) from lotus root were extracted and preliminarily characterized. Among them, the content of polysaccharides and reducing sugars in WSP was the highest, while the homogeneity of CSP were higher compared with others. Moreover, results of UV, fluorescence spectrum and infrared spectrum indicated that there were non-covalent interactions between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols from lotus root through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. And the specific type of cell wall polysaccharide and metal ions as well as pH values could all influence these interactions mentioned above. The antioxidant activity of lotus root cell wall polysaccharides-polyphenols complex at different ratios was significantly greater than that of single cell wall polysaccharides but lower than single polyphenols. When pH value was 7 and their ratio was 1:3, the color and stability of simulated lotus root juice were optimal. Our study has revealed the key regulatory mechanism of the interaction between cell wall polysaccharides and polyphenols in maintaining the stability and color of lotus root juice. It holds significant theoretical guidance and application value for deepening the understanding of component interaction rules in fruit and vegetable processing, improving the quality control technology of lotus root juice, and developing high-quality products.
