Characteristics and outcomes of thromboembolism following cardiac transplant: a multi-institutional retrospective study
Authors: Alexandra A Rizaldi, Michael B Streiff, Ahmet Kilic, Shruti Chaturvedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThromboembolism (TE) is a serious and potentially life-threatening complication following orthotopic heart transplant (OHT) that is not well described in current literature.ObjectivesWe aimed to characterize postoperative venous (VTE) and arterial thromboembolism (ATE) after OHT and describe their impact on survival.MethodsWe identified adult (aged ≥18 years) patients undergoing OHT from 2016 to 2022 in the TriNetX Research Network, which includes data from >140 million individuals from 102 institutions. Our primary outcomes were VTE or ATE within 30 days posttransplant and 1-year mortality. We used multivariable logistic regression to evaluate risk factors associated with TE and the association between TE and 1-year mortality.ResultsAmong 2625 patients from 28 institutions who underwent OHT, the 30-day incidence of TE was 34% (VTE 27%, ATE 16%), and 1-year mortality was 6%. Patients experiencing TE were older (54 vs 52 years, P < .001) and more likely to have comorbidities (P < .05). Risk factors independently associated with VTE were history of hypertension, diabetes, and prior VTE; for ATE, history of diabetes, prior ATE, and prior VTE were significant (P < .05). Risk of 1-year mortality increased with VTE (odds ratio [OR] 1.43, P = .045) and ATE (OR 1.82, P = .002), but this association was not significant after adjusting for covariates (VTE: adjusted OR 0.97, P = .86; ATE: adjusted OR 1.39, P = .12).ConclusionTE is common after OHT and may reflect underlying patient comorbidity, emphasizing the need for high clinical suspicion of TE post OHT to mitigate morbidity and mortality.Keywords: cardiac surgery, cardiac transplant, cerebral infarction, deep vein thrombosis, myocardial infarction, outcomes, posttransplant, pulmonary embolism, risk factors, stroke, thromboembolism, thrombosis

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) affects over 6 million adults over age 20 in the United States and is responsible for 10% of all deaths [1]. Orthotopic heart transplant (OHT) remains the gold standard treatment for selected patients with advanced HF to improve their quality of life, functional status, and survival [2]. Over the past decade, improvements in donor availability and mechanical circulatory support have led to a steady increase in the volume of OHT in the United States to 3668 transplants in 2022 [3], while posttransplant mortality has remained <10% at 1 year [4].

OHT is a complex procedure requiring cardiopulmonary bypass, and patients are at risk of a wide range of complications, including thromboembolism (TE). In the first months after surgery, patients may experience graft-related complications (dysfunction, rejection, and vasculopathy) and non–graft-related complications (infection, kidney dysfunction, and thrombosis) [2] that contribute to morbidity and mortality. As OHT becomes more common, careful monitoring and effective management of postoperative complications such as TE will be crucial for improving outcomes in this population.

TE is a serious and potentially life-threatening complication following OHT. Estimates of TE incidence after OHT are widely variable across single-institution studies, ranging from 9% to 24% for venous thromboembolism (VTE) [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]] and not well reported for arterial thromboembolism (ATE), although studies report 3% to 10% for stroke [10,11]. Anticoagulation strategies after transplant vary widely, all aiming to achieve the delicate balance between bleeding and thrombotic complications. Prior studies have described the incidence and outcomes of TE in OHT recipients at the institutional level, but none have described recent trends in posttransplant TE across a large, representative national sample.

Using a large, multicenter data repository, we characterized the incidence and risk factors of postoperative TE after OHT and describe its impact on survival.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective analysis using the TriNetX Research Network database. Adult patients (≥18 years old) who underwent OHT from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2022 and survived to 30 days post OHT were included. The TriNetX database is a global federated health research network that collects real-time electronic medical data for >140 million individuals across 102 large healthcare organizations and has been described elsewhere [12]. Exclusion criteria included patients with missing data and those who underwent renal, liver, lung, pancreas, heart-lung transplantation, retransplantation, or had multiple OHT dates. This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board (IRB00439355, 04/26/2024).

Clinical data and outcomes

Patient characteristics included demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity) and medical history including but not limited to: hypertension (HTN), hyperlipidemia, diabetes, chronic lower respiratory disease, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation and flutter, coronary artery disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, sleep apnea, prior stroke, prior myocardial infarction (MI), prior deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and prior pulmonary embolism (PE). Demographic variables were derived from TriNetX, and medical history variables were identified using International Classification of Diseases-9 and -10 diagnostic codes (Supplement). Age in years was estimated using birth year and death month/year. The primary outcome was TE within 30 days after OHT, categorized as ATE or VTE. ATE included arterial embolism and thrombosis, cerebral infarction, and intracardiac thrombosis. VTE included phlebitis and thrombophlebitis, portal vein thrombosis, PE, and other venous embolus and thrombosis including DVT. The secondary outcome was 1-year mortality, estimated using death month/year and transplant date.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were compared between TE and non-TE groups using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables, which were determined to be nonparametric after assessing for normality through histogram visualization and Shapiro-Wilk tests.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify risk factors associated with VTE or ATE. Covariates included in the regression analyses were selected based on significant associations in univariate testing (P < .02).

Chi-square tests and univariable logistic regression were performed to assess the association between VTE or ATE and 1-year mortality. Multivariable logistic regression was then used to evaluate the risk of 1-year mortality associated with VTE or ATE, adjusting for patient characteristics selected using univariate analysis with P < .02.

Results

Patient cohort and outcomes

We identified 2625 patients from 28 healthcare organizations who underwent OHT during the study period. The overall cohort was predominantly male (64%) and White (60%) with a median age of 53 years (IQR 33-62 years). The most common comorbidities were: primary HTN (60%), hyperlipidemia (49%), diabetes (45%), chronic kidney disease (53%), and coronary artery disease (45%). The number of transplants included in our cohort represents ∼10% of the total heart transplants that occur in the United States each year; for example, 383 patients transplanted in 2022 were included in our cohort, representing 10.4% of the total 3668 adult heart transplants [13].

At 30 days post OHT, the incidence of thrombosis overall was 34%, and 1-year mortality was 5.8%. VTE occurred in 27% of patients; 24% developed DVT or other VTE, 4% developed PE, 0.8% developed phlebitis or thrombophlebitis, and 0.1% developed portal vein thrombosis. ATE occurred in 16% of patients; 7% experienced cerebral infarction, 6% experienced MI, 4% experienced other arterial embolism and thrombosis, and 3% experienced intracardiac thrombosis (Table 1). Overall, the incidence of TE has increased over time, from 27% in 2016 to 38% in 2022 (Figure).

Risk factors and outcomes of thrombosis within 30 days of OHT

Compared with patients who did not experience TE within 30 days posttransplant, patients who experienced TE (venous or arterial) were older (54 vs 52 years, P < .001), more likely to have a history of HTN (69% vs 55%, P < .001), hyperlipidemia (57% vs 45%, P < .001), diabetes mellitus (54% vs 49%, P < .001), overweight or obesity (34% vs 29%, P = .01), chronic lower respiratory disease (30% vs 22%, P < .001), chronic kidney disease (63% vs 48%, P < .001), atrial fibrillation and flutter (63% vs 59%, P < .001), coronary artery disease (50% vs 43%, P < .001), systemic lupus erythematosus (1.2% vs 0.3%, P = .008), or sleep apnea (34% vs 30%, P = .04), and more likely to have experienced a prior cerebral infarction (25% vs 10%, P < .001), prior MI (31% vs 17%, P < .001), prior DVT (59% vs 24%, P < .001), or prior PE (14% vs 5%, P < .001) (Table 2).

Thrombosis and the risk of 1-year mortality

Among patients who experienced VTE within 30 days posttransplant, the incidence of 1-year mortality was 7% (51/697), while the incidence for those who did not experience VTE was 5% (101/1928), P = .06. Patients who experienced VTE within 30 days posttransplant were at higher risk of 1-year mortality in unadjusted analysis (OR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.00-2.01; P = .05). After adjusting for baseline characteristics that were significant on univariable analysis, this association was not significant (OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.65-1.42; P = .86) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this multi-institutional retrospective analysis of heart transplant recipients, we found that the incidence of TE in the first 30 days following transplantation is high and that ATE is associated with increased 1-year mortality.

Multiple patient and procedure-related factors likely contribute to the high rate of thromboembolic events in the month following OHT. Pretransplant, candidates are medically complex patients with limited mobility and comorbidities such as coronary artery disease [14], hypertension, diabetes, prior malignancy, chronic kidney disease, and obesity—all of which are risk factors for TE [15]. Although VTE and ATE have traditionally been considered to be distinct with separate etiologies, studies have identified shared risk factors underlying these conditions [16]. Obtaining a thorough clinical history may help identify patients at increased risk of these complications.

We also found that the rate of thromboembolic events has increased significantly over time, from 27% in 2016 to 38% in 2022. Two important factors that may have influenced this rising trend are technological advancements in diagnostic tools and changes to pretransplant management following the 2018 allocation policy change. Clinical prediction tools, standardized postoperative protocols, high-sensitivity D-dimer assays, compression ultrasound, computed tomography/magnetic resonance venography, and emerging technologies such as deep learning algorithms has led to improved diagnostic accuracy and earlier detection of TE [17]. Additionally, the October 2018 allocation policy change for adult heart transplant has led to higher-risk candidates undergoing transplant and higher rates of temporary mechanical circulatory support as a bridge to transplant [18]. Greater utilization of mechanical circulatory support with endovascular interventions such as intra-aortic balloon pump, Impella, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation increase thrombotic risk [19] in these transplant patients as well.

Studies have explored alterations in thrombotic homeostasis that may be associated with HF and physiological changes associated with OHT [5]. Intraoperatively, OHT recipients undergo significant surgical trauma over a 6- to 12-hour surgery that includes cardiopulmonary bypass and 5 major vascular anastomoses [20], which further increases the risk of postoperative TE. In the postoperative period, early endothelial dysfunction following transplant may precede cardiac allograft vasculopathy [21], the most common cause of death at 1 year posttransplant [22]. While allograft vasculopathy is a later manifestation of vascular compromise, a study by Arbustini et al. [23] reported a 30% prevalence of atherosclerosis in the native coronary artery in patients who experienced mortality in the first 2 months after transplant. Patients are also more likely to experience a prothrombotic state in their early recovery during which immobility, infection, and inflammation are common [24].

Our finding that VTE and ATE within 30 days posttransplant are associated with 1-year mortality in unadjusted analyses suggests that these complications contribute significantly to morbidity and mortality in the early postoperative period. In studies investigating VTE rates in abdominal transplant recipients, VTE was associated with greater in-hospital mortality, resource utilization, and length of stay [25]. However, the association between overall TE is not significant when accounting for baseline characteristics, suggesting that the increased mortality risk due to TE may reflect a patients’ overall state of health and their pretransplant comorbidity burden. ATE was associated with mortality even after adjusting for comorbidities, which may reflect the life-threatening effects of these thromboses by themselves.

The increasing incidence of TE after heart transplant indicates a drastic need for multifaceted strategies to reduce the rates of VTE and ATE in this population and for early detection and treatment of TE. Patients may not present with classic signs of TE, particularly after an extensive surgery such as OHT, leading to delays in diagnosis that adversely affect outcomes [26]. In the pretransplant setting, developing improved models to identify transplant candidates at high risk of TE is the first step in prevention. Current strategies to identify medical patients at risk are limited, as highlighted by Häfliger et al. [27] in their assessment of the Geneva, Padua, and IMPROVE risk assessment tools. Although the Caprini, Rogers, and Chao-Yang risk assessment models have been demonstrated to improve the identification of surgical patients at high risk for VTE [[28], [29], [30], [31]], further investigation of their utility in cardiac transplant patients specifically is warranted. Improvement of these tools would enable clinicians to develop a more accurate and expeditious index of suspicion for VTE in select patients following transplant.

At the patient level, the care team must determine the need for thromboprophylaxis while balancing the risk for bleeding complications due to anticoagulant use. Anticoagulant use after OHT has been reported in several studies to be safe and effective for therapeutic purposes, but these data did not study prophylactic anticoagulation and are limited due to small sample size and retrospective design [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. A single-center, retrospective study of cardiac transplant patients found that outcomes were similar between patients who were treated with no anticoagulation or prophylactic anticoagulation [36]. Additionally, early aspirin use after OHT has been associated with improved rates of cardiac allograft vasculopathy, but practices vary by institution, and there is no specific guideline regarding aspirin use or early thromboprophylactic strategies after OHT [37,38]. Beyond diagnosis and management, counseling patients about typical and atypical signs of TE may also improve early detection and treatment. There is a lack of awareness of TE complications [39] among patients, and patient education about TE prophylaxis has also been shown to improve medication adherence [40,41].

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis is constrained by the variables available in the TriNetX Research Network database, which does not capture intraoperative or donor characteristics and limits assessment of their influence on TE. These demographic, diagnostic, and procedural variables are subject to variable reporting or coding practices and thus may not completely reflect a patient’s health status. Second, we were unable to include TE etiology (catheter vs non-catheter), anticoagulation strategy, or details on postoperative management including immunosuppression in our analysis, which may have influenced the relationship between patient characteristics, TE, and mortality. Third, as a retrospective analysis, these findings are unable to establish causal associations. Fourth, despite robust adjustment for covariates, unmeasured confounding variables may also influence the results of this study. Finally, because this analysis included about 10% of all heart transplants in the United States, these results may not be fully generalizable to other centers, although our sample size is the largest to date and involves a substantial number of centers compared with other single-institutional studies.

TE in the early postoperative period after OHT is a common complication that reflects underlying patient risk and contributes to worse outcomes. These findings suggest that current strategies for preventing TE are insufficient and warrant reevaluation to accurately estimate TE risk and proactively manage patients at high risk. Identifying risk factors, high clinical suspicion, routine assessment, and patient education can promote early detection and treatment of TE. Future research should improve current risk models for VTE and ATE, evaluate the safety and efficacy of TE prophylaxis in the early postoperative period, and assess the utility of assessment protocols and patient education programs to improve patient outcomes.
