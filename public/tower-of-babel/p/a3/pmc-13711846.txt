The origin and function of the posterior ring in mouse sperm head
Authors: Bingbing Wu, Chenghong Long, Jiayi Liu, Zuobin Zhu, Liling Liu, Qingling Ma, Yan Long, Liying Wang, Huafang Wei, Yanjie Ma, Jingwen Hou, Heying Li, Li Yuan, Wei Li, Chao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite extensive characterization of sperm structures, certain specialized subcellular structures are still not well understood. Among these, the posterior ring has been recognized for over half a century; yet, its molecular composition and biological role remain unknown. This research aims to define the molecular components and functional roles of the sperm posterior ring.Methods3D-rendered confocal microscopy was used to determine the localization and developmental dynamics of SPEM3 and TEX50 in mature spermatozoa and spermatids at different steps of spermiogenesis. Spem3 and Tex50 knockout mouse models were generated to explore their physiological functions. Sperm morphology and ultrastructure were investigated through immunofluorescence along with transmission and scanning electron microscopy, whereas sperm motility was evaluated using computer-assisted sperm analysis. Proteomics, coimmunoprecipitation, and immunoblotting were performed to identify SPEM3 and TEX50 as structurally interdependent interacting components of the posterior ring and to elucidate how their loss disrupts sperm architecture and fertility.ResultsWe identified SPEM3 as a core component of the posterior ring located at the sperm head–tail linkage. The posterior ring arises from the marginal ring at the acroplaxome periphery during early spermiogenesis. Loss of Spem3 led to severe bending of the head–tail linkage, accompanied by a marked reduction in sperm motility, ultimately leading to male infertility. Furthermore, SPEM3 interacts and colocalizes with TEX50, and deficiency of either protein resulted in similar phenotypes, including disorganization of the posterior ring and postacrosomal region as well as impairment of the sperm connecting piece.ConclusionsThese findings establish SPEM3 and TEX50 as critical posterior ring components essential for maintaining postacrosomal integrity and anchoring the sperm head to the tail. By resolving the long unknown molecular composition of the posterior ring, this study provides new mechanistic insight into sperm head–tail integrity and the pathogenesis of certain forms of male infertility.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00997-7.

Introduction

Spermatozoa are indispensable for sexual reproduction, functioning as the sole carriers of paternal genome to the oocyte. To achieve this, they have evolved into highly specialized cells with streamlined morphology optimized for motility, oocyte recognition, and fertilization [1]. Their distinct head, neck, and flagellum are the result of complex differentiation processes [2]. While the general structure of spermatozoa has been extensively characterized, several fine subcellular specializations are still not well understood, particularly those involved in head remodeling and head–tail linkage.

The sperm head is highly polarized and structurally specialized for fertilization. Two major structures, the acrosome and the perinuclear theca (PT), surround the nucleus and contribute to the unique morphology and function of the sperm head [3]. The acrosome, covering the anterior head, contains hydrolytic enzymes required for zona pellucida penetration [4]. The PT, a dense proteinaceous layer beneath the acrosome, is divided into the subacrosomal layer (SAL) and the postacrosomal region (PAR, also known as the calyx). The SAL lies between the inner acrosomal membrane and nuclear envelope [5, 6]. A specialized cytoskeletal structure located within the SAL, termed the acroplaxome [7, 8], it is predominantly composed of F-actin and keratin-5 and serves as a mechanical scaffold that anchors acrosome to the spermatid nucleus. A circumferential structure of the sperm head, termed the posterior ring (also known as the basal belt or striated band), was first described by Gresson and Zlotnik in 1946 using light microscopy [12]. Electron microscopy and freeze-etching studies in the 1970s provided further characterization of its presence in multiple mammalian species [1, 9, 13, 14]. Located at the posterior border of the postacrosomal region, the posterior ring forms a close interface between the plasma membrane and nuclear envelope, demarcating the boundary between the sperm head and neck [1, 9, 15]. At the posterior end of the sperm nucleus, the nuclear envelope forms a concave indentation known as the implantation fossa, which serves as the docking site for the head–tail coupling apparatus (HTCA), the structure that tightly links the sperm head to the flagellum. The HTCA is composed of the basal plate, the capitulum, the centrioles (proximal and distal), and the segmented columns. The basal plate, located on the outer nuclear envelope, anchors the capitulum, which in turn connects to the segmented columns extending toward the proximal centriole, forming a mechanically integrated structure that secures the sperm tail to the head [16–18]. The anatomical position of the posterior ring at the junction between the sperm head and tail suggests that it may contribute to securing and reinforcing the head–tail linkage. While the acrosome, PT, and HTCA have been well-defined [17, 19–22], the molecular composition, developmental origin, and functional significance of the posterior ring remain largely unknown.

Here, we identify SPEM3, a protein conserved among mammals, as a posterior ring component at the sperm head–tail linkage in mouse and human. During spermiogenesis, SPEM3 localizes to the marginal ring encircling the acroplaxome and progressively shifts toward the posterior head. Spem3 knockout mice are infertile, with sperm showing severe bending at the head–tail linkage and drastically reduced motility. Furthermore, SPEM3 interacts and colocalizes with the testis-enriched protein TEX50, and Tex50 knockout males exhibit nearly identical phenotypes, including head–tail bending and motility defects. Deficiency of either SPEM3 or TEX50 destabilizes the implantation fossa of the nuclear envelope, thereby compromising the stability of the head–tail linkage during late spermiogenesis. Additionally, loss of either protein results in defective posterior ring and postacrosomal region in spermatozoa. These findings define SPEM3 and TEX50 as essential structural components of the posterior ring, providing mechanistic insights into sperm head–tail integrity and implicating posterior ring defects as potential contributors to male infertility in humans.

Materials and methods

Animals

The mouse Spem3 gene (ENSMUST00000210714.2) is located on chromosome 11 with 3 exons, all of which were selected as the CRISPR-Cas9 target region. The Tex50 gene (ENSMUST00000135643.8) resides on chromosome 1 and contains two exons. Both exons were targeted for CRISPR-Cas9-mediated disruption. Spem3 knockout mice and Tex50 knockout mice were generated by Rise Mice Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Zhaoqing, China). Spem3-EGFP and Tex50-HA knock-in mice were generated by Cyagen Biosciences using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. For Spem3-EGFP line, a gRNA targeting Spem3, a donor plasmid carrying the “3 × EAAAK–EGFP” cassette, and Cas9 mRNA were coinjected into fertilized eggs. For the Tex50-HA line, a gRNA targeting Tex50, an HA-tag donor cassette, and Cas9 mRNA were similarly injected. F0 founders were screened by PCR and sequencing, followed by breeding with wild-type (WT) mice to confirm germline transmission and obtain F1 offspring. Primer sequences used for genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table S1. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at 23 ± 2 °C with a reverse light cycle and free access to food and water. Ethical approval for all animal experimental procedures was provided by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Ethics No. KTDW-2024–00337).

Phylogenetic analysis and protein sequence alignment

Amino acid sequences of SPEM3 and TEX50 from different species were obtained from UniProt. Phylogenetic analyses were performed in MEGA 11.0 [23] using the Neighbor-Joining method [24] with 1000 bootstrap replicates based on the p-distance model [25]. Multiple sequence alignments generated in MEGA 11.0 and visualized using ESPript 3.0.

Assessment of fertility

The 2-month-old males of each genotype were paired individually with three 7–8-week-old WT C57BL/6 females. Vaginal plugs were checked daily, and plugged females were separated for monitoring. Delivery dates and litter sizes were recorded. Females that had not delivered within 21 days after mating were regarded as nonpregnant and euthanized to confirm pregnancy status.

Sperm motility and sperm count assays

The cauda epididymides were isolated from males of each genotype, and spermatozoa were released into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, catalog no. 10010023, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 10 min. The swim-up fraction was examined using a 10 × phase objective on an Axiolab 5 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, LLC, White Plains, NY, USA) equipped with a CCD camera (Hamilton Thorne, Beverly, MA, USA). Total and progressive motility were quantified by computer-assisted semen analysis (CASA) using CEROS software, and sperm concentration was determined with a hemocytometer.

Histology staining

As described previously [26], testes and cauda epididymides were harvested after euthanasia, fixed in Bouin’s solution overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated through an ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Thereafter, 5 μm sections were placed on slides, deparaffinized, and stained with H&E and PAS for histological analysis.

Human sperm sample preparation

Semen from healthy young Chinese donors was collected following medical screening to exclude genetic or major health issues, smoking, drug use, or heavy drinking. Samples were processed using a 40% PureSperm density gradient and washed with PBS prior to immunofluorescence staining. Informed consent was obtained, and the study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (ID: 2024126A01) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Plasmid

Mouse Spem3 and Tex50 were amplified from mouse testis cDNA and cloned into the pCMV-HA vector using the Clone Express Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (C115, Vazyme).

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells (Cat# GNHu17) were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Cat# C11995500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat# 16000044, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Cat# 15140122, Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells were passaged using trypsin–EDTA (Cat# 25200072, Gibco) and grown to 70–80% confluence before transfection. Expression plasmids were introduced using LipoMax DNA transfection reagent (Cat# 32,110, Sudgen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence

Testes were dissected and fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde for 5 min, placed on slides, gently compressed under coverslips, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. After removing the coverslips, sections were rinsed in PBS, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then with secondary antibodies before DAPI staining. Images were acquired using a Nikon AXR microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

For sperm immunofluorescence, cauda epididymal sperm were released into PBS at 37 °C for 10 min, mounted on slides, air-dried, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 5 min, washed, blocked with 5% BSA, and processed as above for antibody staining and imaging.

Scanning electron microscopy

Spermatozoa were released into PBS at 37 °C for 10 min, collected by centrifugation (500 g, 5 min), washed with phosphate buffer, and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight. Samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol, dried, gold-coated, and examined using a Hitachi SU8010 scanning electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Transmission electron microscopy

Cauda epididymides were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, washed, and cut into 1 mm3 pieces. Tissues were post-fixed in 1% OsO4 for 1 h, dehydrated with graded acetone, embedded in resin, sectioned, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using a JEOL JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunoblotting

Tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 350 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, catalog no. 04693132001, Basel, Switzerland). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C) and used for sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to nitrocellulose, blocking with 5% skim milk, and antibody incubation. Signals were detected using both the Touch Imager Pro (e-BLOT) and the Odyssey infrared imager (LI-COR Biosciences; RRID: SCR_014579). Antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Immunoprecipitation

Testes were lysed in IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, 1 mM DTT, 0.5% NP-40, pH 7.3) with protease inhibitors, briefly sonicated, and incubated on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation (12,000 g, 30 min, 4 °C), supernatants were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Immune complexes were captured using protein A beads for 3 h, washed, and processed for western blotting or liquid chromatography (LC)-mass spectrometry (MS) analysis.

Proteomics analysis

Gel bands were destained with 50% acetonitrile, dehydrated, and digested with trypsin overnight at 37 °C. Extracted peptides were dried, reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid, desalted by StageTip, separated using an EASY-nLC 1200 system, and analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. Full MS scans were collected at 60,000 resolution (350–1800 m/z), and MS/MS scans at 15,000 resolution. The top 20 precursors were selected for fragmentation with 20 s dynamic exclusion. Data were processed in PD v2.4 using Mus_musculus_10090_SP_20231220_add.fasta, with trypsin/P specificity, up to two missed cleavages, fixed carbamidomethylation (Cys), and variable N-terminal acetylation and Met oxidation.

Sample preparation for mass spectrometry

Sperm samples were homogenized using an MP FastPrep-24 instrument and lysed in SDC buffer (5% SDC, 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.5). Lysates were sonicated if needed, boiled for 15 min, and cleared by centrifugation (14,000 g, 40 min). Protein concentrations were measured by BCA assay. Approximately 15 µg of protein per sample were mixed with loading buffer, denatured, separated on 4–20% SDS-PAGE, and stained with Coomassie Blue. Equal volumes of each sample were pooled for quality control. For digestion, disulfide bonds underwent reduction with DTT at 37 °C for 1.5 h, alkylated with IAA at room temperature in darkness for 30 min. Trypsin was added at a 1:50 ratio (wt/wt) and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The digested peptides underwent desalting using an MCX column (OSMCX-1ML/6 mg, omicsolution, Shanghai, China), concentration by vacuum centrifugation, and reconstitution in 20 μL of 0.1% formic acid. Peptide concentration was determined by UV absorbance at 280 nm, and iRT calibration peptides were added for DIA experiments. Analysis of peptides utilized an OrbitrapTM AstralTM mass spectrometer (BRE725600, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, American) coupled to a Vanquish Neo LC system (BRE725600, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, American) in DIA mode. MS1 scans were acquired at 240,000 resolution (200 m/z) over 380–980 m/z, with normalized AGC target of 500% and maximum IT of 5 ms. MS2 scans employed 299 windows, each with 2 m/z isolation width, HCD collision energy 25 eV, normalized AGC target 500%, and maximum IT of 3 ms. DIA data underwent processing with DIA-NN 1.8.1, using trypsin as the enzyme (maximum 1 missed cleavage), carbamidomethylation (C) as fixed modification, and oxidation (M) and N-terminal acetylation as dynamic modifications. Proteins were identified at 99% confidence with FDR < 1%.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times, with results expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons between mean values of different genotypes were conducted using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests. A P-value less than 0.05(*), 0.01(**), 0.001(***), or 0.0001(****) was considered statistically significant.

Results

SPEM3 is a posterior ring component that originates from the marginal ring during spermiogenesis

SPEM3 is highly conserved across mammals (Fig. 1C and D) and contains a conserved transmembrane domain spanning residues 26–48, which was predicted by SMART (https://smart.embl.de/) based on the sequences of nine representative mammalian species (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). It exhibits a testis-specific expression pattern, with expression initiating at postnatal day 28 (P28), coinciding with the progression of spermiogenesis (Fig. 1E and F). SPEM3 exclusively localizes to the postnuclear region at the head–tail linkage in both mouse and human mature spermatozoa (Fig. 1G and H). Three-dimensional Z-axis reconstruction revealed a ring-like distribution at the posterior end of the human sperm nucleus (Fig. 1H), which matches the position and morphology of the posterior ring described in earlier studies [1, 3, 13]. To test whether SPEM3 specifically localizes to the posterior ring, double immunofluorescence with the PAR marker CYLC1 [10] showed that the SPEM3 signal was closely adjacent to the PAR but did not colocalize with it (Fig. 1I). We further generated Spem3-EGFP knock-in (Spem3-KI) mice using CRISPR-Cas9 to visualize SPEM3 in situ within spermatozoa, and western blotting confirmed successful model generation (Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B). Immunostaining with PAR-associated proteins CCIN and CAPZB [11, 21, 27] demonstrated that SPEM3 remains adjacent to the PAR (Fig. 1J and K). Together, these results establish SPEM3 as a posterior ring-specific protein in mature spermatozoa.

Spermiogenesis transforms spermatids into spermatozoa through 16 morphologically distinct steps [28]. To track SPEM3 during this process, EGFP signals were detected in developing spermatids from Spem3-KI testes. SPEM3 exhibited a ring-like pattern encircling the nucleus at different developmental stages (Fig. 2A). The marginal ring, located at the acroplaxome periphery, follows the descending edge of the acrosomal sac as it spreads across the anterior nucleus [29]. During acrosome biogenesis, a transient microtubule-containing structure termed the manchette forms caudal to the acrosome. The perinuclear ring anchors the manchette adjacent to the marginal ring, with a narrow groove separating the two structures [29, 30]. To refine SPEM3 localization, we performed immunostaining with ACTL7A, a marker that is dynamically localized to the acroplaxome and postacrosomal region [31]. Three-dimensional reconstructions across different Z-planes were generated to visualize cellular structures. In step 5–13 spermatids, SPEM3 localizes to the marginal ring at the acroplaxome periphery and progressively descends along the nucleus during acrosome biogenesis (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Movies S1–S3). Immunostaining with the perinuclear ring marker EB3 [32] revealed that SPEM3 is positioned above, rather than colocalizing with, the perinuclear ring, indicating its association with the marginal ring (Supplementary Fig. S3C). In step 14–16 spermatids, SPEM3 localizes to the posterior ring at the posterior end of the sperm head (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Movie S4). Body weight, testis size, testis weight, and testis-to-body weight ratios were comparable between Spem3+/+ and Spem3–/– mice (Fig. 3E–H), whereas sperm counts from the cauda epididymis were significantly reduced in Spem3–/– males (Fig. 3I). Computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) revealed strikingly reduced total and progressive motility (Fig. 3J and K). Histological examination of the cauda epididymis by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that the lumina filled with sperm in Spem3+/+ males, whereas Spem3–/– lumina contained far fewer spermatozoa (Fig. 3L). Additionally, spermatozoa from the cauda epididymis of Spem3–/– mice exhibited aberrant head–tail bending (Fig. 3L, red arrow). Quantification revealed that 92.05 ± 1.37% of Spem3–/– sperm exhibited this defect (Fig. 3M). Consistently, single sperm immunofluorescence analysis with the acrosomal marker sp56 confirmed severe head–tail bending in Spem3–/– spermatozoa (Fig. 3N).

To delineate the sperm head–tail coupling defects in Spem3–/– mice, we conducted transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on longitudinal sections of spermatozoa. In Spem3+/+ spermatozoa, the head–tail linkage was intact, characterized by tight attachment of the basal plate to the implantation fossa and close association of the capitulum and segmented columns with the basal plate. By contrast, Spem3–/– spermatozoa exhibited abnormal junctions, where the abnormal HTCA was detached from the implantation fossa (Fig. 4A, red asterisk). Quantitative analysis showed that 94.98 ± 2.76% of spermatozoa exhibited detachment of the HTCA from the sperm head in Spem3–/– mice (Fig. 4B). To pinpoint when abnormal head–tail coupling arises in Spem3–/– spermatozoa, spermatogenic stages in Spem3+/+ and Spem3–/– testes were examined by periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining [33]. In Spem3+/+ testes, spermatid heads at stages VII–VIII were uniformly oriented toward the basal membrane, whereas in Spem3–/– testes, several spermatid heads showed aberrant orientation toward the lumen. Consistent with these observations, H&E staining of testis sections at stages IX–X revealed spermatids with bent heads in Spem3–/– testes (Fig. 4D). To further validate the timing of defect onset, testicular cell suspensions were analyzed. Normal head–tail attachment was maintained through step 9–14 spermatids in both genotypes (Fig. 4E and F). However, in Spem3–/– spermatids, abnormal curly axonemal structures encircling the sperm head were first detected at steps 15–16 (Fig. 4E). Consistently, quantitative analysis showed that the proportion of spermatozoa exhibiting abnormal head–tail bending increased markedly at steps 15–16 in Spem3–/– mice (14.37 ± 2.95%), compared with Spem3+/+ mice (0.66 ± 0.57%) (Fig. 4F). Together, these findings demonstrate that sperm head–tail bending defects in Spem3–/– mice arise during late spermiogenesis, prior to sperm release. Collectively, Spem3 deficiency results in male infertility, characterized by abnormal head–tail bending and markedly reduced sperm motility.

TEX50 is conserved across reptiles and mammals, and is highly conserved within mammals (Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4B). Domain analysis indicated that transmembrane domains are present in all representative mammalian species (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Notably, TEX50 exhibits testis-enriched expression beginning at postnatal day 28, coinciding with SPEM3 expression (Supplementary Figs. S4D and S4E). Consistently, TEX50 protein levels were significantly reduced in Spem3–/– spermatozoa (Fig. 5D and E). Anti-GFP coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays using Spem3-KI testes, with WT testes as a negative control, further confirmed the interaction between SPEM3 and TEX50 (Fig. 5F and G). Prompted by this interaction, the subcellular localization of TEX50 was examined by immunofluorescence. TEX50 localized to the head–tail linkage in mouse spermatozoa (Supplementary Fig. S4F). S4G and S4H). Coimmunostaining with PAR-associated proteins CCIN and CAPZB further demonstrated that TEX50 signal is also adjacent to the PAR but does not colocalize with it (Fig. 5H and I), confirming its localization at the posterior ring of mature spermatozoa. Reciprocal Co-IP using Tex50-KI testes confirmed the interaction between TEX50 and SPEM3 (Fig. 5J and K).

Spatiotemporal dynamics of TEX50 localization during spermiogenesis were further assessed using Tex50-HA mice. TEX50-HA and SPEM3 colocalized at the marginal ring in step 5–13, and at the posterior ring in step 14–16 and in mature spermatozoa (Fig. 5M and N). Collectively, these findings reveal that SPEM3 and TEX50 exhibit coordinated, dynamic localization during spermiogenesis, sequentially associating with the marginal ring in early spermatids and the posterior ring in late spermatids and mature spermatozoa.

Discussion

The posterior ring marks the basal termination of the postacrosomal region and serves as a consistent landmark in mammalian spermatozoa [1, 9, 15, 18]. Although its anatomical position has been recognized for over half a century, its protein composition, developmental origin, and functional significance have remained largely undefined. Here, we identify SPEM3 as a conserved posterior ring protein in both human and mouse sperm, serving as a core posterior ring component that both interacts with and colocalizes with TEX50. During spermiogenesis, SPEM3 and TEX50 initially localize to the marginal ring at the acroplaxome periphery before locating to the posterior ring during spermatid elongation. Loss of either protein causes male infertility, characterized by severe bending at the head–tail linkage and markedly reduced sperm motility. Mechanistically, SPEM3 or TEX50 deficiency impairs the structural integrity of the posterior ring and postacrosomal region and destabilizes the attachment of the HTCA to the implantation fossa (Fig. 9). Structurally, the posterior ring may function as a belt that fastens the connection between posterior region and the implantation fossa, preventing their loosening and thereby ensuring the integrity of the sperm head and tail. The posterior ring has been proposed to act as a structural boundary separating the cytoplasmic compartments of the sperm head and tail by forming a zone of close association between the plasma membrane, postacrosomal sheath, and nuclear membranes [14, 38]. Our results extend this structural model by defining its functional importance. During spermiogenesis, SPEM3 and TEX50 dynamically localize from the marginal ring at the acroplaxome periphery to the posterior ring in elongated spermatids and mature spermatozoa. The dynamic movement of SPEM3 to the posterior region of the sperm head appears essential for forming a stable anchoring site at the head–tail linkage. In the absence of SPEM3 or TEX50, the nuclear envelope at the posterior region becomes unstable, preventing proper implantation fossa anchoring during the late shaping phase of spermatids. Consequently, HTCA components fail to attach correctly, leading to disorganization and bending of the head–tail linkage, which likely underlies the impaired motility observed in SPEM3- or TEX50-deficient spermatozoa. These findings indicate that the posterior ring functions as a fastening structure for the nuclear envelope at the sperm head base, thereby stabilizing HTCA attachment.

SPEM3 and TEX50 function as interdependent components of the posterior ring. They are located adjacent to the postacrosomal region and likely form an anchoring scaffold that maintains the integrity of both the posterior ring and postacrosomal architecture. Loss of either protein results in significantly downregulation of postacrosomal sheath–associated structural proteins. Accordingly, proteins normally localized to the postacrosomal region, including ACTL7A, CAPZB, CCIN, and CYLC1, are markedly reduced in SPEM3- or TEX50-deficient spermatozoa. These findings highlight their essential role in maintaining structural integrity at the posterior region of the sperm head. In addition to the defects in posterior ring and postacrosomal region, a substantial fraction of spermatids in Spem3–/– and Tex50–/– mice could not be recovered from the cauda epididymis. Histological analysis of seminiferous tubules at stages IX–X revealed retained, unreleased spermatids within the seminiferous epithelium of both Spem3–/– and Tex50–/– mice (Figs. 4C, D, 6N and O), suggesting impaired spermiation. These observations suggest that SPEM3 and TEX50 deficiency may impair spermiation or lead to subsequent phagocytic elimination of defective sperm.

A recent study reported that TEX50 is essential for maintaining acrosome integrity during epididymal transit [39], and suggested that TEX50 deficiency does not affect spermatogenesis but instead leads to sperm defects during epididymal transit. In addition, they used TEX50-mCherry knock-in mice to reveal a ring-shaped signal surrounding the acrosome that progressively shifts toward the posterior region of the sperm head during spermiogenesis. However, the precise subcellular localization of TEX50 and its structural context remain unclear, and the posterior ring was not addressed in their study. In contrast, our study identifies TEX50, together with SPEM3, as integral components of the posterior ring and provides detailed ultrastructural and molecular evidence linking this structure to head–tail coupling. Importantly, we found that loss of TEX50 or SPEM3 leads to defects that arise during the late stages of spermiogenesis, including abnormal head–tail bending (Figs. 4E, F, 6P, and Q) and detachment of the nuclear envelope at the posterior region in step 15–16 spermatids (Fig. 7J). Previous studies have shown that disrupted PT integrity impairs acrosome–nuclear envelope attachment and affects sperm head shaping [10, 21]. Given that SPEM3 and TEX50 localize to the posterior ring in late spermatids and mature spermatozoa, the acrosomal abnormalities observed in TEX50 deficient spermatozoa are likely secondary to posterior region disorganization. It is likely that these structural defects arise during the late stages of spermiogenesis, and that most spermatozoa exhibit head–tail bending during sperm transportation through the epididymis. Together, these findings expand the functional scope of TEX50 and reveal a previously unrecognized role for the posterior ring in coordinating postacrosomal integrity and head–tail coupling.

Additionally, SPEM3 and TEX50 deficient spermatozoa retain cytoplasmic droplet-like remnants at the head–tail linkage, indicating an essential role in cytoplasmic clearance. The significantly upregulated proteins detected in both Spem3–/– and Tex50–/– spermatozoa (Figs. 5B and 8G) likely reflect remnants retained due to defective cytoplasmic clearance. Given the close proximity of the posterior ring to the HTCA and the postacrosomal region, structural abnormalities in this region probably impair the mechanical forces necessary for cytoplasmic droplet migration, thereby explaining the retention of cytoplasmic remnants at the head–tail linkage. Deficiency of other SPEM family members, such as SPEM1 and SPEM2, has also been shown to cause cytoplasmic retention and head bending defects [40, 41]. Both proteins are significantly downregulated in Spem3–/– and Tex50–/– spermatozoa, together with their similar phenotypes, all these results suggest that they may also be posterior ring components. In addition, both Spem3–/– and Tex50–/– spermatozoa share hundreds of downregulated proteins which are conserved across human and mouse (Supplementary Figs. S6A and S6B). Except PT-associated proteins, acrosomal vesicle-related proteins, and flagellar proteins, most of the remain proteins are uncharacterized with respect to their functions and localizations (Supplementary Fig. S7). These uncharacterized proteins may be additional posterior ring components, which explains why the composition of this structure remains incompletely defined. The high conservation of SPEM3 and TEX50 in mammals, together with the posterior ring localization of SPEM3 in both mouse and human sperm, and the severe phenotypes of Spem3–/– and Tex50–/– mice, suggests that posterior ring defects could contribute to certain forms of human male infertility.

Conclusions

Collectively, our study defines the protein composition, developmental origin, and functional significance of the posterior ring. SPEM3 and TEX50 are indispensable for maintaining HTCA anchoring and postacrosomal integrity. These findings provide a molecular framework for understanding how posterior ring disruption leads to structural and functional sperm defects, laying the foundation for future studies aimed at elucidating the complete posterior ring protein network and its role in male fertility.
