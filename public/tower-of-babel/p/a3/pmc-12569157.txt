Villin like suppresses nasopharyngeal carcinoma proliferation by downregulating MYC
Authors: Yueting Wen, Ziying Zhou, Yibo Zhou, Zhongxi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe initial manifestations of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) are subtle and imperceptible, leading to unfavorable clinical outcomes and limited therapeutic alternatives among Southeast Asian populations. Deletion of chromosome 3p is a commonly observed genetic alteration in NPC. In our endeavor to elucidate the key genes and pathways driving NPC growth, we have discovered that Villin like (VILL), located at 3p22.2, functions as a novel tumor suppressor gene (TSG) with significantly diminished expression levels in NPC. This finding underscores the tremendous potential of VILL as an unexplored molecule warranting further investigation. Overexpressing VILL effectively inhibits proliferation by inducing G1 phase arrest in NPC cells while its knockdown yields contrasting effects on cell growth regulation. Furthermore, our study demonstrates that VILL targets MYC. Knockdown of VILL and overexpression of MYC significantly enhance proliferation in an immortal nasopharyngeal epithelial cell line NP69. Moreover, we are the first to report that VILL plays a tumor suppressive role in NPC. Consequently, VILL emerges as a novel prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic candidate for NPC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21495-4.Keywords: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, VILL, Loss of heterozygosity, Proliferation, MYCSubject terms: Cancer genetics, Head and neck cancer, Cell proliferation

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) exhibits a pronounced prevalence in southern China, southeast Asia, and north Africa, where the age-standardized incidence rates, as reported by GLOBOCAN, lie between 4 and 25 cases per 100,000 individuals1–3. Notably, these rates far surpass (by a factor of 50 to 100 times) those recorded in other global regions2,4. The subtypes of NPC include keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma, non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma, and undifferentiated or poorly differentiated carcinoma1. In endemic areas, the non-keratinizing subtype accounts for over 95% of cancers, while in the United States it represents 75% of NPC cases5,6. This notable geographical variance is believed to result from a mix of EBV infection, genetic predispositions and environmental influences7–10. In China, the majority of patients with NPC are linked to EBV infection, which causes changes in the methylation levels of specific host genes1,11,12. This, in turn, leads to the indirect suppression of tumor suppressors, thereby accelerating tumor initiation and progression12.

The early symptoms of NPC are inconspicuous1. Approximately 70% of individuals with NPC receive their diagnosis at an intermediate or advanced stage, or exhibit signs of distant metastasis, significantly impacting their prognosis1,4. Currently, the early diagnosis of NPC remains a formidable challenge13. Tissue biopsy continues to be the gold standard for diagnosing this condition13. The primary treatment modalities encompass radiotherapy and chemotherapy6,11; however, these interventions often lead to severe bone marrow suppression and other adverse effects, thereby contributing to an overall unfavorable prognosis for patients. Therefore, it is imperative to explore novel biomarkers for the early diagnosis of NPC in order to facilitate timely detection and intervention, ultimately reducing the incidence and improving the cure rate of this malignancy.

The chromosomal locus 3p22-21.3 in NPC frequently exhibits abnormalities such as loss of heterozygosity (LOH)14–17. Therefore, as a prototypical tumor suppressor gene (TSG) locus, allele loss at 3p22-21.3 is considered an early premalignant alteration detected in NPC (Genes listed in Table S1)14,15. Multiple genetic and epigenetic investigations have been conducted to identify the associated TSGs at this locus, including RASSF1A16, ZMYND1017, and PLCD118. VILL (Vill-like), a member of the gelsolin superfamily, is located in 3p22.2 (starts at 37987978, ends at 38007185) and potentially functions as a tumor suppressor. The gelsolin superfamily comprises Ca2+-dependent, multi-domain regulators that govern actin cytoskeleton dynamics19. However, different members within this superfamily exhibit varying degrees of Ca2+ binding affinity, leading to differences in their ability to modulate actin dynamics20,21.

At present, there are few studies on VILL in tumors. VILL was markedly reactivated in A549 and H1703 cells infected with miR-26A1 lentivirus while this activation was inhibited by JQ122. Our previous research found that VILL methylation assessment combined with EBV DNA detection presents a promising avenue for non-invasive diagnosis of NPC, particularly benefcial for early detection23. However, the role of VILL in suppressing tumor growth in NPC remains elusive. Therefore, our objective was to investigate the expression level of VILL along with its biological function and underlying molecular mechanisms in NPC.

Results

VILL is specifically methylated in NPC patients

An analysis of the GEO datasets GSE52068 (Table S2) and GSE12452 (Table S3) revealed that 279 genes were down-regulated among the methylated genes (Fig. 1A, Table S4). Specifically, VILL and OSBPL10 were identified as methylated and down-regulated in the 3p22-21.3 locus due to frequent loss of heterozygosity (LOH) observed in patients with NPC (Fig. 1A). To further investigate these three genes, we conducted a search on the UCSC browser and found significant enrichment of H3K27Ac signaling associated with VILL, along with its association with the transcription factor Txn (Fig. 1B). These findings suggest a potential important biological role for VILL in NPC.

(B) VILL and OSBP10 in UCSC browser.

The downregulation of VILL in NPC is attributed to methylation

To explore whether the expression of VILL is associated with methylation aberrations, we examined the levels of VILL in NPC cells treated with or without the demethylating agent, 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (DAC). We found that VILL expression was upregulated in the presence of DAC in NPC cells (Fig. 2A, B). Previously, our study found that VILL methylation could be used as a marker for early diagnosis of NPC23. We performed VILL qMSP detection in the immortalized nasopharyngeal epithelial cell line NP69 and NPC cell lines (HONE1 without EBV, HONE1 with EBV, and NPC43 with EBV), revealing a significantly higher methylation level of VILL in EBV-positive cells (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that downregulation of VILL in NPC cells may be associated with hypermethylation of the VILL gene.

(B) The relative RNA level of VILL in NPC cell lines (CNE1, HONE1, HOEN1 EBV, HK1 and HK1 EBV) upon treatment with 12 μM DAC. VILL is downregulated in nasopharyngeal carcinoma

The RNA expression of VILL in NPC tissues was significantly downregulated compared to normal tissues, as shown by the analysis of GEO dataset GSE12452 (Fig. 3A, Table S2). To further elucidate the clinical significance of VILL in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), we investigated the expression of VILL in a cohort of clinical samples. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on a tissue microarray (TMA) comprising 99 NPC tissues and 32 nasopharyngeal inflammation (NPI) tissues to assess VILL expression. The results showed that NPC tissue expressed lower VILL levels than NPI tissue (Fig. 3B, C). The expression of VILL was significantly reduced in patients with advanced clinical stages (TableS5). Importantly, compared to the high VILL group, low VILL expression was related to shorter survival (Fig. 3D). Additionally, we also observed the expression of VILL in NP69 and various NPC cell lines, revealing either negligible or minimal expression levels in EBV positive cell lines. (Fig. 3E, F).

VILL shows a strong tumor-suppressive ability

The function of VILL was investigated by transfecting it into 2 nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells (S18 and HONE1 EBV). The expression of VILL was significantly upregulated in S18 and HONE1 EBV (Fig. 4A, B). Moreover, CCK8 and colony formation assays revealed that VILL exerted a significant inhibitory effect on cell proliferation (Fig. 4C).

To further explore the tumor-suppressive function of VILL, VILL was efficiently knocked down by two shRNAs (shVILL-1 and shVILL-2) in HONE1 nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells. The effect of silencing was revealed by qPCR and Western blot (Fig. 4F, G). Functional assays were performed to characterize the effect of VILL knockdown in HONE1. The results showed that when VILL is silenced, the rate of cancerous cell growth (Fig. 4H) and the colony formation ability significantly increases (Fig. 4I, J).

VILL inhibits tumorigenesis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells in vivo

A subcutaneous tumorigenesis model was used to evaluate the oncogenic role of VILL in nasopharyngeal carcinoma in vivo. Firstly, Nod-SCID mice were inoculated with S18 VILL- and non-transfected cells in the bilateral dorsal region. The xenograft growth curve showed that tumors induced by VILL-transfected cells grew much slower than tumors induced by non-transfected cells (Fig. 5A). Seven weeks later, mice were euthanized following anesthesia with isoflurane, administered at an output gas flow of 0.6 L/min and induction and maintenance concentrations of 3–4% and 1–2%, respectively; subsequently, the tumors were harvested (Fig. 5B). The average tumor volume and weight induced by VILL-transfected cells were significantly lower compared to tumors volume and weight induced by non-transfected cells (Fig. 5C, D). We also evaluated the cell proliferation index in these solid tumors. The VILL-transfected tumors exhibited decreased BrdU signal as compared to the control cells (Fig. 5E).

VILL arrests cell cycle by regulating the G1/S checkpoint

The EdU staining results revealed a decreased number of EdU-positive cells in the VILL-transfected group compared to the control group, indicating that VILL may exert inhibitory effects on the G1/S phase transition of NPC cells cycle (Fig. 6A, B). The cell cycle was assessed using flow cytometry to confirm that VILL effectively arrests the cell cycle at the G1 phase (Fig. 6C, D). The expression levels of CDK2 and Cyclin E1 were significantly reduced in NPC cells overexpressing VILL (Fig. 6E).

(E) The protein expressions of CDK2 and cyclin E1 were compared with VILL- and non-transfected NPC cells. β-actin was used as a loading control. VILL induce gene expression changes in NPC cells that affect a range of cellular processes and pathways linked to survival

In order to gain further insights into the molecular mechanism of VILL, we conducted RNA-seq analysis in HONE1 EBV cells transfected with VILL and compared it with non-transfected cells. VILL overexpression resulted in 1704 genes altered globally, including 371 up-regulated genes and 1333 down-regulated genes (Fig. 7A, P.value < 0.05 & |LogFC| > 0.45, Table S6). The downregulation of MYC was observed in HONE1 EBV cells transfected with VILL (Fig. 7B,C). Accordingly, the Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that a range of cellular processes and pathways linked to survival, were all significantly changed in VILL group (Fig. 7D). The GSEA analysis further confirmed the inhibition of the growth factor activity pathway in the VILL group (Fig. 7E). Further differential analysis revealed that genes involved in this pathway were all downregulated by overexpressing VILL (Fig. 7F,G), supporting the regulatory roles of VILL in tumorigenesis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma.

VILL promotes the proliferation of NP69

The aforementioned data collectively demonstrate that VILL exerts a substantial inhibitory impact on NPC cell proliferation through its regulation of the cell cycle. Considering the critical roles of proliferation in NPC, we then tested the proliferation of VILL in immortal nasopharyngeal epithelial cell line NP69. We knocked down VILL in NP69 cells and found that MYC expression was upregulated (Fig. 8A, B). It has been reported that MYC plays a pro-oncogenic role in various tumors24. The subsequent downregulation of VILL or ectopically expressed MYC in NP69 cells led to a significant enhancement in cellular growth and proliferation (Fig. 8C-G; Table S7). Altogether, these data suggested that VILL and MYC promote NP69 proliferation.

Discussion

The present study employed methylation and expression difference analysis to identify a novel tumor suppressor gene, VILL, located at 3p22.2 in NPC. Functional experiments confirmed the tumor suppressor role of VILL in NPC progression. Remarkably low expression levels of VILL were observed in NPC, suggesting its crucial involvement in disease progression. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate the anti-cancer effects of VILL in two NPC cell lines (S18 and HONE1 EBV).

In this study, we found a low expression of VILL in NPC. A proliferation test showed that VILL had a strong inhibitory effect on NPC cell growth in vitro and in vivo. In addition, VILL was found to block the G1 phase of the cell cycle and thus inhibit cell proliferation. Furthermore, our data suggests that the knockdown of VILL can enhance the growth and proliferation abilities.

Through RNA-seq, we found that the overexpression of VILL could up-regulate 371 genes and down-regulate 1333 genes. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and subjected to GO and GSEA analysis. VILL overexpression induced significant alterations in gene expression within NPC cells, impacting various cellular processes and pathways associated with cell survival. Among down-regulate genes, MYC has high basal expression abundance in NPC cells, which aroused our interest. We examine published reports and find that MYC plays a pro-oncogenic role in various tumors23. In this study, we conducted VILL knockdown and MYC overexpression in NP69 cells, which resulted in a significant enhancement of cell proliferation. However, further research is warranted to establish direct degradation of MYC by VILL. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the novel function of VILL in regulation of cell cycle progression.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that reduced VILL expression in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) tissue is indicative of an unfavorable prognosis in patients. Moreover, VILL may further enhance the activity of the cyclin E1-CDK2 complex by downregulating MYC expression, thereby positively regulating cell cycle progression and promoting cell proliferation. This is the first report of the function of VILL in NPC biology.

Materials and methods

Tissue microarray and clinical specimens

The tissue microarray (TMA, 5 μm thick), which includes 99 NPC tissues and 32 nasopharyngeal inflammation (NPI) tissues. Enrolled patients signed the informed consent form, and the study was approved by the ethical committees of Guilin Fanpu Biotech, Inc. (FANPU 2018 NO.23). Additionally, immunohistochemistry (IHC) was conducted on the TMA to investigate the protein levels of VILL. The results were independently evaluated by the professional pathologist as follows: staining intensity = 0 (negative), 1 (lightly yellow), 2 (brownish–yellow) or 3 (brown).

Gene expression omnibus (GEO) database

The external validation was performed using independent cohorts from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): The study employed a total of 31 nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) and 10 normal healthy nasopharyngeal tissue specimens obtained from GSE12452, which is an expression profiling by array. The experiment involved methylation profiling by the array, using a total of 24 NPC and 24 normal nasopharyngeal epithelial tissues from GSE52068.

Cell culture

The human NPC cell lines S18 and CNE1, were obtained from the Cancer Institute of Southern Medical University. The cell lines NPC43, HONE1, HONE1 EBV, HK1, and HK1 EBV were generously provided by Prof. George S. W. Tsao from the Department of Anatomy at The University of Hong Kong in Hong Kong, China. These cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Vigonob, Guangzhou, China) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Vigonob, Guangzhou, China) in humidified 5% (v/v) CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. The immortal nasopharyngeal epithelial cell lines NP69 were obtained from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center and cultured in a serum-free keratinocyte medium (K-SFM, GIBCO).

DNA extraction, bisulfite conversion and detection of VILL methylation

DNA was extracted from cells using DNA extraction kit (DP304, TIANGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The bisulfite conversion of DNA was performed using the Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Simplite Bio, JSM530-50). As we previously described in our research methods, we detected VILL methylation in NPC cells and NP69 DNA based on TaqMan probe qPCR, and designed primer probes (Table S8). ACTB was used as an internal control. The 25ul reaction system was comprised of 12.5ul of qPCR premix (CW3332ZXQ, CWBIO), 2.5ul of primer and probe, 5ul of DNA template, and 5ul of ddH2O. The reaction conditions were as follows: predenaturation at 95℃ for 30 s; 60 cycles at 95℃ for 15 s, and fluorescence signal collection at 60℃ for 15s. The qPCR instrument was a Yarui MA-6000. We defined a CT value of VILL less than 50 with a clear S-type amplification curve as being positive for methylation. All valid samples must meet the quality control criterion of CTACTB≤36.

Plasmids, cell transfection, and lentiviral infection

The pTSB02-VILL-GFP-3FLAG-PURO (Cat.TSB180954-1), pTSB-shVILL-PURO (Cat.TSBTRC190113), pTSB-CMV-c-Myc-3FLAG-NEO (Cat.TSB4003146-1) and their blank vector were purchased from TranSheep Biotech (Shanghai, China). Lentiviral vectors were co-transfected into HEK293T cells with packaging vectors psPAX2 (Cat.12260, Addgene) and pMD2.G (Cat.12259, Addgene) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Infectious lentivirus particles were harvested at 48 h after transfection, filtered through 0.45 μm PVDF filters (Cat.SLHV033RB, Millipore), and transduced into cells. Stable clones were then selected using 0.5 µg/mL puromycin (Cat.ST551, Beyotime). Quantitative Real-time PCR or Western blotting assays were used to validate the infection efficiency.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using Trizol (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. For qPCR, RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat.RR036A, Takara). qPCR was performed using an SYBR Green PCR kit (Cat.RR820A, Takara) following the instructional protocol contained in manufacturer’s manual, and the signal detection was obtained using the Lightcycler96 real-time PCR system (Roche, USA). All samples were normalized to GAPDH or U6. Primers used in qPCR are listed in Table S8.

Western blot

NPC cells were washed twice with cold PBS and pelleted. Total protein was obtained using lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat.P0013) containing 1mM PMSF (Beyotime, Cat.ST506), incubated on ice with frequent vortex for 10 min, and the lysate was obtained by centrifugation at 12 000 g for 10 min. Proteins were fractionated by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Cat.ISEQ00010), blocked in 5% nonfat milk or BSA in TBS/Tween-20, and then blotted with specific antibodies overnight at 4℃: anti-VILL (1:500, OriGene, Cat.AP54512PU-N), anti-MYC (1:500, EnoGene, Cat.E11-10262 C), anti-CDK2 (1:500, BBI, Cat.D220395), anti-Cyclin E1 (1:500, Beyotime, Cat.AF6384), anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, Cat.10494-1-AP), anti-β-actin (1: 3000, proteintech, Cat.20536-1-AP), and anti-FLAG-tag (1:1000, CST, Cat.8146), and then with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-rabbit; 1:10,000; Abbkine) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the target protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (BIO-RAD, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

IHC was performed to detect the expression levels of VILL on the tissue microarray (TMA), which includes 99 NPC tissues and 32 nasopharyngeal inflammation (NPI) tissues, and the expression levels of BrdU on paraffin-embedded sections prepared from in vivo experiments. Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to endogenous peroxidase activity blocking, antigen retrieval, nonspecific binding blocking, and incubated with primary antibodies (VILL, 1:200, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat.HPA035675; BrdU, 1:200, Proteintech, Cat.66241-1-Ig) at 4℃ overnight. All sections were scored and validated by the two experienced pathologists.

Cell growth and proliferation assays

The tumor proliferation function of VILL was investigated by overexpressing VILL in NPC cell lines S18 and HONE1 EBV, as well as knocking down VILL in HONE1 and NP69 using shRNAs (shVILL-1 and shVILL-2). The cells transfected with corresponding blank vectors (Vec and shNC) were used as controls.

For the CCK8 assay, 1 × 103 cells in 100 µl medium solution were cultured in 96-well plates for 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h. At each time point, 10 µl CCK8 reagent (DOJINDO, Japan, Cat.CK04) was added to each well and incubated for another 2 h at 37 °C. Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Reader measured the absorbance of each well (Bio Tek, USA) set at 450 nm.

For the colony formation assay, 1 × 103 cells in 2 ml medium were seeded into six-well plates and cultured for 7 or 14 days. Cell colonies were then successively fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, and washed with water. The numbers of colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted.

EdU assays were performed using the EdU Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Ruibo, China, Cat.C10310-1) according to the manufacturer s instructions.

Cell cycle analysis with flow cytometry

VILL- and Vec- cells (5 × 106) were cultivated in a medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The addition of serum to the culture medium was discontinued when 70% of the cells were synchronized with cells at the G1 phase. After 72 h of incubation, 10% FBS was added to the medium, and cells were incubated for an additional 12 h. After that, the cells were fixed with 70% ethanol, stained with propidium iodide, and DNA content was determined using Cytomics FC (BD, USA). The obtained results were evaluated using Cell Quest and ModFit LT2.0 software (BD, USA). Triplicate independent experiments were also performed.

In vivo tumorigenicity study

Nod-SCID mice were purchased from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) and housed in an SPF-grade laboratory animal facility under a standard 12 h:12 h light: dark cycle with free access to water and food. The animal experiment was approved by the Committee review of animal experiments in Guangzhou Medical University (NO.S2024-219), and we confrm that all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant named guidelines and regulations complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

For tumorigenesis assay, S18 cells were transfected with VILL or empty vector, collected and resuspended in PBS. Then, 1 × 107 cells in PBS were subcutaneously injected into one side of the posterior flanks of Nod-SCID mice at 6–8 weeks old (n = 5). Tumor growth was measured every 4 days and calculated using the following formula V = 0.5×L×W2. After 7 weeks, all mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation following anesthesia with isoflurane, administered at an output gas flow of 0.6 L/min and induction and maintenance concentrations of 3–4% and 1–2%, respectively; subsequently, the weight of the xenografts was assessed.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA-seq was processed according to the instructions of NEB Next Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England BioLabs). Briefly, total RNAs were isolated from VILL-transfected or control HONE1 EBV cells using Trizol reagent. Poly(A) RNA was subsequently purified by using Poly Ttract mRNA Isolation System and used to generate cDNA libraries. All samples were sequenced on Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. Sequence reads were mapped to the human genome version hg38 by using Illumina sequence analysis pipeline. The average gene expression values of two independent studies were used for following analysis. These RNA-seq data were normalized for quantification by the TPM method, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using the limma package in the R programming language with thresholds of p-value cutoff < 0.05 and |logFC | > 0.45. GO and GSEA was performed by analyzing the TPM data from VILL-transfected or control HONE1 EBV cells.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise noted, the data were displayed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student t-tests were used to test for significant differences. All the analyses were performed by SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 26.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). P <.05 was considered as statistically significant.
