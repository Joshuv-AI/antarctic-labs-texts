Mean arterial pressure-to-norepinephrine-equivalent dose ratio, rather than achieved mean arterial pressure alone, is associated with mortality in septic shock
Authors: Xiaojun Zhao, Bo-yang Wang, Shi-tong Diao, Yangyanqiu Wang, Mao-mao Cao, Yan Chen, Tian-yuan Zhu, Rong Liufu, Shan Li, Chun-yao Wang, Wen-ying Han, Wei Jiang, Bin Du, Li Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigher mean arterial pressure (MAP) is commonly pursued in septic shock, yet randomized trials have failed to demonstrate a survival benefit—and patients achieving higher MAP often exhibit paradoxically greater mortality. We hypothesized this reflects impaired vascular responsiveness requiring greater vasopressor support, quantifiable by the MAP-to-norepinephrine-equivalent dose ratio (MAP/NEQ).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using two independent critical care databases (MIMIC‑IV and eICU). Adult patients with septic shock receiving vasopressors within 24 h of ICU admission were included. The primary exposure was the 24-h time-weighted average MAP (65–80 mmHg vs. ≥ 80 mmHg); the key secondary exposure was MAP/NEQ. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. Cox regression, restricted cubic splines, and mediation analysis were performed.ResultsA total of 7433 patients were included (MIMIC‑IV: n = 4944; eICU: n = 2489). Despite being younger with fewer comorbidities, patients with MAP ≥ 80 mmHg had higher 28-day mortality (MIMIC-IV: 19.5% vs. 12.6%; eICU: 20.5% vs. 11.0%; both P < 0.001). After adjustment for MAP/NEQ, the association between high MAP and mortality became non-significant, while MAP/NEQ remained independently and inversely associated with mortality in both cohorts (AUC 0.81 and 0.72). Mediation analyses revealed a suppression effect (− 33% and − 26%), indicating higher MAP coexisted with impaired vascular responsiveness. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were consistent.ConclusionsHigher achieved MAP was not independently associated with 28-day mortality after accounting for vascular responsiveness. MAP/NEQ may serve as a clinically accessible tool for early risk stratification and identification of a vasoplegic phenotype in septic shock, though prospective validation is needed before clinical application.

Background

Septic shock is characterized by pathological vasodilation and distributive circulatory failure, carrying a mortality rate of 20–30% in contemporary intensive care unit (ICU) cohorts [1, 2]. Restoration of adequate organ perfusion pressure, typically assessed by mean arterial pressure (MAP), has long been a central goal of resuscitation [3, 4]; current guidelines recommend a minimum MAP of 65 mmHg.

However, evidence supporting higher MAP targets remains inconsistent. Randomized trials comparing higher vs. standard MAP goals have not demonstrated a survival benefit with more aggressive pressure targets. The SEPSISPAM trial found no difference in 28-day mortality between MAP targets of 80–85 mmHg vs. 65–70 mmHg, though post hoc analyses suggested possible benefit in patients with chronic hypertension [5]. More recently, the OPTPRESS trial—conducted across 29 Japanese ICUs in older patients with septic shock—was terminated early due to evidence of harm, with significantly higher 90-day mortality in the high MAP target group and no benefit in any subgroup [6]. Collectively, these findings challenge the assumption that achieving higher arterial pressure translates into improved outcomes.

Yet an important question remains: why do some patients with septic shock experience adverse outcomes despite achieving higher arterial pressure levels? A critical limitation of MAP as a resuscitation target is that it conflates two distinct physiological processes: vascular responsiveness and vasopressor intensity. It is important to acknowledge that achieved MAP and vasopressor requirements are also influenced by other clinical factors, including intravascular volume status, cardiac function, and the intensity of early resuscitation. In clinical practice, achieved MAP represents the net result of how efficiently the vasculature responds to pharmacologic support. In severe vasoplegia, higher arterial pressure may be achieved only through escalating vasopressor doses, a situation that suggests drug-dependent circulatory failure rather than restored vascular tone. Consequently, patients with similar MAP values may differ substantially in the degree of vascular dysfunction.

To address this limitation, Bosch et al. proposed the MAP-to-norepinephrine-equivalent dose ratio (MAP/NEQ) as a functional index of vascular responsiveness—analogous in concept to the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio in respiratory failure, integrating physiological effect with treatment intensity [7]. A higher MAP/NEQ indicates preserved vascular responsiveness, whereas a lower value reflects impaired vascular responsiveness and vasopressor dependence [7, 8]. Emerging data suggest that MAP/NEQ may be associated with mortality, guide the timing of enteral nutrition, and identify patients at risk of requiring renal replacement therapy [9, 10]. However, whether MAP/NEQ explains the paradoxical association between higher achieved MAP and excess mortality in large independent cohorts has not been systematically examined.

We hypothesized that in septic shock, adverse outcomes observed among patients with higher achieved MAP might be more closely related to impaired vascular responsiveness requiring greater vasopressor support than to perfusion pressure itself. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a retrospective cohort study using two large independent critical care databases. Our objectives were: (1) to compare 28-day mortality between patients grouped by MAP thresholds (65–80 mmHg vs. ≥ 80 mmHg) and (2) to evaluate the independent prognostic value of MAP/NEQ and its role in mediating the association between higher achieved MAP and mortality.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective cohort study utilizing two publicly available critical care databases: the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care‑IV (MIMIC‑IV, version 3.1) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU–CRD, version 2.0). The MIMIC‑IV database, maintained by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, contains de-identified clinical data of over 94,000 ICU admissions admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022 [11]. The eICU–CRD includes de-identified records of approximately 200,000 ICU admissions from more than 200 hospitals across the United States between 2014 and 2015 [12]. This retrospective study was reviewed and granted an exemption by the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (IRB approval number: I-26ZM0052).

Study population

Adult patients with sepsis were identified using the Sepsis-3 criteria implemented in the publicly available MIMIC concept SQL code. Briefly, suspected infection was defined by the temporal co-occurrence of microbiological culture sampling and antibiotic administration: culture sampling within 24 h after antibiotic initiation or antibiotic initiation within 72 h after culture sampling. Organ dysfunction was defined as a SOFA score of ≥ 2. Patients satisfying both suspected infection and SOFA criteria were included in the sepsis cohort. Patients were included if they met the following criteria within the first 24 h of ICU admission: (1) recorded MAP values and (2) administration of at least one vasoactive agent.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) age < 18 or > 90 years; (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) ICU length of stay < 24 h; (4) incomplete baseline demographic or clinical data; (5) active malignancy; (6) serum lactate level < 2 mmol/L; and (7) missing data for the primary outcome.

The patient selection process was applied independently to each database. A total of 7,433 patients were included in the final cohort, comprising 4,944 from MIMIC‑IV and 2,489 from eICU. The complete flowchart is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Data extraction

We extracted the following variables from both databases: demographic information (age and sex), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, heart failure, and ischemic heart disease), ICU interventions (e.g., invasive mechanical ventilation and vasopressor use), and clinical outcomes (28-day ICU mortality and ICU length of stay).

Physiological and therapeutic variables during the first 24 h after ICU admission were collected, including MAP, NEQ, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, and laboratory parameters (white blood cell count, lactate, creatinine, and fluid balance).

Exposure definitions

The primary exposure was the 24-h time-weighted average MAP, calculated from all valid invasive arterial pressure measurements recorded during the first 24 h after ICU admission. Only invasively measured MAP values were used to minimize measurement heterogeneity. Patients were categorized a priori into two groups according to MAP level: 65–80 mmHg and ≥ 80 mmHg.

The key secondary exposure was the MAP/NEQ, defined as the 24-h average MAP divided by the concurrently averaged NEQ. NEQ quantifies total vasopressor intensity by converting all administered vasoactive agents to norepinephrine-equivalent doses using established pharmacologic equivalences [13]:

Where NE = norepinephrine, EPI = epinephrine, PHE = phenylephrine, DA = dopamine, MET = metaraminol, ADH = vasopressin, and Ang II = angiotensin II; all doses in μg/kg/min. Norepinephrine doses, initially reported as base equivalents, were converted to salt-formulation doses (tartrate: × 2; bitartrate: × 1.89; hydrochloride: × 1.22) using published conversion factors prior to NEQ calculation [14, 15].

The 24-h average NEQ was computed as a time-weighted mean using the rectangular method [16]. A higher MAP/NEQ indicates preserved vascular responsiveness, whereas a lower value reflects vasopressor dependence and impaired vascular reactivity.

Covariates

Covariates included age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease), fluid balance, APACHE II score, SOFA score, shock index (the shock index is defined as the ratio of heart rate to systolic blood pressure, with a higher value indicating more severe shock [17]), and lactate level at ICU admission.

Outcome

The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay (LOS). Patients discharged alive before day 28 were assumed to be alive at 28 days.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent-samples t test; non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are summarized as counts (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The association between MAP category and 28-day mortality was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models with sequential covariate adjustment. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, fluid balance, APACHE II score, SOFA score, shock index, and lactate. Model 2 further adjusted for MAP/NEQ. Model 3 additionally adjusted for NEQ. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using global Schoenfeld residual tests. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors.

Nonlinear dose–response relationships between MAP/NEQ and 28-day mortality were modeled using restricted cubic spline regression within the Cox framework. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Discriminative performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Mediation analyses were conducted using a counterfactual framework with bootstrap resampling to quantify the proportion of the MAP–mortality association attributable to MAP/NEQ and NEQ, respectively. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed across strata defined by age, sex, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease, with interaction terms tested to assess effect modification. Sensitivity analyses included re-computation of MAP/NEQ using peak NEQ (NEQmax) within 24 h, and dichotomization of MAP/NEQ based on the cohort median.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

When stratified by MAP category (Table S2), patients in the MAP ≥ 80 mmHg group were younger with fewer comorbidities yet had paradoxically higher 28‑day mortality (MIMIC‑IV: 19.5% vs. 12.6%, P < 0.001; eICU: 20.5% vs. 11.0%, P < 0.001), longer ICU stays, and greater vasopressor requirements despite achieving higher MAP levels. Notably, MAP/NEQ was only marginally higher in the MAP ≥ 80 mmHg group (MIMIC‑IV: median 0.89 [0.49, 1.38] vs. 0.84 [0.48, 1.26], P < 0.001; eICU: 0.76 [0.41, 1.24] vs. 0.74 [0.39, 1.18], P = 0.019), which represented a very small absolute difference that contrasted with the substantially higher mortality observed in this group.

Association between MAP Category and 28‑day mortality

On univariable Cox regression (Table S3), patients with MAP ≥ 80 mmHg had a significantly higher 28‑day mortality risk in both cohorts (MIMIC‑IV: HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.10–1.55, P = 0.002; eICU: HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.12–1.74, P = 0.003). After multivariable adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, and illness severity (Model 1), the association remained statistically significant (MIMIC‑IV: HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.18–1.81, P = 0.018; eICU: HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.24–1.95, P = 0.011; Table 2). However, when MAP/NEQ was introduced (Model 2), the association between high MAP and mortality became non-significant in both cohorts (MIMIC‑IV: HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.97–1.35, P = 0.124; eICU: HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.98–1.38, P = 0.384). Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses consistently supported the primary findings (Figure S2). When MAP/NEQ was recalculated using peak NEQ within 24 h (NEQmax), higher MAP/NEQmax remained significantly associated with reduced 28-day mortality after full adjustment in both cohorts (MIMIC-IV: HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.61–0.83; eICU: HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.54–0.85; both P < 0.001). Results were directionally and quantitatively consistent when MAP/NEQ was dichotomized using the cohort median, indicating robustness across alternative analytical strategies.

Discussion

In this large multicenter study of 7433 patients with septic shock from two independent critical care databases (MIMIC‑IV and eICU), we found that the apparent mortality risk associated with higher achieved MAP was largely associated with impaired vascular responsiveness, as quantified by the MAP/NEQ. Patients in the high MAP group (≥ 80 mmHg), despite being younger and having fewer baseline comorbidities, exhibited higher 28‑day mortality compared with those maintained between 65 and 80 mmHg; however, this association was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for MAP/NEQ, whereas MAP/NEQ itself remained independently associated with survival. Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a nonlinear association, and mediation analysis suggested that MAP/NEQ accounted for approximately 26–33% of the association between high MAP and mortality across the two cohorts. These findings were highly consistent across independent datasets and remained robust in subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Collectively, these findings suggest that the prognostic signal attributed to higher MAP may primarily reflect impaired vascular responsiveness rather than perfusion pressure itself.

Since the publication of the SEPSISPAM trial, randomized studies comparing higher (80–85 mmHg) vs. standard (65–70 mmHg) MAP targets have consistently failed to demonstrate a survival benefit with higher pressure goals [5, 18, 19]. However, these trials were designed to compare target strategies and did not clarify why some patients continue to experience poor outcomes despite achieving higher pressures. Although growing evidence indicates that higher vasopressor requirements are associated with worse prognosis, absolute drug dose alone does not capture the efficiency with which vasopressors generate arterial pressure [20–23]. The MAP/NEQ ratio, first proposed as an index of vasopressor responsiveness, provides a quantitative framework that integrates achieved pressure with vasopressor burden [7]. By validating its prognostic value in two large heterogeneous cohorts and directly comparing it with traditional MAP categorization, the present study suggests that the mortality association previously attributed to higher achieved MAP is correlated with MAP/NEQ. Moreover, mediation analysis indicates that impaired vascular responsiveness, quantified by MAP/NEQ, may represent one potential mediator linking higher achieved pressures to adverse outcomes. Although mediation analysis does not establish causality, it strengthens the biological plausibility that vascular responsiveness, rather than pressure alone, is the clinically relevant construct.

Septic shock is fundamentally characterized by vasodilatory failure driven by excessive nitric oxide production, relative vasopressin deficiency, catecholamine receptor downregulation, and activation of ATP‑sensitive potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle [24]. Within this pathophysiological context, MAP/NEQ can be conceptualized as a systems‑level “gain coefficient,” reflecting the efficiency with which the vasculature converts pharmacologic input into pressure output [5]. A higher MAP/NEQ suggests preserved vascular tone and responsiveness, whereas a lower ratio indicates drug‑dependent circulatory failure requiring escalating support to maintain perfusion [7, 25], which is consistent with our observations. Notably, NEQ alone was linked to only a small proportion of the high MAP–mortality association, whereas MAP/NEQ was associated with a substantially larger proportion. MAP/NEQ also demonstrated a suppression effect in mediation models, implying that higher achieved MAP may coexist with lower vascular responsiveness. This contrast between NEQ and MAP/NEQ suggests that prognosis may be more closely linked to the dose–effect relationship (e.g., MAP/NEQ) than to the absolute vasopressor dose. This interpretation may also help reconcile previously inconsistent findings regarding vasopressor dose thresholds, as prior studies have demonstrated a continuous dose–response association between norepinephrine exposure and mortality without a clear inflection point [26]. Furthermore, indices integrating arterial pressure response with vasopressor dose appear to better reflect vascular dysfunction severity than vasopressor dose alone [25]. Although statistically significant interactions were noted for age, sex, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes mellitus, the direction of effect remained consistent across all strata, suggesting that vascular responsiveness represents a broadly applicable physiological determinant rather than a subgroup-specific phenomenon. The attenuation of the association in patients with advanced age, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes likely reflects altered vascular physiology, including endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, and impaired autonomic regulation, which may modify but not negate the prognostic relevance of MAP/NEQ. The consistency of findings across alternative exposure windows further supports the robustness of this association and reduces the likelihood that the results were driven by specific operational choices.

From a clinical perspective, these findings have implications for both risk stratification and individualized hemodynamic management. Current prognostic assessment in septic shock primarily relies on organ dysfunction scores and lactate levels, which largely reflect established injury [27, 28]. In contrast, MAP/NEQ can be calculated within the first 24 h of ICU admission and provides a dynamic measure of vascular functional status. It may, therefore, help identify patients who appear hemodynamically stable according to conventional pressure criteria yet exhibit impaired vascular responsiveness [7, 29]. In both cohorts, MAP/NEQ demonstrated good discriminative performance for 28‑day mortality. Although the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was lower in the eICU cohort than in MIMIC‑IV, the direction and nonlinear pattern of association were consistent and threshold values were comparable. The reduced discrimination observed in eICU may reflect greater illness severity and inter‑institutional heterogeneity, which could dilute the predictive performance of a single physiological indicator.

These findings also challenge the simplicity of applying a uniform MAP threshold. A single pressure target may result in two forms of clinical misclassification: patients with preserved vascular responsiveness who may not benefit from higher pressures, and patients with profound vasoplegia in whom escalating vasopressor doses achieve target MAP at the cost of increased drug burden without correcting the underlying defect [20, 30]. In patients with markedly reduced ratios, further dose escalation alone may offer limited benefit, and alternative strategies—such as early combination therapy with vasopressin or angiotensin II, or more advanced hemodynamic monitoring—may warrant consideration [31, 32]. Whether such responsiveness‑guided approaches improve outcomes will require prospective evaluation.

Several limitations of this study need to be addressed. First, the retrospective design precludes elimination of residual confounding. Although we adjusted for major covariates, unmeasured factors—including pre-ICU fluid resuscitation, time from sepsis onset to ICU arrival, and infection source—may have influenced both vasopressor requirements and outcomes. Consequently, our mediation analysis results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive causal evidence. Second, the use of 24-h averaged MAP/NEQ does not capture real-time physiological dynamics. While this approach reflects early hemodynamic exposure, it fails to account for temporal fluctuations or longitudinal trends in vascular responsiveness during septic shock. Future studies employing time-varying or trajectory-based analyses are warranted. Third, our inclusion criteria may introduce selection bias. Excluding patients with ICU stay < 24 h or serum lactate ≤ 2 mmol/L limits generalizability to those with cryptic shock or early-phase septic shock who present with normal lactate yet may already have significant circulatory dysfunction. Additionally, the eICU–CRD dataset (2014–2015) is relatively outdated and may not fully represent contemporary sepsis care, although the consistent results from the more recent MIMIC-IV cohort (2008–2022) support the robustness of our findings. Fourth, both cohorts originated from the US healthcare system and were restricted to septic shock. Therefore, cautious extrapolation to other distributive shock types or different healthcare settings is recommended. Despite these limitations, the large sample size, inclusion of two independent heterogeneous cohorts, and consistent findings across multiple analytical approaches strengthen the robustness of our conclusions.

Conclusion

In patients with septic shock, higher achieved MAP was not independently associated with mortality after accounting for vascular responsiveness. The excess risk at higher pressures appeared associated with impaired vasopressor responsiveness, quantified by the MAP/NEQ ratio. MAP/NEQ may serve as a simple indicator for early risk stratification and individualized hemodynamic management, although its clinical utility requires prospective validation.
