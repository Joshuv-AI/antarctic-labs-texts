Dissociating hepatocellular necrosis from hepatic encephalopathy: a murine acetaminophen model to identify second-hit drivers of acute liver failure
Authors: Jenna Strickland, Yining Jin, Cheryl E. Rockwell, Anne M. Dorrance, Bryan L. Copple
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acetaminophen (i.e. paracetamol; APAP) overdose is the most common cause of acute liver injury in the United States, yet outcomes range from recovery to acute liver failure (ALF), defined by coagulopathy and hepatic encephalopathy (HE). Clinical decision-making is difficult because routine indices of hepatocellular injury (e.g. ALT/AST) do not reliably predict which patients will recover versus progress to ALF. This underscores the need to define mechanisms that drive progression to ALF and to discover biomarkers that better predict clinical trajectory. A major barrier is the lack of an experimentally tractable model in which severe hepatocellular injury is comparable, but neurological outcome diverges—a defining feature of human APAP toxicity. To address this, we established APAP dosing conditions in mice that produce similar peak liver injury yet distinct clinical courses. Mice treated with 300mg/kg or 500mg/kg APAP exhibited comparable increases in ALT and hepatic necrosis. In contrast, only 500mg/kg APAP caused robust neurological dysfunction consistent with HE, including impaired reflex integrity and motor coordination, accompanied by cerebral edema and reduced cerebral blood flow. Hyperammonemia is widely implicated in HE, but ammonia levels do not always track with HE severity in patients, suggesting additional cooperating factors. Consistent with this clinical discordance, blood ammonia increased to a similar extent in both dosing groups despite clear divergence in neurological impairment. Together, these findings establish a patient-relevant platform that dissociates hepatocellular necrosis (and hyperammonemia) from HE, enabling identification of mediators that drive neurovascular dysfunction and the transition from severe liver injury to ALF.

Introduction

Acute liver failure (ALF) is a rare, life-threatening condition that frequently arises after severe acute hepatocellular injury (Lee et al. 2008; Bernal and Wendon 2013). Clinically, ALF is defined by impaired hepatic synthetic function (coagulopathy) together with hepatic encephalopathy (HE), a neurological disorder that can progress from subtle neurological deficits to coma and death within days (Lee et al. 2008; Bernal and Wendon 2013). HE is not merely a complication of ALF; it is a principal determinant of intensive care utilization and mortality, in part because it can culminate in cerebral edema, intracranial hypertension, and brain herniation. Current management remains largely supportive, with liver transplantation as the only intervention for those that fail to recover. Because of limited organ availability and the potential for rapid clinical deterioration, however, a liver transplant is not always feasible (Stravitz 2008).

In the United States, acetaminophen (i.e. paracetamol; APAP) overdose is the leading cause of ALF. Hepatotoxicity is initiated when APAP is bioactivated by cytochrome P450 enzymes to the reactive metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) (Mazaleuskaya et al. 2015). At therapeutic doses, NAPQI is detoxified by glutathione conjugation and other phase II pathways (Mazaleuskaya et al. 2015). At hepatotoxic doses, detoxification capacity becomes overwhelmed, allowing NAPQI to adduct cellular macromolecules triggering hepatocyte death (Mazaleuskaya et al. 2015). Because hepatocyte injury is a defining feature of APAP overdose, research has largely focused on uncovering the mechanisms driving hepatocyte death (Mazaleuskaya et al. 2015). These hepatocyte-intrinsic mechanisms do not explain, however, why a subset of patients with severe injury develop HE and progress to ALF.

Indeed, after APAP overdose, patients with similar conventional indices of liver injury (e.g. alanine aminotransferase, ALT) can have strikingly different trajectories, ranging from rapid recovery to fulminant neurological deterioration and multiorgan failure (Antoniades et al. 2006; Lock et al. 2013; Kreimeyer et al. 2023). The disconnect between hepatocellular necrosis and outcome indicates that factors beyond the extent of hepatocyte death, govern progression to ALF. Defining these determinants is essential to discover biomarkers to predict progression to ALF and therapies to prevent this from occurring.

Mechanistic progress has been constrained by the limited availability of in vivo models that reproduce this key clinical paradox: comparable peak hepatic necrosis with divergent outcomes. An ideal model would (i) match liver injury severity across groups, (ii) reliably segregate mice that develop HE from those that do not, and (iii) permit interrogation of candidate mediators such as hyperammonemia, inflammatory signaling, and neurovascular dysfunction. Such a model would enable discovery of the hepatic and systemic signals that couple acute liver injury to brain edema and neurological impairment.

Bhushan and colleagues introduced an incremental-dosing strategy in which mice were treated with either 300 mg/kg or 600 mg/kg APAP by intraperitoneal injection (Bhushan et al. 2014). Despite similar peak necrosis, there was clear divergence in liver repair and survival, similar to findings reported in ALF patients (Bhushan et al. 2014). However, whether this approach captures the defining neurological component of ALF—HE—has not been established. Here, we adapted this paradigm to compare 300 mg/kg APAP (APAP-induced acute liver injury; AALI) with 500 mg/kg APAP (APAP-induced ALF; AALF), a dose chosen to minimize deaths within the first 24 h. We then applied neurological scoring methods, alongside direct measures of cerebral perfusion and brain water content, to quantify HE severity. Because hyperammonemia is strongly implicated in HE pathogenesis (Butterworth 2015; Jayakumar and Norenberg 2018), we also tested whether blood ammonia concentrations distinguish HE-positive AALF mice from HE-negative AALI mice.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

Twelve- to sixteen-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratories) were used for all studies. Mice were housed in a 12-hour light/dark cycle under con-trolled temperature (18–21 °C) and humidity. Food (Rodent Chow; Harlan-Teklad) and tap water were allowed ad libitum. Mice were fasted for approximately 16 h prior to the administration of APAP. APAP (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved to a final concentration of 10 mg/ml in sterile saline preheated to 37 °C. Mice were treated with sterile saline vehicle or APAP to a final dose of 300 mg/kg or 500 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection, as described previously (Bhushan et al. 2014). In all studies, rodent chow was returned immediately after APAP challenge. For all endpoints that were evaluated below there was an n = 3 for vehicle treated mice and n = 7 for all other groups. All studies were approved by the Michigan State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Assessment of liver injury

At 24 h after treatment with APAP or vehicle, mice were anesthetized using Fatal-Plus Solution (active ingredient 390 mg/ml pentobarbital; Vortech Pharmaceuticals). Before injection, the Fatal-Plus Solution was diluted to a final concentration of 50 mg/kg pentobarbital. The mice were treated with this diluted solution (4 μl/g of mouse) by intraperitoneal injection to a final dose of 200 mg/kg pentobarbital. Blood was collected, and the livers were removed. A portion of each liver was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. The livers were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The area of necrosis was quantified as described by us previously (Mochizuki et al. 2014). Hepatocyte injury was evaluated by measuring the serum activity of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (Pointe Scientific Inc., Canton, MI). Briefly, an aliquot of serum was mixed with the ALT reagent solution in a microtiter plate. The absorbance at 340 nm was measured over 3 min in a Tecan Infinite 200 Pro plate reader (Tecan, Mӓnnedorf, Zurich, Switzerland). ALT activity was calculated as detailed in the assay manual by Pointe Scientific.

Behavioral assessment of hepatic encephalopathy

A categorical, neurological scoring system, based upon five individual reflexes and the presence of ataxia, was used to estimate the severity of hepatic encephalopathy at 24 h after APAP treatment. This scoring system was proposed by the International Society of Hepatic Encephalopathy and Nitrogen Metabolism (ISHEN) working group for the assessment of hepatic encephalopathy in mice (DeMorrow et al. 2021). The following behavioral endpoints were assessed.

Pinna Reflex: The auditory meatus was touched with a cotton applicator 3 times, and the mouse was observed for ear retraction and/or head movement.

Pinna Reflex: The auditory meatus was touched with a cotton applicator 3 times, and the mouse was observed for ear retraction and/or head movement.

Corneal Reflex: The cornea was gently touched 1 time with a cotton applicator, and the mouse was observed for blinking.

Corneal Reflex: The cornea was gently touched 1 time with a cotton applicator, and the mouse was observed for blinking.

Tail Flexion: The mouse’s tail was pinched 1 time with forceps, and the mouse was observed for tail flexion.

Tail Flexion: The mouse’s tail was pinched 1 time with forceps, and the mouse was observed for tail flexion.

Escape Response: The mouse’s tail was grasped, and the mouse was observed for movement away from the grasp.

Escape Response: The mouse’s tail was grasped, and the mouse was observed for movement away from the grasp.

Righting Reflex: The mouse was placed on its back and observed for its ability to right itself.

Ataxia: The mouse was placed on a 1 cm × 1 cm wire mesh, positioned 5 cm above the surface. The mouse was allowed to move freely and observed for 20 seconds for foot falls through the wire mesh.

Ataxia: The mouse was placed on a 1 cm × 1 cm wire mesh, positioned 5 cm above the surface. The mouse was allowed to move freely and observed for 20 seconds for foot falls through the wire mesh.

A neurological score was determined by assigning a semi-quantitative evaluation to each of the above-mentioned parameters. 0 (no reflex evident/significant ataxia), 1 (weak or delayed reflex/minor ataxia), or 2 (intact reflex/no ataxia). The summation of these six reflexes and ataxia provided a neurological score between 0 and 12.

Brain water content

The mouse brain was removed and immediately weighed (i.e. wet weight) at 24 h after APAP treatment. The brain was then dried for 24 h in an oven at 85 °C and then reweighed (i.e. dry weight). The brain water content was then calculated as a percentage using the following equation: % brain water content = (wet weight − dry weight)/wet weight × 100.

Open field maze

Individual mice were placed in an open field box at 24 h after APAP, and their activity was recorded for 10 min. Various activity measures, including ambulatory time, distance traveled, velocity, and resting time, were quantified using ToxTrac (Rodriguez et al. 2018).

Laser speckle contrast imaging

At 24 h after APAP, mice were anesthetized with 3.5% isoflurane in oxygen. The scalp was removed, and the skull was cleared of connective tissue. A thin coat of clear, shiny nail polish was followed by a thin coat of matte nail polish to reduce glare. After lowering the isoflurane to 1.5% for three minutes, the cerebral perfusion was measured over a minute using a Perimed PeriCam PSI Zoom System and PIMSoft software (Perimed Inc, Las Vegas, NV). Regions of interest (ROI) were drawn after locating the midpoint of the sagittal suture between bregma and sigma as described (Diaz-Otero et al. 2017). The mean perfusion of each ROI was recorded. The average between left and right frontal, parietal, and occipital lobe perfusion was calculated. Total cerebral perfusion was defined as the average of frontal, parietal, occipital, and pial anastomotic regions combined.

Blood ammonia concentration

Ammonia levels in whole blood were measured immediately after collection using the Pocketchem BA Blood Ammonia Analyzer (Woodley Laboratory Diagnostics, Horwich, UK). Briefly, a drop of whole blood was added to the Pocketchem Analyzer which calculated the concentration of ammonia. Blood was collected at 24 h after APAP.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as the mean ± SEM. Data were analyzed using one-way or two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) as appropriate. Data expressed as a percentage were transformed by arcsine square root prior to analysis. Comparisons among group means were made using the Student-Newman-Keuls test. Data from the neurological scoring system was analyzed by using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. The criterion for significance was p < 0.05 for all studies.

Results

Liver injury in mice treated with different doses of APAP

Mice were treated with vehicle, 300 mg/kg, or 500 mg/kg APAP. For our studies, we chose to use 500 mg/kg APAP, because this resulted in no deaths within the time frame of the study. Evaluation of the hepatotoxic response revealed that the activity of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and the area of necrosis were not different 24 h after treatment of mice with 300 mg/kg or 500 mg/kg APAP (Figure 1).

Behavioral assessments in mice treated with APAP

The International Society of Hepatic Encephalopathy and Nitrogen Metabolism (ISHEN) working group recently published guidelines for the assessment of hepatic encephalopathy in mice (DeMorrow et al. 2021). This report proposed measuring 6 separate reflexes, including the pinna reflex, corneal reflex, tail flexion, escape response, righting reflex, and ataxia. These were assessed using a scoring system detailed in the Materials and Methods. The neurological score was lower in mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP when compared to either vehicle or 300 mg/kg APAP-treated mice. By contrast, this score was not different between mice treated with vehicle or 300 mg/kg APAP (Table 1).

Locomotor activity in mice treated with APAP

Next, we evaluated locomotor activity using the open field test. Compared to mice treated with vehicle, ambulatory time, distance traveled, velocity, and resting time were all impacted in mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP (Table 2). These measurements, however, were not different from those of mice treated with 300 mg/kg APAP (Table 2).

Cerebral blood flow and edema in mice treated with APAP

Studies in patients with ALF have demonstrated reduced cerebral blood flow (Bjerring et al. 2018; Milewski et al. 2021). In the present study, we evaluated this by laser speckle imaging. As shown in Figure 2, frontal and parietal blood flow were reduced in mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP when compared to either vehicle-treated or 300 mg/kg APAP-treated mice.

In ALF patients that are critically ill, cerebral edema frequently develops (Butterworth 2015). In mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP, there was an increase in brain water content compared to either vehicle-treated or300 mg/kg APAP-treated mice (Figure 2).

Blood ammonia concentrations in mice treated with APAP

An important function of the liver is to remove excess ammonia from the blood; therefore, severe hepatic dysfunction is expected to cause hyperammonemia. Hyperammonemia is strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of HE, yet whether ammonia levels alone account for neurological decompensation in ALF remains debated (Butterworth 2015; Jayakumar and Norenberg 2018). In the present study, whole-blood ammonia concentrations were increased in APAP-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated mice (Figure 3). Notably, however, ammonia concentrations were elevated to a similar extent in mice treated with 300 mg/kg and 500 mg/kg APAP (Figure 3), demonstrating that hyperammonemia alone is insufficient to account for the dose-dependent neurovascular and neurological abnormalities, implicating additional circulating mediators that cooperate with ammonia to drive HE.

Collectively, these results establish an incremental APAP dosing model in which similar liver injury and hyperammonemia diverge from cerebral hypoperfusion, edema, and HE-like behavioral deficits—an experimental separation that is essential for identifying the mechanistic ‘second hit’ mediators that determine neurological decompensation in ALF.

Discussion

Bhushan and colleagues previously showed that peak hepatic necrosis is similar between mice treated with either 300 mg/kg or 600 mg/kg APAP (Bhushan et al. 2014). Despite this, these two groups of mice had profoundly different outcomes. In mice treated with 300 mg/kg APAP, liver repair was triggered, leading to a full recovery. By contrast, in mice treated with 600 mg/kg APAP, liver repair failed, resulting in a high mortality rate (Bhushan et al. 2014). Notably, these observations mirror a central clinical problem: conventional measures of liver injury do not reliably predict the transition from ALI to ALF or patient outcome after APAP overdose. Because HE is a defining feature of ALF (Lee 2012; Bernal and Wendon 2013), we asked whether a similar dosing strategy could segregate mice, with comparable liver injury, into HE-negative (AALI) and HE-positive (AALF) groups. By selecting 500 mg/kg APAP to reduce early mortality while maintaining severe injury, we identified conditions in which ALT activity and necrotic area were indistinguishable between groups, yet neurological, neurovascular, and cerebral edema endpoints diverged, enabling mechanistic separation of hepatocellular necrosis from HE-related pathophysiology.

To assess HE, we applied an ISHEN-recommended neurological scoring system that captures early deficits in brainstem reflexes and motor coordination (DeMorrow et al. 2021). Mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP exhibited a marked reduction in the composite neurological score relative to vehicle or 300 mg/kg APAP. The righting reflex—a measure of arousal—was largely preserved at 24 h, indicating that these animals are in an early stage of HE rather than deep coma (DeMorrow et al. 2021). In contrast to the ISHEN neurological scoring system, the open-field test was unable to distinguish between mice treated with 300 mg/kg or 500 mg/kg APAP. Most likely, this is because both groups of mice manifest sickness behavior at this time point resulting in a reduction in overall activity. Collectively, these findings emphasize that reflex-based neurological scoring provides specificity for HE-like impairment beyond generalized hypoactivity often observed in acute toxic injury models.

A major determinant of mortality in severe ALF is cerebral edema, which can elevate intracranial pressure and precipitate herniation (Butterworth 2015). In mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP, brain water content was increased, consistent with cerebral edema. This did not occur, however, in mice treated with 300 mg/kg. Although the absolute change in water content in the brains of mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP appeared modest, they are comparable to those reported in other experimental settings associated with clinically meaningful brain swelling (Dzialowski et al. 2004).

HE and cerebral edema are also linked to disturbances in cerebral perfusion and neurovascular coupling (Durham et al. 1995; Strauss et al. 2001; Bjerring et al. 2018). In patients with ALF, a reduction in cerebral blood flow has been reported (Durham et al. 1995; Strauss et al. 2001; Bjerring et al. 2018). In our studies, laser speckle imaging revealed region-selective reductions in frontal and parietal blood flow in mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP that were not present after 300 mg/kg APAP. Although the mechanism by which this occurs after APAP overdose remains unclear, the experimental conditions defined here provide a tractable system to test whether endothelial dysfunction, vasoactive mediator imbalance, microthrombotic events, or disrupted neurovascular coupling mechanistically drive hypoperfusion and edema in HE. Moreover, although not conducted here, a more detailed assessment of brain histopathology could potentially provide insight into the mechanism.

A major function of the liver is to clear ammonia from the systemic circulation. Consequently, severe liver injury is associated with an increase in blood ammonia concentrations, which has been suggested to be a causative factor for the development of hepatic encephalopathy (Butterworth 2015; Jayakumar and Norenberg 2018). In support of this, experimentally induced increases in blood ammonia concentrations, either through genetic manipulations in mice or by supplementing ammonia, are associated with clinical signs of hepatic encephalopathy (Azorín et al. 1989; Qvartskhava et al. 2015). Several clinical studies have also shown an association between elevated blood ammonia levels and evidence of hepatic encephalopathy (Clemmesen et al. 1999; Butterworth 2002; Ong et al. 2003; Anand and Acharya 2024). Whether ammonia alone is sufficient to cause hepatic encephalopathy in ALF patients, however, remains a matter of debate (Jayakumar and Norenberg 2018). While studies demonstrate an association between blood ammonia concentrations and symptoms of hepatic encephalopathy, there are clear instances where ALF patients with elevated ammonia concentrations show no evidence of hepatic encephalopathy (Jayakumar and Norenberg 2018). Consistent with these clinical findings, our data show that whole-blood ammonia concentrations were similarly elevated in HE-negative (300 mg/kg) and HE-positive (500 mg/kg) mice at 24 h. These findings support the concept that ammonia is necessary but not sufficient for HE and that additional signals determine whether hyperammonemia triggers neurovascular dysfunction and brain edema (Jayakumar and Norenberg 2018). A working model is that severe injury after APAP overdose establishes a hyperammonemic milieu, while the AALF condition uniquely amplifies secondary mediators—such as inflammatory cytokines and/or danger-associated molecular patterns, coagulation and endothelial perturbations, or metabolic derangements—that converge on the neurovascular unit to reduce cerebral perfusion and promote edema. Defining these cooperating pathways is essential for developing predictive biomarkers and mechanism-based interventions that prevent progression from drug-induced liver injury to ALF.

Conclusion

Collectively, these studies establish an experimental system in which liver injury severity can be matched while the defining neurological and neurovascular features of ALF selectively emerge. Mice treated with 500 mg/kg APAP exhibit early HE-like neurological impairment, regional cerebral hypoperfusion, and increased brain water content, whereas mice treated with 300 mg/kg APAP do not, despite comparable ALT, necrotic area, and similarly elevated blood ammonia. Thus, circulating ammonia concentration alone does not discriminate HE-positive from HE-negative states in this setting, underscoring the need to identify additional mediators that cooperate with hyperammonemia to drive brain dysfunction. This model provides an essential experimental platform to uncover the hepatic and systemic determinants that govern the transition from acute drug-induced liver injury to ALF and to define mechanistically grounded biomarkers and therapeutic targets for preventing HE and related extrahepatic complications.
