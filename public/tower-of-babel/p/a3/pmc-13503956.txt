Lengthened autumn growing season contributes substantially to Earth’s greening
Authors: Jiaqi Tian, Xiangzhong Luo, Ruiying Zhao, Chi Chen, Zheng Fu, Chaoyang Wu, Yongguang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Terrestrial ecosystems have become greener over the years through two different processes: increasing maximum greenness or extending growing season. The integrated signal of these two processes has resulted in a persistent increase in Earth’s greenness, which serves as key evidence of contemporary global change. However, it remains unclear which process has predominantly driven Earth’s greening, as the mechanisms underlying increased maximum greenness and growing season extension are distinct. Here, we decompose long-term satellite greenness observations and quantify the respective contributions of maximum greenness and growing season length to Earth’s greening. We find that greening in non-forest areas is dominated by increased maximum greenness induced by elevated carbon dioxide and land use change, while greening in extra-tropical forests is primarily driven by growing season extension, particularly a lengthened autumn growing season associated with climate change and elevated carbon dioxide. This substantial contribution of autumn phenology to global greening is not captured by current dynamic global vegetation models, which typically attribute greening to increased maximum greenness. Our study provides a critical process-based examination of global greenness change and highlights the key role of autumn phenology in driving Earth’s greening.

Introduction

Satellite records show that global vegetation has experienced widespread greening over the past 40 years1,2, posing great impacts on land-atmosphere carbon dioxide (CO2) exchange3 and the land surface energy balance4,5. Earth’s greening is typically characterized by an increase in the integrated signal of vegetation greenness during the growing season1,6–8, which can be driven by either an increase in maximum greenness or an extension of growing season2. Although changes in maximum greenness (i.e., leaf area)1,6 and growing season length (i.e., phenology)2,9 have been well-documented separately, their respective contributions to Earth’s greening remain unclear. Importantly, the mechanisms driving changes in leaf area and phenology differ considerably, suggesting substantial ambiguity in our understanding of long-term vegetation dynamics if Earth’s greening is treated as a single signal1,6,10,11.

Previous studies based on satellite observations6,12 and Earth system models1 have consistently reported an increase in leaf area (i.e., increased maximum greenness) across most vegetated regions in recent decades, particularly in the tropics and mid- to high-latitudes. Attribution analyses based on dynamic global vegetation models (DGVMs) suggest that elevated CO2 (eCO2) accounts for around 70% of the observed global increase in leaf area1,2. This conclusion is supported by statistical modelling13 and Free-Air CO2 Enrichment (FACE) experiments14. In addition, climate factors (e.g., warming and changes in precipitation) contribute to greening in northern high latitudes and the Tibetan Plateau, while land cover change and nitrogen deposition (ND) play relatively minor roles1. Meanwhile, anthropogenic land use management has become a dominant driver of regional leaf area increase since the 21st century6, particularly shaping the greening trends in China and India.

Alongside the increase in leaf area, vegetation across much of the Northern Hemisphere9,15,16 and dry tropical regions17 has exhibited a clear extension of growing season length since the early 1980s. This shift is primarily driven by an earlier start and/or later end of growing season (SGS and/or EGS). In most of the Northern Hemisphere, the extension of growing season length is largely attributed to warming16,18, while in arid/semi-arid and tropical regions, it is more closely linked to increased precipitation16,18 and/or prolonged wet seasons17. While previous studies have extensively documented changes in both leaf area and phenology across global vegetation, their respective contributions to observed greening patterns remain unclear.

Motivated by the knowledge gap regarding the respective contributions of increased maximum greenness and extended growing season to Earth’s greening, we aim to address the following questions: (i) what are the respective contributions of increased maximum greenness and extended growing season to global greening? and (ii) what are the potential drivers underlying their changes? To this end, we employ an advanced perturbation analysis to decompose the effects of maximum greenness, growing season length, as well as spring and autumn phenological rates on Earth’s greening signal, using long-term records from remote sensing and a network of ground-based PhenoCam sites, and the estimates from an ensemble of DGVMs (see “Methods”). We further apply a machine learning method to identify key factors driving increased maximum greenness and extended growing season. Our study provides a critical and comprehensive assessment of various vegetation components on greening and advances our understanding of long-term global vegetation dynamics by elucidating their distinct roles in Earth’s greening.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically decomposed Earth’s greening signal into various vegetation growth components and quantified their respective contributions (Fig. 1i). Our results reveal that increased maximum greenness (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{MAX}}}}$$\end{document}MAX) dominated greening in non-forest areas, while extended autumn growing season length (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{AGL}}}}$$\end{document}AGL) dominated greening in extra-tropical forest areas (Fig. 2a, b). The increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{MAX}}}}$$\end{document}MAX was primarily induced by eCO2 and by LUCC in some regions, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{AGL}}}}$$\end{document}AGL lengthening resulted from the effects of CC and eCO2 (Fig. 3a). In comparison, we found that state-of-the-art DGVMs attribute greening primarily to eCO2- and CC-driven increase in MAX, with a small contribution from phenological changes (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8).

We found that greening in non-forest areas (e.g., grasslands, croplands, and shrublands) is predominantly driven by increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{MAX}}}}$$\end{document}MAX (Fig. 2a, b), consistent with earlier evidence from multiple remote sensing-based analysis11,21,23. eCO2 was identified as the key driver of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{MAX}}}}$$\end{document}MAX enhancement (Fig. 2c, d), as it stimulates vegetation photosynthesis by accelerating carboxylation rates24,25, thereby promoting leaf area development (e.g., more green leaves) and increased maximum greenness26. The CO2 fertilization effect is particularly pronounced for vegetation growth in low-LAI regions26 and non-forest ecosystems27–29. For non-forests in arid and semi-arid regions, eCO2 enhances greening through increasing plant water-use efficiency20, mitigating water limitations on vegetation growth21,30. For the greening of non-forests in high-latitude regions, there exists a debate. While previous studies based on remote sensing19 and models21 suggest a strong role of eCO2 in enhancing greening in the region, temperature may also play an important role31.

We also note that several previous studies32,33 have highlighted the role of agricultural intensification and management (e.g., fertilization, irrigation, and improved cultivars) in modulating crop productivity and seasonal land-atmosphere CO2 exchange at a large scale. This implies that greening can be induced by the changes in cropland quality through intensification and management rather than cropland quantity (e.g., LUCC) (Fig. 2c). However, due to the lack of reliable and long-term datasets tracking these specific agricultural management practices33, it remains challenging at this time to consider them in our spatially explicit analysis on greening. Nevertheless, we acknowledge this potential mechanism for cropland greening and recommend further examination of the issue, should reliable and long-term cropland management data on a global scale become available.

In contrast to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{MAX}}}}$$\end{document}MAX-dominated greening in non-forest areas, we found that greening in seasonal forests was primarily driven by the extension of autumn growing season (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{AGL}}}}$$\end{document}AGL) (Fig. 2a, b). The lengthening of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{AGL}}}}$$\end{document}AGL was attributed to both an advanced peak of growing season (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{PGS}}}}$$\end{document}PGS) and a delayed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{EGS}}}}$$\end{document}EGS (Supplementary Fig. 9). Although advanced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{PGS}}}}$$\end{document}PGS34,35 and delayed EGS9,18,36 have been widely documented across the Northern Hemisphere, their underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Specifically, advanced PGS is generally associated with earlier spring phenology18,34 and enhanced springtime carbon assimilation35, whereas delayed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{EGS}}}}$$\end{document}EGS is linked to various factors in autumn (e.g., enhanced photosynthesis37, slower chlorophyll degradation38, and reduced autumn frost risk39), as well as carryover effects of spring phenology18,34. These studies align with our finding that CC and eCO2 play a key role in lengthening \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{AGL}}}}$$\end{document}AGL (Fig. 3a), as they modulate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{PGS}}}}$$\end{document}PGS and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{EGS}}}}$$\end{document}EGS via the aforementioned mechanisms. In particular, for the impact of eCO2 on advancing PGS and delaying EGS (Supplementary Fig. 9), and subsequently increasing AGL, we suspect that this showcases an understudied pathway where photosynthesis interacts with phenology. This line of thought has been examined in several recent studies40,41, however, the direction in which photosynthesis impacts phenology still remains a large knowledge gap—interestingly, some FACE experiments indeed show delayed EGS under eCO242.

Previous studies on the examination and attribution of greening tend to rely on DGVMs1,43,44. However, we found a major discrepancy: remote sensing-based analysis identified a clear contribution of autumn phenology (i.e., AGL) to greening, while DGVMs did not (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). This discrepancy suggests a potential aspect in DGVMs for future examination. Specifically, the main principles used to model autumn phenology traditionally involve cold degree days and photoperiod45–47, while more recent advances incorporate spring-autumn phenology coordination18,48 and photosynthesis-phenology coordination41. The latter mechanisms might introduce further complexity into simulating autumn phenology, as spring phenology and photosynthesis are subject to precursory climate impacts. Although there is substantial evidence from remote sensing that autumn phenology in the Northern Hemisphere has been delayed18,49, DGVMs might not show a similar degree of delay, which could explain the relatively small contribution to the greening trend we identified.

We repeated the analysis using MODIS LAI (LAIRS). Interestingly, we observed that when we used LAIRS, the results showed that Earth’s greening is primarily driven by the increase in MAX, and AGL plays a less substantial role compared to the results derived from NDVIRS (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). This difference was caused by a clear mismatch between the phenological metrics derived from NDVIRS and LAIRS (Supplementary Fig. 12). Considering the importance of the accuracy of phenology in this study, we validated the phenology dates extracted from NDVIRS and LAIRS against ground observations from the PhenoCam network (Supplementary Fig. 12a). We noticed that NDVIRS provided more accurate phenology estimates than LAIRS. This is also consistent with common practice: since LAI retrievals are subject to the underlying assumptions of radiative transfer models50—where errors or fallback solutions can impact phenological signals, continuous vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI and enhanced vegetation index 2) are more commonly used to extract phenology51. These considerations led us to use NDVIRS in our main analysis.

Our study suggested that an increase in MAX contributes ~60% of the greening signal, while the changes in phenology, mainly AGL, contribute the remaining ~40% (Fig. 1g). This is consistent with previous decomposition studies on gross primary productivity (GPP), where the carbon uptake rate (e.g., MAX) plays a more important role than carbon uptake duration (e.g., phenology) in GPP variations and trends52–54. However, a key distinction is that for long-term GPP trends, the GPP increase from SGL contributed more than that from AGL53, while in our study, we found that an extended AGL contributed more to greening than the changes in SGL. We suspect that this difference may reflect a divergence between GPP and greenness. While greenness mostly reflects leaf quantity, biochemical processes and climate factors may exert an additional impact on the photosynthesis of these leaves.

In conclusion, we found that the lengthening of the autumn growing season is a key process of Earth’s greening over the past two decades, highlighting a critical yet poorly understood role of vegetation phenology in driving Earth’s greening. Our study establishes a unified framework for assessing vegetation greenness through the lens of different processes of vegetation growth and identifying key driving factors for greening. These insights are crucial for accurately explaining and predicting long-term vegetation dynamics under future CC.

Methods

NDVI preprocessing

Considering the effects of noise and ice/snow, we first resampled the daily NDVI data (MODIS MCD19A3CMG v061) at a spatial resolution of 0.05° from 2000 to 2020 into half-monthly intervals (i.e., biweekly) using the maximum value composite method55. Meanwhile, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis using weekly composites and found that two temporal composites can produce highly consistent phenology extraction results (Supplementary Fig. 13a–d). Next, we identified ground-freeze pixels using the Freeze/Thaw Earth System Data Record1,56 and replaced their values with the 5th percentile of all clear-sky NDVI values for each pixel36,57. To further minimize the effects of cloud contamination and abnormal outliers, we applied the iterative Savitzky-Golay filter with a smoothing window of 4 and a polynomial degree of 6 to reconstruct high-quality NDVI time series58. Last, we used a spline interpolation method to generate daily NDVI time series from smoothed NDVI composites57.

Defining growing season

For each vegetated pixel, annual growing season was determined using a phenology-based approach that can accurately capture the progression of visible plant greenness and development59,60. Specifically, we extracted the SGS and EGS using a well-established threshold method set at 15% (Fig. 1i), which has been validated against ground observations and shown high consistency with other phenology extraction algorithms17,61,62. Previous studies have also documented that the selection of thresholds solely affects the magnitude of phenological dates, but does not alter their temporal trends17,63,64. The growing season was defined as the period between SGS and EGS for each year (Fig. 1i). To account for phenological dates spanning calendar years, particularly in the Southern Hemisphere, we applied a 3-year moving window to delineate annual growing season17. Consequently, the growing seasons for the first and last years of the study period were not determined. As a test, we compared our phenological extraction results with an official phenology product (i.e., MODIS MCD12Q2) and found that they show strong consistency (Supplementary Fig. 13c–f). Following conventional protocols17,65, we excluded sparsely vegetated areas (i.e., annual NDVImean < 0.1) and pixels with weak seasonality (i.e., annual NDVImax < 1.2 times of annual NDVImin) (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, considering the complexity of vegetated pixels with multiple growing seasons within a single year, we further excluded such pixels (i.e., harmonic ratio > 1.0) using a harmonic analysis17,66,67, which accounts for 16% of global vegetated regions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Note that although some pixels were classified as evergreen forests in the land cover map (Supplementary Fig. 4a), we included them in the analysis because they still exhibited strong seasonality (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Earth’s greening

Positive trends in integrated greenness indicators (e.g., NDVI or LAI) during the growing season indicate greening, while negative trends signify browning1,2,7. We applied the Theil–Sen estimator to quantify the trends (i.e., slopes) of annual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{NDVI}}}}}_{{{{\rm{gs}}}}}$$\end{document}NDVIgs anomalies from 2001 to 201969, and used the non-parametric Mann–Kendall (MK) test to detect statistically significant monotonic trends, whether positive (greening) or negative (browning)1,6,67,70.

For each greening pixel, we first calculated the trend of each component using the MK test combined with Theil–Sen’s slope estimation. Identifying driving factors for dominant components

After identifying the dominant components for greening pixels, we further examined their main driving factors. Based on previous studies, we selected three key factors for analysis: eCO21,2,12, CC1,11,74, and LUCC20. Specifically, we used daily CO2 concentration data at a spatial resolution of 1.4° × 0.7° from Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) greenhouse gas reanalysis75. Considering the combined effects of multiple climate variables on greening, we selected seven key variables in the CC module: air temperature (T), maximum air temperature (Tmax), minimum air temperature (Tmin), precipitation (PRE), solar radiation (RAD), vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and wind speed (WSP). Daily climate data at a spatial resolution of 0.5° were obtained from the Climatic Research Unit (CRU) and Japanese reanalysis (JRA) v2.476. To quantify the intensity of LUCC, we utilized vegetation cover fractions from the MEaSUREs Vegetation Continuous Fields (VCF) Version 1 product (VCF5KYR). The VCF5KYR dataset provides global yearly fractional vegetation cover at 0.05° spatial resolution from 1982 to 201677, including three layers: tree cover (TC), short vegetation (SV), and bare ground (BG). For the 2017–2019 period, VCF data were held constant at 2016 levels to ensure temporal consistency with other datasets. In addition, we used plant-functional-type (PFT) maps from the European Space Agency (ESA) Climate Change Initiative (CCI)78 to recognize specific land cover types. To ensure consistency in spatial resolution, all data used in this study were resampled to 0.5° using the Pixel Aggregation method.

DGVMs estimates

Inspired by previous model-based studies on Earth’s greening1,80,81, we further identified the dominant components and driving factors for greening pixels using LAI estimates of nine TRENDY V12 DGVMs (i.e., CABLE-POP, CLASSIC, JULES, LPJ-GUESS, ORCHIDEE, SDGVM, VISIT, YIBs, and OCN) (Supplementary Table 2)82. These models provide a complete set of simulation scenarios (i.e., S0, S1, S2, and S3), output LAI, and demonstrate reasonable vegetation distribution based on our previous assessment83. Specifically, these models provide monthly LAI estimates by integrating gridded climate data and dynamic atmospheric CO2 concentration under different experimental scenarios, including varying CO2 only (S1), varying CO2 and climate (S2), and varying CO2, climate and land cover change (S3).
