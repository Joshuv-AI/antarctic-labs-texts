Association between simultaneous exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages and cardiovascular health of Brazilian adolescents
Authors: Priscila Bárbara Zanini Rosa, Felipe Vogt Cureau, Beatriz D. Schaan, Juliana Sena de Souza, Michele Drehmer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSocioeconomic inequities affect cardiovascular outcomes in adults, but their cumulative effect in adolescents remains unclear. The aim of this study is to evaluate the association between simultaneous exposure to different socioeconomic disadvantages and cardiovascular health (CVH) in Brazilian adolescents.MethodsThe sample consisted of 34,831 adolescents aged 12 to 17 years, participants of the Brazilian Cardiovascular Risk in Adolescents Study (ERICA), a national, school-based, cross-sectional, multicenter study. CVH was assessed using the Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) score, which ranges from 0 to 100 points and is composed of eight metrics grouped into two domains: health behaviors (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep duration) and health factors (body mass index, non-HDL cholesterol, blood glucose, and blood pressure). Simultaneous exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages was assessed using five variables: parental education, family structure, possession of goods, type of school, and skin color. The total number of exposures ranged from 0 to 5. Linear regression models adjusted for sex and age were used to evaluate the association between simultaneous exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages and CVH.ResultsThe simultaneous exposure to at least two socioeconomic disadvantages was 80.7 %, while the average LE8 score was 75.9 (95 % CI 75.5–76.4). The higher the number of simultaneous exposures to the investigated socioeconomic disadvantages, the worse the CVH of Brazilian adolescents. Those exposed to all the investigated disadvantages simultaneously had a reduction of 4.5 points in the LE8. Physical activity and smoking were the LE8 components most impacted by socioeconomic disadvantages. Belonging to a fragmented family structure appeared to have the greatest impact on CVH.ConclusionSimultaneous exposure to multiple socioeconomic disadvantages was associated with poorer CVH in Brazilian adolescents.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of death worldwide [1]. Most risk factors for the development of CVD are largely modifiable and include poor diet, physical inactivity, nicotine use, and poor sleep quality [2], all of which can be influenced by social and economic factors [3]. The adoption of these risky behaviors can begin in adolescence and often continues into adulthood, highlighting a target group for early assessment and intervention [4].

As a means of early cardiovascular health (CVH) assessment, the American Heart Association (AHA) has used eight essential metrics since 2022, gathered in the Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) [5]. This metric includes four behavioral factors (diet quality, physical activity, nicotine use, and sleep) and four health factors (body mass index, blood glucose, non-HDL cholesterol, and blood pressure). In adolescents, a higher LE8 score is associated with a greater lifespan free of CVD [6] and a lower likelihood of multimorbidity due to chronic non-communicable diseases [7].

Although CVH assessment is primarily measured by individual biological and behavioral risk factors, new evidence has sought to understand the context in which these factors occur [[8], [9], [10]]. In this regard, it has been observed that socioeconomic disparities can directly influence cardiovascular outcome distribution, acting as a social risk factor [11]. Social risk is described as a set of socioeconomic factors associated with social disadvantages that lead to disparities in health outcomes, such as belonging to racial minorities, low income, low educational attainment, and unstable family structures [12].

Among adults, evidence indicates that racial and socioeconomic inequities are associated with an increased risk of CVD, as well as higher incidence and mortality rates [11,13,14]. In adolescents, these relationships remain unclear, although a higher LE8 score has been associated with being white, having a higher maternal education level, and a higher family income [15]. Additionally, most studies analyze social determinants as isolated exposures, overlooking their cumulative effect, that is, simultaneous exposure to multiple socioeconomic disadvantages [16]. Studies that assessed cumulative social risk in adults have shown that exposure to an increasing number of social risk factors is associated with a lower likelihood of achieving healthy levels of cardiovascular risk factors [17], as well as an additive effect on subclinical outcomes, clinical outcomes, and CVD mortality [18].

The influence of cumulative exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages on CVH remains underexplored in adolescents. A prospective cohort study with Finnish adolescents reported that a higher number of favorable psychosocial exposures during youth, such as socioeconomic factors, emotional factors, parents' health behaviors, and stressful events, was associated with a higher ideal cardiovascular health index in adulthood [19]. In the HELENA study [20], adolescents whose mothers had low educational attainment or who were exposed to more than three socioeconomic disadvantages had a higher risk of metabolic syndrome, regardless of their lifestyle. In this context, there is a gap in studies, especially from low- and middle-income countries, that have investigated the effects of simultaneous exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages on adolescent CVH. Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the association between simultaneous exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages and CVH in Brazilian adolescents, measured by the LE8.

Methods

Study design and participants

The participants of this study are part of the Brazilian Cardiovascular Risk Study in Adolescents (ERICA). ERICA is a nationwide, multicenter, school-based cross-sectional study aimed at examining the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors in Brazilian adolescents aged 12 to 17 years.

In summary, ERICA’s sample size was estimated assuming a 4 % prevalence of metabolic syndrome among adolescents, with a maximum error of 0.9 % and 95 % confidence. This required 1821 students under simple random sampling. To account for clustering by school, shift, grade, and class, a design effect of 2.97 was applied. An additional 15 % was added for potential nonresponse and losses. Thus, the final target was 6219 adolescents in each of the 12 strata (6 ages × 2 sexes), totaling at least 74,628 participants. However, only students from the morning shift were eligible for blood sampling. Sampling weights were calculated to ensure representativeness of Brazilian school adolescents in the eligible cities, based on the 2011 Brazilian Educational Census.

The sample was geographically stratified into 32 strata, composed of the 27 state capitals and five sets of municipalities with >100,000 inhabitants from each of the five macro-regions of the country. Two stages of selection were then applied: schools and classes. In the first stage, schools were selected with probability proportional to the number of students in eligible grades, and inversely proportional to the distance between the school’s municipality and the state capital. In total, 1247 schools from 124 eligible municipalities participated in the study. In the second stage, three classes with different grade and shift combinations were selected with equal probabilities. All students from the selected classes were invited to participate in the study. Adolescents outside the eligible age range, pregnant, or with any physical disability were excluded from the data analysis. The eligibility flowchart is shown in Supplementary Figure 1, and more detailed descriptions of the research protocol and ERICA’s sample design can be found in previous publications [21,22].

Data were collected between February 2013 and November 2014. Adolescents were selected from those attending the last three years of elementary school (7th, 8th, or 9th grade) or any of the three years of high school in public or private schools, located in urban or rural areas of Brazilian municipalities with populations of over 100,000 inhabitants.

Adolescents completed a self-administered structured questionnaire, entered into an electronic data collector (personal digital assistant – PDA), which included questions on sociodemographic characteristics, physical activity, sleep, and smoking; food assessment, anthropometry, blood pressure measurement, and blood sample collection were conducted separately by trained researchers. Due to the fasting requirement for serum measurements, only students from morning shift classes were invited for this procedure. Thus, the sample for this study consisted of adolescents who completed all data collection stages and had complete information, totaling 34,831 adolescents.

ERICA was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Studies in Collective Health at the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (Process No 45/2008) and by all other study centers. All participants signed an assent form, and informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians due to blood collection.

Cardiovascular health

Cardiovascular health was assessed using the LE8 score, which consists of eight metrics grouped into two domains: health behaviors (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep) and health factors (body mass index, non-HDL cholesterol, blood glucose, and blood pressure). It uses an ordinal scoring system for each metric, resulting in a score ranging from 0 to 100 points, where higher scores indicate better CVH.

The 24-hour dietary recall was used to assess diet, which was administered using a software developed exclusively for the study [23]. The quantities of each food item consumed were determined and converted into grams based on the Brazilian food composition table [24]. For scoring, the DASH diet score was used, which considers the following food groups: [1] fruits, [2] vegetables, [3] legumes and nuts, [4] whole grains, [5] low-fat dairy, [6] total sodium, [7] red and processed meats, and [8] sugary beverages. Consumption of each group was divided into quintiles, with quintile 1 representing the lowest consumption and quintile 5 representing the highest consumption. For groups 1 to 5 (healthier foods), 1 point was assigned for the lowest consumption, increasing sequentially up to the highest consumption, which received 5 points. For groups 6 to 8 (less healthy foods), the scoring was reversed. Thus, a higher score represents a diet closer to the ideal. At the end, the scores were summed and divided into percentiles. Participants with scores between percentiles 1 and 24 were assigned 0 points; those between percentiles 25 and 49 received 25 points; between 50 and 74, 50 points; between percentiles 75 and 94, 80 points; and those with scores equal to or greater than 95 received 100 points [4].

Physical activity time was measured using the Self-Administered Physical Activity Checklist [25], adapted and validated for Brazilian adolescents [26]. The product of time and frequency for each activity listed on the instrument was calculated, and the total time was summed, excluding light-intensity activities. Based on this, the following scores were assigned according to the weekly physical activity duration range: 0 min: 0 points; 1–119 min: 20 points; 120–239 min: 40 points; 240–299 min: 60 points; 300–359 min: 80 points; 360–419 min: 90 points; ≥420 min: 100 points [4].

Nicotine exposure was assessed by self-reported use of at least one cigarette in the past 30 days and the presence of active smokers in the household. Participants who had never smoked were assigned 100 points; those who had smoked, but >30 days ago received 50 points; and current smokers received 0 points. A deduction of 20 points (unless the score was already 0) was applied to those living with an active smoker [4].

Sleep duration was obtained by the difference between usual bedtimes and wake times on a typical weekday and weekend, excluding extreme values (≤4 or ≥14 h) [27]. The average total weekly sleep was calculated using the formula: [(weekday sleep duration x 5 + weekend sleep duration x 2) / 7]. AHA-recommended sleep ranges for adolescents were used. For 12-year-olds, the ideal range was 9 to 12 h daily, and for those aged 13 or older, it was 8 to 10 h daily [28]. Participants received 0 points if they were 3 or more hours below the ideal range for their age; 20 points if they were between 2 and <3 h below; 40 points if they were between 1 and <2 h below, or equal to or >1 hour above the ideal; 70 points if they were <1 hour below the ideal range; 90 points if they were <1 hour above the ideal; and 100 points if they reached the ideal range for their age [4].

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by height (m) squared, with weight measured using an electronic scale and height using a portable stadiometer. Scores were assigned based on BMI percentiles for sex and age: P5 to P85 - 100 points; ≥P85 to <P95 - 70 points; ≥P95 to <120 % of P95 - 30 points; ≥120 % of P95 to <140 % of P95 - 15 points; ≥140 % of P95 - 0 points [4].

Blood glucose and non-HDL cholesterol were assessed by blood samples collected following standardized protocols with a 12-hour fasting period. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was measured to assess glycemic metabolism. HbA1c values <5.7 % received 100 points; 5.7–6.4 % received 60 points; 6.5–7.0 % received 40 points; 8.0–8.9 % received 20 points; 9.0–9.9 % received 10 points; and ≥10 % received 0 points. Non-HDL cholesterol was determined by subtracting HDL cholesterol from total cholesterol. Values below 100 mg/dL received 100 points; 100–119 mg/dL received 60 points; 120–144 mg/dL received 40 points; 145–189 mg/dL received 20 points; and ≥190 mg/dL received 0 points [4].

Blood pressure (BP) was measured using a validated automatic oscillometric device for adolescents (Omron 705-IT) [29]. After five minutes of rest, three consecutive BP measurements were taken, with a three-minute interval between them. The first measurement was discarded due to potential overestimation, and the average of the last two measurements was used. The BP score was assigned as follows: 100 points for ideal BP, i.e., <120/80 mmHg; 75 points for elevated BP (120–129/80 mmHg); 50 points for stage 1 hypertension (130/80–139/89 mmHg); 25 points for stage 2 hypertension (140/90–160/100 mmHg); 0 points for stage 3 hypertension (≥160/100 mmHg); and a deduction of 20 points for those receiving antihypertensive treatment [4].

For each indicator where the adolescent was classified as "exposed," 1 point was assigned, and 0 points were assigned for non-exposed individuals. The cumulative exposure score was calculated as the sum of the number of exposures, ranging from 0 (absence of disadvantages) to 5 (presence of all investigated disadvantages). Exposure to all possible combinations of the investigated socioeconomic indicators was also explored.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Stata, version 15.0, using the survey (svy) module, which accounts for sample weights and study design effects. For descriptive analyses, proportions were used for categorical variables and means for continuous variables, along with their respective 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CI). The Student’s t-test was used to evaluate differences in LE8 scores according to socioeconomic factors. To evaluate the association between simultaneous exposure to different socioeconomic disadvantages and CVH, as well as their combinations, multiple linear regression was used, adjusting for sex (female and male) and age (12–14 years and 15–17 years).

Data imputation was performed for parental education due to a high missing data percentage (21.2 %). The K-nearest neighbor classification (KNN) method was used for parental education variable imputation, a machine learning algorithm that identifies the "K" nearest neighbors based on training data and uses these neighbors as reference to predict test data outcomes [32]. We choose the KNN algorithm to impute parental education because machine learning–based methods like KNN better capture complex, non-linear relationships than traditional single imputation, which often underestimates sample specificity. Evidence from large cohort studies shows that KNN more effectively reduces the impact of missing data, enhancing accuracy and predictive power [33].

Equiplots [34] were used to present the average LE8 scores according to individual indicators and the cumulative score of socioeconomic disadvantage. This graph visually displays the distance between LE8 score means for the studied components, with greater distances indicating greater inequality in adolescent CVH. A p-value <0.05 in two-tailed tests was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

When exploring all 32 possible combinations of the socioeconomic disadvantages assessed (Supplementary Table 2), a negative association was observed in most of them, particularly for the following combinations: “low parental education and black/brown skin color” (LE8 = 67.9 points [95 % CI 60.5 - 75.2]; β = −10.0 [95 % CI −17.2; −2.7]); followed by “low parental education, black/brown skin color, lower possession of goods, and attending public school” (LE8 = 71.7 points [95 % CI 69.8–73.8]; β = −6.79 [95 % CI −9.9; −3.6]); and “low parental education, black/brown skin color, and lower possession of goods” (LE8 = 71.6 points [95 % CI 68.6–74.7]; β = −6.21 [95 % CI −10.2; −2.2]).

Discussion

This study showed that the greater the number of simultaneous exposures to the socioeconomic disadvantages investigated, the lower the CVH of Brazilian adolescents. Adolescents exposed to five disadvantages simultaneously had an average reduction of 4.5 points in their LE8 score. This relationship was independent of sex and age group. Furthermore, physical activity and smoking were the factors most impacted by socioeconomic disadvantages in the LE8. When examining the individual components of the socioeconomic disadvantage metric, living in a fragmented family structure seemed to have the greatest impact on CVH, followed by family possession of goods and low parental education.

The relationship between socioeconomic disadvantages and cardiovascular health in adults has been widely explored in recent decades. A meta-analysis [35] of 31 studies investigating the association between socioeconomic status (SES), measured by income, education, or housing conditions, found that populations with lower SES have a higher incidence and mortality from CVD. The study's results suggest that this association may, at least in part, be explained by the higher occurrence of smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, physical inactivity, obesity, diabetes, unhealthy diet, and depression among people with lower SES [35]. Other studies have shown that adults with lower education, income, and without employment have a higher risk of mortality from stroke [36], as well as higher overall mortality following acute coronary syndrome [37]. Furthermore, a reduction of 5 points in the LE8 score represents a risk of approximately 6 % for the development of CVD [38].

However, the association between individual and combined socioeconomic indicators and CVH in adolescents has been less explored. In our study, adolescents from families with lower possession of goods at home, a measure of purchasing power, parents with low education, and fragmented family structures were the individual metrics most strongly associated with CVH. In the literature, low family income is associated with a higher prevalence of obesity, pre-diabetes, and type 2 diabetes in adolescents [39], as well as lower CVH [40]. Similarly, adolescents with parents who have lower education have lower ideal CVH scores [41] and a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors, such as higher blood glucose and LDL cholesterol levels, larger waist circumference, higher BMI, and lower HDL cholesterol [42]. On the other hand, Jang et al [43] found that a 1.4-year increase in maternal education level is associated with a 1.7 % reduction in the risk of CVD when adolescents reach adulthood. Moreover, adolescents from single-parent families are at a higher risk for smoking [44,45], as well as more likely to be overweight when compared to those living with both parents [46]. The hypothesis is that adolescents who live with only one parent may experience poorer emotional support and less preventive monitoring regarding substance use, diet and other health-risk behaviors [[44], [45], [46]].

While individual exposures are important for understanding the relationship between socioeconomic disadvantages and CVH, it is essential to note that most of these disadvantages do not occur in isolation. In this study, 8 out of 10 adolescents reported simultaneous socioeconomic disadvantages. In adults, cumulative social risk, defined by simultaneous exposure to lower income and education indicators, belonging to minority ethnic groups, and being single, is associated with a worse risk profile and worst cardiovascular outcomes [47]. Previous studies [12,17] found that compared to individuals with no exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages, those with simultaneous exposures had a progressively higher likelihood of not having ideal CVH. Additionally, a one-unit increase in cumulative social risk score is associated with an 89.4 % higher risk of having a stroke, a 62.7 % higher risk of having a myocardial infarction, and a 63.6 % higher risk of having angina [48], as well as greater probabilities of high levels of systemic inflammation [47], the presence of subclinical atherosclerosis, and an increased risk of all-cause mortality [18]. In adolescents, the presence of multiple socioeconomic disadvantages is associated with a higher risk of metabolic syndrome [20,41] and type 2 diabetes [49]. Lee et al [50] observed that individuals who experienced socioeconomic disadvantages during adolescence had a higher cardiometabolic risk in young adulthood, partly mediated by unhealthy behaviors.

In this study, the scores for physical activity, smoking, and BMI, as well as for LE8, were lower as the number of exposures to socioeconomic disadvantages increased, in line with previous publications [41,42,44]. However, this relationship was inverse for sleep behavior. Although many studies have found opposite results, where SES is associated with worse sleep quality, adolescents with better financial conditions have greater access to mobile phones and streaming services, which can impair sleep duration [51]. A systematic review [52] found that increased screen time in school-aged adolescents was negatively associated with sleep health, which may be related to the replacement of sleep time with screen time, psychological stimulation from media content, or the effects of blue light emitted by devices that directly impact the circadian cycle, sleep physiology, and alertness [53]. Corroborating this, more recent studies have reported similar results, further clarifying that the relationship between technology use and sleep duration is bidirectional: greater time spent using technology is associated with shorter sleep duration and increased daytime sleepiness in adolescence, whereas shorter sleep duration, in turn, contributes to increased technology use over time [54,55].

Boys had higher average LE8 scores compared to girls, but the trend of LE8 reduction with increasing socioeconomic disadvantages was more pronounced in girls. Although, in general, it is more common for boys to have worse health behaviors and consequently a higher likelihood of overweight, obesity, and high blood pressure [56], when placed in a context of inequalities, especially related to the neighborhood, female adolescents may suffer greater impacts on their lifestyle. Studies in adults have shown that neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with obesity in women, but not in men [57]. Likewise, the ability to walk in the neighborhood is inversely related to BMI, overweight, and obesity risk only among women [58,59], which may be related to physical characteristics and neighborhood insecurity, limiting physical activity and access to places with healthier food options [60].

We observed that among the possible combinations of socioeconomic disadvantages, black/brown skin color, lower possession of goods, and low parental education stood out in their associations with LE8. This finding corroborates the longitudinal study by Pergn et al [15], who found that African American youth had a lower likelihood of having a high LE8 score during follow-up, as well as youth with mothers with less than a high school education and lower income, who consistently had a lower likelihood of high CVH throughout childhood and adolescence (ages 4, 8, 13, and 18). Similarly, Connolly et al [61] observed a direct association between more favorable social determinants and better CVH levels in white American adolescents, where favorable determinants included parental education above high school and economic stability.

Therefore, the impact of racial, economic, and educational disparities on the CVH of Brazilian youth is highlighted. Individuals with lower education and black or brown skin color tend to have lower financial conditions and live in neighborhoods more exposed to inadequate infrastructure, fewer quality health and education services, and violence, all of which may lead to an increase in the prevalence of risk behaviors among adolescents, such as sedentary lifestyle, unhealthy diet, smoking, and alcohol consumption, as well as cardiometabolic alterations such as insulin resistance, altered blood glucose and blood pressure, and metabolic syndrome [[62], [63], [64]].

An American study found that a large proportion of mortality from all causes and the CVD risk associated with low or moderate CVH, as measured by LE8, during adulthood could be prevented or even eliminated if everyone achieved a high CVH score in adolescence [65]. Moreover, there is evidence that individuals who manage to achieve or maintain a high CVH score into middle age have greater longevity, better health expectancy, quality of life, and cognitive function, as well as reduced cardiovascular disease rates [66]. Therefore, developing and implementing school-based preventive strategies aimed at improving these modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, such as ensuring and enhancing physical education classes, promoting healthy eating workshops, anti-substance abuse campaigns, and sleep quality guidance, seems to be a promising approach to health education.

Another important point is finding ways to mitigate the effects of exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages. To achieve this, it is essential to invest in affirmative actions aimed at combating the studied socioeconomic disadvantages, such as strengthening national adult education programs to improve the education level of children's and youth's parents, as well as increasing access to the job market to raise family income. Additionally, investing in the quality of public schools attended by adolescents and combating inequalities caused by skin color discrimination are essential actions.

This study has several strengths, such as pioneering the theme of socioeconomic disadvantages associated with CVH in adolescents in low and middle-income countries and applying the LE8 to a nationally representative adolescent sample, where all data were systematically and standardized collected, including behavioral and biochemical data necessary to calculate the LE8 for the adolescents. However, the study also has some limitations. While using a single 24-hour dietary recall is logistically feasible, it may not capture the variability of adolescents' habitual diets, limiting the accuracy of diet measurement. Similarly, self-reported measures of physical activity and other variables are prone to recall bias, which can result in misclassification and reduced estimation precision. However, we use validated instruments and provide standardized interviewer training to mitigate these limitations. Also, a limitation of our approach is the use of an equally weighted composite score, which assumes that all indicators contribute similarly to socioeconomic disadvantage. While this facilitates comparability and reproducibility, it may oversimplify the heterogeneous impact of individual factors. Furthermore, imputing missing data for parental education using the KNN method may have introduced bias in the estimate. While KNN imputation is a widely used non-parametric technique that leverages patterns in the available data to fill in missing values, it assumes that individuals with similar observed characteristics are also similar in the missing variable. This assumption may not hold if key predictors of parental education were unobserved or poorly measured. Therefore, the results involving this variable should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should aim to minimize missingness in key sociodemographic variables at the time of data collection. Finally, as this is a cross-sectional study, causality cannot be inferred; the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal.

Conclusion

Simultaneous exposure to multiple socioeconomic disadvantages is associated with poorer CVH in Brazilian adolescents. From a public health perspective, actions that strengthen equal access to education, employment, and income, as well as support for fragmented families, are important to prevent and modify other risk factors and the early development of cardiovascular diseases. More studies are needed to better understand the complex relationships between socioeconomic determinants and the mechanisms that lead to a decline in CVH from childhood and adolescence, especially among those with the greatest disadvantages.
