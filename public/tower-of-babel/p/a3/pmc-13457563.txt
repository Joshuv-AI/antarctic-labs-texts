Integrated hydrogeochemical assessment and groundwater quality mapping for irrigation suitability and health risk in Kandahar City, Afghanistan
Authors: Bashir Ahmad Karimi, Basir Ahmad Sulaiman, Ahmad Bilal Ahmadullah, Ahmad Shah Irshad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates groundwater quality in Kandahar City, Afghanistan, integrating hydrogeochemical characterization with water quality index (WQI), irrigation suitability assessment, and health risk zoning approaches. Groundwater is a critical drinking-water and irrigation resource in hard-rock aquifers, where quality is governed by complex rock-water interactions and can vary markedly across space. This study integrates hydrogeochemical characterization with a decision-oriented water-quality assessment to evaluate groundwater suitability for domestic consumption and agricultural use. Eighty groundwater samples were analyzed, and drinking-water quality was quantified using a Water Quality Index (WQI), complemented by irrigation hazard diagnostics including sodium percentage (Na%), sodium adsorption ratio (SAR), residual sodium carbonate (RSC), permeability index (PI), Kelly’s ratio (KR), and magnesium adsorption ratio (MAR). Spatial heterogeneity in groundwater quality was mapped using two interpolation approaches-Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) and Ordinary Kriging-to contrast localized versus regional patterns and to identify priority zones for intervention. WQI classification indicates that most samples fall within the Excellent (38.75%) and Good (32.5%) categories, whereas 28.75% of samples are Poor to Undrinkable (15% Poor, 6.25% Very Poor, and 7.5% Undrinkable), highlighting discrete hotspots of degraded quality. Irrigation suitability is generally favorable, with uniformly low SAR (0-6.24; mean 0.91) and KR < 1 for all samples, while localized constraints are indicated by PI and MAR, and by elevated RSC in a subset of samples. A WHO-guideline exceedance-based health risk screening indicates that key concerns are associated with elevated total hardness (48.33% exceedance) and elevated chloride and TDS (40% each), with additional exceedances observed for sulphate (38.33%). Collectively, the findings demonstrate that coupling hydrogeochemical interpretation with WQI-based classification, irrigation indices, and geostatistical mapping provides an actionable framework for prioritizing treatment in high-risk zones, guiding safe allocation of sources for drinking, and sustaining agricultural water use through targeted management measures.

Introduction and literature review

Groundwater is a vital resource for drinking water supply and agricultural production worldwide, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions where surface water availability is limited. Globally, groundwater systems are increasingly under pressure due to a combination of natural and anthropogenic factors, including water scarcity, groundwater quality deterioration, over-extraction, climate variability, and land-use change. The quality of groundwater is controlled by complex interactions between geological processes and human activities, making its assessment essential for sustainable water resource management and public health protection. Similar pressures associated with rapid population growth, urbanization, infrastructure development, and increasing water demand have been widely reported across many developing regions, where groundwater has become the dominant source for domestic, agricultural, and small-scale industrial use1–5. In such water-stressed environments, groundwater often serves as the most reliable and accessible freshwater resource. Globally, groundwater supports more than 39 million hectares of irrigated land and provides nearly half of the world’s drinking water, underscoring its critical role in sustaining food security and domestic water supply6,7.

In Kandahar City, groundwater supplies more than 80% of domestic water demand and sustains nearly all agricultural and small-scale industrial activities, reflecting extreme reliance on subsurface water resources under arid climatic conditions8. Prolonged and unregulated groundwater extraction has raised serious concerns regarding aquifer sustainability and water quality degradation, posing increasing risks to public health and long-term water security9. Recent investigations have reported measurable deterioration in groundwater quality across several parts of Kandahar City, including elevated salinity, excessive fluoride and nitrate concentrations, and the presence of trace metals, all of which represent significant threats to drinking water safety10. Groundwater quality in arid and semi-arid regions such as Kandahar is governed by a complex interaction of natural and anthropogenic processes14,16,17. Natural controls include lithological composition, mineral dissolution, silicate weathering, and geogenic element release, whereas human-induced influences encompass irrigation return flows, salinization, inadequate waste disposal, urban expansion, mining activities, land-use change, and sustained aquifer overexploitation. The combined effects of these processes create substantial challenges for maintaining safe and sustainable groundwater resources. Declining groundwater quality carries far-reaching implications, as access to safe drinking water is fundamental to public health protection, sustainable agriculture, and socioeconomic development11. Addressing groundwater contamination aligns directly with the objectives of Sustainable Development Goal 6, which emphasizes improved water quality through pollution reduction, controlled waste discharge, and mitigation of hazardous substances by 203012. Exposure to contaminated groundwater has been linked to adverse health outcomes, reduced agricultural productivity, and ecosystem degradation, collectively threatening community well-being and environmental sustainability13.

In crystalline and hard-rock aquifer systems, hydrogeochemical evolution is primarily controlled by water-rock interactions involving the dissolution of primary minerals, formation of secondary mineral phases, and cation-exchange reactions, which together shape groundwater chemistry14. In such environments, the weathering profile commonly extends to depths of approximately 100 m, producing a saprolitic zone that overlies a hydraulically conductive fractured bedrock system. Groundwater composition in these settings is strongly influenced by the alteration of silicate minerals such as feldspar, biotite, and amphibole, along with associated geochemical exchange processes15. In Kandahar’s alluvial and hard-rock aquifers, groundwater quality is controlled by mineral-water interactions combined with localized urban impacts, resulting in pronounced spatial variability of physicochemical parameters14. Hydrochemical facies in Kandahar City are commonly characterized by Ca2+-Mg2+-HCO3− compositions, which locally evolve toward mixed Ca2+-Mg2+-Cl−-SO42− types along groundwater flow paths16,18. This evolution reflects ongoing mineral dissolution, cation exchange, and increasing anthropogenic influence within the aquifer system16–18. Ion-exchange reactions, particularly involving the exchange of Ca2+ and Mg2+ with Na+ on clay mineral surfaces, exert a significant control on groundwater chemistry and contribute to observed spatial heterogeneity15,19. Understanding the combined influence of natural geochemical processes and human activities has therefore become essential for interpreting groundwater quality patterns in Kandahar City, where rapid urbanization continues to exert pressure on aquifer systems16,17. Regular evaluation of groundwater chemistry is crucial for identifying vulnerable zones, guiding extraction management, and strengthening waste-management strategies in Kandahar City20,21. While natural filtration systems, such as riverbank and sand-bed filtration, offer practical water-quality improvement options in resource-limited settings22, comprehensive water-quality assessment tools remain essential for identifying contamination hotspots and supporting informed management decisions. Among available assessment approaches, the Water Quality Index (WQI) provides a robust and integrative framework for evaluating groundwater suitability by synthesizing multiple physicochemical parameters into a single quantitative indicator. WQI facilitates spatial comparison of water quality conditions and supports public-health-oriented decision-making23,24. The Weighted Arithmetic Index method is particularly well established due to its simplicity, adaptability, and ability to reflect local hydrogeochemical conditions through parameter weighting25,26. This method has been widely applied for groundwater quality assessment and allows effective identification of spatial variability and contamination-prone zones26,27. Recent Afghanistan-focused studies similarly demonstrate that integrating hydrogeochemical parameters, WQI-based classification, and GIS-driven spatial interpolation is effective for diagnosing groundwater suitability and prioritizing mitigation actions. In Kabul Province, WQI values ranged from 27.5 to 112 (Afghanistan National Drinking Water Quality Standards; ANDWQS) and 33–127.5 (WHO), indicating that 3% (ANDWQS) to 9% (WHO) of sampled groundwater was unsuitable for drinking, while IDW mapping (ArcMap) showed that turbidity, TDS, total hardness, and Mg exceedances are concentrated in the central and eastern parts of the province28. Complementarily, a long-term nitrate analysis in Kabul Province (49 observations) reported a maximum NO3− concentration of 108.0 mg/L (2005) with a minimum of 0.044 mg/L (2010), and highlighted pronounced spatial clustering of nitrate pollution toward the north and west, with evidence that contamination risks remain higher in unconfined shallow aquifers29. At the sub-basin scale, groundwater assessment in the Upper Kabul and Paghman area found multiple chemical compliance issues, including Ca exceedance in 53% of samples (16–340.6 mg/L) and Mg exceedance in all samples above the 30 mg/L limit, alongside localized bacteriological contamination where total coliform reached 32 CFU/100 mL at specific sites (fecal coliform: 0 across sites), reinforcing the need to jointly interpret chemical and microbial indicators for public-health protection30. Finally, hydrochemical profiling of the Kabul Plain indicates that groundwater is slightly alkaline with pH values ranging from 7.0 to 8.0. but very wide electrical conductivity variability (618−16,400 µS/cm), consistent with strong spatial heterogeneity driven by lithology and salinity controls and underscoring why multi-index and season-aware monitoring is required for defensible groundwater management decisions in Afghan urban aquifers31. The observed regional variability in groundwater quality highlights the importance of localized assessments. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate groundwater quality in Kandahar City for both drinking and irrigation purposes using WHO guidelines, water quality index (WQI), irrigation suitability indices, and GIS-based spatial analysis.

Materials and methods

Study area description

Kandahar city covers an area of approximately 273 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{km}^{2}$$\end{document}, representing its official municipal extent. The study was conducted in Kandahar City, Kandahar Province, southern Afghanistan (31.6289° N, 65.7372° E), at an average elevation of approximately 1005 m above mean sea level (Fig. 1). Kandahar City is the provincial capital and the second largest city in Afghanistan, with a population exceeding 557,000 inhabitants, and groundwater is the dominant water source supporting domestic and agricultural demands32. Kandahar City serves as a major center of groundwater use for domestic, agriculture, and industrial activates in southern Afghanistan32. The region has a hot desert climate (BWh) with low annual precipitation of about 176 mm and high summer temperatures, where July is the hottest month (average high 40.2 °C) and January is the coldest (average high 12.2 °C); rainfall occurs mainly between January and March, resulting in limited natural recharge. The city is located north of the Registan Desert on relatively flat terrain near the Arghandab River corridor, and elevation decreases gradually from north to south, influencing groundwater flow and solute transport. The geological setting is dominated by crystalline hard rock units, mainly argentiferous biotite gneiss and argentiferous biotite-sillimanite gneiss containing graphite and kyanite, with minor lithologies including charnockite, calc-granulite, limestone, pegmatite, quartzite, and unconsolidated alluvial deposits of coarse sand and rock fragments (Fig. 1). Groundwater occurs primarily in weathered and fractured zones of the crystalline basement and locally within alluvial sediments, and aquifer properties such as well yield and transmissivity vary spatially depending on the degree of weathering and fracture development32. The city is characterized by rapid population growth, increasing urbanization, and mixed land-use patterns, including residential areas, agricultural lands, and small-scale industrial activities. Groundwater abstraction is primarily driven by domestic consumption, irrigation, and commercial use, contributing to increasing pressure on aquifer systems and potential anthropogenic impacts on groundwater quality.

Sampling collection

Each groundwater sample was collected in a 1.5 L clean plastic container from operational boreholes used for domestic water supply. Prior to sampling, all containers were thoroughly washed and pre-rinsed with the respective groundwater to minimize contamination and preserve sample integrity33. Sampling locations were distributed across all 15 administrative districts of Kandahar City to ensure representative coverage of habitable urban areas. The geographic coordinates of each borehole were recorded in the field using a handheld GPS device. In situ measurements of pH, temperature, turbidity, electrical conductivity (EC), total dissolved solids (TDS), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) were conducted at the time of sampling using portable field instruments34. The samples were then preserved and transported to the laboratory for comprehensive analysis of physicochemical and microbiological parameters, including major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, and K+), major anions, alkalinity components, nutrients, trace metals, and microbial indicators, as summarized in Table 1. All laboratory measurements and quality control procedures were conducted in accordance with internationally recognized standard analytical protocols33.

Spatial analysis and interpolation methods

Spatial distribution mapping of groundwater parameters was performed using Ordinary Kriging and Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW), which are widely applied interpolation methods for analyzing spatial variability in environmental data35,36. Ordinary Kriging is a geostatistical interpolation technique that accounts for spatial autocorrelation through semi variogram modeling and provides statistically based predictions of regional groundwater quality patterns. In contrast, Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) is a deterministic interpolation method that estimates values based on the inverse of the distance to surrounding sampled points, without incorporating spatial autocorrelation.

For the final Water Quality Index (WQI) output, both Ordinary Kriging and IDW were applied to strengthen the robustness of the spatial interpretation by cross-validating the mapped WQI classes using two widely accepted interpolation approaches. IDW provides a rapid distance-weighted representation that highlights localized variability, while Kriging offers a geostatistically optimized surface based on the modeled spatial structure of the data. The combined application of these methods improved confidence that the observed WQI spatial patterns were not an artifact of a single interpolation approach.

The base map of the study area was digitized from Afghan topographic sheets using ArcGIS Pro 10.1 software. Sampling locations were identified directly in the field with the aid of a global positioning system, and their exact geographic coordinates (longitude and latitude) were subsequently imported into the GIS platform. Spatial distribution maps of all physicochemical parameters were generated through ordinary kriging interpolation, following the World Health Organization (WHO) standards for drinking water quality35. Ordinary kriging represents one of the most widely applied geostatistical approaches, offering the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) for unknown attributes. This method accounts for spatial dependence between sampling points, ensuring that predictions are both statistically optimal and scientifically defensible. The general kriging equation is expressed as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Z}_{K}^{*}=\:\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda\:}_{i}{Z}_{i}$$\end{document}

The kriging estimator is formulated as a linear combination of known sample values, with weights determined by the spatial covariance function. These weights are carefully calculated to satisfy two fundamental conditions: (i) the estimator must remain unbiased, and (ii) the estimation variance must be minimized. Through this dual optimization, kriging provides the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) of the unknown spatial variable36.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\:\left\{\left[{Z}_{V}-\:{Z}_{K}^{*}\:\right]\right\}=0$$\end{document}

In kriging, ZV denotes the actual value of the spatial variable at location V, while ZK* represents its estimated value obtained through the kriging process. The estimator is constructed as a weighted linear combination of sampled values, where the weights λi are subject to the fundamental constraint:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda\:}_{i}=1.0$$\end{document}

This condition ensures unbiasedness of the estimator. The estimation variance, also referred to as the kriging variance, quantifies the reliability of the prediction and is expressed as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{K}^{2}=E\:\left\{{\left[{Z}_{V}-\:{Z}_{K}^{*}\:\right]}^{2}\right\}=\:\stackrel{-}{\mathrm{C}}\:\left(V,V\right)+\:\mu\:-\:\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda\:}_{i}\stackrel{-}{\mathrm{C}}\left({v}_{i},V\right)$$\end{document}

Here, C− (V, V) represents the covariance at location V, C− (vi, V) denotes the covariance between the sampled point vi and the prediction location V, and µ is the Lagrange multiplier introduced to satisfy the unbiasedness constraint. This formulation ensures that kriging provides the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) by minimizing the estimation variance while maintaining unbiased predictions. In the ordinary kriging method, the spatial dependence of the variable is characterized through a semi variogram, which evaluates the spatial distribution of sample data. Based on the experimental variogram, an appropriate semi variogram model is fitted using weighted least squares, and it’s parameters-namely the range, nugget, and sill-are determined. These parameters are then applied to compute the kriging weights, ultimately producing an optimal estimator at the prediction point37,38.

Hydro geochemical analysis methods

Statistical analysis was conducted by compiling physicochemical parameters from 80 groundwater samples into a structured Microsoft Excel database. Water Quality Index (WQI) values were calculated using standardized formulas, and descriptive statistics (minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation) were obtained for each parameter. The results were visualized using OriginPro 2024 to support quantitative interpretation of groundwater quality.

The hydrogeochemical evolution and controlling processes of the aquifer system were evaluated using an integrated analytical framework that combined hydrogeochemical diagrams and process identification tools. Hydrochemical facies classification, dominant process assessment, mineral equilibrium evaluation, and ion exchange analysis were applied to interpret groundwater composition and evolution34,39,40.

Hydrochemical facies were identified using the Piper diagram40, based on the relative proportions of major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+ + K+) and anions (HCO3−, SO42−, Cl−), expressed in percentage equivalents. This approach enabled classification of groundwater types and provided insight into dominant water–rock interaction processes.

Dominant hydrogeochemical processes were evaluated using the Gibbs diagram, which relates total dissolved solids (TDS) to ionic ratios (Na+/ (Na+ + Ca2+) and Cl−/ (Cl− + HCO3−)) to distinguish between precipitation dominance, rock–water interaction, and evaporation–crystallization mechanisms34.

Mineral saturation indices (SI) were calculated to assess the thermodynamic equilibrium state of groundwater with respect to major minerals and to evaluate tendencies toward dissolution or precipitation41,42. The saturation index was computed as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:SI=\mathrm{log}\left(\mathrm{I}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{P}\:/\mathrm{K}\mathrm{t}\right)$$\end{document} where IAP represents the ion activity product and Kt is the equilibrium solubility constant. Based on SI values, groundwater was classified as undersaturated (SI < − 0.5), near equilibrium (− 0.5 ≤ SI ≤ 0.5), or supersaturated (SI > 0.5), indicating dissolution, equilibrium, or precipitation conditions, respectively43,44.

Positive CAI values indicate normal ion exchange, whereas negative values reflect reverse ion exchange. These indices were used to interpret exchange processes influencing groundwater chemistry and spatial variability.

Water quality and irrigation assessment

Groundwater suitability for drinking was evaluated using the Water Quality Index (WQI) based on WHO guidelines. Irrigation suitability was assessed using indices such as Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR), Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC), Permeability Index (PI), Kelly’s Ratio (KR), and Magnesium Adsorption Ratio (MAR)42,43. The groundwater quality index serves as a robust, comprehensive metric that synthesizes a range of hydro chemical parameters, providing a quantitative assessment of aquifer suitability for various applications, including potable water consumption, agricultural irrigation, and industrial use. This approach facilitates a clearer understanding of groundwater quality and supports informed decision-making for its sustainable utilization46,47. The Weighted Arithmetic Index method by48 was employed to evaluate the groundwater quality specifically for drinking water purposes49. For the spatial representation of Water Quality Index (WQI), both ordinary kriging and inverse distance weighting (IDW) were applied to generate interpolation maps. This dual approach was used to ensure robustness and reliability of the spatial distribution of WQI and to evaluate the consistency of identified groundwater quality patterns. The dataset includes a sufficient number of spatially distributed sampling points, making it suitable for geostatistical interpolation. Kriging was used to incorporate spatial autocorrelation and model the underlying spatial structure of WQI, while IDW was applied to highlight local variations and enhance detection of potential contamination hotspots. For irrigation assessment, irrigation water quality is a critical factor influencing crop productivity, soil properties, and sustainable agricultural practices50. Irrigation water quality was evaluated using multiple indices, including Sodium Percentage (Na%), Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR), Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC), Permeability Index (PI), Kelley’s Ratio (KR), and Magnesium Adsorption Ratio (MAR)51–53.

These indices collectively provide a comprehensive assessment of groundwater suitability for irrigation purposes by evaluating salinity, sodicity, and potential soil permeability effects. Additionally, the Wilcox diagram was used to classify groundwater based on sodium hazard and electrical conductivity54,55, while the United States Salinity Laboratory (USSL) diagram was applied to assess salinity and sodicity hazards56. Results and discussion.

Groundwater quality is critical because elevated concentrations of dissolved ions and contaminants can compromise potability and increase public health risks. In this study, the measured physicochemical and microbiological results from 80 groundwater samples were evaluated against the World Health Organization drinking-water guideline values35. The comparative assessment, summarized in Table 4, provides a clear basis for judging the suitability of Kandahar City groundwater for drinking and for identifying the parameters that exceed permissible limits and therefore require priority attention in monitoring and management.

Physical parameters

Groundwater pH in Kandahar City is influenced by mineral-water interaction within the local crystalline hard-rock aquifer system, which is dominated by biotite gneiss and biotite-sillimanite gneiss, together with localized alluvial deposits. Under the arid climate and limited recharge conditions of the study area, these interactions contribute to the generally neutral to slightly alkaline character of groundwater57. The hydrolysis of silicate minerals such as feldspars and micas release major cations (Na+, K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) while consuming H+ ions, resulting in slightly alkaline groundwater conditions58. In the present study, pH values ranged from 6.7 to 8.15, with a mean of 7.59 (Fig. 4a), indicating generally neutral to mildly alkaline water. Most samples fall within the WHO (2024) recommended range of 6.5 to 8.5, with only 2.5% of samples marginally exceeding the upper limit. The observed pH distribution reflects effective buffering within the aquifer system and intensified water-rock interaction associated with prolonged residence times59. Elevated EC values in Kandahar City are associated with prolonged groundwater residence time within fractured crystalline aquifers and enhanced mineral dissolution of gneissic lithologies under arid climatic conditions, where limited recharge and strong evaporation promote solute concentration60. The breakdown of silicate minerals in garnetiferous biotite gneiss and biotite-sillimanite gneiss contributes significantly to ionic enrichment, resulting in EC values ranging from 448 to 4550 µS/cm, with a mean of 1498.57 µS/cm. Elevated mean EC values indicate enhanced mineral dissolution driven by extended groundwater-rock contact and evaporative concentration under arid climatic conditions61. Approximately 42.5% of samples exceeded the WHO guideline value of 1500 µS/cm, demonstrating strong lithological control on groundwater mineralization. These elevated EC levels highlight the continuous contribution of dissolved ions from silicate weathering processes within the aquifer system62.

Total dissolved solids (TDS) concentrations are similarly controlled by mineral dissolution and prolonged aquifer-rock interactions. Weathering of carbonate and silicate minerals, combined with evaporation-driven concentration, resulted in TDS values ranging from 235 to 2300 mg/L, with a mean of 800.87 mg/L (Fig. 4b). Although the mean value remains below the WHO (2024) permissible limit of 1000 mg/L, 73.75% of samples exceeded this threshold. The high proportion of elevated TDS reflects strong geological influence and reduced dilution under arid conditions, which may adversely affect water palatability and long-term domestic usability. Elevated TDS levels also increase the risk of scaling in water distribution systems, emphasizing the need for regular groundwater quality monitoring in Kandahar City. Total hardness (TH) in groundwater is primarily governed by the dissolution of carbonate and silicate minerals that release Ca2+ and Mg2+ into solution63. In particular, the weathering of calcite and dolomite plays a significant role in controlling hardness levels. Total hardness values ranged from 52 to 520 mg/L, with a mean of 313.83 mg/L (Fig. 4c). While most samples comply with the WHO (2024) guideline value of 500 mg/L, approximately 5% of samples exceeded this limit, indicating localized zones of intensified mineral dissolution. Overall, groundwater hardness in Kandahar City remains within acceptable limits for domestic use, reflecting the buffering capacity of the aquifer geology and its role in stabilizing water quality.

Hydrochemical characteristics of groundwater

Major ion chemistry in the groundwater samples shows a cationic dominance of Ca2+ > Na+ > Mg2+ > K+ and an anionic dominance of SO42− > Cl− > HCO3−, indicating that calcium and sulphate largely control the hydrochemical character of Kandahar City groundwater34. This ionic pattern is consistent with weathering and dissolution processes in gneissic and other metamorphic lithologies under semi-arid conditions, where mineral-water interactions supply Ca2+ and associated anions, while the formation of secondary clays can further modify groundwater chemistry through ion-exchange reactions34,70,71. In particular, exchange processes on clay surfaces may reduce Ca2+ in solution and contribute to relatively higher Mg2+ in localized zones. Although sulphate remains dominant overall, chloride becomes relatively more pronounced at some sites, which may reflect the mobilization of soluble salts from the unsaturated zone during recharge events and/or irrigation return flows. Given the conservative and highly mobile behavior of Cl−, such inputs can be rapidly expressed in groundwater and help explain spatial variability in anion composition across the study area.

Breaking down the specific cationic constituents, calcium (Ca2+) concentrations in the groundwater samples ranged from 2 to 190 mg/L, with a mean value of 30.76 mg/L (Table 4; Fig. 3b). While the average concentration remains below the WHO (2024) guideline value of 75 mg/L, approximately 6.25% of samples exceeded this limit, indicating localized calcium enrichment. Elevated Ca2+ levels are primarily attributed to the dissolution of calcium-bearing minerals such as calcite, anorthite-rich plagioclase feldspars, and calcium-rich garnet phases within the metamorphic bedrock. Additional contributions may arise from ion-exchange reactions involving calcium-rich clay minerals, reflecting strong lithological control on groundwater chemistry in Kandahar City64. Similarly, magnesium (Mg2+) concentrations exhibited substantial variability, ranging from 0.35 to 360 mg/L, with a mean of 81.42 mg/L (Table 4). A significant proportion of samples (56.25%) exceeded the WHO (2024) guideline value of 50 mg/L, indicating widespread magnesium enrichment across the study area. Elevated Mg2+ concentrations are likely associated with the weathering and dissolution of magnesium-bearing minerals such as dolomite, magnesium-rich biotite, and garnet, as well as ion-exchange processes involving Mg-rich clay minerals within the metamorphic formations. These geochemical interactions further highlight the dominant role of lithology in controlling groundwater composition and explain the observed exceedances. Furthermore, sodium (Na+) concentrations ranged from 4.6 to 260.7 mg/L, with an average of 104.8 mg/L (Fig. 5e). While the mean remained below the WHO (2017) guideline of 200 mg/L, a few samples exceeded this limit, reflecting localized lithological influence. Overall, sodium levels are generally acceptable for drinking purposes65. The occurrence of Na+ in the groundwater is primarily attributed to the weathering of plagioclase feldspars, a process characteristic of the gneissic lithologies that dominate the region66. The observed variation in Na+ concentrations is primarily controlled by differences in rock-water interaction time and the inherent heterogeneity of the aquifer materials67. Potassium (K+) concentrations ranged from 0 to 25 mg/L, with a mean value of 3.7 mg/L (Table 4). All measured K+ concentrations were within acceptable limits for drinking water, with its presence mainly attributed to the weathering of potassium-bearing minerals such as feldspars and micas, along with a limited contribution from ion-exchange reactions within the aquifer system.

Corresponding to the cationic distribution, the anionic chemistry is largely governed by sulphate and chloride. Chloride (Cl−) concentrations ranged from 16.5 to 72 mg/L, with a mean value of 40.76 mg/L (Table 4; Fig. 3h). All measured concentrations were well below the WHO (2024) guideline value of 250 mg/L, indicating that chloride does not pose a widespread drinking-water concern in Kandahar City. The presence of Cl− is likely associated with the dissolution of chloride-bearing accessory minerals in the bedrock, the leaching of salts from the unsaturated zone, and mobilization during recharge events, reflecting both geological control and hydrological processes68,65. Sulphate (SO42−) concentrations ranged from 40 to 400 mg/L, with an average value of 197.8 mg/L (Table 4; Fig. 3j). Approximately 22.5% of the samples exceeded the WHO (2024) guideline value of 250 mg/L, indicating localized sulphate enrichment. Elevated SO42− levels are primarily attributed to the oxidation of sulphide minerals, such as pyrite and pyrrhotite, commonly present in garnet-bearing gneisses, which release sulphate into the groundwater system during weathering processes46,47. Bicarbonate (HCO3−), represented indirectly through alkalinity measurements, also showed elevated concentrations across the study area. Total alkalinity values ranged from 75 to 570 mg/L, with a mean of 300.35 mg/L, and 80% of samples exceeded the WHO (2024) guideline value of 200 mg/L (Table 4). This elevated alkalinity reflects significant bicarbonate enrichment, primarily linked to the dissolution of carbonate minerals such as calcite and dolomite within the gneissic lithologies, as well as prolonged water-rock interaction under semi-arid conditions69,26. These processes contribute substantially to the buffering capacity and hydrochemical evolution of groundwater in the region.

Beyond the major ionic constituents, the concentrations of trace elements and heavy metals were generally low, with only localized exceedances observed (Table 4; Fig. 3). Arsenic (As) ranged from 0 to 2 mg/L and exceeded the WHO (2024) limit in 2.5% of samples, indicating isolated hotspots. Chromium (Cr) ranged from 0 to 0.07 mg/L, with 6.25% of samples above the guideline value, suggesting localized mobilization within the aquifer. Manganese (Mn) showed minor exceedance (2.5%), while Fe, Zn, Cu, Al, and boron remained within guideline values across the entire dataset (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Finally, an assessment of nutrient parameters and related species showed mixed conditions within the aquifer (Table 4; Fig. 3). Nitrate (NO3−) and nitrite (NO2−) remained below WHO (2024) limits in all samples, and ammonia (NH4+) was also within permissible levels. In contrast, phosphate (PO43−) was consistently elevated, with 98.75% of samples exceeding the guideline value, indicating widespread enrichment across Kandahar City. Cyanuric acid also exceeded the guideline in 23.75% of samples (Table 4; Fig. 3), reflecting additional localized anthropogenic inputs.

Hydrogeochemical processes and groundwater evolution

Hydrogeochemical evaluation of groundwater in Kandahar City indicates that groundwater evolution is predominantly controlled by rock-water interaction processes operating within the crystalline aquifer system. Variations in major-ion chemistry, hydrochemical facies distribution, saturation states, and ionic relationships demonstrate the combined influence of mineral dissolution, ion exchange, evaporation, and localized anthropogenic inputs on groundwater composition. These controlling mechanisms were interpreted using established hydrogeochemical diagnostic approaches, including Gibbs diagrams, Piper facies analysis, saturation indices, chloro-alkaline indices, and ionic ratio relationships.

Gibbs diagrams were employed to evaluate the dominant hydrogeochemical processes controlling groundwater chemistry in Kandahar City by examining the relationship between Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) and the ionic ratios Cl−/(Cl− + HCO3−) and Na+/(Na+ + HCO3−) (Fig. 4). These plots provide a first-order assessment of the relative influence of rock-water interaction, evaporation, and precipitation on groundwater composition70. The anion Gibbs plot (Fig. 4a) indicates that the majority of groundwater samples cluster within the rock-weathering dominance field, suggesting that mineral dissolution processes play a primary role in controlling solute acquisition. This distribution reflects prolonged groundwater residence time and intense water–rock interaction, particularly involving silicate and carbonate minerals. A limited number of samples trend toward the evaporation dominance field, indicating localized evaporative concentration under semi-arid climatic conditions. No samples plot within the seawater influence field, confirming the absence of marine intrusion in the study area.

Similarly, the cation Gibbs plot (Fig. 4b) shows that most samples fall within the rock-weathering dominance zone, reinforcing the conclusion that groundwater chemistry34,70 is largely governed by lithological control and mineral weathering. A secondary grouping of samples extends toward higher Na+/(Na+ + HCO3−) ratios with moderate to relatively elevated TDS values, highlighting the additional influence of evaporation in concentrating dissolved constituents in certain zones. The limited distribution of samples toward the precipitation dominance field suggests that direct rainfall recharge plays a subordinate role in shaping groundwater chemistry during the sampling period. Overall, the Gibbs diagrams demonstrate that rock-water interaction is the dominant process controlling groundwater geochemistry in Kandahar City, with evaporation acting as a secondary modifying mechanism, while precipitation influence and external saline sources remain minimal.

The Piper trilinear diagram (Fig. 4c) was used to classify groundwater samples based on their major cation and anion compositions and to identify dominant hydrogeochemical facies. In the cation triangle, most samples plot toward the calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+) apices, indicating the dominance of alkaline earth elements over alkali metals. This distribution reflects strong lithological control associated with the weathering of carbonate and silicate minerals within the gneissic aquifer system. In the anion triangle, groundwater samples are primarily distributed between the bicarbonate (HCO3−) and sulphate (SO42−) fields, highlighting the influence of carbonate dissolution and sulphate-bearing mineral weathering. The central diamond field reveals that the majority of samples fall within the Ca2+-Mg2+-HCO3− facies, characteristic of groundwater influenced by carbonate weathering and silicate dissolution. A secondary group of samples trends toward the Ca2+-Mg2+-SO42− facies25,40, indicating localized sulphate enrichment likely associated with the oxidation of sulphide minerals and dissolution of sulphate-bearing phases such as gypsum or anhydrite.

No samples plot within the sodium-chloride (Na+-Cl−) facies, confirming the absence of dominant saline intrusion and indicating that groundwater chemistry is not controlled by marine or evaporite-derived salinity sources. Overall, the Piper diagram confirms that groundwater chemistry in Kandahar City is primarily governed by natural geochemical processes, particularly rock-water interactions within gneissic and carbonate-rich lithologies, with localized sulphate enrichment reflecting mineralogical heterogeneity and secondary geochemical modification25,64,71,72.

To further evaluate mineral equilibrium conditions and ion-exchange mechanisms controlling groundwater chemistry, saturation-state analysis and chloro-alkaline indices were examined. Figure 5a presents the percentage of groundwater samples classified as undersaturated, at equilibrium, or supersaturated with respect to calcite, aragonite, and dolomite. Only a small fraction of samples are undersaturated for all three minerals, while most samples fall in the equilibrium-to-supersaturated range. Calcite and aragonite are largely near equilibrium with a substantial supersaturated portion, indicating carbonate buffering and a tendency toward precipitation under some conditions41,42. Dolomite is predominantly supersaturated, reflecting stronger Mg-carbonate control and typically slower dolomite reaction kinetics in groundwater systems.

Ion exchange processes were evaluated using Chloro-alkaline indices (CAI1 and CAI2), which provide diagnostic insight into cation exchange reactions between groundwater and clay minerals (Fig. 5b). The CAI1 versus CAI2 cross-plot indicates a strong predominance of normal ion exchange, as the vast majority of groundwater samples plot in the positive CAI1 domain. This pattern reflects the replacement of Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions on clay mineral exchange sites by Na+ and K+ ions from groundwater, demonstrating the significant role of ion exchange in regulating groundwater chemistry within the crystalline aquifer system. The distribution of CAI2 values further indicates variability in exchange intensity across the study area44,45, reflecting heterogeneous hydrogeochemical conditions related to differences in groundwater residence time, flow path development, and the spatial distribution of weathered clay-rich zones. Samples exhibiting higher CAI2 values suggest localized enhancement of clay-water interaction, likely associated with deeper weathering profiles or structurally controlled zones within the gneissic terrain.

Reverse ion exchange is limited, with only a small fraction of samples plotting in the negative CAI1 domain near the origin (Fig. 5b). This indicates that reverse exchange reactions, where Ca2+ and Mg2+ displace Na+ and K+ from exchange sites, are minor and spatially restricted. The dominance of normal ion exchange suggests sustained supply of Ca2+ and Mg2+ from mineral weathering and a mature exchange equilibrium between groundwater and clay minerals, characteristic of long-term water-rock interaction in crystalline aquifer environments

Source identification using SO42−/Cl− vs. HCO3−/Cl− plot

The relationship between SO42−/Cl− and HCO3−/Cl− ratios (Fig. 6a) provides insight into the dominant hydrogeochemical processes controlling groundwater chemistry in the study area. Most groundwater samples plot at relatively high HCO3−/Cl− ratios (approximately 2–5) with moderate to high SO42−/Cl− ratios, indicating that rock-water interaction processes, particularly carbonate dissolution and silicate weathering, are major controls on groundwater evolution. Elevated HCO3−/Cl− ratios reflect substantial bicarbonate inputs generated by dissolution of carbonate minerals (e.g., calcite and dolomite) and by silicate weathering reactions, whereas generally low Cl− suggests limited influence from marine intrusion or halite-controlled salinity. A smaller group of samples with very low HCO3−/Cl− ratios (near 0) and variable SO42−/Cl− values indicate localized Cl− enrichment, which may be linked to evaporative concentration and/or site-specific anthropogenic impacts. Overall, Fig. 6a confirms that natural weathering processes dominate groundwater chemistry across Kandahar City, while Cl− enrichment is spatially restricted.

Source identification using Ca2+/Mg2+ vs. SO42−/Cl− plot

The Ca2+/Mg2+ versus SO42−/Cl− ratio plot (Fig. 6b) further clarifies sulphate sources and carbonate mineral contributions in the groundwater system. Most samples show low Ca2+/Mg2+ ratios, indicating relative Mg2+ dominance over Ca2+, which is consistent with dolomite dissolution within carbonate-bearing and gneissic lithologies. Several samples exhibit elevated SO42−/Cl− ratios, suggesting SO42− enrichment driven by sulphide mineral oxidation and/or dissolution of sulphate-bearing phases (gypsum/anhydrite), rather than Cl−-driven salinity sources, because Cl− behaves conservatively. Samples combining high SO42−/Cl− with low Ca2+/Mg2+ indicate that SO42− inputs are partly decoupled from Ca2+ release, highlighting non-carbonate sulphate sources in specific locations. A limited number of outliers with higher Ca2+/Mg2+ ratios likely reflect localized calcite dissolution or lithological heterogeneity and shorter residence time conditions (Fig. 6b).

Carbonate system analysis using (Ca2+ + Mg2+) vs. (HCO3− + SO42−) plot

The (Ca2+ + Mg2+) versus (HCO3− + SO42−) plot (Fig. 6c) is a widely applied diagnostic tool for identifying dominant weathering processes controlling groundwater chemistry. In Fig. 6c, most samples plot below the 1:1 equiline, indicating an excess of (HCO3− + SO42−) relative to (Ca2+ + Mg2+). This deviation from stoichiometric balance suggests that carbonate and sulphate dissolution alone do not explain the observed chemistry and that additional contributions from silicate weathering, together with ion-exchange processes in the fractured crystalline aquifer, influence groundwater composition. This deviation implies that, in addition to carbonate dissolution, silicate weathering contributes appreciably to HCO3− generation without proportional release of Ca2+ and Mg2+. A smaller subset of samples plotting near the equiline suggests locations where carbonate and sulphate mineral dissolution dominate groundwater composition. Overall, Fig. 6c indicates mixed geochemical control, with silicate weathering and carbonate dissolution jointly shaping groundwater chemistry across the study area.

Hydrogeochemical source-diagnostic plots (Fig. 6a–c) show that Kandahar City groundwater chemistry is mainly controlled by natural rock-water interaction. Most samples exhibit high HCO3−/Cl− with moderate-to-high SO42−/Cl−, indicating dominance of carbonate dissolution and silicate weathering, while Cl− influence is generally minor and only localized in a few samples (Fig. 6a). The Ca2+/Mg2+ vs. SO42−/Cl− plot suggests widespread Mg2+ contribution consistent with dolomite influence and identifies localized SO42− enrichment likely linked to sulphide oxidation and/or gypsum/anhydrite dissolution (Fig. 6b). In Fig. 6c, most groundwater samples plot below the 1:1 equiline, indicating relative Mg2+ enrichment and suggesting the influence of dolomite dissolution and ion exchange processes, which is consistent with established hydrogeochemical interpretations34,70.

While groundwater chemistry in Kandahar City is dominantly controlled by natural geochemical processes, anthropogenic activities exert localized but significant impacts that modify water quality in specific areas. These human-induced influences do not alter the regional hydrochemical facies but introduce site-specific anomalies that are superimposed upon the geogenic background. Recognizing these secondary controls is essential for accurate interpretation of groundwater quality patterns and for effective groundwater management in rapidly urbanizing arid environments73. Agricultural contamination sources

Agricultural activities influence groundwater quality primarily through fertilizer application and irrigation practices, which alter nutrient concentrations and ionic balances at local scales. Potassium (K+) and nutrient-related species show enhanced sensitivity to agricultural inputs compared to major ions that are largely governed by lithology, indicating anthropogenic amplification of selected parameters74. Irrigation return flow under arid climatic conditions promotes salt mobilization from cultivated soils and enhances evapotranspiration-driven concentration of dissolved constituents, thereby intensifying local groundwater mineralization without fundamentally changing the dominant geogenic control75. These effects are spatially restricted and most pronounced in intensively cultivated zones lacking efficient drainage systems.

Domestic and urban contamination

Urban expansion and population growth contribute to groundwater quality degradation through inadequate sanitation infrastructure and wastewater disposal practices. Septic systems, unlined pits, and poorly protected boreholes facilitate the downward migration of contaminants into shallow aquifers, producing localized degradation that masks natural hydrochemical signatures76. The presence of microbiological indicators, including total coliforms, fecal coliforms, and Escherichia coli, demonstrates direct hydraulic connectivity between surface sanitation sources and groundwater in vulnerable locations. These impacts pose significant public health risks despite the persistence of geogenic control over major-ion chemistry.

Land use change impacts

Land-use transformation from agricultural and open land to urban and semi-urban areas alters recharge conditions and contaminant transport pathways. Expansion of impervious surfaces reduces natural dilution, enhances contaminant retention, and increases the influence of point-source pollution on groundwater quality77.

Consequently, spatial variability in groundwater quality reflects a combination of dominant rock-water interaction processes and localized anthropogenic disturbances, particularly in densely populated and poorly serviced zones. Although industrial activity is limited, small-scale workshops and storage facilities represent potential point sources that warrant continuous monitoring to prevent long-term aquifer degradation.

Anthropogenic influences on groundwater quality

Anthropogenic activities exert a localized but significant influence on groundwater quality, superimposed on the dominant geogenic controls. The spatial distribution of the Water Quality Index (WQI) (Fig. 7) highlights distinct degradation zones that coincide with agricultural land use and densely populated urban areas. Elevated WQI values in these zones reflect the cumulative impact of fertilizer application, irrigation return flow, and domestic wastewater infiltration. Agricultural practices primarily affect groundwater through nutrient and salt inputs, with potassium (K+) emerging as the only parameter showing statistically significant anthropogenic sensitivity, while most major ions remain governed by water-rock interaction. Irrigation return flow enhances chloride (Cl−) and total dissolved solids (TDS) locally, producing salinity anomalies that deteriorate WQI values, particularly in poorly drained areas.

Urban and domestic sources further contribute to groundwater deterioration through microbial contamination. Elevated total coliforms, Escherichia coli, and fecal coliform concentrations significantly increase WQI values at several sampling locations, indicating direct contamination from sanitation infrastructure and septic systems. As shown in Fig. 7, these biologically driven impacts result in localized transitions from “good” to “poor” or “undrinkable” groundwater classes, underscoring the need for improved wastewater management despite the overall buffering capacity of the aquifer system.

Water quality index (WQI)

The spatial and categorical variation of the Water Quality Index (WQI) reflects the combined influence of geochemical processes, mineralogical composition, and hydrological conditions controlling groundwater quality in the study area. The distribution of groundwater samples across WQI classes is summarized in Table 6, while the spatial patterns derived using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) and Ordinary Kriging are illustrated in Fig. 8a,b, respectively. As shown in Table 6, most groundwater samples fall within the Excellent (38.75%) and Good (32.5%) categories, indicating generally acceptable water quality. However, a notable proportion of samples are classified as Poor (15%), Very Poor (6.25%), and Undrinkable (7.5%), reflecting localized deterioration in groundwater quality. These degraded zones are spatially clustered in Fig. 8a, where IDW interpolation highlights localized hotspots of elevated WQI values, while Fig. 8b (Ordinary Kriging) reveals broader regional trends78. The occurrence of poor to undrinkable WQI classes is primarily attributed to elevated electrical conductivity (EC), total dissolved solids (TDS), and total hardness (TH), resulting from prolonged rock-water interaction, enhanced mineral dissolution, evaporative concentration, and reduced dilution under low-recharge conditions55. Elevated Na+ and K+ concentrations are linked to the weathering of albite, K-feldspar, muscovite, and biotite in gneissic rocks under high-temperature and low-flow conditions65,79. Increased Ca2+ levels in several samples reflect the dissolution of calcite, anorthite, and Ca-rich garnet minerals46,64. High Cl− concentrations observed in multiple samples are attributed to saline fluid inclusions and the weathering of Cl-bearing accessory minerals, while elevated SO42− levels result mainly from sulphide mineral oxidation (particularly pyrite) within garnet-bearing gneisses80,94]. Elevated HCO3− concentrations in a limited number of samples are associated with carbonate mineral dissolution under prolonged water-rock interaction.

Overall, Fig. 8 demonstrates that groundwater quality is spatially heterogeneous, with localized degradation superimposed on a generally acceptable hydrogeochemical background. These findings indicate that wells located in areas persistently classified as Poor to Undrinkable require treatment or alternative water sources, while zones with consistently low WQI values remain suitable for domestic use.

The health risk assessment conducted in accordance with the WHO drinking water quality guidelines (2017, 2024) indicates that groundwater in the study area poses variable but locally significant public health risks, driven by exceedances of key physicochemical parameters (Table 8). Among all parameters, total dissolved solids (TDS) represent the most critical concern, with 73.75% of samples exceeding the WHO permissible limit (1000 mg/L). Elevated TDS levels are commonly associated with unpleasant taste, gastrointestinal irritation, and laxative effects, particularly affecting sensitive populations such as children and the elderly under prolonged exposure90. Total hardness exceeded guideline values in 5% of samples, reflecting localized enrichment of calcium and magnesium derived from carbonate and silicate mineral weathering. Long-term consumption of hard water has been linked to urolithiasis (kidney stone formation) and increased cardiovascular stress, especially in regions with limited water treatment infrastructure91,92. Sulphate (SO42−) concentrations exceeded the WHO limit (250 mg/L) in 22.5% of samples, reaching values up to 400 mg/L, which may induce diarrhoea and dehydration, particularly among infants and individuals with compromised health93. Electrical conductivity (EC) exceeded 1500 µS/cm in 42.5% of samples, indicating elevated mineralization and cumulative ionic loading. High EC values degrade palatability and act as a proxy for increased exposure to dissolved salts, which may aggravate chronic health conditions under long-term consumption. Deviations in pH were limited (2.5% of samples), yet such departures from the recommended range (6.5–8.5) can enhance metal solubility, accelerate pipe corrosion, and alter taste characteristics, thereby indirectly affecting water safety.

Overall, the health risk assessment demonstrates that geogenic processes dominate groundwater chemistry, but the high frequency of parameter exceedances-particularly TDS, EC, sulphate, and hardness-necessitates targeted mitigation measures. These include household-level treatment (e.g., filtration or blending), systematic monitoring, and prioritization of alternative drinking water sources in high-risk zones to reduce chronic exposure risks.

The hydrogeochemical results and WQI classification indicate that groundwater quality is generally acceptable across Kandahar City; however, it remains spatially and temporally variable. According to the WQI classes (Table 6), 38.75% of the samples are in the excellent class and 32.5% are good, while 7.5% of samples (6 out of 80 locations) fall in the undrinkable category and require treatment prior to consumption. This spatial variability suggests that groundwater quality is influenced by localized hydrogeological conditions. Groundwater quality concerns are concentrated in zones underlain by garnetiferous biotite gneiss, where elevated hardness, chloride, and total dissolved solids were observed.

Irrigation water quality (IWQ)

Irrigation water quality was evaluated using multiple indices, including sodium percentage (%Na), sodium adsorption ratio (SAR), permeability index (PI), Kelly’s ratio (KR), and magnesium adsorption ratio (MAR). The integrated assessment indicates that the majority of groundwater samples fall within permissible limits for irrigation, suggesting generally suitable water quality for agricultural use in the study area. However, some localized variations were observed, which may be attributed to evaporation effects under arid climatic conditions, soil-water interaction, and agricultural practices. The combined interpretation of these indices provides a comprehensive evaluation of irrigation suitability and reflects the influence of both geochemical processes and anthropogenic activities on groundwater quality81,82.

Magnesium adsorption ratio (MAR)

MAR values range from 4.43 to 98.27, with a mean of 73.17. Seventy samples fall within the Suitable category (MAR < 50), while ten samples exceed this threshold (Table 7). Elevated MAR values indicate magnesium dominance, which can negatively affect soil aggregation and reduce permeability if such water is used continuously for irrigation53,61,87.

Residual sodium carbonate (RSC)

RSC values range from − 24.93 to 6.35, with a mean of − 2.66. According to standard classification, 53 samples fall within the good category (RSC < 1.25), while 12 and 15 samples are classified as Doubtful and Unsuitable, respectively (Table 7). Elevated RSC indicates excess carbonate and bicarbonate, which may precipitate calcium and magnesium, leading to soil alkalinity and reduced permeability if irrigation is continued without management.

Permeability index (PI)

PI values range from 0.08 to 116.77, with a mean of 50.44. Based on Doneen’s classification, 24 samples are categorized as Excellent, 26 as Good, and 30 as Unsuitable (Table 7). The spatial distribution shown in Fig. 10b indicates that although much of the study area supports adequate soil permeability, localized zones may experience infiltration and drainage problems under long-term irrigation51,52,61,82,88.

Kelley’s ratio

Kelly’s ratio varies between 0 and 0.98, with all samples exhibiting KR < 1, classifying the groundwater as suitable for irrigation (Table 7). This confirms the absence of excessive sodium dominance relative to calcium and magnesium and indicates minimal risk of sodicity-induced soil degradation47,89.

The combined evaluation of Na%, SAR, RSC, PI, KR, and MAR demonstrates that groundwater in the study area is largely suitable for irrigation, with salinity and magnesium hazards posing localized constraints. These limitations can be effectively mitigated through proper soil and water management practices, including gypsum application, controlled irrigation scheduling, and improved drainage. The graphical assessment of irrigation suitability further supports these findings. Conclusion

Groundwater in Kandahar City is primarily controlled by natural hydrogeochemical processes, especially rock–water interaction within gneissic and carbonate-bearing formations. Major-ion chemistry, Gibbs diagrams, ion-ratio relationships, and mineral saturation results collectively indicate that silicate weathering and carbonate dissolution are the dominant mechanisms governing groundwater evolution, while evaporation plays a secondary role in localized areas. Ion-exchange processes also contribute to aquifer geochemical development, as confirmed by chloro-alkaline indices indicating a predominance of normal ion exchange. Saturation indices further show that calcite and dolomite are generally near equilibrium to supersaturated conditions, reflecting effective carbonate buffering within the aquifer system.

Water Quality Index (WQI) analysis reveals spatial variability in groundwater suitability, with samples ranging from excellent to undrinkable categories. This variation is mainly driven by elevated total dissolved solids, hardness, sulphate, fluoride, microbial contamination, and localized arsenic concentrations. Comparison with WHO drinking-water guidelines indicates that certain areas pose potential health risks, particularly where high salinity, fluoride, turbidity, and microbial contamination are present.

For irrigation purposes, groundwater is generally suitable; however, moderate salinity, permeability hazards, and elevated magnesium in specific locations may negatively affect soil quality and crop productivity if not properly managed. Therefore, appropriate agricultural water management practices are required to prevent long-term soil degradation.

To ensure sustainable groundwater utilization in Kandahar City, integrated management strategies are recommended, including targeted treatment of high-risk areas (e.g., reverse osmosis and point-of-use filtration), promotion of rainwater harvesting to reduce aquifer stress, and improved irrigation efficiency with proper drainage systems. Continuous monitoring, protection of high-quality aquifer zones, controlled abstraction, and artificial recharge should also be prioritized under a coordinated groundwater management framework supported by community participation.

Future research should focus on quantitative health-risk assessment, isotope-based source identification, long-term monitoring of microbial and chemical contamination, and evaluation of climate variability impacts on groundwater recharge to strengthen sustainable water resource management in the region.
