Electronic band and core-shell structure engineering enables ultrahigh energy storage in high-entropy ceramics
Authors: Yunting Li, Peng Li, Haihua Huang, Hairui Bai, Fei Xu, Jigong Hao, Peng Fu, Juan Du, Zhongbin Pan, Wangfeng Bai, Wei Li, Jiwei Zhai, Zhenxiang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRelaxor ferroelectric ceramics are promising energy-storage candidates for high-power electronic systems owing to their high energy density and fast charge-discharge speed. However, achieving ultrahigh energy density still poses challenges due to the inherently inverted coupling relationship between polarization (P) and breakdown electric field (Eb). Here, we propose a high-entropy strategy to decouple polarization from breakdown electric field. The high-entropy design exerts a triple effect, which involves flattening electronic band to restrict the transport of charge carriers, driving the formation of core-shell heterostructure to suppress electrical breakdown, and stabilizing polymorphic polar phases to promote polarization rotation. The triple synergy effect led to an ultrahigh Eb and a maximized polarization disparity (ΔP = Pm - Pr). As a result, the high-entropy ceramics exhibit an ultrahigh recoverable energy density (Wrec) of 10.23 ± 0.99 J/cm3 and a satisfactory efficiency (η) of 85.44% ± 3.34%, alongside good cycling reliability and temperature stability. This work provides an innovative design paradigm for achieving excellent energy storage performance of dielectric capacitors.Subject terms: Ceramics, Energy storage

Introduction

Dielectric capacitors are key components in pulsed power systems, electric vehicles, and grid-scale renewable energy storage. Their advantages include instantaneous high power, fast charge-discharge speed, and good thermal stability1–4. The superior performance is attributed to a unique energy storage mechanism, which is primarily governed by rapid ionic displacement polarization under an applied electric field5,6. The assessment of energy storage performance in dielectric capacitors relies primarily on two parameters, i.e., Wrec and η. The Wrec is determined by the applied electric field (E) and consequent dielectric polarization (P), Wrec=∫PrPmEdP, where Pr and Pm are the remnant polarization and maximum polarization, respectively. The η is determined by Wrec and total energy density (Wtotal), η=Wrec/Wtotal (see Fig. S1 for a schematic illustration). However, the inherent contradiction between the Eb and ΔP in the dielectric ceramics limits their energy storage performance7,8.

To achieve excellent energy storage performance, considerable research has been directed toward decoupling the inverted coupling relationship between the Eb and P. Domain and phase engineering strategies have been proposed to enhance ΔP, while maintaining a high Eb, thereby resulting in excellent energy storage performance9–12. Grain orientation engineering offers an innovative approach that can significantly enhance Eb by mitigating strain and field inhomogeneity, thereby enabling excellent energy storage performance13. More recently, a high-entropy strategy achieved by incorporating multiple ionic species into the matrix has emerged as a promising approach. This approach reduces both domain size and polarization switching hysteresis while concurrently boosting Eb14–18. The high-entropy strategy enables (Bi, Na)TiO3 (BNT)-19, BaTiO3 (BT)-20, and (K, Na)NbO3 (KNN)-based14 ceramics to achieve both an energy density above 10 J/cm3 and an efficiency over 90%. In addition, the construction of a core-shell heterostructure has been proven to be effective in synergistically improving both ΔP and Eb. Leveraging the ingenious design of polar core and weak polar shell, outstanding energy storage performance was achieved in lead-free NaNbO3-based antiferroelectrics21,22, and (Bi, Na)TiO3-23, BaTiO3-based relaxor ferroelectrics24. Recently, multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs) have exhibited significant improvement in energy storage performance compared with their bulk ceramic counterparts25–28. This advancement arises from the synergistic optimization of dielectric layer thickness, interfacial engineering, and field distribution, which simultaneously boosts Eb and ΔP, paving a promising pathway toward high-energy-density and high-efficiency capacitive energy storage.

Despite these significant advances, some gaps remain unexplored in high-entropy energy storage ceramics. First, existing strategies often operate in isolation. The correlation between the high-entropy composition and the formation of core-shell heterostructure, as well as their synergistic effect on the energy storage performance, has not yet been explored. Second, the relationship between high-entropy composition and electronic band structure, as well as their impact on energy storage performance have not been comprehensively investigated. In this work, a series of elements, including Sr, La, Ba, Mg, and Ta, were selected to tailor the configuration entropy (Sconfig) of the (1-x)(Bi0.34Na0.30Sr0.28La0.04)TiO3-xBa(Mg1/3Ta2/3)O3 (BNSLT-xBMT) ceramics. A transformative triple synergy effect was achieved in the high-entropy ceramics. First, the flattening of the electronic band manipulated by chemical disorder and lattice distortion restricted carrier transport, thereby enhancing Eb. Second, the difference in ionic diffusion kinetics during crystallization drove the formation of the core-shell heterostructure, which suppressed the electrical breakdown and thus further enhanced Eb. Third, the high-entropy design stabilized polymorphic polar phases, which maximized the ΔP. The decoupling between Eb and ΔP yields an ultrahigh Wrec of 10.23 ± 0.99 J/cm3, and a satisfactory η of 85.44% ± 3.34% in high-entropy ceramics (x = 0.10). This study establishes a transformative paradigm that integrates electronic band and microstructure engineering for developing high-performance dielectric capacitors.

Results and discussion

Composition (entropy)-driven structural evolution

The Sconfig is defined as Sconfig=−R[(∑i=1Nxilnxi)cation−site+(∑j=1Mxjlnxj)anion−site], where R, N(M) and xi(xj) represent the ideal gas constant, atomic species and contents, respectively, at the equivalent cation or anion sites29. The tailoring of entropy in BNSLT-xBMT ceramics was achieved by introducing heterogeneous elements Sr, La, and Ba into the Bi and/or Na sites, and Ta, Mg into the Ti sites. The Sconfig progressively increases from 0.69 R (low-entropy) for the virgin BNT to 1.21 R (medium-entropy) for the x = 0 ceramic, and finally to ≥1.63 R (high-entropy) for the ceramics with x = 0.06–0.15 (Fig. S2). The rise in Sconfig resulted in overall grain refinement, as confirmed by SEM analysis (Fig. S3). The grain refinement effect and high relative density (average relative density >95%, Fig. S4, Table S1) are conducive to enhancing the Eb of the high-entropy ceramics30,31. The high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and bright-field scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) images of x = 0.10 ceramic unambiguously reveal the formation of multinuclear-type (Fig. 1a and Fig. S5a, b), and onion-like (Fig. 1d) core-shell grains. The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) line profiles (Figs. 1b, S5c and S6b) and EDS elemental mapping images (Figs. 1c, S5d and S6c) clearly demonstrated a pronounced enrichment of Sr, while a deficiency of other component elements (Bi, Na, La, Ba, Ta, Mg) within the core. To decipher the formation mechanism of the core-shell heterostructure, we performed first-principles calculations and experimental verification. To calculate the diffusion energy barrier of the doping elements within the BNT host lattice during crystallization, we constructed a probable migration pathway, as schematically shown in Fig. 1e. The results of first-principles calculation demonstrate that Sr exhibits the highest diffusion energy barrier among all the dopants during crystallization, regardless of whether it migrates to the Bi- or Na-site (Figs. 1f, S7). This implies significantly slower diffusion kinetics for Sr than those of other elements during sintering, leading to its entrapment and accumulation in the core region. The results (Figs. S8–S11) of our artificially designed sintering experiments indicate that prolonging soaking time progressively homogenizes the composition and eliminates the core-shell heterostructure in the sintered ceramics, which directly links its formation to diffusion kinetics.

It is worth noting that a series of well-defined core-shell interfaces (Figs. 1g, S5e) observed in the x = 0.10 ceramics are conducive to enhancing Eb by preventing the electrical treeing progression. The high-magnification morphological images (Fig. S6d) and the inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) images of lattice fringes (Fig. S12) of the core and shell regions reveal the ubiquitous presence of nano-scaled polar nanoregions (PNRs). The geometric phase analysis (GPA) of the x = 0.10 ceramic reveals nanoscale continuous fluctuations (~10%) in the εxy, εxx and εyy strain components (Figs. 1h, S6e, f), indicating strong lattice distortion and relaxor behavior. The dielectric relaxor behavior is quantitatively assessed using the modified Curie-Weiss law (Fig. S13). The diffuseness coefficients (γ) monotonically increase from 1.76 for virgin BNT (low entropy) to 1.96 for x = 0.15 ceramic (high entropy), indicating a significantly enhanced relaxor behavior with respect to raised Sconfig. The phase evolution was determined by XRD (Fig. S14) and Rietveld refinement (Fig. S15). The phase structure evolves progressively from a single rhombohedral (R) phase for BNT (low-entropy, Sconfig = 0.69 R) to the coexistence of R and cubic (C) phases for x = 0 ceramic (medium-entropy, Sconfig = 1.21 R), and finally to a multiphase coexistence of R, orthorhombic (O), tetragonal (T), and C phases for x = 0.06-0.15 ceramics (high-entropy, Sconfig ≥ 1.63 R) (Fig. 1i)

Energy storage performance

The statistical breakdown electric field (Eb) was evaluated using a two-parameter Weibull distribution function: P(E) = 1 – exp(–(E/α)β), where E refers to the measured breakdown field, P(E) signifies the cumulative probability of electric failure, α is the characteristic breakdown strength which corresponds to a ~63.2% probability of failure and β is the slope parameter that assesses the dispersion of the test data32,33. The Eb values increase rapidly with the rise of Sconfig, reaching a maximum of 865 kV/cm in the high-entropy ceramic x = 0.10 (Fig. 2a). The unipolar polarization-electric field (P–E) hysteresis loops for BNT and BNSLT-xBMT ceramics, measured at their respective Eb, are given in Fig. 2b. As the Sconfig increases, the P - E loops become progressively slimmer. The energy storage performance (Wrec and η) obtained from five samples (Figs. S16–S21) for each composition is summarized in Fig. 2c. An ultrahigh Wrec of 10.23 ± 0.99 J/cm3, and a satisfactory η of 85.44% ± 3.34% are achieved in the high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10) at Eb. The evolution of the P–E loops with electric field and the associated energy storage performance for the x = 0.10 ceramic are presented in Figs. 2d and 2e, respectively. The P–E loops remain extremely slim with near-zero Pr over a wide electric field range due to strong relaxor behavior29. Both Pm and ΔP increase linearly with the applied electric field, while η remains consistently high (>89.1%). A radar chart (Fig. 2f) provides a holistic comparison of the key metrics (Wrec, η, Eb, Pm, ΔP) for the low- (BNT), medium- (x = 0), and high-entropy (x = 0.10) ceramics, vividly illustrating the comprehensive superiority of the high-entropy design. The overall energy storage performance surpasses that of most recently reported lead-free ceramics (Fig. S22), positioning our high-entropy ceramic at the forefront of dielectric energy storage.

Structural origins of the superior energy storage performance

To uncover the structural origins of the superior energy storage performance, we conducted atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM measurements (Figs. 3a, b, S23) to analyze the polarization configurations of the x = 0.10 ceramic. HAADF-STEM images of both core and shell reveal polarization vectors pointing from the center of a B-site cation to the center of its four nearest neighboring A-site cations, as depicted in Fig. 3c. The polarization vector images (Figs. 3a, b, S23) indicate the T, O, and R phases embedded in the C phase matrix in both the core and shell. The results are consistent with the Rietveld refinement of XRD patterns. The size of the polymorphic polar phase region was determined to be 2.68 ± 0.47 nm (within the size range of PNRs) through the statistical analysis of atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images (Figs. S24, S25). We further analyzed the angles (Fig. 3d, e) and magnitudes (Fig. 3f, g) of the polarization vectors in both core and shell. The broad distribution of polarization angles indicates significant local disorder and polarization rotation flexibility, which lowers the energy barrier for polarization rotation and is conducive to maintaining a low Pr under high electric fields. Furthermore, the fluctuation in polarization magnitude indicates a reduction in local polarization, further reinforcing the relaxor behavior34,35. For the low-entropy BNT, the τ at 300 K (τ(300K)) approaches zero (Fig. S26), indicating that it is a classical ferroelectric material without any relaxor characteristics. Electronic band structure and charge carrier transport

First-principles calculations were employed to elucidate the electronic band structure evolution with increasing Sconfig36–38. The supercell models of low-entropy BNT (Fig. 4a), medium-entropy BNSLT (x = 0, Fig. 4b), and high-entropy BNSLT-0.10BMT (x = 0.10, Fig. 4c) ceramics were constructed using a special quasirandom structure (SQS) approach to mimic the cationic disorder and calculate electronic band structure. As shown in Fig. 4d, the partial density of states (PDOS) of BNT indicates that the valence band maximum (VBM) is dominated by O-2p orbitals, while the conduction band minimum (CBM) is primarily composed of Ti-3d orbitals. Doping with Sr, La, Ba, Ta, and Mg (Fig. 4e, f) introduces states deep within the valence and conduction bands, while leaving the band gap (Eg) essentially unchanged. It is worth noting that the introduction of high-entropy engineering exerts a profound influence on the electronic band structure, as shown in Fig. 4g–i. Compared to low-entropy BNT and medium-entropy x = 0 ceramics, the high-entropy x = 0.10 ceramic exhibits significantly flattened electronic bands, particularly near the CBM. The flattening of the electronic band structure primarily originates from the disordered potential field induced by chemical disorder and lattice distortion in high-entropy compositions. For the high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10), the large m* of the carriers implies a low carrier mobility (σ), according to the relation: σ=ne2τm*, where m* is the effective mass of charge carriers, n is the carrier concentration, e is the elementary charge, and τ is the relaxation time.

Electrical conduction-related measurements serve as experimental validation of charge carrier transport. The linear correlation of Hall resistance with respect to magnetic field (Fig. 4j) indicated that all compositions exhibit n-type conduction. With increasing Sconfig, the electrical resistivity rises markedly (Fig. 4k), while both the carrier concentration (Fig. 4l) and mobility (Fig. 4m) decrease substantially. This excellent agreement between first-principles calculations and carrier mobility measurements confirms a carrier confinement effect induced by the high-entropy design in the x = 0.10 ceramic. The carrier confinement effect can also be demonstrated by the dielectric loss-dependent frequency spectrum (Fig. S27). Obviously, high-entropy ceramics exhibit suppressed conduction-related loss over the frequency range from 100 Hz to 100 kHz, as compared to their low-entropy and medium-entropy counterparts. The flattened electronic band severely restricted the transport of charge carriers, which reduced leakage current and energy loss, thus enhancing Eb. Therefore, the flattened electronic band structure accounts for both the ultrahigh Wrec and satisfactory η in the high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10).

Role of the core-shell structure on energy storage performance

To elucidate the specific role of the core-shell heterostructure on enhancing energy storage performance, finite element simulations were performed based on the observed grain morphologies39,40. Three representative models (Fig. 5a–c) were constructed, corresponding to low-entropy large-grained ceramic without core-shell structure, high-entropy fine-grained ceramic without core-shell structure, and high-entropy fine-grained ceramic with core-shell structure. One can see model A exhibits pronounced electric field concentration at grain boundaries (Fig. 5a1–a6), leading to rapid propagation of breakdown channels across the entire sample even under a low simulated electric field of 380 kV/cm. Grain refinement in model B effectively hinders the electrical treeing propagation. As a result, under the same simulated electric field (380 kV/cm), the electrical treeing only propagates to approximately half the depth of the sample (Fig. 5b1–b3). Complete electrical breakdown of the ceramic bulk occurred at an applied electric field of 680 kV/cm (Fig. 5b4–b6). However, the introduction of core-shell heterostructure into fine grains (model C) dramatically reshapes the electric field distribution by homogenizing the local electric field and suppressing boundary hotspots through the dielectric contrast between the core and shell regions. Furthermore, core-shell interfaces act as barriers that deflect and pin electrical treeing progression. Consequently, the breakdown progression is markedly suppressed in the core-shell structured ceramic with fine grains (Fig. 5c1–c6) even at an ultrahigh external electric field (e.g., 680 kV/cm). These simulations prove that the synergy of core-shell heterostructure and grain refinement effectively suppressed breakdown propagation, thereby enabling a substantial rise in the Eb of the high-entropy ceramics. This mechanistic insight directly accounts for the ultrahigh Eb in the high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10), which is thus crucial for underpinning its ultrahigh energy storage performance.

Charge-discharge properties, cycling and temperature stability

For practical applications, long-term cycling reliability and temperature stability of the energy storage performance are critical metrics for assessing the dielectric capacitors. Therefore, we evaluated the cycling and temperature stability of energy storage and charge-discharge performance of the optimal x = 0.10 ceramic. The high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10) exhibits a high Wd of 4.2 J/cm3 under 550 kV/cm coupled with an ultra-fast discharge time (t0.9, time to release 90% of the stored energy) of merely 26.6 ns. The underdamped discharge current curves are shown in Fig. 6c. The current density (CD = Imax/S, where S is the electrode area, Imax denotes the maximum current) and power density (PD = E · Imax/(2S)) reach ultrahigh values of 1330.5 A/cm2 and 365.9 MW/cm3, respectively, under 550 kV/cm. As shown in Fig. 6d, e, the Wrec and η exhibit negligible degradation by 2.8% and 4.9%, respectively, after 105 cycles at 25 °C. It is worth noting that even after 105 cycles at 100 °C, both Wrec and η remain excellent stability, with variations of less than 9.4% and 12.1%, respectively (Fig. 6f, g). Furthermore, Wd shows a slight change of less than 6.6% and 8.6% after 104 cycles at 25 °C (Fig. 6h) and 100 °C (Fig. 6i), respectively. These results confirm that the high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10) possesses a good cycling reliability and temperature stability of the energy storage and charge-discharge performance. The remarkable long-term cycling reliability arises from both high-entropy induced carrier confinement effect and flexible polarization rotation, which collectively suppressed leakage current and reduce joule heating during electrical cycling. The good temperature stability originates from the high-entropy-stabilized polymorphic polar phases over a broad temperature range (Fig. S28), which prevents abrupt structural transitions and minimizes polarization hysteresis19,42. These advances endow the high-entropy ceramics with reliable long-term operation under elevated temperatures, fulfilling critical requirements for energy storage applications.

In summary, we proposed a high-entropy strategy to manipulate a triple synergy of electronic band, core-shell heterostructure, and polymorphic polar phases to enhance the energy storage performance of BNT-based ceramics. The flattening of the electronic band suppressed the electrical conductivity, while the core-shell heterostructure prevented breakdown progression, which significantly enhanced Eb. Furthermore, the polymorphic polar phases reduced the polarization rotation barrier and enhanced the flexibility of polarization reorientation, thereby maximizing the ΔP. The coordinated approach successfully overcame the traditional trade-off between polarization and breakdown electric field. As a consequence, excellent energy storage performance with an ultrahigh Wrec of 10.23 ± 0.99 J/cm3 and a satisfactory η of 85.44% ± 3.34% have been achieved in high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10). Furthermore, high-entropy ceramic (x = 0.10) exhibits excellent charge-discharge performance, along with good cycling reliability and temperature stability. This work demonstrates that synergistic tailoring of electronic band structure and microstructure is a feasible strategy to design new dielectric ceramic capacitors with high energy-storage performance.

Methods

Ceramics preparation

Polycrystalline samples of virgin BNT and (1-x)(Bi0.34Na0.30Sr0.28La0.04)TiO3-xBa(Mg1/3Ta2/3)O3 (BNSLT-xBMT, x = 0, 0.06, 0.08, 0.10, 0.15) were synthesized using a conventional solid-state reaction route. High-purity powders of Na2CO3 (99.8%), Bi2O3 (99%), TiO2 (99.9%), SrCO3 (99.95%), La2O3 (99.9%), BaCO3 (99.99%), MgO (99.99%) and Ta2O5 (99.5%) were pre-dried and subsequently weighed according to the stoichiometric ratios. The powders were mixed by planetary ball milling in ethanol for 24 h. After drying, the mixtures were calcined at 850 °C for 3 h in air and then milled again under identical conditions to ensure homogeneity. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) binder was added to the resulting powders, which were then granulated and sieved. The granules were uniaxially pressed into pellets with a diameter of 10 mm. After burning out the binder at 600 °C, the pellets were sintered in air at 1100–1150 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 3 °C per minute, then cooled to 500 °C at a cooling rate of 5 °C per minute, and finally furnace-cooled to room temperature.

Structural characterization

Crystal structure and phase purity were examined using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, TD-3700, Dandong Tongda Technology Co. Ltd., China) with Cu Kα radiation. Microstructure analysis was performed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Merlin Compact, Zeiss, Germany). Grain size statistics were derived from SEM images using Nano Measure software. The core-shell heterostructure, elemental mapping, and domain morphology were observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos F200X, America) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM measurement was carried out using a dual spherical-aberration corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM, Spectra 300, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Atomic column positions were determined via two-dimensional Gaussian fitting. Polarization vectors were extracted using custom MATLAB scripts.

Electrical performance measurement

For ferroelectric measurements, all ceramics were polished to a thickness of 20-50 µm and coated with 1 mm-diameter gold electrodes. Polarization-electric field (P–E) hysteresis loops were measured using a ferroelectric test system (PolyK Technologies PK-CPE1901) to evaluate the energy storage performance. The temperature- and frequency-dependent dielectric properties were measured by an inductance-capacitance-resistance (LCR) meter (E4980A, Agilent, USA) accompanied by a temperature control system. Charge-discharge performance was evaluated using a charge and discharge tester (CFD-003, Tongguo Technology, China) based on an RC circuit. Hall resistance was measured using a SourceMeter (Keithley 2400) and a Nanovoltmeter (Keithley 2182 A).

First-principles calculations based on the density functional theory (DFT)

DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) with the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was employed for the exchange-correlation function. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV was chosen to describe the electron-ion interactions for the geometry optimization and electronic structure calculations. The orbitals of 6s26p3, 2p63s1, 4s23d2, 4d105s2, 5p66s2, 5d16s2, 5s25p65d36s2, 2p63s2, and 2s22p4 were treated as valence electrons for Bi, Na, Ti, Sr, Ba, La, Ta, Mg, and O, respectively. The 2 × 2 × 1 supercell constructed by the SQS approach was used as a starting configuration. The Brillouin zone was sampled with Γ-centered k-point meshes of 8 × 8 × 4 for structural optimization and electronic band calculations with the energy convergence criterion of 10−6 eV. The optimized crystal structures were achieved within a convergence criterion of 10−3 eV/Å for Hellmann-Feynman forces. Diffusion energy barrier was calculated using the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method.

Finite element simulations

Finite element simulations of electric field distribution and breakdown propagation were performed based on three models, i.e., (A) low-entropy large-grained ceramic without core-shell structure, (B) high-entropy fine-grained ceramic without core-shell structure, and (C) high-entropy fine-grained ceramic with core-shell structure. Each model was discretized into a 2D grid of 170 × 238 points over an area of 2.5 × 3.5 µm2.
