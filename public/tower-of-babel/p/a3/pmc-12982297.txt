Outcomes in children receiving single-fraction Gamma Knife radiosurgery for arteriovenous malformation
Authors: Steven K Montalvo, Tu Dan, Kimberly Taing, Strahinja Stojadinovic, Kiran A Kumar, Zabi Wardak, Robert D Timmerman, Bradley Weprin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is used for arteriovenous malformations (AVM), but the risk for secondary malignancy (SM), radiation necrosis (RN), obliteration rates (ObR), and late neurologic toxicities in pediatric patients is not well defined. To address this, we reviewed our two-decade-long institutional experience treating pediatric AVMs with SRS.MethodsRetrospective review of patients receiving single-fraction SRS for AVM between 1/2006 and 12/2023. ObR and RN were determined on neuroimaging, and toxicities by clinical evaluation. Overall follow-up was 56 months (range 6–193); 59 patients had follow-up > 36 months (median 85 months).ResultsEighty-nine patients/101 AVMs were treated at average age of 12.9 years (range 2.36–20.1). Median follow-up was 56 months (range 6–193); 59 patients (66.3%) had > 36 months of follow-up (median 85 months). Average AVM volume was 2.85 cm3 (range 0.034–24.95 cm3), and 80.4% were < 3 cm. Eloquent areas were involved in 65.3%; 56.4% had deep venous drainage. Most had Spetzler-Martin (SM) grade 2 or 3 (39.6 and 36.6); SM grade 1 (12.9%), 4 (8.9%), and unknown (2.0%) were also treated. Most had pre-SRS hemorrhage (70.8%), neurologic deficit (37.1%), or seizure (21.3%); few were incidental (12.4%). Before SRS, 13.5% were embolized, 7.9% resected, and 6.7% both; most (71.9%) had no intervention. Radiographic obliteration by imaging was 55.0% at 3 years, 73.1% at 4 years, and 83.0% at 5 years. In patients with long-term follow-up, ObR after SRS alone was 90.6%. Eight AVMs (7.9%) failed to obliterate by 3 years and were retreated: five with SRS, two with embolization, and one with resection. Obliteration was achieved in 7/8 (87.5%) of these residual AVMs; overall ObR was 97.6%. No patients developed SM, hemorrhage, or died post-SRS, but 13% had residual seizures, 14.6% had persistent headaches, and 39% had neurologic deficits. Seven (8.5%) developed symptomatic RN.ConclusionSingle-fraction SRS for AVMs is effective, with a high obliteration rate and low radionecrosis rates. No secondary malignancies, hemorrhages, or deaths occurred.Keywords: Pediatric AVM, AVM radiosurgery, SRS toxicity

Introduction

Arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are a leading cause of non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage in children and require therapeutic intervention given the risk of life-threatening hemorrhage or permanent neurologic deficit [1]. Microsurgical extirpation is a commonly recommended approach for AVMs due to its high rate of nidus obliteration, but alternative or adjunctive therapies are often necessary [2]. Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is one such effective treatment for arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), particularly in surgically challenging locations or inoperable patients [3, 4]. SRS outcomes data for pediatric AVMs are somewhat heterogeneous regarding obliteration rates which range between 64 and 77% at 5 years, though reported adverse event rates are generally low for post-SRS hemorrhage (~ 5–7%), secondary tumors (< 1%), and radiation necrosis (~ 8%) [5–7]. Within the largest multicenter registry study, single-fraction obliteration rates are reported at 68.7% (71.8% in patients with SRS as the only treatment) and did not discern between MRI-confirmed versus DSA-confirmed ablation. These reported obliteration rates and concerns for both early and late effects of SRS in the pediatric population are incongruent with our clinical experience, which suggests excellent obliteration rates with little associated toxicity in our treated patient population [8]. Thus, we hypothesized that our SRS obliteration rates exceeded those previously reported and we aimed to describe patient outcomes and explore features associated with treatment success. This study achieves this by characterizing a large cohort of children treated with a homogeneous radiosurgery approach over a two-decade period at a single institution.

Methods

This study was approved by the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center institutional IRB. We retrospectively reviewed all patients aged < 21 years at the time of presentation who received single-fraction SRS on Gamma Knife (Elekta, Sweden) between 2006 and 2023. Patients were not included in this analysis if they had multi-fraction stereotactic radiotherapy or multi-staged SRS. Patients were treated with stereotactic head frame placement under general anesthesia followed by high-resolution MR angiogram (MRA) and intra-arterial digital subtraction angiogram (DSA). In some cases, CT angiogram was additionally used for targeting. All treatment images were fused in GammaPlan (Elekta, Sweden), where the nidus was identified and targeted. The SRS treatment planning goals were selectivity > 0.8 and coverage > 0.95 and 20 Gy, but no less than 18 Gy, prescribed to the margin. Post-SRS evaluation workflow included MRA every 6 months and DSA 3 years post-SRS or sooner if there appeared to be obliteration on MRA. After obliteration was confirmed on DSA, MRA evaluations continued at 3, 5, and 10 years. MRA may have been obtained at other timepoints for clinical concerns and infrequently followed by repeat DSA.

Outcomes were determined using available follow-up data in the electronic medical record. Academic difficulties and neurologic deficit assessments were determined by clinician report based on neurology or neurosurgical evaluation. Overall follow-up time was based on the last available encounter in the electronic medical record. Imaging follow-up time was based on the last MRA/DSA or first DSA with obliteration. Angiographic obliteration was defined by an absence of anomalous arteriovenous shunting on the DSA. Radiographic obliteration was defined as the absence of a nidus or flow void on MRA or as confirmed angiographic obliteration. The need for salvage therapies, including embolization, resection, or repeat SRS, was collected. AVM data including size, location, nidus type, and SM grade were determined by initial imaging. Radiosurgery plan features and quality, including treated volume and volume receiving 12 Gray (V12Gy), were determined from the Gamma Knife treatment planning system. Symptomatic radiation necrosis was defined as the presence of radiographic changes on T2-weighted or FLAIR MRI sequences requiring steroids, hyperbaric oxygen, or anti-VEGF therapy. Radiation changes were defined by asymptomatic T2- or FLAIR changes. Academic difficulty was determined by recorded use of individualized education plans, special education services, or other academic accommodations.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were compared using a chi-squared test, while non-parametric continuous data were compared using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Cumulative incidence of obliteration curves was determined by the Kaplan-Meier method using imaging follow-up intervals. Secondary malignancy incidence was determined using the overall follow-up interval. Simple logistic regression was used to fit curves for radiation necrosis based on the V12Gy. Statistics and figures were generated with GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA).

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included 89 pediatric and adolescent patients treated at a single institution, with a total of 101 AVMs undergoing single-fraction SRS. Table 1 describes patient characteristics in detail. Patients were treated with SRS at an average age of 12.9 years (range 2.36–20.1 years). Median overall follow-up time was 56 months (range 6–193 months), and imaging follow-up was 29 months (range 6–160 months). Fifty-nine cases (66.3%) had at least 36 months of follow-up, with a median overall follow-up time of 85 months in this group. Eloquent areas were involved in 65.3% of cases, with deep venous drainage in 56.4% of AVMs. Average AVM volume was 2.85 cm3 (range 0.034–24.95 cm3), and most were under 15 cm3 (82.2%), roughly the volume of an AVM < 3 cm diameter. SM grading was as follows: grade 1, 12.9%; grade 2, 39.6%; grade 3, 36.6%; grade 4, 8.9%; and unknown in 2%. Patients often presented with hemorrhage (70.8%) and neurologic deficit (37.1%), and less commonly with seizure (21.3%) or as an incidental finding (12.4%). Most patients had no prior intervention before SRS (71.9%), though 13.5% of patients underwent embolization, 6.7% embolization and surgical intervention, and 7.9% surgical intervention alone.

SRS characteristics

Table 2 describes SRS treatment features and post-SRS outcomes. The median prescription SRS dose was 20 Gy (range 14–26 Gy) administered to a median isodose line of 50% (range 38–62%), and the median maximum dose was 40 Gy (range 28–52.6 Gy). Ninety percent of AVMs (91/101) were treated with 18–20 Gy to the 45–60% isodose line. The median target coverage was 0.98 (range 0.68–1.0); Paddick conformality index was 0.75 (range 0.31–0.88), and gradient index was 2.650 (range 2.390–4.128). Lastly, the median V12Gy was 5.381 (range 0.208–32.940). When comparing patients with and without obliteration, there were no significant differences in V12Gy, coverage, selectivity, gradient index, conformality index, or age at presentation. However, median treatment dose was statistically lower in the non-obliterated group (18 Gy vs. 20 Gy, p < 0.05).

Obliteration rate

Cumulative incidence of radiographic obliteration by angiography or MRI was 55.0% at 3 years, 73.1% at 4 years, and 83.0% at 5 years in full patient cohort (Fig. 1). The median time to obliteration in was 31 months. In 59 patients (66%) with 64 AVMs (63.4%) with > 36 months of follow-up, obliteration rate after single-fraction SRS alone was 90.6%. Angiographic obliteration was confirmed in 63 patients, with a cumulative incidence of angiographic obliteration of 45.5% at 3 years, 67.4% at 4 years, and 76.2% at 5 years (Fig. 1). Eight AVMs (7.9%) required a second treatment: five with repeat SRS, two with embolization, and one with resection (Table 2). Obliteration was then confirmed in 7/8 (87.5%) of these residual AVMs with the solitary failure in a patient treated with post-SRS embolization. Thus, the overall obliteration rate in the 59 patients with > 36 months of follow- up was 97.6%. Obliteration rate did not appear to change by SM grading (Fig. 2). Using chi-squared testing, the presence of pre-treatment neurologic deficits was negatively associated with post-treatment obliteration (p = 0.046); however, this association was non-significant when applying Bonferroni’s correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted significant p value = 0.01). In contrast, embolization, attempted surgical resection, or surgical decompression before SRS was negatively associated with obliteration using chi-squared testing (p = 0.004); logistic regression estimated the odds ratio to be 4.706 (95% CI 1.554–14.250, p = 0.006) for non-obliteration due to pre-SRS intervention. These patients were more likely to have SM grade 3 or 4 lesions, less likely to be incidentally found, and more likely to present with neurologic deficit or seizure. However, AVM volume and treatment dose were no different between patients with versus without pre-SRS intervention.

Toxicity outcomes

Eighty-two patients had sufficient follow-up for toxicity evaluation. No patients developed a secondary neoplasm. No patients died following SRS, nor did any experience a post-SRS hemorrhage. Persistent or bothersome headaches, persistent seizures, and residual neurologic deficits were noted in 13.5%, 12.4%, and 36.0% of patients, respectively. In patients without a history of intracranial hemorrhage, the rates of persistent seizures were 7.3%, headaches were 6.1%, and persistent neurologic deficits were 3.7%. However, there were no post-treatment neurologic deficits among patients with an incidentally discovered AVM, those without a pretreatment neurologic deficit, or those who did not undergo an invasive procedure prior to SRS. Academic difficulty was noted in 13 children (15.9%), though academic achievement prior to diagnosis of AVM was not captured in this analysis and so change from baseline is not evaluable. Seven patients (8.5%) developed symptomatic RN and were treated with steroids, but one required the addition of hyperbaric oxygen. An additional eight patients exhibited asymptomatic radiographic changes on post-SRS MRIs that were monitored. The simple logistic regression curve for development of radiation necroses indicates a point estimate odds ratio of < 10% when V12Gy equals 10 cm3 (Fig. 3). The presence of a pre-SRS neurologic deficit, bleed, or rupture was associated with increased odds of developing a post-SRS neurologic deficit (OR 2.119, 95% CI 1.410–3.183, p < 0.001). Progressive worsening of neurologic deficits or academic performance was not noted in this cohort.

Discussion

This study strongly supports the safety and efficacy of single-fraction stereotactic radiosurgery for appropriate pediatric arteriovenous malformations within our follow-up period. Functional damage to vascular tissue, a “late” radiation effect, is remote in time from the point of treatment. In our series, this desired effect for AVM obliteration consistently occurred months to years after treatment, as expected [8]. Our data reveals a quarter of patients had obliteration by 2 years while the obliteration rates exceeded 90% with longer follow-up, comparable to surgical series; notably, half of these patients were categorized as Spetzler-Martin grade 1 or 2, who are less represented in most SRS series as they are typically treated surgically [9]. Yet, they too demonstrated excellent outcomes and drive the population outcomes in our patients. These obliteration rates are nominally superior to previously reported multi-institutional international radiosurgery database and pooled analysis which range between 63.6 and 76.1% at 5 years, perhaps related to the patient volume treated at our center and our consistent management approach [6–8]. Even among single-session SRS as the only treatment, whose demographics better matched our treated cohort, AVM obliteration was only 71.8% using a definition for obliteration identical to radiographic obliteration in our study, though it is unclear whether patients in the latent phase are included. In our patients with adequate follow-up and excluding those within the latent phase, the obliteration rate was 90% after SRS alone and increased to 98% when non-responsive patients were salvaged with SRS, surgery, or embolization. SM grade 4 patients had relatively good obliteration outcomes with only one patient requiring post-SRS embolization, though these patients were treated more often in the early portion of this study period and with a lower SRS dose; our current practice involves a staged radiosurgery approach for these complex lesions to maintain optimal dose delivery while respecting normal brain tissue tolerance. Further, there were few radiation-related toxicities and no instances of post-treatment hemorrhage or secondary malignancy within this study’s follow-up period, though given the rarity of these events broad conclusions cannot be drawn. We also found neurologic deficits after SRS were related to pre-existing conditions, such as prior hemorrhage or persistent neurologic deficits.

Features associated with non-obliteration in our cohort were any prior surgical or endovascular intervention, though this association may be confounded by other clinical features not captured in this analysis. The patients who underwent pre-SRS intervention had higher SM grade AVMs, but our data suggest SM grading does not impact the likelihood of obliteration. There is a distinction for the goal of embolization as a component of surgical versus radiosurgical management of AVM. Embolization is used prior to surgery to reduce hemorrhage risk, but prior studies have shown that pre-SRS embolization can negatively impact AVM obliteration rates due to the interference of the embolic material on AVM targeting [2, 10]. Further, embolic techniques suffer from low rates of complete obliteration and comparatively higher acute complication rates for definitive management of AVMs [11–13]. In one series, even when selecting optimal patients for definitive management, endovascular techniques achieve obliteration in only 58% of patients with a 24% complication rate, leading the study authors to argue that embolization should be used only as an adjunct to curative surgical or radiosurgical strategies [14]. To contrast, our data show that radiosurgery delivered as a first-line treatment in patients amenable to single-fraction SRS results in obliteration rates ~ 90% with low rates of acute and late post-treatment toxicity. Thus, routine embolization should be reserved for clinical indications other than as an adjunct to SRS given the already high rates of AVM obliteration in the absence of embolization and the known decrease in SRS efficacy.

These data also confirm the natural history of SRS-treated small AVMs. There is a high rate of obliteration over time, and early invasive angiograms may be predictive of overall outcomes, but perhaps not necessary [15]. Given the low risk of post-SRS hemorrhage and high rate of nidal obliteration over time in these small AVMs, one could question the necessity of routine 3-year invasive angiograms in AVMs that show a favorable response to treatment on MR angiography. MR angiography has high sensitivity for intracranial vascular malformations and has been used instead of DSA after definitive therapy in other pediatric vasculopathies due to its ease of use, decreased acute risk, and reduced cost [16, 17]. About half of our patients had radiographic obliteration at 3 years, and it was rare that failure to obliterate at 3 years led to a second intervention; instead, most patients were observed and eventually had confirmed obliteration within 4 to 5 years of SRS. The eight patients who required subsequent intervention had radiographically persistent AVMs that had little change over longer term follow up. Thus, either MRI confirmation of no residual nidus or a stable nidus over time on MR angiography may help identify patients who warrant earlier invasive angiography for potential intervention. This paradigm would avoid the toxicities of multiple invasive angiograms, including procedural radiation dose, iodinated contrast exposure, and, in most cases, anesthesia exposure, and increase the yield of a negative invasive angiogram in children with favorable AVMs.

There are important limitations to these data. First, this is a retrospective analysis that relies on patients maintaining contact with our academic system, which includes a children’s hospital, academic quaternary hospital, and charity county hospital. The follow-up is adequate to show these children are not developing early radiation-related malignancies or severe neurologic sequelae, but very long-term follow-up is still necessary for to confirm late AVM recurrences and establish the life-time risk of secondary malignancy which continues to exist > 10 years from time of radiation exposure [18, 19]. Few but some patients were lost to follow-up or never completed a confirmatory angiogram. Additionally, there may be bias in coding toxicities as they relied on the review of electronic health record documentation. Importantly, this series describes patients with AVMs amenable to single-fraction radiosurgery. Larger AVMs are approached with staged treatment at our institution, with the goal of sclerosing the AVM to convert patients into surgical candidates. These patients are intrinsically different and require dedicated investigation.

Conclusion

We demonstrate a high obliteration rate among pediatric patients receiving single-fraction SRS for AVM without rebleeding events, secondary neoplasms, or death. Radiation necrosis was rare and associated with V12Gy. Other toxicities post-SRS were moderate and more common in patients with pre-SRS hemorrhage. SM grading did not influence the success of SRS, but pre-SRS intervention increased the odds of non-obliteration. Overall, SRS is a valuable tool in the treatment of pediatric AVMs, but very long-term follow-up is still necessary to continue to characterize the lifetime risk for children treated with SRS.
