Potential unlocked: an atlas of cloned wheat genes for genome engineering and breeding
Authors: Abdulrahman Alhabsi, Francisco Miguel Ayala, Jiawei Pan, Yan L. Wang, Amira M. I. Mourad, Peter Dracatos, Brande B. H. Wulff, Yagiz Alagoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBread wheat (Triticum aestivum) production is increasingly threatened by biotic and abiotic stresses. Developing varieties with improved stress tolerance and desirable end‐use qualities is crucial for meeting growing global demand. Genome‐editing technologies, particularly Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats and CRISPR‐associated proteins (CRISPR/Cas)‐derived systems, represent powerful tools to accelerate trait discovery and crop improvement. Here, we present a comprehensive atlas of cloned wheat genes and discuss examples and strategies for using it to identify candidate targets for CRISPR/Cas‐mediated improvement. Finally, we recommend ways to integrate gene editing into breeding timelines and accelerate the incorporation of desirable alleles.

Introduction

Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) is a staple food, supplying c. 20% of global caloric and protein needs. This allohexaploid species contains three subgenomes, A, B, and D, each from a different diploid wild progenitor. Many wheat breeding programs focus on developing varieties with traits that mitigate challenges posed by pests, pathogens, and abiotic environmental factors, such as drought and salinity stress. The annual loss in wheat yields due to pests and pathogens alone is estimated to be c. 21.5% of total projected wheat production (Savary et al., 2019). In addition, drought stress affects wheat agriculture in regions with limited rainfall, and many irrigated areas depend on unsustainable extraction of water from aquifers. Wheat cultivation usually requires agrochemical applications throughout the growing season; if projected yields drop during later stages of growth, these inputs are wasted, representing a sustainability concern. Another focus is enhancing essential micronutrient content (i.e. zinc and iron) in wheat kernels.

Gene editing and genome engineering offer powerful tools for developing wheat varieties with superior traits (Ayala et al., 2024). Here, we provide a foundation for identifying and selecting candidate genes for editing and breeding by creating the atlas of cloned wheat genes, a comprehensive directory of 745 cloned wheat genes. We highlight cloned genes with demonstrated potential for enhancing wheat performance based on gene knockout results and describe examples and strategies for using the atlas to engineer improved wheat varieties via state‐of‐the‐art Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats and CRISPR‐associated proteins (CRISPR/Cas) systems. Finally, we provide an overview of the regulatory landscape for the commercialization of gene‐edited lines and explain how they can be integrated into breeding programs.

A short history of gene cloning in wheat

Gene cloning in wheat has historically been hampered by its large, highly repetitive hexaploid genome. Nevertheless, many wheat genes have been cloned using conventional mapping and homology‐based approaches, such as VERNALIZATION (Yan et al., 2006) and GRAIN WIDTH AND WEIGHT 2 (Su et al., 2011). Genome‐wide association studies and RNA‐seq have also contributed to gene identification, including the transcription factor genes TaNAC071‐A (Mao et al., 2022), TaGAMYB (Yang et al., 2022), and TaWRKY74s (X‐Z. Li et al., 2024; Supporting Information Table S1).

Recent technological advances have accelerated gene cloning in wheat (Fig. 1). Cost‐effective, high‐throughput short‐read sequencing technologies (e.g. Illumina and DNA Nanoball Sequencing) and long‐read platforms (e.g. PacBio, Oxford Nanopore, and CycloneSEQ) enable the generation of high‐quality genome assemblies (Avni et al., 2017; Zimin et al., 2017; Athiyannan et al., 2022). In parallel, improvements in transcriptome resolution have been driven by methods, such as PacBio isoform sequencing (Iso‐seq; Y. Wang et al., 2023). Additionally, genome sequencing of diversity panels has expanded genomics resources (Ayala et al., 2024). Mutational genomics, which combines mutagenesis, sequencing, and bioinformatics, has enabled the cloning of at least 159 wheat genes, including 35 rust resistance genes (Table S1; Sánchez‐Martín et al., 2016; Thind et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2019; Y. Wang et al., 2023).

Functional validation of candidate genes is typically achieved through genetic complementation assays or by knocking out the candidate gene in a cultivar exhibiting the trait of interest. Low regeneration efficiency and genotype‐dependent transformability have long presented obstacles to functional validation of wheat genes. Several innovative solutions were recently developed to overcome these barriers, including the use of Agrobacterium tumefaciens engineered with a type III secretion system (Raman et al., 2022) and the integration of regeneration‐promoting morphogen‐encoding genes into T‐DNA (Debernardi et al., 2020; K. Wang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024; Fig. 1). These advances have greatly improved the transformation of diverse wheat genotypes, facilitating gene editing and genetic engineering in wheat for both basic and applied research.

Building the atlas of cloned wheat genes

The first cloned wheat gene, Reduced height‐1, was identified in 1999 using conventional mapping of a semi‐dwarf mutant (Peng et al., 1999). Since then, sequencing, assembly, and annotation of the hexaploid wheat genome have revealed 107 891 high‐confidence gene models (IWGSC et al., 2018). In parallel, researchers have genetically identified c. 3500 loci (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/GG3/content/wheat‐gene‐catalogue‐2024‐released‐covering‐all‐wgc‐curations‐until‐2024), most by analyzing natural or induced phenotypic variation followed by crossing and linkage mapping, allowing loci to be assigned to defined regions of the wheat genome before molecular cloning and functional characterization.

We considered a gene cloned if it was genetically and/or physically defined by positional mapping, genome‐wide association mapping, homology‐based methods, target sequence capture, transcriptome deep sequencing (RNA‐seq), expression analysis, physical interaction studies involving the encoded protein, or similar strategies, followed by functional validation. Using these criteria, we surveyed PubMed‐indexed studies for wheat genes cloned between 1999 and early 2025 and compiled an atlas of 745 cloned wheat genes. Of these, 634 genes were functionally validated in planta, in wheat cultivars or heterologous plants, such as Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), rice (Oryza sativa), or tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum or Nicotiana benthamiana), using CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout, RNA interference (RNAi), virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS), overexpression, or complementation assays. The functions of the remaining 111 genes were validated in nonplant in vivo systems (e.g. yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae)) or through in vitro analysis (e.g. enzymatic assays; Fig. 1; Table S1).

Harnessing the wheat gene atlas for next‐generation engineering and breeding

Optimization of CRISPR/Cas gene editing has accelerated functional validation of genes in wheat. At least 102 of the genes in our atlas were functionally validated using CRISPR/Cas (Table S1). Among the CRISPR‐edited wheat lines studied thus far, 66 showed improved agronomic performance, with 14 out of 66 or without 19 out of 66 growth or yield penalties. In the remaining 33 cases, penalties were not comprehensively investigated (Table S2). For a detailed review of CRISPR applications in wheat, see Zhou et al. (2023). Here, we focus on current and emerging CRISPR‐based genome‐editing technologies and strategies for integrating editing into the breeding of elite wheat cultivars for next‐generation agriculture, highlighting key examples and considerations.

One major approach involves knocking out genes that negatively influence desired agronomic traits, thereby producing improved wheat lines. However, mutating all three homoeologs (also termed homoeoallelic genes) across the three subgenomes can have detrimental effects, compromising growth and yield‐related performance. Accordingly, CRISPR can be used to fine‐tune gene expression in polyploid wheat through dosage‐dependent modulation, a concept we refer to as CRISPR‐mediated dosage adjustment (CDA). CDA can be achieved by modifying one or more homoeologs, resulting in desired or intermediate phenotypes. For instance, targeted mutation of SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN‐LIKE 6 in the D subgenome enhanced salinity tolerance without impairing growth or grain yield, whereas genetically inactivating this gene in the A and B subgenomes severely affected spike growth, resulting in fewer grains per spike (M. Wang et al., 2024). In another example, when one or two Grain width on chromosome 7 (GW7) homoeologs, GW7‐D1 and/or GW7‐B1, were knocked out via CRISPR, the resulting plants had wider and heavier grains to varying degrees (Wang et al., 2019).

Another CDA approach adds or removes cis‐regulatory elements (silencers and enhancers) within the promoter and/or untranslated regions (UTRs) to flexibly modulate target gene expression levels in a dosage‐dependent manner. The use of CRISPR/Cas for promoter editing in wheat was first demonstrated by multiplexed expression of guide RNAs (gRNAs) delivered via a barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV) system to target multiple sites within the promoter of the transcription factor gene Q in transgenic wheat expressing Cas9 (W. Wang et al., 2022). Although applying CDA to hexaploid wheat is challenging due to the presence of up to six homoeoalleles, recent advances in multiplex CRISPR editing suggest that CDA is a feasible goal (Ni et al., 2023), making CDA‐based editing a promising strategy for next‐generation wheat improvement.

CRISPR/Cas9 was also used to eliminate the recognition site of mature microRNA 156 (miRNA156) in the 3′ UTRs of SPL13 homoeologs, resulting in higher SPL13 transcript abundance and thus early flowering and enhanced grain yield (Gupta et al., 2023). Similarly, CRISPR/Cas9 was used to remove translational suppressors in the 3′ UTRs of LOW‐TEMPERATURE TOLERANCE 7 and SHORTENED BASAL INTERNODES in rice, generating engineered alleles with higher translation efficiency of their encoded proteins. Plants bearing these alleles are cold‐tolerant and semi‐dwarf, respectively (H. Wang et al., 2024).

We are not aware of any studies demonstrating targeted integration of enhancer cis‐elements to achieve CDA in wheat. Several reports in other grass species, such as rice, highlight the potential of this approach. For instance, the 5′ UTR of DWARF AND LOW‐TILLERING was modified by introducing or extending upstream open reading frames (uORFs) to fine‐tune the brassinosteroid pathway, yielding rice lines with varied plant height and tiller number (Xue et al., 2023). Similarly, inserting an upstream start codon (uATG)‐containing element into the 5′ UTR of WAXY reduced its expression by 19–89%, generating rice lines with lower grain amylose content (Shen et al., 2024). Inserting or substituting the enhancer element ‘AAA’ or the silencer element ‘TTT’ in the 5′ UTR of rice SBI or HIGH TILLERING AND DWARF 1 (also known as CAROTENOID CLEAVAGE DIOXYGENASE 7), respectively, just upstream of the ATG start codon enabled the precise control of strigolactone accumulation, thereby modulating shoot and root architecture (H. Wang et al., 2023).

New and future editing technologies for next‐generation wheat breeding

RNA‐guided non‐CRISPR platforms, such as those based on transposase B (TnpB), were recently developed for plant genome editing. The TnpB system is composed of a right‐end element (reRNA) and the transposon‐encoded transposase TnpB, c. 400 amino acids (aa) long, which is considered hypercompact compared with CRISPR/Cas‐type editors (c. 1000–1400 aa). TnpB‐based editing has been demonstrated in proof‐of‐concept studies in rice and Arabidopsis (Karmakar et al., 2024; Q. Li et al., 2024; Weiss et al., 2025).

Cas enzymes can be fused to enzymes, such as reverse‐transcriptases, integrases, and transposases to enable both editing and targeted integration of relatively large DNA segments into crop genomes. For example, an 18.8‐kb fragment was successfully integrated into the genome of rice protoplasts using the programmable chromosome engineering (PCE) system (Sun et al., 2025). Scar‐free homology‐directed recombination‐mediated knock‐in of a herbicide resistance gene into the polyubiquitin locus (Ubi7DL) was achieved in wheat using a 5′ exonuclease::Cas9 fusion (Schreiber et al., 2024). A fusion between a transposase and Cas9 or Cas12 was employed for the targeted integration of regulatory elements (e.g. enhancers, ORFs) and transcriptional units into the genomes of Arabidopsis and soybean (Glycine max) plants (Liu et al., 2024).

Furthermore, emerging technologies for targeted integration, though not yet tested in plants, have shown promising results in mammalian systems. One such method, programmable addition via site‐specific targeting elements (PASTE), combines the integrase Bxb1 from the mycobacteriophage Bxb1, the M‐MLV reverse‐transcriptase, and a Cas9 nickase to enable the insertion of large DNA fragments of up to c. 36 kb (Yarnall et al., 2023). These technologies have garnered significant attention, particularly for generating breeding lines that incorporate genes from the same or closely related species or from the breeder's gene pool.

Strategies to combine editing with breeding

Bringing an edited crop variety to market typically takes roughly 1 yr for stably edited plant generation (Yu et al., 2024), followed by 3 yr for field validation and seed multiplication (Ferrie & Polowick, 2020). Moreover, regulatory approval is required in most jurisdictions, with timelines that vary by regulatory framework.

Diseases caused by highly dynamic pathogen populations are the main drivers of germplasm replacement (Singh et al., 2025). A rapid variety turnover presents challenges for the timely release of gene‐edited products. Indeed, after selecting the best‐performing variety for genome editing, followed by the release of the edited variety, the performance of the original variety may have declined, which shortens the market lifespan of the resulting edited product due to the reduced added value of the new trait compared with newer, more competitive varieties.

To mitigate this risk, genome‐editing strategies should be introduced at either the beginning or the middle of a breeding program (Fig. 2). While editing parental lines at the start of a breeding cycle allows high‐performing new varieties carrying desirable traits to be produced, this approach requires a full breeding cycle of at least 10 yr (Ferrie & Polowick, 2020). Alternatively, editing advanced lines derived from crosses between selected parents at later stages of breeding can shorten this timeline while still allowing the release of competitively edited varieties. Such advanced lines can be selected from materials subjected to regional field trials, as these lines typically require only a few more years of testing and seed multiplication. Although this approach initially requires editing more lines, the added cost is justified by the longer market lifespan and greater commercial value of the resulting varieties.

Concluding remarks

The functional characterization of cloned wheat genes was performed using a variety of methods, thereby establishing their function; however, most of these genes were not further investigated or introduced into breeding programs to fully leverage their function. In our gene atlas, we provided a detailed list of cloned and functionally validated wheat genes, including information on their function, cloning and validation methods, and sub‐categorized them according to their involvement in major biological processes, including (1) morphology and domestication (%7), (2) physiology and metabolic processes (%17), (3) reproduction and phenology (%7.9), (4) quality and yield (%9.3), and (5) biotic and abiotic stresses (%58.8). This revealed that most of the cloned genes were involved in biotic and abiotic stress, emphasizing the emerging importance of studies in this field.

Although the number of cloned wheat genes is rapidly increasing, studies on genome editing and engineering in wheat remain limited due to the complexity of its genome. Focusing here on CRISPR‐based editing and engineering of wheat, we also provided a list of CRISPR‐edited wheat genes and explained the CDA concept as a strategy to avoid penalties in agronomic performance. For selecting target genes for editing in hexaploid wheat, we recommend the following criteria: (1) identify the target gene that modulates the manifestation of the desired trait and determine whether it functions as a positive or negative regulator; (2) characterize the target gene's copy number across the A, B, and D subgenomes; (3) assess penalties based on the degree of perturbation to decide whether to knock out a single or multiple homeologous genes or to cis‐edit the promoter, 5′ UTR, and 3′ UTR by inserting or deleting segments to enable higher or lower gene expression or translation. Ideally, generating a dosage series of edited lines would enable the identification of optimal expression levels that modulate desired agronomic traits in a polyploid background.

In conclusion, we have provided a resource and guide for gene selection in future wheat improvement studies using genome‐editing methods, and we have discussed strategies for incorporating edited wheat lines into breeding programs for rapid deployment. We consider the major challenge in next‐generation wheat breeding to be rapidly introducing multiple traits into an elite cultivar using multiplex genome editing. Editing multiple genes that control different traits simultaneously could offer a reliable path toward trait stacking. Emerging editing technologies promise precise, large‐scale modifications that could enable the targeted integration of multiple genes, creating elite cultivars that can be rapidly tailored to evolving environmental conditions and breeding goals.
