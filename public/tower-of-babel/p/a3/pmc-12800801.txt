Longitudinal Changes in Diastolic Dysfunction in Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction: Clinical and Echocardiographic Associations
Authors: Maha Al-Mohaissen, Terry Lee, Mohammad Bara Qattea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiastolic dysfunction (DD) in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) is relatively understudied, particularly regarding longitudinal changes in DD after HFrEF diagnosis and their clinical and echocardiographic associations.MethodsThis retrospective study included 360 patients with HFrEF who visited heart failure (HF) clinics in King Salman Heart Center, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, between October 2019 and January 2020. Associations between DD grades and patients’ clinical characteristics and echocardiographic parameters at diagnosis and during follow-up were evaluated.ResultsAt diagnosis, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 26.7% ( ± 7.2%), and 43.1%, 29.4%, and 27.5% of patients had grade III, II, and I DD, respectively. After 3.4 years, 65% and 57.5% of patients with grades III and II DD, respectively, improved by at least one grade. Concordant improvements in almost all diastolic parameters were observed. DD grade improvement was predicted by increased LVEF (P < 0.001), nonischemic HF etiology, and absence of myocardial infarction (P < 0.001). No associations were found with HF medications or cardiovascular risk factors. DD improvement was independently associated with improved New York Heart Association class (P = 0.008), lower B-type natriuretic peptide level (P < 0.001), and higher systolic blood pressure (P < 0.001), regardless of LVEF, age, and time since diagnosis.ConclusionsIn HFrEF, DD improves with better LVEF and absence of ischemia or infarction. DD improvement is independently associated with improved New York Heart Association class, systolic blood pressure, and B-type natriuretic peptide level, suggesting that in HFrEF, diastolic HF coexists with and parallels systolic HF severity, and contributes independently to symptoms and hemodynamics.Keywords: Diastolic dysfunction, diastolic heart failure, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, echocardiography, longitudinal changes, clinical implications

Systolic and diastolic dysfunction (DD) often coexist in patients with heart failure (HF), regardless of their ejection fraction (EF).1 In HF with reduced EF (HFrEF), DD of varying grades is considered universal.2 Evidence suggests that DD in HFrEF has prognostic3,4 and clinical relevance5; however, most studies have focused on a single diastolic parameter,4,6, 7, 8 or less commonly, DD grades in selected HFrEF populations,3 typically evaluated at a single time point.

The distribution of DD grades at HFrEF diagnosis in unselected populations, and their associations with clinical and echocardiographic characteristics, remains poorly understood. Follow-up data on changes in DD grades and related associations are also lacking. Consequently, whether diastolic abnormalities in HFrEF reflect transient filling pressure elevations or persistent dysfunction is unclear.4

In this study, we aimed to do the following: (i) evaluate the distribution of DD grades2 in patients with de novo HFrEF at diagnosis during the acute or subacute phase9; (ii) assess the longitudinal changes in DD grades; and (iii) explore the association of DD grades and their future changes with the patients’ clinical characteristics and echocardiographic variables.

Methods

Study sample

This retrospective follow-up study was conducted between April 2024 and March 2025, at King Salman Heart Centre, King Fahad Medical City, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Adult patients with ongoing or prior diagnosis of HFrEF (left ventricular [LV] ejection fraction [LVEF] ≤ 40%) who presented to HF clinics at the outpatient department between October 2019 and January 2020 were identified retrospectively through hospital records and were considered for inclusion.

The inclusion criteria included the following: (i) age ≥ 18 years at the time of HFrEF diagnosis; (ii) availability of the first transthoracic echocardiogram (ECHO1) that documented HFrEF (LVEF ≤ 40%), performed during the acute (within 30 days) or subacute (30 days to 1 year) phase9 of de novo HFrEF; and (iii) availability of the follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram (ECHO2) performed around the time of their clinic visit.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) the presence of conditions that would preclude accurate classification of DD grade on ECHO1 or ECHO2, including atrial fibrillation or flutter, tachycardia with merged early (E) and late (A) mitral inflow waves, or indeterminate or paced rhythms; (ii) moderately severe or severe mitral regurgitation (MR), moderate or severe aortic regurgitation, and stenosis, repair, clip, or replacement of any valve; (iii) congenital heart disease other than a patent foramen ovale or small atrial septal defect or bicuspid aortic valve without significant stenosis or regurgitation; (iv) moderate to severe pericardial effusion; and (v) indeterminate diastolic function attributable to inadequate criteria of the 2016 American Society of Echocardiography and European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (2016 ASE/EACVI) guidelines algorithm (Fig. 1).2 Technically difficult, technically limited, or nonretrievable transthoracic echocardiograms also were excluded.

Study timeline and collection of data

The time of study entry was defined as the date of the first clinical visit and ECHO1, whereas the time of the follow-up assessment corresponded to the date of the follow-up visit to the HF clinic and ECHO2.

Data regarding HF etiology, presence of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, prior revascularization, and medications were collected from the clinical records at the time of the follow-up visits. Conversely, the patients’ New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and heart rate (HR) were collected at the time of the initial HFrEF diagnosis (NYHA1, BMI1, SBP1, DBP1, and HR1) and during follow-up (NYHA2, BMI2, SBP2, DBP2, and HR2). Creatinine (Cr) and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels were recorded only during follow-up (Cr2 and BNP2) because of changes in the hospital’s electronic system resulting in incomplete documentation of these parameters at the time of the initial HFrEF diagnosis.

The ECHO1 and ECHO2 of each patient were reviewed and analyzed. Measurements were performed according to the recommendations for cardiac chamber quantification. The left atrial (LA) volume was determined using the area-length method. Biplane LV volumes were measured by tracing the blood-tissue interface in the apical 4- and 2-chamber views at end-diastole and end-systole. These measurements were then used to calculate the biplane LVEF. The biplane LV end-diastolic volumes (LVEDVs) were indexed to the body surface area (BSA) at the time of echocardiography to obtain the indexed biplane LVEDV (biplane LVEDV/BSA).10 Assessment of the right side of the heart was performed according to established recommendations.11 Diastolic function was classified according to the 2016 ASE/EACVI guidelines.2

Statistical analysis

All collected data were analyzed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Continuous variables are expressed as means and standard deviations (SDs) or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05. Clinical and echocardiographic variables were compared based on DD grades at the time of ECHO1 and changes in DD grades from ECHO1 to ECHO2 using analysis of variance, the Kruskal–Wallis test, the χ2 test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To identify an LVEF cutoff value that could predict improvement in DD grade, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted, and the cutoff value was determined based on the Youden J statistic. To assess the presence of trend association between DD grades at ECHO1 and other variables, the Cochran–Armitage and Jonckheere–Terpstra tests were performed for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Echocardiographic parameters assessed at ECHO1 and ECHO2 were compared using the paired t-test or the Stuart–Maxwell test, as appropriate. Given that the association between changes in DD grades at follow-up and certain variables can be confounded by age, biplane LVEF, and the time between HFrEF diagnosis and ECHO2, we conducted a logistic regression analysis to adjust for these confounders.

Overall, 360 patients met the study criteria (Fig. 2). The mean patient age at diagnosis was 51.4 years ( ± 13.1). Most of the patients (83%) were first evaluated and underwent ECHO1 during the acute phase of HFrEF. Distribution of DD grades during HFrEF diagnosis (ECHO1) and their associations with patients’ clinical characteristics and echocardiographic variables

Table 2 shows the distribution of DD grades at ECHO1 and their associations with patients’ clinical characteristics and echocardiographic variables. Most patients (43.1%) had grade III DD, whereas 29.4% and 27.5% had grade II and I DD, respectively. No significant differences in the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia were observed among patients with any DD. However, patients with grade II DD were older, more likely to be female, less likely to be smokers, and less likely to have ICMP. Worsening of the NYHA1 class was observed with increases in DD grade. Significant differences in HR1 (P = 0.003) among the 3 DD grades, with a significant upward trend (P < 0.001), were observed. Significant differences in DBP1 among the 3 groups were observed (P = 0.039), with significantly lower values in patients with grade II DD than in those with grade I DD (P = 0.015). Although a trend of decreasing SBP1 was observed, it was not statistically significant (P = 0.068). However, patients with grade III DD had significantly lower SBP1 values than patients with grade II DD (P = 0.034).

Regarding the echocardiographic variables, an increase in DD grade correlated with decreasing LVEF1, increasing LVEDV1, worsening of MR1, rising right atrial (RA) pressure1, and worsening of right ventricle (RV) function1 (P for trend < 0.001). Among the assessed diastolic parameters, an increase in DD grade was associated with significant upward trends in E1 wave velocity, E/A1 ratio, E to early diastolic mitral annular tissue velocity (E/e’1) ratio, and peak velocity of the tricuspid regurgitation (TR) jet (TR jet velocity1). Conversely, downward trends in A1 wave velocity and septal early diastolic mitral annular (septal é1) velocity with increasing DD grade were noted (P for trend < 0.001). Although lateral é1 and average é1 differed significantly between patients with varying DD grade, significant trends were not observed.

Differences in DD grades and echocardiographic variables between ECHO1 and ECHO2

Table 3 shows the results of ECHO1 and ECHO2. Overall, significant improvements in DD grades (P < 0.001) and all diastolic parameters were noted, except for septal é, for which the improvement was not statistically significant, possibly because of the inherently lower velocities of septal é. Additionally, significant improvements in the LVEDV/BSA, LVEF, RV function, RVSP, RA pressure, TR and MR severity, and detectable LV thrombus were observed.

Predictors and associations of changes in DD grades during follow-up

Associations between changes in DD grades and changes in LVEF are shown in Table 5. Changes in DD grades closely paralleled changes in LVEF (P < 0.001). Improvements in LVEF (mean [SD] 16.4% [13.0]) was correlated with improvements in DD grades, whereas worsening LVEF (–1.8% [8.2]) was correlated with worsening DD grades. A smaller increase in LVEF (5.4% [10.1]) was correlated with DD stability. A future LVEF increase of 9.38% was the optimal cutoff for predicting improvement in the DD grade, with a sensitivity of 75%, specificity of 68%, positive predictive value of 73%, and negative predictive value of 71%, and an area under the ROC curve of 0.78.

Other predictors and associations with longitudinal changes in DD grades are presented in Table 6. Patients with nonischemic cardiomyopathy (NICMP) and those without documented MI were more likely to exhibit improvement in DD grades than their counterparts (P < 0.001). Prior revascularization, either by percutaneous coronary intervention (P < 0.001) or coronary artery bypass grafting (P = 0.011), was not associated with improvements in the DD grade. In fact, these patients were more likely to experience persistent or worsening DD, likely because of the underlying ischemic etiology. No significant association was observed between age, sex, BMI, history of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking status, heart failure medications, or cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) and changes in DD grades. However, the number of patients may be inadequate for some of these comparisons.

Discussion

The present study showed that DD is not a fixed condition in HFrEF but rather a modifiable process that often improved alongside systolic function, and more so in patients with nonischemic HF and no history of MI. An important observation from this study is that improvement in DD in patients with HFrEF was associated with improved NYHA class, BNP levels, and SBP, independent of changes in LVEF, age, and time since HFrEF diagnosis. This finding indicates that DD in HFrEF is a diastolic HF that parallels the severity of systolic HF and contributes independently to the patients’ symptoms and hemodynamics. In addition, we observed no direct association between HFrEF medications and changes in DD, similar to findings in prior reports by other investigators,3 nor between CVD risk factors and DD. These findings call for additional therapeutic interventions that target the main contributors to DD in patients with HFrEF, particularly ischemia and fibrosis. Our study may advance the ongoing dialogue around precision medicine and further phenotypic characterization of patients with HF as the field shifts toward more-personalized approaches in diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis.

Studies on DD in HFrEF

Studies investigating DD in HFrEF are limited and are focused mainly on its prognostic implications.3,4,6,7 Additionally, most of these studies evaluated a single diastolic parameter only, commonly E/A6,7 or E/é.4,8 In a large study involving patients with HF and LVEF < 50%, Benfari et al. assessed the association between E/é and the patients’ clinical and echocardiographic characteristics at the time of diagnosis. A higher E/é ratio was associated with more severe HF at diagnosis, including a worse NYHA class, lower LVEF, greater LV diameter, worse MR, worse RV function, and higher pulmonary artery pressure. Additionally, increased E/é was associated with increased mortality, independent of all presenting characteristics.4 However, reliance on a single diastolic parameter has limitations,1,12, 13, 14 including the modest correlations of individual parameters with LV filling pressure,14 which limits their usefulness for diagnosing increased LV end diastolic pressure or DD.8,12,15

Evaluation of several echocardiographic parameters is essential for accurately classifying diastolic function.14 According to the 2016 ASE/EACVI guidelines, an algorithm that incorporates combinations of mitral flow velocities, é velocity, E/é ratio, TR jet velocity, and the LA volume index is required to evaluate and classify DD in grades.2 Only one study has evaluated DD grades in patients with HFrEF; however, this study focused on the prognostic implications, evaluated DD grades at a single time point, and did not assess their longitudinal changes.3 Our study is unique because it evaluated the changes in the DD grade over time and explored the predictors of and associations with these changes.

Echocardiographic associations and predictors of DD in HFrEF

We observed a strong direct association between LVEF and DD at the time of HFrEF diagnosis and during follow-up. The worsening of DD with declining LVEF can be attributed to the reduction in restoring forces as a result of impaired contractility.13 These restoring forces, which are generated during ventricular contraction and released with relaxation, contribute to the lowering of LV pressure during diastole. As they are generated during systole, their magnitude is determined by systolic function.16 Additionally, myocardial injury, whether acute17 or chronic,18 besides causing systolic dysfunction,17,18 leads to impaired LV relaxation17 and increased stiffness,18 which are key contributors to DD.13 The associations of increased TR jet velocity and RA pressure with DD are in accordance with previous reports of an independent association between DD and pulmonary hypertension.5 Similarly, the associations of RV dysfunction and functional MR with DD may be attributed to DD alone or the concurrent systolic dysfunction that accompanies DD.19,20

Regarding diastolic parameters, at the time of HFrEF diagnosis, we observed significant trends for increasing E, E/A ratio, E/é, RA pressure, and TR jet velocity with increasing DD grade, with significant improvement in these parameters with improved diastolic grade during follow-up, which confirms the concordance of individual diastolic parameters with DD grades,3,14 despite their inadequate accuracies for DD diagnosis.14 A significant trend for decreasing septal é values, but not lateral é, with worsening DD grade at diagnosis, was also noted. During follow-up, only increases in septal é values were associated with improved DD. This correlation is anticipated, as septal é is a better indicator of longitudinal LV relaxation and DD than lateral or average é, and it has a superior diagnostic value for DD.21

Echocardiographic algorithms show a good correlation with the invasive assessment of DD.14 Although guidelines recommend using E/A to assess diastolic function and E/e’ to estimate the LV filling pressure,15 our findings of improved E/A and E/é, alongside improved DD grades during follow-up, and the persistence or worsening of DD in patients with ICMP and prior MI, suggest that DD at the time of HFrEF diagnosis reflects elevated filling pressures in the acute phase and a distinct myocardial dysfunction that may improve, persist, or worsen.

Clinical implications of DD in HFrEF

At HFrEF diagnosis, we observed a higher prevalence of ICMP among patients with grades I and III DD than among those with grade II DD. However, patients in the grade I and III DD groups were more commonly male and more commonly smokers, which may have led to the higher prevalence of ICMP among them. Additionally, the severity of DD in ICMP is not fixed, and it varies according to infarct size and extent of scarring.22,23 Our findings regarding the associations of female sex, BMI, and LVEF with DD grades at HFrEF diagnosis are in accordance with findings in previous reports.3 Overall, a worse DD grade at the time of HFrEF diagnosis was associated with a worse NYHA class, lower DBP1, and higher HR1. Although a trend for lower SBP1 levels was also detected, it was not statistically significant, which may be attributed to the concomitant use of inotropes in patients during the acute phase.

During follow-up, we observed a higher likelihood for DD improvement in patients with NICMP, and persistence or worsening of DD in patients with ICMP and prior MI, which was not improved by revascularization. This finding parallels the reported worsening of LVEF, NYHA class, and BNP levels in treated patients with ICMP during follow-up,24 and it is likely a result of the extensive negative impact that ischemia exerts on LV function. Both persistent ischemia and its complications, including hibernating myocardium, interstitial fibrosis,25 and scar tissue formation,26 are associated with persistence of DD following revascularization.25 Although recovery of LVEF and systolic function improves energy storage in systole, which is an early physiological contributor to diastole, persistent ischemia and scarring hinder several other diastolic components beyond energy storage, including impairment of LV untwisting and reduction in compliance and relaxation.15 Therefore, changes in LVEF and presence of ICMP should be regarded as separate precipitants of DD in individuals with HFrEF.

No significant associations between the presence of diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia and changes in DD grade at follow-up were detected. This finding suggests that in HFrEF, the mechanisms of coexistent DD are different from those associated with HF with preserved EF (HFpEF). In HFrEF, ischemia and its complications prevail, while in HFpEF, metabolic CVD risk factors dominate.27 The lack of significant associations between HFrEF medications and DD grades has been reported previously by Gori et al. in an analysis involving 1155 patients from the Multicenter Automatic Defibrillator Implantation Trial With Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (MADIT-CRT).3 Additionally, the presence of severe DD in HFrEF reduces the efficacy of CRT in improving LV remodelling and systolic function.28 The lack of association between guideline-directed medical therapy and future improvement in DD in HFrEF aligns with the known therapeutic challenges posed by HFpEF27 and ICMP phenotypes.24,29, 30, 31 Additional novel therapies that target DD, particularly in ICMP,25 are needed. Therapies aimed at other mechanisms of myocardial injury and ischemia beyond the establishment of epicardial coronary artery patency, including the signaling pathways of myocardial fibrosis,32 reperfusion injury,33 inflammation,34 and residual ischemia at the microvascular35 and cellular levels,36 are warranted. In addition, increased implementation of cardiac magnetic resonance and positron emission tomography for tissue characterization26 in patients with DD, regardless of etiology, may be useful for both the risk stratification and management of these patients.

The observed increases in SBP with improvement in DD can be attributed to the improvement in cardiac output that accompanies improvement of DD, which is independent of LVEF changes.37 This finding is consistent with the reported lower BP values in patients with HFrEF with worse DD.3 Similarly, the improvement of NYHA class with improved DD is in line with its association with the E/é ratio at the time of HFrEF diagnosis.4 However, follow-up data regarding future changes in symptoms or hemodynamic parameters, associated with changes in the E/é ratio, are not available. To the best of our knowledge, associations between increased BNP levels and worsening DD grade in HFrEF have been reported in only one study.3

The potential coexistence of HF with “preserved” EF and HFrEF has been debated.27 As LVEF cannot be concomitantly preserved and reduced,27 the term “diastolic dysfunction with HFrEF” is used to describe this overlap.38 Our findings support the use of a more specific term—HFrEF with diastolic HF (HFrEF-dHF)—because of the independent contribution of DD in HFrEF in worsening HF symptoms and hemodynamics.

Study limitations

The main limitation of this study is its retrospective design, which may have led to variability in follow-up duration, patient loss to follow-up, and incomplete laboratory data. Excluding patients with indeterminate diastolic function or unclassifiable DD grades also reduced the sample size and limited conclusions regarding the impact of HFrEF medications on DD progression. However, similar findings have been reported in larger studies.3 To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to shed light on the longitudinal changes in DD and its associations in patients with HFrEF; however, further prospective studies are warranted to investigate this important phenotype.

Conclusions

DD parallels systolic dysfunction in the acute and subacute phases of HFrEF and may improve, persist, or worsen over time. Improvement in DD in HFrEF is governed by improvement in LVEF and the absence of ischemia or infarction, with no significant association detected with HFrEF medications or metabolic risk factors. An important finding from this study is that improvement in DD in HFrEF was associated with improvements in the NYHA class, SBP, and BNP levels, independent of changes in LVEF, suggesting that diastolic HF coexists with systolic HF and independently contributes to the symptoms and hemodynamic status of patients with HFrEF.
