Contrast-Enhanced Mammography for High-Risk Women: Implications for Latin America and Developing Regions
Authors: Gonzalo Andrés Montaño Rozo, Javier Alejandro Lamprea Ardila, María José Gutierrez Sierra, Gloria Inés Palazuelos Jiménez, Javier Andrés Romero
Published: 2026

In Latin America, as in the rest of the developing world, contrast-enhanced mammography (CEM) is an emerging technique with the potential to be an adjunctive study in the screening of women at high risk for breast cancer. Having read the comprehensive review by Nissan and Sung [1], we wish to share our perspective, supported by evidence-based data.

Breast density, which has gained worldwide importance due to its high prevalence, increases the risk of cancer [2]. Approximately 43% of women aged 40–74 years in the United States have dense breast tissue [3]. Due to their higher risk, this group of women needs to undergo additional screening procedures for the early detection of breast cancer. In fact, the sensitivity of mammography decreases as breast density increases, from 93% in fatty breasts to 30% in extremely dense breasts [3].

Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides the most robust data and is superior to other modalities for cancer detection in women with dense breasts, CEM demonstrates a similar performance [4]. The primary disadvantage of the MRI, particularly in our region, is its high cost. A study published by the Society of Breast Imaging analyzed the cost of performing breast imaging using a time-driven activity-based costing method in a medical center in the United States. The results demonstrated that the most expensive imaging modality for breast screening is MRI, exceeding the value of CEM by almost two-fold [5].

Additionally, MRI is a sophisticated technique that requires additional training for radiologists in comparison to CEM. Based on our experience, many surgeons, gynecologists, and general practitioners prefer CEM, given their greater familiarity with mammography.

Since the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved CEM in 2011, significant advancements have been made in this field. Incremental cancer detection rates ranging from 8.6 to 13.1 per 1,000 patients have been reported with supplemental screening following negative 2D mammography, based on the findings of Berg et al. [6]. In another study conducted by Mayo Clinic in Arizona in 2023, during the prevalence round, the technique achieved an incremental cancer detection rate of 23.9 per 1,000 patients, highlighting the potential of this method in high-risk populations [7].

Another determining factor is patient’s preference for CEM in comparison to MRI. This was demonstrated by a survey-type study in patients who underwent both modalities of examination in 2022. The results revealed that 70% of patients preferred CEM [8].

Multicenter studies with larger populations will be needed in Latin America to validate these results. Particularly in developing countries, CEM represents an alternative for the supplementary screening of patients with dense breasts and a high risk of breast cancer.
