Implementation strategies to improve adoption of screening and linkages for non-medical drivers of health in care management using enabling technologies: study protocol for a cluster randomised trial
Authors: Nicole Cook, Rose Gunn, Brenda M McGrath, Jenna Donovan, Maura Pisciotta, Constance Owens-Jasey, Hannah L Fein, Anna Templeton, Zoe Larson, Rachel Gold
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPractice guidelines recommend addressing patient non-medical drivers of health such as access to nutritious food and transportation as part of whole-person care. Emergent electronic health record (EHR)-based tools can enable non-medical needs care coordination, but adoption commonly faces workflow and infrastructure barriers. Targeted implementation support strategies (eg, training, practice facilitation) can enhance technology adoption in healthcare settings, but no prior research has assessed if implementation strategies can improve how care managers use enabling technologies for non-medical needs care coordination. This study will test whether providing implementation support to primary care health centre care management teams improves the adoption of EHR-based enabling technologies to address patients’ non-medical needs.Methods and analysisThis hybrid implementation-effectiveness type 2 pragmatic trial has a mixed methods design. The primary outcomes include: (1) Whether patients enrolled in care management programmes have been screened for unmet non-medical health-related needs and (2) Whether patients with identified unmet non-medical health-related needs received a referral to a community organisation to address their need. The secondary outcomes include: (1) Whether referrals for financial-related non-medical needs had a documented outcome in the EHR, such as successful connection to services, service unavailability or other disposition statuses, (2) Whether the referral outcomes indicated ‘successful connection to services’ and (3) Clinical markers including hypertension and diabetes control. Formative evaluation of barriers and facilitators to using EHR tools to conduct non-medical needs screening, referrals and tracking of receipt of services will include semi-structured interviews and a ‘guided tour’ of enabling technology used by care managers. A modified Delphi process will then inform the development of a set of implementation strategies for inclusion in the intervention. The intervention will be piloted in three health centres, refined, then tested in a stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial in 20 health centres.Ethics and disseminationWe obtained ethics approval for all study activities from Advarra Institutional Review Board (registration number #00000971). Results will be disseminated to Health Centres and Health Centre network nationally at meetings and we will disseminate to researchers via manuscripts in peer-reviewed journals and scientific meetings.Trial registration numberNCT06489002.

Introduction

Decades of research show that non-medical drivers of health are disproportionately prevalent in low-income populations served by the nation’s healthcare safety net. Patients’ with unmet non-medical needs, including food, housing, financial and transportation, contribute to sociocultural and contextual barriers to chronic disease management13 and thus to differences in health outcomes.35

A growing body of evidence supports the potential benefits of healthcare providers referring patients to community organisations to address their unmet non-medical needs,69 and numerous experts recommend addressing non-medical needs in this way as part of comprehensive care for prevalent chronic diseases like hypertension and diabetes.1012 Adoption of such ‘non-medical needs-targeted care’ activities is further encouraged through emergent value-based payment models which include non-medical needs care activities such as screening, referring and coordinating services as part of comprehensive, high-quality care delivery.1318 Care management and population health programmes (referred to hereafter as ‘CM’) are two value-based payment model strategies that support screening of non-medical needs and linking patients to community organisations to address identified needs as part of individual, whole person care plans for patients who are at high risk of poor health outcomes and/or medically complex (eg, Medicare’s Chronic Care Management (CCM) programme and the California Advancing and Innovating Medi-Cal (3-4) (CalAIM) programmes).9 19 20

Such clinical-community linkage activities are increasingly supported by ‘enabling technologies’ such as electronic health record (EHR)-based care coordination applications and care management software products. For example, Compass Rose (CR), an application integrated within the Epic EHR, is designed to support comprehensive care coordination and high-touch care management activities including individualised care plans, facilitating transitions of care and care team communication.21 22 CR features also enable and support non-medical drivers of healthcare coordination, including assessing patients’ non-medical needs, referring patients with unmet non-medical needs to community organisations, and tracking referral outcomes. While these new technological tools have great promise for supporting clinical-community linkages, little is known about effective strategies for optimising their systematic and sustained adoption, especially in safety-net clinical care settings serving complex patients.8 23 Developing effective strategies for supporting clinical-community linkages requires understanding the barriers to using enabling technologies in these care settings and testing strategies designed by health centre and community partners to address these barriers. One known barrier is the up-front investment in workflow redesign and technology infrastructure needed to use these technologies to facilitate bi-directional referrals.24 Few care management and population health reimbursement mechanisms cover these investments, which is a barrier to under-resourced primary care teams’ ability to adopt enabling technologies that could support clinical-community linkages.

Evidence from the implementation of other care management programmes2529 and health information technologies (HIT)30 31 may inform operationalising the adoption of such enabling technologies. Evidence-based implementation strategies shown to advance the adoption of other technologies in health centres include leadership support,32 staff training,3336 local champions,3741 practice facilitation42 and audit and feedback.2529 Applying these strategies effectively requires consideration of the specific needs and context of different health centres as well as barriers specific to the adoption of enabling technologies to support clinical-community linkages.252843 44 It is critical to engage health centre users as partners to guide the development of a set of evidence-based implementation strategies to address such barriers. A structured approach to interactively fine-tuning implementation strategies is needed to develop an implementation support intervention that can then be formally tested to generate evidence for replication in health centres using any EHR system and enabling technologies.

While this study is situated within a US-based network of community health centres using a centrally hosted version of the Epic EHR, challenges related to the adoption of enabling technologies for non-medical drivers of health screening and care coordination are universal.45 For example, in the UK, efforts to integrate health and social care have faced challenges in record-sharing and data interoperability.46 To support broader applicability from this study, findings will be disseminated in an EHR-agnostic format, emphasising implementation strategies that can be adapted across diverse health system and EHR contexts.

This study protocol describes a National Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded study called DEDICATE: advancing care management adoption in community health centres (R01NR021115). The study will develop and evaluate the effectiveness of implementation strategies designed to support care management teams’ sustained use of EHR-based applications and functionalities that support clinical-community linkages. It will test the hypothesis that health centre partners’ adapted implementation strategies applied to enabling technologies will improve the adoption of these technologies and thus improve screening, community referrals and receipt of services by patients with unmet non-medical needs. The study’s overarching goal is to improve clinical-community linkages through care management and care coordination programmes that serve high-risk patients with complex health and/or unmet non-medical needs.

Methods and analysis

Setting

This study will be conducted among health centres that are members of OCHIN, a multi-state network of community health organisations that serve more than 6 million people in rural and low-income communities nationwide. OCHIN is a national non-profit health IT consultancy that provides a fully hosted and shared EHR platform with tailored tools and expertise, including practice management tools, to >2400 care delivery sites across 40 states. While all OCHIN members share a single instance of the Epic EHR, each is an independent organization with its own governing board. Conducting this study in a diverse group of health centres improves the likelihood that its results will be generalizable to other primary care settings, an important feature of pragmatic trials.

OCHIN Epic includes a robust suite of tools designed to support collecting and acting on non-medical drivers of health data, many developed through NIH-funded studies.4753 The CR tool, which is specific to Epic and includes functionalities to support assessing patients’ non-medical needs and making and tracking referrals, was made available to OCHIN health centres in 2022. In early 2024, an estimated <10% of OCHIN health centres use CR to make referrals and track referral outcomes.

OCHIN health centres will be eligible for the trial if they have activated the CR tools in their EHR, participate in a CM programme requiring non-medical drivers of healthcare coordination and have more than 10 active patients enrolled in such a programme during the April–June 2025 period. Eligible programmes include the Centres for Medicare and Medicaid Services’ (CMS) Chronic Care Management (CCM), Principal Care Management (PCM), transitional care management, Cal-AIM enhanced care management and high-risk care management. There are 78 OCHIN health centres that meet the inclusion criteria.

Conceptual model

The study will test a suite of implementation support strategies (training, champion development, practice facilitation and audit and feedback). They will be refined in the study’s formative phase, per guidance from health centre partners and technology subject matter experts, to optimise how they support the provision of clinical-community linkages by CM teams. Intervention development and study outcomes are guided by the Integrated Technology Implementation Model (ITIM)54 55 which considers facilitators and barriers to technology adoption and applies quality improvement methods to facilitate and sustain adoption. Its intended use is to support a deep understanding of the interplay of internal and external concepts important to implementing enabling technologies (such as the CR application). This knowledge will support the selection and iterative refinement of the implementation strategies comprising the study intervention. Study concepts mapped to ITIM concepts are outlined in table 1.

Formative intervention refinement

The planned key components of the intervention will include 12 weeks of evidence-based implementation support strategies provided by an implementation support team comprised of practice facilitators (also called practice coaches) who support transformation and change activities within an organisation and study team staff with expertise in quality improvement, non-medical drivers of health, and EHR workflows and optimisation. Expected support strategies include training, championship, practice facilitation and audit and feedback; these were selected based on evidence that they support the adoption of similar HIT tools.

A formative mixed methods approach will focus on understanding how individual, health centre and environmental factors influence care team use of enabling technologies to support non-medical care coordination activities, and how the implementation strategies to be tested can optimally promote such technologies’ adoption. To ensure a comprehensive understanding of CR utilisation for non-medical care coordination activities, we will first review OCHIN network data. These data will be extracted from the OCHIN Epic EHR and used to quantify the number of patients at each OCHIN health centre who were enrolled in specific CM programmes through CR. Health centre patient volume, non-medical health-related needs screening rates, number of CR patients with screens, and procedure codes associated with billing for CMS care management programmes will be extracted as a potential way of assessing care management occurring in these health centres.

The study team will collaboratively identify and recruit four health centres based on variation in CR utilisation, screening for non-medical drivers of health and geographic characteristics. One care management staff member from each health centre will participate in a 1 hour semi-structured interview. Informed by prevalent human factors engineering and human-centred design principles, interviews will include a ‘guided tour’ of CR and other related EHR tools to understand how enabling technologies are currently used to support non-medical needs activities.

Concurrently, the study team will conduct semi-structured interviews with five OCHIN subject matter expert staff, including practice coaches, population health leaders and clinical informatics experts. The staff in these roles work with health centres to support the adoption of workflows and technologies to facilitate care for non-medical needs. These care manager and subject matter expert interviews are intended to provide information about how OCHIN member health centres currently use enabling technologies for non-medical drivers of health screening and clinical-community linkages, and to identify facilitators and barriers to the adoption and use of these technologies.

The study team will also seek feedback via rapid, online surveys (<5 questions) from hundreds of OCHIN member health centre clinical, administrative and population health users who attend standing workgroup and committee meetings. Results from these rapid surveys will further the study team’s understanding of facilitators and barriers in clinics’ adoption of CR and other EHR-based CM tools.

Quantitative and qualitative data collected during formative activities will be integrated and examined using a convergent triangulation design, guided by the study’s conceptual frameworks. Qualitative data analysis will use a rapid analytic approach to identify contextual barriers and facilitators related to the adoption of CR and other EHR tools for non-medical drivers of health screening and referrals. This approach supports the rapid identification of key insights into the applicability and feasibility of implementation strategies that will be further iterated through a modified Delphi process.27 59

From this foundational assessment, we will conduct a modified Delphi process to obtain community partner consensus on the operationalisation of related implementation strategies in study health centres. Participants will include health centre staff from the four health centres participating in the formative phase described above, and additional health centre staff that express interest following presentations at relevant OCHIN member workgroups and committees. Participants will also include OCHIN subject matter experts in practice facilitation, clinical informatics, population health and learning content development.

The modified Delphi process will include three sequential rounds of virtual surveys administered via REDCap with statements on the potential implementation strategies’ content, format for delivery and cadence, with multiple-choice responses and a free text option. After each survey round, members of the research team will analyse results and remove or revise statements that did not receive consensus agreement. Data from each round will be summarised in an easily interpretable format and given to all modified Delphi participants before the next round. Following round three, the proposed implementation strategies will be shared with participants with an opportunity for comment, after which the study team will finalise the first intervention iteration.

Next, we will further refine the intervention by pilot-testing it in three health centres. We will recruit health centres that (1) Used any CR functions for an average of 15 patients or more per month, (2) Participate in at least one care management or care coordination programme that requires screening and referrals for unmet non-medical drivers of health and (3) Express interest in providing feedback on application of CR to support clinical-community organisation linkages. We will conduct a baseline survey with health centre care management/population health leadership to assess readiness, context, barriers and external factors related to care coordination using enabling technology. The survey’s open-ended and closed-ended questions will be informed by the ITIM framework and administered via REDCap.

The intervention pilot will involve delivering the implementation strategies over 12 weeks to health centre CM teams. During this period, the study team will collect qualitative data through periodic reflections with the health centres’ implementation teams during regularly scheduled check-ins. Periodic reflections are guided discussions informed by ethnographic methods and will be conducted twice during the intervention period at weeks 4 and 8. We anticipate that each health centre will implement site-specific adaptations to the intervention to increase its effectiveness within the local context. Data collected will provide detailed, systematically captured information on intervention activities, implementation settings, contextual factors, adaptations to the proposed intervention and implementation team sense-making and learning. Postintervention semi-structured interviews with two to three clinic staff per health centre will also be conducted to understand experiences with intervention implementation, recommendations for future implementations and potential implementation strategy support adaptations that should be considered.

These formative activities will engage with health centre partners to support a deep understanding of barriers to the adoption of enabling technologies and identify which support strategies are most likely to address such barriers and the specifics of how they should be implemented (eg, the cadence, content, format and scope of audit and feedback reports and care management team training). Ongoing engagement will identify the most acceptable approach to providing this implementation support to health centres and be used to further refine the implementation strategy intervention. If results indicate the need to consider implementation strategies other than those proposed, the team will consider using them if feasible. The intervention to be tested in the study’s trial phase will comprise the final set of implementation strategies developed through this process.

Main trial study design

Following the formative intervention development described above, a stepped-wedge cluster randomised trial (SW-CRT) will be conducted across 20 OCHIN member health centres, with health centres serving as the clusters. A consolidated standards of reporting trials flow diagram outlining the randomisation and intervention allocation is provided in figure 2.60 In this trial, the CM teams within each health centre will undergo a 12-week implementation support intervention. The stepped-wedge design enables the phased delivery of the intervention to small groups of health centres (five per wedge; four wedges). This approach optimises study resources while maintaining methodological rigour, leveraging the higher statistical power in this design compared with other CRT designs.486163 It also ensures that all participating health centres eventually receive the intervention, potentially enhancing study recruitment.

Consolidated standards of reporting trials SW-CRT flowchart diagram. HC, Health centre; SW-CRT, Stepped-wedge cluster randomised trial.

The 20 recruited health centres will be randomly assigned to one of four wedges using a random number generator independently by the study’s statistician for staggered receipt of the intervention. The statistician will have no direct interaction with the health centres and will not be involved in the recruitment of these health centres into the study. Recruitment efforts will be led by the study’s Project Manager and Project Director. These health centres will initially start in the control condition, with each wedge sequentially transitioning (per randomisation) to the intervention condition every 3 months. This process continues until all health centres receive the full 12-week intervention (online supplemental table 1). Before receiving the intervention, all study health centres will contribute at least 6 months of baseline data. After receiving the intervention, all study health centres will continue to be followed until the end of the main trial period, which will span from approximately October 2025 (start of baseline data collection) to June 2027 (end of follow-up for all wedges). The intervention will be delivered to health centres from April 2026 to March 2027. The final wedge to receive implementation support will be followed for 3 months in the intervention condition. Participating health centres will receive an impact payment for their involvement.

Outcomes

This hybrid implementation-effectiveness type 2 pragmatic trial has a mixed methods design. The primary outcomes include: (1) Whether patients enrolled in care management programmes have been screened for unmet non-medical health-related needs and (2) Whether patients with identified unmet non-medical health-related needs (eg, food insecurity, housing, transportation, utilities) received a referral to a community organisation to address their identified non-medical need. The secondary outcomes include (1) Whether referrals for unmet non-medical health-related needs had any documented outcome in the EHR (eg, successful connection to services, service unavailability, or other disposition statuses), (2) Whether the documented outcome of the referrals specifically indicated ‘successful connection to services’ and (3) Clinical outcomes including controlled hypertension and controlled diabetes. Controlled hypertension is defined as having blood pressure <140/90 mm Hg at their most recent visit and controlled diabetes is defined as having HbA1c ≤9.0 percent at their most recent visit. These thresholds align with the Health Centre Programme uniform data system clinical quality measures used for annual federal reporting and serve as standardised benchmarks for evaluating population-level outcomes in Federally-qualified Health Centres.64 Patients with hypertension and/or diabetes who had a visit but no HbA1c measurement or blood pressure measurement will be classified as having ‘uncontrolled’ disease.64 Sub-analyses will focus specifically on food insecurity, the non-medical need most commonly screened for and identified across participating health centres, to explore referral patterns and outcomes in greater detail. Inclusion criteria

The trial’s health centres will be recruited from OCHIN members that use CR for at least one care management task that includes screening for non-medical drivers of health and care coordination to address unmet non-medical needs for >10 enrolled patients in a 3-month period (April–June 2025). Trial centres must also have care management staff to support programming and provide primary care. Although a health centre may use multiple care management programmes, only one programme will be included from each health centre due to the complexity of providing implementation support for multiple care teams within a single health centre. Patients at the participating health centres will be included in analyses if they are enrolled in the included CR programme with non-medical drivers of health activities at a given measurement point.

Data

All quantitative data will be extracted from the OCHIN EHR, including clinical-community care coordination activities performed by care management/population health providers using CR, and patients’ demographic, medical, laboratory and non-medical drivers of health data.65 66 To optimise our ability to extract structured/unstructured data from the OCHIN Epic EHR, a key task in the first year of the study is to develop and validate our data extraction methodology. We have extensive experience conducting similar Epic-based data extraction in multiple prior studies. Qualitative data will come from periodic reflections and interviews with CM staff. Using similar methods as applied in formative activities, periodic reflections will be conducted at intervention period weeks 4 and 8. Semi-structured interviews with one key informant (eg, care manager, health centre champion) per health centre will be conducted three to 6 months postintervention.

Analyses

Primary outcomes will be measured at baseline and at the end of each of the four wedges’ receipt of the intervention during the CRT. At each time point, all active CM patients receiving care management in a CR programme that incorporates non-medical drivers of health activities (eg, CalAIM, CCM) will be included. The analyses use an open cohort design, as some patients will be measured across multiple time points and others only once. As the main outcomes are binary and patients are nested in health centres, we will use generalised linear mixed models with a binomial distribution and a logit link and include a random effect for the health centre. Patient-level covariates will include age, race/ethnicity, gender, federal poverty level, insurance type, care management programme (eg, CCM, CalAIM), and a health centre-level indicator of treatment arm (intervention or control) for each time point.

Qualitative data collected during and after the trial will be uploaded to NVivo QRS for data management and analysis. Analysis will be conducted following an immersion-crystallisation approach, which entails multiple iterations of data immersion and analytic reflection to identify patterns in the data. First, the qualitative team will meet weekly to read periodic reflections and interview transcripts to develop inductive codes. As the identification of new codes slows and the team reaches agreement on code definition and appropriate application, the team will begin coding outside of weekly meetings. Continuing to meet to discuss potential revisions to codes and their definitions, the team prepares for in-depth analysis of queried codes. Once coding is complete, code queries will be generated for further analysis. In this step, reports from the queried codes are divided among team members for immersive analysis. The team will meet weekly to share content from analytic memos, outliers in the data, and begin to identify prospective themes. During this process, the qualitative team will bring emerging themes to the larger study team for further discussion, refinement and sense-making. This process, which further strengthens analytic validity and rigour, will occur until interpretation saturation is reached.67 68 Informed by the 2019 National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine report on integrating non-medical drivers of health and medical care,8 our analysis will focus on understanding how individual, health centre and environmental factors interact to influence care management team use of CR to screen and refer for non-medical needs and coordinate clinical-community linkages, particularly among groups that have been economically marginalised. We will highlight factors related to intervention outcomes (eg, reach, adoption, effectiveness) and generalisability of the implementation strategies and associated adjustments across health centre contexts.

Sample size calculation

We assessed power616971 to detect differences in the percentage of patients with non-medical needs who are referred to community organisations, which is a primary outcome of this study. We used a two-sided significance test with α=0.05. The study’s pre-specified total sample size is 20 health centres with a stepped-wedge open cohort design72 with four wedges each of five health centres (clusters). Based on preliminary data of high-acuity patients (defined as three or more chronic diseases on the Multimorbidity Weighted index7375 and a completed non-medical drivers of health screen), the expected number of eligible patients is 163 per health centre. This number represents the denominator for the primary outcome and the number of patients in each cluster. The proportion of patients with non-medical needs who are referred to services (ie, the numerator) varies widely, ranging from 10%76 to 86% in the literature.24 Therefore, we assume a baseline referral rate of 48%, which is the midpoint between the ranges. We also assume that the intervention would improve clinical-community linkages to between 63.3%77 and 72%23 based on other studies. To determine the power of this trial, we examined the absolute differences in screening rates for a range of intraclass correlations, which quantifies the degree of similarity in outcomes within each cluster (health centre). We conservatively assumed a cluster autocorrelation and the individual autocorrelation of 0, as there are no sufficient data-driven estimates of these measures,78 and a block exchangeable decay structure. The cluster autocorrelation refers to the correlation between the population means from the same group at two different periods, while the individual autocorrelation is the correlation between the outcome variable for the same individual at two different periods.79 If the intervention improves screening rates to 63.3%, as observed in Lian et al then we would have >80% power to detect this difference with an intraclass correlation as high as 0.15. If the intervention improves screening rates to 72% as observed in Richman et al, we would have >80% to detect this difference with an intraclass correlation as high as 0.30. Power was calculated using the Shiny app80 for the swCRTdesign81 package in R.

Patient and public involvement

A patient advisor, who is a licensed practical nurse, and a provider advisor, who is a Nurse Practitioner and a Chief Information Officer from a health centre, actively participate in the design of the intervention, instrument development, and will inform interpretation of results.

Discussion and limitations

Although non-medical drivers of health are an important consideration for high-risk, medically complex care managed populations, evidence suggests that screening for non-medical needs and providing linkages to community organisations to address identified patient unmet non-medical needs can be hampered by barriers to enabling technologies. As health centres serving low-income populations increase their capacity for documenting and addressing patients’ non-medical needs, it is imperative to identify implementation strategies that support the adoption of enabling technologies and workflows to advance effective clinical-community linkages. While little is known about how to support implementation of technologies specifically focused on clinical-community linkages, evidence is available on implementation strategies that are effective in promoting the adoption of other EHR-based tools. Refining these implementation strategies with health centre input is expected to support improving the intervention’s ability to conduct non-medical drivers of health screening and place needed referrals among patients with unmet needs. This is time-sensitive, as chronic disease guidelines and value-based payment models are increasingly stipulating screening, referring and supporting receipt of services for patients’ non-medical needs. To our knowledge, this is the first study to test a set of implementation strategies to improve clinical-community linkages among safety-net primary care health centres. Results will be disseminated to guide primary care clinics on using implementation strategies to further adoption of enabling technologies targeting care for non-medical needs via clinical-community linkages.

Limitations and considerations

This pragmatic trial will be conducted with a focus on one enabling technology, CR, an application in the Epic EHR. To our knowledge, CR is the first instance of a seamlessly integrated EHR module designed to meet the unique, complex needs of care coordinators providing care to patients within primary care health centre settings. As care for non-medical drivers of health is advanced through care guidelines and payors, it would be expected that additional applications will be developed in other EHR systems. To address this, disseminated study findings will be presented in an EHR-agnostic manner. Additionally, the study design does not directly address other known factors that influence clinical-community linkages including funding, infrastructure, staff resources, local documentation practices, local organisational and implementation capacity, and patient preferences. Our team will work closely with health centres and community partners to identify the broadest possible range of internal and external factors that may influence outcomes and will consider these in results interpretation and dissemination.

Duration of the study

Formative activities started in September 2024 and are anticipated to be completed by June 2025. The SW-CRT will be delivered from April 2026 to March 2027, with follow-up data collected for 3 months after the intervention is delivered to the fourth wedge.

Ethics and dissemination

The study received ethical approval from Advarra Institutional Review Board. Qualitative and quantitative data collection, storage and analysis comply with current data protection regulations. Findings, which have the potential to improve care coordination for non-medical social drivers of health in primary care settings, will be disseminated across health centre national networks and in peer-reviewed publications.

Trial status

The study was reviewed by the Advarra Institutional Review Board (Pro00074727) which provided approval for quantitative data collection and analysis and the modified Delphi, semi-structured interviews and guided tour of EHR that comprise formative activities). (Aim 1 and 2: 15 January 2024; Aim 3: 20 October 2023). The institutional review board will receive the protocol again before the pilot and main trial. No research with human subjects will be conducted until the proper approvals have been received. CIRBI@ADVARRA.org.

Ethical approval and informed consent statements

The DEDICATE trial is registered with Clinicaltrials.gov registration as #NCT06489002. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06489002?term=NCT06489002&rank=1. The protocol was approved by the Advarra Institutional Review Board.

A waiver of consent and a full waiver of HIPAA authorisation was received for patient data that will be collected from the EHR for evaluating patient outcomes. This research involves no more than minimal risk to the individual as patients continue to receive usual care. Our study provides implementation supports (eg, workflow redesign, training) to health centre staff to help them improve the conduct of tasks and activities that are part of the standard of care. The intervention does not change the services patients receive and, as there is no direct patient intervention, there are no known risks to patient safety.

Consent to participate: informed consent will be gathered for participants that participate in interviews by study team staff. A member of the study team will provide an Institutional Review Board-approved information sheet to participating health centre staff and answer questions before obtaining verbal consent. Receipt of verbal consent will be documented in REDCap.
