Effectiveness of Mobile Health Applications Compared to Conventional Motivational Interviewing in Improving Toothbrushing Duration and Its Impact on Dental Caries Among 6–12-year-old Children: 12-month Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Devanshe Chaudhary, Inder Kumar Pandit, Neeraj Gugnani, Monika Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundToothbrushing for 2 minutes twice daily is a well-established preventive strategy against dental caries. However, compliance among children remains poor, and traditional motivational methods often fail to sustain behavior change. mHealth apps offer interactive features to improve brushing habits, yet evidence linking their use to improved brushing duration and clinical outcomes remains limited. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of two dental apps (Brush DJ app and Brushing Time app) with conventional face-to-face motivational interviewing in improving toothbrushing duration and to assess their impact on oral hygiene and caries incidence among children aged 6–12 years.Materials and methodsA 12-month, 3-arm, parallel-group, assessor-blinded randomized controlled trial was conducted with 120 children (40 per group). Participants were randomized to Brush DJ app, Brushing Time app, and motivational interviewing. Brushing duration, OHI-S Debris Index, and dmfs/DMFS index were assessed at baseline and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. Data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAt 12 months, Brush DJ achieved the highest mean brushing duration (104.9 seconds), significantly greater than the face-to-face group (82.0 seconds; p = 0.033). The Brushing Time app recorded a mean of 100.5 seconds, not significantly different from Brush DJ. OHI-S scores improved most in the Brushing Time app group (1.0), followed by Brush DJ (1.03), both significantly better than face-to-face (1.4; p < 0.05). Caries incidence (dmfs/DMFS) showed marginally lower scores in the app groups (Brushing Time app: 4.6; Brush DJ app: 4.8) compared to face-to-face (5.1), though differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionBoth the Brush DJ app and the Brushing Time app were more effective than face-to-face counseling in improving brushing duration and oral hygiene.Clinical significanceFreely available mHealth apps offer a promising, accessible, and sustainable adjunct to traditional counseling in promoting better toothbrushing habits and oral health in children.

Introduction

Toothbrushing is the most widely recommended method for maintaining oral hygiene and preventing plaque-related diseases such as dental caries and periodontal disease.1 Twice-daily brushing with fluoridated toothpaste for at least 2 minutes is endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD), European Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (EAPD), and other professional organisations.2 Research also shows that children who brush more frequently and for longer durations (e.g., twice a day for 2 minutes) tend to have lower caries prevalence compared to those who brush less often or for shorter periods.3

Despite strong evidence supporting this simple measure, dental caries remains one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide.4 In India, the prevalence is 54.16%, with higher rates in mixed dentition (58%) and certain regions such as western India (72%).5 The disease burden is greater among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, and untreated caries can impair growth, school performance, and quality of life.6

Healthy brushing habits are best developed early in life, yet behavior change is challenging.7 Conventional strategies—verbal instructions, brochures, posters, videos, and parental modeling—are widely used but often fail to ensure long-term compliance, especially among children who perceive brushing as tedious.8 Motivation and interactive engagement are crucial to sustaining the practice.9

The increasing availability of smartphones and mobile health (mHealth) technologies offers new opportunities for promoting oral hygiene.10 Dental apps can provide reminders, timers, gamified incentives, and educational content, potentially enhancing motivation and adherence.11 “Brush DJ” is a free app that plays 2 minutes of music, delivers age-specific advice, and sets oral hygiene reminders, while “Brushing Time” app incorporates personalized avatars, brushing logs, and reward points to reinforce behavior and enable longitudinal monitoring.12

Mobile health (mHealth) applications have gained attention as interactive tools to improve oral hygiene behaviors, particularly by encouraging children to brush for the recommended duration.13 Brushing duration is a critical determinant of oral hygiene effectiveness, as longer and consistent brushing ensures more thorough plaque removal.14 However, despite the potential of mHealth apps to make brushing for 2 minutes more engaging, their impact on actual brushing duration and subsequent caries prevention has not been well established.15

This trial was therefore designed to compare the effectiveness of two freely available dental apps with conventional face-to-face motivational interviewing in promoting optimal toothbrushing and to evaluate their impact on caries incidence in 6–12-year-old children over 12 months, using the dmfs/DMFS index and self-reported oral hygiene practices.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a 3-arm parallel-group, assessor-blinded, randomized controlled trial (RCT) designed to evaluate and compare the efficacy of two dental mobile applications—the Brush DJ app and the Brushing Time app—and conventional face-to-face motivational interviewing in promoting optimal toothbrushing duration (2 minutes) among 6–12-year-old children. The effect of brushing for 2 minutes on caries incidence was also assessed over the study period.

The study was conducted at the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, DAV (C) Dental College and Hospital, Yamuna Nagar, Haryana. Recruitment was carried out between October 2023 and January 2024, with the final follow-up completed in November 2024. The trial followed a 1:1:1 allocation ratio and adhered to CONSORT 2010 guidelines for RCT reporting.

Ethical Approval and Trial Registration

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee of DAV(C) Dental College and Hospital, Yamuna Nagar (IEC/DAV/2023/PD/02) and the Review Board of Pandit Bhagwat Dayal Sharma University of Health Sciences, Rohtak. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians following the standard GCP guidelines. Verbal assent was also sought from all participating children before enrollment.

Participants

Participants were recruited from two primary sources: children reporting to the Outpatient clinic of the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, and students enrolled in local schools within the Yamuna Nagar district. Both private and public schools were chosen to avoid any bias in the selection process. All potential participants were subsequently screened to ensure eligibility based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Randomization, Allocation Concealment, and Blinding

A total of 120 participants, fulfilling the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, were enrolled in the study. For the purpose of randomization, a computer-based random sequence was generated using a freely available online portal (Research Randomizer, www.randomizer.org). The participants were allocated into three groups, with 40 children in each.

Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE), which were prepared in advance by an independent staff member not involved in the recruitment or assessment of participants. Each envelope contained the group assignment, and using these, the participants were allocated their respective groups in a sequential manner as they were enrolled in the study.

Blinding of participants and operators was not feasible due to the nature of the interventions. However, the examiner who recorded the outcome measures and the statistician performing data analysis were blinded to group allocation.

Interventions

All participants received standardized baseline oral health education emphasizing the need and importance of brushing twice daily for 2 minutes using a fluoridated toothpaste.2 They were also taught about the amount of fluoride toothpaste required as per their age to avoid any risk of fluorosis. This ensured that every child had a uniform foundation of oral hygiene knowledge before the interventions were introduced. Following this, participants were instructed about their assigned intervention groups.

Group I—Brush DJ App

Children in this group were instructed to use the Brush DJ mobile application twice daily under parental supervision. The application plays music for a duration of 2 minutes, provides visual cues, and delivers age-appropriate guidance aimed at reinforcing optimal brushing practices. Its design integrates auditory and visual stimulation to maintain attention and promote adherence to recommended brushing duration.

Group II—Brushing Time app

Participants in this group were instructed to use the Brushing Time application twice daily. The application incorporates a 2-minute timer, enables the creation of personalized avatars, records brushing history, and awards points based on compliance. These gamified and motivational features are intended to enhance engagement and sustain long-term adherence to oral hygiene practices.

For both groups I and II, participants underwent initial training and calibration in the use of the respective applications. Each child logged into their account and demonstrated app usage under supervision at the first session, during which any questions related to functionality or interface were addressed to ensure uniformity and accuracy of use.

Group III—Face-to-face Motivational Interviewing

In addition to the baseline oral health education provided to all groups, children in this group received structured verbal counselling using motivational interviewing techniques delivered by the investigator. The sessions focused on correct brushing technique, appropriate duration, and the significance of maintaining oral hygiene. Participants were instructed to refrain from using freely available mobile applications or other external interventions during the study period to prevent confounding influences.

All of the children used only manual toothbrushes.

Parents of all groups supervised their child's brushing throughout the 12-month follow-up.

Baseline Assessment and Outcome Evaluation

At enrollment, each child underwent a comprehensive baseline evaluation, with primary emphasis placed on the measurement of brushing duration. Brushing duration was objectively recorded using a digital stopwatch, ensuring accurate assessment of the child's natural brushing behavior prior to the intervention. In addition, oral hygiene status was determined using the Simplified Oral Hygiene Index–Debris component (OHI-S Debris Index, Greene and Vermillion, 1964), which provided a quantitative measure of plaque accumulation. To further assess caries experience, both primary and permanent dentitions were examined and scored according to the dmfs/DMFS index based on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria (2013). Collectively, these baseline assessments established a reliable foundation for subsequent evaluation of intervention outcomes, with brushing duration serving as a critical behavioral parameter reflecting adherence to recommended oral hygiene practices.

Participants were recalled at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months for supervised measurement of brushing duration, assessment of OHI-S Debris Index, and evaluation of dmfs/DMFS index scores.

The primary outcome was brushing duration recorded under direct observation, while secondary outcomes assessed its impact on caries experience (dmfs/DMFS index), oral hygiene status (OHI-S Debris Score), and compliance with twice-daily brushing verified by parental diaries and app logs.

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

Data were recorded on case report forms and entered into Microsoft Excel. Analysis was performed using SPSS (version 20; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Data distribution was checked for normality. Intergroup comparisons used Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

The normality of brushing duration, dmfs/DMFS, and OHI-S data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated that all three variables violated the assumption of normal distribution. Consequently, the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was employed to compare the distribution of these variables among the three groups at each follow-up interval. For intergroup comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied, and the p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons.

Brushing Duration

At the 12-month follow-up, group I (Brush DJ) demonstrated the highest mean brushing duration (104.88 seconds), followed by group II (Brushing Time app) with a mean of 100.45 seconds, while group III (no app) recorded the lowest duration (82.02 seconds). Intergroup comparison at this time point revealed a statistically significant difference in brushing duration (p = 0.028). Post hoc analysis further showed a significant difference between group I and group III (p = 0.033), whereas the differences between group I and group II (p = 0.594) and between group II and group III (p = 0.285) were not statistically significant. Overall, the results indicate that the Brush DJ app was most effective in improving brushing duration, followed by the Brushing Time app, with the lowest outcomes observed in the group without an app (Fig. 1).

dmfs/DMFS Scores

At the 12-month follow-up, the lowest mean dmfs/DMFS score was recorded in group II (Brushing Time app) at 4.60, followed closely by group I (Brush DJ) with a mean score of 4.80, while group III (no app) exhibited the highest mean score of 5.10. However, the differences observed across all time points (baseline, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months) were not statistically significant; therefore, no further comparative analysis was undertaken. Overall, the findings suggest that Brushing Time app demonstrated slightly better outcomes in reducing caries experience, followed by Brush DJ, with the least favorable results seen in the no app group (Fig. 2).

OHI-S Scores

At the 12-month follow-up, the lowest mean OHI-S score was observed in group II (Brushing Time app) at 1.0, followed closely by group I (Brush DJ app) at 1.03, while group III (no app) showed the highest mean score of 1.40. Intergroup comparison at this time point demonstrated a statistically significant difference in OHI-S scores (p = 0.008). Post hoc analysis indicated a significant difference between group I and group III (p = 0.018) as well as between group II and group III (p = 0.027), while the difference between group I and group II was not significant (p = 1.00). Overall, these results suggest that Brushing Time app achieved the most favorable improvement in oral hygiene status, followed closely by the Brush DJ app, with the least improvement observed in the no-app group (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the effectiveness of two freely available mobile dental applications—Brush DJ app and Brushing Time app—in comparison with conventional face-to-face motivational interviewing in improving toothbrushing duration and oral health outcomes among children aged 6–12 years. The findings demonstrated that both mobile apps significantly improved brushing duration and oral hygiene status compared to face-to-face counseling, while caries incidence showed nonsignificant but favorable trends in the app groups.16,17

At 12 months, Brush DJ achieved the longest mean brushing duration (104.9 seconds), followed closely by the Brushing Time app (100.5 seconds), whereas motivational interviewing yielded a substantially lower mean (82.0 seconds). Although the recommended duration is 120 seconds, the improvement observed in the app groups represents a meaningful behavioral shift from the baseline (approximately 46 seconds). These results align with previous studies demonstrating the utility of digital interventions in extending brushing duration.18,19 Underwood et al. reported that Brush DJ improved children's adherence to 2-minute brushing routines through music-based timers and visual cues, reinforcing engagement and sustaining attention.20 Similarly, Scheerman et al. observed that gamified apps employing avatars and rewards significantly increased brushing compliance among adolescents compared to traditional education.19

The superior performance of Brush DJ over motivational interviewing in our study may be attributed to its audiovisual cues and ability to transform brushing into a fun, music-driven experience.21,22 The Brushing Time app also demonstrated strong performance, possibly due to gamification and progress-tracking features, which support self-monitoring—a recognized determinant of sustained behavior change.23

The OHI-S debris index improved significantly in both app groups compared to motivational interviewing. Brushing Time app achieved the best results (mean OHI-S = 1.0), closely followed by Brush DJ app (1.03), while the face-to-face group lagged behind (1.40). These findings suggest that increased brushing duration, reinforced through engaging app features, translated into clinically measurable reductions in plaque accumulation.24

Previous studies corroborate these findings. Aleksejuniene et al. conducted a study evaluating a theory-based reminder strategy grounded in the Health Belief Model and found that delivering oral health education through this approach effectively enhanced children's oral self-care practices and their knowledge of oral health.25 Similarly, Alkadhi et al. highlighted that smartphone-based brushing timers and feedback systems improved oral hygiene scores over a 6-month period.26 Our results extend this evidence by demonstrating effectiveness over a longer follow-up of 12 months, underscoring the sustainability of digital interventions when parental supervision is ensured.27

Although both Brush DJ app and Brushing Time app groups exhibited slightly lower dmfs/DMFS scores compared to motivational interviewing at 12 months, the differences were not statistically significant. This may be due to the relatively short follow-up period, as dental caries develops over a longer timeframe.28 Moreover, all participants received baseline oral health education and parental supervision, which may have minimized intergroup differences in caries progression.29

Similar findings were reported by Scheerman et al., who found that app-based interventions significantly improved self-reported brushing behaviors and plaque control but did not yield significant reductions in caries within one year.19 Thus, while apps hold promise in improving intermediate outcomes such as brushing duration and plaque control, longer-term trials are needed to establish their effect on hard outcomes, such as caries.

Motivational interviewing is widely recognized as an effective counseling approach for health behavior change.30 However, in the present trial, it was less effective than app-based interventions in sustaining toothbrushing behaviors. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in delivery. While motivational interviewing relies heavily on the interpersonal skill of the counselor and periodic reinforcement, mobile apps provide daily, consistent, and interactive reminders that integrate seamlessly into children's routines. Moreover, children may perceive face-to-face counseling as prescriptive, whereas apps deliver self-directed, gamified experiences that align with their digital interests.21

The success of mHealth apps in this study can be explained by several behavioral science principles. Mobile health applications such as Brush DJ app and Brushing Time app appear effective largely because they incorporate multiple behavioral reinforcement strategies. First, they provide cueing and reminders, which help reduce forgetfulness and support the establishment of regular brushing routines. Second, gamification and rewards, particularly in Brushing Time app through avatars and point systems, tap into children's intrinsic motivation and reinforce adherence to recommended practices.31 Third, Brush DJ adds entertainment value by transforming a mundane activity into a music-driven experience, thereby reducing boredom and increasing engagement. Finally, the element of parental involvement—with supervised app use—likely strengthened accountability and ensured consistent brushing behavior, a critical factor in shaping sustainable oral hygiene practices among children.29

This study is one of the few RCTs in India to evaluate mHealth dental apps over 12 months with both behavioral (brushing duration) and clinical outcomes (dmfs/DMFS and OHI-S). The high retention rate enhances internal validity. Additionally, the inclusion of both private and public schoolchildren improves generalizability across socioeconomic groups.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, brushing duration was observed in a supervised setting, which may have induced reactivity (Hawthorne effect).32 Second, although parental supervision was mandated, actual home compliance could not be independently verified beyond diaries and app logs. Third, the study duration of 12 months may not be sufficient to detect significant differences in caries incidence.28 Fourth, only manual toothbrushes were used, whereas powered brushes combined with digital aids might yield different outcomes.33 Finally, the study evaluated only two specific apps; results may not generalize to all available mHealth tools.

Future research should extend follow-up periods to 2–3 years to capture changes in caries incidence more reliably. Additionally, combining mHealth interventions with professional reinforcement (hybrid models) may maximize effectiveness.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that mobile dental applications, specifically Brush DJ app and Brushing Time app, were more effective than conventional face-to-face motivational interviewing in improving brushing duration and oral hygiene status among children aged 6–12 years over a 12-month period. Brush DJ was most effective in prolonging brushing duration, while Brushing Time app yielded slightly better outcomes in oral hygiene and caries prevention, though differences in caries incidence were not statistically significant. These findings highlight the potential of mHealth apps as practical, engaging, and scalable tools for fostering sustainable oral hygiene behaviors in children. In conclusion, mHealth applications represent a promising adjunct to conventional preventive strategies, offering a child-centered approach that aligns with digital lifestyles.

Clinical Significance

Dental apps such as Brush DJ app and Brushing Time app represent low-cost, scalable, and engaging adjuncts for promoting oral hygiene in children. Integrating such tools into school-based oral health programs or pediatric dental counseling could enhance adherence to recommended brushing practices. Furthermore, apps may be particularly valuable in low-resource settings, where regular professional reinforcement is challenging.
