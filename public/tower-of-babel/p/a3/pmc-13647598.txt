TrLPMO9A drives oxidative cellulose depolymerization in Trichoderma reesei and is enhanced by heterologous cellobiose dehydrogenase expression

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne of the most prominent mechanisms for plant cell wall deconstruction in nature, and widely employed in industry, relies on the coordinated action of hydrolytic and oxidative enzymes. However, how redox networks sustain synergistic biomass deconstruction remains incompletely understood, particularly in the industrial workhorse Trichoderma reesei. This fungus lacks a cellobiose dehydrogenase (CDH), a pivotal redox partner for lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) in many fungal systems. Here, we investigated the oxidative machinery of T. reesei and the contribution of key redox-active enzymes to lignocellulose deconstruction.ResultsWe demonstrate that the oxidative capacity of the T. reesei secretome is largely driven by a single enzyme, TrLPMO9A, the most abundant oxidoreductase in the secretome. Proteomic analyses also revealed a lower abundance of other redox-active enzymes, including TrLPMO9B and AA5 oxidase. Although deletion of TrLPMO9B and TrAA5 had a less pronounced impact on saccharification efficiency compared with TrLPMO9A, the secretome remodeling triggered by their deletion, along with the associated decrease in saccharification performance, indicates that these redox enzymes play distinct, non-redundant roles. They likely play a system-level role within a cooperative redox network that fuels oxidative cellulose deconstruction, potentially extending beyond direct catalysis to processes associated with redox balance or protein secretion. Finally, we challenged the CDH-lacking paradigm by heterologously expressing a CDH in T. reesei. In vivo reconstitution of this redox duet increased biomass saccharification by 13–19%, demonstrating a strong synergistic relationship between LPMOs and CDHs even in a native CDH-lacking host.ConclusionThese findings define the core oxidative machinery underlying biomass deconstruction in T. reesei, revealing the major cellulose-oxidative role of TrLPMO9A and the importance of a cooperative redox network for efficient lignocellulose depolymerization. Moreover, successful reconstitution of CDH activity in a naturally CDH-deficient host establishes redox engineering as a promising strategy to enhance industrial biomass conversion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02788-w.Keywords: Lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases, Cellobiose dehydrogenases, Redox system, Biomass conversion, Trichoderma reesei

Background

The depolymerization of recalcitrant polysaccharides such as cellulose and complex hemicelluloses constitutes a major bottleneck both in natural ecosystems and industrial biomass conversion [6, 57, 75]. Over decades, the prevailing model for the breakdown of these polysaccharides relied primarily on the cooperation of functionally complementary glycoside hydrolases (GHs) such as endoglucanases, cellobiohydrolases and β-glucosidases [48, 67]. This model was fundamentally revised with the discovery of lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs), a class of copper-dependent enzymes that cleave glycosidic bonds through an oxidative mechanism [66].

By introducing chain breaks within polysaccharides, LPMOs extended the depolymerization potential of classical enzymatic systems, having an instrumental role in enzymatic technologies for biomass conversion [2, 51, 53, 66]. More recently, this paradigm was further modified with the discovery of a new class of peroxygenase active on cellulose [56], indicating that distinct redox mechanisms can contribute to overcome the recalcitrance of plant cell walls by microbial life.

LPMOs are considered to play a pivotal role in the saprophytic lifestyle of many filamentous fungi by enabling efficient breakdown of plant cell walls [3, 27, 71]. Some highly proficient fungi in biomass utilization, such as Thermothelomyces thermophilus and Neurospora crassa, encode a plethora of LPMOs, which are differentially expressed depending on type of biomass and specific growth conditions [29, 69]. Moreover, many species growing on lignocellulose co-secrete LPMOs with partner oxidoreductases, majorly cellobiose dehydrogenases (CDH, CAZy family AA3_1) [5, 9, 27, 46].

By oxidizing cellobiose and transferring electrons via its cytochrome domain, CDH enables reductive activation of LPMOs, thereby promoting oxidative cleavage of polysaccharides [60]. The functional relevance of this cooperation was first demonstrated in vitro by Langston et al., [39], who showed synergistic effect between TaAA9A from Thermoascus aurantiacus (formerly TaGH61A) and HiCDH from Humicola insolens. Subsequent studies have further supported this finding in vitro (reviewed by [1, 68], (Kracher & Ludwig, [33])) as well as their biological relevance has been investigated in distinct filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus nidulans [63], Podospora anserina [62], Neurospora crassa [51, 74], and Trametes versicolor [20].

Trichoderma reesei, despite a key industrial cellulase producer and model for plant cell wall breakdown, exhibits a very limited repertoire of LPMOs (only three compared to dozens in Magnaporthe grisea, Neurospora crassa and Phanerochaete chrysosporium for instance) that is further aggravated by the lack of a CDH gene in its genome [13, 28, 44]. This limitation is evidenced by multiple studies in which supplementation of T. reesei secretome with both fungal and bacterial LPMOs and CDHs improves biomass saccharification [4, 15, 65].

TrLPMO9A (also known as HjLPMO9A), formerly designated Cel61A, has been extensively characterized and considered a main representative of fungal AA9 LPMOs. Following its reclassification as an oxidative enzyme, TrLPMO9A was shown to oxidatively cleave cellulose and lignocellulosic substrates, generating both native and oxidized cello-oligosaccharides [52, 61]. Mechanistically, TrLPMO9A can operate predominantly through a peroxygenase reaction, in which H₂O₂ serves as the preferred co-substrate, while external electron donors are required for the initial reduction of the copper active site [38]. Detailed kinetic analyses have revealed high catalytic efficiency toward H₂O₂-driven oxidation and demonstrated a strong dependence of enzyme performance on reaction pH, substrate availability, and reductant supply [30, 36, 37].

Regarding regioselectivity, TrLPMO9A is capable of both C1 and C4 oxidation of cellulose and is therefore classified as a mixed C1/C4-oxidizing AA9 LPMO. While initial studies reported C1-oxidized products as the predominant reaction products, subsequent analyses demonstrated that C4 oxidation is at least equally important and may represent the preferred oxidation pathway depending on substrate and reaction conditions [52, 61]. Structural studies further revealed that factors such as N-glycosylation and aromatic residues on the substrate-binding surface influence the C1/C4 oxidation ratio [17, 18].

Recent studies have demonstrated that lignin-derived phenolic compounds released during biomass pretreatment can support LPMO catalysis by acting as electron donors and promoting in situ H₂O₂ generation, thereby sustaining oxidative cellulose depolymerization even in the absence of dedicated redox partner proteins [31]. Consequently, although CDH is widely regarded as a native redox partner of fungal LPMOs, its contribution to biomass conversion is expected to depend on the chemical environment and the availability of alternative reducing agents and H₂O₂-generating systems within the substrate.

However, the functional impact of co-expressing a CDH within a hyper-cellulolytic T. reesei strain remains underexplored, leaving the potential of reconstituting this redox partnership for biomass depolymerization elusive, principally under industrially relevant conditions. Together, this limited understanding of the endogenous redox system in T. reesei, including the CDH-deficiency paradigm for biomass conversion, represents a critical knowledge gap for developing effective multi-enzyme blends for conversion of non-food materials into value-added products.

In this study, we evaluate the contribution of extracellular oxidoreductases to biomass saccharification in T. reesei, identifying TrLPMO9A as the primary driver of oxidative cellulose deconstruction. In parallel, analysis of two other oxidative enzymes, TrLPMO9B and a TrAA5 oxidase, reveals that their deletion triggers disproportionate remodeling of the secretome relative to their impact on saccharification, suggesting that they may participate in a cooperative redox network supporting oxidative cellulose deconstruction. Furthermore, we reconstituted the LPMO–CDH redox system in T. reesei demonstrating a boosting effect in biomass saccharification by 13 to 19% with a single genetic modification.

Materials and methods

Strain and maintenance

T. reesei OpEn strain [22], the derived knockout strains, ΔTrLPMO9A, ΔTrLPMO9B and ΔTrAA5 as well as the strains producing CDHIIA from N. crassa (OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA) and that with further TrLPMO9A deletion (OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA_ΔTrLPMO9A) were routinely maintained in potato dextrose agar plates and cultivated for 24–48 h at 28 °C in the dark, followed by 3–5 days of incubation under mild natural light at room temperature until conidiation.

Genetic modification in T. reesei

T. reesei strain was genetically modified using a customized CRISPR–Cas9 system as previously described [22]. Gene-specific guide RNAs were designed and cloned into the pCas9gRNA vector, flanked by hammerhead and HDV ribozyme sequences, and further validated by sequencing. Homologous recombination cassettes containing upstream and downstream flanking regions were constructed for targeted deletions of TrLPMO9A (jgi accession 73643), TrLPMO9B (jgi accession 120961), and TrAA5 (jgi accession 124282) genes. In addition, a substitution cassette was designed to replace xyn5 (jgi accession 112392) with NcCDHIIA (also known as CDH-1; NCU_0026; XM_951498). Subsequently, the TrLPMO9A gene was knocked out in the engineered OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA strain using the same above-described strategy. Cassettes were assembled by in vivo recombination in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Oligonucleotides sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Fungal transformations were performed following polyethylene glycol-mediated protocol. Germinated mycelia were enzymatically digested to generate protoplasts, which were subsequently transformed with CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids and linear donor DNA using polyethylene glycol. Transformants were selected on hygromycin-containing medium and purified for obtaining monokaryotic strains. Genomic DNA was subsequently extracted using the Yeast DNA Preparation Kit (Jena Bioscience), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transformants were validated by diagnostic PCR (Supplementary Fig. S1). Loss of the CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid was confirmed by the hygromycin sensitivity phenotype.

Genomic DNA extraction for sequencing

T. reesei OpEn mycelium was frozen in liquid nitrogen and pulverized in an oscillating ball mill (TE-350, Tecnal Inc.). Homogenized samples were used for microbial DNA extraction according to Yu & Morrison [72], with modifications. Briefly, 0.25 g of sample was transferred to a Lysing Matrix E Tube from the FastDNA Spin Kit (MP Biomedical, Inc.). For cell lysis, 1 mL RBB + C buffer was added to each sample, followed by homogenization in a FastPrep FP120 (MP Biomedical, Inc.). Precipitation of proteins was obtained upon the addition of 260 µL of 10 M ammonium acetate. Samples were incubated on ice for 5 min and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 16,000 × g. Precipitation of nucleic acids was obtained upon addition of 1 mL isopropanol. Samples were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged for 15 min at 16,000 × g at 4 °C. The pellet was recovered and washed with 70% (v/v) ethanol, followed by drying at room temperature. The nucleic acid pellet was dissolved in 100 μL of ultrapure water. RNA was removed with the addition of 2 µL of DNase-free RNase from stock solution at 10 mg.mL− 1. DNA purification was performed using DNeasy PowerClean Pro Cleanup Kit (Qiagen). Finally, electrophoresis on 0.8% (w/v) agarose gel was run to separate the DNA fragments and DNA quality assessment. The DNA solution was stored at − 20 °C.

PacBio library preparation and long read sequencing

For sequencing long fragments (long reads) using the Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) technology platform, approximately 5 µg of gDNA from OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA strain was required to prepare the library. The gDNA was previously quantified using the Qubit® dsDNA BR kit (Thermo Fisher) and quality parameters (Absorbance ratios—260/280 and 260/230) were evaluated using NanoDrop (ThermoFisher). The material was then fragmented using g-TUBE tubes (Covaris) and the DNA resuspended in 150 µL of low TE buffer and transferred to the g-TUBE tube.

Library preparation was performed using the SMRTbell prep. Kit 3.0 (PacBio), following recommendations specified in the manufacturer’s protocol. The material was subjected to successive enzymatic reactions for repair and addition of an adenine (A) base at the 3'-ends, ligation of adapters (Barcoded) and treatment with nuclease, respectively. After all preparation steps, the material was purified with SMRTbell cleanup beads, following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The library was reassessed using the Qubit® dsDNA BR kit (Thermo Fisher).

Genomic DNA from the samples was annealed using the Sequel II Primer 3.2 sequencing primer and then ligated to DNA polymerase 2.2 using the Sequel II Binding Kit 3.2 according to the manufacturer's recommendations (Pacific Biosciences). Sequencing was performed on the new PacBio Sequel IIe System (Pacific Biosciences), at the High-performance sequencing facility from Brazilian Biorenewables National Laboratory (LNBR) (Campinas, Brazil), using the Sequel II Sequencing Kit 2.0 and 1 SMRT Cell 8 M, single use tray, with 30 h final run time.

Genomic analysis

HiFi long-read sequencing data were assembled using hifiasm v.0.20.0 [14], Canu v.2.2 [32], and pbIPA v.1.8. (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbipa). Assembly quality and completeness were assessed with QUAST v.5.2 [24], and the highest quality assemblies were selected for subsequent analyses. Gene prediction and functional annotation were performed using Funannotate v.1.8.17 [49] in combination with InterProScan v.5.60.92 [73].

Enzyme cocktail production

For production in shaking flasks, the strains: OpEn, OpEn-ΔTrLPMO9A, OpEn-ΔTrLPMO9B and OpEn-ΔTrAA5 were cultivated in 250-mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of molasses-based industrial medium (20 g.L− 1 ammonium sulfate, 20 g.L− 1 whole cell yeast (dry mass), 50 g.L− 1 of total reducing sugars (TRS) from sugarcane molasses, pH adjusted to 4.5) [22] at 200 rpm, 5 days at 28 °C. After cultivation, broths were centrifuged (4000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C) and the supernatants were used for protein, enzymatic activity and lignocellulose saccharification assays.

For bench-scale production, the fungal strains OpEn, OpEn-ΔTrLPMO9A, OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA and OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA_ΔTrLPMO9A were cultivated in bioreactors BioFlo/CelliGen 115 system (Eppendorf) and water-jacketed 3-L vessels containing industrial medium. Inoculum was prepared by cultivating spores (106 spores/mL) in Erlenmeyer flasks containing industrial medium at 200 rpm, 4 days and 28 °C. Cultivations were initiated with 1.0 L volume, including a 10% (v/v) inoculum. Aeration was maintained at a constant rate of 0.7 slpm compressed air, and an agitation cascade (400–1000 rpm) was employed to ensure dissolved oxygen concentration above 20%. During the cultivations, samples were withdrawn at regular intervals, centrifuged (14,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C) and the supernatants stored at − 20 °C. The supernatants were then used for protein, enzymatic activity and lignocellulose saccharification assays.

Secretome analysis by mass spectrometry (MS)

Secretome samples (100 µg prot.) obtained from cultivation in flasks and bioreactors were processed using a filter-aided protocol [43] with modifications [56]. Briefly, proteins were reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin on 30 kDa cut-off filters. Resulting peptides were recovered by centrifugation, dried under vacuum, and subjected to StageTip desalting. Purified peptides were resuspended in 4% (v/v) acetonitrile, quantified at 280 nm using UV spectrophotometry, and 250 ng were used for LC–MS analysis. Peptide separation was performed on a nanoElute 2 system (Bruker Daltonics) using an IonOpticks Aurora Ultimate column (25 cm × 75 µm ID, 1.5 µm particles, 120 Å pore size) coupled to a 5 µm emitter. Mobile phases consisted of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (B). The gradient was as follows: 2% B (0–2 min), 2–23% B (2–20 min), 23–35% B (20–23 min), 35–90% B (23–27 min), 90% B (27–37 min), and 95% B until 40 min. Eluting peptides were analyzed on a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) operated in positive ion mode with a CaptiveSpray source. MS1 and MS/MS spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 100–1700 using [45] DDA-PASEF with a long ion mobility gradient (1/K₀ = 0.6–1.6 V·s·cm⁻2). TIMS was operated with 100 ms accumulation and ramp times, resulting in a 100% duty cycle and a ramp rate of 9.42 Hz. A total of 10 PASEF ramps were used per cycle (1.17 s cycle time), selecting precursors with charge states 0–5. Fragmentation was performed using ion mobility-dependent collision energies ranging from 20 to 59 eV. Active exclusion was enabled for 0.4 min.

Proteomic data analyses

Raw data were processed using FragPipe with default settings for DDA-PASEF data. MS/MS spectra were searched using MSFragger [70] against a custom protein sequence database generated from the predicted proteome derived from an in-house genome assembly of the OpEn fungal strain. The search was performed assuming tryptic specificity, allowing up to two missed cleavages, and a minimum peptide length of seven amino acids. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation were considered as variable modifications. Precursor and fragment mass tolerances were defined to 20 ppm for both precursor and fragment ions. Isotope error was set to 0/1/2/3, and a minimum of five matched fragment ions was required for peptide identification. Peptide-spectrum matches were filtered using a target–decoy strategy to control the false discovery rate (FDR) below 1% at both peptide and protein levels. Match-between-runs was disabled throughout the analysis.

Label-free quantification was performed within FragPipe using the IonQuant [70] module, and protein intensities were calculated using the MaxLFQ [16] algorithm as implemented in FragPipe. The resulting protein-level intensity values were exported and further processed in Perseus [16]. For statistical analysis, only proteins quantified in all three biological replicates for both conditions were considered. Then, pairwise comparisons between the parental and mutant strains were evaluated using a two-sample t-test. Statistical significance was determined using an S0 value of 0.1 with permutation-based FDR control set to 0.05 (250 randomizations). Volcano plots were generated by plotting log₂ fold change between conditions on the x-axis and the –log₁₀ p-value on the y-axis. Additionally, presence/absence analysis was based on detection consistency across biological replicates. Proteins were considered reliably present in each condition when detected in all three biological replicates. Proteins detected in only a single replicate were classified as not reliably detected and therefore treated as absent in that condition, whereas proteins detected in two out of three replicates were considered inconsistently detected and were excluded from presence/absence classification.

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using STRING (string-db.org) [59]. Protein lists of interest were submitted to the platform using organism-specific annotations when available. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, including biological process, molecular function, and cellular component categories, was carried out using the default parameters implemented in STRING. Statistical significance was assessed using the built-in enrichment framework of the platform, and multiple testing correction was applied as provided by STRING (false discovery rate control). No additional parameter adjustments were performed.

Biochemical assays

Protein concentration and enzymatic activities of collected fermentation samples were determined as previously described [21]. Briefly, proteins were precipitated with ice-cold acetone and then quantified using the BioRad (Hercules, CA, USA) DC protein assay kit, which is based on the Lowry method [42]. Bovine serum albumin was used as a standard. Enzymatic activities such as β-glucosidase, β-xylosidase, endo- β-1,4-glucanase and endo- β-1,4-xylanase were determined as previously described by Fonseca et al. [22], and filter paper activity (FPase) was determined as described by Ghose [23].

Evaluation of ascorbic acid as reductant

The enzyme cocktails from the knockout mutants were evaluated in saccharification reactions with standard crystalline cellulose Avicel. Small-scale reactions were performed in 2-mL tubes (600 µL total volume) containing 5% (w/v) Avicel PH101® (Sigma) in 0.05 M sodium acetate buffer pH 5.0, and 4 mg of protein from the enzyme cocktail per g of Avicel, in the presence or absence of 1 mM freshly prepared ascorbic acid. The reactions were incubated at 1000 rpm and 50 °C for 24 h, stopped by heating (5 min, 95 °C), and centrifuged (20,000 g, 5 min). The supernatants were filtered through a Millex 0.22 µm syringe filter and glucose was quantified by HPLC, as described below.

Heterologous expression in Pichia pastoris

The gene encoding NcCDHIIA (full-length containing a CBM1 and native signal peptide) was subcloned into the pPICZ vector for heterologous expression in Pichia pastoris. Positive clones were selected on medium containing zeocin (100 µg/mL) and gene integration into the yeast genome was confirmed by PCR using AOX1 gene primers. Cultures were grown in Erlenmeyer flasks containing YPD medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, and 2% glucose) and incubated under 200 rpm agitation at 30 °C. After glucose depletion (≈24 h), expression was induced by the addition of 0.5% (v/v) methanol, which was supplemented every 24 h for 5 days. At the end of fermentation, the supernatant was recovered and concentrated using a tangential flow filtration system with a 10-kDa cutoff. Protein purification was initially performed by ion-exchange chromatography using a DEAE-Sepharose column, after adjusting the pH to 8.0 with 0.1 M Tris–HCl buffer. Elution was carried out using a 100–500 mM NaCl gradient. Fractions containing the target protein were identified by SDS-PAGE and subsequently subjected to size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column, using 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.5 containing 150 mM NaCl.

The purified enzyme was then used to supplement OpEn cocktail on in vitro saccharification assays at 1 and 3% (w/w) of total protein loading (10 mg.g of dry biomass− 1) in Eucalyptus saccharification. Reactions were performed for 72 h at 50 °C.

Pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass

Eucalyptus biomass was obtained from a wood pellet company located in Porto Feliz, São Paulo state, Brazil. The material was initially submitted to alkaline deacetylation using 0.4% (w/w) NaOH at 70 °C for 60 min with a 1:10 solid-to-liquid ratio (adapted from [41]), followed by acid-catalyzed steam explosion using 0.25% (v/v) sulfuric acid at 190 °C for 5 min. After pretreatment, the pH was adjusted to 5.0 using 5 M NaOH. Sugarcane bagasse was pretreated at the pilot plant of LNBR (Brazilian Biorenewables National Laboratory, Campinas, SP, Brazil). Hydrothermal pretreatment was subjected to steam explosion in an AdvancedBio reactor at 190 °C for 10 min. Following pretreatment, the pH was adjusted to 5.0 using 5 M NaOH. The pretreated biomasses were dried at room temperature to approximately 50% moisture and stored at 4 °C. The resulting pretreated Eucalyptus residue consisted of 58.7% cellulose, 34.54% lignin and 3.18% hemicellulose, whereas the pretreated sugarcane bagasse contained 58.2% cellulose, 30.93% lignin and 8.94% hemicellulose.

Enzymatic saccharification and product analysis

Saccharification reactions were performed in 50 mL Nalgene® Oak Ridge centrifuge tubes, and consisted of 20.0 g total mass, 15% (w/w) solid content, enzyme dosage of 10 mg.g of dry biomass− 1, 0.02% (w/w) NaN3 and 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer pH 5.0. Two stainless steel spheres (AISI 304, 8 mm diameter) were inserted into each tube to ensure homogeneous reactions. The tubes were incubated in FINEPCR combi-D24 hybridization incubators (Gunpo-si, South Korea) set to the maximum rotation and 50 °C. The experiments were performed as three independent replicates and blank reactions were prepared by substituting the enzyme cocktail for distilled water. Samples were collected after 24, 48 and 72 h, homogenized, centrifuged and filtered through a 0.22 µm Millex syringe filter before sugar quantification, as described below.

For glucose quantification, high-performance liquid chromatography was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity equipped with a refractive index detector (RID) and an Aminex HPX-87H column (300 mm × 7.8 mm) coupled to a guard column (30 mm × 4.6 mm) (Bio-Rad). Both the column and the RID were maintained at 35 °C. An isocratic elution was carried out using 5 mM sulfuric acid as mobile phase at 0.6 mL·min⁻1 flow rate. Gluconic acid (Supelco, catalog no. 64188) and cellobionic acid (Biosynth, catalog no. OC61665) were detected and quantified as previously described [56]. Each reaction was analyzed using an ICS 6000 HPAEC-PAD system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a CarboPac PA1 analytical column (2 × 250 mm) and a guard column (2 × 50 mm), at 30 °C, running at 0.1 mL.min⁻1 flow rate and gradient elution using 0.1 M NaOH (eluent A) and 1 M sodium acetate (eluent B). Electrochemical detection was carried out using a gold working electrode and an Ag/AgCl pH reference electrode. Appropriate commercial standards were used for external calibration.

Results

Uncovering the biological roles of secreted redox enzymes in T. reesei

Proteomic analysis of the engineered T. reesei OpEn strain (also known as Br_TrR03), cultivated in molasses-based industrial medium and carrying the constitutively active xyr1-V821F allele that alleviates glucose-mediated carbon catabolite repression and enables cellulase and hemicellulase expression in glucose-containing media, revealed high secretion levels of TrLPMO9A under industrially relevant conditions contrasting to the markedly lower abundance of TrLPMO9B and no detection of TrLPMO9C in the secretome (Supplementary dataset). In addition, another potentially relevant secreted redox enzyme was identified corresponding to a family AA5 member (TrAA5), previously characterized as lignin copper oxidase [19]. Mutants harboring targeted deletion of these redox enzymes (∆TrLPMO9A, ∆TrLPMO9B and ∆TrAA5) produced similar levels of extracellular protein, and the key enzymatic activities (FPase and CMCase) remained comparable to those of the parental strain (Supplementary Fig. S2).

To further investigate the impact of these deletions on the extracellular enzymatic machinery, we performed a comparative analysis of the secretomes produced by the mutant and parental strains. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear differences in secretome composition, with ∆TrAA5 and ∆TrLPMO9B forming distinct clusters, while ∆TrLPMO9A was more closely associated with the parental strain (Fig. 1A). Consistently, distinct subsets of protein were exclusively detected in each mutant comparing to parental strain, with seven proteins unique to ΔTrLPMO9A, whereas 54 and 67 proteins were uniquely identified in the ΔTrLPMO9B and ΔTrAA5 secretomes, respectively (Supplementary Table S2 and S4). Interestingly, a subset of proteins was shared between the ΔTrAA5 and ΔTrLPMO9B secretomes, further supporting this trend. These proteins included several glycoside hydrolases (GH1, GH3, GH27, GH43, and GH128), as well as proteases and hypothetical proteins (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4), suggesting a conserved compensatory response to the loss of these redox enzymes. In contrast, the ∆TrLPMO9A secretome displayed minimal changes, indicating a distinct physiological response likely associated with the dominant catalytic role of this enzyme rather than to broader secretome remodeling.

Differential secretome analysis further confirmed that the ΔTrLPMO9A strain exhibited minimal changes in protein abundance, with most proteins showing no significant variation relative to the parental strain (Fig. 1B). In contrast, both ΔTrLPMO9B and ΔTrAA5 exhibited extensive alterations in protein abundance profiles, indicating substantial remodeling of the secretome compared to the parental OpEn strain (Fig. 1C, E). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of upregulated proteins in the mutant strains highlighted distinct responses between mutants. On this regard, ∆TrLPMO9B exhibited a restricted enrichment profile (Fig. 1D), primarily associated with carbohydrate metabolic processes, whereas ∆TrAA5 showed broader enrichment across multiple biological processes, including carbohydrate and polysaccharide metabolism, peptide metabolism, translation, and biosynthetic pathways, suggesting more extensive cellular reprogramming which involves both metabolic and protein synthesis functions (Fig. 1F).

In silico analysis of the genomic contexts of the three target genes revealed the TrLPMO9A locus as the only associated with genes encoding for plant cell wall degrading enzymes, including, acetyl xylan esterase (axe1), and cellulose-induced secreted protein (cip1) (Supplementary Fig. S3). In addition, neighboring genes downstream of TrLPMO9A, such as ret3, cog4, and pmr1, are involved in vesicular trafficking and Golgi function, processes that are essential for the proper folding, modification, and secretion of extracellular enzymes [47]. The presence of these genes suggests that the TrLPMO9A locus lies within a genomic region associated not only with lignocellulose-responsive enzymes but also with cellular pathways supporting protein secretion. In contrast, TrLPMO9B and TrAA5 appear to be located within regions dominated by general cellular metabolic functions (Supplementary Fig. S3). Although genes encoding carbohydrate-active and oxidative enzymes are often dispersed throughout the genomes of filamentous fungi and primarily coordinated through transcriptional regulation rather than physical clustering [35, 44], the local enrichment of plant cell wall-related and secretion-associated genes surrounding TrLPMO9A points to a functionally specialized genomic neighborhood.

Regarding the saccharification performance, deletion of TrLPMO9A had a pronounced negative impact reducing glucose yield by approximately 25% on both pretreated substrates (sugarcane bagasse and Eucalyptus residue), whereas deletions of TrLPMO9B and TrAA5 resulted in smaller, yet consistent reductions after 72 h (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Table S5). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in xylose release among the strains (Supplementary Fig. S4). The stronger effect observed for TrLPMO9A deletion likely reflects its markedly higher secretion levels, which are up to 1000-fold greater than those of TrLPMO9B and TrAA5. (Supplementary dataset). Interestingly, the addition of ascorbic acid enhanced saccharification of microcrystalline cellulose (Avicel) by the parental OpEn secretome whereas this effect was strongly diminished in all three mutant strains (Fig. 2C). This result further supports the dominant oxidative role of TrLPMO9A, indicating that the increased product release in the parental strain is primarily dependent on the presence of redox-active enzymes, particularly TrLPMO9A, capable of productively utilizing reducing equivalents and reactive oxygen species generated in the reaction system. The reduced response observed in the knockout strains suggests that non-enzymatic redox reactions alone are not sufficient to account for the enhanced saccharification observed in the parental strain.

Together, these results demonstrate that TrLPMO9A acts as the primary driver of oxidative cellulose deconstruction in T. reesei, while TrLPMO9B and TrAA5, despite their lower direct impact on saccharification, contribute to the system through broader roles associated with redox balance and secretome modulation, consistent with their involvement in a cooperative redox network.

Reconstitution of LPMO-CDH system boosts oxidative machinery in T. reesei

To challenge the CDH-deficiency paradigm in T. reesei, we engineered the OpEn platform to express CDHIIA from Neurospora crassa (AA3_1; also known as CDH-1), which corresponds to the most abundant protein secreted under cellulolytic conditions, exhibiting superior performance and stability compared to another CDH found in this fungus [25, 58, 74]. NcCDHIIA was integrated into the truncated xyn5 locus, which encodes a truncated and non-functional GH11 xylanase [54]. Although the corresponding protein has not been detected in the secretome, internal transcriptomic analyses of the parental strain cultivated in molasses-based industrial medium indicate that the xyn5 locus is highly transcribed. Therefore, this locus was selected as a target for heterologous gene integration because it combines the absence of a functional secreted enzyme with a transcriptionally active genomic environment. Genome sequencing confirmed the correct insertion of NcCDHIIA at the expected locus as a single-copy integration, with no off-target events detected (Supplementary Fig. S5). The resulting strain was named OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA (Fig. 3A).

The cultivation of OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA in bioreactors under industrially relevant conditions resulted in similar protein titers to the parental strain, indicating that the genetic modification had no adverse effect on strain productivity or overall secretory performance during fed-batch cultivation. Remarkably, the secretome produced by the OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA strain exhibited improvement in the cellulolytic potential featured by higher FPase activity (Supplementary Fig. S6). This positive change in the cellulolytic potential reflected in the saccharification performance with glucose yields increased by 13% for pretreated sugarcane bagasse (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S6) and 19% for pretreated Eucalyptus residue, observed at 72 h-reactions (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table S6). Meanwhile, no major differences were observed in xylose release among the different strains (Supplementary Fig. S7).

The effectiveness of the NcCDHIIA co-expression in the T. reesei redox machinery was further supported by the increase of LPMO-mediated oxidized products without being detrimental to final glucose yields in 72 h of saccharification. Specifically, the OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA secretome increased cellobionic acid in 23% and 111% with sugarcane bagasse and Eucalyptus residue, respectively, compared with the parental OpEn strain (Fig. 3D). Therefore, these results support the establishment of a functional oxidative system in which CDH contributes to electron transfer processes and/or hydrogen peroxide that promote LPMO activity, ultimately leading to a boosting effect in biomass saccharification.

To demonstrate that the impact of the co-expression of NcCDHIIA is directly related to the majorly secreted TrLPMO9A, we generated the double mutant OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA_ΔTrLPMO9A (Fig. 3A). The secretome of this double mutant showed a reduction in both FPase activity and saccharification performance compared to OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA (Fig. 3B, C and Supplementary Fig. S6), supporting the direct correlation between NcCDHIIA and TrLPMO9A for the enhanced saccharification performance. Consistent with the glucose release profiles, oxidized products were also reduced in the OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA_ΔTrLPMO9A preparation relative to OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA (Fig. 3D, E). As expected, the ∆TrLPMO9A strains did not produce C4-oxidized products (Supplementary Fig. S8), while the OpEn-Δxyn5::NcCDHIIA demonstrated an enhanced TrLPMO9A activity due to the accumulation of C4-oxidized products compared to the parental strain, further supporting the role of TrLPMO9A in the oxidative machinery of the OpEn cocktail. Likewise, expression of NcCDHIIA resulted in increased gluconic acid accumulation compared with the parental OpEn cocktail, whereas deletion of TrLPMO9A markedly decreased gluconic acid levels in both genetic backgrounds (Fig. 3D, E). This trend was observed for both substrates, however, gluconic acid values obtained from pretreated sugarcane bagasse are reported as apparent concentrations due to analytical interference from matrix-derived compounds, whereas values from eucalyptus residue saccharification represent reliably quantified concentrations. Collectively, these findings indicate that NcCDHIIA expression enhances the oxidative capacity of the OpEn cocktail, stimulating LPMO-driven reactions and increasing the accumulation of oxidized products during lignocellulosic biomass saccharification (Fig. 3D, E and Supplementary Fig. S8).

Of note, CBH1 is one of the most abundant enzymes in the secretome, corresponding to 13%, while TrLPMO9A represents 3% and NcCDHIIA accounted for 0.4% of the extracellular proteome (Supplementary dataset). To understand the impact of NcCDHIIA concentration in the synergistic effect considering the complexity of fine-tuning this parameter in vivo, the enzyme was expressed in Pichia pastoris and supplementation assays were conducted with the purified enzyme. According to these results, increasing CDH loading to similar levels of TrLPMO9A (circa 3%) negatively affected biomass saccharification compared with the lower supplementation level (1%), indicating that CDH should be supplied at levels lower than those of LPMO to sustain efficient catalysis (Supplementary Fig. S9). This result demonstrates that CDH-mediated electron transfer and/or hydrogen peroxide can enhance polysaccharide cleavage within an optimal concentration window, highlighting the importance of fine-tuning the redox balance of the LPMO-CDH system. In contrast, excessive CDH activity may result in electron delivery rates that exceed the capacity of LPMOs to productively utilize these reducing equivalents for polysaccharide oxidation. Such a redox imbalance may promote non-productive redox reactions and/or excessive hydrogen peroxide accumulation, potentially leading to oxidative inactivation of LPMOs and reduced saccharification efficiency, as previously reported [11, 55].

Together, these results highlight the central role of the highly secreted TrLPMO9A in biomass deconstruction and clearly demonstrate the effective implementation of a CDH-LPMO synergistic duet. Ultimately, this finding is particularly relevant for industrial applications, as CDH-mediated LPMO activation may reduce the dependence on externally supplied reductants in processes where endogenous reducing agents are insufficient, thereby improving process robustness. Therefore, the introduction of a heterologous CDH represents a promising strategy to establish a self-sufficient enzymatic redox system in T. reesei.

Discussion

Our results support a model in which TrLPMO9A plays a central catalytic role in the lignocellulose-degrading system of OpEn, whereas other oxidoreductases contribute in a more indirect manner. The limited changes observed in the secretome of the ΔTrLPMO9A strain, combined with the strong reduction in saccharification efficiency upon its deletion, reinforce the notion that this LPMO is a key driver of cellulose oxidation and substrate accessibility during biomass deconstruction. In contrast, deletion of TrAA5 and TrLPMO9B was associated with pronounced remodeling of the extracellular enzyme repertoire, despite their comparatively smaller impact on saccharification. Although these enzymes are present at much lower abundance in the secretome, their consistent negative effect on biomass conversion indicates that they contribute, albeit to a lesser extent, to cellulose deconstruction. This apparent decoupling between phenotypic output and proteomic response suggests that their role extends beyond direct catalysis, likely involving broader system-level functions associated with the extracellular redox environment and secretome organization. Such a role is consistent with emerging views that oxidative biomass degradation relies on a coordinated redox network in which multiple oxidoreductases contribute to the controlled generation and utilization of reactive oxygen species that fuel LPMO activity and other oxidative reactions [12, 50].

In this context, CDHs have been investigated since the 1970s [7], and their ability to enhance saccharification was first demonstrated by improved depolymerization of microcrystalline cellulose upon supplementation of T. reesei cellulases with CDH from Phanerochaete chrysosporium [8]. Following the discovery of LPMOs [66], CDHs were readily recognized as key enzymatic reductants in oxidative cellulose degradation, a role supported by their co-expression under cellulolytic conditions [26, 33, 34, 40]. In the current mechanistic framework, CDHs are proposed to enhance LPMO turnover through both direct electron transfer and controlled hydrogen peroxide generation [1]. Consistent with this model, our results demonstrate that the low secretion level of NcCDHIIA is sufficient to potentiate native TrLPMO9A activity, increasing cellulose accessibility and ultimately leading to enhanced glucose release during saccharification.

A key aspect in evaluating oxidative boosting strategies is maintaining the balance between productive depolymerization and the accumulation of oxidized carbohydrate products generated by LPMO activity, which may not be readily converted into desired fermentation products by conventional industrial microorganisms. Previous studies supplementing T. reesei systems with exogenous CDHs have reported concentration-dependent and often detrimental effects. For instance, increasing the dosage of CDHs from Phanerochaete chrysosporium [8], Pycnoporus cinnabarinus [10], Coprinopsis cinerea and Podospora anserina [64] resulted in reduced glucose yields across different lignocellulosic or standard cellulose substrates.

In line with these observations, our results also demonstrate that higher CDH levels have become detrimental to saccharification efficiency. This suggests that optimal LPMO activation requires a balanced redox environment in which CDH provides sufficient electrons and/or hydrogen peroxide to sustain oxidative cleavage without promoting excessive oxidative turnover. Notably, the relative abundance of these enzymes in the engineered T. reesei secretome indicates that CDH is present at substantially lower levels than LPMOs, supporting the notion that only limited CDH activity may be required to sustain LPMO catalysis. In contrast, excessive CDH activity may disrupt the optimal redox balance of the LPMO–CDH system, promoting reactions that do not contribute to productive polysaccharide oxidation and potentially resulting in oxidative self-inactivation of LPMOs.

Importantly, these findings were obtained under industrially relevant conditions, using enzyme cocktails produced in fed-batch bioreactors and saccharification assays performed at high-solid loadings of pretreated biomasses, reinforcing their practical relevance. Furthermore, the enhanced glucose release was consistently observed across distinct lignocellulosic substrates (grass and wood), subjected to hydrothermal pretreatment, suggesting that the benefits of CDH-mediated LPMO activation are not restricted to a single biomass type under the conditions evaluated in this study.

Overall, these findings highlight that enhanced enzyme cocktail performance for biomass saccharification can be achieved by re-establishing an evolutionarily conserved oxidative strategy within a CDH-deficient industrial host. This improvement, however, requires rational tuning of LPMO:CDH ratio to direct oxidative chemistry toward productive depolymerization, while minimizing carbon loss into inhibitory intermediates, thereby enhancing the efficiency of biomass conversion. Ultimately, this approach highlights the potential of redox pathway engineering to complement classical hydrolytic mechanisms in the development of next-generation lignocellulose biocatalysts.
