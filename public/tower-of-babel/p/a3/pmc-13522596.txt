AI for predicting exacerbations in KIDs with asthma (AIRE-KIDS)
Authors: Hui-Lee Ooi, Nicholas Mitsakakis, Margerie Huet Dastarac, Roger Zemek, Amy C. Plint, Jeff Gilchrist, Khaled El Emam, Dhenuka Radhakrishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recurrent acute care visits are a common yet preventable outcome for many children with asthma. Machine learning (ML) applied to electronic medical records (EMR) may help identify children at high risk and enable targeted referral to preventative care. We developed ML models to predict repeat asthma-related emergency department (ED) visits or hospital admissions within one year among children with a prior asthma ED visit at a tertiary children’s hospital. Retrospective pre-COVID-19 data (Feb 2017–Feb 2019, N = 2716) from the Children’s Hospital of Eastern Ontario (CHEO) were linked with environmental pollutant exposure and neighborhood marginalization data to train models. We evaluated boosted tree methods (LGBM, XGBoost) and three open-source large language models (DistilGPT2, Llama 3.2 1B, Llama-8b-UltraMedical). Models were tuned, calibrated, and validated using a post-COVID-19 dataset (Jul 2022–Apr 2023, N = 1237). Performance was assessed using AUC and F1 scores, with SHAP values identifying key predictors. LGBM performed best (AUC 0.712, F1 0.51), outperforming the current best practice (F1 0.334). Key predictors included prior asthma ED visits, triage acuity, medical complexity, food allergy, prior non-asthma respiratory ED visits, and age. AIRE-KIDS models accurately predict future acute care use and could support ED-based decision-making to improve equitable access to preventative asthma care.

Introduction

Asthma is one of the most prevalent chronic childhood diseases1 and a leading cause of repeated acute care visits (i.e., emergency department (ED) visits and hospital admissions) for the management of acute exacerbations. Up to 25% of children with an asthma ED visit will have a repeat visit in the following year, despite numerous evidence-based treatments available to optimize asthma control2. This is both because current shortages in primary care 3limit the application of evidence-based preventative treatments for most children with asthma2, and because there is limited capacity in specialized care settings to see all children with a single asthma ED visit. One approach to this gap is to target specialized asthma care4 to patients who are most at risk for repeat asthma ED visits directly from the ED setting. However, there is a lack of guidance or agreed consensus on how to identify this vulnerable group of children who are most at risk for repeat asthma-related acute care visits. Given the increase in the use of electronic medical records (EMR) at many hospitals and the consequent availability of large amounts of routinely collected clinical data available for children with asthma healthcare encounters, there may be an opportunity to apply machine learning models to address this gap.

While there is a larger literature on predicting repeat asthma exacerbations and acute care visits in adults, the number of studies in children is limited. These previous pediatric studies are heterogeneous in how asthma exacerbations were defined and generally used traditional biostatistical approaches to determine the importance of various predictors for this outcome5–11. The definition of asthma exacerbation12 in prior studies ranges from requiring oral corticosteroids (OCS), hospitalization, asthma related emergency visits or even death8,10,11,13–23. Among the studies that defined severe exacerbation as a repeat ED visit or hospitalization, the observation windows from the initial visit varies from 15 days to one year8,14,16–19,24,25. However, as asthma is a seasonal disease, with exacerbation risk dependent on triggers that may be more or less prevalent at different times of the year, a one-year observation for outcomes is the most relevant timeframe for long term management and prevention of exacerbations and acute care visits. Previously reported predictors of repeat asthma acute care visits over one year have included prior acute care visits, patient-level characteristics obtained from the patient chart or EMR and environmental information6,10,14–17,26.

Logistic regression and machine learning (ML) models, such as tree-based approaches, ensemble methods as well as deep learning techniques have been applied to predict asthma exacerbation9,10,16–21,27,28, including (MLP)8,17,20,29, recurrent neural networks30–32 and long short-term memory models (LSTM)33. Recently, large language models (LLMs)34–37 have gained momentum in the prediction of health outcomes but have not yet been applied to predicting asthma exacerbations and acute care visits. The benefit of LLMs over previous ML techniques lies in their improved potential for application into clinical care as these models may be more transferable across sites with minimal retraining since they are already pre-trained on a large amount of general and medical information. Despite the considerable potential of leveraging these large data sources, a recent article concluded that there remain concerns that LLMs cannot perform at a comparable level to ML models38. This type of comparison has not been performed on asthma exacerbation or acute care visit prediction, hence the importance of evaluating LLM performance against traditional ML models in this specific clinical context. Multiple LLM approaches have been investigated and implemented in the literature, and we can categorize them into open-source model fine-tuning34,39,40 and larger proprietary model in-context learning35–37. In clinical settings open-source models where inference can be performed locally are preferred due to strict privacy rules that limit the ability of transferring personal health information to external sites that do not meet regulatory security requirements.

The aim of the current study was to evaluate several machine learning (ML) approaches, including 3 different LLMs, to best identify children with an asthma ED visit who are at risk for repeat asthma ED visits or hospital admissions over a one-year timeframe, using routinely collected information in the Epic EMR at a tertiary care pediatric centre in Ontario, Canada. Future implementation of an accurate ML algorithm into clinical workflows in the ED would allow streamlined preventative care for children most at risk for repeat asthma ED visits or hospitalizations.

Results

In this study, we used retrospective structured Epic EMR data to train ML models for predicting repeat asthma emergency department visits or hospital admissions within one year, among patients discharged following treatment for an asthma exacerbation (index visit) in the ED at the Children’s Hospital of Eastern Ontario (CHEO), a tertiary care centre in Ontario, Canada serving a catchment of 1.5 million people. These “high risk” patients would be referred to the CHEO asthma program, a specialized asthma care program that triages patients to receive asthma education41 by a certified asthma educator and/or referral to an asthma specialist at the discretion of the asthma educator42.

ML model performance

We considered two types of boosted decision trees and three open-source LLMs with finetuning. The two types of boosted trees evaluated were LGBM44 and xgboost45. Tree-based models are the most common type of ML prognostic methods used in clinical research46–51, they perform better than linear models, such as logistic regression47–51, and better than deep learning on tabular datasets52,53. In order to use an LLM for tabular data, we applied a textual encoder34 which transforms each tabular record into text.

Final AIRE-KIDS models

Based on the feature importance from the SHAP analysis, we chose a smaller set of features for both outcomes and retrained the LGBM models. These more parsimonious models would be easier to deploy in practice and would have similar performance as the models with all of the features included.

For the primary ED visit outcome, the selected features in the final AIRE-KIDSED model based on the highest ranked predictors in Fig. 1 were: prior asthma ED visit, CTAS, complexity, food allergy, prior ED visits for non-asthma respiratory diagnoses, and age. Using these features only and maximizing the AUC value on the training dataset with nested cross-validation we obtained an AUC of 0.712, and F1 of 0.51 at a cutoff threshold of 0.250. A sensitivity model for the outcome of asthma ED visit (without specialized asthma care) had 5 out of 6 of the same top-ranked predictive features (i.e., CTAS, prior asthma ED visit, age, complexity, prior ED visits for non-asthma respiratory diagnoses), plus triage duration which replaced food allergy as a new feature that was not in the primary model. Discussion

This study describes the development of the AIRE-KIDS ML models, designed to predict repeat asthma acute care visits within one year of a prior asthma ED visit at a tertiary care pediatric ED. The LGBM model yielded the best performance on a large validation dataset demonstrating good discrimination for predicting repeat asthma ED visits or future hospital admission. These models additionally demonstrated improved performance compared to the current ED-based CHEO best practice alert in use at our centre for identifying children at risk for repeat asthma acute care visits.. Therefore, the deployment of the LGBM model would result in a meaningful improvement in the decision-making process.

AIRE-KIDS also outperformed all tested LLM-based prediction approaches. While LLMs are gaining popularity as they offer flexible and broad applications and can be easier to deploy, they lack the precision required for targeted clinical decision-making. The LGBM model used here offers a task-specific, interpretable alternative, with greater transparency and trustworthiness critical for clinical adoption. This improved performance represents a major opportunity to more effectively triage children who would most benefit from comprehensive asthma care to reduce the volume of repeat acute care visits at our centre.

While the AUC is a traditional benchmark of discriminative performance in ML models, it is insufficient in clinical decision-making contexts where system constraints and resource limitations must be explicitly considered. For example, the CHEO asthma program is limited in its annual capacity to accommodate new referrals, and it is not feasible to accept every child predicted to be at risk of a repeat asthma acute care visit into the program. Accordingly, the prediction objective was to prioritize identification of true high-risk cases, maximizing precision to ensure that referrals represent children most likely to benefit from specialized care, thereby improving efficient use of limited specialized asthma care resources. At the same time, overly restrictive models risk missing clinically important cases; therefore, recall was equally optimized to minimize false negatives. For these reasons the AIRE-KIDS models were tuned to jointly optimize precision and recall, with the F1 score prioritized as the primary criterion for model selection, providing a more clinically meaningful assessment of performance than AUC alone. As such the AIRE-KIDSED model could be considered the optimal model for clinical implementation at our centre given its higher F1 score relative to the baseline.

The most predictive features for predicting repeat ED visits in the AIRE-KIDSED model included previous asthma-related ED visits, medical complexity, triage acuity (using CTAS), food allergy, prior non-asthma ED visits and age. The PRAM score at triage and average ED wait time were additional key features in the AIRE-KIDSHOSP for predicting future hospitalization. Although environmental and social variables (e.g., air quality, marginalization index) were considered, they did not significantly contribute to model performance and were excluded from the final models to improve parsimony and clinical utility.

There is a large literature on predicting asthma acute care visits in children54–56, with previous studies ranging from descriptions of patient-facing algorithms that require direct input of symptom data (e.g., questionnaires14,15) to predict short term deterioration or immediate hospital admission from the ED19, to studies involving large health administrative data10 to predict acute care visits in a longer timeframe. While these latter studies are similar to ours and could be used to identify children at risk for repeat acute care visits to enable optimization of preventative asthma management, many rely on predictors that may not be immediately available at the ED point of care. For example, Wang et al. looked at claims data for 28,378 pediatric patients and developed ML models with higher AUC’s (0.84) than AIRE-KIDS but lower F1 scores (0.2) for predicting repeat ED visit in 3 months17. Reliance of these models on certain administrative data (e.g., insurance status, medication dispensation) which have a reported lag-time of 3–9 months implies this information would not be readily available to clinicians, making real-time deployment challenging. In contrast, AIRE-KIDS achieves a more balanced performance using real-time data at the point of care, increasing its feasibility for clinical deployment directly from the ED.

Our study methods and findings align with those by Das et al.6, and Gorham et al.16 who also developed ML models using EMR data from US centers to predict asthma-related repeat ED visits over a 1-year window. While these models showed comparable performance (AUC 0.76–0.86) and identified certain key features that are similar to AIRE-KIDS (e.g., previous ED use, age), other features such as insurance status were not applicable to our models. These differences in important feature highlight contextual differences between countries and healthcare systems and demonstrate the challenges in broad generalizability of clinical ML models and the importance of locally trained and validated models for most effective implementation.

One limitation to the AIRE-KIDS models is whether they would be generalizable to other centers, and this would require additional validation to ascertain. Likely additional training or calibration would be required to reflect local case mix and practice variation, even within a similar healthcare system16. However, by prioritizing transparency, and parsimony with fewer features to allow for easy clinical deployment and integration into EMRs with limited computing resources available at the point-of-care, AIRE-KIDS offers a practical solution for real-world settings that could likely be scaled to other centers with minimal retraining.

An additional limitation of our study includes that we were restricted to using local healthcare visit data and may have missed some outcomes when children visited other centers for asthma exacerbations. However, as more than 94% of all children living within a 50 km radius attend CHEO for their acute care, the magnitude of these missed outcomes is likely small, and would not have significantly changed results.

Certain variables were missed from inclusion as they are difficult to obtain from EMR data, including adherence to asthma controller medications or exposure to cigarette smoke in the home; nonetheless even without these variables the performance of the AIRE-KIDS models offer a significant improvement over current practice in our ED and would be considered adequate for clinical adoption. Future iterations of the AIRE-KIDS model could consider the inclusion of unstructured data (e.g., NLP scripts) that might capture these additional variables from clinical notes and improve model performance.

Including receipt of specialized asthma care (asthma education or respirology clinic visits) within the composite outcomes of repeat asthma ED visit or future asthma hospitalization may introduce misclassification by implicitly equating these different types of encounters. However, in sensitivity models retrained on restricted definitions of the outcomes, limited to repeat asthma ED visits and hospitalizations only identified largely the same top predictive features and demonstrated comparable performance. This indicates that that inclusion of specialized asthma care in the original outcomes did not meaningfully bias model performance.

Our dataset excluded patients with a prior attendance to a respiratory appointment or those who received comprehensive asthma education prior to the index visit, or those admitted during that same hospital encounter. These patients already receive an intervention and therefore this reduces their likelihood of future asthma ED visits or asthma hospitalizations. Their inclusion would have negatively impacted the performance of our predictive models since that would weaken the relationships between the features and the outcomes. Centers without an existing baseline intervention would include all patients in their modeling.

To conclude, AIRE-KIDSED and AIRE-KIDSHOSP are new ML models for accurately predicting repeat asthma related ED visits or future asthma-related hospitalizations in children, and are superior to the current standard of care process for triaging referrals for specialized asthma care from the ED. LGBM remains a superior ML approach for modeling this type of clinical prediction, outperforming newer LLMs, despite their popularity in other applications, though ongoing research in this area is needed as LLM’s continue to evolve.

The next steps to realize the clinical utility of the AIRE-KIDS models include ED clinician assessment of acceptability and readiness for implementation followed by prospective silent validation to ensure stable real-time performance. This could eventually lead to integration into a point-of-care clinical decision support system for local deployment and prospective evaluation. Future successful and broad implementation of AIRE-KIDS could redefine how we triage and manage pediatric asthma in the acute care setting, supporting more equitable access to comprehensive or specialist care while reducing the morbidity associated with repeat exacerbations that result in ED visits and hospitalizations and their burden on the healthcare system, children and families.

Methods

Study design

For our outcome measures, repeat asthma ED visits or hospital admissions were defined as events with asthma listed as the most responsible diagnosis within one year following an index asthma ED visit. The ‘index visit’ was defined as the first ED visit with a diagnosis of asthma (i.e., International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems version 10 (ICD-10) codes J45, J46) within the defined accrual period (see Fig. 4). We used two different retrospective datasets for training and validating models. The training dataset (pre-COVID-19) accrued patients over 2 years between February 2017 and March 2019, with outcomes observed up to March 2020. Similarly, we created a post-COVID validation dataset with an accrual period from July 2022 to April 2023, with outcomes observed up to April 2024. Current standard of care

The ML models were compared with the current standard of care at CHEO, which relies on a rules-based best practice alert within the EMR. This alert flags children in the ED who meet a small number of predefined criteria. These criteria were derived from a local retrospective study and are associated with a higher risk of repeat asthma acute care visits58. (see Fig. 3). Whenever a child with asthma meeting these criteria arrives in the ED, an alert pops up in the EMR, directing the ED clinician to refer the child to the CHEO asthma program.

This study is reported according to Consolidated Reporting of Machine Learning Studies (CREMLS) reporting guidelines59. The reporting checklist is included in the Supplementary Information.

Predictors/features

Among the features extracted from the EMR, we included measures of prior health care utilization, including previous asthma-related ED visits or admissions, as well as prior-non asthma respiratory ED visits or admissions within one year prior to the index visit.

We captured operational features related to each of these healthcare visits including factors reflecting the volume of patients and wait time in the ED such as day and month of the index visit, time from arrival to triage or disposition, the hourly average number of patients present in the ED as well as the average wait time during each patient’s visit. Demographic features included age during the index visit, sex, whether the patient has a primary care physician, the distance of the hospital from the postal code of the patient’s home and the province of the patient’s home as patients from both Ontario and the neighboring province of Quebec are treated at CHEO.

We additionally included features reflecting the severity of the asthma presentation at the index visit such as the first oxygen saturation recording, timing of order and administration of oral corticosteroids, the Pediatric Respiratory Assessment Measure (PRAM) score at triage, and the Canadian Triage Acuity Score (CTAS). PRAM is a previously validated tool to indicate the severity of respiratory symptoms during an asthma exacerbation and informs algorithm based asthma management in the ED (i.e., score 1–3 = mild, 4–7 = moderate, 8–12 = severe)60, and CTAS is a triage scale ranging from one to five that is used for all ED patients (level 1 = highest) to identify illness acuity and the time frame for assessment61.

Among the clinical variables we included were the presence in the previous one year of: food and environmental allergies, lab test results such as serum eosinophils count and immunoglobulin E levels, medical complexity as reflected in prior outpatient visits to CHEO subspecialty clinics other than respirology over the previous year, and the presence of specific comorbidities that are shown to correlate with asthma acute care visit risk (e.g., atopic dermatitis, sinusitis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, preterm gestation, gastroesophageal reflux, recurrent aspiration, obesity, anxiety, depression).

For a subset of the cohort, that is patients residing in Ontario, we additionally included features related to air pollution and neighborhood marginalization quintile. Specifically, the Air Quality Health Index (AQHI), a composite measure of air pollution that is shown to correlate with health and emergency department use62, as well as mean and highest Nitrogen Dioxide, Ozone, and PM2.5 readings were considered at different intervals (i.e., 1 day, 2 days and 3 days prior to the index visit) as measured at specified postal codes in the catchment area of CHEO. The residence postal code of each patient was matched to the nearest postal code where air pollutants were measured, to allow linkage of these features. Similarly, we used residence postal code to link with each child’s neighborhood marginalization quintile as defined by the Ontario marginalization (ON-Marg) index, a validated tool that classifies marginalization in 4 domains: households and dwellings – a reflection of family and neighborhood stability, material resources, age and labor force – a measure of disability and dependence, and racialized and newcomer populations.

Data and study population

We included all children 0-18 years of age, presenting to the CHEO ED for management of an asthma exacerbation (ICD code J45, J46) during the study accrual period. We have previously reported on the accuracy of identifying asthma patients at our centre using these codes in the EMR (i.e., positive predictive value 0.91 (95%CI 0.86–0.94), negative predictive value 0.92 (95%CI 0.89–0.94). Patients were excluded if they had attended specialized asthma care (i.e., an asthma specialist appointment or received asthma education) prior to the index visit, or if they were admitted to hospital during the same encounter (i.e., on the same day) as the index asthma ED visit. Data related to clinical characteristics and hospital, outpatient or ED visits were collected from the hospital Epic EMR system while social and environmental variables were obtained from public data sources63.

Ethics review

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board (REB) of the Children’s Hospital of Eastern Ontario Research Institute under protocol number CHEOREB# 22/11X on 14th February 2022. The study involved the secondary analysis of de-identified data for internal analysis by researchers within the hospital research institute only, and therefore the REB determined that the study did not require participant informed consent - the consent requirement was waived by the research ethics board.

Outcomes

Children or families who participate in the CHEO asthma program and receive specialized asthma care may have a reduced risk of repeat asthma acute care visits. This potential source of ‘contamination’ of the dataset was accounted for by defining both the repeat ED visit outcome or admission outcome as: (1) patients who returned to the ED for asthma, or who received specialized asthma care, all within one year of the index asthma ED visit, and (2) patients who were admitted to hospital for asthma, or who received specialized asthma care, all within one year of the index asthma ED visit. (3) As sensitivity analyses, we additionally modeled the more restricted version of each of these outcomes, (i.e., repeat asthma ED visits, and future asthma admission) without the inclusion of specialized asthma.

ML models

The hyperparameters of boosted tree models were tuned and optimized using Bayesian optimization with 5-fold cross-validation64. The range for the tuning parameters, specific to each model, was defined as recommended in the literature65–68. For binary classification using boosted trees, the predicted values are not true probabilities that reflect likelihood of an outcome among patients69,70. Therefore, these pseudo probabilities were calibrated after the fact using beta calibration, which provides optimal results for small data sets and boosted trees modeling approaches71.

The LLMs, initially pretrained to generate text, were fine-tuned to predict the outcome variables from the data. This is explained further below. To address model stochasticity, all predictions were generated five times for each patient and the predicted values were averaged.

Following the privacy rules at our centre, we were not able to utilize the commercial off-site LLM’s for this study and were limited to those that could be executed locally. We fine-tuned three pretrained open-source LLMs available on HuggingFace72: DistilGPT2, Llama 3.2 1B73 and Llama-8b-UltraMedical. The model DistilGPT2 is a lightweight LLM obtained from applying knowledge distillation74 on GPT-275, a model from OpenAI. DistilGPT2 has been used in most publications that tackle fine-tuning LLMs for tabular data34,39,76. Llama 3.2 1B is a more recent model released by Meta and trained on general semantic data and Llama-8b-UltraMedical is larger and has been specialized on medical data by using the UltraMedical dataset77.

Fine tuning LLMs

Our protocol is based on previous works such as GReaT from Borisov et al.34 and Pred-LLM from Nguyen et al.78. The process can be divided into three steps: tabular serialization, fine-tuning process, inference prompting.

Textual encoding or serialization

To leverage from LLM pretraining, we transform our tabular data into text. Multiple approaches with various complexities have been explored by Hegselmann et al.40 and the best performing one was to simply encode each feature value into “column name is feature value,” and concatenate all features constituting the record, as shown Fig. 5. Missing data is handled by adding “missing” string instead of feature value to keep all column names for each record. A missing feature would be encoded as “column name is missing,”. Fine-tuning process

We utilize the Pred-LLM permutation scheme where the correlation between the outcome variable Y and the other features X is preserved by always placing Y at the end of the sentence and shuffling the rest of the features. Since LLM decoders are trained with masked attention, placing the outcome variables at the end ensures the LLM can attend to them through all previously generated features.

The LLMs are then fine-tuned by predicting successive tokens for a certain number of epochs. Following Borisov et al. the number of epochs was set to 200 and we used low-rank adaptation method (LoRA)79 to efficiently fine-tune the LLM on one NVIDIA A6000 GPU.

Inference prompting

At inference time, the features get serialized following the textual encoding in Fig. 5 and we create the prompt but with the outcome variable missing and the LLM fills in the tokens to produce the answer as shown Fig. 6. Evaluating ML model performance

Capacity within the CHEO asthma program is resource-limited and may not be available to all patients with the predicted outcome. Therefore, we aimed to develop a model that maximizes the proportion of patients that were predicted as high risk that were actually high risk (precision), hence ensuring that limited resources are used most effectively. However, a model can trivially achieve high precision by correctly predicting a single patient as high risk. Therefore, it is also important to consider the proportion of high risk patients that were correctly predicted as high risk (recall). Therefore, the F1 score was deemed to be the most appropriate evaluation metric for our context, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

To compute recall and precision from the predicted probability, we experimented with different cutoff thresholds used in previous studies, for example, by selecting the number of positive predictions80, selecting a cutoff for a pre-determined specificity9, top percentile14,15,81, the use of a validation set82, and lowest cost from the cost function on the training cohort83. However, to reflect the aim of our study which was to identify the most accurate ML model to predict repeat asthma ED visits or hospital admission to streamline referral of patients directly from the ED to the CHEO asthma program, we selected cutoffs that would maximize the F1 score.

Using 5-fold cross-validation on the training dataset, the F1 score was maximized by setting different thresholds (cutoff values at which the predicted outcome is considered positive or negative) for precision and recall, ranging from 0.05 to 0.99. The threshold that achieved the highest F1 score was averaged across the five folds and subsequently selected as the threshold of choice. This optimized threshold was then applied when running the model on the validation dataset.

Evaluating performance of baselines, including current standard of care

Since the value of the F1 score is affected by prevalence, its proper interpretation requires comparison against one or more baselines. We established three baselines: (a) the CHEO best practice alert that is currently used in the ED to identify high risk patients (i.e., current standard), (b) a naïve prediction rule that predicts that everyone is high risk, and for the F1 score this gives the largest possible naïve value, and (c) a random prediction of risk status based on a 0.5 probability. The evaluation of the CHEO best practice alert did not consider those who received specialized asthma care as a positive outcome, so as not to positively bias the results.

In this study we also reported the AUC to enable comparison with previous studies, although the AUC does not consider the optimal cutoff for decision making, especially in a real-world setting.
