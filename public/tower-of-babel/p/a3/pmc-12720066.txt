Early Autumn: Drought Accelerates Leaf Senescence in Temperate Tree Species
Authors: Siyu Wang, Bridget K Murphy, Noelle Perkins, Ingo Ensminger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClimate warming has led to an increase of drought events with consequences on physiological processes in trees and implications for carbon sequestration. Drought may lead to changes in the timing of autumn phenology and premature senescence. However, mechanisms linking physiological responses to drought stress and changes in phenological events in tree species remain poorly understood. We investigated how drought impacts autumn phenology and the timing of leaf senescence in three temperate tree species, along with the associated physiological responses. For this purpose, we designed a factorial field experiment with the evergreen species white pine, and two deciduous species, red maple and white oak, and used rainout structures to reduce ~50% of precipitation in drought‐stressed plots compared to watered control plots. To assess drought impacts on autumn phenology and senescence we measured growth, photosynthesis, and leaf spectral reflectance. Drought‐stressed deciduous seedlings exhibited premature senescence compared to controls. Water deficit during the growing season also decreased photosynthetic rates, height growth and biomass accumulation. The photochemical reflectance index effectively captured drought‐induced changes in leaf physiology and phenology in white pine and the chlorophyll carotenoid index tracked the seasonality in deciduous trees, demonstrating that carotenoid‐based vegetation indices are promising tools for monitoring drought stress responses in temperate forests.Keywords: chlorophyll carotenoid index (CCI), chlorophyll fluorescence, drought, phenology, photochemical reflectance index (PRI), photosynthetic pigments

Introduction

Climate change and the increase in the intensity and frequency of drought events have a detrimental impact on forest ecosystems (Anderegg et al. 2018; Chan et al. 2018; Dai 2011; Jiang et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2021). In temperate forests, drought is mainly caused by low precipitation and often coupled with high temperature (Gampe et al. 2021) and high vapor pressure deficit (VPD; Dannenberg et al. 2022; De Boeck and Verbeeck 2011). Drought leads to a decrease in leaf water potential, impairs water uptake and transport, and triggers structural, functional, and physiological responses that aim to minimize water loss (Griffiths and Parry 2002).

Responses of plants to drought events during the growing season have been widely documented (Dao et al. 2021; Koç et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2017). However, the impact of drought on vegetation phenology, and whether drought will advance or delay phenological events such as leaf senescence is unclear and inconclusive (Buermann et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2022), with some studies reporting no effect, others showing advanced, and some suggesting delayed senescence. For example, using leaf spectral reflectance data, Mariën et al. (2021) reported that drought did not affect the timing of autumn leaf senescence in mature European temperate deciduous forests. Similarly, a previous study found that reduced soil moisture alone had minimal impact on the autumn senescence of European beech and pubescent oak (Grossiord et al. 2022). Based on MODIS Global Vegetation Phenology data and flux tower data, Zohner et al. (2023) observed that drought did not affect the timing of autumn senescence in temperate forests but increased spring activity of vegetation premature senescence. Several other studies suggest summer drought can advance autumn senescence. Descals et al. (2023) identified widespread early leaf shedding across Europe following drought events based on Sentinel‐2 satellite data. Using satellite‐based vegetation indices and ground observation data from the Pan European Phenology Project, Wu et al. (2022) showed that drought caused premature autumn senescence in the northern hemisphere, and this trend has been becoming more significant over the past three decades. By contrast, studying central European tree species, Zani et al. (2020) reported delayed autumn senescence following drought. However, they observed that increased productivity in spring and summer can lead to early autumn senescence. In a field experiment where rainfall was excluded, Arend et al. (2016) observed a delayed onset of autumn senescence in drought‐stressed European beech trees as a consequence of temporary limitations of photosynthesis. Drought severity differentially affects the timing of leaf senescence across plant functional types (Zhang et al. 2025). The mechanisms connecting drought‐induced physiological stress to the timing of phenology remain poorly understood, creating uncertainty about drought impacts on the timing of autumn senescence and phenological events (Gallinat et al. 2015).

Autumn senescence in trees is a complex process that is not limited to the abscission of leaves in tree species. It also involves the downregulation of photosynthetic CO2 assimilation and changes in pigment composition that include decreasing chlorophyll content and increasing carotenoid to chlorophyll ratios (Busch et al. 2007; Ensminger et al. 2006, 2004). Downregulation of photosynthesis during autumn also involves changes in photoprotective processes, such as transient decreases of dynamic non‐photochemical quenching and an increased capacity for sustained non‐photochemical quenching in cold‐hardy conifers (Chang et al. 2021; Fréchette et al. 2016).

Changes in non‐photochemical quenching and pigment composition during autumn senescence can be tracked through chlorophyll fluorescence (Fréchette et al. 2020; Maxwell and Johnson 2000) and leaf spectral reflectance measurements (D'Odorico et al. 2015; Junker and Ensminger 2016b; Richardson et al. 2018). The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) is commonly used as a proxy for greenness and is driven by changes in leaf area and chlorophyll content (Huete et al. 2002; Tucker 1979). However, NDVI is less effective in tracking phenology in evergreen conifers due to limited variations in chlorophyll and leaf area throughout the seasons (D'Odorico et al. 2020; Wong et al. 2019). By contrast, carotenoid‐based vegetation indices such as the photochemical reflectance index (PRI) or chlorophyll carotenoid index (CCI) seem to be better suited for tracking phenology in evergreen conifers (Wong et al. 2019). PRI reflects dynamic photoprotective processes in the chloroplast, which are mediated by short‐term changes in the composition of the pigments of the xanthophyll cycle (Gamon et al. 1992). The PRI is a valuable tool for monitoring changes in the de‐epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle and pigment pool sizes in evergreen conifers (Busch et al. 2009; Fréchette et al. 2020; Wong et al. 2022). The CCI tracks the seasonality of the ratio of carotenoids to chlorophylls (Car/Chl) and can effectively detect the start and end of the growing season in evergreens (D'Odorico et al. 2020; Gamon et al. 2016). Unfortunately, canopy or satellite‐scale remotely sensed spectral reflectance, which usually is a mix of signals that represents all trees and species within the field of view of the sensor (Zeng, Hao, et al. 2022). This mixed signal complicates the quantification of drought responses for individual trees and species. Therefore, to elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying seasonal dynamics in temperate forests, it is crucial to untangle the mixed spectral signals.

This study aimed to elucidate how drought influences autumn senescence during early life stages, given that seedlings are generally more sensitive to environmental changes than mature trees, and to assess whether remote sensing–based vegetation indices can effectively detect drought‐induced advancement of senescence. For this, we conducted a field experiment comparing drought‐stressed and control seedlings of three key tree species of east‐central Canada's northern temperate forest: red maple, white oak, and white pine. We investigated how prolonged drought (i) impacts photosynthetic activity, height growth, and biomass partitioning, (ii) how it may affect the timing of leaf senescence, and (iii) whether changes in spectral reflectance and vegetation indices can detect drought‐induced premature senescence in red maple, white oak, and white pine.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Field Site and Plant Material

The experiment was conducted at the outdoor growth facility at the University of Toronto Mississauga campus located in Mississauga, Ontario, Canada (43°32′54″ N, 79°39′46″ W). Two‐year‐old seedlings of Quercus alba L. (white oak), Acer rubrum L. (red maple), and Pinus strobus L. (white pine) were obtained from a local nursery (Somerville Seedlings, Everett, ON, Canada). Ninety seedlings per species were planted in six custom‐built wooden raised beds (1 × 2 m) on 05 May 2022 before bud burst. Each raised bed was split into three sections, with 15 seedlings of each species planted in each section for a total of 45 seedlings in each raised bed (Figure 1a). The experiment included two treatments: a drought treatment, where rainfall was reduced by ~50% with rainout structures made from transparent plexiglas (Figure 1b), and a control treatment where the plexiglas rainout structures were reversed, so that rainfall would not be excluded from the boxes and the light conditions were maintained consistent across the two treatments (Figure 1c). This resulted in a total of three raised beds as a control and three raised beds for the drought treatment. First measurements began on 14 June 2022. Rainout structures were installed on 27 June 2022. On 23 November 2022, 18 randomly selected seedlings were harvested for above and below ground biomass measurements. The remaining seedlings remained untreated over the winter and were used for phenology observations during spring 2023. Field measurements and sampling were done at weekly to biweekly intervals for a total of 15 measurement and sampling time points. This included six non‐destructive measurement campaigns (fluorescence and spectral measurements) and nine destructive measurement campaigns (gas exchange, pre‐dawn water potential, tissue sampling, fluorescence, and leaf spectral reflectance measurements).

Meteorological data were obtained from the UTM Meteorological Station using HOBO U30 data loggers equipped with sensors monitoring air temperature and precipitation every half‐hour. Soil moisture sensors (TEROS12, Meters group) were installed in each box, measuring soil moisture at a depth of 15 cm at 15‐min time intervals.

Pre‐Dawn Leaf Water Potential Measurements

Pre‐dawn leaf water potential was measured biweekly between 2:30 and 5:00 a.m. with a Scholander pressure chamber (model 1505D, PMS Instruments). Water potential was determined using individual leaves and needles as described by Rennenberg et al. (1996). During each campaign, measurements were taken from two seedlings per species in each raised bed, with a total of three raised beds per treatment.

Photosynthesis and Chlorophyll Fluorescence Measurements

A portable gas exchange system (Li‐6400XT, LICOR) was used to measure photosynthesis. Measurements were performed using a Li‐Cor 6400‐40 measuring cuvette on a bundle of pine needles or a single deciduous leaf. During measurements, leaves remained attached to the seedling. The system was set to a flow rate of 400 mL/min, 55% relative humidity and a CO2 concentration of 400 ppm. Cuvette temperature was set to match ambient temperature. Maximum CO2 assimilation (A max) and stomatal conductance (g s ) were measured at 1000 μmol photons m−2 s−1 photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) once steady‐state assimilation was achieved. Immediately after measurements, leaves were collected. Leaf area was measured using the WINSEEDLE package, an image analysis system for needles and seeds (Regent Instruments Inc.).

Fluorescence measurements were conducted using a Dual‐PAM‐100 fluorometer (Heinz Walz GmbH) with a leaf clip (DLC‐8, Walz). Prior to measurements, leaves and needles underwent a 30‐min dark adaptation. Minimal fluorescence (Fo) was first recorded, and a short saturation pulse was applied for the determination of maximal fluorescence (F m ) of the dark‐adapted leaves/needles. Spectral Reflectance Measurements

Changes in optical properties of the leaves were assessed through spectral reflectance measurements using a portable spectrometer (UniSpec‐SC, PP Systems) equipped with fiberoptic (Part No. UNI684, PP Systems). A field of view (FOV) restrictor (Hypo‐tube, Part No. UNI688, PP Systems) was used to limit the FOV to a 20° angle. The spectral resolution of the UniSpec is 10 nm with a ∼3 nm sampling interval. During measurements the fiber optic was kept at a distance of 15 cm from a zenith angle above the apical bud of the seedling, which created a footprint with a diameter of approximately 5 cm2. The UniSpec‐SC captures spectral reflectance from 400 to 1100 nm. Reflectance of each band was calculated by dividing radiance by the radiance from a white reference standard (Spectralon, Labsphere) taken prior to the measurement. A dark current correction scan preceded white reference scans to correct for dark current instrument noise. Measurement integration time was set to 10 ms, and 10 scans were automatically averaged for each recorded measurement. Analysis of Photosynthetic Pigments

Immediately after the reflectance measurements, samples of sun‐exposed leaf were selected to match the spectral measurement footprint and then flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in a −80°C freezer in the laboratory. Frozen leaf samples were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Pigments were extracted using a buffer that contained 98% methanol and 2% 0.5 M ammonium acetate and filtered using a 0.45 μm nylon filter following the protocol of Junker and Ensminger (2016a). Pigment extracts were analyzed with an Infinity 1200 series high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with a UV‐diode array detector (Agilent Technologies) to separate and quantify the pigments (Junker and Ensminger 2016a). Isoprenoids were separated on a reverse‐phase YMC C30‐column (5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm, YMC Co. Ltd.). The mobile phase consisted of a methanol gradient, water buffered with 0.2% ammonium acetate, and methyl tert‐butyl ether. The analysis was performed at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. Pigments were detected at 450 and 656 nm. Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b were quantified using standards from Sigma‐Aldrich. Antheraxanthin, b‐carotene, lutein, neoxanthin, violaxanthin and zeaxanthin were quantified using standards from DHI Lab Products (Hørsholm, Denmark). Total chlorophyll (Chl) content was expressed as the sum of Chl a and Chl b on a fresh weight (FW) basis (μmol g−1). Total carotenoids per total Chlorophyll (Car/Chl) was expressed as the sum of violaxanthin (V), antheraxanthin (A), zeaxanthin (Z), neoxanthin, lutein, α‐carotene, and β‐carotene, divided by Chl content (mmol mol−1). Height Growth and Biomass Measurements

Height growth was measured for all the seedlings on a weekly to biweekly basis using a measuring tape. The measuring tape was carefully positioned perpendicular to the ground, extending from the base to the top of the seedlings. Measurements were recorded in centimeters. Cumulative height growth was calculated as the difference between the seedling height measured on each observation date and its initial height recorded at the start of the experiment.

In early October, clear plastic nets (1.75 × 2.38 cm mesh size) were placed around the deciduous seedlings to collect abscised leaves. Leaves were dried and kept in paper bags for dry weight determination. Following the measurement campaign on November 23, 2022, entire seedlings were excavated from the soil to estimate the total biomass in the lab. Following the removal of soil and the cleaning of fine roots, the seedlings were divided into leaf, shoot, and root components. Dry weight was determined using a precision balance (NewClassic MF, MS3002S/03, Mettler Toledo) after samples were dried in an oven at 60°C for 72 h.

Determination of Phenophases

Phenophase observations were performed to quantify the onset and duration of phenological stages of plants according to Denny et al. (2014). The percentage of colored leaves and the proportion of the canopy covered with leaves were recorded (i.e., 5, 25, 50, 75, and 100%) to estimate leaf colouration, leaf abscission, and leaf growth accordingly. These observations were conducted every 3–5 days. We then calculated the date when 50% of the leaves were either colored or had fallen using linear interpolations between two monitoring dates when necessary.

To estimate the timing of the start of autumn senescence, we also fitted chlorophyll fluorescence parameters and vegetation indices using the “phenopix” R package (Filippa et al. 2023) in version 4.2.2 (R Core Team 2013). This method fits a double logistic function to time‐series leaf physiology and vegetation indices data. The start of down regulation (SOD) was then identified using the first local maximum (turning point) in the fitted curve, marking the transition from peak greenness to the decline phase as defined by Gu et al. (2003). This day signifies the transition from a relatively stable phase in the middle of the growing season to the subsequent rapid decline observed during the senescence phase (Gu et al. 2009).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2; R Core Team 2013). To evaluate the effects of treatment on time series data including photosynthetic gas exchange, chlorophyll fluorescence, vegetation indices, photosynthetic pigment parameters and height at each time point, a linear mixed‐effects model (LMM) was applied (Bates 2009). Treatment was included as a fixed effect, while plot was treated as a random effect to account for potential variation at the plot level. The model was fitted independently for each species and each variable, using data from the different days of the year. We then used a pairwise comparison to determine the significant difference between drought and control seedlings (Lenth et al. 2025).

To evaluate differential responses of energy partitioning, we used a two‐way ANOVA that included the interaction term between treatment (drought, control) and season (summer, autumn). Differences across treatments and seasons were then examined, with post hoc comparisons using Tukey's test. A paired‐sample t‐test was used to identify differences in the timing of leaf senescence between drought and control treatment. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Seasonal Dynamics of Temperature, Precipitation and Soil Water Content

Precipitation during the experiment was typical for the temperate climate of southern Ontario, with moderate rainfall throughout spring, summer, and autumn (Figure 2a). The seasonal variation in daily mean temperature from 02 May to 23 November 2022 ranged from 28°C to −4.9°C. Following the installation of the rainout structures on 29 June, and despite a series of heavy rain events during mid‐July, we observed a transient and significant decrease in soil water content in the rainout treatment. From mid‐July to the end of the experiment, the average soil water content was 0.24 m3/m3 in the control treatment and only 0.14 m3/m3 in the drought treatment (Figure 2b).

Leaf Physiology in Response to Drought

On 14 June, before the installation of any rainout structures, we conducted the first measurement to obtain baseline values for all seedlings; on this date we did not observe any significant differences between the seedlings (Figure 3). For pre‐dawn leaf water potential, we observed the first significant differences between control and drought‐stressed seedlings for white oak on 24 August (Figure 3b) and on 08 September for red maple and white pine (Figure 3a,b). On 08 September, eight drought‐stressed seedlings showed the lowest pre‐dawn leaf water potential with about −1.5 MPa across all species, following a period of high air temperatures, minimal precipitation, and low soil water content. During this period, leaf water potential in control seedlings remained high with values of −0.5 MPa and above.

Differences in leaf water potential corresponded with the differences in the rate of assimilation (A max) and stomatal conductance (g s ; Figure 3). The highest A max and g s values for control seedlings in all three species were observed during August, followed by decreasing values during October. A max and g s of the drought‐stressed seedlings showed a significant decrease compared to the control seedlings after 02 August for red maple and white pine, whereas A max and g s in drought‐stressed white oak did not decrease until 24 August (Figure 3e,h).

Similar trends between drought‐stressed and control seedlings were also observed through chlorophyll fluorescence measurements. Maximum quantum yield F v /F m decreased in control seedlings of all species towards the end of the growing season (Figure 3j–l). In drought‐stressed seedlings, F v /F m decreased 2–3 weeks earlier compared to control seedlings.

In control seedlings, dynamic non‐photochemical quenching (Φ NPQ) showed an initial decrease on the 24 August in oak seedlings, followed by a decrease on the 08 September for maple and pine seedlings (Figure 3m–o). Towards the end of the growing season the final decrease in Φ NPQ occurred in sequence, starting first in red maple on the 14 October, then in white oak on the 24 October, and finally in white pine on the 03 November (Figure 3m–o). The drought‐stressed seedlings showed a markedly different pattern. These seedlings displayed increased Φ NPQ from mid‐August onwards, followed by an early decrease starting in maple on 29 September, followed by oak on 24 October. This pattern demonstrates that drought‐stressed seedlings responded both earlier and more rapidly compared to control seedlings.

The seasonal dynamics in Φ NPQ shown in Figure 3m–o point towards re‐arrangements in excitation energy partitioning during summer and autumn, and among control and drought stressed seedlings. Figure 4 compares differences in excitation energy partitioning under control or drought conditions for the different species and illustrates how absorbed light energy is converted into photochemical energy (Φ PSII), and how much of the absorbed light exceeds the capacity for photochemical quenching and is instead quenched via dynamic Φ NPQ or sustained non‐photochemical quenching (Φ f,D). Drought stress caused a decrease in Φ PSII during summer in all three species compared to control seedlings. This drought‐induced decrease in Φ PSII was paralleled by an increase in Φ NPQ, while Φ f,D remained stable during the summer in all treatments and species (Figure 4a; Table S1). For drought stressed deciduous seedlings, we observed an increase in energy quenching via Φ f,D, at the expense of Φ NPQ in autumn (late October) compared to the control (Figure 4a,b; Table S1). For white pine, drought‐stressed seedlings didn't show significant higher Φ f,D in late October compared to control seedlings until late October (Figure 4c; Table S1; Figure S1).

In autumn, the ratio of carotenoids to chlorophylls (Car/Chl) increased in control seedlings of all species (Figure 5d–f). Red maple saw a tenfold increase in October, while white oak and white pine had moderate increases of 2.5 times and 1.25 times towards November and December, respectively. In contrast, drought‐stressed white oak and red maple seedlings showed significant increases in Car/Chl as early as August, with white pine showing an increase in November.

For all three species, the de‐epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle pigments (DEPS) decreased in summer, followed by an increase in autumn. Starting in August, drought‐stressed white pine seedlings consistently had higher DEPS levels compared to control seedlings (Figure 5g–i).

Height Growth in Response to Drought

Height growth commenced in all seedlings in mid‐June and ceased in October. Red maple showed the greatest cumulative height growth, followed by white oak and white pine (Figure 6). We also observed differences in height growth and timing of growth cessation between species and in response to the drought treatment. As autumn approached, the differences between control and drought‐stressed maple became most pronounced, with average heights of 54.18 ± 4.76 cm for control seedlings and 23.84 ± 3.35 cm for drought‐stressed seedlings. In contrast, the average height in control and drought‐stressed white pine was 18.54 ± 2.78 cm and 14.87 ± 1.21 cm, respectively, and this difference was not significant. Although we observed significant species‐specific variability in growth, none of the species displayed signs of drought‐induced mortality during the duration of the experiment.

Early Leaf Abscission in Autumn and Delayed Leaf Growth in Spring in Response to Summer Drought

Deciduous seedlings in the drought treatment exhibited earlier autumn senescence in 2022 and delayed leaf‐out in spring 2023 compared to controls (Figures 7 and S3). The drought‐stressed red maple and white oak exhibited leaf coloration 15.6 days and 21.3 days earlier than seedlings in the control treatment (Figure S3; Table S2). Leaf abscission occurred 12 days earlier in drought‐stressed red maple than in the control (Figure S3), nevertheless, the timing of 50% leaf abscission did not differ significantly between the drought and control treatments, even though the progression was delayed (Figures 7d and S3b). In addition, drought‐stressed red maple and white oak seedlings exhibited carry‐over effects in late spring 2023, with delayed spring leaf growth of 4.6 and 3.3 days respectively compared to the controls (Figures 7e,f and S3). In contrast to the deciduous species, seedlings of the evergreen conifer white pine maintained a consistent green needle color during autumn. Bud break of white pine was visually inspected, but no differences were observed between the treatments.

Leaf Spectral Reflectance in Response to Drought

Leaf spectral reflectance measurements were performed to capture phenology and the impact of drought (Figure 8). During summer and autumn, drought‐stressed red maple and white oak seedlings exhibited higher reflectance in the visible spectrum (450–700 nm) but lower reflectance in the near‐infrared spectrum (700–1000 nm) compared to control seedlings. For white pine, the difference in leaf spectral reflectance between summer and autumn was in the visible part of the spectrum (400–700 nm; Figure 8). In autumn, the reflectance spectra of drought‐stressed red maple and white oak showed a prominent shift of the reflectance peak in the visible spectrum and a smoother increase towards the red edge (670–760 nm). Drought‐stressed white pine seedlings showed a similar pattern but with slightly higher values across the full range of wavelengths (Figure 8c,f). In addition, there's an enhanced reflectance observed around 970 nm, which corresponds to a water absorption band in the control seedlings relative to the drought‐stressed seedlings in the spectral reflectance in autumn for all three species (Figure 8d–f).

We extracted the vegetation indices NDVI, CCI, and PRI from the leaf spectral reflectance data to assess phenological changes during the summer‐autumn transition. Significant treatment effects were observed on all three vegetation indices (Figure 9). NDVI showed strong seasonality for red maple and white oak, indicated by decreases in autumn, but remained relatively constant for white pine (Figure 9a–c). CCI effectively tracked seasonality in deciduous species, as indicated by divergence in the CCI signal between drought‐stressed and control seedlings at the onset of senescence (Figure 9d,e), but not for white pine (Figure 9f). In contrast, PRI tracked drought stress responses and seasonality in white pine, with decreased PRI values during summer drought and faster decreases in autumn compared to control seedlings (Figure 9h).

To identify sensitive predictors of drought stress and advanced leaf senescence, we estimated and compared the start of the downregulation of photosynthesis for several parameters related to photosynthetic activity (Figure 10). We used the start of the downregulation of photosynthesis determined by F v /F m measurements as the reference point to mark the onset of downregulation of photosynthesis. Red maple seedlings showed no significant difference in the onset of downregulation of photosynthesis between drought and control treatments, despite senescence being advanced by an average of 10 days. For red maple, CCI provided estimates for the onset of the downregulation of photosynthesis that were most comparable to those derived from F v /F m measurements (Figure 10a). In white oak, the downregulation of photosynthesis occurred 23 days earlier under drought stress based on F v /F m measurements. In white oak, CCI also provided estimates for the onset of photosynthesis downregulation that closely matched those derived from F v /F m measurements (Figure 10b). For white pine, the start of the downregulation of photosynthesis determined by F v /F m did not reveal any difference between drought‐stressed and control seedlings; similar estimates were obtained using PRI (Figure 10c).

Discussion

Decreased Photosynthetic Activity, Height Growth and Biomass Accumulation in Response to Summer Drought

We manipulated water availability in a rainout experiment which led to a significant decrease in soil moisture in the drought treatment and a decrease in pre‐dawn leaf water potential to below −1.5 MPa in drought‐stressed seedlings. Across all three species, drought‐stressed seedlings consistently exhibited lower maximum CO2 assimilation rates, decreased height growth, and lower biomass accumulation (Figure S2). A decrease in CO2 assimilation under drought stress in temperate trees is usually driven by a decrease in stomatal conductance (Figure 3g–i) to prevent water loss through transpiration (Cornic and Fresneau 2002). Nevertheless, we observed differences between the tree species, such that white oak maintained higher photosynthetic rates and stomatal conductance under drought conditions, whereas white pine and red maple revealed more flexible stomatal control by closing their stomata earlier under drought (Figure 3g–i). These findings align with a previous study by Zweifel et al. (2009), which concluded that downy oak maintains higher stomatal conductance under drought stress, while Scots pine closes its stomata almost completely during summer drought.

The decrease in CO2 assimilation coincided with decreased height growth in all three tree species exposed to drought (Figure 6). The changes in leaf physiology and height growth under drought stress further led to significantly lower biomass for red maple and white pine at the end of the growing season. In contrast, the biomass of white oak did not significantly differ between control and drought plots (Figure S2). Our results were different from a previous study where a higher root/shoot ratio was found in white pine compared to balsam fir and concolor fir (Koç et al. 2022). It might be attributed to the fact that aboveground biomass responded only about half as much to experimentally imposed drought events compared to natural droughts (Kröel‐Dulay et al. 2022), and it could also be because the experimental drought was not sufficiently severe.

In addition, it's worth noting that seedlings often exhibit higher vulnerability to drought and temperature extremes and possess distinct carbohydrate dynamics compared to mature trees (Hartmann et al. 2018; Pearson and D'Orangeville 2022). Although seedlings offer valuable insights into recruitment and establishment processes, they cannot be regarded as direct proxies for mature tree responses due to fundamental differences in physiology and stress tolerance (Lee and Schaffer‐Morrison 2024).

Premature Senescence and Legacy Effects on Subsequent Spring Phenology in Response to Drought

Drought may alter the timing of phenological events in temperate forests, yet the response of autumn senescence to drought conditions is poorly understood (Gallinat et al. 2015). Our rainout experiment discerned premature senescence in response to drought. The timing of autumn senescence, estimated using 50% leaf coloration as an indicator, showed that drought had the strongest effect on white oak, followed by red maple (Figure 7). 50% leaf abscission was not a reliable indicator for white oak due to the well‐reported leaf retention phenomenon among oak species (Abadia et al. 1996; Hoshaw and Guard 1949). White oak further demonstrated this effect, with F v /F m showing significant differences between drought‐stressed and control seedlings (Figure 10b). Drought‐stressed white pine showed a faster decrease in F v /F m towards the end of the growing season (Figure 3l), although the start of downregulation remained unchanged (Figure 10c). This aligns with findings that phenological changes in evergreen needleleaf forests are less sensitive to environmental conditions than those in deciduous broadleaf forests (Richardson et al. 2010). As a result, evergreen forests may benefit from an extended period of carbon assimilation and biomass accumulation. On the contrary, a previous study showed that severe early summer drought (May to August) causes a transient limitation of photosynthesis, which may extend the lifespan of beech tree leaves (Arend et al. 2016). This difference is likely due to drought occurring at different times of the year with different water availability conditions in each experimental setting.

Notably, drought‐stressed deciduous seedlings showed significant delays in leaf growth the following spring, suggesting that drought‐induced advanced senescence can further lead to substantial carry‐over effects (Figure 7e,f). A delayed onset of leaf growth in spring was also observed in Mediterranean woody perennial species where earlier defoliation in autumn affected the accumulation of non‐structural carbohydrates (Tixier et al. 2019; Amico Roxas et al. 2021). These stored carbohydrates are crucial for deciduous trees, as they rely on them to facilitate bud burst and the initial phases of spring growth until leaves transition from energy sinks to sources (Hartmann and Trumbore 2016). Despite smaller buds, we did not observe differences in the timing of the onset of the growing season between previously drought stressed versus control white pine seedlings in the subsequent spring. Similar phenomena were observed for other conifer species in an alpine dry valley rainfall exclusion experiment where low water availability had a minor impact on the onset of aboveground growth of Scots pine ( Pinus sylvestris ) and Norway spruce ( Picea abies ) due to the utilization of stored water, but induced advanced growth cessation (Swidrak et al. 2013).

PRI and Chlorophyll Carotenoid Index (CCI) Track Drought‐Induced Premature Senescence

Trees modulate chlorophyll and carotenoid composition in response to environmental stress, including cold and drought (Wong et al. 2020; Chang et al. 2021; D'Odorico et al. 2020, 2021). Variations in chlorophyll and carotenoid composition therefore provide important insight into how environmental factors affect vegetation indices, helping us to evaluate how effectively these indices capture stress‐induced changes in photosynthetic processes. Our results showed a transient decrease in total chlorophyll during autumn for the two deciduous species (Figure 5). We also observed a shift in the visible reflectance peak and the smoother red‐edge slope under drought stress (Figure 8), which reflects chlorophyll degradation and mesophyll structure changes in leaves, producing a more gradual increase between 670 and 760 nm. This decrease in chlorophyll could be usually detected using the NDVI (Gamon et al. 1995; Gonsamo et al. 2012; Tucker 1979). However, premature senescence did not lead to a decrease in total chlorophyll in white pine compared to control (Figure 6), emphasizing that NDVI is not an adequate tool to track photosynthetic phenology in evergreen conifers (Pierrat et al. 2021; Wong et al. 2019). This is partly because NDVI saturates at relatively low chlorophyll concentrations (~10 μg/cm2) (Bushmann and Nagel 1993), and also because it is influenced by plant structure and background reflectance (Zeng, Chen, et al. 2022). NDRE revealed differences between the control and drought treatments at the early stage, but did not capture the premature senescence (Figure S3).

CCI effectively tracked premature senescence for deciduous species (Figures 9 and 10). This effectiveness results from the sensitivity of the CCI to seasonally changing chlorophyll/carotenoid pigment ratios in deciduous species (Figure S4p,q). During autumn, the decrease in CCI is caused by a substantial increase in the carotenoid to chlorophyll (Car/Chl) ratio, which occurs as chlorophylls begin to degrade in deciduous trees while carotenoid pigments remain relatively stable (Figure 5d–f). Previous studies reported increases in the Car/Chl ratio during autumn senescence (Adams III et al. 2002) and linked pigment dynamics with spectral reflectance changes in red maple, white oak (Wong et al. 2019), and white pine. Our study integrates pigment composition and spectral reflectance to assess drought‐induced premature senescence. The relatively low correlation between CCI and Car/Chl in white pine is likely attributable to the narrow range of observed values.

PRI proved to be an informative vegetation index for detecting drought‐induced advanced senescence in white pine, as it revealed significant differences between drought and control seedlings during autumn (Figure 9h). This effectiveness is likely due to PRI's sensitivity to changes in the de‐epoxidation state of the xanthophyll cycle (DEPS), which consistently differed between drought and control conditions in white pine throughout autumn (Figure 5g), as well as the relatively high correlation between DEPS and PRI shown in Figure S5 (R 2 = 0.51). These findings indicate that PRI effectively captures physiological responses to environmental stress (Magney et al. 2016; Wong et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2020; Fréchette et al. 2016) and serves as a reliable indicator for advanced senescence in evergreen conifers.

Response to Drought Stress and Premature Senescence Are Driven by Processes Operating at Different Time Scales

While the magnitude and timing of these responses varied among species, the overall progression followed a similar order across all species (Figure 11). Almost immediately after the installation of the rainout structures in early summer, decreases in leaf pre‐dawn water potential were found in drought‐stressed seedlings. This was followed by the decrease in assimilation (A max) and stomatal conductance (g s ). Approximately 2 weeks after the decline in A max, drought‐stressed seedlings exhibited higher levels of dynamic non‐photochemical quenching (Φ NPQ). Φ NPQ was approximately 0.2 higher in drought‐stressed seedlings (Figure 4), indicating that these plants dissipated 20% more light energy as heat under drought conditions. This process was primarily driven by photoprotective pigments, as indicated by increased Car/Chl and DEPS under drought conditions (Figure 5). These shifts in pigment composition subsequently led to changes in the carotenoid‐based vegetation indices PRI and CCI. In late October, premature senescence in the drought‐stressed seedlings became evident, as indicated by the decrease in F v /F m , which preceded the autumn decrease in F v /F m of the control seedlings. Simultaneously, the decrease in Φ NPQ, accompanied by an initial rise in Φ f,D, also occurred earlier in drought‐stressed seedlings (Figures 4 and S1), along with a subsequent increase in zeaxanthin and DEPS levels (Figure 5). This indicates that drought induced an earlier transition from dynamic to sustained non‐photochemical quenching (NPQ). Notably, the decrease in chlorophyll content and increase of xanthophyll cycle pigments across all three species did not immediately result in a decrease in F v /F m under summer drought. The observed photosynthetic pigment pool adjustments, which serve as a critical photoprotective response, were modulated independently of any measurable decline in maximum photochemical efficiency.

Conceptual summary of seasonal physiological responses under control and drought treatments. Diagram illustrates responses of red maple (a), white oak (b), and white pine (c) seedlings in early summer, late summer, and autumn under control (blue) and drought conditions (red).

Conclusions

Understanding how forest tree species respond to changes in water availability is crucial for predicting ecosystem behavior under global climate change. Our study shows that summer drought affects photosynthesis and triggers premature senescence in seedlings of white pine, white oak, and red maple. Our results further demonstrate that the remote sensing‐based Chlorophyll/Carotenoid Index (CCI) is a proxy for detecting drought‐stress responses and phenological shifts in deciduous tree species, while the PRI is a reliable proxy for detecting drought‐stress responses and phenological shifts in evergreen tree species. Both indices are effective due to their sensitivity in detecting leaf spectral changes that correspond with variations in the carotenoid pigment pool. This pigment pool undergoes highly regulated seasonal variations in both conifers and deciduous species and maintains a strong relationship with photosynthetic CO2 assimilation as well as with photochemical and non‐photochemical quenching processes.
