Emergency medical team interventions involving pregnant patients of the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship (Poland) in 2020–2022
Authors: Ewa Kowalska, Paweł Jastrzębski, Marcin Nowak, Tadeusz Miłowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy is a natural and extraordinary time in a woman’s life. Nonetheless, it is important to note that even during a normal course of pregnancy, there is a possibility of the manifestation of symptoms that may occur and require instantly care. The aim of this study was to analyze Emergency Medical Team (EMT) dispatches to pregnant women in 2020–2022 in the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship (Poland).MethodsThe study was conducted using retrospective analysis. 400,251 patients were screened, of which 3116 pregnant women (0.79% of total patients) were included in the study. Calls to Ground-Based Emergency Medical Teams were classified under the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10). The data were also analyzed according to date of call (year, quarter, semester), territory covered by the EMT, scene of the emergency, patient age, medical procedures applied, selected vital parameters, type of emergency (life-threatening vs. not life-threatening), and patient outcome (left at the scene vs. hospitalization).ResultsGeneral analysis showed the mean age of pregnant women covered by this study to be 28.60 (SD 6.75) years. 2020 saw the highest number of dispatches (n = 1140). Most of the deployments were to locations with a population of less than 10,000 (52.98%) and to the patient’s home (90.50%). 31.00% of patients received a labor-related ICD-10 diagnosis, including preterm labor and complication of labor. Placement of intravenous access (66.43%) and monitoring of vital signs (51.99%) were the most common procedures performed during the interventions. A life-threatening emergency was found in 41.8% of the women. A chi-square test showed statistically significant relationships between: patient age and life-threatening emergency (chi-square (df = 1) = 4.00; p = 0.045), life-threatening emergency and date of call (chi-square (df = 5) = 22.39; p < 0.001), as well as the area of intervention and scene of emergency (chi-square (df = 2) = 57.17; p < 0.001).ConclusionThe study showed that Emergency Medical Team interventions involving pregnant women most often concerned patients staying at home and living in smaller localities. The most common reasons for dispatch were labor and its complications, as well as other pregnancy-related conditions. The prevalence of life-threatening emergencies was higher among pregnant women aged 36 years and older. An increase in life-threatening emergencies was observed from the second half of 2020 onward, which may have been associated with the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. The findings indicate the need to enhance Emergency Medical Team training in the management of pregnant patients and obstetric emergencies. Particular attention should also be paid to the accurate assessment and documentation of basic vital signs. Special care and monitoring should be provided to pregnant women aged 36 years and older due to their increased risk of life-threatening emergencies.Keywords: Emergency Medical Team, Pre-hospital care, Paramedics, Pregnancy, Pregnant women

Introduction

Pregnancy is a natural and unquestionably extraordinary time in a woman’s life. However, even in the normal course of pregnancy, troubling symptoms may occur, which are often unexpected and require appropriate care [1].

In order to provide assistance to every person experiencing a medical emergency (whether life-threatening or otherwise), a system of National Medical Emergency Services has been established in Poland. The system networks hospital emergency departments and emergency medical teams (ground-based and air-based). Ground-Based Emergency Medical Teams in Poland are divided into 2-3-person basic teams with paramedics and/or nurses, and specialist teams which include a medical doctor [2].

There are 81 Emergency Medical Teams and 1 Air Emergency Medical Team operating within the limits of the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship. The voivodeship also encompasses 42 hospitals, 11 of which have an emergency department. These institutions provide healthcare coverage to the 1,374,699 residents of the voivodeship across an area of 24,173 km2. Women and men make up 51.53% and 48.47% of the voivodeship’s population, respectively. The majority of the population is of working age. 59.2% of the voivodeship’s citizens live in urban areas, with the remaining 40.8% residing in rural areas [3]. The large area and poor road accessibility (mainly due to the underdeveloped road network) [4] of the region present a challenge for the ground-based units of the National Medical Emergency Service system.

The purpose of the study was to analyze Emergency Medical Team dispatches to the niche patient group of pregnant women in 2020–2022 in the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship (Poland).

Methods

Ethics approval and data

Prior to the study, approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Warmia and Mazury in Olsztyn (Decision No 11/2023 of the Scientific Research Ethics Committee). The analysis concerned a medical database composed of EMT intervention data from 2020 to 2022 in the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship (Poland). The study was conducted using retrospective analysis. The analysis was conducted using a database managed by the Governor of the Warmia and Mazury Voivodeship in Olsztyn. The data were accessed for research purposes on the 6th of June 2023. Authors did not have access to information that would identify individual participants during or after data collection. Data included electronic records covering emergency medical procedure documentation and EMS team dispatch documentation.

Study criteria

Out of 400,251 patients, 3,116 pregnant women (0.79% of total patients) – i.e. those who had the “pregnancy” box checked in their emergency medical services form – were included in the study. We used the following exclusion criteria: patient’s absence on site and missing data about age. The stated reason for the call was classified under the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10). The data were also analyzed according to date of call (year, quarter, semester), territory covered by the EMT, scene of the emergency, patient age, medical procedures performed, selected vital parameters, type of emergency (life-threatening vs. not life-threatening) and patient outcome (left at the scene vs. hospitalization). A life-threatening condition (a medical emergency) was defined as a condition characterized by the sudden or imminent onset of symptoms indicating a deterioration in health, which may directly result in serious impairment of bodily functions, physical injury, or death, and which requires immediate emergency medical intervention and treatment [2]. The patient’s life-threatening status was assessed at the scene by the leader of the Emergency Medical Team.

Data analysis

The results were processed empirically using an array of statistical methods, including frequency analysis and cross tabulation to comprehensively assess distribution and relationships between variables. A chi-square test of independence was used to identify statistically significant relationships between nominal variables or between a nominal (dependent) and ordinal (independent) variable (χ²). The results are presented in the tables, with ‘n’ representing nominal values, and percentages being divided into three categories: row %, column % and total %. A dependent-samples Student’s t-test was used to compare the means between two related groups and assess trends within the same research units across different conditions or time. The statistical significance of all tests is judged at the p < 0.05 significance level. Differences and relationships were considered significant when the probability of the result was less than 5%. IBM SPSS Statistics, Jamovi, Jasp and MS EXCEL were used for the statistical computation.

Discussion

EMT engagements with pregnant patients of the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship in 2020–2022 accounted for 0.79% of all dispatches. According to a study by Wierzbik-Strońska et al. [5], most of the interventions carried out by the Voivodeship Ambulance Service in Katowice in 2018 concerned injuries and poisoning, symptoms, signs, abnormal findings on diagnostic tests (ICD-10 R00-R99), and cardiovascular diseases. This would seem to suggest that requests for dispatch to pregnant women are rare, and the available literature data on the subject focuses primarily on calls involving out-of-hospital birth [6, 7]. Research on emergency interventions for pregnant women rarely looks beyond obstetric-related calls [8], which speaks to the need for studies such as ours. Because of limited exposure to pregnant patients, many EMT responders feel unprepared or even fear having to extend pre-hospital care to pregnant patients [9]. A crucial consideration in dealing with a pregnant patient – one that makes the engagement all-the-more stressful for EMTs – is that the care extends not only to the mother, but the child or children (in multiple pregnancies), and thus the responders have more than one patient to worry about [10].

In our 3-year study, dispatches to pregnant women were at their highest in 2020. This may be attributed to the nascent SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in Poland and the resultant restrictions on individual travel and hospital admissions. Conversely, a study by Hadley et al. [11], conducted in the early stage of the pandemic, showed the exact opposite trend, with the United States Emergency Medical Services recording a decrease in obstetric-related calls. The authors posit that women may have decided to self-transport in order to avoid multiple points of interaction with the healthcare system.

In our study, most of the EMT deployments to pregnant patients were to areas with a population of less than 10,000. Similar findings have been reported by Bills et al. [12], who noted that most paramedic interventions involving pregnant women were in rural areas. Regardless of the area of intervention, the majority of calls covered by our study concerned patients at home. This is in line with data from Statistics Poland, according to which at-home interventions formed the bulk (78.00%) of all completed EMT dispatches in Poland in 2022 [13]. Our statistical analysis demonstrated a significant relationship between area of intervention and scene of emergency. Public-space emergencies were much more common in regions with 10k+ population compared to the less populated areas. This may be attributed to the fact that pregnant women in urban areas tend to be more focused on work and exercise [14], which translates to greater mobility and more time away from home. The analysis did not find a statistically significant relationship between the scene of emergency and patient age. At-home interventions were by far the most common both in the 35-and-under and 36-and-over groups.

Mean patient age in our study is convergent with Statistics Poland data, which indicate that the Polish mothers’ mean age at first birth is 28.8 [15]. Socio-economical development is certainly a factor with regard to women’s childbearing age. Most developed countries have seen the share of pregnant patients aged 35 and over rise significantly over the past 30–40 years [16]. Thus, the average age of a pregnant patient assisted by Australian ambulance services is 28.8 years old [17], whereas the mean age of pregnant women calling ambulance services in India is 23.6 [18].

Rzońca et al. [19] reported that in 51.59% of interventions involving pregnant women with suspected miscarriage, treatment by EMTs included placement of intravenous (IV) access. This is similar to our findings.

A reading of the mother’s blood pressure can be crucial in identifying danger to the woman and her unborn child. Arterial hypertension during pregnancy runs the risk of developing eclampsia [20]. According to Agrawal and Wenger [21], arterial hypertension during pregnancy is defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, measured in two separate readings. Our results show systolic blood pressure to be 140 mmHg or higher in 27.79% of the pregnant patients. Notably, the data indicates that some pregnant women have not had their blood pressure taken. This means that the measurement was either not actually performed, or that the reading was not documented in the medical records completed by the EMTs. Medics should thus take more care when conducting the exam or when filling out the medical documentation. The same applies to Glascow Coma Scale (GCS) assessments, because some pregnant patients not being examined or not having their score documented. 98.30% of the women scored GCS 13 or higher, which is similar to the results presented by Rzońca and Pinkas [22].

In terms of the health problems of EMT-treated pregnant women, labor (including preterm labor and complication of labor) was the subject of 31.00% interventions. Maternal care for other conditions predominantly related to pregnancy, hemorrhage, miscarriage and “O-coded” problems (pregnancy, childbirth and puerperium) together accounted for 35.79% dispatches. Finally, abdominal and pelvic pain was reported in 11.36% calls. A study by Freitas et al. [23] analyzed dispatches to pregnant women in a small town in north-eastern Brazil. In contrast to our findings, this study showed labor to be the most common reason for the call (50.9%), followed by “other” – a category grouping lower abdomen/back pain, nausea, vomiting and other typical pregnancy signs/symptoms (14.2%). It should be noted that the sample studied by Freitas et al. numbered 558 pregnant women, compared to the 3116 in our study.

It is also important to remember that pregnant women, just like any other patient, can suffer from a variety of conditions. Over 21% of the calls in our study were for non-pregnancy related diseases and abdominal/pelvic pain. McLelland et al. [24] reported that the most common conditions among Australian pregnant women were: asthma, mental disorders, addictions, epilepsy, and diabetes. Flanagan et al. [25] point to diabetes and arterial hypertension as the most prevalent problems. In India, on the other hand, pregnant patients requesting EMT assistance tended to suffer from anemia and hypertension [26].

Our study on 3116 pregnant women found that over 41.00% of them had a life-threatening emergency. Torloni et al. [27] reported that an estimated 295,000 women die every year due to complications of pregnancy and childbirth. Main causes of serious illness and maternal death are: postpartum hemorrhage, pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, and sepsis. Beaird et al. [28] pointed out that unplanned out-of-hospital birth was, by nature, a problematic situation, associated with elevated perinatal mortality and morbidity risks both for the newborn and the mother. Perinatal mortality rates for unplanned out-of-hospital births are twice as high as for in-hospital ones [29].

A chi-square test showed a statistically significant relationship between patient age and life-threatening emergency. The life-threatening emergency rates were higher for the 36-and-over group at 45.60%. Zhou et al. [30] cautioned that mothers faced a 1.5-fold increase in miscarriage risk for every 5 years over age 35. Potential complications for mothers over 35 years’ old have also been noted by Glick et al. [31], including increased risk of spontaneous miscarriage, preterm labor, gestational diabetes mellitus, pre-eclampsia, stillbirth, and Caesarean delivery. Fetal chromosomal abnormalities are also more likely in this group of women.

Since our study period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic (with the first case reported in March 2020), our analysis found a significant relationship between life-threatening emergency and date. The test showed that life-threatening emergency rates increased in the second half in 2020 and remained high throughout the rest of the study period. The increase is statistically significant, which suggests that the elevated rates were not coincidental and may have been linked with COVID-19 and its effect on female patient health. Wang et al. [32] have demonstrated that pregnant women may develop more severe symptoms from respiratory viruses due to physiological changes to the immune and cardiopulmonary systems during pregnancy. Global estimates of the indirect effects of COVID-19 suggest [33] a 38.6% increase in monthly maternal mortality and a 44.7% increase in monthly child mortality in 118 low- and middle-income countries.

The lack of data regarding gestational age constitutes a significant limitation of our study, as this parameter substantially affects the course of prehospital interventions and the clinical decisions undertaken. In emergency medical practice, knowledge of gestational age is of crucial importance because maternal–fetal risk and permissible treatment strategies vary with the progression of pregnancy. The choice of analgesic therapy is inherently associated with gestational age. As reported by Peter Marhofer et al. [34], the safety profile of analgesics changes throughout pregnancy, and particular caution should be exercised when using nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) during the third trimester. Gestational age also influences resuscitation management in pregnant patients, as advancing pregnancy necessitates modifications to cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and consideration of procedures such as resuscitative hysterotomy [35].

Unfortunately, our database does not include procedural data related to childbirth management. The absence of these variables limits our ability to assess the potential impact of perinatal management practices on neonatal outcomes. As highlighted by Beaird et al. [36], out-of-hospital deliveries are associated with a higher risk of adverse perinatal outcomes, therefore, analysis of Emergency Medical Teams interventions, including detailed medical records, is essential for improving the quality of care and personnel preparedness.

Strengths and limitations

A notable strength of this work is that the study covered all calls to pregnant women from January 2020 to December 2022 in the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship, not just those related to childbirth. Despite the valuable insights gained from this study, the limitations of the research must be considered. One of these is the retrospective nature of the study - the analysis is limited to information derived from the EMS electronic medical records, with some records exhibiting incompleteness, for instance, with respect to gestational age or precise location of delivery. Another limitation is that our medical database does not include procedural data such as the timing of umbilical cord clamping, Apgar scores, or details of postnatal care.

Furthermore, we do not have information on the follow-up care provided to the patients after they had been transferred to hospital or on patient condition in cases where the woman was left at home. These limitations affect the overall quality of the study and provide a rationale for further analyses in future research.
