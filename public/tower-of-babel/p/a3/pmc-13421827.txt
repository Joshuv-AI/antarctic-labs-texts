Effect of cerebellar vermis intermittent theta-burst stimulation on balance function in frail older adults: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xin Tian, Neng Huang, Yuan Shi, Kehong Zhao, Yi Chen, Huixin Tan, Lan Zheng, Zuoyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the increasing population of frail older adults, their compromised balance functions pose risks of falls and mortality. Effectively enhancing balance function could mitigate adverse health outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) on balance function in frail older adults.MethodsFourty-eight subjects were randomly assigned to receive iTBS and sham stimulation in a randomized control trial design over a period of 4 weeks. Before and after intervention, related balance evaluation data was collected, including berg balance scale (BBS) score, Time-up and go (TUG) test, Sensory Organization Test (SOT) and Limit of Stability (LOS) data obtained through the Balance Master system. Additionally, cortical activity was recorded using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), fNIRS was used as an exploratory neuroimaging measure. The Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) was also assessed.ResultsA total of 45 subjects (mean [SD] age, 67.69 [8.16] years; 33 females [73.7%]) completed the trial were enrolled and randomized to receive iTBS (n = 23) or sham stimulation (n = 22). After intervention, the BBS (p<0.001), and TFI (p<0.001) score statistically significant improved in the iTBS group. The SOT (p<0.05) also improved. And the LOS had statistically significant improvement. However, no statistically significant difference was found in the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test between the two groups (p > 0.05). And study showed a significant difference in HbO levels for the left DLPFC (p < 0.05; 95% CI: -0.066, -0.003) and bilateral SMA (p<0.05, 95% CI: -0.061, -0.007; p<0.05, 95% CI: -0.072, -0.016) before and after the intervention. No significant adverse effects were reported on all subjects.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that iTBS could promote balance function, as evidenced by an increase in the BBS score, improvements in SOT and LOS, as well as elevated levels of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and supplementary motor area (SMA).Clinical trial numberThis clinical study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR, ChiCTR2500097468; registered on 19/02/2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07684-4.

Background

With the intensification of population aging, the number of elderly individuals is continuously increasing, and their health-related issues have garnered widespread attention [1]. It is estimated that by 2050, the global population aged 65 and above will reach 20% of the world [2]. Frailty is a geriatric syndrome associated with aging, characterized by a decline in physiological reserves, which leads to increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes [3]. This condition typically manifests as weight loss, psychological stress, decreased levels of physical activity, diminished strength, and reduced gait speed [4]. A systematic review has revealed that the prevalence of frailty among community-dwelling elderly individuals is 42% [5], while among Chinese elderly populations it was 10.1% [6]. Frailty may negatively impact balance, mobility, and muscle strength in older adults, consequently elevating the risk of adverse events such as falls, hospitalizations, disabilities, and mortality [7, 8]. This not only heightens the burden on individuals and families but also imposes significant strain on society at large. Simultaneously, it undermines the quality of life for older adults and increases medical-related expenses [9–11]. Research suggests that medical costs associated with adverse events stemming from frailty are significantly higher than those incurred by other elderly individuals [12].

The balance function and gait are fundamental skills crucial for maintaining posture, positioning, and executing daily activities within the human body [13]. Furthermore, they serve as significant indicators of physical function among the elderly population [14]. Relevant evidence indicates that balance impairment represent a significant symptom of aging among older adults, with their prevalence increasing as age advances [15]. Elderly individuals frequently experience a certain degree of decline in the balance abilities, which may be associated with frailty and age-related deterioration in both balance and postural stability [16]. These conditions may adversely affect the daily activities of older adults and increase their risk of falls [17]. Frail elderly individuals frequently exhibit compromised balance capabilities, which are characterized by reduced static balance (such as standing on one foot) and dynamic balance (such as walking or turning) [18]. This decline is linked to multi-system functional impairments associated with frailty, which encompass diminished muscle strength, reduced nerve conduction velocity, and impaired proprioception [15]. These physiological changes could profoundly affect an individual’s capacity to maintain balance. Notably, frailty reflects the overall systemic vulnerability, whereas balance impairment represents a key functional manifestation that contributes to adverse outcomes (such as falls and disability). Consequently, it is essential to comprehend the mechanisms that underlie frailty and balance impairment in order to develop effective early detection methods and intervention strategies [15].

Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) is a specialized form of patterned repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) which would replicate the activation patterns of hippocampal neuron spike waves [19]. The iTBS delivers an equivalent number of stimulation pulses to the targeted brain region within a condensed timeframe, thereby inducing a comparable or even heightened level of excitability [20]. The vermis of the cerebellum, located at the center of this structure, plays a crucial role in regulating trunk balance, maintaining stability in the body’s center of gravity, and coordinating axial movements [21]. Abnormal functioning of the vermis could result in balance impairment characterized by symptoms such as widened gait and unstable standing [22]. Previous research has demonstrated that iTBS applied to the cerebellar vermis could enhance balance function and has primarily been utilized for patients with conditions such as stroke and cerebellar multiple system atrophy [23–25]. However, there is a relative paucity of studies investigating the application of iTBS on the cerebellar vermis specifically aimed at improving balance function among frail elderly individuals.

In sum, to better reveal the effect of iTBS on balance function improvement in frail older adults and provide new insight for its future application, our study aimed to: (1) compare berg balance scale (BBS) score between iTBS and sham stimulation; (2) evaluate the impact of iTBS on balance function measured by objective method and device; (3) exploratory analysis about the fNIRS data collected in the study.

Methods

Subjects

A total of 48 subjects participated in the study, with 2 subjects dropping out before the intervention, and 1 subject didn’t finish all intervention procedure. Ultimately, 45 subjects (12 males, 67.69 ± 8.16 years) completed the trial and were included for analysis. All subjects had no severe cognitive impairment and iTBS contraindications and the trial was performed in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at a tertiary hospital in Chengdu, China. All provided written informed consent. The first subject was recruited on March 1, 2025, and ended on August 31, 2025. The recruitment process was performed by XT and NH. Inclusion criteria: (1) participants aged 60 years or older, (2) the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) score less than 56, indicating balance dysfunction, (3) the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) score of 5 or higher, indicating frailty, (4) no contraindications for TMS, including the metal implants in the brain, pacemaker, and a history of epileptic seizures, (5) Mini-Mental State Examination‌ (MMSE) of 17 or above, indicating no severe cognitive impairment, (6) stable vital signs, (7) informed consent obtained from subjects or their supervisors. Exclusion criteria: (1) presence of other diseases that significantly impair individuals’ balance function, in addition to frailty, such as hemiplegia and paraplegia, (2) severe cognitive impairment (MMSE < 17 points) or possessing other conditions that hinder the completion of treatment, (3) contraindications of TMS, (4) participation in other clinical trials that may affect the final assessment results of this trial. Criteria for study termination included: (1) the occurrence of severe adverse events, (2) medical conditions that may interfere with the assessment of iTBS results, and (3) withdrawal of consent.

Randomization and interventions

Randomization

This study was designed as a placebo-controlled, blind, randomized control trial. Subjects were randomly assigned to iTBS Group and sham Group with 1:1 ratio through the sequential distribution of sealed envelopes, each containing a computer-generated randomization number. This process was conducted by a researcher who was not involved in subject inclusion, protocol implementation, and data collection. Before the generation of random numbers, the researchers predetermined that an odd number represented iTBS Group and an even number represented Group sham. In the iTBS group, subjects received iTBS for 4 weeks, 20 days, subjects in sham group received sham stimulation for 4 weeks, too. All subjects were blinded to the group allocation.

Interventions

Cerebellar vermis iTBS

The iTBS devices were produced by Wuhan Yiruide Medical Equipment New Technology Co., Ltd., a CCY-I type magnetic field therapy device, features an “8”-shaped coil with an inner diameter of 70 mm. The stimulation protocol was meticulously designed in accordance with the safety guidelines and recommendations established by the International Federation for Clinical Neurophysiology. We used a Magstim Rapid2 stimulator (The Magstim Company Limited) connected to a 70 mm figure-of-8 Double Rapid2 Air Cooled Coil (P/N 3910–00) to stimulate the cerebellar vermis. The center of the coil will be placed tangentially to the target scalp area, and the coil current direction will point downwards. Stimulation target: Vermis of the cerebellum; the surface location is situated 1 cm inferior to the inion. Stimulation Intensity: 80% of the Active Motor Threshold (AMT) [the delivered intensity is 79.6 ± 5.3% AMT; 75.0–88.0% AMT]- which is defined as the minimum stimulation intensity necessary to elicit motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) with amplitudes > 200 µV in the abductor pollicis brevis muscle during 10% of the maximum voluntary contraction measured by a dynamometer. If the participant cannot elicit MEPs or cannot tolerate the preset standard stimulus intensity, the stimulator output intensity will be set to the participant’s maximum tolerated intensity. Stimulation Mode: iTBS consists of a total of 600 pulses, delivered in bursts of 3 pulses each. The intra-burst frequency is set at 50 Hz, with each burst lasting for 0.04 s. There are an inter-burst interval of 0.16 s and an inter-burst frequency of 5 Hz. A continuous stimulation sequence comprises 10 bursts, followed by an inter-burst interval of 8 s, culminating in a total stimulation duration of 200 s. In the iTBS stimulation group, the “8” shaped coil is positioned tangentially to the scalp at the stimulation site, effectively intersecting with the magnetic field in the cerebral cortex and generating an induced current to achieve the desired stimulation effect.

Sham stimulation

In the sham stimulation group subjects were treated identically to those in the iTBS group, except the Magstim sham coil (P/N 3950–00) will be used to deliver the sham stimulation, the “8” shaped coil is oriented perpendicularly to the scalp at the stimulation site. The sham coil has the same external appearance, parameters and application methods for stimulating the sensation produced by the real coil without inducing a magnetic field. Therefore, it can sufficiently ensure that the patients remain blind to the intervention.

Conventional physical therapy

Considering ethical factors, both groups of subjects simultaneously received conventional exercise training after iTBS or sham stimulation aimed at enhancing balance function, including muscle stretching, active-assisted mobilizations, progressive neuromuscular training balance exercises, and gait training lasting 90 min including rest periods between exercises. All the training was implemented by two trained therapists which were blind to the group of subjects. All subjects were hospitalized; they demonstrated high treatment adherence and cooperative engagement and completed the prescribed therapy.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographics and clinical characteristics were collected at baseline during the initial face-to-face interviews with the subjects. Demographic characteristics include age, gender, education level, marital status, income source, and exercise habits. Clinical characteristics encompassed medication usage, chronic disease, as well as histories of drinking, smoking and falls. Physical examinations were conducted by a trained researcher who measuring height and weight. Additionally, subjects were required to complete balance-related assessments along with other relevant examinations.

Outcome measures

All related outcome parameters were evaluated before and after intervention.

Primary outcome

BBS: BBS demonstrates high reliability and internal validity, consisting of 14 items, each rated on a scale from 0 to 4, yielding a maximum total score of 56. A higher score reflects superior balance function in the test subject.

Secondary outcomes

Time-up and go (TUG) test, TFI, and the NeuroCom Balance Master system were utilized to assess balance function and related indicators measured by functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS).

Sensory Organization Test (SOT) and limit of stability (LOS)

The balance ability of subjects was evaluated using the NeuroCom Balance Master balance device (NeuroCom® International Inc. USA). This system consists of a computer connected to a force plate, which captures data with the assistance of piezoelectric crystal transducers. The force plate data included the X (± 0.08 cm) and Y (± 0.25 cm) positions of the center of vertical force, as well as the total vertical force (± 0.1 N), recorded at a sampling rate of 100 Hz. With this system, the transducers transmit pressure data to a computer every 10 ms. Subsequently, the dynamic center of gravity of the subject is calculated, and the sway velocity over a defined period is obtained. The equipment demonstrates high reliability and reproducibility among researchers, with an intraclass correlation coefficient ranging from 0.53 to 0.81 [26, 27]. The NeuroCom Balance Master® features multiple testing protocols specifically designed to assess balance [28]. SOT: The SOT assesses the static balance capabilities of participants. Sensory organization is the ability to process multiple environmental cues to execute appropriate movement in balance. This test provides objective and quantitative assessments of human body balance function, assessing an individual’s capacity to prioritize the most accurate sensory inputs necessary for maintaining static balance. The NeuroCom Balance Master employs SOT to gather data on the effectiveness of an individual’s proprioceptive, visual, and vestibular senses during a series of balance tasks. This is achieved by systematically isolating and challenging various sensory modalities to assess the individual’s capacity to utilize these senses for maintaining balance. The assessment consists of six test conditions, and the composite score is measured [29]. LOS: The LOS evaluates dynamic balance abilities. This assessment is designed to record performance in dynamic balance tasks. The subject is instructed to move the center of gravity as directly and quickly as possible towards 8 different targets (forwards, forwards-right, right, backwards-right, backwards, backwards-left, left and forwards-left) on the display screen while standing on stable force plates. During this test, the distance to target point (maximum excursions [MXE], %) was measured.

Exploratory outcome

fNIRS measurement

A multichannel fNIRS system (NirScan, HuiChuang, China) was employed to monitor changes in oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) in the cortex of the supplementary motor area (SMA) and dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Data was collected at a frequency of 10 Hz [30]. Fifty-five channels were established for measurement purposes, defined as the midpoint between corresponding pairs of light sources and detectors. This configuration utilized 20 light sources and 20 detectors. These channels were symmetrically distributed across the left and right cerebral hemispheres of the subjects. The center of the middle probe set row was positioned approximately at FPz, in accordance with the 10/20 international system [31]. The optodes were placed over the left and right DLPFC (left-DLPFC: S10-D4, S10-D9, S11-D23 and S14-D23; right-DLPFC: S8-D2, S8-D8, S13-D17 and S13-D20) as well as the left and right SMA (left-SMA: S14-D15 and S15-D15; right-SMA: S12-D14 and S12-D20). Data Collection Process: Initially, 30 s of resting-state data were collected. Following this period, the subject received the instruction to “Please Walk onto the mat and maintain your balance,” which was executed over a duration of 20 s. Subsequently, the subject was instructed to “Please stand and rest,” allowing for a rest period of 30 s. The fNIRS is an exploratory process.

fNIRS data preprocessing

The raw fNIRS data was preprocessed using NirSpark software, which includes format conversion, motion correction, artifact processing, bandpass filtering and channel quality. Initially, raw signals were transformed into optical density, followed by motion artifact correction using a cubic spline interpolation algorithm (STDEV threshold = 6.0, AMP threshold = 0.5). To address physiological noise and head motion artifacts, temporal derivative distribution repair (TDDR) will be employed to remove spike artifacts and baseline shifts. This sequence was repeated to collect a total of 3 min of data. A bandpass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz) was applied to attenuate low-frequency fluctuations and high-frequency noise while preserving task-specific frequency band of the neural signals [31].

Sample size estimation

Sample size calculation was performed using G Power (version 3.1.9.2) software. The primary outcome was the BBS score. Based on previous experimental findings from our team (Liao, 2021 [32]), the estimated effect size f was determined to be 0.380. With a significance level α set at 0.05 (two-tailed), a power β of 0.10, a correlation between repeated measurements of 0.5, and a non-sphericity correction ε of 1, the required sample size was calculated to be 40 subjects. Considering potential factors such as dropout rates and loss to follow-up, we increased the sample size by 20%, resulting in a final total sample size of 48 participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Normality tests for all continuous variables were performed utilizing the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was employed to evaluate the effects of group (iTBS vs. sham) and time (before vs. after-intervention). This model was employed to evaluate interaction effects (group × time) to ascertain the differential impacts of the interventions across groups. The fNIRS data from ROI regions’ channels were also analyzed utilizing GLMM. In light of our exploratory analysis about fNIRS data, reported statistical calculations including p-values pertaining to cortical activation are not confirmatory and are to be interpreted with caution. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and the p value of equal to or less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial provided evidence that 4 weeks, 20 days iTBS combined with conventional physical therapy could improve balance function in frail older adults. The BBS score, LOS and SOT significantly increased after iTBS intervention combined with physical therapy.

This study demonstrated a significant increase in the BBS scores of subjects following 4 weeks of iTBS combined with conventional physical therapy. A systematic review [33] has indicated that iTBS could enhance static balance abilities in stroke patients, as evidenced by a marked improvement in BBS scores post-intervention. Numerous prior studies have explored the application of iTBS to improve balance function in individuals who have experienced strokes. Research conducted by Giacomo Koch et al. [34] found that 3 consecutive weeks of cerebellar iTBS facilitated recovery in both gait and balance among stroke patients, transitioning them from a state requiring assistance to walk to one capable of independent ambulation, which may attribute to cerebellar cortical plasticity. Liao et al. [35] assessed the effects of 3 weeks of cerebellar iTBS, applied to the primary motor cortex (M1), alongside sham stimulation, on balance capabilities and motor recovery within stroke populations. Their findings conclusively demonstrated that cerebellar iTBS significantly enhanced balance function. Our study was consistent with previous research; however, it is noteworthy that our subjects were frail older adults, which was relatively underexplored concerning improvements in their balance through iTBS interventions. The mechanisms underlying how iTBS enhances balance functions among frail older adults remain unclear, and existing limited studies do not adequately elucidate the differential impacts based on target sites relating to overall balance enhancement. The TFI is a widely used instrument employed for the evaluation of frailty among older adults, comprising 15 items that address three dimensions: physical frailty, social frailty and psychological frailty [36]. The significant reduction in TFI scores observed in the study may be attributed to enhancements in physical function and balance resulting from iTBS. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies could expand the sample size to further investigate the association between improvements in frailty and enhancements in balance function. The study provides essential support for subsequent clinical practices by indicating that non-invasive and low-cost physical therapy options may yield effective strategies for improving balance function among elderly individuals.

As individuals age, the dynamic ability for physical activity among older adults undergoes significant changes [37], Factors such as frailty increase their risks to adverse events,, including falls and mortality [38]. Our study assessed the impact of iTBS on functional activity through TUG test, and the result wasn’t statistically significant between two groups. However, data assessed by BBS and NeuroCom Balance Master system were significantly improved by iTBS, which may be related to the TUG reflects functional mobility and speed, which may be less sensitive to the effects of iTBS than postural control and sensory integration assessed by BBS, SOT, and LOS. Systematic reviews addressing whether iTBS can enhance functional activity in stroke patients, as measured by TUG, have yielded inconsistent findings [33, 39]. Zhu et al. [40] reported that 2 weeks continuous cerebellar iTBS could significantly improve walking capability in stroke patients, evidenced by reduced TUG times. Conversely, Xie et al. [41] found no significant improvement in TUG test after 2 weeks of iTBS among strokes patients; nonetheless, they noted a trend toward time reduction that is consistent with our findings. The variability in functional activity levels among frail older adults may contribute to these results [42]. Additionally, it is important to recognize that the mechanisms through which strokes and frailty influence physical activity differ considerably. Research investigating the effects of iTBS on activities in frail populations remains limited; thus, future studies should integrate insights from clinical practice to further elucidate these mechanisms and provide empirical evidence that may enhance clinical applications.

This study demonstrated that 4 weeks of iTBS significantly improved the SOT and LOS. The composite equilibrium score of the SOT reflects the impairments present in individual sensory systems and assesses postural control [43]. iTBS improved the postural control ability after iTBS in our study. Yang et al. [44] found that iTBS applied to the DLPFC could enhance postural control abilities in patients with chronic low back pain, as well as improve their muscle activation patterns. A systematic review indicated that iTBS targeting the cerebellum may enhance postural control in stroke survivors [45], potentially linked to the modulation of neural excitability within neural networks. Effective motor recovery necessitates a coordinated restoration of direct motor output alongside adaptive control mechanisms [46]. Furthermore, improvements in postural control among frail elderly individuals may also be associated with enhancements in muscle function and the regulation of neural networks. iTBS could enhance the maximum range of centers of gravity movement while maintaining balance, thereby improving participants’ control abilities in our study. A study investigating the impact of Pilates on LOS control found that a 2-week Pilates significantly improved the control abilities of patients in the early stages of Parkinson’s disease [47]. Accelerated iTBS combined with balance training could increase in the LOS by 88% in individuals with corticobasal syndrome [48]. However, our study is the first one to explore the effect of iTBS on balance function in frail older adults. The findings presented above provide clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of physical therapy in enhancing postural control abilities among frail elderly individuals.

The DLPFC and SMA function declines with age in older adults [49, 50]. Previous research has demonstrated that DLPFC and SMA play crucial roles in balance tasks [51, 52]. Our study found that 4 weeks iTBS could increase HbO levels in the left DLPFC and bilateral SMA compared with sham intervention in frail older adults; however, the fNIRS analysis in our study is exploratory due to limited sample size. Kim et al. observed that the DLPFC plays a vital role not only in working memory and attentional processes but also in motor control in stroke patients [53]. Additionally, researchers have shown that cerebellum iTBS significantly enhanced the cortical activation in the DLPFC [54]. Tan et al. [55] found that cerebellar vermis iTBS significantly increased the mean HbO in the SMA compared with baseline in healthy individuals. Ghosh S’ [56] review found 1 Hz rTMS could increase regional cerebral blood flow in the right prefrontal area and further improve gait and balance. This finding is inconsistent with our study results, which may be attributed to differences in rTMS stimulation parameters (including frequency, stimulation pattern and total number of pulses), target sites and study population heterogeneity. The SMA is a key brain center that regulates gait and balance functions [57]; however, limited research clarifies the relationship between rTMS, SMA and balance function in frail older adults. The enhancement of balance function observed in our study in frail older adults may elucidate the improvements noted in the functions of the DLPFC and SMA. Subsequently, larger sample size, multicenter randomized controlled trials could be conducted to validate the clinical evidence regarding fNIRS data in effect of iTBS for older adults with frailty and balance dysfunction.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits the possibility of generalizing the findings, and the subjects were assessed immediately after the 4-week intervention, lack of follow-up data, future studies could expand the sample size to further investigate the association between improvements in frailty and enhancements in balance function with follow-up. Second, our subjects received stimulation with one parameter, which could not obtain the effects of iTBS at different frequency. iTBS stimulation with multiple sessions could yield more data and help understand the effects of iTBS on balance function. Third, this study included older adults with frailty and balance impairment, so the findings may not be generalizable to all frail older adults. Fourth, the fNIRS data in the study is exploratory analysis, the result in this part is explained carefully. Future studies should aim to expand the participant pool to establish a more robust intervention cohort, including more intervention frequencies and conduct long-term follow-up periods, which is helpful to promote the clinical application of iTBS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study has demonstrated that iTBS combined conventional physical therapy improved the balance function and frailty in frail older adults with balance impairment. However, it didn’t significantly decrease the TUG time. Our study revealed the effect of iTBS on balance function from a neural function aspect, adding to our knowledge of its usage in improving balance function.
