A global assessment of the level of trust in information sources and adherence to COVID-19 related preventive health behaviors: cross-sectional survey study
Authors: Mishell Barreno Caicedo, Maximilien V. Dialufuma, Rubee Dev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInitial management of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) focused on preventing spread and providing supportive care. Authorities disseminated disease information and enforced preventive measures. This intricate task was affected by factors such as initial emotional distress or perceived risk, which influenced adherence to public health recommendations. This study examined the association between trust in local health authorities and national/regional leaders who have provided information about COVID-19 and adherence to COVID-19 preventive behaviors.MethodsData from the International COVID-19 Awareness and Responses Evaluation (iCARE) study was used for the analyses (surveys 5–15, n = 14,453) from 116 countries (September 2020-January 2022). The main predictor was participants' trust, which was categorized into four levels. The primary outcome was adherence to preventive behaviors: (1) handwashing, (2) mask wearing, (3) physical distancing, (4) avoiding gatherings, and (5) self-quarantining. Additional variables included demographics, socioeconomic status, health history and COVID-19 perceptions. We conducted multivariable logistic regressions to determine the association between the level of trust in local health authorities and national/regional leaders and adherence to COVID-19-related preventive health behaviors.Results14,453 respondents worldwide, 2852 (25.43%) reported no trust or no trust at all in political authorities, and 1847 (16.84%) reported no trust or no trust at all in healthcare authorities. The unadjusted regression models revealed that those without trust in political and health authorities were less likely to adhere to all the included preventive behaviors. Multivariable models indicated that those who did not trust at all in political leaders had lower odds of adopting self-quarantine (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.49–0.99) and of avoiding gatherings (OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.42–0.84). Distrust in healthcare authorities also lowered the odds of adherence to protective behaviors, particularly avoiding gatherings (OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.25–0.53), and self-quarantine (OR = 0.49, 95% 0.33- 0.72).ConclusionOur findings revealed a positive association between trust in health and political authorities, and adherence to preventive health behaviors, independent of demographic, socioeconomic, or perceptual/belief factors. Therefore, prioritizing trust between public health institutions and people is crucial for effectively managing infectious disease outbreaks. Future steps must be tailored to each population, addressing privacy concerns, enhancing community communication, and encouraging public engagement in health policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28320-1.

Background

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was a global health emergency that challenged healthcare and public health systems [1–3]. The lack of knowledge about the disease and its underlying pathophysiology made management extremely complex [1, 2]. The main obstacles to controlling the disease were the high transmission rate, heterogeneous symptomatology, the absence of a vaccine, and the lack of targeted therapy [2–4]. Therefore, before approved vaccines were available, situational management prioritized stopping the spread of the disease and providing supportive care [2–4]. During pandemics, local, national, and global authorities are responsible for disseminating accurate information and enforcing preventive measures to control the spread of the disease [5, 6]. Some of these behaviors included the use of face masks, stay-at-home orders, hand washing, travel restrictions, and physical distancing [7]. However, dissatisfaction with political authorities or healthcare services is a common barrier to implementing recommended protective behaviors [8].

In addition to dissatisfaction, initial emotional distress, the level of trust in the general population related to information sources and/or perceived risk could influence the unconditional willingness to adhere to public health recommendations [6, 9, 10]. A longitudinal study in Chile found that government trust indirectly influenced the acceptance of protective behaviors against COVID-19 in young adults [11]. Similarly, a longitudinal cohort study in Switzerland showed that young adults with low government trust had less compliance with public health measures [12]. Additionally, the level of trust among information sources varies across stakeholders [8]. While healthcare professionals are often among the most trusted sources of information, trust in public media is more heterogeneous and may vary depending on the nature, consistency, and ideological orientation of messaging [6, 13].

Moreover, various sociodemographic characteristics that evolve over time, significantly influence the adoption of preventive health behaviors. A meta-analysis of respiratory infectious disease epidemics and pandemics showed that women were 50% more likely to adopt non-pharmaceutical, preventive health behaviors than men were [14]. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, a literature review on the influenza pandemic concluded that elderly women who were well-educated and self-identified as non-white were associated with increased chances of adopting protective measures behaviors [15]. On the other hand, a more recent analysis of COVID-19 stated that adhering to face mask recommendations among women and men was less likely in the employed group or if they were living in a country with greater gender equity [16]. Therefore, understanding the numerous factors influencing adherence to preventive health behaviors is critical.

However, most studies are limited in their geographical scope or focus on specific populations [11]. Consequently, from a public policy standpoint, it is imperative to understand the factors that influence adherence to preventive measures globally [14–16]. This study aims to evaluate the association between the level of trust of the general population in local health authorities and national/regional leaders who have provided information about COVID-19 and adherence to COVID-19 preventive behaviors. We hypothesized that a higher level of trust in the information source would increase adherence to preventive health behaviors among the participants.

Methods

Study design

Data from the International COVID-19 Awareness and Responses Evaluation (iCARE) study (https://icare.mbmc-cmcm.ca/) led by the Montreal Behavioural Medicine Centre, Quebec, Canada was used for the analyses. The design and methods of this study have been comprehensively outlined in a prior publication [17]. This cross-sectional survey study was conducted across multiple countries and waves, and focused on public awareness, attitudes, and reactions to COVID-19 public health policies. The surveys included questions about sociodemographic characteristics, physical/mental health history, beliefs and behaviors related to COVID-19, vaccination, and testing [17, 18]. Data were collected from the iCARE global convenience and snowball sampling strategy, and adapted and modified for the country-specific context. The survey was administered online via LimeSurvey© (Montreal, Canada) and through a network of more than 200 international collaborators [17].

Participants

This study included data from Survey 5 to Survey 15 (n = 14,453), collected between September 15, 2020, and January 11, 2022, as the measure of trust in actors who provide COVID-19 information was introduced during those survey waves. The study included participants aged 17 years or older from 116 countries, all of whom provided informed consent.

Independent variable: trust in information sources

The study’s main predictor was participants’ level of trust in entities that have provided information about COVID-19. It was assessed with the question: “Please rate your level of trust in the following people and organizations who have provided information about COVID-19: a. My local health authorities, b. my national political leader and c. regional political leader”. National and regional political leaders were combined into a single variable called political leaders after initial analysis revealed similar trust levels across these groups, with only a very weak association between leadership level and trust (Cramér's V = 0.07). The level of trust variable was then categorized as follows: a. I trust to a great extent, b. I trust somewhat, c. I do not trust (distrust), d. I do not trust at all. Detailed information on the iCARE questionnaires for each period of data collection can be found online (https://osf.io/nswcm/).

Outcome measures

The main outcome was the adoption of preventive behaviors. It was assessed by the frequency with which participants adopted each behavior in the previous seven days. The following behaviors included (1) hand washing with soap and water, (2) wearing a face mask, (3) staying at least 1–2 m away from other people, (4) avoiding gatherings (working at home rather than going to work or school, avoiding going out to bars/pubs/restaurants, avoiding large social gatherings, avoiding small social gatherings, avoiding indoor social), and (5) self-quarantining if someone had the virus or believed they had the virus. The answers were categorized as binary variables, such as adherence (most of the time) or non-adherence (otherwise). We include those variables because they were proposed as evidence-based preventive measures and align with recommendations from global public health authorities (7).

Statistical analysis

This analysis was conceptually guided by constructs from the COM-B model and the Health Belief Model, which informed the selection of variables and interpretation of relationships. In this framework, adherence to preventive behaviors is influenced by capabilities, opportunities, and motivations, as well as perceptions [16]. Trust in the actors who provided information sources is conceptualized in this study as a key motivational and perceptual factor that may influence adherence behaviors.

Descriptive analysis was performed to summarize participant characteristics, stratified by level of trust in political and healthcare authorities. Observations were excluded from the analysis if data were missing or if participants indicated the response ‘I don’t know/I prefer not to answer’. A bivariate analysis was conducted to examine differences in characteristics as a function of trust levels in local health authorities and political governments. Bivariate analyses were conducted using Pearson’s chi-square tests to examine (1) differences in participant characteristics across levels of trust in local health authorities and political governments, and (2) the association between trust levels and adherence to preventive behaviors.

Afterward, multivariable logistic regression models were conducted. We included the following variables as part of the analysis country of residence categorized by Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), age, educational attainment, perception of home income, living area, chronic disease, mental disease, employment status, living alone or with more persons, living with kids, the perception of the importance of preventive measures, the perception of the authorities’ actions, and the perception of the risk of being infected. We assessed variance inflation factors to rule out multicollinearity. Odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated and reported for each model. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, United States).

Discussion

This study examined the association between trust in actors who provided COVID-19 information, including local health authorities and political leaders, and adherence to COVID-19 preventive behaviors across a large, international sample. Overall, higher levels of trust were consistently associated with an increased likelihood of adherence to recommended preventive measures. This finding aligns is consistent with previous research conducted during the influenza A (H1N1) outbreaks in Hong Kong and Spain, which reported that trust in government-provided information was positively associated with compliance with hand-washing and social distancing measures [19, 20]. Although these studies focus specifically on information sources, the findings are broadly aligned with our results, suggesting that trust in information providers more generally plays an important role in shaping adherence to public health recommendations.

However, our findings highlight variations in behavioral responses across different contexts and demographic groups. For instance, females demonstrated a higher overall adherence than males, consistent with previous research indicating that women are more likely to engage in preventive behaviors, particularly in contexts of low trust [21]. Age-related differences were also observed. Younger individuals were less likely to practice social distancing, whereas mask-wearing did not significantly differ by age. These results are consistent with other studies that have shown that older adults are more likely to adhere to protective behaviors [21, 22].

Interestingly, this study did not find consistent associations between adherence and factors such as medical or psychological conditions, educational attainment, household composition, or employment status after adjusting for the level of trust in health authorities and political leaders. This contrasts with studies that reported that higher education or the presence of a chronic medical condition increased the likelihood of adopting preventive behaviors [23, 24].

In our study, the country’s SDI was strongly associated with adherence, suggesting that broader socio-economic conditions may be more influential than individual-level characteristics. Individuals in low- and middle-development status were generally less likely to follow protective measures. This may be influenced by broader cultural and political contexts [25, 26]. For instance, cultural orientations—such as power distance and uncertainty avoidance—mediate the effect of trust on behavioral intentions across countries, suggesting that structural and societal norms play a critical role in shaping public health responses [25]. This could also influence the different strategies adopted and endorsed across countries regarding preventive behavior [27].

Our study further revealed that trust in local health authorities had a stronger association with adherence to protective behaviors than trust in political leaders. While previous studies identified healthcare professionals and government communications as the most trusted sources of information [23, 28], our findings suggest that trust in health-specific institutions may be a more robust driver of behavioral compliance than general political trust. This distinction is critical for designing effective public health messaging and interventions.

These findings have important implications for public health policy. They underscore the need to ensure consistent messaging across sources and to adapt communication strategies for populations in diverse global contexts to support effective public health responses. In particular, involving local healthcare professionals can improve adherence to recommended measures. The lack of consistent associations between adherence and some individual-level characteristics after adjustment for trust suggests that interventions focusing solely on individual attributes may be insufficient. Instead, the strong association observed with country SDI highlights the importance of broader structural determinants in shaping adherence such as addressing contextual barriers that may limit the feasibility of preventive behaviors.

This study has several limitations. First, we conceptualize the association as primarily direct at the level of statistical modelling; however, we acknowledge that underlying behavioral pathways are likely complex and may involve unmeasured mediating mechanisms [10]. Furthermore, given the cross-sectional design, the observed association between trust levels and behavioral adherence should not be interpreted as causal. Second, response bias may be present, as participants who chose to respond could differ systematically from non-respondents [29]. Additionally, the sample mainly consisted of individuals from high-SDI countries with high education levels. These may limit its generalizability. Third, given the global scope and timeframe, we were unable to fully account for context-specific events or temporal dynamics that may have influenced trust in political and healthcare representatives or the adoption of preventive behaviors. Although we adjusted for a range of sociodemographic and perceptual variables, residual confounding due to unmeasured contextual and time-varying factors cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

Trust has an important role in fostering adherence to recommended health measures. The findings of our study reveal a positive association between trust in health and political authorities and adherence to preventive health behaviors, independent of demographics, socioeconomic factors, or perceptual factors. These results underscore the importance of promoting trust in public institutions among the general population, particularly in preparedness for future public health crises. While further research is needed, these findings have practical implications for improving the delivery of health measures before and during pandemics. Strategies should be tailored to each population's specific needs and characteristics. Key priorities include strengthening community-based communication efforts and actively involving the public in health decision-making and policy development.
