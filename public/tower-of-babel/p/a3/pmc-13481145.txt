Palopegteriparatide treatment for hypoparathyroidism in autoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy: a case series and literature update
Authors: Haley Akers, Lucas Maxey, Kadin Ashley, Florence Lima, Madhumathi Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy (APECED), also known as autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 1, is an autosomal recessive disorder of thymic T cell negative selection that results in autoimmune destruction of various endocrine and extra-endocrine tissues. The classic clinical triad of the syndrome includes hypoparathyroidism (hypoPT), chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC), and adrenal insufficiency, while involvement of thyroid, pancreas, gonads, and ectodermal tissues are also frequently observed. Historically, treatment for hypoPT, the most common endocrine manifestation of APECED, has posed a unique challenge. Conventional therapy with oral calcium and active vitamin D analogs is often complicated by the presence of malabsorption and esophageal disorders, which are prevalent in APECED patients. Moreover, supplementation with oral calcium and active vitamin D analogs without correction of the underlying PTH insufficiency can result in renal and other soft-tissue calcifications. Short-acting PTH therapies, which have been used off-label in the treatment of hypoPT, fail to mimic normal parathyroid physiology due to their brief half-lives and can result in fluctuations of serum calcium levels between hypocalcemia and hypercalcemia. Long-acting PTH analogs have recently been developed to more closely mimic physiologic PTH activity. One such agent, palopegteriparatide, a prodrug of PTH (1,34), has shown promise to be the first hormone replacement therapy for hypoPT. We report our experience in 4 patients with APECED-associated hypoPT who initiated treatment with palopegteriparatide and were followed over the subsequent 4-15 mo period. We observed a robust calcemic response to treatment during this follow-up window with wide fluctuations in serum calcium during this period of dose titration. These early observations highlight the effectiveness of palopegteriparatide in patients with APECED, enabling the discontinuation of oral calcium and active vitamin D supplements. They also highlight the question of whether etiology-specific differences in responsiveness may influence dose requirements and calcemic stability.

Introduction

Autoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy (APECED), also known as autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 1 (APS-1), is an autosomal recessive genetic disorder with an estimated prevalence of 2.6-10 cases per million individuals.1 Autoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy is caused by a mutation in the autoimmune regulator (AIRE) gene.2 Dysfunction in the transcription factor encoded by AIRE results in inadequate negative selection of immature thymic T cells, leading to autoreactive leukocytes in peripheral tissues. Direct infiltration of inflammatory cells and the formation of autoantibodies against various tissues leads to the constellation of symptoms associated with APECED.3,4

Despite the monogenic nature of the disease, there is a wide heterogeneity of phenotypes.5 The classic clinical triad of hypoparathyroidism (hypoPT), adrenal insufficiency, and chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC) is seen in approximately 57.3% of patients,6 while 7% of patients never develop any clinical features associated with the triad.1 Hypoparathyroidism is the most common endocrine manifestation (79%), followed by primary adrenal insufficiency (72%). Other endocrine dysfunction—including thyroid (4%), pancreatic beta islet cells (12%), and ovaries/testes (60%/14%)—is not uncommon. Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis is present in at least 77% of APECED patients.3 In addition to CMC-associated esophagitis, other gastrointestinal (GI) manifestations of APECED include chronic gastritis and pernicious anemia, autoimmune hepatitis, and various malabsorptive conditions. Gastrointestinal involvement is estimated to occur in 25% of patients.7 Ectodermal involvement includes enamel defects (77%), nail dystrophy (52%), vitiligo (13%), and alopecia (29%).5

Autoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy also has a significant impact on kidney function and bone health. Renal dysfunction is noted in up to 10% of APECED patients. The most common etiologies are nephrocalcinosis, as a result of hypoPT-induced hypercalciuria, and tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN), due to immune cell infiltration and autoantibody formation in some cases.8 Approximately 23% of APECED patients have either osteopenia or osteoporosis, those with more severe APECED phenotypes having reduced BMD compared to those with fewer manifestations of the disease.9 Several APECED features—including malabsorption, the potential for supraphysiologic hydrocortisone dosing in the treatment of adrenal insufficiency, premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), and growth hormone deficiency—can contribute to impaired bone structure and lower BMD.9

Current treatment for APECED revolves around replacement of the above-mentioned hormonal insufficiencies, antifungals for the treatment of CMC, symptomatic treatment for various other clinical manifestations, and routine monitoring for signs of other presentations of the disease.5 Newer agents, such as ruxolitinib, are in clinical trials and may revolutionize the management of the condition if they are able to limit the autoimmune damage to endocrine organs.10 However, since their impact on metabolic derangements, such as hypoPT, is currently unknown, optimizing replacement therapies remains a priority. Due to the lack of a physiological hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for hypoPT, the management of this component of APECED has been challenging. More recently, long-acting PTH analogs have been developed for the treatment of hypoPT.11 One such analog, palopegteriparatide has been approved by the FDA for use in adults with hypoPT and shows promise to be the first PTH replacement therapy. Herein, we describe our experience with 4 patients with APECED-associated hypoPT who initiated treatment with palopegteriparatide.

Case descriptions

Patient 1 is a 48-yr-old female referred to UK Healthcare Bone and Mineral Metabolism (UKHC BMM) Clinic for evaluation of osteoporosis. She had previously visited the National Institutes of Health (NIH), where she was confirmed to have APECED through genetic testing, which demonstrated 2 pathogenic variants in AIRE including c.967_979del13 (p.Leu323fs). Her endocrine-related complications of APECED included hypoPT with baseline PTH <4 pg/mL, adrenal insufficiency requiring chronic corticosteroid supplementation, insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, and POI necessitating HRT. Other complications include CMC with subsequent esophageal stricture causing malnutrition (BMI 14.98 kg/m2). Prior to receiving short-acting PTH therapy, management for hypoPT consisted of 0.75 mcg of calcitriol daily with 1.5-3 g of calcium supplementation daily that she was using long term. During this time, serum calcium levels varied considerably between 6.0 and 9.0 mg/dL, and 24-h urinary calcium was 72 mg/d. From 2023 to 2024, she was treated with PTH replacement therapy using abaloparatide 80 mcg twice daily and was able to decrease her daily calcitriol dose to 0.25 mcg. While undergoing treatment with abaloparatide, she had brief periods of normocalcemia, but they were not sustained. Her urinary calcium-creatinine ratios ranged from 0.04 to 0.32 during this time. In December 2024, she started palopegteriparatide therapy at a starting dose of 18 mcg and was able to discontinue both calcium supplementation and calcitriol. Palopegteriparatide dose adjustments were required due to multiple episodes of hypercalcemia, and she was ultimately stabilized on 12 mcg daily of palopegteriparatide, with most recent serum calcium values within normal limits. From 2018 to 2019, she received PTH replacement therapy with rhPTH(1-84) 50 mcg daily, with only transient improvement in hypocalcemia. She discontinued rhPTH(1-84) in 2019 due to reported intolerance and overall poor medication adherence. Throughout her treatment course, she rarely adhered to calcium and calcitriol supplementation, citing dysphagia and symptoms related to esophageal stricture. Palopegteriparatide was started in February 2025 at a starting dose of 18 mcg. After an initial period of nonadherence that gradually evolved into acceptance, palopegteriparatide dosing was titrated to 12 mcg daily. Patient 4 is a 39-yr-old male referred to UKHC BMM Clinic for hypoPT. He was diagnosed with hypoPT at age 4 following the development of tetany in his hands and feet with baseline PTH <6 pg/mL. A clinical diagnosis of APECED was made in his 20s, confirmed by genetic testing in 2020 showing 2 pathogenic variants in AIRE. Prior to PTH replacement therapy, he was treated with 0.5 mcg of calcitriol daily with intermittent calcium supplementation. During this time, he had frequent episodes of iatrogenic hypercalcemia. The 24-h urinary calcium was 108 mg/d at this time. Palopegteriparatide therapy was started in August 2025 at a starting dose of 18 mcg, allowing discontinuation of calcitriol. Dose titration was marked by episodes of hypercalcemia eventually stabilizing him at the current dose of 9 mcg daily with calcium supplementation as needed for symptoms of hypocalcemia. Serum calcium values since starting palopegteriparatide have been relatively stable and often within normal limits. Discussion

Autoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy patients are heterogeneous in terms of clinical presentation, with varying combinations of endocrine organ derangements. Historically, treatment of hypoPT, the most common endocrinopathy associated with APECED, has posed a unique challenge in this population. Standard treatment in patients with hypoPT included multiple calcium supplements and active vitamin D analogs (oral calcitriol) to increase GI calcium absorption and mitigate hypocalcemia.5 The presence of malabsorption in a significant portion of APECED patients complicates treatment with these oral agents and necessitates intermittent i.v. therapy in some cases. Additionally, replenishment of calcium and active vitamin D without correction of the underlying hypoPT may have detrimental effects on the kidneys, such as nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis, as well as brain, vascular, and other soft tissue calcifications.5,13,14 Adjunctive therapy with rhPTH(1-84) was available between 2015 and 2019 in the US market when it was recalled, leaving teriparatide and abaloparatide as the only (off-label) treatment option for hypoPT. In pediatric patient populations, short-acting PTH therapy has shown some promise to maintain serum calcium, BMD, and normal bone growth and development for up to 10 yr.12,15 However, short-acting therapy ultimately fails to mimic normal physiology, requires multiple dosings daily due to its transient effects, and results in hypercalcemia and hypercalcuria.16 Due to these shortcomings, long-acting PTH analogs, such as palopegteriparatide, have recently been developed. Palopegteriparatide is a prodrug of PTH (1-34) administered once daily, providing physiologic levels of PTH exposure throughout the 24-h period.17 The phase 3 PaTHway trial has shown approximately 95% of patients with hypoPT treated with palopegteriparatide will achieve independence from conventional therapy and nearly 81% achieve normocalcemia by 1 yr, with normalization of mean 24-h urine calcium and improvement in renal function.18,19

Herein, we report 4 patients with APECED who initiated PTH replacement therapy with palopegteriparatide. In terms of clinical presentation, the 4 patients reported here are representative of the heterogeneity of phenotypes seen in APECED. Distribution of APECED-associated clinical phenotypes and organ involvement in patients 1-4. Stacked bars are color-coded to represent clinical manifestations of APECED. The combination of segments differs across patients, illustrating variability in organ involvement.

These 4 patients also represent the burden of the sequelae of hypoPT. CKD was seen in all 4 patients, with one requiring kidney transplant. Three of the four patients had confirmed vascular calcifications, with variable involvement of coronaries, aorta, and carotids. Two patients had confirmed calcifications of brain tissue. Two of the four had calcifications in other soft tissues, leading to impaired wound healing and soft tissue necrosis.

In all 4 patients, there was a progressive correction of serum calcium after initiation of palopegteriparatide that had not been seen with their previous therapies (Figure 2). In the case of patients 1, 2, and 4, there were periods of hypercalcemia during initial palopegteriparatide dose titration. In the case of patients 1 and 2, there was a magnitude of elevation in serum calcium that had not been documented prior to starting palopegteriparatide. In the case of patient 3, serum calcium values have approached the normal range after initiating palopegteriparatide despite issues with adherence to medication regiments, including an inability to tolerate oral calcitriol and calcium supplementation. The apparent volatility in serum calcium seen in Figure 2 can, in part, be attributed to these patients still being in a period of dose titration. This is also a period characterized by more frequent calcium checks compared to the time prior to starting palopegteriparatide. Additionally, even small changes in dose were noted to cause wide variations in response. These serum calcium trends are consistent with results seen in clinical trials,18 with our patients achieving independence from traditional therapy within a year of initiating palopegteriparatide.

In addition to the early variability in calcemic response, we also observed dosing variability as patients progressed to a steady state. In APECED patients, factors, such as malabsorption, the use of concurrent steroid treatment which can worsen hypercalcuria, and variable residual PTH secretion, may all contribute to varying dose requirements. It is also possible that APECED patients may require different doses of PTH replacement compared to other etiologies, such as post-surgical, again for the aforementioned reasons. Winer et al. reported on the different effects of short-acting PTH therapies by etiology of hypoPT—post-surgical, APECED, activating calcium-sensing receptor mutation, and idiopathic. Their study showed that those with APECED-associated hypoPT required higher doses of PTH (1-34) to achieve serum calcium in the low-normal range compared to those with post-surgical hypoPT.20 Whether these potential etiology-specific differences exist with long-acting PTH analogs remains unclear, as palopegteriparatide responses across different etiologies of hypoPT have not yet been well defined. Larger studies comparing the dosage requirement and treatment response to palopegteriparatide across etiologies while accounting for other patient factors, such as BMI, residual PTH secretion, magnesium levels, and the duration of hypoPT, would be required for better understanding of dosing requirements.

Although the short duration of follow-up and the case series design limit any meaningful long-term inferences regarding management, we believe this represents the first report describing the use of palopegteriparatide in a series of patients with APECED including a patient with a kidney transplant. Our study reports our early experience with the use of palopegteriparatide in patients with a rare cause of hypoPT.
