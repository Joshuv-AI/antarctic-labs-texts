Validating cephalometric angular reconstruction for determining vertical dimension in edentulous patients
Authors: Aishwarya Mahajan, Vivek Vivek, Anuj K Shukla, Chandana Nair, Mudita Chaturvedi, Ghazala Suleman, Tushar Vitthalrao Bhagat, Mohammed A Al-Qarni, Nishant K, Vishwanath Gurumurthy, Saurabh Chaturvedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate vertical dimension of rest (VDR) is critical in complete denture fabrication. Along with conventional phonetic and electromyographic (EMG) methods; cephalometric angular reconstruction (CAR) has recently been proposed as a reliable approach that minimizes reliance on patient dexterity. This in-vivo study aimed to determine the effectiveness of the CAR method by comparing it with conventional and electromyographic methods in determining VDR in edentulous subjects.Materials and methodsTwenty completely edentulous patients were enrolled. For each patient, VDR was measured using phonetic, CAR, and EMG methods. Data were analyzed with SPSS version 23 using one-way ANOVA and independent t-tests. A sample size of 20 provided 80% power with an effect size of 0.8 at a significance level of 0.05. One month after denture insertion, patient satisfaction was assessed with a structured questionnaire.ResultsThe mean VDR (cm) was 5.36 ± 0.61 for the conventional method, 5.35 ± 0.74 for CAR, and 5.64 ± 0.62 for EMG. Although the EMG method yielded slightly higher values, independent t-tests revealed no statistically significant differences between conventional and CAR (p = 0.962), conventional and EMG (p = 0.181), or CAR and EMG (p = 0.167). Patient satisfaction scores indicated favorable outcomes across all groups.ConclusionThere was no statistically significant difference among the three methods, confirming that CAR can serve as a viable alternative to conventional and EMG approaches.Clinical implicationsThe CAR method may be particularly useful for patients with neuromuscular issues, psychological challenges, or limited cooperation during traditional VDR recording.Keywords: Geriatric, Complete denture, VDO, VDR, EMG, Cepahlometry

Introduction

Edentulism, or complete tooth loss, is a common condition among the geriatric population, significantly impacting their oral function, facial aesthetics, phonetics, and overall quality of life [1]. As the global population continues to age, the need for effective and accurate prosthodontic rehabilitation strategies becomes increasingly critical. Prosthetic rehabilitation aims to restore the stomatognathic complex to achieve a harmonious balance between teeth, bones, joints, and muscles [1, 2].

The use of a complete denture is one of the most common rehabilitation methods for completely edentulous patients. One of the most important steps in fabricating a complete denture is determining the vertical dimension of rest (VDR). Accurate assessment of the VDR is essential, as it directly influences both the functional and aesthetic outcomes of dentures [3–5].

The vertical dimension of occlusion (VDO) and the VDR have a close interdependent relationship. The interocclusal rest space (typically 2–3 mm) acts as a buffer between the two. Any deviation (increase/decrease) in VDO affects muscular tone and facial proportions, which in turn leads to adaptation in facial height [6, 7]. Despite advancements in techniques, technology, and materials, there is still no universally accepted or entirely accurate method for assessing VDR in edentulous patients.

Multiple techniques have been reported in the literature for the VDR, such as profile tracing, preextraction records, rest position assessment, intraoral measurements, phonetics, swallowing, neuromuscular perception, and craniometrics [8]. However, the reproducibility and reliability of these methods remain a subject of concern, as soft tissue reference points tend to be unstable [9]. The instability of soft tissue landmarks has led researchers to explore alternative methods, particularly those that provide objective assessments of the VDR, such as electromyography (EMG) techniques and cephalometry [10–14].

The use of electromyography (EMG), which measures the electrical activity of muscles, is recommended in the literature. EMG is a noninvasive technique that records muscle activity and has been proposed as a potential method for assessing the mandibular rest position by recording muscle activity in the temporal and masseter muscles [14]. Supporters of this method argue that it provides a more physiological basis for determining the VDR, as it directly measures muscle tone and activity. However, the accuracy and reliability of EMGs have been debated, with studies showing mixed results. Factors such as electrode placement, muscle fatigue, and patient variability can influence EMG recordings, making it a less consistent method for clinical use [12–14].

The cephalometric technique involves cephalometric landmarks, which are more stable and reliable for determining the VDR. Cephalometry has been used as a diagnostic aid in prosthetic dentistry for over five decades. The technique is advantageous because of its affordability, minimal radiation exposure, and high accuracy in estimating the VDR [10]. However, despite these benefits, the complexity of cephalometric procedures has limited their widespread adoption among clinicians.

To overcome these limitations, the cephalometric angular reconstruction (CAR) method was developed as a simplified approach that integrates cephalometric analysis with facial proportions [11]. This method relies on bony markers that remain unchanged in edentulous patients, making it a potentially reliable and repeatable technique for determining the VDR.

Despite the numerous methods available for determining the VDR, no single approach has been universally accepted as the gold standard. Conventional methods are often subjective and depend on clinician expertise [15]. Cephalometric analysis provides a more objective framework but is not commonly utilized because of its complexity [11, 12]. EMG offers a technologically advanced solution but has raised concerns regarding accuracy and consistency. When comparing clinical applicability, cephalometric anthropometric records (CAR) are generally more time-consuming, requiring specialized equipment, multiple procedural steps, and technical expertise, which may prolong chairside time and increase treatment costs. In contrast, surface electromyography (EMG) offers a relatively faster and non-invasive approach that can be performed directly in the clinic without extensive preparation, potentially reducing overall appointment duration. From a cost perspective, CAR involves repeated radiographic imaging and laboratory processing, adding to patient expense, whereas EMG—though requiring initial investment in equipment—can become more cost-effective in the long term due to its efficiency and repeatability. Thus, EMG demonstrates a relative advantage in clinical time management and cost-effectiveness, provided the operator has adequate training and the method is standardized for consistent outcomes.

Given these challenges, there is a need for a comparative analysis of these three approaches to assess their reliability, ease of use, and effectiveness in determining VDR in edentulous patients. The CAR method being simplest might help clinicians enhance the precision of denture fabrication in geriatric patients by enhancing retention [16, 17], reduce chairside adjustments, and improve the long-term functionality with or without implants use [18] and comfort of complete dentures. Furthermore, a cephalometric-guided approach may allow for standardized protocols in vertical dimension reconstruction, promoting evidence-based practice in prosthodontics. For the aging population, this means better outcomes, improved oral health-related quality of life, and the restoration of facial and masticatory function with greater clinical confidence [19].

The determination of vertical dimension at rest (VDR) is a fundamental step in the fabrication of complete dentures, as it directly influences patient comfort, functional efficiency, and esthetic outcomes. Employing a technique that offers accuracy, reproducibility, and clinical simplicity is essential for optimizing prosthetic performance, minimizing patient discomfort, and streamlining the overall treatment process. This comparative evaluation is expected to provide valuable evidence on the relative advantages and limitations of different approaches, thereby guiding their practical application in routine clinical practice. Thus, the present study aimed to determine the effectiveness of the CAR method by comparing it with conventional and electromyographic methods in determining VDR in edentulous geriatric subjects. The null hypothesis was that there would be no statistically significant difference in the accuracy of VDR determination among conventional, cephalometric angular reconstruction, and electromyographic methods in edentulous patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and study participants

The study protocol was developed, and ethical clearance was obtained from the institutional ethics committee (approval number EC/NEW/INST/2021/2230NECRBHR, DHR, MOHFW, New Delhi). The chief researcher recruited patients who needed complete dentures from the outpatient department of the Department of Prosthodontics and Crown and Bridge, Institute of Dental Sciences, Bareilly, India. Informed written consent was obtained from all the participants before the start of the study. To standardize the study protocol, a synchronized flowchart was prepared for the fabrication of various complete dentures.

Sample size determination

A power analysis was performed via G*power, version 3.1.9.7. A sample size of 19 subjects would yield 80% power to detect significant differences, with an effect of 0.8 [20] and a significance level of 0.05. Effect size d = 0.8; α err prob = 0.05; Power (1-β err prob) = 0.95; Number of Groups = 2. Output: Noncentrality parameter λ = 3.4871192; critical t = 1.7340636; df = 18; total sample size = 19; actual power = 0.9560779]. A total of 20 patients were selected for this study on the basis of strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients with well-formed and highly rounded, completely healed maxillary and mandibular ridges with Class I skeletal patterns were selected (the Class I skeletal pattern was determined via the YEN angle [11], (Fig. 1), which is based on landmarks such as the midpoint of the sella; the midpoint of the premaxilla; and the center of the largest circle tangent to the internal inferior, anterior and posterior surfaces of the mandibular symphysis, measured at the midpoint of the premaxilla). If this angle was between 117 and 123 degrees, it was considered a Class I skeletal pattern [21, 22], and those who agreed to sign a consent were included in the study. Patients with temporomandibular joint disorder, muscular hyperactivity, neuromuscular or mental disorders, highly resorbed ridges and structural or functional abnormalities of the stomatognathic system that would interfere with the determination of the VDR via EMG were excluded.

A patient information sheet was given to each patient, and a detailed case history was recorded by the chief researcher.

Fabrication of complete dentures

From primary impressions to jaw relations, facial Bow (Hanau Spring-Bow; Whipmix, Louisville, KY, USA) and interocclusal bite (polyether bite registration material; RAMITEC, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) records were obtained from patients in the conventional manner of CD fabrication [23].

In this process, the primary impressions were recorded in E-Dentulous Non-perforated rim lock trays. (UNICORN DenMart Ltd, API, New Delhi, India) using medium fusing impression compound (DPI Pinnacle Impression Compound, Mumbai, India) and was poured into type II dental plaster to fabricate a primary cast over which custom trays were fabricated with a spacer thickness of 1 mm. Border molding was performed using a low fusing green stick compound (DPI Pinnacle Impression Compound, Mumbai, India). A secondary impression was made via the use of zinc oxide eugenol (DPI Impression paste, Mumbai, India) via the selective pressure technique after the spacer was removed, and the sample was then poured into type III dental stone to fabricate a secondary cast. On the secondary cast denture base and wax, occlusal rims were fabricated for recording the jaw relation. The orientation jaw relationship was recorded via a maxillary occlusal wax rim and a Hanau Spring Bow facebow (Hanau Spring-Bow; Whipmix, Louisville, KY, USA), which was transferred to a Hanau Wide Vue articulator (HanauArticulator; Whipmix, Louisville, KY, USA). Special care was taken to record the vertical jaw relationship.

The patients were randomly allocated into 3 groups via the lottery method for the fabrication of complete dentures, with VDs recorded via one of the three methods used in the study. Participants were randomly allocated using the lottery method. Each subject’s identification number was written on identical slips of paper, folded uniformly, and placed in a container. An independent researcher mixed and drew the slips sequentially, assigning participants to groups with equal probability. The process was repeated until both groups reached the required sample size, ensuring transparency and minimizing selection bias. For the vertical jaw relation record, two markings were placed, one at point A just below the nose and the other at point B above the chin. The VDR was recorded for each patient via the above three methods:

In this method, two points are marked on the patient one at the base of the nose, denoted as point A, and another on the most prominent part of the chin, denoted as point B. The patient is asked to say the word with the syllable “M” a bilabial sound [23–25] and hold the position at which the lips first meet while speaking. At this position, the distance between point A and point B was measured via a divider and scale. This distance was taken as the VDR via the conventional method.

CAR method

A lateral cephalogram was recorded via the cephalogram machine. The patient stood in the erect position in front of the sensor and X-ray source of the cephalostat. Ear rods were placed in both ears, and a nasal probe was adjusted on the bridge of the nose to stabilize the patient for the radiograph. The recorded lateral cephalogram radiograph was taped to the view box, with the image of the patient facing right. The radiograph was overlaid with a matte acetate film, which was attached to the radiograph and the view box.

After the patient was selected, the VDR was calculated via the following landmarks: Porion (P), Nasion (N), Gonion (G), Gnathion (Gn) & Anterior Nasal Spine (ANS). These points were marked via a sharp pencil on the acetate film placed on the lateral cephalograph (Fig. 2a). The marked points were connected together to form angles: N-ANS-G (by linking the N, ANS, and G landmarks) and P-G-ANS (by joining the P, G, and ANS landmarks) (Fig. 2b). To locate point Gn’ (reconstructed), the two angles N-ANS-Gn’ and P-G-Gn’ were computed via the following formulas: N-ANS-Gn’ (in degrees) = 1.271 N- ANS-G (in degrees) + 24.83; P-G-Gn’ (in degrees) = 0.987 P-G-ANS (in degrees) + 35.93. Following this, these angles N-ANS-Gn’ and P-G-Gn’ were traced (Fig. 2c). The VDR in the cephalogram was calculated as the distance between the reconstructed points Gn’ & ANS (Fig. 2d). For tracing, the distance between the N & ANS (x’) and the distance between the ANS & Gn’ (y’) were measured. Electromyographic method

To record the VDR via this method, the patient was instructed to sit in a relaxed manner without any head support. Before the electrodes were placed, the skin was cleaned with a 99.5% alcohol swab, and a skin preparation conductive gel was applied to reduce impedance. The neutral electrode (green in color) was placed on the center of the forehead for grounding. Out of the two reading electrodes, the red electrode was placed on the tendon, and the black electrode was placed on the belly of the muscle to be recorded. For the masseter muscle, the red electrode was placed on the skin just ahead of the tragus, and the black electrode was placed on the skin near the angle of the mandible. For the temporalis muscle, the red electrode was placed on the skin over the infra nuchal line just above the ear, and the black electrode was placed over the skin near the anterior border of the ramus. After these occlusal wax rims, which were adjusted 4 mm short of the VDR recorded via the conventional method, were placed in the patient’s mouth, and the patient was asked to bite the rims. This provides data for maximum muscle activity. From this position, the patient was asked to release the pressure on the rims by slowly relaxing the mouth. The position of the minimum muscle activity was recorded (Figs. 4a and b and 5a and b). At this position, the distance between points A and B was recorded via a divider and a scale, which was considered the VDR by the electromyographic method [26].

The central jaw relation was recorded via the nick and notch method, and the record was transferred to the Hanau Wide Vue articulator. The teeth were arranged in bilateral balanced occlusion, and wax try-in was performed. Denture fabrication was performed via heat-cured denture base acrylic resin (Dentsply Trubyte, USA) and a long curing cycle (9 h in a water bath at 73∘C ± 1∘C followed by 1/2 hour in boiling water, as recommended by the manufacturer) following the manufacturers’ instructions and basic guidelines for the compression molding technique [27]. After complete finishing and polishing, denture insertion was performed following the established conventional protocol.

One month following insertion, the patient’s satisfaction rate was assessed through 3 tick-mark questions. 1- Satisfaction was measured via a visual scale ranging from 1 to 5 (1 denoting dissatisfaction and 5 indicating extreme satisfaction), and the patients rated their denture satisfaction, which was recorded and analyzed descriptively. 2-Reasons for satisfaction (if satisfied) included proper fit, comfort, aesthetics, clear speech, improved chewing, and the absence of pain. - reasons for dissatisfaction included issues such as improper fit, discomfort, lack of aesthetics, speech difficulties, inability to chew with the denture, or temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain.

Results

A total of 20 patients participated in the present study. One month after denture insertion, patient satisfaction was evaluated via a visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from 1 (dissatisfaction) to 5 (extreme satisfaction). The results revealed a mean satisfaction score of 4.6, with scores ranging from 4 to 5, indicating high overall satisfaction.

The graphical and tabulated data revealed consistent positive responses (Table 1; Fig. 6). Patients reported satisfaction due to factors such as proper fit, comfort, enhanced aesthetics, clear speech, better chewing ability, and the absence of pain. These elements were frequently cited as contributing to a favorable denture experience. In contrast, dissatisfaction was linked to issues such as poor fit, discomfort, speech impairment, difficulty in mastication, aesthetic concerns, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain. These findings suggest that well-fit dentures substantially improve patient outcomes, especially in terms of function and comfort.

The data obtained from the present study revealed that the mean vertical dimension at rest (MVDR) (in cm) was 5.36 ± 0.61 cm (95% CI: 5.07–5.64) for the conventional method, 5.35 ± 0.74 cm (95% CI: 5.00–5.69) for the CAR method and 5.64 ± 0.62 cm (95% CI: 5.35–5.93) for the EMG method (Table 2; Fig. 7). The Shapiro‒Wilk test of normality revealed that p > 0.05 indicated a normal distribution of the sample data. Next, one-way ANOVA was adopted to compare the means of the groups (Table 3). The highest MVDR was reported for the EMG method, followed by the conventional method and the CAR method. A comparison of the three methods revealed that the MVDR with the CAR method was lower than the MVDR with the conventional method and the EMG method, but there was no statistically significant difference in the MVDR among these three methods (F (2,57) = 1.265, p = 0.290). The associated effect size was small (η² = 0.043), indicating that only about 4.3% of the variance in MVDR was explained by the method used. The remaining variance (95.7%) was attributable to within-group variability.

Discussion

The field of prosthodontics has advanced significantly in terms of the materials and methodologies employed for dental rehabilitation [28, 29]. However, clinical protocols for the accurate assessment of the vertical dimension of occlusion (VDO) have not undergone proportional development. In the case of edentulous patients, the loss of posterior teeth directly influences the VDO, making its assessment increasingly critical. It has been suggested that restored dimensions should replicate the VDO that exists prior to tooth loss [12], as this is important for successful denture fabrication and overall oral functionality [30, 31]. Prosthodontists face several challenges when determining the VDO during the fabrication of complete dentures. These challenges are compounded by various anatomical and physiological factors. Conditions such as temporomandibular joint disorders, muscle dystonias, soft tissue atrophy, and existing resorption of the alveolar bone can compromise the precision of VDO measurements [32]. Additional factors, such as [33] phonetics, aesthetics, and overall patient comfort, also play a significant role. Age, emotional stress, physical activity, and head position further complicate the matter, as they may affect muscle tension and jaw positioning during the assessment [34]. Moreover, it is essential to recognize that the determination of VDO must be performed adequately by incorporating various methodologies, including physical measurements and subjective assessments from patients regarding comfort and functionality [35]. The present study was conducted with the same aim of comparing different commonly used methods for determining the VDR. The results of the present study revealed that the assessment methods had no statistically significant effect on the determination of the VDR, with a p value of 0.290. Hence, the null hypothesis formulated was accepted.

Studies have demonstrated the importance of establishing a stable VDO to ensure harmonious function between occlusal contacts and the supporting structures of the masticatory system [36, 37]. Consequently, achieving an accurate VDO is not merely a technical task but also one that necessitates sensitivity to the biological and psychological needs of the patient, especially in edentulous cases where oral functionality and quality of life can be significantly compromised [35].

It has been documented in the literature that a VD that is either increased or decreased is detrimental to the underlying ridges and the TMJ; therefore, the location of the rest position to determine the correct VDO is very important. The present study compared the conventional phonetic method, cephalometric angular reconstruction, and electromyographic methods. The most commonly used methods in clinics are Niswonger’s method (swallow and relax) [38] and Swerdlow’s method (phonetics) [39, 40]. Cephalometric methods were suggested by Atwood [41], who compared clinical techniques with the help of cephalometry.

The drawback of the conventional phonetic method is that the clinician is entirely dependent on the patient’s understanding and cognitive ability for accurate measurements of the VDR, as the patient must hold the mandible in a position for a specific time while speaking the words ending with the syllable “M”.

Similarly, for cephalometric analysis, there is a need for a radiographic setup that might not be available at all dental clinics. Radiation exposure is also a concern.

The main shortcoming of conventional cephalometric methods is that the suggested formulae used for determination of the VDR rely on only one cephalometric dimension, which could affect the measurement accuracy because of the presence of soft tissue when replicated clinically. A simple CAR method was used in the present study, as it is evaluated on dentulous individuals and has been proven to be quite accurate. Because the human face has precise dimensions, the reconstruction of facial dimensions via angles revealed statistically significant positive relationships. CAR methods depend on more subjective or static assessments, such as facial landmarks and predefined anatomical dimensions. It incorporates cephalometric data that could theoretically enhance accuracy; however, it is still constrained by inherent limitations in capturing the kinetic nature of muscle behavior, as realized through EMG observations [42].

The EMG method was suggested by Shpuntoff and Shpuntoff [43], Hickey et al. [44] and a few others. Researchers have reported that the VDR is a specific point in the space where the jaw muscles are most relaxed and that the position should be identified and established for denture fabrication; the same procedure was used in the present study. The most critical problem with the EMG method is its machine at the dental chair side, which is not feasible in dental practice because of its cost and time consumption.

There is a close relationship between EMG activity and the active tension developed by a muscle during isometric contraction. Few studies have included all four muscles, such as the masseter, temporalis, anterior digastric and medial pterygoid muscles, while other studies have included either two muscles, the temporalis and masseter, or only one muscle [45]. The masseter is the most superficially located muscle where electrodes can be placed with ease; hence, the masseter muscle was employed to predict the remaining VDs. The temporalis muscle is also important in the control of MRP. It is recommended that at least two muscles be used to predict the position of the mandible at rest. The temporalis functions in two directions, elevation and retrusion, indicating that more effort is needed, as depicted in the EMG recordings. Therefore, the comparative elevation in the amplitude of the EMG for the temporalis could be attributed to the possibility that it is a fan-shaped muscle and functions at a greater distance than the masseter. The muscle is also placed slightly deeper than the masseter muscle [46].

In the present study, the minimal EMG activity of the temporalis muscle was greater than that of the masseter muscle, and the difference between the amplitudes of the readings was also smaller. However, the distance measured between points A and B was almost identical for both muscles. Surface electrodes (disk type) were preferred over needle electrodes to avoid painful stimuli that would interfere with the determination of the VDR. Nevertheless, both types of electrodes were found by others to be equally efficient. The use of surface electrodes is a less invasive procedure and hence was used in our study [26].

The mean vertical dimension at rest (MVDR) recorded via EMG yielded the highest mean (5.64 ± 0.62 cm), followed closely by the conventional method (5.36 ± 0.61 cm) and the CAR method (5.35 ± 0.74 cm); this result is in accordance with previous studies [11, 47]. The analysis of variance indicated that while the EMG method resulted in a slightly greater MVDR, no statistically significant differences were found among the three methods (p > 0.05); similar findings were reported in previous research by Hamdany & Kassab in 2010 [48]. This result was also reported in previous studies, suggesting that various methods for determining the VDR may yield similar results, even though there are variations in the specifics of measurement used [49, 50].

The EMG results were high because of the use of dynamic and real-time assessments of muscle activity. The muscles involved in mandibular movement might exhibit considerable variability in their activation patterns on the basis of the physiological state of the patient as well as external stimuli [51]. Moreover, research highlights that electromyographic (EMG) signals reflect not only the electrical activity of the muscles but also their functional state, including factors such as muscle tension and fatigue [52]. Sierpińska et al. emphasized the need for reliable and accurate methods for determining vertical dimensions crucial for the construction of dentures [53]. This is confirmed by our results, which indicate consistent reliability across methods despite the lack of statistically significant differences [25].

Independently applied t tests further strengthened these findings by confirming that there were no significant differences between any pair of methods (p values for conventional vs. CAR: 0.962, conventional vs. EMG: 0.181, CAR vs. EMG: 0.167) [54]. This finding reinforces the notion that while techniques may differ, the underlying physiological response relevant to the VDR may be universalized across measurement methods [55, 56]. These results are crucial for practitioners in prosthodontics, as they imply that selecting a method may be influenced more by available resources, patient comfort, and the clinical setting rather than by a stark difference in accuracy among them [25, 57]. By including confidence intervals and effect sizes, the analysis confirms that the apparent numerical differences among conventional, CAR, and EMG methods do not represent clinically or statistically meaningful variation. The narrow 95% CIs indicate precision in the estimates across groups. Effect size calculations further support this interpretation: the comparison between conventional and CAR methods yielded a negligible effect size (d = 0.01), while both comparisons involving EMG showed only small-to-moderate effects (d = − 0.46 and − 0.42). Importantly, the ANOVA effect size (η² = 0.043) suggests that methodological differences accounted for less than 5% of the total variance, reinforcing that the three methods are largely comparable in determining vertical dimension at rest.

The present study assessed patient satisfaction one-month postinsertion of complete dentures fabricated via various vertical dimension determination techniques, yielding a high mean satisfaction score of 4.6 on a 5-point visual analog scale. This elevated score underscores a significant level of patient approval, with primary contributors including optimal fit, comfort, aesthetics, clear speech, enhanced masticatory function, and the absence of pain. These findings align with earlier studies [19, 58], which emphasized the critical role of accurate vertical dimensions in achieving proper occlusal balance, thereby minimizing TMJ discomfort and speech issues. Moreover, the data reinforce the conclusions of Zarb & Bolender [23], who highlighted the correlation between vertical dimension accuracy and denture retention and stability. The present findings also support those of previously reported works demonstrating that patients receiving dentures constructed with phonetic and facial measurements reported higher satisfaction than those whose dentures were made via conventional methods alone [59, 60].

Although the current study provides valuable insights into the efficacy of the compared methods, further research is needed to validate these findings in broader clinical scenarios. Larger sample sizes, studies with longer time frames, and in vivo studies determining comfort, aesthetics, and overall satisfaction are suggested to evaluate the clinical relevance of various methods.

Overall, the results of this study validate the CAR approach, as clinicians can increase the precision of denture fabrication in geriatric patients, reduce the need for chairside adjustments, and improve the long-term functionality and comfort of complete dentures. Furthermore, a cephalometric-guided approach may allow for standardized protocols in vertical dimension reconstruction, promoting evidence-based practice in prosthodontics. For the aging population, this means better outcomes, improved oral health-related quality of life, and the restoration of facial and masticatory function with greater clinical confidence [8].

Conclusion

On the basis of the limitations observed in the present study, several important observations can be made regarding vertical dimension determination techniques. The conventional clinical method, specifically the phonetic method using bilabial sounds, depends heavily on the patient’s ability to maintain a position for a period of time, making it reliant on patient dexterity and cooperation. In contrast, the cephalometric analysis of radiographs (CAR) method offers an advantage in that it uses stable skeletal landmarks from radiographs for vertical dimension calculation, rendering it independent of the patient’s dentate status or ability to cooperate. This characteristic makes CAR a potentially more objective and consistent approach for certain patient populations. The electromyographic (EMG) method provides another alternative for measuring electric signals generated during muscle activity to determine the vertical dimension at the point of minimal muscle contraction, which is considered the mandibular rest position (MRP). While each technique offers unique advantages, the study revealed no statistically significant differences in patient satisfaction outcomes across the three methods. These findings suggest that CAR techniques may serve as an effective alternative for conventional methods, especially in clinical situations involving patients who struggle with the cooperative demands of traditional VDR recording. These findings support the inclusion of CAR in the clinician’s toolkit for a more flexible and patient-centered approach to denture fabrication.
