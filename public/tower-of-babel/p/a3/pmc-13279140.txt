Treatment Patterns of Antiseizure Medication for Poststroke Prophylaxis Among Older Adults
Authors: Shuo Sun, Rafaella Cazé de Medeiros, Julianne D. Brooks, Joseph P. Newhouse, Lee H. Schwamm, Sebastien Haneuse, Lidia M. V. R. Moura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntiseizure medications are commonly prescribed for poststroke seizures, yet long‐term use lacks clear clinical guidance. We examined medication treatment patterns among older adults who initiated levetiracetam, the most frequently prescribed antiseizure medication, within 30 days after an acute ischemic stroke discharge.MethodsWe analyzed a national 20% random sample of US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years, who were hospitalized for a first acute ischemic stroke between 2009 and 2021 and initiated levetiracetam within 30 days of discharge. To analyze time‐varying Levetiracetam treatment patterns over the first year after initiation, we calculated an adjusted proportion of days covered that accounts for prescription overlap, hospitalization, and non‐persistence. Adherence to levetiracetam treatment strategy was defined as having a proportion of days covered ≥80%. A latent‐class mixed model on proportion of days covered trajectories was applied to identify and characterize different treatment patterns, adjusting for clinical factors.ResultsIn our sample of 1697 levetiracetam initiators, the mean age was 77.5 years, and 58% were women. The latent‐class mixed model on proportion of days covered trajectories identified 3 distinct levetiracetam treatment patterns: (1) 907 patients (53%) were nonadherent to treatment after 2 months, (2) 99 patients (6%) were nonadherent to treatment after 10 months, and (3) 691 patients (41%) remained consistently adherent to treatment for at least 1 year. White patients and women were more likely to be adherent to the levetiracetam treatment strategy.ConclusionsApproximately 41% of older adults remained consistently adherent to the levetiracetam treatment strategy within a year after outpatient initiation, with race and sex significantly associated with adherence‐to‐treatment probabilities.

Stroke affects over 700 000 individuals per year. 1 Patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) can experience poststroke seizures, with contemporary estimates suggesting early seizures in ≈2% to 23% of patients and late seizures in 3% to 67%, depending on stroke subtype and cortical involvement. 2 Considering the high risk of seizure recurrence in patients following stroke and its negative impact on clinical prognosis, therapy with antiseizure medications (ASMs) is common in older adults. 3 , 4 , 5 Among the different available ASMs, levetiracetam is typically chosen as a first‐line agent. 6

The long‐term use of antiseizure treatment is a subject of considerable debate. 7 A significant challenge in managing ASM use in patients following stroke is the lack of clear guidelines on its use. 7 , 8 , 9 Some patients discontinue ASMs due to adverse effects, 4 , 8 while others maintain high adherence and remain on treatment indefinitely, influenced by what is known as “therapeutic inertia” or “physician inertia,” where the treatment is continued without a clear clinical indication because stopping the medication is not actively considered. 10 , 11

Different objective methodologies can be used to identify and measure these treatment patterns, including proportion of days covered (PDC), medication possession ratio, and others. 12 , 13 Previously used in several studies, PDC calculates the proportion of days within a defined period that a patient can access their medication, considering the number of days in which the medication refills. 14 , 15 For the purpose of this analysis, “adherence to Levetiracetam treatment” is defined as the extent to which a patient's medication‐taking behavior aligns with the prescribed dosing regimen, quantified using the PDC.

This study hypothesizes the existence of considerable variation in the patterns of levetiracetam treatment among older adults following an acute ischemic stroke discharge. We examined patients initiating levetiracetam therapy within the first 30 days after discharge to identify and characterize treatment patterns within 12 months.

Methods

Data Availability

The data supporting this study's findings are collected routinely by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services for billing purposes and were made available by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services with no direct identifiers. 16 Restrictions apply to the availability of these data, which were used under license for this study. Medicare data are available through the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services with their permission. The code that produced the findings is available in Data S1.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines. 17 The requirement for informed consent was waived in our study as we performed a secondary analysis of data routinely collected for billing. All results were aggregated following the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services cell suppression policies.

Data Sources

Demographic variables, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, and insurance status, were obtained for all study patients from Medicare's Master Beneficiary Summary File. Hospitalization and mortality information were extracted from the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review database on the basis of principal diagnosis codes for AIS. 18 We used a validated strategy to capture acute stroke in administrative databases using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) codes 433, 434, 436, and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes I63. 19 Outpatient prescription claims are available in the Medicare Part D Event File. 20 Prescription claims include records of when an individual fills an outpatient medication from the pharmacy and it is billed through insurance. The claims include the date the prescription was filled, medication name, National Drug Code, quantity dispensed, and days' supply, as well as other variables for new prescriptions and refills. Part D data do not include medication indication and do not include inpatient prescriptions, as these are billed as part of hospital stays.

Study Population

We conducted a retrospective analysis of a 20% random sample of US Medicare beneficiaries who were discharged following an AIS between April 1, 2009, and September 30, 2021, and initiated levetiracetam within 30 days after discharge in an outpatient setting.

For the patients with >1 stroke episode during the study period, the first stroke hospitalization was considered their AIS admission index date. We excluded patients who initiated levetiracetam during any hospitalizations after their AIS admission index date. Eligible patients were aged ≥65 years and had continuous enrollment in traditional Medicare Part D (drug prescription coverage) for 1 year before the index stroke hospitalization and at least 1 year plus 30 days following their index stroke hospitalization or died within that period. For each patient, time 0 is defined as the date of the first levetiracetam prescription filled in Medicare Part D after discharge from their index stroke hospitalization. Baseline seizures were identified using ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 diagnosis codes (345 and G40, respectively) indicating seizure events documented within a 1‐year period before AIS admission.

Hospitalization‐Related Supply Adjustment

While Medicare Part D claims data contain records of outpatient prescriptions, individuals may also receive medication if they are admitted to inpatient settings, during which time they typically receive medication from the inpatient pharmacy. To account for hospitalization occurring after the initial levetiracetam prescription while the patient was still taking the medicine, 21 we established the following rules: Hospitalization lasting ≤14 days: We assumed that patients continued taking levetiracetam (provided by the hospital) during their stay. When they returned home and restarted their own supply, they would likely have extra pills. This could make it seem as if they were late refilling their prescription, which would underestimate their adherence to levetiracetam treatment for that time. If patients did not refill their prescription within 90 days of discharge, the days following the hospital admission were classified as nonadherent to treatment, and their subsequent PDC values were set to 0.

Measuring Treatment Patterns

The standard PDC calculates the proportion of days within a defined period that a patient has access to their medication, considering the number of days available refills cover the medication. 22 , 23 It is defined as: PDC=Number of days coveredbymedicationLength of time interval.

To acknowledge hospitalization, prescription overlap, nonpersistency, and death, we used a series of pragmatic strategies to construct an adjusted PDC. When calculating the PDC, prescription overlap was defined as refilling a prescription before the previous supply was exhausted. For example, if a patient picked up a 30‐day supply of levetiracetam 5 days early, they would have 35 tablets on hand, potentially inflating the PDC beyond 100%. To account for this, refill dates were shifted forward by the number of overlapping days. The same adjustment was applied to additional doses dispensed during short hospital readmissions (considered nondisruptive to outpatient therapy as described in the Hospitalization‐Related Supply Adjustment section). If leftover doses overlapped with subsequent refills, the supply was adjusted accordingly to ensure accurate adherence calculations. After these adjustments, the PDC numerator represented the total number of days covered by levetiracetam, and the denominator was defined over 60‐day intervals, censored at the date of death when applicable.

Patients who did not refill their levetiracetam prescriptions within 90 days after the previous prescription's end date were considered nonpersistent with the treatment. Nonpersistent periods were incorporated into the adjusted PDC calculation by treating days without medication as nonadherent to treatment days. This adjustment ensures a comprehensive assessment of the patient's time‐varying treatment patterns, encompassing both active medication use and gaps due to nonpersistence. An example of the adjustments to account for overlaps and hospitalizations is illustrated in Figure 1. Dose and formulation information were available in the claims; however, prescribed directions for use, including dosing frequency, were not captured. Because formulation type does not reliably reflect patient‐specific dosing schedules, these variables could not be used to construct a consistent adherence metric. We therefore used the proportion of days covered as the primary adherence measure.

Statistical Analysis

We used a latent‐class mixed model 24 on adjusted PDC trajectories to identify and characterize patients' time‐varying treatment patterns over the first 12 months after levetiracetam initiation. This approach allowed us to detect behaviors such as prescription inertia and gradual shifts toward chronic use and set the stage for evaluating downstream harms or benefits. To construct the trajectories, we divided the 12‐month follow‐up period into consecutive 60‐day intervals, with adjusted PDC estimates calculated for each patient at each interval. This resulted in adjusted PDC trajectories with up to 7 distinct time points per patient. For each 60‐day slicing interval, patients were classified as adherent to treatment (PDC ≥80% in a given slicing interval) or nonadherent to treatment (PDC values <80%). 23 , 25

The latent‐class mixed model conceptualizes the patient population in question as a mixture of G latent classes, each characterized by a unique mean trajectory. The G mean trajectory profiles are defined as functions of time and covariates through latent‐class–specific mixed‐effects models. The difference with a standard linear mixed model is that both fixed effects and random effects can be class specific. Throughout, we used a logistic model for the binary outcome (nonadherence to treatment/adherence to treatment) and selected the number of classes and covariates for our optimal model based on the Bayesian information criterion. Age was standardized by centering at 65 years and scaling in 5‐year units. The analysis was performed using the “lcmm” package in R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Cumulative adjusted PDCs over the first year after levetiracetam initiation are also calculated and summarized in Data S1.

We present sample characteristics in Table 1 and sample inclusion and exclusion criteria in Figure S1. The 1697 included patients presented with a mean age of 77.5 years (median, 77 interquartile range, 71–83); 57.5% were women, and 79% were White individuals. Among these patients, 1016 (60%) were hospitalized within 1 year plus 30 days after their initial discharge following an AIS admission, and 473 (27.9%) died within the same period. Within the 1016 patients with at least 1 hospitalization episode during the study period, 81.4% had a length of stay of 14 days or fewer (median, 5; interquartile range, 3–11). The average number of hospitalizations was 2.6 (median, 2; interquartile range, 1–3), and the average time from initial discharge to first hospitalization was 75.8 (median, 31; interquartile range, 9–105) days.

Latent‐Class Mixed Model

For computation and interpretation purposes, age was categorized in 5‐year intervals, starting at 65 years. We considered class numbers from 1 to 4 under various model specifications (see Tables S1 and S2).

The optimal model identified the 3 distinct treatment patterns (or “classes”) as follows: class I (early nonadherence to treatment): 907 patients (53.45%) demonstrated an early and rapid onset of nonadherence to treatment, typically becoming nonadherent within the first 2 months after initiation (median time to first nonadherence to treatment was 60 days); class II (gradual nonadherence to treatment): 99 patients (5.83%) showed a progressive decline in adherence to treatment, with adherence gradually declining and first signs of nonadherence typically occurring around 10 months after initiation (median time to first nonadherence was 300 days); class III (consistent adherence to treatment): 691 patients (40.72%) maintained high and stable adherence to treatment for at least 1 year following initiation. Discussion

Our study assessed a national US Medicare sample to identify patterns of adherence to levetiracetam treatment in acute ischemic stroke survivors aged >65 years. By examining these patterns, we aimed to characterize treatment adherence using PDC and to identify factors associated with variation in treatment adherence.

We used PDC to measure levetiracetam treatment adherence, as it considers medication overlap and excess in its methodology, to gain insights into how long patients continue to take medications. 22 , 23 This tool and its importance have been explored in previous studies for adherence to ASM treatment and other drug treatments with cumulative PDC, including the use of claims‐based data. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 In our study, we successfully delineated adherence to ASM treatment by adding time‐varying PDC and identifying different factors related to drug adherence. Additionally, our analysis was built on assumptions about prescription overlap and hospitalization (short/long stay, extra tablets) and nonpersistence assumptions. We introduced a novel framework by making the assumptions explicit in the methodology, providing a transparent replication pathway in future studies.

The indication for ASM prophylaxis in older patients with seizures following stroke remains uncertain, and current clinical guidelines provide little direction on when treatment should be discontinued. 3 , 30 This ambiguity is particularly important in older adults, who are more vulnerable to adverse drug reactions and harmful interactions in the setting of polypharmacy. 31 , 32 , 33 Rather than focusing on prescribing duration itself, our work highlights heterogeneity in medication availability adherence patterns over the first year. Our analysis was designed to characterize adherence trajectories rather than provider‐level treatment decisions, and therefore the patterns we observe reflect medication availability over time rather than prescribing indication, agent selection, or intended duration. Interpretation of these trajectories is complex. Because indication is not directly observable in claims, persistent levetiracetam use beyond 1 year may represent a mix of clinical scenarios. 8 Some patients may remain on treatment without an ongoing indication, consistent with potential overtreatment or therapeutic inertia. Others may experience recurrent events despite maximally tolerated doses, suggesting possible undertreatment or a need for therapy adjustment. Persistent use may also reflect appropriate long‐term management among patients with unequivocal poststroke seizures who achieve seizure control with continued therapy. Early discontinuation is similarly heterogeneous. It may signal gaps in follow‐up, refill barriers, or uncertainty about the intended duration of therapy but may also reflect appropriate discontinuation when the initial prescription was precautionary or of unclear clinical necessity. Identifying these distinct patterns offers a way to better understand real‐world ASM use and patient outcomes and may help inform future studies on outcomes and optimal treatment duration.

In our study, non‐White populations presented with a higher probability of nonadherence to levetiracetam treatment. Increased nonadherence to treatment rates in non‐White populations, such as Black, Hispanic, and other populations, has been documented in the literature, posing a significant social barrier to antiseizure treatment. 34 , 35 Our results also highlighted a higher probability of nonadherence to treatment in male patients, as seen in previous studies. 36 These findings are consistent with prior studies, which attributed nonadherence to ASM treatment to barriers such as forgetfulness, concerns about medication side effects, and negative beliefs about treatment. 37 , 38 , 39 Delineating adherence to treatment trajectories can help identify gaps in follow‐up and alert providers to reexamine patients' medications, thus resulting in an essential tool for surveillance to prevent adverse outcomes and improve health care. 40 , 41 , 42

Only 7.8% of patients in our cohort had a coded seizure diagnosis at baseline, yet all received levetiracetam within 30 days of discharge. This pattern suggests that a substantial proportion of levetiracetam use in this population may reflect empiric poststroke prophylaxis rather than clear secondary prevention, although some seizures are likely underascertained in claims, and levetiracetam may have been prescribed for other neurologic indications. Our decision to focus on levetiracetam was intentional, as its use in this population is largely confined to seizure prophylaxis, which reduces heterogeneity in prescribing indication compared with agents such as lamotrigine or oxcarbazepine that are frequently used for mood or pain. At the same time, we acknowledge that seizure events may be incompletely captured in administrative data (eg, undetected clinical seizures, reported but uncoded events, or outpatient diagnoses not recorded in claims), so individual prescribing rationale cannot be definitively determined. Current guidelines do not recommend routine prophylactic ASM use after ischemic stroke in the absence of seizures, and the observed prescribing patterns therefore highlight ongoing uncertainty in clinical practice about which patients merit initiation and continuation of ASM therapy.

This study is critical to understanding variations and addressing inconsistencies in the antiseizure prophylaxis of discharged patients with AIS. Our findings are consistent with previous findings in the literature, further confirming the differences in nonadherence to ASM treatment across different racial and sex groups. The existing divergent periods of nonadherence to treatment highlight again the need for guidelines for poststroke seizure treatment choice and indication in older populations.

In interpreting these findings, it is important to note that our cohort consisted primarily of older adults enrolled in traditional Medicare. As a result, treatment patterns observed here may not fully reflect those in younger, more diverse populations or patients enrolled in other insurance programs.

Limitations

This study presents limitations. First, generalizability may be limited because the cohort consisted exclusively of older adults enrolled in traditional Medicare (mean age, 77 years; 79% White individuals). Treatment patterns may differ in younger patients and in populations with more racial and ethnic diversity. Additionally, individuals enrolled in Medicare Advantage (Part C), Medicaid, or commercial health plans may have different demographic profiles, comorbidity burdens, care access, and prescribing patterns, which could influence ASM utility trajectories. Therefore, the treatment patterns identified here should be interpreted primarily within the context of older adults insured through traditional Medicare. Second, our claims‐based design does not capture medication administration in inpatient, skilled nursing, or inpatient rehabilitation settings. Although our PDC algorithm incorporated standard adjustments assuming continued ASM use during hospitalization and additional tablets available at discharge, inpatient prescribing patterns cannot be directly observed. As a result, adherence may be overestimated or underestimated for patients with substantial postacute care use, and findings may be less generalizable to populations with frequent transitions across care settings. Third, our study did not reflect patients discharged to skilled nursing facilities or inpatient rehabilitation units, as our study focused on outpatient patterns of medication use.

The use of claims‐based data, despite their reliability, can present common limitations. Entry errors and missing data, such as baseline variables, may exist that would add to our analysis. We also did not consider daily dosages of treatment or drug switches in the analysis. This may bias the adherence to treatment estimates without considering the start of a new therapy with a different ASM. Adherence to treatment was estimated using claims‐based PDC, which assumes that medications dispensed were taken as prescribed. This method does not capture actual ingestion or patient‐level reasons for nonadherence and may therefore misclassify true adherence to ASM treatment.

Fourth, our data set did not include a direct measure of socioeconomic status, and our data use agreement did not allow linkage to external socioeconomic indices such as the Area Deprivation Index or the Social Vulnerability Index. 43 As a result, we could not evaluate whether the observed differences in adherence by race or sex were attenuated after adjustment for socioeconomic status. Race may partially reflect underlying socioeconomic disadvantage, with associations with lower health literacy, reduced continuity of drug coverage, and barriers to care access, and may therefore be collinear with adherence patterns attributed to race. The direction of association for sex is less consistent, with biological, behavioral, and social influences that may affect adherence in either direction. Future studies with linkable socioeconomic data are needed to disentangle these intersecting factors and better characterize inequities in ASM adherence after stroke.

Our data set did not contain information on outpatient follow‐up adherence or the timing of postdischarge neurology visits, which may influence opportunities for reassessment of ASM use. Prior studies show that only 24% to 27% of patients have a neurology visit within 30 days after ischemic stroke and that median wait times for outpatient stroke care can exceed 50 days. 44 , 45 , 46 The absence of follow‐up measures limits our ability to evaluate how continuity of care may relate to early discontinuation or persistent use of levetiracetam.

Finally, our analysis characterizes adherence patterns rather than treatment patterns, and important distinctions exist between these constructs. Treatment patterns, including agent selection, prescribing indication, intended duration, and provider decision making, cannot be inferred from claims. Our trajectories reflect medication availability over time and do not capture the clinical rationale underlying initiation or discontinuation. As a result, the study cannot determine optimal treatment duration or treatment effectiveness, and any implications for future research relate only to the use of adherence patterns as a framework for generating hypotheses. These limitations underscore the need for additional studies linking treatment decisions, adherence behavior, and poststroke outcomes.
