Linking career construction styles to career adaptability among university students: a canonical correlation analysis
Authors: Olcay Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates the relationship between university students’ career construction styles and their career adaptability using Career Construction Theory (CCT) as a guiding framework. Career construction styles—crystallizing, exploring, and deciding—were examined in relation to the adaptability dimensions of concern, control, curiosity, and confidence.MethodsThis study utilized a quantitative, correlational research approach to investigate the association between university students’ career construction styles and their career adaptability resources. Canonical correlation analysis was chosen as the principal statistical method, with the first variable set encompassing three dimensions of career construction (Crystallizing, Exploring, and Deciding), and the second variable set consisting of four dimensions of career adaptability (Concern, Control, Curiosity, and Confidence). This study utilized a “Personal Information Form” together with the “Student Career Construction Inventory” and “Career Adaptability Scale” as instruments for collecting data and a cohort of 780 Turkish university students participated in research.ResultsAmong the career construction dimensions, Crystallizing and Deciding showed the highest structure coefficients within the canonical variate representing Career Construction Styles. However, the proportion of variance in one set associated with the canonical variate of the other set was modest, suggesting that while these forms of behaviour contribute meaningfully to adaptability. Among adaptability resources, concern demonstrated the most substantial canonical loading, indicating that students who are able to define vocational identities and make informed career decisions tend to display stronger future orientation. Although the canonical correlation was statistically significant, redundancy indices indicated a modest proportion of variance in one variable set that is associated with the canonical variate derived from the other set, underscoring the multifaceted nature of career adaptability.ConclusionThese findings reinforce the value of fostering vocational identity formation and decision-making skills in enhancing students’ adaptability, particularly during school-to-work transitions. The study offers implications for culturally informed career counseling interventions and emphasizes the need for longitudinal and multi-method approaches in future research to capture the dynamic interplay between career construction and adaptability.

Introduction

In recent decades, the career landscape has experienced significant alterations driven by globalization, technological advancements, economic crises, and extraordinary global occurrences like the COVID-19 epidemic. These alterations have disrupted conventional career trajectories and caused considerable uncertainty in the job market [1]. The conventional notion of stable, linear career paths has been replaced by more flexible, fractured, and personalized trajectories. In such an environment, individuals—especially university students entering the workforce—experience increasing demands to cultivate skills that facilitate not only finding employment but also long-term career sustainability.

Career construction theory and career adaptability

In such an atmosphere, Career Construction Theory (CCT), proposed by Savickas [2, 3], offers a significant framework for comprehending how individuals actively create meaningful career trajectories in the face of uncertainty. Grounded in Super’s developmental theories [4, 5] and enhanced by narrative methodologies, CCT asserts that this process reflects how individuals transform life experiences into coherent career narratives that fit their own values with societal roles, thereby maintaining adaptability across transitions [6, 7]. This theory emphasizes the narrative aspect of career development in its practical implications. CCT asserts that career behavior exceeds job selection, encompassing the creation of a significant life narrative through work [7]. Students are not simply choosing occupations; they are actively constructing their professional identities. Individuals develop coherent professional narratives that integrate life experiences and project prospective goals through employment, reconstruction, and coconstruction processes [3, 8]. According to Savickas and supported by narrative counseling literature, successful career development relies on the capacity to convert personal concerns into societal contributions, a process that occurs through storytelling and self-construction.

Within Career Construction Theory, career adaptability has been defined as a psychological meta-competency that enables individuals to deal with uncertainty [3, 9]. It includes four linked dimensions—Concern, Control, Curiosity, and Confidence—which together represent proactive attitudes and self-regulatory capacities that promote effective career management [9]. These adaptation resources are considered crucial for managing the developmental challenges, transitions, and traumas linked with occupational life. In this context, career adaptability signifies the attitudes, skills, and behaviors individuals employ to navigate vocational development tasks, overcome challenges, and capitalize on opportunities in an unpredictable work environment [10].

To enhance understanding of these processes, Savickas and colleagues [11] developed the Student Career Construction Inventory (SCCI), which evaluates adaptive responses—particular vocational behaviors and attitudes that reflect career construction efforts. The SCCI classifies those responses into four categories: (a) Crystallizing a vocational self-concept, (b) Exploring to gather information about occupations, (c) Deciding to commit to an occupational choice, and (d) Preparing to execute that choice. These domains reflect the phases in which students participate in deliberate and proactive career development. Savickas and colleagues also developed the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale (CAAS) to operationalize the concept of adaptability, assessing the four C’s: Concern, Control, Curiosity, and Confidence across various populations. When combined with the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale (CAAS), the SCCI allows researchers to investigate the complete adaptation cycle described in CCT [11]. Such integrative models are essential for understanding the development of career-related competences in students and for developing interventions to improve career readiness [12, 13].

Background and present study

Research has consistently demonstrated positive correlations between career adaptability and various adaptive career outcomes, including employability [14], job satisfaction [15], career decidedness [16], subjective career success [17], reduced career decision-making difficulties [18], resilience [10], and psychological well-being [19]. It has also been shown to forecast several favorable outcomes, such as career satisfaction, employability, well-being, and resilience throughout work-related transitions [10, 20]. University students, particularly those transitioning from adolescent to emerging adulthood, are especially vulnerable to the uncertainties of career development. During this crucial period, individuals are anticipated to make substantial educational and vocational decisions that influence their long-term trajectories. According to Hartung and Cadaret [19] and Yıldız and Dirik [21], career adaptability is crucial for navigating transitions, as it provides students with the self-regulatory skills necessary to cope with developmental challenges and occupational setbacks. As such, career adaptability has not only theoretical relevance but also practical significance, particularly for university students who are transitioning from educational environments into the workforce. These students frequently encounter developmental, contextual, and psychological obstacles as they initiate the formation of their vocational identities and execute career goals [16]. Thus, understanding how students develop their careers—via actions such as defining vocational self-concepts, investigating career alternatives, making informed decisions, and readying for employment—yields significant insights into their adaptability and prospective success [11].

Research also suggests that adaptability is influenced by personal traits such as conscientiousness, cognitive flexibility, and proactive personality [22], as well as contextual factors like parental support, socio-economic status, and educational environment. In particular, cognitive flexibility and environmental exploration have been identified as significant contributors to adaptability, mediating its relationship with broader career management strategies. Such findings align with the view of adaptability as a dynamic interplay between individual agency and contextual affordances, reinforcing the necessity for culturally grounded interventions.

Empirical evidence for this model has been accumulating. Li, for instance, demonstrated that adaptability resources mediate the relationship between personality traits (e.g., proactivity, cognitive flexibility) and adaptive responses (e.g., career planning and decision-making) among recent graduates [1]. The research additionally validated those adaptive reactions enhance perceived career success and person-job fit, thus strengthening the applicability of this framework for understanding school-to-work transitions. However, as highlighted by Hirschi et al. [16] and Rudolph et al. [20], a significant portion of the empirical literature has concentrated predominantly on individual elements of the adaptation cycle instead of examining the model comprehensively. Despite the robust theoretical framework, empirical research often focuses on isolated segments of this model, underlining the need for more holistic investigations that integrate all components—from adaptivity to adaptation results [23]. Recent calls by scholars such as Babarovic and Sverko [18] and Rudolph et al. [20] underscore the need for comprehensive investigations that test all dimensions of the CCT model.

This gap is particularly evident in non-Western countries like Turkey, where certain socio-economic factors—elevated young unemployment, centralized educational systems, and cultural collectivism—complicate the career development process. The Turkish adaptation of the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale [24] and the Student Career Construction Inventory [25] has facilitated research into the manifestation of career adaptability among university students in Turkey, thereby enhancing interest in this theory. These instruments have enabled an advanced awareness of the impact of demographic characteristics, socio-economic status, and educational backgrounds on adaptability and career construction methodologies [21].

The Turkish context provides a particularly compelling backdrop for this inquiry. Rapid socio-economic transformation, a highly competitive labor market, and increasing youth unemployment rates require a more profound understanding of students’ career development processes [24, 26]. Moreover, the latest adaptations of CAAS and SCCI into Turkish have facilitated culturally relevant evaluations of career adaptability and construction behaviors [25, 27]. The present study builds upon this foundation to examine how university students’ career construction styles associate with their levels of career adaptability. This research seeks to enhance the knowledge of student career development by merging the concepts of career adaptability and career construction responses, thereby informing targeted interventions in career counseling and educational planning.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to explore the multivariate associations between two sets of observed variables, namely career construction styles (first variable set) and career adaptability dimensions (second variable set) using canonical correlation analysis to identify maximally correlated linear combinations of variables from each set, focusing on how linear combinations of these dimensions relate to one another rather than testing predictive or causal hypotheses among Turkish university students. This emphasis is particularly important due to the scarcity of research linking these two constructs in non-Western, collectivist settings, where cultural and economic influences impact students’ career development experiences. Using canonical correlation analysis, the study adds to the literature both methodologically and conceptually by exploring the overall links between self-construction processes and adaptation resources in a multivariate way instead of solely looking at single variable associations. In practical terms, the findings can enhance university career counseling programs by highlighting essential career construction behaviors that facilitate adaptability and readiness for transitions from school to employment.

Method

The present study employed a quantitative, correlational research design and canonical correlation analysis (CCA) is preferred over other multivariate methods because it allows to identify linear composites (canonical variates) within each variable set that are maximally correlated with one another [28]. The first variable set included three dimensions of career construction (Crystallizing, Exploring, and Deciding), while the second variable set comprised four dimensions of career adaptability (Concern, Control, Curiosity, and Confidence). Data were collected using validated psychometric instruments, and total scores for each subscale were computed prior to analysis. The study design ensured that all statistical assumptions relevant to CCA were evaluated and met, supporting the validity and interpretability of the results.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the TED University Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval Decision No. 02.08.2022–18630). Data collection commenced upon the completion of ethical approvals and at the onset of the study, participants were provided with comprehensive information regarding the nature of the study, privacy policy, and conditions of participation using a written informed consent to participate which was obtained from all of the participants.

Participants

This study employed the convenience sampling method for ease of access. Although this sampling strategy enabled access to a large and diverse sample of students across different disciplines, it may limit the generalizability of the findings. The study sample N was composed primarily of students from TED University (36.7%), Hacettepe University (10.0%), and Ankara University (5.0%), with additional representation from a variety of other Turkish universities. Participants were enrolled across diverse academic programs, with the largest proportion majoring in Psychological Counseling and Guidance (28.1%), followed by Physics (10.9%), Philosophy (8.1%), Psychology (7.7%), and Economics (7.4%). Smaller percentages of students were drawn from disciplines such as Education, Business, Engineering, and Performing Arts. This distribution reflects a relatively broad cross-section of academic fields, enhancing the generalizability of the findings within a university student population. The mean age of the participants was 20.96 years (SD = 1.87; range = 18–26), comprising 637 females (82%) and 143 males (18%).

Instruments

This study employed a “Personal Information Form” developed by the researcher, including inquiries regarding age, university, and department, alongside the “Student Career Construction Inventory” and “Career Adaptability Scale,” which are detailed below, as data collection instruments.

Student career construction inventory (SCCI)

This inventory, created by Savickas et al. [11], seeks to assess adaptive responses and coping mechanisms associated with occupational thoughts and behaviors pertinent to career decision-making. The inventory, designed for high school, university, and graduate students, comprises 18 items assessed across four dimensions: (a) Crystallizing a vocational self-concept, (b) exploring occupational information, (c) Committing to an occupational decision, and (d) Preparing to execute that decision. The SCCI employs a five-point Likert-type scale, where the minimum score achievable on the SCCI is 18, while the maximum is 90. A higher score on the scale indicates that the individual demonstrated the required professional behaviors necessary for career advancement. The factor analysis conducted for construct validity during scale development confirmed a 4-factor structure with 18 items (crystallizing, exploring, deciding, preparing).

Various authors have conducted adaptation studies of SCCI into Turkish [25, 27, 29]. This study will utilize the measurement tool used in the adaption study by Sevinç and Siyez [25] with university students, as it shares similar characteristics with the sample of this research. The adaption research of SCCI involved a group of 309 students (161 female, 158 male) enrolled at Dokuz Eylul University. Confirmatory factor analysis was performed as part of the validity research of the SCCI. The confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the fit indices for the three-factor structure were better. The Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient for the scale was 0.87, with α = 0.65 for the “Crystallizing” sub-dimension, α = 0.72 for the “Exploring” sub-dimension, and α = 0.87 for both the “Preparing” and “Decision-making” sub-dimensions. The Student Career Construction Inventory demonstrates adequate validity and reliability in assessing university students’ attitudes, competences, and beliefs in choosing a career. However, in the present study, internal consistency for the Deciding subscale was α = 0.58 (see Table 1), which is lower than values reported in the original and adaptation studies. This sample-specific reliability coefficient may have attenuated associations involving this subscale.

Career adaptability scale (CAAS)

The Career Adaptability Scale, developed by Savickas and Porfeli [9], assesses individuals’ adaptability to professional roles, their acquisition of essential skills, and their preparedness for future career endeavors. It formed following a survey encompassing 13 countries. In the pilot study, exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the scale comprising 55 items, resulting in a reduction to 24 items. The results from the EFA yielded a 4-factor structure: Concern (planfulness), Control (deliberateness), Curiosity (inquisitiveness), and Confidence (assuredness). Subsequently, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and Mean and Covariance Structural Analysis were employed to assess the validity of the results across several countries, concluding that the findings were appropriate. The minimum score obtainable on the scale is 24, while the maximum score is 120 and a rise in the score from the scale indicates that the individual has developed the skills necessary to navigate present and future professional challenges, job transitions, and stressors, and is prepared for these kinds of situations.

In the validity study on the scale performed by Buyukgoze-Kavas [24], CAAS was adapted to Turkish. Data were gathered from 669 high school and university students in a Turkish sample to determine the factor structure and psychometric characteristics of the scale. Internal consistency estimates for subscales and overall scores varied from good to excellent. The factor structure of the Turkish version of CAAS matched the hierarchical four-component model of the original version. The internal consistency reliability of the CAAS was reported as 0.83 for concern, 0.74 for control, 0.79 for curiosity, and 0.85 for confidence, with an overall reliability of 0.92 for all items. Given these results, it was asserted that CAAS is a valid and reliable instrument for application among Turkish high school and university students.

Data analysis

Canonical correlation analysis was selected as the primary analytic technique for this study because the research aim involved examining the multivariate association between two sets of observed variables—career construction styles and career adaptability dimensions—without imposing a predictive or causal structure. Rather than examining relationships between the variable sets directly, CCA identifies linear combinations of variables within each set (canonical variates) that are maximally correlated with one another. This approach allows for the simultaneous examination of how configurations of variables from each domain are associated at a multivariate level [28, 30].

In comparison with multiple regression or individual bivariate correlations, CCA maintains the multivariate characteristics of both variable sets, therefore providing a more comprehensive insight into the interactions among complex constructs [31]. Importantly, canonical variates represent linear composites of observed variables rather than latent constructs in the factor-analytic sense [32, 33]. Accordingly, the present analysis focuses on associations between canonical variates and does not involve interaction effects, outcome variables, or dynamic processes. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, interpretations are limited to static multivariate associations rather than temporal or developmental dynamics.

It can be observed that (see Table 1) the three career construction styles (Crystallizing, Exploring, and Deciding) are positively correlated with the four career adaptability dimensions (Concern, Control, Curiosity, and Confidence). Among the career construction variables, Crystallizing displayed moderate positive correlations with Concern and Deciding. Similarly, Deciding was moderately correlated with Control and Concern. Regarding the adaptability resources, Curiosity and Confidence were moderately associated, while Concern was moderately related to Control and Curiosity. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the measures indicated acceptable internal consistency (ranging from 0.58 to 0.65). These intercorrelations suggest that the constructs are meaningfully but not redundantly related, supporting the use of a multivariate approach to explore the relationships among the constructs.

Assumption testing for canonical correlation analysis

Before conducting the CCA, relevant assumptions were assessed to ensure the validity of the results. First, CCA assumes that the relationships among variables within and across sets are linear [30]. Inspection of the correlation matrices revealed moderate positive relationships among the first variable set r = .21 to 0.48) and the second variable set r = .28 to 0.40). None of the intercorrelations exceeded 0.90, suggesting acceptable linearity and no evidence of non-linear associations. It is also recommended that variables approximate multivariate normality for accurate canonical results [31]. Skewness and kurtosis statistics were examined: first variable set exhibited skewness between − 0.87 and − 0.79, and kurtosis between 0.08 and 0.28; the second variable set exhibited skewness between − 0.63 and − 0.39, and kurtosis between − 0.41 and 0.31. According to Kim [34], skewness values between ± 2 and kurtosis values between ± 7 are acceptable for most multivariate techniques, indicating that the assumption of multivariate normality was reasonably met. Another assumption, homoscedasticity requires that the variance of residuals be constant across levels of predicted values [30]. Finally in terms of absence of multicollinearity and singularity, it is reported that high multicollinearity (correlations > 0.90) among variables can distort canonical results [31]. Examination of the first and the second variable set correlations confirmed that none approached problematic levels, and no singularities (perfect correlations) were present. Thus, the assumption of absence of multicollinearity and singularity was met.

As for the adequate sample size, CCA requires a sufficiently large sample size to ensure stable estimates. Recommendations suggest a minimum of 10 observations per variable [28, 31]. The present analysis included 780 participants, exceeding the minimum requirement for 7 variables, and thus provided adequate statistical power. In summary, evaluation of assumptions supported the appropriateness of conducting canonical correlation analysis on the present data. Minor deviations from perfect normality were observed but deemed acceptable given the large sample size and robustness of canonical methods under mild violations [30].

Canonical correlation analysis results

A canonical correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between the Career Construction subscales (Crystallizing, Exploring, Deciding) and the Career Adaptability subscales (Concern, Control, Curiosity, Confidence). Interpretation of the canonical functions primarily relied on structure coefficients, as they indicate the strength of association between each observed variable and the canonical variate, whereas standardized canonical coefficients were used to describe the relative contribution of variables to the formation of the variates. Three canonical functions were extracted. The first canonical correlation was statistically significant, r = .427, Wilks’ λ = 0.789, χ² (12) = 183.59 p < .001, suggesting a moderate multivariate relationship between the two variable sets. Subsequent canonical functions were not statistically significant and explained minimal proportion of variance associated with the canonical variate.

Standardized canonical coefficients for the Career Construction subscales indicated that Crystallizing (β = 0.865) and Deciding (β = 0.475) made the strongest contributions to the first canonical function (Table 2). Exploring associated less strongly (β = 0.162) to the variate. These findings suggest that within the canonical variate representing Career Construction Styles, Crystallizing and Deciding showed the highest structure coefficients. Regarding the canonical variates, standardized canonical coefficients showed that Concern (β = 0.960) showed the strongest association with the first canonical variate, with smaller contributions from Control (β = 0.263), Curiosity (β = 0.064), and Confidence (β = 0.070). Thus, Concern appeared to be the most strongly associated Career Adaptability dimension with the participants’ Career Construction Styles.

Discussion

The present study investigated the multivariate relationship between university students’ career construction styles (Crystallizing, Exploring, Deciding) and their career adaptability resources (Concern, Control, Curiosity, Confidence) using canonical correlation analysis. Consistent with the observed bivariate correlations, dimensions that were significantly related at the bivariate level (e.g., Crystallizing and Deciding with Concern and Control) also emerged as key contributors in the canonical correlation analysis. Thus, the primary contribution of the CCA lies in elucidating the multivariate configuration of these relationships rather than identifying entirely new associations.

Consistent with Career Construction Theory [3, 8], the results demonstrated that the canonical variate representing career construction styles—characterized primarily by crystallizing and deciding—was positively associated with the canonical variate representing career adaptability resources. In particular, Crystallizing and Deciding showed the highest structure coefficients, a finding that aligns with recent empirical evidence suggesting that proactive self-construction is strongly associated with career adaptability resources within the multivariate configuration identified [35, 36]. As a meta-competency alongside identity, one’s clarity about self [37–39], career adaptability involves the capacity and skills to make changes in self and situations needed for managing tasks, transitions, and traumas associated with career exploration, career choice, and work adjustment [19]. Crystallizing one’s vocational self-concept was associated with clearer goal orientation within the multivariate configuration identified [40], while effective decision-making was associated with commitment and engagement with career development tasks. The alignment of the present findings with previous research may be partially attributed to the social dynamics inherent in Turkish higher education. In a collectivist cultural context where familial expectations and social approval significantly influence career choices, students’ tendency to crystallize and make judgments could suggest adaptive strategies for balancing internal goals with external demands. Additionally, the Turkish labor market can be quite competitive, and centralized university placement procedures are typical. This suggests that people want to commit early and set goals, which shows how important Concern and Deciding are for helping people be adaptable. These contextual factors may thus strengthen the value of structured decision-making and goal-setting as strategies for adaptation during the transition from education to work.

Within the adaptability resources, Concern demonstrated the strongest loading onto the first canonical variate. This pattern is consistent with longitudinal findings by Rudolph, Lavigne, and Zacher [41], who found that career concern was the most developmentally sensitive dimension of adaptability during the university-to-work transition phase. There are some contradictory findings in the literature. In one study conducted by Ebenehi et al. [42] on the predictors of career adaptability skills, it was concluded that the most strongly associated dimensions of students’ career adaptability skills were career self-efficacy sources and students’ career adaptability skills. However, they also found that the concept of vocational personality did not statistically predict career adaptability skills. Yet, recent studies have further suggested that concern was strongly represented within the canonical variate, suggesting its central role within the multivariate configuration of adaptability resources [43, 44].

Notably, although the canonical correlation was moderate and statistically significant, the redundancy indices indicated that the amount of proportion of variance in adaptability resources associated with construction styles was modest. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as indicating that career construction behaviors contribute meaningfully—but modestly—to adaptability. It also suggests that adaptability develops from a complex interaction of various factors, including personality, motivation, and contextual opportunities, extending beyond the construction styles explored in this study. This finding echoes results from other multivariate studies [45, 46], suggesting that career adaptability is influenced by a complex interplay of psychological, social, and contextual factors. Consequently, even though there is a statistically significant relationship between career construction styles and adaptability, its practical effect is modest, suggesting that adaptability development is affected by numerous factors. Psychological strengths such as self-efficacy [47, 48], career optimism [49], and resilience [50] have all been identified as complementary predictors of adaptability beyond career construction behaviors. In addition, this result is consistent with prior multivariate research in career development, where statistically significant canonical functions frequently produced low to moderate redundancy levels [28, 33, 51]. Such modest effect sizes are common in psychological research and they demonstrate that career adaptability is affected by many factors, including individual career construction styles and broader psychosocial and contextual factors like social support, cultural expectations, and economic conditions [10, 20, 36].

The finding that only the first canonical function was statistically significant also indicates that the relationship between construction and adaptability was concentrated along a single major dimension. This suggests a relatively focused, but not all-encompassing, relationship — a conclusion that is in line with the idea of adaptability as a dynamic psychosocial resource responsive to but not wholly determined by individual career strategies [9, 52].

Another noteworthy aspect of these results is the important role of Exploring particularly during the early stages of career development — while its standardized coefficients were lower than those of Crystallizing and Deciding, it still demonstrated meaningful structure coefficients. Exploration behaviors were associated with adaptability dimensions and may reflect active engagement with career-related information. From a practical point of view, counselors can help students explore several career pathways by giving them structured career exploration activities, job-shadowing programs, and mentoring opportunities. These experiences might make adaptation resources like curiosity and confidence more potent, which are important for making successful career transitions. This finding aligns with research highlighting the importance of exploratory behaviors, particularly in early career stages, co-occur with curiosity and adaptive career planning [53, 54]. Engaging in career exploration may thus serve as a mechanism for sustaining adaptability over time, even if it plays a less immediate role than crystallization or decision-making at the point of measurement.

Although several dimensions demonstrated statistically significant bivariate correlations (particularly Crystallizing and Deciding with Concern and Control), the canonical analysis clarified how these dimensions operate jointly within a multivariate configuration. These findings demonstrate the value of CCA in integrating existing bivariate associations into a coherent multivariate pattern, thereby illustrating how specific dimensions jointly contribute to the overall relationship between the two variable sets. Therefore findings suggest that adaptability may be related not to any single style in isolation, but rather to the synergistic combination of crystallizing, exploring, and deciding.

Taken together, the findings of the present study reinforce the value of supporting identity development, proactive decision-making, and future-oriented planning as key strategies for enhancing career adaptability. Research demonstrated that career adaptability resources mediated the relationship between adaptivity (proactive personality, cognitive flexibility) and adaptive responses (proactive career behavior, career construction behavior), asserting that an understanding of the career construction model of adaptation will facilitate the school-to-work transition process [1]. There is an obvious need for broader models that incorporate both individual psychological processes and environmental factors, such as institutional support and labor market conditions, to fully explain the development of adaptability among university students [52, 55]. As Koen et al. [56] and Savickas [2, 3] suggested, to be successful in the school-to-work transition, university graduates need to understand their vocational selves, while proactively developing skills and competencies that will help them find a suitable person-work fit.

Conclusion

Overall, the study underscores the role of career construction styles in supporting adaptability among university students; however, this relationship should be considered within its modest explanatory scope. The results demonstrate a statistically significant correlation; nevertheless, the relatively small proportion of variance suggests that these factors constitute merely one facet of the entire career development process. Subsequent research is needed to take into consideration further personal and contextual factors to comprehensively address the multifaceted aspects of career adaptability.

Practical implications and future directions

The findings of this study have several implications for career counseling, education, and student development programs. Given the modest redundancy indices identified, the practical implications outlined here should be interpreted cautiously and considered complementary to other evidence-based interventions. For practitioners, the findings suggest the importance of utilizing interventions that strengthen students’ career crystallization and decision-making capacities. Career interventions may consider incorporating activities related to crystallization and decision-making as one component of broader career development programs. Structured activities that promote self-exploration, goal setting, and decision-planning (e.g., career narrative interventions, life-design counseling) may be particularly effective [39, 57]. Additionally, educators and counselors may take into account that fostering a sense of future orientation (Concern) is an important component in preparing students to cope with career uncertainty. Programs designed to build students’ proactive engagement with career planning, such as internships, career workshops, and mentorship programs, may reinforce both career construction competencies and adaptability outcomes. University counseling services may implement adaptability modules into career courses and mentorship activities to assist students in managing transitions and uncertainty. The current findings underscore the necessity for researchers to explore more variables of adaptability, including personality traits, self-efficacy, and social support, employing longitudinal or mixed methods designs to reflect the dynamic and contextual characteristics of career development processes. Finally, given that adaptability is likely shaped by multiple influences, interventions might be supported with a holistic developmental perspective, addressing not only self-construction processes but also psychosocial supports, emotional coping skills, and environmental resources [58, 59].

Limitations

Despite its contributions, the current study has several limitations that should be noted.

First, the reliance on self-report measures may introduce response biases, such as social desirability effects, which could artificially inflate observed relationships [60]. Future research could incorporate multi-method approaches, such as peer evaluations or behavioral assessments, to reduce method variance.

Another major drawback is related to the cross-sectional nature of the present study. The canonical correlation showed that there were statistically significant associations between career construction styles and adaptability resources. However, the design fails to provide any inferences about directionality. Consequently, the identified associations are supposed to be considered correlational rather than causative. It is also possible that higher levels of career adaptability may promote improved career construction behaviors over time, indicating a bidirectional or reciprocal relationship between these dimensions. Although the theoretical framework suggests that career construction skills precede adaptability, longitudinal or experimental designs are necessary to test directional hypotheses more rigorously [8].

The use of convenience sampling and recruitment from only three universities represents another limitation. Institutional imbalances—such as differences in academic programs, counseling resources, or socioeconomic contexts—may have influenced students’ career development and adaptation patterns. The sample was drawn from a university student population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or individuals in different life stages or cultural contexts. Given that adaptability develops across the lifespan and is shaped by contextual factors [17], replication with diverse samples would strengthen the external validity of these results. Therefore, caution should be taken when generalizing these findings to other student populations. Further research may utilize stratified or random sample methods and involve colleges with distinct institutional features to improve representativeness.

Additionally, a primary shortcoming of this work is related to the modest redundancy indices identified in the canonical model. The first canonical correlation was statistically significant, although the variable sets —Crystallizing, Exploring, and Deciding—only associated with a modest proportion of the variance associated with Career Adaptability. This suggests that these dimensions are only one aspect of a larger set of factors that affect adaptation. Other undiscovered elements, including personality traits (e.g., conscientiousness, proactivity), perceived social support, socio-economic conditions, and institutional or labor market dynamics, are likely to significantly influence students’ adaptability. Subsequent research that integrates these characteristics and utilizes longitudinal or mixed-method approaches may yield a more thorough comprehension of the complex dimensions of career adaptability.

It is also important to note that the modest redundancy values observed may be due in part to methodological issues like the cross-sectional design and the use of self-report instruments. These factors may diminish associations or obscure the dynamic nature of the constructs, underscoring that the identified canonical relationship constitutes merely one element among numerous influences that shape career adaptability. Finally, it should be noted that the relatively low reliability of the Deciding subscale in this sample (α = 0.58) may have reduced its observed associations within the canonical model.
