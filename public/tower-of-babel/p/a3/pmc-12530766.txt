Clinical features of atlas occipital assimilation: A retrospective study
Authors: Hisaaki Uchikado, Takehiro Makizono, Jin Kikuchi, Yosuke Hashimoto, Takayasu Ando, Gohsuke Hattori, Motohiro Morioka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Atlas occipital assimilation (AOA) is a rare developmental anomaly of the craniocervical junction, with a poorly understood natural history. Therefore, in 15 cases, we reassess the clinical profile of three types of AOA.Methods: For 15 patients, we applied the 3 classifications: type 1 (4 patients; unilateral joint union (hemi-assimilation), type 2 (7 patients; bilateral joint union with atlas cleft (three-way), and type 3 (4 patients; bilateral joint union and complete union of the posterior arch (circumferential). Variables studied included age, sex, neurological status, pathological magnetic resonance findings, and clivo-axial angles (CAAs).Results: For these 15 patients, we discovered no significant gender differences. However, type 1 AOA affected younger people, type 2 involved more middle-aged/elderly people, and type 3 had most elderly population. Interestingly, all 7 type 2 patients were asymptomatic, and symptoms for the remaining patients included cough, headache, upper limb numbness (i.e., due to Chiari type 1 malformation [CM1] in type 1 patients), and myelopathy (i.e., due to spinal canal stenosis at C1-2 in type 3 patients). In addition, 75% of type 3 AOA patients had C2-3 fusions. The average CAA was 127.5° for type 1 and 139° for types 2 and 3. Decompressive surgery was performed for type 1 and decompression with fusions for type 3 patients.Conclusion: Half of the AOA cases are asymptomatic. However, symptomatic cases require attention to CM1 in young people and atlanto-axial dislocation in elderly people.

INTRODUCTION

Atlas occipital assimilation (AOA) is a rare cranio-cervical junction (CCJ) anomaly that affects 0.67–2% of the population.[10] AOA leads to partial and complete fusion of vertebrae due to the abnormal development of the occiput-atlas complex. [3] It is important to understand developmental anomalies of the atlas, the most common of which is the posterior arch/cleft.[5] Further, with AOA, the occipital muscles on the fused side are often poorly developed.[1] Clinically, AOA can cause complications such as Chiari type 1 malformation (CM1) and C1-2 instability of cervical vertebrae.[9] The purpose of this study was to clarify the clinical and radiographic pathology accompanying AOA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study included 1,652 patients seen from 2012 to 2024 who underwent cervical spine three-dimensional computed tomography (3D_CT) scanning. CT studies were utilized to classify patients into one of the 3 types of AOA: type 1, unilateral joint union (hemi-assimilation); type 2, bilateral joint union with atlas cleft (three-way); and type 3, bilateral joint union and complete union of the posterior arch (circumferential) [Figure 1]. Multiple clinical and magnetic resonance parameters were evaluated. 3DCT was used to confirm AOA in detail. MRI was used to evaluate syringomyelia. Cerebellar ptosis, retro-odontoid pseudotumor (ROPT), intramedullary hyperintensity, basilar invagination (BI), and measurement of the clivo-axial angle (CAA) were also investigated.

RESULTS

Out of 1,652 patients undergoing 3D_CT examinations, we identified 15 cases (0.9%) of AOA. In each of the three groups, patients averaged 22.5 years (13–48) for type 1, 43.9 years (10–69) for type 2, and 71.5 years (60–79) for type 3, respectively [Figure 2]. Four cases (100%) of type 1 and four cases (100%) of type 3 were symptomatic, while all type 2 cases were asymptomatic. Tonsillar ptosis (measuring ≥ 5 mm from the foramen magnum) was seen in four type 1 cases (100%) and two type 2 cases (28.5%) but not in any type 3 cases. The degree of cerebellar tonsillar ptosis was 14.3 mm on average (7–20) in type 1 and 6.5 mm (6–7) in type 2. Three of four (75%) type 1 CMI cases were accompanied by syringomyelia. Myelopathy was seen in four (100%) type 3 patients, two of whom had ROPT; and the remaining two cases showed myelomalacia, and surgical treatment was abandoned. The mean CAA in type 1 was 127.5° (range 115–135); in type 2, it was 139° (range 115– 148); and in type 3, it was 139° (range 135–142) [Figure 3]. C1 posterior arch clefts were seen in four (100%) type 1 cases and six (86%) type 2 cases. C2-3 fusion was seen in one case (14%) of type 2 and three (75%) of type 3 patients. Surgical decompression was performed in four cases (100%) of type 1, and decompression fusion in 2 type 3 (50%) in cases [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

AOA and CCJ anomalies

The prevalence of AOA was previously reported to be 0.33%,[8] a number similar to the 0.9% prevalence seen in our 3D_CT study. Koc et al., found that all cases of AOA had CCJ abnormalities, including suboccipital muscle (100%), vertebral arteries (95%), C2-3 fusion (33%), BI (23%), and CM1 (5%).[8] Ferreira and Botelho reported that in 78 CCJ anomalies (51 CM I, 10 BI type 1, and 17 BI type 2), the incidence of AOA was 18%, 100%, and 75%, respectively.[2] Furthermore, they reported that BI is classified into type 1 with instability and type 2 without instability and that vertical dislocation of the odontoid process is seen in type 1.[2] Ho et al., classified AOA into anterior arch fusion, posterior arch fusion, and both arches and concluded that C2-3 fusion is involved in anterior-posterior instability.[6] Anatomical studies have shown that cases with AOA present with stenosis of the foramen magnum outlet.[12]

Symptomatic AOA

According to a review by Joaquim et al., AOA is a complete or incomplete pro-atlas segmentation anomaly, with many cases being asymptomatic; they reported insufficient decompression in cases with CM1 and CAA of 130–135°, with late instability in patients with accompanying C2-3 fusion.[7] Shah et al., reported that for 393 cases of central or axial atlanto-axial dislocation (AAD) who underwent fixation surgery, 119 cases (30%) had accompanying AOA.[11] Henderson et al., reported that corrective reduction and fixation were useful in patients with ventral brainstem compression with CAA’s of ≤135°.[4] Yin et al., reported that in cases of AOA and C2-3 fusion, AAD occurs, with required posterior fixation.[14] Yahanda et al., reported that for 57 CM1 patients with syringomyelia who underwent decompression alone in 38 patients or occipito-cervical fusion (OCF) in 19 patients, 53% in the OCF group developed AOA.[13] Therefore, careful follow-up is required for type 1 cases.

CONCLUSION

Half of the AOA cases are asymptomatic. However, symptomatic cases require attention to CM1 in young people and AAD in elderly people.
