Association of 90-day post-transplant low magnesium exposure with new-onset post-transplant diabetes mellitus in older kidney transplant recipients
Authors: Rongxin Chen, Zebin Guo, Guanghui Li, Jiali Fang, Jialin Wu, Luhao Liu, Zheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost‑transplantation diabetes mellitus (PTDM) is common after kidney transplantation, and older recipients are particularly vulnerable. Hypomagnesemia occurs frequently early after transplantation, but whether persistently low serum magnesium in the first 90 days is associated with subsequent PTDM is unclear.MethodsWe conducted a single‑center ambispective cohort study of kidney‑only recipients aged ≥ 65 years transplanted between January 2014 and June 2024, using a prespecified day‑90 landmark. Serum magnesium measured during post‑transplant days 0–90 was summarized as a time‑weighted average (TWA‑Mg90) using trapezoidal integration. Low magnesium exposure was defined as TWA‑Mg90 below the laboratory lower limit of normal (LLN; 0.72 mmol/L). PTDM was adjudicated using standard venous plasma glucose criteria. Diabetic‑range glycemia in days 0–45 was classified as early post‑transplant hyperglycemia rather than PTDM. Adjusted cause‑specific Cox models including baseline metabolic risk, immunosuppression, day-90 kidney function, and transplant year estimated adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of incident PTDM after day 90. Modified Poisson regression estimated adjusted risk ratios (aRR) for PTDM occurring between day 90 and day 365.ResultsOf 742 screened recipients, 652 were eligible at day 90. After excluding 61 PTDM cases on days 46–90, 591 entered the landmark cohort. Magnesium was measured a median of 9 times during days 0–90, and 306/591 (51.8%) had low magnesium exposure. After day 90, 160/591 (27.1%) developed PTDM (138 by day 365). Low magnesium exposure was associated with higher PTDM hazard (aHR 1.52, 95% CI 1.14–2.03; p = 0.004) and higher day 90–365 risk (aRR 1.23, 95% CI 1.05–1.44). Each 0.1 mmol/L lower TWA‑Mg90 was associated with increased PTDM hazard (aHR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05–1.35), with no strong evidence of nonlinearity (p = 0.21).ConclusionsIn older kidney transplant recipients, a higher persistent low‑magnesium during days 0–90 was associated with later PTDM. Because magnesium is routinely monitored after transplantation, sustained early hypomagnesemia may serve as a pragmatic risk marker to support targeted, guideline-consistent glycemic assessment within routine follow-up.Keywords: Kidney transplantation, Post-transplant diabetes mellitus, Magnesium, Hypomagnesemia, Older adults, Landmark cohort

Introduction

Post‑transplantation diabetes mellitus (PTDM) is a major metabolic complication of kidney transplantation and is associated with adverse cardiovascular, infectious, and graft outcomes [1–3]. Reported incidence varies substantially across cohorts and diagnostic strategies, with contemporary estimates commonly in the range of approximately 15%–30% in the first post‑transplant year, and higher rates observed in higher‑risk populations and when systematic oral glucose tolerance testing (OGTT) is used [1, 2].

Older adults (≥ 65 years) represent a growing proportion of kidney transplant candidates and recipients [4, 5]. Despite higher baseline cardiometabolic risk, kidney transplantation can provide survival and quality‑of‑life advantages over continued dialysis for carefully selected older candidates, although risks and benefits vary by donor quality and recipient comorbidity/frailty [6–9]. PTDM is of particular concern in older recipients because aging‑related insulin resistance, reduced β‑cell reserve, frailty, polypharmacy, and intercurrent illness may amplify diabetogenic stress during the early post‑transplant period [3–5, 10, 11]. Immunosuppressive regimens further contribute to PTDM since tacrolimus can impair β‑cell function and glucocorticoids increase insulin resistance [1, 12].

Hypomagnesemia is common early after kidney transplantation, largely due to renal magnesium wasting associated with calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), especially tacrolimus, and is influenced by renal function and tacrolimus exposure [13–16]. Magnesium plays a role in insulin signaling, insulin‑mediated glucose uptake, and metabolic homeostasis, and magnesium deficiency has been described as part of a “vicious circle” with insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes [17–19]. Several kidney transplant studies have reported associations between lower serum magnesium and subsequent PTDM, and meta‑analytic evidence supports an overall positive association [20–24]. However, an evidence gap remains regarding cumulative early hypomagnesemia exposure during the highest‑risk early post‑transplant window. Prior analyses relied on single magnesium measurements or simple averages, which may misclassify exposure in the context of fluctuating magnesium levels due to evolving graft function, tacrolimus titration, gastrointestinal losses, and intercurrent illness [13, 20–24].

We therefore aimed to determine whether low average serum magnesium during post‑transplant days 0–90 is associated with incident PTDM in older kidney transplant recipients. We focused on recipients aged ≥ 65 years a priori because they represent an increasingly common, clinically high‑risk subgroup and restriction of the age range reduces heterogeneity in baseline metabolic vulnerability relevant to PTDM [3–5, 10, 11]. We quantified early magnesium burden using a time‑weighted average (TWA‑Mg90) and complementary deficit‑based metrics capturing both duration and severity (percent time below LLN and area below LLN). We hypothesized that lower TWA‑Mg90 and greater deficit burden would be associated with higher PTDM risk.

Methods

Study design and reporting

We conducted a single‑center ambispective cohort study evaluating whether low serum magnesium exposure during the first 90 days after kidney transplantation is associated with subsequent incident PTDM in older recipients.

The study was performed at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University and included kidney‑only transplants performed between January 2014 and June 2024. This was an ambispective observational cohort: baseline and early post-transplant clinical/laboratory data were obtained from the institutional transplant registry and electronic medical record, and follow-up outcome adjudication used the same prespecified PTDM criteria throughout the study period. In a subset of recipients followed under more structured post-transplant metabolic surveillance, OGTT at 10–13 weeks was targeted when feasible; Outside this subset, OGTT was performed when clinically indicated.

The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. Informed consent was waived for the retrospective component and obtained from participants in the prospective component. Data were de‑identified before analysis and stored on secure institutional servers with access restricted to study personnel. Total serum magnesium (tMg) was measured by the hospital’s routine automated clinical chemistry platform (ionized magnesium was not routinely available). The laboratory lower limit of normal (LLN) used for the primary exposure definition was 0.72 mmol/L; we additionally performed a robustness analysis using LLN‑normalized magnesium (Mg − LLN) to address potential reference‑interval variation over time.

Participants and day-90 landmark cohort

Eligible participants were kidney‑only transplant recipients aged ≥ 65 years at transplantation with sufficient serum magnesium measurements during post‑transplant days 0–90 to derive prespecified exposure metrics. We excluded recipients with (1) pre‑transplant diabetes, defined by documented diabetes diagnosis prior to transplant, (2) use of glucose‑lowering therapy prior to transplant, (3) pre-transplant laboratory values meeting diabetes thresholds (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2‑hour OGTT glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [48 mmol/mol]) when available, (4) multi‑organ transplantation, or (5) insufficient magnesium data to compute 0–90‑day exposure measures. When pre‑transplant laboratory data were unavailable, pre‑transplant diabetes status was determined from documentation and medication history.

For the primary landmark cohort, we further excluded recipients who did not survive with a functioning graft to day 90 and those who met PTDM diagnostic criteria before the landmark. Diabetic‑range hyperglycemia during post‑transplant days 0–45 was classified as early post‑transplant hyperglycemia rather than PTDM, and PTDM diagnosed between days 46 and 90 was treated as pre‑landmark PTDM and excluded from the landmark cohort to ensure exposure preceded outcome.

Early post-transplant serum magnesium summary

The primary exposure was serum magnesium during post‑transplant days 0–90 summarized as a time‑weighted average (TWA‑Mg90), intended to approximate the average measured serum magnesium concentration over this clinical window while accounting for unequal spacing between routine laboratory measurements. TWA‑Mg90 was calculated using trapezoidal integration (piecewise‑linear interpolation between adjacent measurements) divided by 90 days. Because magnesium is not always measured exactly on day 0 and day 90, the nearest available measurements were used to anchor the window (the first observed value applied from day 0 to the first measurement and the last observed value applied from the last measurement to day 90). Given the frequent monitoring in this cohort, this anchoring generally covered short boundary intervals. Nonetheless, TWA‑Mg90 should be interpreted as a pragmatic summary of observed serum magnesium values rather than a mechanistic model of magnesium physiology or total‑body magnesium stores.

Low magnesium exposure was defined a priori as TWA‑Mg90 < LLN (0.72 mmol/L). TWA‑Mg90 was also modeled continuously (per 0.1 mmol/L lower) to provide clinically interpretable effect estimates. Secondary summaries describing persistence and severity of hypomagnesemia (% time below LLN and area below LLN) were treated as supportive secondary analyses.

Magnesium supplementation and replacement were provided according to routine clinical care. Because supplementation practices are not standardized and may be incompletely captured in retrospective records, serum magnesium in this study represents the net result of renal handling, gastrointestinal losses, and clinical replacement, and is evaluated primarily as a risk marker. For exploratory analysis, recipients with low magnesium exposure were additionally classified according to whether oral or intravenous magnesium supplementation/replacement was documented at any time during days 0–90. Because dose, duration, and adherence were not uniformly captured, this treatment analysis was considered descriptive and hypothesis‑generating. Secondary exposure metrics captured both duration and severity of deficiency: percent time below LLN, and area below LLN (deficit burden), computed as the integral of (LLN − Mg[t]) when Mg[t] < LLN over the 0–90‑day window, reported in mmol·day/L.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome was incident PTDM occurring after the day‑90 landmark. PTDM was adjudicated using standard venous plasma glucose criteria consistent with consensus guidance: Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (≥ 126 mg/dL), or 2‑hour plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (≥ 200 mg/dL) on a 75‑g OGTT, or Random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia or clinician‑documented diagnosis prompting treatment.

Initiation of insulin or oral glucose‑lowering therapy in the immediate peri‑operative period was considered supportive clinical context but a PTDM event required meeting the prespecified venous plasma glucose criteria (with confirmation when required). For the “random glucose” criterion, “classic symptoms” were defined as clinician‑documented polyuria, polydipsia, unexplained weight loss, or hyperglycemic crisis.

In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, abnormal results required confirmatory testing on a separate day. When different diagnostic tests were discordant, the abnormal test was repeated and diagnosis was based on the repeated result.

Consistent with consensus frameworks recognizing frequent transient hyperglycemia early after transplantation, diabetic‑range hyperglycemia within post‑transplant days 0–45 was classified as early post‑transplant hyperglycemia rather than PTDM. PTDM diagnosed between days 46 and 90 meets PTDM assessment windows used in consensus frameworks, but was excluded from the landmark cohort solely to define a PTDM‑free baseline at day 90 and strengthen temporal ordering between exposure (days 0–90) and outcome ascertainment.

In the prospective component, OGTT was targeted at approximately 10–13 weeks post‑transplant when feasible to improve sensitivity for post‑challenge hyperglycemia. HbA1c was not used as a stand‑alone diagnostic test within the first 3 months post‑transplant due to potential non‑glycemic influences that can reduce interpretability. HbA1c obtained after month 3 was considered supportive but not used as the sole basis for diagnosis.

The event date was defined as the earliest date diagnostic criteria were met (and confirmed when required). For analyses of first‑year risk, PTDM events were classified as those occurring between day 90 and day 365 after transplantation.

Follow-up and censoring

For time-to-event analyses, follow-up began at the prespecified day-90 landmark and continued until the earliest of incident PTDM, death, graft loss/return to dialysis, loss to follow-up (last available clinical contact), or administrative end of study (date of database lock). In cause-specific Cox models, death and graft loss were treated as censoring events. Competing-risk analyses treating death and/or graft loss as competing events were performed as sensitivity analyses.

Covariates and baseline definitions

Baseline covariates were prespecified and captured either pre‑transplant (demographics, comorbidities, donor type, and pre‑transplant glycemic markers) or during the early post‑transplant period up to day 90 (immunosuppression characteristics and day‑90 kidney function). Age, sex, BMI, pre‑transplant fasting plasma glucose, and pre‑transplant HbA1c were included to capture baseline metabolic risk. Delayed graft function, living donor status, induction therapy category, and transplant year were included to account for early clinical course and secular practice changes. For expanded sensitivity model, additional available variables were documented HCV serostatus, mTOR inhibitor exposure within days 0–90, early post‑transplant hyperglycemia during days 0–45 (yes/no), and HLA mismatch count (0–6). Family history of type 2 diabetes and maternal history of gestational diabetes were not routinely recorded in a standardized manner and were therefore unavailable for robust adjustment.

Immunosuppression was modeled using tacrolimus‑based regimen (yes/no), steroid pulse exposure within days 0–90 (yes/no), and mean tacrolimus trough concentration during days 0–30 among tacrolimus‑treated recipients. To permit inclusion of the trough term in the full cohort model, recipients not receiving tacrolimus were assigned a trough value of 0 and the tacrolimus regimen indicator was retained. Thus, the trough coefficient is interpreted within tacrolimus users.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD when approximately normally distributed and as median [IQR] otherwise. Categorical variables were summarized as n (%). Baseline comparisons used Welch’s t‑test for approximately normal continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for skewed variables, and χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables as appropriate. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to quantify imbalance independent of sample size. Baseline p‑values were interpreted descriptively rather than used for covariate selection.

The primary association between magnesium exposure and time to incident PTDM after day 90 was estimated using cause‑specific Cox proportional hazards regression with time zero at day 90, reporting adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The binary low‑exposure definition (TWA‑Mg90 < LLN) was the single prespecified primary exposure contrast. The prespecified adjustment set for the primary Cox and modified Poisson models included age, sex, BMI, pre‑transplant fasting plasma glucose, pre‑transplant HbA1c, hypertension, coronary artery disease, living donor status, delayed graft function, induction therapy category, tacrolimus‑based regimen, mean tacrolimus trough during days 0–30, steroid pulse exposure within days 0–90, day‑90 eGFR, and transplant year. Other magnesium parameterizations (continuous TWA‑Mg90 and deficit‑style summaries) were treated as secondary/sensitivity analyses to evaluate robustness and clinical interpretability. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and visual diagnostics.

Dose–response was evaluated by modeling TWA‑Mg90 using restricted cubic splines with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of TWA‑Mg90, using 0.75 mmol/L as the reference value. Nonlinearity was assessed using a Wald test for the spline terms beyond the linear component.

For first‑year risk (PTDM between day 90 and day 365), adjusted risk ratios (aRRs) were estimated using modified Poisson regression with robust variance among recipients who remained under observation with a functioning graft through day 365. Competing risks were evaluated in sensitivity analyses using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards models treating death and/or graft loss as competing events. Statistical inference used two‑sided tests with α = 0.05 for the prespecified primary hypothesis.

Sensitivity analyses and robustness checks

We repeated the primary Cox analysis: (1) restricting PTDM ascertainment to OGTT‑adjudicated cases when available, (2) excluding events occurring shortly after the landmark (days 90–120) to reduce reverse causality, (3) additionally adjusting for the number of magnesium measurements during days 0–90, and (4) redefining exposure using LLN‑normalized magnesium, and (5) additionally adjusting for documented HCV serostatus, mTOR inhibitor exposure within days 0–90, early post‑transplant hyperglycemia during days 0–45, and HLA mismatch count. We also performed a exploratory Cox model within the low‑magnesium exposure group comparing recipients with versus without documented magnesium supplementation/replacement during days 0–90. Competing risks were assessed with Fine–Gray models.

Results

Cohort assembly and follow-up

Among 742 older kidney‑only transplant recipients screened, 90 were excluded prior to the day‑90 landmark due to pre‑transplant diabetes, multi‑organ transplantation, death or graft loss before day 90, or insufficient magnesium data for exposure construction (Fig. 1). The remaining 652 met inclusion criteria and survived with a functioning graft to day 90. After excluding 61 recipients who met PTDM criteria between days 46 and 90, a total of 591 recipients entered the day‑90 landmark cohort (Fig. 1).

Study flow diagram: Flow diagram of cohort construction for the day-90 landmark analysis. Participants with PTDM diagnosed before the landmark were excluded to ensure the 0-90-day magnesium exposure window was fully observed and preceded PTDM ascertainment after day 90, reducing reverse-causality concerns

During post‑transplant days 0–90, serum magnesium was measured a median of 9 times (IQR 7–13). The cohort median TWA‑Mg90 was 0.71 mmol/L (IQR 0.67–0.76); low magnesium exposure (TWA‑Mg90 < LLN) occurred in 306/591 recipients (51.8%) and 285/591 (48.2%) had no low magnesium exposure (Fig. 1).

Over a median follow‑up of 3.0 years (IQR 1.6–4.9) from the day‑90 landmark, 160/591 participants developed incident PTDM after day 90 (27.1%), including 101 events in the low magnesium exposure group and 59 events in the no low exposure group (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

Compared with recipients without low magnesium exposure, those with low magnesium exposure were slightly older (70.2 ± 2.9 vs. 69.3 ± 3.0 years; p < 0.001) and had a higher BMI (24.1 ± 2.5 vs. 23.6 ± 2.4 kg/m2; p = 0.010). They also had higher pre‑transplant glycemic risk markers including fasting plasma glucose (5.5 ± 0.4 vs. 5.3 ± 0.4 mmol/L; p < 0.001) and HbA1c (5.8 ± 0.3 vs. 5.6 ± 0.3%; p < 0.001), with SMDs indicating meaningful imbalance in metabolic risk between groups (Table 1).

Tacrolimus‑based immunosuppression was more frequent in the low magnesium exposure group (90.2% vs. 80.7%; p = 0.002), and mean tacrolimus trough concentration during days 0–30 was higher among tacrolimus users (9.3 ± 1.7 vs. 8.8 ± 1.7 ng/mL; p < 0.001). Induction therapy distribution differed overall (p < 0.001), with more frequent ATG use in the low exposure group (29.7% vs. 16.1%; Table 1). Kidney function at day 90 was lower among those with low magnesium exposure (eGFR 45.6 ± 14.0 vs. 52.2 ± 15.7 mL/min/1.73 m2; p < 0.001). Delayed graft function, hypertension, coronary artery disease, and steroid pulse exposure within 0–90 days were similar between groups (Table 1).

Magnesium profiles and monitoring

Magnesium monitoring intensity was similar between groups, with a median of 9 measurements in both groups (low exposure 9 [7–12] vs. no low exposure 9 [7–13]; p = 0.653, FDR q = 0.653) (Table 2). As expected, exposure metrics showed marked separation between groups: recipients classified as low magnesium exposure had lower TWA‑Mg90 (0.67 [0.65–0.69] vs. 0.77 [0.74–0.79] mmol/L; p < 0.001, q < 0.001), substantially greater time below LLN (90.9% [80.2–98.6] vs. 11.2% [3.1–23.4]; p < 0.001, q < 0.001), and higher deficit burden (area below LLN 3.9 [3.0–4.8] vs. 0.2 [0.0–0.5] mmol·day/L; p < 0.001, q < 0.001) (Table 2).

Incidence of PTDM after day 90

Incident PTDM after the day‑90 landmark occurred in 160/591 recipients (27.1%), corresponding to a crude proportion of 33.0% (101/306) in the low magnesium exposure group and 20.7% (59/285) in the no low exposure group. Kaplan–Meier estimates of the cumulative probability of PTDM after day 90 (1-KM), censoring at death or graft loss, separated early and remained separated over follow-up (Fig. 2). Association between early magnesium exposure and PTDM risk

In the prespecified multivariable cause‑specific Cox model, low magnesium exposure (TWA‑Mg90 < LLN) was associated with a higher hazard of incident PTDM after day 90 (aHR 1.52, 95% CI 1.14–2.03; p = 0.004, FDR q = 0.011; Table 3). When modeled continuously, each 0.1 mmol/L lower TWA‑Mg90 was associated with increased PTDM hazard (aHR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05–1.35; p = 0.007, q = 0.011; Table 3). Restricted cubic spline analysis showed no strong evidence of nonlinearity (p for nonlinearity = 0.21) and was consistent with an approximately monotonic increase in PTDM risk as early magnesium exposure decreased (Fig. 3).

Secondary exposure metrics were concordant with the primary findings, with higher PTDM hazard per 10% increase in time below LLN (aHR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.09; p = 0.012, q = 0.012) and per 1 mmol·day/L increase in deficit burden (aHR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05; p = 0.003, q = 0.011; Table 3). In the first‑year risk analysis (day 90–365), low magnesium exposure was associated with higher PTDM risk (aRR 1.23, 95% CI 1.05–1.44; p = 0.010, q = 0.012; Table 3).

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

The association between low magnesium exposure and incident PTDM remained consistent across prespecified sensitivity analyses (Table 4). Restricting PTDM ascertainment to OGTT‑adjudicated cases yielded a similar estimate (aHR 1.48, 95% CI 1.05–2.09; p = 0.026), and excluding events occurring shortly after the landmark (days 90–120) did not materially change the association (aHR 1.46, 95% CI 1.08–1.98; p = 0.014). Additional adjustment for the number of magnesium measurements during days 0–90 produced nearly identical results (aHR 1.50, 95% CI 1.12–2.01; p = 0.006), as did recalculation using LLN‑normalized magnesium (aHR 1.49, 95% CI 1.12–1.99; p = 0.006). Documented HCV seropositivity was present in 19/591 (3.2%), mTOR inhibitor exposure within days 0–90 in 41/591 (6.9%), and early post‑transplant hyperglycemia during days 0–45 in 148/591 (25.0%); the median HLA mismatch count was 3 [2–4]. In a expanded model additionally adjusting for these variables, the association between low magnesium exposure and incident PTDM remained materially unchanged (aHR 1.49, 95% CI 1.11–2.00; p = 0.008). Among recipients with low magnesium exposure, 184/306 (60.1%) had documented magnesium supplementation/replacement during days 0–90; crude PTDM incidence after day 90 was 31.5% (58/184) in documented‑treated recipients versus 35.2% (43/122) in those without documented treatment, and the exploratory adjusted association was not statistically significant (aHR 0.93, 95% CI 0.63–1.37; p = 0.710). In competing risk analyses treating death and/or graft loss as competing events, results were similar (Fine–Gray sHR 1.47, 95% CI 1.10–1.96; p = 0.009). There was no evidence that the association differed by retrospective versus prospective ascertainment component (p for interaction = 0.41; Table 4).

Discussion

In this single‑center ambispective cohort of older kidney‑only transplant recipients using a prespecified day‑90 landmark design, we found that low serum magnesium during post‑transplant days 0–90 was associated with subsequent incident PTDM after day 90.

Magnesium influences insulin signaling and insulin‑mediated glucose uptake, and magnesium deficiency has been described as interacting with insulin resistance in a reinforcing cycle relevant to diabetes pathophysiology [17–19]. The early post‑transplant period is also characterized by iatrogenic drivers of hypomagnesemia. Tacrolimus and other CNIs promote renal magnesium wasting, and clinical studies demonstrate that hypomagnesemia is common among tacrolimus‑treated transplant recipients and correlates with tacrolimus exposure and renal function [13–15]. Mechanistic work further supports a role for calcineurin signaling pathways in tacrolimus‑associated magnesium handling [15]. In parallel, tacrolimus and glucocorticoids are established contributors to PTDM through β‑cell toxicity and insulin resistance, respectively, raising the possibility that sustained early magnesium deficit may amplify diabetogenic vulnerability, particularly in older adults with reduced physiological reserve [1, 12]. Our results are consistent with prior kidney transplant literature linking hypomagnesemia with PTDM/NODAT, including early cohort work and later analyses incorporating more refined exposure handling [20–23]. Meta‑analytic evidence and a recent systematic review also support an overall association between hypomagnesemia and PTDM across transplant cohorts, although heterogeneity in definitions and ascertainment is substantial [23, 24]. We extend this literature by focusing on older recipients and by operationalizing early magnesium burden as a cumulative exposure during days 0–90, which may reduce misclassification compared with single measurements in a period where magnesium levels can fluctuate due to evolving graft function, tacrolimus titration, illness, and gastrointestinal losses [13, 14, 20–24].

Clinically, magnesium is already routinely monitored after kidney transplantation, making sustained early hypomagnesemia a pragmatic signal that could complement existing PTDM risk stratification approaches [13, 25]. International consensus guidance emphasizes standardized PTDM diagnosis and evaluation after the early unstable phase and highlights OGTT as a preferred diagnostic/screening tool, including around ~ 10–13 weeks when feasible [2, 26]. In our cohort, most post‑landmark PTDM events accrued within the first year after transplantation, suggesting a practical pathway: recipients with sustained magnesium deficit during days 0–90 may help prioritize which recipients undergo guideline-consistent glycemic assessment (particularly OGTT around 10–13 weeks when feasible) within existing follow-up visits, rather than implying more frequent universal phlebotomy [2, 26, 27].

Our findings should be interpreted as observational associations rather than proof that magnesium supplementation prevents PTDM. The association also remained stable in an expanded model including HCV serostatus, mTOR inhibitor exposure, early post-transplant hyperglycemia, and HLA mismatch burden, which reduces, but does not eliminate, the possibility that the magnesium signal reflects these other clinically relevant PTDM determinants [1, 2]. Randomized evidence in people with or at risk of diabetes suggests oral magnesium may modestly improve glycemic parameters, but transplant‑specific efficacy data remain limited, and safety considerations, particularly hypermagnesemia in reduced kidney function, require protocolized dosing and monitoring [28, 29]. If interventional magnesium‑optimization studies are pursued, they should be preceded by feasibility and safety work and should not be interpreted as being justified by causality from this cohort alone; any such trial would require standardized PTDM endpoints with guideline‑consistent ascertainment.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The single‑center design may limit generalizability to centers with different immunosuppression protocols, laboratory reference ranges, or PTDM screening practices. Residual confounding is possible because dietary magnesium intake, gastrointestinal losses, and other determinants of magnesium balance and glucose metabolism were not captured. We measured total serum magnesium, which is the most available clinical assay, but it is an imperfect proxy for biologically active magnesium status. Future work could incorporate ionized magnesium. In addition, family history of type 2 diabetes and maternal history of gestational diabetes were not consistently recorded, precluding adjustment for inherited predisposition. Our age‑restricted design improves clinical specificity for older recipients but limits direct extrapolation to younger transplant populations. Finally, the landmark design conditions on survival with a functioning graft to day 90 and being PTDM‑free at that point, which may introduce selection effects and should be considered when generalizing estimates to all older transplant recipients.

In conclusion, among older kidney‑only transplant recipients, low serum magnesium during the first 90 days after transplantation was associated with incident PTDM after the day‑90 landmark. Given routine post‑transplant magnesium testing and the concentration of PTDM events in the first post‑transplant year, sustained early hypomagnesemia may serve as a pragmatic risk marker to support targeted guideline-consistent glycemic assessment with routine follow-up.
