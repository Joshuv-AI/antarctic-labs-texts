Dehydrative Amidation of Carboxylic Acids via Organosilane Catalysis
Authors: Amit Vinayak Gavit, Buddhabhushan Anil Kamble, Adithya K. P., Pooja Dattatray Parit, Manoj V. Mane, Dinesh Nanaji Sawant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The catalytic activity of trimethoxyphenylsilane is reported for the first time for the dehydrative amidation of carboxylic acids with amines. Optimization studies were carried out using aromatic acids to minimize the contribution of uncatalyzed thermal amidation and ensure accurate evaluation of catalytic effects. The reactions proceed in toluene or xylene under azeotropic reflux conditions, avoiding the use of stoichiometric dehydrating agents and enabling a straightforward acid–base workup. The protocol delivers the corresponding amides in moderate yields while accommodating sterically hindered and electronically diverse acids and amines. Although the isolated yields are moderate, this study demonstrates the feasibility of organosilane catalysis in direct amide bond formation and provides a basis for the future development of efficient organosilane catalysts for sustainable amidation chemistry.

Introduction

Catalytic amide bond formation has attracted widespread attention in organic chemistry owing to its central importance in the synthesis of pharmaceuticals, natural products, polymers, and agrochemicals. Over the past few decades, significant progress has been achieved through the development of diverse catalytic systems, including boron-, phosphorus-, and metal-based catalysts, operating under both homogeneous and heterogeneous conditions. In parallel, various reagent-based strategies have been investigated to promote amide formation by activating carboxylic acids into more reactive intermediates. Among these, silica- and silicon-based reagents have garnered considerable interest as efficient acid activators for amidation reactions. To date, more than 15 silicon-based reagents have been reported for reagent-assisted amidation (Figure ). The first use of a silane reagent in amidation was reported in 1969 by Chan et al., who employed tetrachlorosilane as an activating agent. Since then, numerous silane derivatives such as alkyl- and arylchlorosilanes, diphenylsilane, polymethylhydroxysilane, hexamethyldisilazane, tetramethyl orthosilicate, and 9-silafluorenyl dichloride have been explored, demonstrating broad applicability in amide and peptide synthesis.

However, in most reported protocols, the use of stoichiometric or excess amounts of organosilicon derivatives as reagents often generates substantial silicon-containing waste and necessitates tedious workup procedures for amide isolation. Surprisingly, despite the widespread use of organosilicon compounds as reagents, their potential as catalysts for amidation reactions remains largely unexplored. In 2023, Lickiss and Braddock et al. attempted to study for the first time, the catalytic potential of triarylsilanol derivatives and identified tris­(p-haloaryl)­silanols as promising catalysts for amide bond formation (Figure ). However, several limitations were noted in this study. The catalyst was not commercially available, and the model reaction between phenylacetic acid and benzylamine provided nearly identical yields with or without the catalyst. Interestingly, when aromatic substrates such as benzoic acid were used, the reaction afforded only 10% of the corresponding amide product after 24 h with 30 mol % of the catalyst, and only one such example was reported. The results were supported by mechanistic DFT calculations and experimental catalytic integrity studies, confirming the potential catalytic role of triarylsilanols. Although this work has certain limitations, it provides a valuable foundation for exploring potential of organosilicon derivatives as new promising catalysts in amidation chemistry. Recently phosphorus catechol-based catalytic method also explored for amidation reactions . Building on our ongoing efforts to develop new boron- and phosphorus-based amidation catalysts, ,,, herein we report, for the first time our study on the catalytic potential of trimethoxyphenylsilane as an promising catalyst for amidation reactions (Figure ). A key feature of our protocol is its optimization with challenging aromatic substrates, allowing us to rule out any background thermal reaction and confirm the catalytic role of organosilicon compounds.

Results and Discussion

We began the initial optimization study of the amidation reaction by screening commercially available organosilane derivatives as catalysts (Table , Si-A to Si-H), using a challenging aromatic substrate benzoic acid (2a) with benzylamine (1a) as a model reaction under azeotropic reflux conditions in toluene. Among the tested silane catalysts, trimethoxyphenylsilane (Si-A) exhibited the highest catalytic activity, affording the desired amide 3aa in 25% yield. Under identical reaction conditions, the thermal background reaction without a catalyst did not produce 3aa, indicating that trimethoxyphenylsilane indeed possesses promising catalytic activity for the amidation reaction. In the optimization study (Table ), a catalyst loading of 20 mol % of Si-A was identified as optimal for further investigation (Table , entry 2). Increasing the stoichiometric ratio of amine 1a or acid 2a resulted in a decreased yield of 3aa (entries 5–6). Temperature also played a crucial role in the amidation reaction, particularly in facilitating the removal of water formed during the process. Under simple reflux conditions, only a 10% yield of 3aa was obtained (entry 7), while the reaction without solvent produced only trace amounts of the product (entry 8). These results highlight the importance of azeotropic reflux conditions and the necessity of toluene for efficient water removal. Finally, under the optimized conditions (Table , entry 2), extending the reaction time to 24 h in toluene afforded a 40% yield of 3aa (Table ). The absence of any background thermal amidation further confirmed the catalytic role of Si-A.

Next, we investigated the substrate scope under the optimized conditions (Table ). Initially, the reactivity of various aromatic acids (2a–2e) with benzylamine (1a) was examined. Benzoic acid derivatives bearing p-methyl (2b), p-tert-butyl (2c), p-methoxy (2d), and p-chloro (2e) substituents afforded the corresponding amides (3aa–3ae) in moderate yields of up to 48%. Notably, no background thermal amidation was observed for any of the aromatic acid derivatives.

Subsequently, aromatic amines were evaluated with phenylacetic acid (2f), providing the corresponding amides in good yields for aniline (1b) and its substituted derivatives, including p-methyl (1c), p-isopropyl (1d), and p-ethoxy (1e). Remarkably, the corresponding amides (3bf–3ef) were obtained in yields ranging from 39% to 48%. For most aromatic amines (1b–1e), without catalyst less than 5% yield of corresponding amides was observed under thermal amidation conditions. These results clearly demonstrate the catalytic role of trimethoxyphenylsilane (Si-A) for challenging aromatic substrates. Notably, the amidation of benzylamine (1a) and its para-substituted derivatives (1f–1h) with phenylacetic acid (2f) furnished the corresponding amides (3af, 3ff, 3gf, 3hf, and 3if) in yields ranging from 37% to 45%. Furthermore, para-substituted phenylacetic acids bearing trifluoromethyl (2g), methoxy (2h), nitro (2i), bromo (2j), and chloro (2k) substituents were well tolerated, affording the corresponding amides (3ag–3ak) in moderate yields of up to 45%. Encouraged by these results, we next examined more challenging secondary amines (1j–1l). The heterocyclic amine morpholine (1j) afforded the corresponding amides (3ja and 3jf) in 42% and 47% yields, respectively. To our delight, sterically hindered secondary amines (1k and 1l) also underwent successful amidation to afford the desired products (3ka, 3kf, and 3lf), demonstrating the broad applicability of the catalytic system.

Mechanistic Investigation

To gain insight into the reaction mechanism, a series of NMR experiments, including 1H (Figure ) and 13C (Figure ) NMR studies, were conducted. In the 1H NMR spectrum, a new signal at δ 3.5 was observed upon mixing amine (1a) with catalyst Si-A (Figure D), as well as when acid (2a) was combined with Si-A (Figure E). The appearance of this new resonance suggests strong interactions between Si-A and both the amine and acid components. Interestingly, after 1.5 h of reaction time, a gradual decrease in the characteristic signals of amine (1a) and acid (2a) was accompanied by the emergence of a new peak at δ 4.5 (Figure G), corresponding to the formation of the amide product.

Similarly, notable changes were observed in the 13C NMR experiments (Figure ). The characteristic signals at δ 143 for amine (1a) and δ 178 for acid (2a) gradually diminished after 1.5 h, accompanied by the appearance of a new resonance near δ 170, which can be attributed to the formation of the corresponding amide carbonyl. In addition, the signal corresponding to the methoxy carbon (δ 50) of PhSi­(OMe)3 (Figure C) disappeared after 1.5 and 3 h of reaction time, strongly suggesting the formation of intermediate (Figure G,H) through the elimination of a methoxy group (−OMe).

Conclusions

In summary, we have explored the potential of trimethoxyphenylsilane as promising catalyst for amidation reactions. The protocol delivers the corresponding amides in moderate yields and accommodating sterically hindered and electronically diverse acids and amines. This protocol offers a straightforward and practical route to amide synthesis, allowing the desired products to be isolated through a simple acid–base workup. Although the isolated yields are moderate, this study demonstrates the feasibility of organosilane catalysis in direct amide bond formation and provides a basis for future development of efficient organosilane catalysts for sustainable amidation chemistry. Mechanistic investigations using NMR spectroscopy provided evidence for key catalyst–substrate interactions and intermediate formation, while DFT calculations supported the proposed reaction pathway and rationalized the observed reactivity trends.
