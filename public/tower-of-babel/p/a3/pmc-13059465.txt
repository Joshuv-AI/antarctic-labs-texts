Efficacy and safety of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in juvenile myasthenia gravis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoling Zhong, Yu Xie, YanMei Li, Hua Yang, Rong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn the present meta-analysis, we aimed to explore the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators for the treatment of juvenile myasthenia gravis (JMG).MethodsWe conducted a systematic search for studies published between January 1st, 2000 and July 28th, 2025, in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 16.0). Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic were used to assess the heterogeneity among the included studies. If significant heterogeneity existed (I2 ≥50% or P < 0.05), the random effects model was used; otherwise, the fixed effects model was used to calculate the pooled results.ResultsA total of 3029 articles were retrieved. This meta-analysis included 9 cohort and case-control studies, 11 case series, 3 single-arm studies, and 1 randomized controlled trial, focusing on tacrolimus, glucocorticoids, monoclonal antibodies, and intravenous immunoglobulin. Regarding tacrolimus, 9 studies involving 310 patients assessed the efficacy of tacrolimus for treating JMG. The results showed a significant reduction in both the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) and Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) scores. Moreover, tacrolimus treatment allowed for a reduction in steroid dosage, with a response rate of 0.862 (95% CI: 0.716–0.967). For monoclonal antibodies, 6 studies with 67 patients analyzed the efficacy for JMG. The response rate of monoclonal antibodies was 0.993 (95% CI: 0.935-1.000). Descriptive analyses were conducted for glucocorticoids and IVIG. Besides, 5 studies with 348 patients assessed the efficacy of glucocorticoids for JMG. Included studies showed that the efficacy rate of glucocorticoid monotherapy for isolated ocular myasthenia gravis (OMG) was higher than that for patients with both OMG and generalized myasthenia gravis (GMG). Finally, regarding the use of IVIG, 4 studies reported efficacy for JMG. These investigations reported a response rate ranging from 47.06% to 94.3% for IVIG therapy.ConclusionsIn summary, this was the first comprehensive meta-analysis of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in JMG. However, most included studies were single-center retrospective observational studies. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to further investigate the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in JMG.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction, and it is divided into ocular MG (OMG) and generalized MG (GMG) [1]. Key symptoms include double vision, drooping eyelids, and muscle weakness in the respiratory, trunk, and limb muscles, often exacerbated by physical activity [1]. The prevalence of MG varies between 15 and 179 cases per million [2]. Although Juvenile MG is uncommon in Europe and North America, it is more prevalent in Asian countries such as China, where nearly 50% of MG patients are children [3, 4].

The pathogenesis of juvenile MG (JMG) remains unclear and whether it is the same as that in adults is still uncertain [5]. Although the clinical phenotype is similar to that of adults, there are still many differences between JMG and adult MG (AMG), such as clinical severity, antibody titers, and thymic histology [6]. These differences indicate that the therapeutic response and safety profile of immunosuppressants/immunomodulators in JMG may differ from those in AMG. Currently, standard treatments for AMG are diverse, with the main approaches involving managing symptoms with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, glucocorticoids, thymectomy, plasma exchange, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and immunosuppressants [7]. However, current practices for JMG are derived from treatment guidelines or personal experience with AMG [8, 9]. Certain patients are unable to tolerate the side effects of prolonged corticosteroid treatment, which may include infections, bone marrow suppression, and hormonal imbalances [7, 10]. Therefore, it is crucial and urgent to explore applicable treatment regimens for JMG.

While there is substantial research on novel therapeutic agents for AMG, including meta-analyses evaluating their efficacy and safety, much remains unclear. For instance, a previous study conducted a descriptive systematic review of IVIG in AMG, but was unable to draw definitive conclusions regarding its efficacy [11]. Yongbo Ma, et al. [12] assessed the efficacy of 10 targeted drugs for AMG and found that batoclimab demonstrated the best efficacy and safety for GMG, ranking first among the drugs studied. However, further well-designed long-term follow-up studies are necessary to fully evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of these medications. A systematic review and meta-analysis of neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) inhibitors in MG patients revealed favorable outcomes in terms of efficacy and safety, particularly for efgartigimod and nipocalimab [13]. Furthermore, Jian Gu, et al. concluded that all the treatments analyzed were more effective than placebo, with rozanolixizumab and batoclimab potentially offering the greatest benefit [14].

However, there is currently no meta-analysis on the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in JMG. The gap in clinical evidence highlights the urgency of conducting a meta-analysis to integrate existing data on immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in JMG. Therefore, this meta-analysis aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these agents in JMG, providing a comprehensive overview of treatment options for JMG and laying a foundation for the development of new pharmacotherapeutic strategies for JMG in the future.

Methods

This systematic review was prospectively registered in the INPLASY database (Registration No.: INPLASY202530109) and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15].

Literature search strategy

The literature search followed the PRISMA extension framework for systematic reviews. A comprehensive and structured search strategy was developed in advance, guided by the methodological standards of the Cochrane Collaboration. Databases searched included PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science, with no language restrictions. To ensure relevance and comparability of the included studies and in accordance with contemporary clinical practice, the search period was from January 1st, 2000 to July 28th, 2025. Additionally, references from eligible studies were reviewed to identify supplementary articles. The search terms were as follows: (“pediat” or “paediat” or “adolescent” or “juvenile” or “childhood” or “children” or “child”), AND (“myasthenia gravis” or “myasthen*”), and the search fields were limited to Title/Abstract.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Two reviewers (XL Zhong, Y Xie) independently assessed the titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (H Yang). A full-text review of each article was then conducted according to predefined inclusion criteria. Studies included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), single-arm studies, observational studies (retrospective or prospective), and case series with a sample size of ≥ 3 cases, all of which must meet the following criteria: (1) patients diagnosed with MG who were under 18 years of age; (2) studies including immunosuppressants and immunomodulators used as interventions; (3) studies that reported clearly defined outcome measures.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicate studies, conference abstracts, case reports, reviews, letters, commentaries, and expert opinions; (2) neonatal transient myasthenia and congenital myasthenia; studies that did not report outcome measurement; (3) not offering sufficient data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers screened the articles, extracted the data, and cross-checked their findings. Any missing data were sought by contacting the authors. The literature screening process involved an initial review of titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review to determine final inclusion. Data extraction was performed using Excel 2024, and the information collected included: (1) basic details of the included studies, such as first author, country, year, and study type; (2) key characteristics of the study participants, including age, gender, characteristics of the study populations, sample size, type of intervention, regimen and so on; (3) primary outcomes and outcome measurements data, such as Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) score, Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) score, and adverse events (AEs), etc.

Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias of the included studies and cross-checked the evaluations. For RCTs, the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool was employed, with each item categorized as low risk, high risk, or some concerns [16]. Cohort and case-control studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), while single-arm and case series studies were assessed by the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist (JBI). In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 16.0). For single-arm and pairwise studies, pooled effect sizes (ES) and their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. A random-effects model was given priority due to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity. For continuous outcomes, mean changes were pooled for efficacy analysis. For single-arm and case series studies, the response rate was summarized as (number of events [E]/total number [N]) × 100%. Studies with a comparator group, were herein referred to as “pairwise studies”. To provide an overall estimate of treatment response, an initial analysis pooled data from all studies evaluating a given drug. To assess the influence of study design, a prespecified subgroup analysis was then performed to separate single-arm/case series studies from those with a comparator group (cohort, case-control, or RCT). Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and I² statistic. Significant heterogeneity was defined as I² ≥ 50% or P < 0.05, for which a random-effects model was used; otherwise (I² < 50% or P ≥ 0.05), a fixed-effects model was applied. For studies with significant heterogeneity, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, meta-regression analysis, and publication bias testing were performed to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on populations, patient subtypes, study design, follow-up duration, and whether combination therapy was administered. If heterogeneity among studies was excessively substantial and quantitative synthesis was not feasible, descriptive analysis was used.

Results

Study selection

A total of 3027 articles were retrieved from the four databases using relevant subject headings and keywords, which were PubMed (850), Cochrane (98), WOS (455), and Embase (1624). Additionally, 2 studies were identified through manual search. After removing duplicates (1208), 1821 articles remained for further screening. After reading abstracts, 81 articles were selected, and finally, after full-text review, 24 eligible studies [17–40] were included. The screening process and results are shown in Fig. 1.

Data synthesis

Tacrolimus

Efficacy

Between 2017 and 2024, nine studies involving a total of 310 patients were identified that evaluated the efficacy of tacrolimus in JMG. These studies originated predominantly from China (n = 6) and Japan (n = 3).

Primary outcomes: definite response rate (DRR)

Table 1B presents the definition of “response” in each individual study. The results showed substantial heterogeneity (I²= 78.373%, p = 0.000); therefore, a random-effects model was applied. The pooled DRR reached 0.862 (95% CI: 0.716–0.967), further supporting its therapeutic benefit (Fig. 2A).

Fig. 2Forest plots for efficacy outcomes of tacrolimus: (A) definite response rate; (B) glucocorticoid dosage; (C) MG-ADL Scores

Forest plots for efficacy outcomes of tacrolimus: (A) definite response rate; (B) glucocorticoid dosage; (C) MG-ADL Scores

Secondary outcomes

MG-ADL score

Across studies, consistent improvements were observed in both MG-ADL and QMG scores. For MG-ADL scores, the pooled mean reduction was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.42–1.16) at 1 month, increasing to 4.21 (95% CI: 2.95–5.47) at 3 months. Although the reduction was 2.65 (95% CI: 2.30-3.00) at 6 months appeared more modest, a marked improvement of 7.36 (95% CI: 0.46–14.26) was again noted at 9 months. By 12 months or at the final follow-up, MG-ADL scores had decreased by 2.82 (95% CI: 2.48–3.16), with an overall pooled reduction of 2.21 (95% CI: 2.01–2.41). Statistical heterogeneity existed among the studies (I²=88.5%, p < 0.001, Fig. 2C).

QMG score

Two pairwise studies [17, 21] and one single-arm study [23] provided comparative pre- and post-treatment QMG scores, with Bi ZJ et al. reporting a reduction from 6.22 ± 2.58 to 1.68 ± 2.49; Liu CC et al. documenting a decrease from 5.00 ± 1.73 to 1.54 ± 2.11 at the 1-year follow-up, and Wang RY et al. noting a decline from a median of 9.5 (5.0, 20.0) to 2.0 (0, 7.0) at the 1-year follow-up, respectively.

Glucocorticoid dosage

Regarding 12-month treatment outcomes, tacrolimus therapy was associated with a significant reduction of 15.59 mg/day (WMD, 95% CI: 11.03–20.15) in glucocorticoid dosage. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 74.2%, p = 0.009), which may be attributable to differences in different study design, intervention regimens, follow-up durations, and cross-study consistency (Fig. 2B).

Serum acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibody titers

Four trials [18, 19, 21, 26] reported a decrease in AChR antibody titers following treatment, with three of them providing pre-treatment and post-treatment comparative data. Matsuura R et al. reported a post-treatment decrease in AChR antibody titer of 0–1 nmol/L but did not provide specific values. In addition, Bi ZJ et al. demonstrated that the AChR antibody titer declined from 4.649 ± 2.564 nmol/L to 2.283 ± 1.250 nmol/L, with one patient achieving MuSK antibody seronegativity. Additionally, Li JW et al. noted that the mean AChR antibody titer among 13 patients was 1.7 ± 1.3 nmol/L; of these, 5 underwent re-examination 6–12 months after treatment, with their titers decreasing from 4.67, 2.29, 3.18, 1.23, and 0.82 nmol/L to 3.19, 1.22, 1.77, 0.87, and 0.36 nmol/L, respectively. Furthermore, Liu CC et al. showed that the AChR antibody titer decreased from 1.95 ± 2.62 nmol/L to 0.70 ± 1.04 nmol/L.

Safety

Across 310 treated patients, 44 AEs were reported, yielding an AE incidence of 14.2%. The most frequently observed events included asymptomatic hypomagnesemia (n = 5) and renal dysfunction (n = 3). Importantly, AEs were generally mild and rarely necessitated drug discontinuation, indicating that tacrolimus is well tolerated in pediatric patients.

Glucocorticoids (GC)

Efficacy

To provide a comparative context, seven publications comprising 2 cohorts and 3 case series studies including 348 patients (2007–2017) evaluated the efficacy of glucocorticoids in JMG. These studies were conducted in China (n = 2), Japan, Thailand, and Brazil. The analysis revealed significant heterogeneity (I² = 95.297%, p = 0.000); therefore, a descriptive analysis was conducted.

Liu CT et al. compared methylprednisolone and prednisolone in the treatment of OMG, showing similar efficacy and recurrence rates at 6, 12, and 24 months of follow-up; both therapies had higher efficacy than pyridostigmine monotherapy. Additionally, Qin NN et al. evaluated prednisolone in 29 OMG patients, with efficacy ratio of 19/23, 8/11, and 2/3 at 6, 12, and 24 months of follow-up, respectively. In the study by Sri-udomkajorn S et al., prednisolone monotherapy achieved clinical remission in 0/3 patients with OMG and GMG, while the combination of prednisolone and pyridostigmine was effective in 12/79 patients with OMG or GMG. Morita MP et al. reported a response ratio of 4/9 in MG patients treated with the intervention. In addition, Nomura Y et al. documented an effective ratio of 79/136 in patients with OMG and GMG. Included studies showed that the efficacy rate of glucocorticoid monotherapy for isolated OMG was higher than that for patients with both OMG and GMG. No MG-ADL or QMG data were available for quantitative synthesis, limiting further interpretation of functional outcomes. Furthermore, no studies reported the decrease in AChR antibody levels following glucocorticoids treatment.

Safety

Among 348 patients, 42 AEs were reported (12.07%). Only two studies provided detailed AE events, in which moon face, obesity, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, and hypertension were mainly documented. These findings highlighted both the therapeutic value and the well-recognized burden of GC-associated side effects in children.

Monoclonal Antibodies (mAbs)

Efficacy

From 2017 to 2024, six studies involving 67 patients—originating from China, the United States, France, and the United Kingdom—investigated the use of monoclonal antibodies in JMG. Of the 6 included studies, 3 were multicenter and 3 were single-center investigations. In terms of study design, there were 3 case series, 2 cohort studies, and 1 single-arm study. Regarding patient subtypes, 4 studies exclusively included patients with GMG, 1 study recruited both OMG and GMG patients, while the remaining study did not specify the MG subtype. Furthermore, the therapeutic regimens varied across the different studies, as detailed in Table 1B.

The heterogeneity across studies was I² = 0%, p > 0.05; therefore, a fixed-effects model was applied for the pooled analysis. Despite the relatively small sample size, the findings were strikingly consistent. The pooled 12-month DRR was 0.993 (95% CI: 0.935-1.000), indicating that mAbs may offer a highly effective therapeutic option for selected patients (Fig. 3). A total of 27 AEs were reported (40.3%), primarily infusion-related reactions such as allergic responses and rash. Although more frequent than with tacrolimus, these reactions were generally manageable and aligned with the known safety profile of biologic agents.

IVIG

Efficacy

To further contextualize available therapeutic options, four studies with 84 patients published between 2000 and 2024 evaluated IVIG treatment for JMG. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 83.439%, p = 0.000). Due to differences in study design, we conducted a descriptive analysis of the studies. The included studies comprised 1 RCT, 1 case-control study, 1 single-arm study, and 1 case series. These studies were conducted across China, the United States, Canada, and Japan. Enrolling patients with both OMG and GMG, these investigations reported a response rate from 47.06% to 94.3% for IVIG therapy. Notably, three of these studies [38–40] consistently specified a total IVIG dose of 2 g/kg, administered over 2 to 5 days, which was indicated for the acute phase of MG or as salvage therapy for patients who had failed treatment with cholinesterase inhibitors, other immunosuppressants, or plasma exchange.

MG-ADL score

Only one study [40] reported a modest reduction in MG-ADL scores following treatment, with the median score decreasing from 2.0 (interquartile range, IQR: 1–18) to 1.0 (IQR: 0.8–2.3).

Serum AChR antibody titers

Only one study [39] reported a decrease in AChR levels following IVIG treatment, without specifying the exact magnitude of the decrease.

Safety

AEs occurred in 21 patients (25%), most commonly mild fever and headache. These events were transient and consistent with the expected tolerability of IVIG. Because of the small number of included studies and the predominance of single-arm designs, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias testing could not be conducted.

Heterogeneity

Subgroup analysis

Tacrolimus: study design

Of the included studies, 3 employed a pairwise design and 3 were case series. Statistical heterogeneity was primarily observed among the case series (I² = 67.030%, p = 0.028). The pooled response rate was 0.785 (95% CI: 0.523, 0.971) for case series and 0.911 (95% CI: 0.698, 1.000) for cohort and case-control studies. Generally, these pairwise studies applied more stringent inclusion criteria and statistical analysis, resulting in lower heterogeneity (Fig. 4A).

Tacrolimus: population

Three studies were conducted in Japan and four in China. Heterogeneity was predominantly derived from the Chinese studies (I² = 82.522%, p = 0.001). The pooled response rate of tacrolimus was relatively higher in Chinese population (0.872, 95% CI: 0.713, 0.977) than in Japanese population (0.793, 95% CI: 0.298, 1.000). However, interpretation of this finding should be cautious due to the high heterogeneity among Chinese studies (Fig. 4B).

Monoclonal antibodies

Clinical heterogeneity was possible across studies in terms of study design, single-/multi-center setting, different population, and therapeutic regimen. Nevertheless, subgroup analysis could not be performed due to the small number of included studies.

Glucocorticoids

All included studies were single-center investigations, comprising cohort studies and case series. Clinical heterogeneity may exist across multiple dimensions, including patient subtypes, populations, therapeutic regimen, and whether pyridostigmine was combined.

IVIG

The included studies exhibited diverse designs, including single-/multi-center, cohort, case series, single-arm, and RCT, which contributed to methodological heterogeneity. Patient subtypes enrolled included both OMG and GMG. Substantial clinical heterogeneity was observed regarding populations, therapeutic regimen, and whether other interventions were combined.

Meta-regression analysis

Meta-regression analysis was not performed due to the small number of included studies combined with numerous potential confounding factors. Instead, descriptive analysis was conducted for these potential influencing factors.

Publication bias

We performed funnel plots for studies included in the analyses of tacrolimus and monoclonal antibodies. Asymmetry of the funnel plot suggested potential publication bias in the tacrolimus studies (Fig. 5A), whereas the symmetric funnel plot in Fig. 5B indicated no significant publication bias.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first meta-analysis evaluating the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in JMG. Notably, the studies included in this meta-analysis predominantly involved Asian populations, and the incidence rate of JMG in the Asian population is higher than that in the European and North American populations [6].

All immunosuppressants and immunomodulators have demonstrated favorable efficacy in the treatment of JMG. For mAbs, small, uncontrolled studies reported high response rate in refractory patients, with the pooled 12-month DRR of 0.993 (95% CI: 0.935-1.000). In AMG, rituximab targets CD20-positive B cells, thereby inhibiting the synthesis of pathogenic antibodies [41]. Besides, studies have demonstrated that the response rate of AMG following rituximab treatment was significantly higher than that in the placebo group [42].

Eculizumab binds to complement component C5, reducing the formation of the membrane attack complex and thus preventing damage to the neuromuscular junction, with rapid onset and durable efficacy in AMG [43–45]. Previous research has shown that eculizumab exerted substantial therapeutic effects, as it significantly reduced patients’ QMG Scores and led to clinical symptom improvement in nearly 90% of patients in AMG [44–46]. The limited data in JMG showed generally consistent results. Currently, eculizumab has been approved in multiple countries for the treatment of GMG in adults and children aged ≥ 6 years old [47]. Additionally, a comparative study between eculizumab and rituximab indicated that the eculizumab achieved a higher remission rate in AMG [48]. However, we cannot draw a similar conclusion through the limited data in our study because of small sample size.

For tacrolimus, multiple cohort and case series studies suggested a benefit in symptom improvement, function scores and steroid sparing. A therapeutic response was defined as a ≥ 2-point reduction in these scores, and following tacrolimus treatment, the average daily corticosteroid dosage was reduced by 15.59 mg/d, with a remission rate of 86.2%. The reduction in corticosteroid dosage was consistent with previous meta-analyses in AMG and JMG [49, 50]. This study tends to support that tacrolimus leads to a decrease in corticosteroid dosage, though additional studies were needed to confirm these findings.

Tacrolimus effectively reduces the QMG and MG-ADL scores in MG patients, which was consistent with the results of previous meta-analysis in JMG [49]. Tacrolimus may serve as a supplement or combination therapy for patients with inadequate responses to glucocorticoids. Tacrolimus was used for JMG by specifically inhibiting T cell activation and proliferation [51]. MG is primarily mediated by AChR antibodies and the production of AChR antibodies is T cell-dependent [52–53]. The improvement in clinical symptoms accompanied by a decrease in AChR antibodies titers was consistent with our study. While tacrolimus can be used in combination with glucocorticoids to reduce the dosage of glucocorticoids and associated AEs in AMG, our limited data support the extrapolation of these findings in JMG [54]. The literature included in this study mainly focuses on combination with glucocorticoids, with only one study exploring efficacy of tacrolimus monotherapy. Compared to glucocorticoid monotherapy, tacrolimus monotherapy demonstrated a faster onset of action and fewer adverse effects [55]. Thus, future studies may explore the potential of tacrolimus as a monotherapy for JMG.

Five studies evaluated the efficacy and safety of glucocorticoids in JMG, — four focused on prednisone and one on methylprednisolone. Among these, most studies used glucocorticoids combined with pyridostigmine, while one study used glucocorticoids alone.Glucocorticoids exerted anti-inflammatory effects by targeting the postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular junction to inhibit local inflammatory responses, and also exerted immunosuppressive effects such as reducing the production of complement components and inducing B cell apoptosis in AMG [56–59]. A previous report showed that only 64.1% of patients who received low-dose prednisolone therapy achieved the treatment goal after 3 years in AMG [60]. Glucocorticoids may be initiated when pyridostigmine monotherapy is ineffective, or used in combination with pyridostigmine.

Four studies evaluated the efficacy of IVIG in JMG, with heterogeneous regimens, involving variations in infusion frequency, dosage, and treatment course: the total IVIG dose ranged from 1.2 to 2 g/kg, administered over 2–5 days, and some patients required maintenance therapy every 4–6 weeks. Previous studies had reported that high-dose IVIG (1–2 g/kg), acting as an anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory agent, was applicable for JMG treatment, while our limited data supported these findings [61]. Currently, IVIG is used as a rescue intervention to prevent disease progression to myasthenic crisis and exhibits a relatively rapid onset of action in AMG [62], which may be also applicable for JMG. The mechanism of IVIG in JMG remains not very clear, but it may involve the regulation of T and B cell processes, modulation of the FcRn inhibitors, and complement downregulation [63]. Another mechanism of IVIG is the neutralization of autoantibodies via anti-idiotypic antibodies, which has been confirmed in anti-AChR antibodies in AMG [64]. Few studies have explored the therapeutic efficacy of IVIG in JMG, thus more homogeneous RCTs were needed to enroll MG patients with different serotypes and unify the standards for IVIG treatment regimens, efficacy, and safety assessment. Additionally, comparisons of efficacy between IVIG and novel therapies (e.g., FcRn inhibitors, complement inhibitors) were required to clarify the role of IVIG in the long-term management of MG.

Limitations

This meta-analysis has several important limitations. First, most of the limitations stemmed from the observational nature of the included studies. Such studies were inherently prone to selection bias and reduced the quality of evidence. Additionally, most included studies adopted a retrospective design with single-center small sample size, which not only introduced allocation bias and confounding factors but also further weakened the evidential strength. Second, current studies primarily focus on the efficacy of individual drugs, without direct comparison of therapeutic effects between different types of immunosuppressants/immunomodulators. Future research should prioritize comparative efficacy analyses of various treatment regimens. Third, significant heterogeneity was observed in populations, subtypes, treatment regimens, outcome measures, and follow-up durations among the included studies, which may have impacted the results. It was crucial to emphasize that the follow-up durations among the included studies were inconsistent, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding the long-term safety and efficacy of the interventions. Furthermore, the effect estimates derived from this study, such as the response rates of tacrolimus and monoclonal antibodies in JMG, carried substantial uncertainty and risk of bias. The high heterogeneity observed in the analytical results also highlighted the fragility of the current evidence base. Therefore, the conclusions of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution. This study did not aim to provide definitive answers, instead, it ought to reflect the limitations of existing data and urgently called for more rigorous and standardized research in future. This meta-analysis could not determine a uniform effective regimen, which may rely on clinicians’ clinical experience, highlighting the need for individualized treatment for patients.

Conclusions

This was the first meta-analysis comprehensively evaluating the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in JMG. These agents, including tacrolimus, glucocorticoids, mAbs, and IVIG, might alleviate symptoms in most JMG patients with few severe AEs. Specifically, tacrolimus treatment reduced glucocorticoid dosage, QMG scores, MG-ADL scores, and serum AChR antibodies. However, most included studies were single-center retrospective observational studies. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to further explore the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressants and immunomodulators in JMG.
