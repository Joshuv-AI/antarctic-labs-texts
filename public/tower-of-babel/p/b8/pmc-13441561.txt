Skin Lipid Dysregulation in Atopic Dermatitis and Related Inflammatory Skin Diseases
Authors: Han Bi Kim, Jinseo Park, Chan Sol Lim, Ji Young Um, Bo Young Chung, Chun Wook Park, Dong-Kyu Lee, Hye One Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidermal lipids are essential for skin barrier function and actively influence cutaneous immune homeostasis. Recent advances have transformed our understanding of skin lipid dysregulation in allergic diseases, revealing complex bidirectional relationships between barrier dysfunction and immune activation. This review provides a comprehensive analysis of the 2020–2025 literature on skin lipid metabolism in allergic and inflammatory diseases, with a particular focus on molecular mechanisms, diagnostic biomarkers, therapeutic advances, and implications for precision medicine. Key discoveries include the finding that sphingomyelin deacylase, long linked to ceramide (CER) deficiency, is actually the β-subunit of acid ceramidase. In atopic dermatitis, this enzyme shifts its substrate specificity to sphingomyelin, depleting the barrier lipid pool. Type 2 cytokines, particularly interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13, suppress lipid biosynthesis through Janus kinase (JAK)/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 6-mediated downregulation of fatty acid (FA) elongases. This suppression, particularly affecting elongation of very long-chain fatty acid (ELOVL) enzymes, such as ELOVL1, ELOVL3, and ELOVL6, disrupts the synthesis of ultra-long-chain CERs essential for barrier function. JAK inhibitors effectively reverse T helper 2 (Th2)-mediated lipid suppression, while optimized topical lipid ratios (3:1:1 CER: cholesterol: FA) enhance barrier repair. Innovations, such as lipid nanoparticles and microbiome-modulating therapies, further support personalized treatment strategies. The integration of barrier repair with targeted immunomodulation—guided by lipidomic and genomic profiling—marks a paradigm shift toward predictive and preventive approaches in allergic skin diseases. Early intervention strategies that simultaneously address immune dysregulation and lipid metabolism hold promise for preventing the atopic march and sustaining long-term remission.

INTRODUCTION

The skin, the largest organ of the human body, serves as a critical interface between internal and external environments.1 Beyond its role as a physical barrier, the skin actively participates in immune surveillance, allergen recognition, and inflammatory responses that are central to the pathogenesis of allergic diseases.2 The traditional “brick and mortar” model of the stratum corneum (SC)—in which corneocytes function as “bricks” and intercellular lipids serve as “mortar”—has evolved to include the skin microbiome as a third critical component, metaphorically described as “roof shingles.” This expanded model reflects a dynamic biological ecosystem.3 Recent spatial profiling further demonstrates that microbial colonization, particularly by Staphylococcus aureus, can actively modulate epidermal lipid metabolism, providing mechanistic support for this conceptual framework. Similar lipid dysregulation patterns are observed across inflammatory skin diseases, including psoriasis, contact dermatitis, and ichthyosis, suggesting shared pathogenic mechanisms linking barrier dysfunction to systemic inflammation.245

Advances in lipid biochemistry and precision medicine between 2020 and 2025 have significantly transformed our understanding of allergic skin diseases. Key discoveries include clarification of the molecular basis of ceramide (CER) deficiency in atopic dermatitis (AD)—notably, the paradigm-shifting discovery that sphingomyelin deacylase (SMD) is actually the β-subunit of acid ceramidase (ASAH1), fundamentally redefining lipid metabolism pathways in inflammatory skin diseases. This mechanistic insight has transformed our understanding of barrier dysfunction from passive structural defects to active enzymatic mechanisms.

Dupilumab, a targeted immunomodulator, has demonstrated direct barrier-restoring effects, with up to 52% reduction in transepidermal water loss (TEWL) within 16 weeks. Lipid-based biomarkers—showing significant elevation in lesional skin—are now being evaluated for their utility in disease prediction and monitoring.67

For clinicians managing allergic skin diseases, these discoveries have important clinical implications. Skin barrier dysfunction is increasingly recognized as not only a consequence but also a driver of allergic sensitization, establishing a bidirectional cycle between barrier integrity and immune activation that perpetuates the atopic march.8

Through Janus kinase/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 6 (JAK/STAT6) signaling, type 2 cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13, suppress the expression of fatty acid (FA) elongation pathways, thereby compromising CER biosynthesis, and initiate a self-reinforcing loop of barrier breakdown and immune activation (Fig. 1).

Recent findings have also identified the elongation of very long-chain fatty acids (ELOVL) 1 as essential for ω-O-acylceramide production and long periodicity phase (LPP) formation. Its deficiency results in profound acylceramide depletion and severe barrier defects, further implicating elongase enzymes in the pathogenesis of AD.9

This evolving paradigm necessitates a comprehensive understanding of lipid metabolism, innovative therapeutic approaches, and precision strategies targeting both barrier repair and immune modulation.

This review synthesizes the current evidence on skin lipid dysregulation in allergic diseases, emphasizing actionable insights for clinical practice and emerging therapeutic strategies with the potential to transform patient care.

MOLECULAR ARCHITECTURE AND BIOSYNTHESIS OF SKIN BARRIER LIPIDS

SC lipid composition and organization

The SC contains approximately 5%–15% lipids by mass.10 These lipids are organized into intercellular lamellar structures, delivered via lamellar bodies, forming an extracellular lipid matrix. This matrix primarily consists of CERs (~50%), cholesterol (CHOL, ~25%), and free fatty acids (FFAs, ~15%)—proportions conventionally reported as weight percentages (w/w) in lipidomic studies.11

This composition differs significantly from typical cellular membranes, which are rich in phospholipids with esterified FAs. In contrast, the SC shows the near-complete degradation of phospholipids into FFAs.10 SC CERs, a subclass of sphingolipids with amino-bearing sphingoid bases, exhibit unique subclass compositions not found in other tissues.10

In the SC, CHOL modulates membrane fluidity and lipid packing, thereby contributing to the formation of LPP.1213 Unlike its fluidizing role in phospholipid bilayers, CHOL in the SC promotes lamellar organization and enhances miscibility between CERs and FFAs.9 This specialized architecture is essential for skin barrier function.14

CERs diversity and functional architecture

CERs are the most structurally diverse lipid class.15 Current classification recognizes 25 free CER subclasses, based on sphingoid base—dihydrosphingosine (DS), sphingosine (S), phytosphingosine (P), 6-hydroxysphingosine (H), and 4,14-sphingadiene (SD)—and FA modifications, including non-hydroxy (N), α-hydroxy (A), β-hydroxy (B), ω-hydroxy (O), and esterified ω-hydroxy (EO).1516

Mass spectrometry (MS) has identified over 1,327 unbound and 254 protein-bound CER species in the human SC.16 Comprehensive descriptions of molecular structures and nomenclature of CER classes can be found in recent reviews focusing on CER diversity and skin lipid organization.1115

These sphingolipids consist of sphingoid bases linked to FAs of varying chain lengths (C14–C36), with very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs, > C22) particularly critical for barrier integrity. Notably, esterified ω-hydroxyceramides (ω-hydroxyCER), such as CER[EOS] and CER[EOP], incorporate ultra-long-chain fatty acids (ULCFAs) and are thought to contribute to the formation of the LPP, a characteristic lamellar phase of the SC.14

Recent research indicates that CER chain length distribution is as important as total CER content. AD patients exhibit the characteristic patterns of reduced long-chain CERs and increased short-chain species.17 More specifically, van Smeden et al.4 and Janssens et al.17 defined short-chain CERs as those with total carbon number < C34, while CERs with ≥ C42 total carbons represent the long-chain fraction. Some studies have used C40 as an intermediate cutoff, but the primary functional distinction lies in the shift from longer (≥ C42) to shorter (< C34) species, which directly correlates with altered lipid organization and decreased barrier function in AD.17

Protein-bound CERs as essential barrier components

Beyond the free intercellular lipids described above, protein-bound CERs constitute a critical component of the corneocyte lipid envelope, a covalently bound lipid layer on the outer surface of corneocytes within the SC.512

Protein-bound CERs are primarily ω-hydroxyCERs, which become covalently attached to the cornified envelope through enzymatic processing involving transglutaminase 1 and lipoxygenase.18 Recent studies have identified two models for protein-bound CER formation: the P-O model, involving direct attachment of ω-hydroxyCERs, and the P-EO model, in which epoxy-enone CERs form stable conjugates with cysteine residues via Michael addition.15

In healthy skin, protein-bound ω-hydroxyCERs comprise approximately 46%–53% of total protein-bound lipids, with levels significantly decreased in AD lesions.19 Functionally, these covalently bound CERs form a scaffold at the corneocyte surface, anchoring the intercellular lipid lamellae to the corneocyte envelope and maintaining the integrity of the SC’s lamellar architecture, particularly under mechanical stress. Protein-bound CERs are commonly categorized into five structural classes (P-ODS, P-OS, P-OP, P-OH, and P-OSD).5

CER biosynthesis and regulatory networks

De novo CER synthesis occurs in the viable epidermis at a physiological pH of ~7.4.20 It begins with the condensation of L-serine and palmitoyl-Coenzyme A (CoA) by serine palmitoyltransferase to form 3-ketosphinganine, followed by reductions and elongation steps that generate mature CERs.20 CerS3, in particular, incorporates ULCFAs > C26, essential for lamellar structure.21

ELOVL enzymes, especially ELOVL1–7, extend FAs to the lengths required for specific CER species.12 Structural studies have revealed a conserved HxxHH motif in ELOVLs responsible for the formation of acyl-imidazole intermediates.22 Yamamoto et al.9 provided a comprehensive comparative analysis of skin barrier abnormalities in ELOVL-deficient mouse models, demonstrating that Cyp4f39, Elovl1, and Fatp4 knockout mice exhibit distinct CER profile alterations, with varying degrees of barrier dysfunction. Recent reviews have highlighted that ELOVL4 is specifically required for the synthesis of ULCFAs (C28–C36), which are essential for acylceramide production. In AD, impaired ELOVL-dependent FA elongation has been linked not only to the disrupted compartmentalization of CER synthesis but also to broader dysregulation across multiple steps of epidermal CER biosynthesis and processing.23

Among these enzymes, ELOVL3 and ELOVL6 have shown particular importance in epidermal barrier maintenance, with their expression significantly reduced in AD through IL-4/IL-13-mediated STAT6 signaling.24

In addition to the de novo synthesis pathway, alternative pathways include sphingomyelin hydrolysis by sphingomyelinases (SMases) and the salvage pathway, which recycles sphingosine back to CERs.11

In contrast to de novo synthesis occurring at a physiological pH, the acidic pH of the SC (4.5–5.5) promotes the activity of lipid processing enzymes, including β-glucocerebrosidase (β-GCase) and acidic SMase, which convert glucosylceramides and sphingomyelin into mature CERs in the extracellular SC matrix.25

An elevation in SC pH, as observed in AD and aged skin, impairs these enzymatic processes while activating serine proteases, thereby disrupting barrier homeostasis.25

Complementary lipid components and optimal ratios

CHOL comprises approximately 25%–27% of SC lipids and contributes to the organization and phase behavior of the intercellular lipid matrix.4

FFAs, which account for 10%–15% of SC lipids, possess antimicrobial properties and modulate inflammatory responses beyond their structural roles.3 The major FFAs are enriched in very long-chain species, with lignoceric acid (C24:0) representing approximately 25% of total FFAs, followed by behenic acid (C22:0) at ~15% and hexacosanoic acid (C26:0) at ~10%. In contrast, palmitic acid (C16:0) and stearic acid (C18:0) each constitute only about 10% of the total.426

These C22–C26 FAs primarily contribute to the lateral packing within the lipid matrix, promoting the denser structural arrangements, such as orthorhombic packing, rather than directly influencing lamellar organization.14 Extensive research over the past two decades, using biophysical techniques, such as X-ray diffraction and infrared spectroscopy, supports the concept that FFA chain length is a major determinant of lateral packing density, with longer chains favoring tightly packed orthorhombic structures over the less dense hexagonal phase.27

The optimal 3:1:1 ratio (CER:CHOL:FFAs) or 3:1:1:1 when including essential FAs, has been shown to maximize barrier recovery rates and reduce TEWL.28 Importantly, recent studies suggest plasticity in barrier repair mechanisms, whereby increasing the concentration of any single component three-fold can achieve comparable barrier restoration. This finding offers cost-effective alternatives to high-purity CER formulations.28

This plasticity carries practical therapeutic implications. For effective barrier repair, exogenously applied lipids must integrate into the existing intercellular lipid lamellae rather than forming a superficial occlusive layer. Formulations that mimic physiological lipid ratios and contain appropriate chain lengths facilitate such integration, while non-physiological compositions may provide only temporary occlusion without achieving true structural barrier restoration.14

Spatial distribution and microenvironmental diversity

Skin lipids exhibit region-specific and compositionally distinct patterns of heterogeneity across microenvironments and along the depth axis. These include skin surface lipids (sebaceous-derived sebum), SC lipids (associated with barrier function), and sebaceous gland lipids (linked to immune regulation).4

Lipid composition varies across the SC layers, with triglyceride content decreasing and CER concentrations increasing in deeper regions.10 The composition of sebaceous lipids plays a crucial role in shaping the skin microbiome, as specific microorganisms metabolize these lipids into bioactive mediators that influence cutaneous immune responses.3

Bioactive functions of CERs and metabolites

Beyond their structural roles, sphingolipids act as potent signaling molecules that regulate key cellular processes, including cell survival, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammation.11

In inflammatory contexts, cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), can induce CER accumulation (e.g., via SMase activation), and CER signaling can, in turn, influence downstream inflammatory pathways, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), thereby influencing immune cell function.1112

Key bioactive metabolites include CER 1-phosphate, which activates cytosolic phospholipase A2 and leads to the synthesis of inflammatory mediators, and sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P), which regulates immune cell trafficking through its receptors (S1PR1–5).6

Type 2 inflammation and lipid metabolism

Accumulating evidence supports a bidirectional relationship between immune activation and lipid dysregulation in allergic skin disease. Type 2 cytokines, particularly IL-4 and IL-13, systematically disrupt epidermal lipid homeostasis via the shared IL-4Rα/IL-13Rα1 receptor complex, which activates JAK1, JAK3, and Tyrosine Kinase 2. These kinases, in turn, phosphorylate STAT6.2930

Activated STAT6 suppresses the transcription of multiple lipid-synthetic enzymes (e.g., ELOVL family members) and enzymes involved in CER maturation, including β-GCase and acid SMase.2930 This suppression leads to reduced VLCFA production, shortened CER chain length, impaired lamellar body formation, and diminished barrier hydrophobicity.1729

The clinical consequence is a self-perpetuating cycle in which barrier dysfunction facilitates allergen penetration, further amplifying type 2 inflammation and deepening the disruption of lipid metabolism.830

A notable finding is that psoriasis (predominantly Th1/Th17-driven) and AD (predominantly Th2-driven) have been reported to exhibit the overlapping patterns of CER abnormalities, despite their divergent immunological underpinnings.31 This apparent paradox may be explained by several mechanisms: (1) pro-inflammatory cytokines common to both diseases, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, disrupt lipid metabolism regardless of the predominant T helper profile; (2) CER alterations may reflect a generalized state of epidermal barrier dysregulation driven by chronic inflammation, rather than being unique to each cytokine milieu; and (3) both conditions involve a bidirectional barrier-inflammation loop, wherein barrier dysfunction promotes inflammation, which in turn further impairs lipid synthesis.32

Consistent with the inflammation-linked nature of lipid disturbance, several studies indicate that non-lesional skin shows less pronounced lipid abnormalities compared to lesional sites.33

Multifaceted inflammatory mechanisms

Recent insights reveal additional inflammation-driven mechanisms that directly impair lipid metabolism. SMD, reported to be elevated in AD, is now understood as the β-subunit of ASAH1. In AD, disulfide bond disruption between α- and β-subunits alters substrate specificity from CERs to sphingomyelin. This mechanistic shift represents a direct mechanism by which inflammation compromises barrier function.28

Epithelial cell-derived inflammatory mechanisms: Epithelial keratinocytes produce a range of inflammatory mediators that link barrier disruption to immune activation and lipid dysregulation. Classical epithelial alarmins, including thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), IL-33, and IL-25, play key roles in this process.34

TSLP activates dendritic cells, promoting Th2 polarization,35 while IL-33 functions both as a damage-associated molecular pattern and an active cytokine initiating type 2 responses.36

Beyond these alarmins, keratinocytes also release pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, in response to barrier disruption and bacterial colonization. Notably, recent evidence demonstrates that these cytokines, especially when induced by S. aureus colonization, can suppress FA elongation pathways in keratinocytes, including the expression of ELOVL3 and ELOVL4, creating a feed-forward inflammatory loop that further impairs lipid synthesis and exacerbates barrier dysfunction.3437

IL-25–mediated dual disruption: IL-25 impairs barrier function through a dual mechanism.383940 First, it directly suppresses filaggrin (FLG) synthesis in keratinocytes by signaling through the IL-17RA/IL-17RB receptor complex and activating STAT3 signaling pathways.3839 Second, IL-25 activates group 2 innate lymphoid cells to secrete IL-13, which in turn suppresses lipid-synthetic enzymes and barrier genes such as FLG, CDSN, TGM1, and OCLN1.40

Mechanistically, IL-25 binding recruits the Act1 adapter protein and activates multiple downstream pathways, including NF-κB, MAPK, and JAK1/2-STAT3 signaling cascades. These cascades collectively impair both structural protein expression and lipid biosynthetic pathways that are critical for barrier integrity.383940

Complement system involvement: Emerging evidence also implicates the complement system in epidermal lipid regulation. The complement fragments C3a and C5a are anaphylatoxins—bioactive peptides generated via proteolytic cleavage of complement components C3 and C5 during complement activation. These fragments act through their respective G-protein coupled receptors, C3aR and C5aR1, which are expressed on keratinocytes and immune cells.41

In the skin, C3a and C5a exert multiple pro-inflammatory effects, including mast cell degranulation, neutrophil chemotaxis, and keratinocyte activation.42 Importantly, these fragments also affect lipid homeostasis by modulating FA synthesis (e.g., via acetyl-CoA carboxylase), influencing keratinocyte insulin sensitivity, and promoting lipid peroxidation—all of which converge to impair the epidermal barrier.43

DISEASE-SPECIFIC LIPID DYSREGULATION PATTERNS

AD: the prototype lipid-barrier disease

AD exemplifies the complex interplay between genetic predisposition, immune dysfunction, and barrier failure.44 The disease is characterized by both quantitative and qualitative CER deficiencies, which create a permissive environment for allergic sensitization.1744

Comprehensive lipidomic analyses reveal distinct dysregulation patterns (Table 2), including reduction in total CERs, depletion of long-chain species (≥ C42), and enrichment of short-chain CERs (< C34), altered sphinganine:sphingosine ratios correlating with disease severity (r = −0.69), and elevated glucosylceramide levels in lesional skin.6

Validation studies have confirmed glucosylceramide as a robust biomarker with significantly elevated levels in AD lesions and showing the strongest correlation with TEWL (r2 = 0.60, P < 0.001).617

FLG mutations, present in up to 30% of patients, represent the strongest genetic risk factor for AD. These mutations lead to reduced natural moisturizing factor production and elevated skin pH.45 However, FLG expression and lipid synthesis appear to operate through independent pathways.

van Drongelen et al.46 demonstrated that FLG knockdown in 3D-skin equivalents did not affect SC lipid organization or composition. Similarly, Jungersted et al.47 found no significant differences in SC CER levels between AD patients with and without FLG mutations. Together, these findings support that CER abnormalities in AD are primarily driven by inflammation-mediated suppression of lipid synthetic enzymes rather than FLG deficiency.4647

AD-associated microbial dysbiosis, particularly S. aureus overgrowth, also contributes directly to lipid abnormalities through specific enzymatic inhibition.

Kim et al.34 demonstrated that both methicillin-sensitive (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant (MRSA) strains indirectly suppress ELOVL3 and ELOVL4 in keratinocytes via the induction of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-33). This results in a characteristic shift toward short-chain CER species, such as CER[NS] containing C16:0 (often denoted as N-16:0 NS-CER) and a reduction in VLCFAs (C22–C28).34

MRSA colonization produced more pronounced inhibition of elongase activity than MSSA and was associated with higher TEWL in colonized versus non-colonized AD skin.34

Comparative patterns in related inflammatory diseases

Contact dermatitis: Both allergic contact dermatitis (ACD) and irritant contact dermatitis (ICD) involve the significant disruption of the SC, albeit through distinct mechanisms.48

In ACD, hapten penetration through a compromised barrier triggers Th1-dominant immune responses, with accompanying oxidative stress and complement activation, further exacerbating lipid degradation and barrier dysfunction.48

In contrast, ICD results from direct chemical-induced lipid extraction, particularly by solvents or surfactants, which dismantles the intercellular lipid lamellae, alters SC pH, impairs lipid-processing enzymes, and denatures structural proteins. These changes elicit inflammatory responses that mimic allergic processes.1

Psoriasis: Psoriasis is associated with lipid metabolic abnormalities linked to keratinocyte hyperproliferation and chronic inflammation. Research has revealed specific CER alterations, including increased levels of AS (α-hydroxy fatty acid–sphingosine) and NS (non-hydroxy fatty acid–sphingosine) CERs, as well as decreased levels of EOS (esterified ω-hydroxy fatty acid–sphingosine), AP (α-hydroxy fatty acid–phytosphingosine), and NP (non-hydroxy fatty acid–phytosphingosine) CERs.49 In addition, accumulation of linoleic acid–derived oxylipins such as 9-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (9-HODE) and 13-HODE has been observed (Supplementary Table S1).49 These lipid changes are accompanied by enhanced oxidative phosphorylation and reactive oxygen species generation, which contributes to epidermal hyperproliferation.49

Ichthyosis: Ichthyosis refers to a group of inherited skin disorders characterized by abnormal keratinization, scaling, and often inflammation.50

In Harlequin Ichthyosis, loss-of-function mutations in the ABCA12 gene impair lipid transport in epidermal keratinocytes, leading to severe skin barrier defects.50 In Lamellar Ichthyosis, mutations in the TGM1 gene disrupt transglutaminase-1, a key enzyme required for cornified envelope formation.50

Netherton Syndrome is a syndromic form of ichthyosis characterized by prominent immune dysregulation. It results from mutations in SPINK5, leading to the deficiency of lymphoepithelial Kazal-type-related inhibitor, a serine protease inhibitor. This deficiency causes uncontrolled protease activity, barrier breakdown, and inflammation.51

Detailed lipid profiling studies have reported a combination of alterations in Netherton Syndrome, including shorter FFA chain lengths, increased levels of monounsaturated FFAs, and a shift toward shorter-chain CERs, accompanied by a relative depletion of longer-chain fractions.552

A subsequent study by van Smeden et al.37 demonstrated that Netherton Syndrome patients exhibit elevated serine protease activity, along with altered lipid-processing enzyme function—specifically, reduced β-GCase activity coinciding with increased acid sphingomyelinase activity. These enzymatic changes correlate with abnormal CER composition and are associated with disease severity.

Collectively, these gene–lipid associations offer a mechanistic framework for understanding the pathogenesis of ichthyosis and support the development of targeted therapy strategies.

Liquid chromatography-tandem MS remains the gold standard for both untargeted (global profiling) and targeted lipidomic analyses. In contrast, direct-injection MS approaches, such as shotgun lipidomics, enable rapid and sensitive analysis, but they are limited by ion suppression and isomer overlap.53

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is the most widely employed method for analyzing both esterified and non-esterified FAs, as well as CHOL. FA determination typically involves acid hydrolysis followed by chemical derivatization.50 For CHOL analysis, silylation-based derivatization is commonly used, allowing for the detection of CHOL and its various derivatives, including hydroxycholesterols.13

Electron impact ionization in GC-MS produces characteristic fragmentation patterns that support structural elucidation, while GC-MS’s high separation efficiency enables the simultaneous profiling of a broad range of FAs in biological matrices.1350

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) techniques, such as secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI), investigate the spatial distribution of lipid molecules on complex surfaces, effectively functioning as chemical microscopes.18

Among these, MALDI-MS is most widely used for mapping lipid profile heterogeneity within skin layers and cellular compartments. With optimal matrix deposition, MALDI can achieve a spatial resolution of ≥ 5 μm without compromising molecular information.18 In contrast, SIMS achieves ionization by irradiating the sample with a primary ion beam and does not require a matrix.18 Due to higher spatial resolution of the ion beam compared to MALDI’s laser, SIMS enables lipid distribution mapping from the tissue level down to individual cells.18

These MSI approaches support the transition from bulk lipidomics to spatial lipidomics, facilitating high-resolution mapping of lipid composition across specific skin microenvironments.1828

In addition to MS-based methods, non-invasive spectroscopic techniques offer complementary insights into skin barrier lipid structure. Confocal Raman Spectroscopy (CRS) is a biophysical tool that provides depth-resolved assessment of lipid chain conformation and lateral packing within the SC, enabling distinction between orthorhombic and hexagonal phases.5455

Similarly, Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, particularly attenuated total reflectance FTIR mode, assesses lipid organization and water content.13 The CH2 scissoring band is especially useful for distinguishing lateral packing states: a doublet peak indicates orthorhombic packing, whereas a singlet peak signifies hexagonal arrangement. Although CRS and FTIR provide valuable structural and phase information, they do not offer molecular species identification or quantitative compositional data, which remain the domain of MS–based techniques.14

Precision diagnostic innovations

Barrier assessment technologies have transformed clinical evaluation.55 TEWL measurement remains a widely used functional barrier assessment method, quantifying water vapor flux across the SC as an indicator of barrier integrity.56 Electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) offers greater stability under variable environmental conditions compared to TEWL, providing more consistent readings across changes in humidity and temperature.55 Although, both TEWL and EIS have well-recognized limitations, but their proven reliability and standardization make them valuable foundation tools for integrative barrier assessment.5556 When combined with advanced molecular biomarkers, these functional assessments provide comprehensive barrier evaluation that bridges traditional clinical measurements with precision diagnostic insights. This integrative approach has become essential for personalized treatment strategies in allergic skin diseases.1

In contrast, optical coherence tomography (OCT) enables real-time, cellular-resolution imaging to monitor disease progression and treatment response.57 CRS also allows the real-time assessment of treatment response, though quantitative limitations persist.54

Validated biomarkers include glucosylceramide (significantly elevated in AD lesions, with the strongest correlation to TEWL) and CCL17, which is considered the most robust for assessing disease severity and treatment response.630 Emerging biomarkers under investigation include protein-bound ω-hydroxyl sphingosine (POS) CERs.7

Genetic and molecular profiling offers predictive insights for personalized therapy. Key pathogenic variants include CERS3 and PNPLA1 (involved in CER synthesis), ELOVL4 and CYP4F22 (involved in FA metabolism), and FLG mutations, which remain the strongest known genetic risk factor, conferring approximately three-fold increased susceptibility to AD.45

Beyond genetic predisposition, transcriptomic profiling has revealed clinically relevant disease heterogeneity. Single-cell RNA sequencing has identified distinct endotypes of AD, such as eosinophil-high and eosinophil-low subtypes, which correlate with differential immune activation profiles and therapeutic responses.58 These transcriptomic insights are guiding the personalization of therapeutic strategies, although the standardization of analytic protocols remains a key challenge for widespread clinical implementation.

Integrative precision medicine implementation

The convergence of lipidomics, immunology, and genomics is catalyzing a paradigm shift that emphasizes simultaneous barrier restoration and immune modulation. This emerging approach focuses on physiological lipid replacement, molecular endotype-tailored interventions, microbiome modulation, and topical measures that support the normalization of SC pH.32528

Clinical implementation is increasingly guided by precision medicine algorithms, incorporating circulating biomarkers (e.g., TARC/CCL17) alongside genetic and transcriptomic profiling to inform therapeutic selection.258 Machine learning approaches are being employed to integrate complex biomarker profiles for treatment prediction, although robust validation still requires large, well-characterized patient cohorts.1258

Ongoing treatment monitoring through serial TEWL assessments and lipidomic profiling allows for real-time adjustments of therapy, while microbiome characterization supports targeted interventions.312

When selecting therapy for allergic skin disease, clinicians should consider integrating multiple complementary assessment modalities—including non-invasive barrier assessments (TEWL and EIS), structural or compositional imaging (OCT, and CRS, if available) and molecular profiling (e.g., CER profiling, FLG genotyping, and circulating CCL17 quantification)—to align treatment decisions with the patient’s barrier phenotype and inflammatory endotype (Fig. 3).

THERAPEUTIC STRATEGIES: FROM LIPID RESTORATION TO IMMUNE MODULATION

Lipid-targeted approaches

Given the central role of CERs in skin barrier integrity, targeting CER synthesis and degradation enzymes has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy.1120 Effective lipid-targeted therapies can restore epidermal barrier function through several distinct mechanisms: (1) topical formulations containing physiological lipid ratios integrate into existing intercellular lamellae, rather than forming a superficial occlusive layer; (2) enzyme modulators, such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors agonists, enhance endogenous CER production by upregulating ELOVL enzymes and CER synthases; and (3) anti-inflammatory biologics indirectly restore lipid homeostasis by alleviating cytokine-mediated suppression of lipid synthetic enzymes.

The key distinction from conventional emollients is that true barrier repair requires the structural integration of supplied lipids into the lamellar architecture, an outcome that depends on delivering the appropriate lipid composition, chain length, and molar ratio necessary for functional restoration.

Targeted immunomodulation

JAK inhibitors and barrier restoration: JAK inhibitors, such as Upadacitinib and Abrocitinib, have emerged as particularly effective barrier-restorative therapies by directly counteracting the cytokine-mediated suppression of lipid synthesis.638

The clinical superiority of JAK inhibitors in achieving rapid symptom control stems from their ability to simultaneously disrupt multiple inflammatory cascades at the intracellular signaling hub. Unlike receptor-targeted biologics that must compete with circulating cytokines, JAK inhibition blocks the convergent point where IL-4, IL-13, IL-22, and IL-31 pathways integrate, explaining their consistently superior response rates.5960

The enhanced real-world efficacy observed with JAK inhibitors compared to controlled trial settings reflects their broad anti-inflammatory spectrum, which effectively addresses the heterogeneous inflammatory profiles encountered in clinical practice.60 Abrocitinib’s rapid pruritus relief advantage stems from its selective JAK1 inhibition, directly targeting IL-31 signaling pathways responsible for itch sensation.6061 Baricitinib’s efficacy across age groups reflects its balanced JAK1/JAK2 selectivity profile, while topical ruxolitinib achieves localized benefits by circumventing systemic exposure concerns.6062

IL-4/IL-13 pathway inhibition: The mechanistic advantage of dual IL-4/IL-13 receptor blockade over single cytokine targeting becomes evident when comparing therapeutic outcomes. Dupilumab’s superior barrier restoration relative to IL-13 selective inhibitors stems from comprehensive pathway disruption.76364 Blocking IL-4Rα prevents both IL-4 and IL-13 from accessing their shared signaling cascade.2 In contrast, IL-13 selective approaches leave IL-4-mediated lipid suppression intact, explaining their consistently lower barrier restoration efficacy and delayed onset profiles.76364

The universal efficacy of dupilumab across FLG genotypes reveals a critical insight: IL-4/IL-13 pathway disruption can overcome genetic barrier defects through direct restoration of lipid synthetic capacity.65 This mechanistic independence from underlying genetic vulnerabilities explains why receptor-level blockade achieves more consistent outcomes than therapies targeting downstream consequences of barrier dysfunction.65

Recent IL-4/IL-13 pathway additions demonstrate the therapeutic value of pathway redundancy. Lebrikizumab and tralokinumab provide alternative options for patients with incomplete dupilumab responses, while pediatric approvals extend precision immunotherapy to developmental stages where early intervention may prevent disease progression.636466

Emerging immune targets: The successful introduction of novel therapeutic targets reflects an expanding understanding of AD pathophysiology beyond the traditional type 2 paradigm, encompassing immune neural barrier crosstalk that ultimately converges on epidermal lipid homeostasis. Nemolizumab’s IL-31RA inhibition addresses the critical gap between inflammation control and symptom relief by targeting the neuroimmune axis responsible for persistent pruritus.67 By reducing itch-driven scratching behavior, IL-31 blockade indirectly limits secondary barrier disruption and lipid disorganization within the SC, helping preserve epidermal lipid integrity even in patients with otherwise controlled inflammation.67

In parallel, Tapinarof’s aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) modulation represents a paradigm shift toward dual-mechanism therapies that simultaneously regulate inflammation and barrier function.68 Beyond suppressing both Th17 and Th2 signaling, AhR agonism promotes keratinocyte differentiation and the upregulation of barrier proteins essential for proper lipid organization and lamellar structure formation. This coordinated restoration of the protein and lipid components of the SC enables comprehensive disease modification through a single molecular target, thereby explaining tapinarof’s broad therapeutic spectrum.68

The development of dual-target biologics like GB12-09 (IL-4Rα/IL-31Rα) further reflects the recognition that effective management of complex inflammatory skin diseases requires multifactorial interventions.69 By concurrently attenuating type 2 inflammation and neurogenic itch, this strategy not only improves clinical symptoms but also reduces inflammation- and scratching-induced perturbations of epidermal lipid synthesis and organization. Such mechanistic complementarity may ultimately translate into superior patient-reported outcomes through sustained restoration of the lipid barrier (Table 4).69

Current evidence supports combination protocols that integrate physiologic lipid replacement with targeted immunomodulation, resulting in superior clinical outcomes compared to either approach alone.9

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND CLINICAL IMPLEMENTATION

Advanced therapeutic technologies

The development of novel drug delivery systems represents a critical frontier in barrier-targeted therapy. Conventional topical CER formulations have limited penetration into the deeper layers of the SC, particularly for very-long-chain CERs. Lipid-based carriers, including solid lipid nanoparticles and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC), enhance epidermal targeting while ensuring biodegradability and the sustained release of barrier-active compounds.49 NLCs, as second-generation delivery systems, offer higher drug-loading capacity and improved physical stability, making them particularly beneficial for delivering CER-based formulations to compromised skin barriers.

Microemulsions and nanoemulsions facilitate drug penetration by transiently disrupting SC lipid architecture and increasing solubility of lipophilic barrier repair agents; however, challenges remain in terms of manufacturing scalability and cost-effectiveness.42

Biomimetic lipid formulations incorporating synthetic CERs—such as CER[NP] (CER 3) and pseudo-CERs—offer higher stability than their natural counterparts and enhance barrier function by more effectively integrating into the existing intercellular lipid lamellae.15 Next-generation formulations, with optimized lipid ratios, promote barrier repair through self-assembly and stimulation of endogenous repair pathways.428

Microbiome modulation strategies have emerged as a key regulator of epidermal lipid homeostasis.3 Prebiotics, probiotics, and postbiotics collectively represent a multilayered strategy to support skin barrier function.3

Among microbiome-derived metabolites, SCFAs, notably propionate and butyrate, have been implicated in immune regulation and epidermal barrier maintenance. Collectively, these approaches mark a shift from passive lipid supplementation to the active modulation of the skin’s microbial ecosystem for sustainable barrier restoration.3

Precision medicine integration and implementation challenges

The validation and clinical implementation of lipid-based biomarkers represent a high-priority focus in current dermatologic research. While glucosylceramide levels are significantly elevated in AD lesions and show a strong correlation with TEWL, large-scale studies are still required to establish reference ranges across diverse populations and age groups.671217 Future diagnostic capabilities are expected to be transformed by the development of point-of-care devices for CER quantification, validation of POS CERs as early predictive markers, and the integration of single-cell lipidomics technologies.2653

Despite remarkable progress, lipidomics research continues to face key challenges, particularly in the accurate quantification of individual lipid species and the lack of standardized protocols.1947

The future of personalized dermatology lies in the integration of multi-omics platforms, combining genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics to achieve comprehensive patient profiling.5358

Single-cell lipidomics offers the promise of revealing cellular heterogeneity and uncovering novel therapeutic targets within the skin barrier; however, technical limitations in sensitivity and standardization remain substantial.53

Machine learning algorithms are being developed to integrate complex biomarker profiles, with the goal of predicting therapeutic responses and guiding individualized treatment selection as part of emerging precision medicine strategies.58

Near-term priorities over the next 1–2 years include preparing for the deployment of commercial genetic profiling platforms, developing clinical and research expertise in microbiome assessment, and establishing standardized protocols for evaluating skin barrier function.125358 In the longer-term (3–5 years), priorities will extend to the integration of artificial intelligence-driven treatment algorithms, the implementation of single-cell lipidomics to gain patient-specific insights, and the advancement of gene therapy approaches targeting severe genetic defects.505358

Research imperatives and global implementation

Key research imperatives include harmonizing lipidomics methodologies across institutions to ensure data reproducibility, conducting long-term outcome studies to evaluate the preventive potential of early intervention strategies, and assessing the cost-effectiveness of precision medicine compared to conventional therapy.21253 The relationship between skin microbiome composition and lipid metabolism warrants systematic investigation, particularly regarding how specific microbial species influence CER synthesis and the regulatory role of SCFAs in skin barrier integrity.3610 Clinical trials evaluating targeted probiotic interventions in combination with lipid replacement therapies are essential to validate multilayered treatment approaches that simultaneously address microbial dysbiosis and epidermal barrier dysfunction.328

Effective long-term prevention strategies will require large-scale, longitudinal studies following high-risk pediatric populations from birth through adolescence. These studies aim to validate interventions targeting early barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation, while determining optimal timing, clinical efficacy, and comparative cost-effectiveness relative to conventional treatment pathways.1258

International collaboration is critical to establish standardized analytical techniques, quality control protocols, and regulatory frameworks to support the clinical application of precision lipidomics. Currently, efforts must be made to develop simplified, scalable, and cost-effective approaches suitable for use in resource-limited settings.53

Understanding how environmental exposures and population-specific genetic variations influence skin barrier function will guide the development of culturally tailored interventions and enhance global accessibility to precision dermatology innovations.712 The continued exploration of microbiome-lipid-immune system interactions is likely to uncover novel therapeutic targets and broaden the potential of barrier-directed therapeutic strategies.31170

CONCLUSION

The period from 2020 to 2025 has marked a paradigm shift in our understanding and management of allergic and inflammatory skin diseases. Mechanistic discoveries, such as the identification of SMD and the elucidation of JAK/STAT-mediated suppression of FA elongation pathways, including ELOVL enzymes, have clarified how immune dysregulation disrupts epidermal lipid metabolism. These insights have reframed therapeutic strategies, shifting the focus from conventional immunosuppression to barrier-centric interventions aimed at restoring lipid homeostasis.

Therapeutic and diagnostic advances have aligned with this shift: (1) dupilumab has been shown to reduce TEWL by 52% within 16 weeks; (2) JAK inhibitors effectively reverse cytokine-mediated lipid dysfunction; and (3) precision diagnostics have proposed glucosylceramide and protein-bound CERs as candidate biomarker classes for disease activity and barrier impairment. Collectively, these developments provide a strong rationale for barrier-focused, multimodal strategies combining lipid replacement with targeted immunomodulation, although direct comparative evidence for additive or synergistic benefit remains limited.

The integration of lipidomics, genomics, and immunology paves the way for personalized dermatology, emphasizing prevention through early biomarker-guided intervention and treatment optimization through molecular profiling. This evolving paradigm enables clinicians to move beyond reactive symptom management toward proactive, precision-based strategies that aim to prevent disease progression and sustain long-term remission. However, the successful implementation of this approach will require the continued validation, standardization, and resolution of practical challenges related to clinical translation and accessibility.
