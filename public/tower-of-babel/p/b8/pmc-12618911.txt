Storage duration of platelet concentrates and clinical outcomes in cardiac surgery patients
Authors: Fabienne M A van Hout, Camila Caram‐Deelder, Aad Pors, Rutger A Middelburg, Robert J M Klautz, Peter M J Rosseel, Hendrik J F Helmerhorst, Leo M G van de Watering, Jean‐Louis Kerkhoffs, Meindert Palmen, Johanna G van der Bom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe changes in platelet structure and function arising from its collection and storage are collectively called “platelet storage lesion.” In patients this might potentially be associated with impaired hemostasis and adverse events. We performed a hypothesis‐generating retrospective study in cardiac surgery patients transfused with platelets in two hospitals between 2005 and 2017.Study Design and MethodsWe explored whether longer storage duration of platelets is associated with changes in in‐hospital mortality, blood loss after surgery, reoperation for bleeding, stroke, myocardial infarction, infection, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, shock, and multi‐organ failure in cardiac surgery patients. Platelet concentrates were classified as platelets with shorter storage duration (PSSD; stored 1–3 days) or platelets with longer storage duration (PLSD; stored 4–7 days). Associations between storage duration and endpoints were quantified using logistic regression adjusted for potential confounders.ResultsIn patients transfused with PLSD, in‐hospital mortality (212/2117; 10.0%) was significantly higher than in patients transfused with PSSD (109/1439; 7.6%) (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.47, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.13–1.91). Patients transfused with PLSD experienced postoperative blood loss ≥1000 mL (102/285; 35.8%) significantly more frequently than patients transfused with PSSD (87/326; 26.7%), aOR 1.74 (95% CI 1.19–2.52). In patients transfused with PLSD, the need for reoperation for bleeding (99/285; 34.7%) was significantly higher than in patients transfused with PSSD (87/326; 26.7%) (aOR 1.62, 95% CI 1.12–2.35). There was no notable association with other endpoints.ConclusionIn cardiac surgery patients, transfusion of PLSD was associated with higher in‐hospital mortality, more blood loss, and reoperations for bleeding, compared to transfusion of PSSD.Keywords: hospital mortality, platelet transfusion, postoperative hemorrhage, reoperation, thoracic surgery

INTRODUCTION

Patients undergoing cardiac surgery are at risk of excessive bleeding and surgical re‐exploration, and both excessive blood loss and reoperation for bleeding are associated with increased postoperative mortality and morbidity. 1 , 2 , 3 Therefore, an important aspect of perioperative cardiosurgical care is effective prevention of bleeding and treatment of increased bleeding tendency. Inevitably, surgical trauma leads to postoperative bleeding, even when hemostasis is pursued meticulously. In addition, a significant proportion of postoperative bleeding can be explained by acquired hemostatic defects. 4 , 5 Impaired platelet function, mainly due to the effects of cardiopulmonary bypass and preoperative anti‐platelet drug therapy, is one of the most important hemostatic factors leading to postoperative bleeding. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Platelet transfusions are administered to prevent or treat such bleeding. 9

A possible adverse effect of red blood cell storage duration on clinical outcome in cardiac surgery patients has been studied extensively. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 A possible detrimental effect of storage duration of platelet concentrates on safety and efficacy has been suggested but, thus far, scarcely supported by quantitative clinical evidence. 14 In vitro studies show that, during storage, platelets undergo multiple changes in structure and function, collectively known as “platelet storage lesion.” 15 , 16 , 17 Such damage due to storage may reduce hemostatic capacity of the platelet concentrate. 18 Most clinical studies addressing this subject have been performed in non‐bleeding hemato‐oncological patients undergoing transfusions to treat thrombocytopenia. 14 However, whether these findings can be extrapolated to cardiac surgery patients is debatable.

Besides leading to reduced hemostatic capacity, several features of the preparation and storage processes of platelet concentrates lead to platelet activation. 19 Activated platelets might have improved hemostatic effects, but they also show increased CD62P exposure and GPIbα release, which is associated with decreased platelet survival after transfusion. 20 , 21 It is unclear whether platelet activation affects outcomes of cardiac surgery patients, and if so, whether platelet activation has a favorable or detrimental effect in these patients. Another concern is the release and accumulation of bioactive substances, like sCD40L and microparticles, during storage of platelets. 18 , 22 , 23 , 24 These bioactive substances are associated with pro‐inflammatory and thrombotic events. 18 , 25 , 26 , 27

The aim of this hypothesis‐generating study was to explore in cardiac surgery patients whether transfusion of platelet concentrates, stored for more than 3 days, is associated with a higher occurrence of in‐hospital mortality, bleeding, reoperation for bleeding, and infectious, inflammatory, or thromboembolic complications, when compared with platelets with shorter storage duration (PSSD).

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

Study design

We performed this retrospective hypothesis‐generating study in a cohort of adults who underwent cardiac surgery and received one or more platelet transfusions in the Amphia Hospital in Breda, the Netherlands (hospital 1), between January 2005 and December 2017, or in the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC; hospital 2) in Leiden, the Netherlands, between January 2006 and December 2017. We excluded patients who received single‐donor platelets harvested by an apheresis procedure because they are used for specific indications and could thereby influence the results. Decisions regarding patient care were made by the responsible physician. Decisions regarding transfusions were made independently from the storage duration of the concentrates, as the physicians were not aware of and cannot influence the storage time. In the Netherlands, the concentrate is issued by the blood transfusion department according to the “first‐in, first‐out” principle. The medical ethical committees of both hospitals approved the study protocol and granted a waiver for informed consent (reference P14.008).

Platelet product

The studied transfused platelet concentrates were pre‐storage leukoreduced by filtration, prepared using five whole blood‐derived buffy coats collected and produced by Sanquin Blood Bank (Amsterdam, the Netherlands). The platelet concentrates were not irradiated or pathogen inactivated. The platelets were resuspended either in 100% plasma from one of the five whole blood donations (plasma‐platelets) or in 65% platelet additive solution (PAS) and 35% plasma. Two types of PAS were used during the studied period: PAS‐B (a.k.a. PAS‐2/T‐Sol; Baxter (Nivelles, France)) and PAS‐C (a.k.a. PAS‐III /Intersol; Fenwal, a Fresenius company, La Châtre, France). In hospital 1 from January 2005 to December 2012, platelets were stored up to 5 days in PAS‐B, and from December 2012 platelets were stored up to seven days in PAS‐C. In hospital 2, from January 2006 to November 2014, platelets were stored up to 7 days in plasma and from November 2014 they were stored up to 7 days in PAS‐C. Data regarding platelet products were extracted from the national database of Sanquin Blood Bank (eProgesa, Paris, France). Storage time of platelet concentrates was defined as the number of calendar days between the first donation contributing to the pool and the date of transfusion, with the donation date as Day 0. The storage times were classified into two categories: 1–3 days, called PSSD throughout this manuscript, and 4–7 days, called platelets with longer storage duration (PLSD). Patients transfused with multiple platelet concentrates were included if all concentrates were in the same storage time category.

Clinical outcomes

Beforehand we planned to evaluate the following endpoints: blood loss within 12 h after surgery, reoperation for bleeding, stroke, myocardial infarction, infection, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), shock, multi‐organ failure, and in‐hospital mortality. In‐hospital mortality was available in both hospitals, but all other postoperative endpoints were only structurally available in hospital 1. The endpoints from hospital 1 were retrieved from its prospective perioperative clinical data registry, which is fully compliant with the national cardiac surgery data registry. 28 Herein, stroke was defined as a new persistent cerebrovascular event leading to neurologic defects diagnosed by a neurologist. Acute kidney failure was defined as the need for postoperative renal replacement therapy when this was not indicated before and/or an increase in serum creatinine of more than 100%. The diagnosis of postoperative myocardial infarction was made based on either the occurrence of new Q‐waves on the electrocardiogram or ischemic ST‐changes in combination with abnormal postoperative troponin‐T levels (troponin‐T level >0.5 μg/L for coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgery, troponin‐T level >0.8 μg/L for valve surgery and troponin‐T level >1.0 μg/L for combined CABG and valve procedures). Both serum creatinine and troponin‐T were routinely measured in all patients postoperatively. Infection was categorized as pneumonia, mediastinitis, sepsis, and other infections. Diagnosis was stated if relevant organisms were isolated from cultures. SIRS was diagnosed if two or more of the following criteria were present: temperature greater than 38 or less than 36°C; tachypnea (>20 breaths per min) or hypocapnia (pCO2 < 32 mmHg); tachycardia (>90 beats/min); or need for mechanical ventilation and leukocyte count >12 or <4 × 109/L. Multi‐organ failure was defined as simultaneous or sequential dysfunction or failure of two or more organ systems. Shock was identified as a clinical diagnosis made by the attending physician and as registered in the clinical data registry of the hospital. Amount of blood loss was analyzed as being high or low with 1000 mL as the cut‐off point.

Statistical analyses

Main analyses

A statistical analysis plan was prepared and agreed upon before starting the analyses. FMAvH and CC‐D performed the analyses. All authors had full access to the data, and the corresponding author had final responsibility. Continuous variables were described as mean (and standard deviation) or median (and interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables were described as a percentage. The number of missing values per variable is presented in Table S1. Missing postoperative outcome variables were coded as “no,” we assumed the outcome had not occurred. For the preoperative variable EuroSCORE, there were eight patient records with missing values. These values were imputed using the median of the non‐missing. The missing values for the preoperative variables left ventricular (LV) function and recent myocardial infarction were not imputed; in the logistic regression models, missingness was handled as a separate indicator category.

The associations between storage time of the platelet concentrate and the clinical endpoint incidences were evaluated using logistic regression‐derived odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We calculated both unadjusted (uOR) and adjusted ORs (aORs). The variables included in the analysis were selected in advance based on the experts' opinion: EuroSCORE, number of transfused platelet concentrates, age, female sex, LV function, recent myocardial infarction, isolated CABG (yes/no), and hospital. EuroSCORE is a scoring system for the prediction of early mortality in patients undergoing cardiac surgery based on objective risk factors. 29

Kaplan–Meier curves were made in which mortality was compared between patients receiving PSSD and patients receiving PLSD during the first 100 postoperative days. The adjusted Kaplan Meier curves were adjusted using inverse probability weighing. First, we calculated for all patients their predicted estimates of receiving PLSD using a logistic regression model with storage time as dependent variable and all confounders (listed above) as independent variables. Next, weights were assigned to all patients as the inverse of those predicted values. The presented adjusted Kaplan Meier curves are thereby adjusted for the described confounders. 30

Subpopulations

For the analysis in which we studied mortality, we were able to include all patients who had received platelet concentrates at any time during their hospital stay, so both intraoperatively and postoperatively. To evaluate whether the results were similar in different storage media and hospitals, we repeated the analyses stratified by hospital and by storage medium.

For all other endpoints, we restricted the analysis merely to patients who received platelet transfusions intraoperatively, so in the operating theater. Hereby we assured that the transfusion took place before the occurrence of the endpoint.

Sensitivity analyses

To explore the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses in the selection of patients who had received not more than one platelet concentrate during their hospital stay. Also, we repeated the analyses in patients without any missing values for postoperative outcomes (Supporting Information S1).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

The database comprised a total of 33,758 patients, of whom 4317 received one or more platelet transfusions. After exclusion of patients transfused with single‐donor platelets harvested by an apheresis procedure or with a mix of platelets of different storage time categories, 3556 patients were included in the analyses (Figure 1). The baseline characteristics per storage time category are shown in Table 1. A small majority of the patients was male (56%), the mean age was 67 years, and most patients had a good LV function (69%). In total, 2117 patients received transfusion with PLSD and 1439 with PSSD. Most patients 2452/3556 (69%) received only one platelet concentrate during admission (Figure 1).

In‐hospital mortality

Among patients transfused with PLSD 212/2117 (10.0%) died in the hospital, whereas among those transfused with PSSD 109/1439 (7.6%) died in the hospital, with a significant aOR of 1.47 (95% CI: 1.13–1.91) (Table 2). In the stratified analysis (Table 2), the aORs of all subgroups had a direction and magnitude similar to those in the main unstratified analysis. However, in all subgroups, the confidence interval became wider, and in several subgroups, the aOR did not reach significance. However, in the largest subgroup of the plasma‐platelets, the aOR clearly remained significant, with an aOR of 1.88 (1.16–3.03) and a p‐value of .010.

Other outcomes

Patients transfused with PLSD more often experienced blood loss ≥1000 mL (102/285; 35.8%) than patients transfused with PSSD (87/326; 26.7%), aOR 1.74 (95% CI 1.19–2.52). Patients transfused with PLSD more often needed reoperation for bleeding 99/285 (34.7%) than patients transfused with PSSD (87/326; 26.7%) (aOR 1.62, 95% CI 1.12–2.35). There was no clear association between transfusion of PLSD versus PSSD and the occurrence of the other endpoints (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

Among 2452 patients who received one concentrate of platelets, the aOR for in‐hospital mortality was similar; however, it had a broader, non‐significant confidence interval (1.24 95% CI 0.88–1.74) (Table 2). Comparable results were observed for blood loss ≥1000 mL, aOR 1.50 (95% CI 0.94–2.37) and for reoperation for bleeding, aOR 1.95 (95% CI 1.23–3.10) (Table 3). For the other postoperative outcomes, the results of the sensitivity analyses were similar to those of the main analysis.

In the main analysis, missing postoperative outcome variables were coded as “no,” that is, we assumed the outcome had not occurred. To explore the effect of this assumption, we selected the patients who did not have missing values on bleeding complications and repeated the main analysis. The results of this sensitivity analysis were similar to the main analysis, confirming the robustness of our findings (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

The main finding of this study is that, in a cardiac surgery population, transfusion of PLSD was associated with a significantly higher in‐hospital mortality when compared to transfusion of PSSD. Moreover, in patients transfused with PLSD, both reoperation for bleeding and blood loss ≥1000 mL in the first 12 h after surgery were observed significantly more frequently when compared to patients transfused with PSSD.

We also investigated the potential influence of different platelet storage fluids and the two different hospitals. In the stratified analysis, all subgroups yielded ORs with similar direction and magnitude. However, in all subgroups, the confidence interval was wider, and the OR in several subgroups failed to reach significance. This might be explained by the fact that these subgroups obligatory contain a smaller population, lacking the power to reach significance, or it might indicate that the association between longer storage duration and hospital mortality does not occur in these subgroups. It is plausible that the influence of storage duration on a platelet concentrate differs per storage medium and might therefore give different clinical outcomes, but it was not the aim of our study to determine if that is the case. The OR of hospital 1, not reaching significance in the stratified analyses, may be explained by the different patient profile (academic vs. non‐academic) resulting in a different case mix of the hospitals and/or by the storage media the hospitals used and/or by the fact that these subgroups are smaller than the population of the main analysis, resulting in a lack of statistical power.

The decision to administer platelets was made by the physicians who were not aware of storage time and could not influence the storage time of the platelet concentrates administered to the patient. Consequently, one can assume that assignment to PSSD or PLSD was not dependent on the clinical condition of the patient and was performed by physicians who were blinded to platelet storage duration. Platelets are issued according to the “first‐in, first‐out” principle. Therefore, the storage time can be influenced by the number of platelets a patient receives, because the more concentrates a patient receives at once, the bigger the chance that patient will get PSSD at that moment (as these concentrates lie in the back of the shelf). When a patient receives only one platelet transfusion, the chance is probably higher that the patient gets PLSD. Hereby, there might be an association between the risk profile and prognosis of patients, their tendency to receive more platelet concentrate units and the storage duration of the platelets. To minimize this potentially confounding factor, our analyses were adjusted for the number of platelet transfusions administered. Even if residual confounding remained despite the applied adjusted analyses, we would expect it to result in an underestimation rather than an overestimation of the actual effect, because the patients with a less favorable prognosis were more likely to receive PSSD than the patients with a better prognosis. The analyses were also adjusted for EuroSCORE, age, sex, LV function, recent myocardial infarction, isolated CABG, and hospital. By adjusting for EuroSCORE, factors that are considered relevant for prognosis in cardiac surgery are taken into account.

The literature on the association between platelet storage time and clinical outcomes after thoracic surgery is scarce. In our study, hospital mortality was higher than in the general cardiac surgery population, which can be explained by the patient population that was selected. Patients with an indication for platelet transfusion are generally in a worse condition than patients who do not require transfusions. In our analysis we compared PLSD (stored 4–7 days) to PSSD platelets (stored 1–3 days), but the fact that we observed differences between those groups does not necessarily mean that there is a tipping point between storage Day 3 and 4. Possibly there is another tipping point or there is a more gradual deterioration of the concentrates. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study suggesting a possible negative effect of longer platelet storage time on clinical outcome. Only one other study by Welsby et al. investigated the association between platelet storage time and clinical outcomes in 2578 cardiac surgery patients and reported no significant association between storage of platelets and clinical outcomes. 31 The discrepancy between the findings of this study and our findings might be attributed to the patient populations and type of platelet concentrates. The other study included only non‐emergent CABG patients transfused with single‐donor platelets harvested with an apheresis procedure with a maximum storage of 5 days, while our study included both elective and non‐elective cardiac surgery patients (only 39.1% isolated CABG) transfused with pooled buffy‐coat platelets with a maximum storage of up to 7 days. Hence, our study population contains more high‐risk patients who might also be more vulnerable for the potential negative effects of transfusion with PLSD. This difference in risk profile of the populations is also reflected in the hospital mortality (5.2%) observed in the other study, that is lower than the mortality we observed (9.0%). Furthermore, it is possible that the nature and magnitude of the “platelet storage lesion” is different in single‐donor platelets harvested with an apheresis procedure than in pooled platelets, especially, when platelets are stored for 2 days longer.

We observed that mortality differences occurred around Day 6 and were mostly discerned in the first 20 days after surgery. Multiple pathways may lead to postoperative mortality, and perioperative bleeding complications are likely to translate to patient‐centered outcome differences. We did not specifically study the pathways and concurrent timeline following transfusion, but hemodynamic changes, reoperation, pulmonary complications, and inflammatory effects (like SIRS) may all contribute to clinically significant differences even when subtle differences did not reach significance in the overall population.

Our findings suggest that in‐hospital mortality, postoperative blood loss >1000 mL, and reoperation for bleeding occur more frequently following transfusion of PLSD when compared to PSSD.

A possible explanation for our findings could be that PLSD are less effective in preventing bleeding or arresting ongoing bleeding, resulting in an increase in postoperative blood loss and a more frequent need for surgical re‐exploration, but this cannot be determined based on our study. Diminished platelet function as explanation for our observations seems plausible when regarding the discoveries in earlier studies; longer storage times resulted in increasing platelet activation, decreasing platelet responsiveness and declining platelet viability and function, together called the “platelet storage lesion.” 14 , 15 , 17 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 In addition to the possibly reduced efficacy of PLSD compared to PSSD, other factors that could influence in‐hospital mortality could be a higher risk of transfusion‐related acute lung injury (TRALI), transfusion‐associated circulatory overload (TACO), other transfusion reactions, or other complications that were associated with a longer storage duration of transfused platelets. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Our database did not contain data about these type of complications, so we could not confirm nor disprove this hypothesis.

A limitation of our study is that it spans a rather long time period in which there have been changes concerning the storage medium and maximum storage duration of platelet concentrates, in addition to changes in the field of cardiac surgery and perioperative blood management. This may have led to a less homogeneous study population and may have diluted the results. Furthermore, we are aware that, despite several efforts that have been made, bias and confounding cannot be ruled out completely due to the retrospective design of this study. By performing the stratified analyses, adjusting the logistic regression for potential confounders and the sensitivity analyses, we endeavored to diminish the possible influence of bias and residual confounding. Thus, the associations we observed should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating and warrant a call for further study into this matter to see whether these observations are confirmed.

Our study leads to the hypothesis that transfusion of PLSD more frequently leads to blood loss ≥1000 mL in the first 12 h after surgery and reoperation for bleeding, and moreover to a higher in‐hospital mortality when compared to transfusion with PSSD. These findings warrant further research, preferably executed in a RCT, to verify these findings. This would be an important step to determine whether a change in our daily clinical practice is indicated in order to improve the outcome of patients receiving platelet transfusions. Preparations for an RCT are currently in progress.
