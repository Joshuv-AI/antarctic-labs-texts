Arthroscopic shoulder surgery for gouty long head of biceps tendinitis: a case report
Authors: Xingrui Huang, Jianhua Wang, Zhigang Xiong, Qirong Dong, Bo Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe objective of the case report is to analyze the clinical manifestations and imaging features of gouty long head of biceps tendinitis, and to summarize the methods and effects of shoulder arthroscopic surgery.Clinical presentation and interventionThe clinical data of a 39-year-old Han Chinese female with gouty long head of the biceps tendinitis was retrospectively analyzed, and the clinical manifestations, imaging features, and diagnosis and treatment were analyzed. The patient presented with pain and limited movement of right shoulder joint. Computed tomography showed irregular high-density shadows above the glenoid and adjacent to the coracoid process of the right shoulder. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed superior labrum anterior and posterior injury with edema in the upper recess and axillary sac. After arthroscopic surgery, the “tofu residue” tissue of the long head of the biceps was removed, and the postoperative pathological examination proved that it was gout stone.ConclusionGouty long head of the biceps tendinitis is a rare disease. Arthroscopic surgery can probe the structural lesions of shoulder cavity in all aspects, improve the surgical accuracy, and reduce the trauma.

Introduction

Long head of biceps tendinitis is a common orthopedic injury. It is mainly caused by repeated friction of the long head of biceps tendon in the humerus intertubercular sulcus, resulting in local inflammation, degeneration, and adhesion of the tendon sheath, which eventually lead to serious physiological dysfunction of the tendon. The clinical manifestations of the patient are pain and discomfort in front of the shoulder, limited mobility of the shoulder joint, and so on, which can cause serious impact on daily life and work. Gout is a metabolic disease caused by long-term purine metabolism disorders. Gout stones usually deposit in joints, tissues around joints, and under the skin. Gouty bicipital long head tendinitis is rare in clinic, usually caused by gout stones deposited in the glenoid tubercle of the long head tendon. The clinical data of a case of gouty long head of biceps tendinitis treated by arthroscopic operation were retrospectively analyzed to provide reference for clinical treatment of this type of patient.

Case report

The patient, a 39-year-old Han Chinese female, was admitted to the Orthopedics and Traumatology Department of Wujiang District Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine in Suzhou City for 1 week due to right shoulder joint pain and limited movement for 18 months. The patient presented with front pain of right shoulder joint without obvious inducement 18 months ago that was sometimes good or bad and was occasionally accompanied by dysfunction, with no local redness, swelling, and fever. The pain suddenly worsened after heavy weight 1 week prior to presentation, and she came to the hospital for treatment. Upon physical examination, we found acute pain appearance, no swelling of the right shoulder joint, no “square shoulder” or other deformity, obvious tenderness of the upper front of the shoulder joint, obvious restriction of active shoulder movement, increased pain on passive activity, active forward bending of the right shoulder joint about 70°, internal rotation of the thumb to L4, external rotation of 15°, and abduction of 45°. The right shoulder joint passively extended about 80°, the thumb internally rotated to L3, the thumb externally rotated 20°, and the abduction was 60°. Job’s sign (+), Neer’s sign (+), Belleypush sign (+), O’Brein sign (+), Haukin’s sign (−) and Speed (+), right peripheral blood flow, sensation, and movement were normal, and the patient was previous healthly. After admission, computed tomography (CT) scan of the right shoulder joint showed an irregular high-density shadow above the glenoid and adjacent to the coracoid process of the right shoulder, indicating possible avulsion fracture (Fig. 1). The MRI of the right shoulder joint suggested a superior labrum anterior and posterior (SLAP) injury, excessive fluid accumulation in the glenohumeral joint space, and edema in the upper recess and axillary sac (Fig. 2A–C). X-ray images of the right shoulder joint showed that a high-density shadow (0.5 × 1.0 cm) was visible above the glenoid of the right shoulder joint (Fig. 2D). Laboratory examination showed that the white blood cell count was 10.14 × 109/L. The neutrophil percentage was 88.21%, lymphocyte percentage was 7.9%, and blood uric acid was 397 μmol/L (normal value: 120–-20), and the rest of the test results were normal. All preoperative examinations were completed, no contraindications were confirmed, and right shoulder arthroscopic exploration was performed after full communication with the patient. Extensive hyperemia was observed in the synovial cavity of the right shoulder joint during the operation. In addition, white calcium-like material deposits were observed on the synovial and glenohumeral articular cartilaginous surfaces. It was found that there was an accumulation of white lime powder that was 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm in size at the attachment of the upper recess and the insertion point of the biceps long tendon (Fig. 3a). After planing and cleaning, a tear of about 0.3 cm was found at the junction of the upper recess and the insertion point of the biceps long tendon. Planing was repeated, and it was washed repeatedly until all the white material was removed. A plasma knife head was used for electric coagulation and contraction of the breach. The glenoid lip at the attachment point of the long head tendon of the biceps was checked to be stable without obvious relaxation, and other tissue structures were investigated without abnormality. After the subacromial space was examined, no obvious white crystal deposition was found on the subacromial structure, deltoid bursae, and other synovial surfaces. Finally, the joint cavity was repeatedly rinsed to finish the operation. After operation, the normal scan of the right shoulder joint found that the irregular high-density shadow above the right shoulder glenoid disappeared (Fig. 3C). After the operation, the pain of the right shoulder joint was significantly relieved. The patient was instructed to perform functional exercise of the right shoulder joint and was given oral allopurinol (Beijing Yanjing Pharmaceutical Co., LTD., H11020432; 50 mg once or twice a day; treatment for 1 month), sodium bicarbonate [Yuanda Pharmaceutical (China) Co., LTD., Sinopath H42021753, 1 tablet at a time, 3 times a day oral, treatment for 1 month] comprehensive uric-acid-lowering treatment. At 2 months after surgery, the patient’s right shoulder pain disappeared completely, and the range of motion returned to normal. Plain scan reconstruction of the right shoulder joint revealed that the irregular high-density shadow previously visible above the glenoid had disappeared, further confirming the effectiveness of surgical treatment.

Discussion

Gout is a typical metabolic disease that results in abnormally high uric acid levels in the body due to a long-term disorder or disorder of purine metabolism. When the concentration of uric acid exceeds its saturation point in the blood, urate will crystallize and deposit in joints, surrounding soft tissues, and subcutaneously, causing a series of pathological changes [1–4]. Gout not only destroys the joints but also may trigger nephritis, urinary calculi, and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and sometimes even cause systemic inflammation. Gout stones, a typical pathological feature of gout, usually form in the subcutaneous tissue near the joint capsule or at the end of the limb. These turgout stones are essentially composed of foreign body granulomas and urate crystals of different sizes, neutrophils, giant cells, epithelioid cells, and fibrous tissues [5, 6]. It is easy to cause damage to the joint cartilage, which can lead to joint pain and dysfunction. For joint damage caused by gouty stone, it is often difficult to achieve satisfactory results with simple conservative treatment. Studies have shown that surgical treatment of gout and its associated joint problems can achieve better curative effect [7, 8].

SLAP refers to the anterior and posterior tears of the upper lip of the glenoid margin, affecting the attachment point of the long head tendon of the biceps muscle. SLAP is one of the causes of chronic shoulder pain or shoulder instability. However, the injury mechanism of SLAP is complex and variable, and the clinical manifestations lack specificity. Currently, diagnosis of SLAP faces many challenges, and imaging studies are limited due to insufficient sensitivity. MRI is helpful not only in diagnosing SLAP injury but also in identifying other possible shoulder lesions, such as rotator cuff tears, calcifying myositis, or scapulohumeral periarthritis. However, MRI diagnosis of SLAP injury has a certain false positive rate, especially due to the possible presence of normal anatomical variations similar to SLAP injury in the upper pelvic lip [9, 10]. Arthroscopy is the “gold standard” for diagnosing SLAP injuries, but it is somewhat invasive and cannot be used as a routine examination. As a result, diagnosis of SLAP is difficult in orthopedic research, and it is easily confused with other joint diseases.

In this case, the patient was a young woman with intermittent pain in the right shoulder joint for nearly a year and a half, and no discomfort in other joints. Based on the imaging and clinical findings, the initial consideration was a SLAP injury in the right shoulder. However, during surgery, it was found that there were large amounts of white crystals deposited in the joint cavity on the long head of the biceps tendon insertion, synovial membrane, and cartilage surface, which ruled out the possibility of SLAP injury. Combined with the pathological examination results after surgery, the final diagnosis was gouty right shoulder long head of biceps tendinitis. Therefore, even if the blood uric acid test results are negative during clinical diagnosis and treatment, the possibility of gouty arthritis cannot be completely ruled out [11]. It should be combined with clinical manifestations, imaging examination, necessary surgical exploration results, and even postoperative pathological examination to determine the diagnosis and timely treatment. We used shoulder arthroscopic surgery to treat this case, and the results showed that the operation could successfully remove the gouty stone that affected the joint function, and the postoperative drug treatment could effectively control the uric acid level in the patient, thus reducing the joint symptoms and restoring the joint function. It is suggested that arthroscopic exploration can be used for the diagnosis of shoulder joint diseases with unclear diagnosis. For gouty arthritis patients, when conservative treatment is not good, surgical removal of gouty stone can be considered to improve the treatment effect.

However, the follow-up period for this study was slightly shorter. Mainly because the patient felt that the postoperative effect was very good, pain completely disappeared, and the joint motion returned to normal, she was not willing to come to the hospital for follow-up consultation. This is indeed a limitation of our study, which might have been more perfect if the follow-up period had been longer.

Conclusion

Gouty long head of biceps tendinitis causes shoulder joint pain and limited movement. The symptoms are similar to those of other shoulder diseases, and it is easy for it to be misdiagnosed or even missed. For these diseases, arthroscopic exploration should be considered when there is no obvious effect after routine diagnosis and treatment. Arthroscopic technology not only facilitates accurate diagnosis but also provides minimally invasive treatment options that facilitate rapid recovery.
