Not all older adults are viewed alike: feeling thermometer ratings toward older-adult subgroups in Japan
Authors: Yuho Shimizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study examined whether feeling thermometer ratings toward older adults vary across older-adult subgroups and how such ratings are positioned relative to other common prejudice targets. Using an online sample of 520 Japanese adults aged 18–64, we assessed feeling thermometer ratings (0 = coldest, 100 = warmest) for 14 targets: nursing-home, on-deathbed, handicapped, solitary, senile, on-welfare, and grumpy older adults, as well as immigrants and refugees, foreign tourists, sexual minorities, racial minorities, and persons with mental, physical, or intellectual disabilities. We also tested whether these evaluations differed by participants’ age group (younger and middle-aged adults).ResultsFeeling thermometer ratings differed significantly across targets, indicating that older adults were not evaluated as a homogeneous category. Relatively lower ratings were observed for some subgroups, particularly on-welfare and grumpy older adults, suggesting that evaluations of older adults may vary depending on the specific attributes associated with the target. Ratings also differed by participant age group: middle-aged adults tended to report warmer evaluations than younger adults across most of the targets, including all older-adult subgroups, although these differences should be interpreted cautiously given the small interaction effect.

Introduction

Ageism toward older adults has social consequences across a wide range of domains, including employment, healthcare, and everyday social interactions. Previous research has often positioned older adults as a group perceived as “warm but incompetent,” characterizing them as targets of ambivalent stereotype [1]. However, this perspective often treats “older adults” as a single, homogeneous category and therefore fails to adequately capture the heterogeneity within this population. Older adults differ substantially in such characteristics as health status and economic circumstances, and social evaluations of them may likewise vary rather than remain uniform.

A recent cross-cultural study addressed this issue by examining stereotypes toward multiple older-adult subgroups [2]. That study demonstrated that older adults are associated with diverse images and that certain subgroups (e.g., nursing-home, on-deathbed, handicapped, senile, and on-welfare older adults) are consistently characterized by low warmth and low competence across cultural contexts. However, such research has mainly examined stereotype content, that is, beliefs about the traits associated with older-adult subgroups, such as warmth and competence. These beliefs are important, but they are not identical to affective evaluations. A feeling thermometer asks respondents to indicate how warmly or coldly they feel toward a target group and is commonly used as a global measure of affective favorability or unfavorability toward social groups, typically rated on a scale from 0 to 100 [3]. Thus, whereas stereotype-content measures assess what people believe a group is like, feeling thermometer ratings capture the affective component of prejudice. Because stereotype content can shape affective reactions and related behavioral tendencies toward groups [4], measuring feeling thermometer ratings toward older-adult subgroups can clarify whether subgroup differences in stereotypes are also reflected in affective favorability or unfavorability.

Furthermore, to understand attitudes toward older-adult subgroups, it is also important to situate them in comparison with other targets of prejudice. In Japan, exclusionary attitudes toward immigrants, refugees, and foreign tourists have attracted increasing attention in recent years [5, 6], while sexual minorities, racial minorities, and persons with disabilities have also been widely examined as targets of prejudice, stereotyping, and discrimination [7]. Examining older-adult subgroups alongside these groups makes it possible to more clearly identify how affective evaluations of older adults are positioned relative to other social categories.

Therefore, this study measured feeling thermometer ratings toward 14 target groups in a sample of Japanese adults. Regarding older adults, we specifically focused on seven subgroups that were evaluated particularly negatively in the Japanese data reported by the previous study [2] and compared the subgroups with other social targets. The aims of this study were threefold: first, to examine whether feeling thermometer ratings differ across older-adult subgroups; second, to investigate how negatively these subgroups are evaluated relative to other targets of prejudice; and third, to explore whether these patterns of evaluation vary across participants’ age groups. In doing so, this study provides a basis for reconceptualizing ageism not as a uniform attitude toward “older adults” in general, but as a selective affective response to specific images of older adults.

Methods

Power analysis

Assuming a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with feeling thermometer ratings as the dependent variable, participants’ age group (younger vs. middle-aged; between-participants factor), and target group (14 targets; within-participants factor) as the independent variables, we calculated the required sample size (u = 13, α = 0.05, 1–β = 0.80). Assuming a medium effect size (partial η2 = 0.06 [8]), the total of required sample size was N = 291. Because this value was an approximation calculated using the pwr package in R [9], we decided to recruit a somewhat larger sample.

Participants

Participants were recruited through Intage, a major survey company in Japan. Eligible participants were Japanese adults aged 18–64 years. Participants aged 65 years or older were not recruited because the present study aimed to examine evaluations of older-adult subgroups among non-older adults and to avoid overlap between respondents and the target age category. In this study, age 65 was used as an operational cutoff for older adulthood because Japanese official statistics commonly report the older population as those aged 65 years and over [10].

The sample consisted of 520 Japanese adults aged 18 to 64 years (M = 42.87 years, SD = 11.95). There were 266 men and 254 women. For the exploratory age-group comparison, participants were divided into a young adult group aged 18–39 years (n = 265) and a middle-aged group aged 40–64 years (n = 255). The cutoff at 40 years was selected to distinguish adults in earlier adulthood from non-older adults who were closer to older adulthood. This grouping was also broadly consistent with age classifications used in Japanese adult health research, in which adults aged 20–39 years are often distinguished from those aged 40–64 years [11]. However, this classification was operational and was not intended to imply fixed or universal boundaries between young and middle-aged adulthood. The study was conducted with ethics approval from the institution to which the first author was affiliated (The University of Tokyo; approval number: UTSP-24030).

Procedure

The study was conducted online using a web-based survey platform administered by Intage. Participants first read an explanation of the study and provided informed consent to participate. They then responded to a feeling thermometer item toward each of the 14 target groups listed below. The order of presentation of the targets was randomized. Finally, participants reported their age and sex.

For the older-adult subgroups, the target consisted of the seven categories that received the most negative evaluations (i.e., low competence and low warmth) in the Japanese data reported by the previous study [2]. Specifically, these were nursing-home, on-deathbed, handicapped, solitary, senile, on-welfare, and grumpy older adults. These labels followed the category names used in the previous study [2].

In addition to these groups, we included immigrants and refugees and foreign tourists, both of which have increasingly become targets of prejudice research in Japan [5, 6]. We also included sexual minorities, racial minorities, persons with mental disabilities, persons with physical disabilities, and persons with intellectual disabilities, all of which have long been examined in prejudice research [7]. The data and analysis code were made publicly available on the Open Science Framework (OSF) repository (https://osf.io/ezhyq/ (public version, not anonymized version for peer-review)).

Feeling thermometer

Participants were instructed to indicate their feelings toward each target by assigning a score, where 0 represented the coldest feeling and 100 represented the warmest feeling [3]. In this study, the scores were interpreted as a global index of affective favorability or unfavorability toward each target group.

Figure 1 shows the feeling thermometer ratings toward each target among the younger and middle-aged groups. To examine whether the ratings differed by participants’ age group and target group, we conducted a two-way ANOVA with feeling thermometer ratings as the dependent variable, participants’ age group (younger vs. middle-aged; between-participants factor), and target group (14 targets; within-participants factor) as the independent variables. The results revealed significant main effects of participants’ age group (F(1, 518) = 18.83, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.04), and target group (F(13, 6734) = 83.08, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.14), as well as a significant interaction between participants’ age group and target group (F(13, 6734) = 3.00, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.01). In addition, the results remained largely unchanged when the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied to address the assumption of sphericity.

To clarify how older-adult subgroups were positioned relative to the other social targets, Holm-adjusted multiple comparisons among the 14 target groups were conducted within each age group (Appendix B on the OSF). Grumpy older adults received the lowest mean rating among all targets and were rated significantly lower than all other target groups. On-welfare older adults were also rated relatively low: they were rated lower than the remaining five older-adult subgroups, sexual minorities, and persons with physical disabilities. In contrast, nursing-home, on-deathbed, handicapped, solitary, and senile older adults received ratings in the mid-to-higher range. These results indicate that colder affective evaluations were concentrated in specific older-adult subgroups rather than applying uniformly to older-adult subgroups.

Multiple comparisons using Holm’s method also showed that, for 11 of the 14 target groups, excluding immigrants and refugees, foreign tourists, and racial minorities, feeling thermometer ratings were higher among the middle-aged group than among the younger group (Table 2). However, because the effect size of the interaction was small, these multiple-comparison results should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

The first implication of this study is that older adults should not be treated as a monolithic target, as feeling thermometer ratings may differ across their subgroups. Older adults have often been discussed as targets perceived as “warm but incompetent,” but our findings suggest that such an average-level understanding is insufficient. Future discussions of ageism should therefore move beyond “older adults” as a general category and clarify what kinds of older adults are being assumed.

The second implication of this study is that the focus of negative feelings may be directed not toward the category of older adults itself, but toward images of older adults with specific attributes. More specifically, feeling thermometer ratings were lower toward grumpy older adults, who are assumed to have interpersonal problems, and toward on-welfare older adults, who are more likely to be seen as dependent on others or on younger generations. The low rating for grumpy older adults should be interpreted cautiously because the label itself contains a negative interpersonal connotation. This result does not indicate that participants evaluated these older adults as low in stereotype warmth; rather, it indicates that participants reported colder affective evaluations toward a subgroup label implying interpersonal difficulty. Similarly, the low rating for on-welfare older adults may reflect affective reactions to perceived dependency or welfare use, which is related to prescriptive age stereotypes concerning older adults’ consumption of social resources [12]. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that negative feelings intensify whenever older adults deviate from stereotypical expectations. Because the present study examined only selected older-adult subgroups, many of which contained negative or impairment-related connotations, the results more cautiously suggest that colder affective evaluations may be especially likely for older-adult images that combine age with negatively valenced, dependency-related, or interpersonally difficult attributes.

The Japanese context is important for interpreting these findings. Japan is a highly aged society: the 2025 Annual Report on the Ageing Society reported that 29.3% of the population was aged 65 years or older and that Japan had the highest ageing rate in the world [10]. In this context, public images of older adults may be closely tied to concerns about social security, welfare, care, and intergenerational resource allocation. The relatively low ratings for on-welfare older adults may therefore reflect reactions to perceived dependency on public support, rather than to older age alone. Similarly, the low ratings for grumpy older adults suggest that negative affect may also be shaped by everyday interpersonal expectations. From a global health perspective, these findings suggest that ageism-reduction efforts should be culturally and institutionally contextualized. However, these interpretations require direct cross-cultural examination.

In this study, the middle-aged group showed higher feeling thermometer ratings than the younger group across all older-adult subgroups. This pattern is consistent with previous research suggesting that middle-aged adults tend to view older adults more positively than younger adults do [13]. In contrast, no age-group differences were found for immigrants and refugees, foreign tourists, or racial minorities. This suggests that age differences do not emerge uniformly across all social targets, but may instead vary depending on the nature of the target category.

The practical implication of this study lies in demonstrating the limitations of treating ageism as a unitary attitude toward older adults in general. In fact, feeling thermometer ratings were particularly low for older adults perceived as having interpersonal problems and for those seen as highly dependent. This suggests that, in practice and education aimed at reducing ageism, general messages such as “older adults are also competent” may be insufficient because they may fail to address affective evaluations of older adults who do not fit such portrayals. In addition, by subdividing both older adults and persons with disabilities into multiple subgroups and measuring feeling thermometer ratings toward each, this study also provides useful baseline data for future research on ageism and disability prejudice.

Limitations

This study has five major limitations. First, the only measure used in this study was the feeling thermometer, which primarily captured affective evaluations of each target. Therefore, the study did not directly assess other aspects of prejudice, such as stereotypes consisting of competence and warmth or tendencies to avoid contact.

Second, the older-adult subcategories used in this study were selected from subgroups that had been evaluated relatively negatively in previous research [2]. Accordingly, our findings cannot be generalized to attitudes toward other subgroups, such as healthy, active, or employed older adults. Moreover, some labels, such as grumpy, senile, and on-deathbed older adults, contained overtly negative or impairment-related connotations. These connotations may have lowered feeling thermometer ratings independently of the age category itself. Thus, the present findings should be interpreted as affective responses to specific older-adult images that combine age with other attributes, rather than as negative feelings toward older adults as a whole. Future research should compare negatively valenced, neutral, and positively valenced older-adult subgroups.

Third, the older-adult subgroups simultaneously included multiple attributes, such as age category, health status, economic status, and interpersonal characteristics. As a result, it is not possible to clearly determine whether the low feeling thermometer ratings observed in this study reflected negative evaluations of being older per se, or negative reactions to attributes such as welfare receipt, personality, or cognitive functioning.

Fourth, this study recruited younger and middle-aged adults but not older adults because the primary aim was to examine how non-older adults evaluate older-adult subgroups while avoiding overlap between respondents and the target age category. However, this design cannot determine whether similar affective patterns are observed among older adults themselves, whose evaluations may involve self-relevant attitudes or identification with older adulthood. Future research should compare evaluations across younger, middle-aged, and older age groups.

Finally, because the feeling thermometer was a single-item measure, it could not fully distinguish affective liking or disliking from perceived familiarity, relational closeness, or actual contact with members of the target group. Future research should combine feeling thermometer ratings with separate measures of contact experience, stereotypes, and behavioral tendencies.

Taken together, these points suggest that future research needs to examine the structure of prejudice toward older-adult subgroups in a more multifaceted way by combining multiple measures rather than relying solely on feeling thermometer ratings. In addition, because this study was a cross-sectional survey of an online adult sample in Japan, future research should include comparative studies involving more diverse age groups and cultural contexts, as well as longitudinal studies tracking how evaluations of older-adult images change with aging [13]. Accumulating such evidence will make it possible to understand ageism more precisely, not as a uniform attitude toward older adults in general, but as a selective negative reaction to particular images of older adults.
