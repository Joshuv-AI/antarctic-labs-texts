Subjective well-being mediates the association between caregiver ability and psychological resilience in postoperative older patients with hip fractures
Authors: Yuan Liu, Minyan Ju, Jingjing Jiang, Mengmeng Wang, Wenting Lin, Li He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe postsurgical rehabilitation process of older patients with hip fractures is prolonged. During rehabilitation, good psychological resilience aids patients in maintaining an optimistic attitude and enhancing their ability to deal with the associated stress. However, the caregiver ability on the psychological resilience of patients is not clear. This study aimed to examine the associated with the caregiver ability on the psychological resilience and the subjective well-being of patients.MethodsThis study is a cross-sectional study and was conducted using a random sampling method. This study recruited 148 older patients with hip fractures. A cross-sectional survey was conducted using the Family Caregiver Ability Inventory, the General Well-Being Scale, and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale. The data were subjected to descriptive statistical analysis, independent sample t-test, correlation analysis, and linear regression analysis. The mediation effect was tested using the PROCESS Model 4.ResultsThe caregiver ability was positively correlated with the psychological resilience and subjective well-being of patients. Linear regression analysis revealed that childlessness, out-of-pocket medical insurance payment method, complications, and higher education level were the main factors influencing the subjective well-being and psychological resilience of patients. Additionally, the subjective well-being of patients affected their psychological resilience. Bootstrap analysis revealed a small but statistically significant indirect association: subjective well-being partially accounted for the cross-sectional relationship between caregiver ability and psychological resilience, explaining approximately 7.3% of the total association.ConclusionDuring postoperative rehabilitation of older patients with hip fractures, psychosocial support must be provided to patients in addition to medical care. Additionally, the caregiver ability must be improved. Furthermore, the psychological resilience of patients must be improved by enhancing their subjective well-being. Comprehensive intervention strategies should be developed to promote the psychological adaptation of patients after surgery and improve their mental health status.Keywords: Hip fracture, Ability to care, Psychological resilience, Subjective well-being

Introduction

Osteoporotic fractures, especially hip fractures, are one of the leading causes of disability and death among the older population [1, 2]. The global incidence of osteoporosis-related hip fractures is expected to increase in the future, resulting in enhanced healthcare and social security costs [3, 4] and social burden. Hip fractures can affect the mental health of older patients, contributing to the development of negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression [5]. The consequences of negative emotions in older patients with disability are multifaceted, adversely affecting the quality of life and physical health and increasing suicidal tendencies [6]. The caregivers assume the responsibility of caring for the older during the recovery period after hip fracture surgery. Family caregivers experience high levels of care burden, financial pressure, and difficulties coping with work and nursing tasks due to the need to simultaneously handle multiple aspects (such as patient care, examination, and work) for a prolonged duration [7, 8].

Care capacity refers to the ability of family caregivers to utilize relevant medical and social resources to provide services for the older [9]. In addition to improving the health status and quality of life of the older, good caregiver ability can contribute to the mental health of the older. Subjective well-being refers to the overall and subjective positive evaluation that an individual makes of their quality of life based on their own standards. It is not merely about short-term happiness, but rather a relatively stable and comprehensive psychological state, typically reflecting an individual’s satisfaction with life and emotional balance. The subjective well-being of individuals is dependent on the positive experiences of life. Additionally, subjective well-being, which is the major life goal for individuals, is considered one of the important indicators for evaluating the quality of life. Negative emotions are negatively correlated with subjective well-being. Various diseases invoke negative emotions, such as helplessness, fear, and depression, adversely affecting subjective well-being [10]. Thus, both external and internal factors affect subjective well-being. The external factors include social support [11]. Social support indicates receiving support (including emotional support) from various social groups to mitigate the negative consequences of adverse events or environments. Caregivers, who are the main companions living with the older after hip fracture surgery for a prolonged duration, are the main source of social support for older patients. Thus, the caregiver ability may affect the subjective well-being of the older post-hip fracture surgery.

Resilience refers to the dynamic process that enables individuals to rapidly recover and adapt to major adverse experiences. Thus, resilience is a positive psychological resource that aids in dealing with pressure, difficulty, setbacks, and other maladaptive situations. Previous studies have demonstrated that psychological resilience can improve confidence in overcoming the disease and consequently decrease the levels of anxiety and depression [12]. In this cross-sectional analysis, higher psychological resilience was indirectly associated with better outcomes in older patients after hip fracture surgery.

Currently, most studies have focused on one-way, individual-level indirect associations. The indirect association involving the interaction of caregiver ability and patient psychological resilience from a dyadic perspective has not been previously evaluated. This study analyzed the correlation between the subjective well-being, psychological status, and resilience of patients who have undergone hip fracture surgery and the caregiver ability. The findings of this study will provide a scientific basis for improving the quality of life of older patients after hip fracture surgery and reducing the social burden.

Methods

Patients

This study adopted simple random sampling to select elderly patients who had undergone hip fracture surgery and were hospitalized in the Orthopedics Department of Jiangnan University Affiliated Hospital from March 2022 to March 2025 and met the inclusion criteria. The sampling frame was the list of all hospitalized patients meeting the criteria during the same period (a total of 426 cases); random numbers were generated using SPSS software, and 152 patients were randomly selected for the study. A total of 152 questionnaires were distributed, and 148 valid questionnaires were retrieved, with an effective response rate of 97.4%. Finally, 148 patients were included.

This study is a cross-sectional study and was conducted using a random sampling method. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged ≥ 60 years; conscious patients who can cooperate and complete the questionnaire; patients who meet the diagnostic criteria of hip fracture and conditions for undergoing surgery (preoperative blood routine, coagulation, and liver and kidney function indices are within the surgical tolerance range).

The exclusion criteria were as: patients aged < 60 years; patients with serious underlying diseases, such as cardiopulmonary dysfunction, liver and kidney dysfunction, malignant tumor, or coagulation dysfunction, and low surgical tolerance, as well as those who are unable to undergo surgery or safely survive the perioperative period; patients with pathological fractures caused by factors, such as osteosarcoma; patients with mental illness before surgery or exhibit the onset of mental illness at the time of fracture. Data with a missing rate exceeding 10% were excluded.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was estimated based on the primary aim of testing indirect associations using bias-corrected bootstrap methods. A conservative rule of thumb for regression-based path analysis suggests at least 50 + 8k participants, where k is the number of predictors; with 6 predictors, this yields a minimum of 98 participants. Considering a potential attrition rate of approximately 20%, the target sample size was set at ≥ 118. The final sample of 148 older patients with hip fracture therefore provided adequate power for the planned cross-sectional indirect association analyses.

General information

The general information included demographic data (such as gender, age, education level, place of residence, marital status, children, income, payment method of expenses, and the relationship between main caregivers and patients) and clinical data (complicated with conditions, such as diabetes, hypertension, and complications). Complications include but are not limited to pleural effusion, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, pulmonary encephalopathy, pericardial effusion, emaciation, and infection. One month after the surgery, the overall information of these patients was collected.

Data collection process

The data for this study were collected from elderly patients with hip fractures and their caregivers. All data were collected in the hospital ward one month after the surgery, when the patients’ vital signs were stable, there were no severe postoperative complications, their consciousness was clear, and they had the ability to communicate. The data were collected through one-on-one on-site guidance and self-administered questionnaires by the patients and their caregivers. For patients with visual or writing impairments, the researchers, who had received unified training, read out the questionnaire items without guidance and recorded the responses accurately to ensure the independence of the responses. The distribution, guidance, collection, and quality verification of the questionnaires were completed by two orthopedic graduate students. After the data were collected, they were entered by two people, logically verified, and abnormal values were excluded to ensure the quality of the data.

Assessment of caregiver ability

The assessment of the caregiver ability was formulated by American scholars Clark and Rakowski in 1983 to help family caregivers of the older people with chronic diseases improve their care skills. This study assessed the caregiver ability using the Chinese version of the 25-item Family Caregiver Ability Inventory (revised by Lee et al.) that comprised the following domains: adapting to the caregiving role (5 items), adapting to assist (5 items), dealing with personal emotions (5 items), evaluating family and community resources (5 items), and adjusting life to meet care needs (5 items). All items were scored using the 3-point Likert scale with “not difficult” being scored 0. To facilitate the study without affecting the results, the items of the scale were scored in reverse in the data analysis (score 0: “very difficult”; score 2: “not difficult”). The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.786.

Assessment of subjective well-being

The General Well-being Schedule (GWB), developed by the National Center for Health Statistics, was used to evaluate the level of subjective well-being of patients. The 18-item scale comprised six dimensions (Concern about Health, Vigor, Satisfaction and Interest in Life, Depression or Cheerfulness, Control over Emotions and Behavior, and Relaxation vs. Tension/Anxiety). The maximum score on the scale was 120. The score was directly proportional to the level of subjective well-being. GWB can quantify the subjective well-being of patients and provide a scientific basis for developing individualized psychological support and treatment plans. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.812.

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)

CD-RISC was developed by Connor-David-Son [13]. Zhang Jianxin and Yu Xiao et al. (2007) divided the scale dimensions into strength, tenacity, and optimism using the 3-dimension method. This scale comprised 25 items, which were scored using the 5-point Likert scale (0–4). Each item was scored 0–4 points from “never” to “always.” The sum of item scores was the total score of the scale. The total score was in the range of 0–100 points. The CD-RISC total score was directly proportional to the level of resilience. The resilience levels were scored as follows: poor: < 60 points; general: 61–69 points; good: 70–79 points; excellent: ≥ 80 points. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.876.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 28 (IBM SPSS, USA) and PROCESS Model 4. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Before conducting the t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, we performed normality tests and homogeneity of variance tests on each continuous variable. Count data are expressed as frequency and percentage. Means between groups were compared using the t-test. If the sample sizes of the two groups are significantly different or the variances are not homogeneous, then the Mann-Whitney U test should be used. The correlation was analyzed using Pearson reporting. Before conducting the linear regression, a multicollinearity test was performed. The VIF values were all less than 10, which generally indicates that there are no significant collinearity issues among the various factors. Linear regression analysis was used to analyze the mediation effect of caregiver ability on the resilience and subjective well-being of patients after controlling for general data. In the multiple linear regression analysis, nominal variables were encoded using dummy variables, and the continuous variables were directly entered into the model. The bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method in the Process plug-in was used to test the mediation effect. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline data

This study recruited 148 older patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. The characteristics of the patients were as follows: males (n = 68); females (n = 80); relative caregivers (n = 83); and non-relative caregivers (n = 65). The CD-RISC scores of the patients were not significantly different after considering age, gender, residence, family monthly income, marital status, surgical method, hypertension, and diabetes (p ≥ 0.05). However, the CD-RISC scores of patients were significantly different after considering caregiver type, parental status, education level, and medical insurance payment method (p < 0.05). Patients with non-relative caregivers, lower education levels, no children, and self-paid medical care exhibited decreased CD-RISC scores, indicating that these patients had poor psychological resilience (Table 1). The scores of CD-RISC, subjective well-being assessment, and each dimension of the Caregiver Ability Scale are shown in Table 2. In the CD-RISC scale, the score of the resilience dimension was the highest, followed by the strength dimension, while the score of the optimism dimension was relatively lower. In the General Well-being Scale (GWB), there were significant differences in the scores of each dimension. The relaxation (compared to tension/anxiety) dimension had the highest score, indicating that the overall emotions of the sample were relatively relaxed, while the energy and concern about health dimensions had relatively lower scores. Among the five dimensions of the caregiving ability scale, the score distribution was relatively concentrated. The dimension of assessment of family and community resources had the highest score, followed by coping with personal emotions and adjusting life to meet caregiving needs. The preparedness to provide assistance and the score of adapting to the role of the caregiver were slightly lower. For the main caregivers, the relationships with the cared-for individuals were that of spouses and children, accounting for 37.2% and 40.5% respectively.

Testing of mediation effect

To further test the mediation effect of the subjective well-being of patients, the Bootstrap method in the SPSS Process macro program was used to conduct 5000 repeated sampling.

As shown in Table 6, the total association between caregiver ability and psychological resilience was 0.391 (95% CI 0.313–0.485). The bootstrap analysis indicated a significant indirect association via patients’ subjective well-being. Both the indirect and direct associations were greater than zero, suggesting that subjective well-being partially accounted for the cross-sectional relationship between caregiver ability and psychological resilience, explaining approximately 7.3% of the total association (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The incidence of hip fractures, especially femoral neck fractures, is increasing among the older people in China. According to the current health status and health-related quality of life scores, the recovery duration required by older patients is at least 6 months. However, the postoperative prognosis is not always satisfactory [14, 15]. In addition to support from medical staff, post-surgery rehabilitation requires support from families, social workers, and caregivers. Hip fracture in older patients is adversely associated with the physical, psychological, and psychosocial aspects of life, which is associated with the quality of life of patients [16]. Therefore, the psychological state of older patients post-hip fracture surgery must be considered for recovery.

In this study, childlessness, out-of-pocket medical insurance payment, complications, and higher education levels related the subjective well-being and psychological resilience of patients. Istianah U et al. [17] revealed that patients with hip fractures experienced various complications within 30–90 days and 1–3 years of surgery, including infection, pressure sores, recurrent fractures, and deep vein thrombosis. The prolonged postoperative recovery period of patients is a psychological burden, especially in cases of complications, and may be associated with the loss of positive psychological impact of rehabilitation, and may be adversely associated with the prognosis. Charles M M Peeters [18] et al. demonstrated that mental state and function in social and psychological fields were associated with the postoperative recovery of patients. In particular, supportive psychotherapy and home rehabilitation programs were associated with better health status of the patient. Therefore, the nutritional status of patients after surgery must be optimized, and supportive rehabilitation programs must be implemented in professional institutions and at home, which may be associated with improve the overall health status and function of patients. Patients must be offered adjusted psychological counseling when necessary. Additionally, family caregivers play an important role in the long-term care system. The ability and confidence of caregivers are reported to significantly impact the maintenance and management of diseases. Good caregiver ability is positively associated with the rehabilitation process of patients [19, 20]. Caregivers with high self-efficacy actively participate in the disease rehabilitation plan and increase their care knowledge [21]. The caregiving ability can be improved by empowering caregivers [22], enhancing skill learning, and implementing information-knowledge attitude practice [23],which are positively associated with promote the rehabilitation process of patients. Most of the main caregivers for older patients are their relatives. This study demonstrated that patients without children tend to have lower resilience, which is associated with more negative emotions and poorer psychological adaptation [9]. In contrast, enhanced resilience correlates with better psychological adaptation, emotional regulation, and subjective well-being, rather than directly reflecting improvements in physical function or clinical outcomes. Thus, medical staff should pay close attention to patients’ mental health and provide psychological support as needed, which may be associated with enhance psychological adaptation and alleviate negative emotions [24, 25].

This study demonstrated that the subjective well-being of patients partially mediated the correlation between caregiver ability and resilience. Subjective well-being is positively correlated with resilience and has a positive predictive value for resilience. The mobility of older patients is challenging after surgery, often requiring the care of caregivers throughout the day. Although this intermediary pathway is statistically valid, its actual effect strength is relatively small. The positive correlation between caregiving ability and psychological resilience is mostly (about 92.7%) achieved through direct association or other potential mechanisms that were not measured in this study. This finding suggests that in future research and intervention practices, while focusing on improving caregiving ability, more attention should be paid to and explored other potentially more critical direct protective factors or intermediary pathways.

The indirect association through subjective well-being, although statistically significant, was small in magnitude (7.3% of the total association). This is perhaps unsurprising given that psychological resilience in older patients after hip fracture is likely influenced by a multitude of factors-including physical recovery, pain, social support, and premorbid personality—of which caregiver ability and subjective well-being represent only two. However, even a modest indirect pathway may be clinically relevant if subjective well-being is modifiable through targeted psychosocial interventions during rehabilitation. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to determine whether changes in subjective well-being produce clinically meaningful improvements in resilience, or whether this small cross-sectional association is merely a statistical artifact due to shared method variance or unmeasured confounding.

This study revealed that specific patient factors-such as being childless, the presence of postoperative complications, and the use of out-of-pocket payment-were significant risk factors associated with lower resilience. Additionally, a higher caregiver ability was demonstrated to be positively correlated with greater patient resilience. Furthermore, the subjective well-being of patients exerted a partial mediation effect in the relationship between caregiver ability and patient resilience. Thus, clinicians must focus on the postoperative psychological health of older patients with hip fractures, particularly those who are childless, have developed complications, or bear personal financial burden for treatment. The psychological resilience of these patients can be enhanced by actively improving caregiver ability through targeted training and support, and by directly providing psychological interventions to boost patient well-being.

The limitation of this study was that it was a single-center study with no specific intervention program for patients, which limits generalizability. Although we employed an intermediary analysis model to explore the complex interrelationships among variables and reported the statistically significant indirect effects, all the analyses were based on data collected at the same point in time. Cross-sectional data cannot establish the temporal sequence between variables or eliminate the influence of unmeasured confounding factors, thus making it impossible to confirm the causal relationship between the variables. Furthermore, a major limitation of current mainstream research is that it focuses solely on the one-way effects at the individual level. The study did not collect data on fracture severity, baseline physical function, postoperative pain, cognitive status, depressive history, rehabilitation intensity, or caregiver mental health. These unmeasured factors may have confounded the associations among caregiver ability, subjective well-being, and psychological resilience.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study suggests that, during the postoperative rehabilitation of older patients with hip fractures, there are significant positive associations among caregiver ability, patient subjective well-being, and psychological resilience. Statistical analysis indicated that subjective well-being may serve as a statistical mediator between caregiver ability and psychological resilience (with an indirect effect accounting for 7.3% of the total association), though the effect size was modest. Therefore, alongside optimizing medical care, future research and interventions could explore comprehensive psychosocial support focusing on enhancing caregiver ability and patient well-being may improve postoperative psychological adaptation and reduce psychological distress.
