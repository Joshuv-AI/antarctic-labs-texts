Genetic dissection of formate hydrogenlyase-2 function: a metalloenzyme involved in bacterial hydrogen production
Authors: Thomas CP Reed, Alexander J Finney, Magali Roger, Frank Sargent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFormate hydrogenlyases are metalloenzymes that can either produce molecular hydrogen gas or use H2 to convert carbon dioxide to formic acid, thus potentially contributing doubly to a sustainable energy future. The structure of formate hydrogenlyase reveals a membrane-bound redox enzyme that shares a common ancestor with the mitochondrial complex I (NADH dehydrogenase). As such, formate hydrogenlyase falls into a category of so‐called ‘complex‐I‐like’ enzymes, that are found in bacteria, archaea and eukaryotic organelles. They share a common core structure of a membrane arm (most likely involved in proton or ion translocation) and a peripheral arm containing metal cofactors and involved in electron transfer. In this work, we clone a gene cluster from Pectobacterium atrosepticum encoding formate hydrogenlyase-2 (FHL-2). A bank of Escherichia coli host strains, themselves devoid of various combinations of native formate hydrogenlyase genes, are employed to characterise FHL-2. We demonstrate that P. atrosepticum FHL-2 is active in an E. coli host in that it can generate H2 under fermentative growth conditions. Unlike native E. coli formate hydrogenlyase-1 (FHL-1), recombinant P. atrosepticum FHL-2 cannot perform the reverse reaction and generate formic acid from H2 and CO2. By testing different combinations of genes by taking an in vivo cross-complementation approach we conclude that the extended membrane arm exhibited by FHL-2 is a major factor in controlling directionality of the enzyme.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00775-026-02168-7.Keywords: Mixed acid fermentation, Anaerobic metabolism, Molecular genetics, Hydrogenase, Formate dehydrogenase, Formate hydrogenlyase

Introduction

The model organism Escherichia coli is a facultatively anaerobic γ-Proteobacterium and therefore possesses a flexible metabolism that enables survival in a range of environmental conditions [1]. In the presence of oxygen, E. coli will preferentially perform aerobic respiration using short, quinone-dependent, electron transport chains for oxidative phosphorylation [2]. Under anaerobic conditions, however, E. coli can adapt its respiratory electron transport chain to utilise alternative terminal electron acceptors, such as nitrate, fumarate and dimethyl sulfoxide [2, 3]. In the complete absence of exogeneous terminal electron acceptors, E. coli will instead conduct ‘mixed-acid’ fermentation [4], where glucose is metabolised via the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas glycolytic pathway to generate ATP by substrate level phosphorylation [5]. This process results in the excretion of ethanol, acetic acid, formic acid and lactic acid as waste products [6]. Extracellular accumulation of formic acid, and the associated pH decrease, induces the production of the formate hydrogenlyase-1 complex (FHL-1), which then functions in pH regulation by catalysing the disproportionation of formic acid to molecular hydrogen and carbon dioxide [7]. This formate-dependent H2 evolution reaction can be considered the physiological ‘forward’ reaction of FHL-1. However, FHL-1 is also readily reversible and can carry out the ‘reverse’ reaction as a hydrogen-dependent CO2 reductase (HDCR) where formic acid can be generated from gaseous substrates under permissive environmental conditions [8].

The FHL-1 complex is encoded by the hycABCDEFGHI operon and the distally located fdhF gene. Expression of both the hyc operon and fdhF is mediated by the transcriptional activator FhlA and repressor HycA [9, 10]. The functional FHL-1 complex is comprised of a membrane arm made of two integral membrane proteins, HycC and HycD, connected to a cytoplasmic, catalytic, peripheral or soluble arm. The peripheral arm is comprised of the molybdenum- and selenium-dependent formate dehydrogenase-H (FdhF) connected to a group 4 [NiFe] hydrogenase (HycEG) by two Fe-S proteins (HycB and HycF) [9, 11, 12]. The hydrogenase component is called hydrogenase-3 (‘Hyd-3’) in E. coli. There is sequence similarity between all of the FHL-1 subunits and subunits of the respiratory NADH dehydrogenase complex I [13]. Formate hydrogenlyase can therefore be considered a member of a large group of membrane-bound enzymes collectively termed the ‘NADH dehydrogenase-like’, or ‘complex I-like’, enzymes.

The apparent evolutionary link between FHL-1 and complex I is another membrane-bound hydrogenase termed FHL-2. The FHL-2 complex is predicted to be comprised of a peripheral arm constituting a formate dehydrogenase (FdhF) linked to a group 4 [NiFe] hydrogenase [14] (this time termed ‘Hyd-4’) via three Fe-S cluster proteins (HyfA, HyfH and HyfI). The key difference between FHL-1 and FHL-2 type proteins is that FHL-2 is predicted to comprise a larger five-subunit membrane arm, that much more closely resembles the larger membrane arm found in bona fide complex I and other complex-I-like proteins.

The E. coli genome contains a gene cluster (hyfABCDEFGHIJR-focB) that would encode an FHL-2 complex [15]. However, the evidence for physiological FHL-2 activity in E. coli K-12 is inconsistent, with some studies highlighting that the FHL-2 complex is possibly not transcribed under natural [16] or other laboratory conditions [17]. Furthermore, E. coli strains lacking functional FHL-1 are usually incapable of producing hydrogen gas [18], while the inactivation of FHL-2 can have little or no effect on overall hydrogen production levels [19]. Nevertheless, it should be noted that there is some data pointing to FHL-2 activity in E. coli [20, 21].

Although E. coli FHL-2 remains to be more fully understood, research based on alternative model organisms has identified functional FHL-2 complexes in Vibrio tritonius [22], Trabulsiella guamensis [23] and Pectobacterium atrosepticum [24]. The FHL-2 from P. atrosepticum is encoded by the hyfABCDEFGHIJK-hyfR operon adjacent to the fdhF-hydN operon with the two predicted to be transcribed from divergent promotors [24, 25]. The hydN gene encodes a protein very similar to HycB and HyfA (Supplementary Figures S1-S10), which are considered the small subunits of the formate dehydrogenase in FHL-1 and FHL-2, respectively. Interestingly, the FdhF formate dehydrogenase of P. atrosepticum FHL-2 is predicted to display a catalytic cysteine residue at its active site rather than selenocysteine, as seen in E. coli FdhF [24, 26]. Similar to E. coli FHL-1 and FHL-2, the peripheral arm of the FHL-2 complex from P. atrosepticum contains the catalytic large subunit of a group 4 [NiFe] hydrogenase (encoded by hyfG). This protein is the equivalent to HycE in FHL-1 (Supplementary Figures S1-S10). The hydrogenase large subunit is linked directly to a hydrogenase small subunit (HyfI in FHL-2 and HycG in FHL-1) and the dimer is connected to the formate dehydrogenase by another Fe-S cluster protein (HyfH in FHL-2 and HycF in FHL-1) (Fig. 1B). The membrane arm of FHL-2 is comprised of 5 integral membrane subunits: the direct membrane anchor for the peripheral arm - HyfC; three antiporter-like protein homologs (HyfB, HyfD and HyfF); and the HyfE protein, which is not found in FHL-1 but shares some sequence similarity with NuoK from complex I [24].

P. atrosepticum is a phytopathogenic enterobacterium known to cause soft rot diseases [25]. This, combined with its slow growth rate and preference for relatively low temperatures, make it a suboptimal host for large scale biochemical or structural studies. Therefore, in this work, the genes encoding FHL-2 from P. atrosepticum were characterised using a plasmid-based approach. This enabled the FHL-2 complex to be studied in an E. coli host, where it was shown to be capable of producing molecular hydrogen during fermentative growth. Additionally, unlike FHL-1, the recombinant FHL-2 complex appears unable to function in reverse as a hydrogen-dependent carbon dioxide reductase (HDCR). The inability of FHL-2 to function bidirectionally may be a consequence of the biochemical properties of the Hyd-4 component of FHL-2 rather than the formate dehydrogenase component.

Materials and methods

Strain construction

The strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. Gene deletions were conducted by homologous recombination using pMAK705 protocol from the parental strain FTD89 (MC4100 ΔhyaB, ΔhybC) [27]. FTD200, which lacks all functional hydrogenases, was constructed from FTD89 through the removal of the hyc and hyf operons. First, 500 bp up- and downstream of the hyc operon (hycA-I) were amplified by PCR and assembled in pMAK705 [27]. Following this, the hyf operon was deleted using pGS1039 [17]. FTD250 was constructed from FTD200 via the deletion of fdhF using a pMAK705 vector, constructed as previously described, to provide a ‘clean’ platform strain for the expression of non-native FHL complexes. The strain 89F was made using the same protocol to allow for the testing of non-native formate dehydrogenases in complex with Hyd-3.

Plasmid construction

For the expression of FHL-2 in an Escherichia coli host, genes encoding FHL-2 were amplified from the genome of P. atrosepticum SCRI1043 using Phusion™ High Fidelity DNA polymerase (Thermo Scientific) and assembled into pT7.5/Litmus. Due to the size of the operon encoding FHL-2 it was amplified as two fragments using the internal BglII site within hyfE. The downstream fragment was amplified via PCR and digested using HindIII and BglII, while the upstream fragment was amplified before digestion with BglII and XbaI. The fragments were assembled into pT7.5/Litmus using the HindIII and XbaI sites. The resultant plasmid was named pTotal. Differences between the cloned sequence and that of the SCRI1043 genome sequence are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

All complementation plasmids were constructed using HiFi Assembly (NEB) using DNA amplified from the genome of P. atrosepticum SCRI1043 or E. coli MG1655. Genes were cloned into pSU-PROM which contains the constitutive tatA promoter [31].

Whole-cell H2 production

Hydrogen production of anaerobic cultures in sealed Hungate Tubes was quantified by gas chromatography [32]. Cells were grown in LB Miller medium supplemented with 0.4% (w/v) glucose and 0.2% (w/v) formate at 37 °C for 20 h. 1 ml of headspace gas was collected using a gas-tight syringe with Luer lock valve (SGE). Samples were injected into a Shimadzu GC-2014 gas chromatograph fitted with a capillary column and TCD detector. Hydrogen was quantified using a hydrogen standard curve and normalised to culture density (OD 600 nm).

Whole-cell formate production

Cells were grown in LB medium supplemented with 0.4% (w/v) glucose under anaerobic conditions before being harvested by centrifugation for 10 min at 2773 x g and 4 °C. Cells were washed thrice with 20 mmol/L 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (MOPS) buffer, pH 7.4, before being resuspended to an OD600nm of 1.0 +/- 0.02. No anaerobic precautions were taken during these washing steps though cell resuspension was carried out gently using a glass homogeniser. Washed cells were transferred to Hungate tubes before the headspace was sparged with N2 followed by H2 and finally was subjected to the injection of 5 ml gaseous CO2. Cells were incubated at 37 °C without agitation for 20 h. Liquid samples were taken via syringe and clarified before HPLC analysis using a an UltiMate 3000 uHPLC system (Thermo) equipped with an Aminex HPX-87 H column (BioRad) using a RefractoMax521 refractive index detector (Thermo) and a Variable Wavelength Detector-3100 at 210 nm (Thermo). Samples of 10 µl previously clarified through a 0.45 μm filter were injected into the HPLC system that had been equilibrated with 5 mmol/L H2SO4 with a flow of 0.8 mL/min at 50 °C. Formate was quantified using a standard curve.

Results

Production of functional FHL-2 in E. coli

To enable characterisation of P. atrospeticum FHL-2, an E. coli K-12 host strain was required that was devoid of hydrogenase activity and all endogenous FHL-1 and FHL-2 structural genes. The FTD150 strain (ΔhyaB ΔhybC ΔhycE ΔhyfB-R, Table 1) was therefore used as a starting point [18]. This E. coli strain already carries in-frame deletions in the catalytic subunits of the three active [NiFe]-hydrogenases in E. coli [33] as well as a deletion of the hyfBCDEFGHIJ-hyfR cluster encoding Hyd-4 [17]. Note that an ‘in-frame deletion’ carefully removes all coding sequence from a gene while preserving start codon, stop codon, and any internal regulatory sequences related to adjacent genes (such as ribosome binding sites). This approach is particularly important when studying multi-cistronic operons to minimise downstream (or ‘polar’) effects on translation. To further modify this strain, first the entire hyc operon (hycA-I) was deleted from FTD150 resulting in the E. coli strain FTD200 (ΔhyaB ΔhybC ΔhycA-I ΔhyfB-R, Table 1). Then, the gene encoding the FdhF formate dehydrogenase was deleted from strain FTD200, resulting in the strain FTD250 (ΔhyaB ΔhybC ΔhycA-I ΔhyfB-R ΔfdhF, Table 1). When incubated under anaerobic, fermentative growth conditions, the new FTD250 strain was found to be incapable of biological H2 evolution (Fig. 2).

Next, the entire 15 kbp gene cluster encoding P. atrosepticum FHL-2 (i.e. the 11-cistron operon encoding the Hyd-4 module and its associated accessory proteins, hyfA-R, as well as the region comprising the genes encoding FdhF and HydN, Fig. 1A) were cloned into a single plasmid including the native, divergent, promotors of both operons (Fig. 1C; Table 2). Given the formate-responsive regulator HyfR would be present on this construct, and the relatedness of P. atrospeticum to gamma-Proteobacteria like E. coli, it was considered that the two operons could be anaerobically expressed in an E. coli host and there would be little need for synthetic overexpression strategies at this stage.

A two-step strategy was devised for cloning the gene cluster due to the presence of a unique BglII restriction site within the hyfE gene. First, the hyfR-E region was amplified by PCR and cloned into pT.7-Litmus (AmpR), which was followed by amplification of the hyfE-fdhF region by PCR and addition to the new vector. This resulted in the p-Total (AmpR) plasmid encoding the full hyfR-fdhF gene cluster (Fig. 1C; Table 2). The p-Total plasmid was sequenced and, most likely due to its construction by PCR, found to contain several point mutations that are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Note that no non-sense mutations were introduced. Next, the p-Total plasmid was modified by the addition of a 10-His tag sequence between codons for Gly-93 and Glu-94 within the hyfG gene primarily to facilitate future protein purification studies but potentially also to be used as an epitope tag in immunological studies. The location of the internal 10-His loop in HyfG was selected based on previous work conducted on E. coli FHL-1where identically-His-tagged HycE enabled the successful purification of the FHL-1 complex with minimal disruption to complex stability or activity [29]. Purification of FHL-2 is not an aim of this particular study, however characterisation of the in vivo physiological activity of His-tagged FHL-2 certainly is. Following construction, the pTotal-hyfGHis vector was sequenced and no additional mutations were identified.

To test if either the pTotal or pTotal-hyfGHis vectors encoded hydrogenase activity, FTD250 was transformed with the plasmids and anaerobic cultures were incubated in sealed Hungate tubes containing LB medium supplemented with 0.4% (w/v) glucose and 0.2% (w/v) formate. Following 20 h growth at 37 °C the headspace gas was analysed by gas chromatography (Fig. 2). FTD250[pTotal] produced 3.6 mM of hydrogen per OD, while FTD250[pTotal-hyfGHis] produced 3.4 mM of H2 per OD (Fig. 2). These values were clearly lower than the 15.5 mM/OD of H2 produced in strain FTD89 that contains native FHL-1 (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, it was evident from these results that the cloned FHL-2 complex from P. atrosepticum was active in an E. coli host.

Next, it was pertinent to determine if FHL-2 could function in reverse as a hydrogen-dependent carbon dioxide reductase. To explore this, anaerobically-grown cultures were harvested and washed in MOPS buffer (pH 7.4) and sealed in Hungate tubes before being supplied with the gaseous substrates. Following incubation with a CO2/H2 mixture, clarified samples were analysed by HPLC to quantify formate levels in the aqueous phase. No formate was detected in experiments using FTD250[pTotal] or FTD250[pTotal-hyfGHis] (Fig. 2). Conversely, the positive control experiment using FTD89 (producing active FHL-1) was capable of producing 4 mM formate under the same conditions (Fig. 2).

The role of the membrane domain in FHL-2 function

The FHL-2 complex is predicted to have major differences from FHL-1, including three additional membrane proteins (HyfDEF). To assess their role in recombinant FHL-2 activity mutant plasmids were constructed, including: pTotal hyfGHis ΔhyfD-F (lacking genes encoding HyfD, -E, and -F) and pTotal hyfGHis ΔhyfB-F (lacking genes encoding HyfB, -C, -D, -E, and -F) (Table 2). When the host strain FTD250 was transformed with pTotal hyfGHis ΔhyfD-F some FHL-2 activity was detected, however the mutant plasmid was found to produce less than 30% H2 than normally recorded for FTD250 expressing the whole FHL-2 complex (Fig. 3). The deletion of genes encoding all five membrane subunits of FHL-2 (ΔhyfB-F) resulted in an even greater reduction in hydrogen production activity, though it should be noted that trace levels of H2 could still be quantified here (Fig. 3).

Next, the genes encoding the FHL-2 membrane arm were cloned into the pSU-Prom vector (KanR) (Table 2), which is compatible for co-expression with pLitmus (AmpR) derivatives. This enabled a series of in trans complementation experiments, which demonstrated full restoration of hydrogen production activity (Fig. 3).

Finally, hydrogen-dependent CO2-reduction experiments were carried out on FTD250 carrying pTotal hyfGHis ΔhyfD-F or pTotal hyfGHis ΔhyfB-F, but no formate was detected in any of the samples (Fig. 3).

The role of formate dehydrogenase subunits in FHL-2 function

Next, the importance of the P. atrosepticum formate dehydrogenase in FHL-2 activity was investigated. In P. atrosepticum the catalytic subunit of the formate dehydrogenase (fdhF) is encoded upstream of the hyf operon, on a divergent promoter, along with hydN. The HydN protein shares sequence similarity with HyfA and is therefore predicted to encode an alternative small subunit for the formate dehydrogenase. Therefore, a deletion in both fdhF and hydN together was made in the pTotal hyfGHis plasmid (Table 2). The resultant pTotal hyfGHis ΔfdhF ΔhydN vector was then tested in the FTD250 host strain and it was found that deletion of both fdhF and hydN together resulted in the complete loss of hydrogen production activity by FHL-2 (Fig. 4). Complementation in trans with pSU fdhF (KanR), which carries only one gene, restored hydrogen production activity to ~ 71% of that of that normally recorded for the whole FHL-2 complex (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the full rescue of FHL-2 activity was observed when pTotal hyfGHis ΔfdhF ΔhydN was co-expressed with a second plasmid encoding both fdhF and hydN together (Fig. 4). No hydrogen-dependent CO2-reductase activity was detectible in any of these experiments (Fig. 4).

Cross-complementation between FHL-1 and FHL-2 subunits: FdhF

E. coli strain FTD200 differs from FTD250 in that it contains a native fdhF gene. Both strains lack native hydrogenase activity and therefore produce no H2 via FHL-1, nor are they capable of producing formate via HDCR (Fig. 2). However, when FTD200 was transformed with pTotal hyfGHis ΔfdhF ΔhydN hydrogen production activity was rescued to a high level - almost double that recorded for intact FHL-2 in FTD250 (Fig. 4). This suggests E. coli FdhF from FHL-1 can interact with P. atrosepticum Hyd-4 from FHL-2. Moreover, it should be noted that trace but detectable amounts of formate (61 nM) were detected in samples of FTD200 pTotal hyfGHis ΔfdhF ΔhydN when incubated under an H2/CO2 atmosphere (Fig. 4). This represents only 1.5% of the total normally recorded in native FHL-1 experiments.

Next, the ability of the formate dehydrogenase catalytic subunit from P. atrosepticum FHL-2 to substitute for that of native E. coli FHL-1 was tested. First, the E. coli strain 89F was constructed, which is devoid of Hyd-1 and Hyd-2 activity and carries a deletion in fdhF (Table 1). E. coli 89F displays no FHL-1-based H2 production activity (Fig. 5). When E. coli 89F was transformed with a plasmid encoding P. atrosepticum FdhF (pSU-fdhF) a partial restoration of hydrogen production activity was observed (Fig. 5). Interestingly, complementation with a plasmid containing both P. atrosepticum fdhF and hydN together resulted in less hydrogen production (5.2 mM/OD) than with the fdhF gene alone (8.8 mM/OD) (Fig. 5).

Hydrogen-dependent CO2 reduction experiments were also conducted in these strains (Fig. 5). As previously reported [8], E. coli strain FTD89, which has functional FHL-1, was able to generate formate from a H2/CO2 mixture at ambient pressure. Deletion of the gene encoding the formate dehydrogenase component of FHL-1 blocked this activity (Fig. 5). Complementation of strain 89F with P. atrosepticum fdhF alone, and in combination with P. atrosepticum hydN, produced chimeric complexes that were capable of HDCR activity and resulted in detectable formate generation from H2 and CO2 substrates (Fig. 5).

Cross-complementation between FHL-1 and FHL-2 subunits: HyfG/HycE

The possibility of cross-complementation between genes encoding the large subunits of Hyd-3 and Hyd-4 was investigated. A version of pTotal was prepared that carries an in-frame deletion of hyfG (pTotal ΔhyfG) (Table 2). The E. coli host strain FTD250 containing pTotal ΔhyfG was found to be devoid of hydrogen production activity (Fig. 6). A partial rescue in FHL-2 activity could be achieved through complementation in trans with a pSU-hyfG plasmid, and resulted in approximately half of the levels of H2 accumulation when compared to FHL-2 expressed from the intact operon. Interestingly, the gene encoding the large subunit of E. coli Hyd-3 (hycE) can also partially complement the hyfG deletion (Fig. 6). No hydrogen-dependent CO2-reductase activity was detectible in any of these experiments (Fig. 6).

Cross complementation between FHL-1 and FHL-2 subunits: membrane domain

A key point of similarity, and a key point of difference, between FHL-1 and FHL-2 is the membrane domain. FHL-1 contains two subunits in the membrane domain, HycCD. The equivalent proteins exist in FHL-2 (HyfBC), with HyfD, -E, and -F being additional components. First, it was determined if the membrane domain of P. atrospeticum FHL-2 could complement the E. coli FHL-1 complex lacking its own membrane domain. E. coli strains MR60 (contains active FHL-1) and Tom001 (as MR60 but ΔhycCD) were transformed with constructs containing FHL-2 membrane subunits in varying combinations before hydrogen and formate production activities were determined (Fig. 8). The control experiments with MR60 and Tom001 alone displayed activities consistent with previous research [30], where the ΔhycCD retains a low level of H2 accumulation activity compared to the parent strain. When E. coli Tom001 was transformed with pSU-hyfBC a partial restoration of hydrogen production activity was observed reaching ~ 53% of that normally recorded for native FHL-1 (Fig. 8). Transformation of Tom001 with pSU-hyfDEF, encoding the three membrane subunits of FHL-2 not found in FHL-1, could not rescue H2 production activity (Fig. 8). Taken together, this showed a functional chimera could be formed using the soluble arm of FHL-1 and core conserved subunits from the membrane arm of FHL-2.

Discussion

P. atrosepticum FHL-2 is functional in an E. coli host

In this work, we set out to identify a suitable, physiologically active FHL-2 that could be produced in E. coli to allow direct comparison with the native FHL-1 isoenzyme. Here, it has now been shown that the P. atrosepticum FHL-2 is functional in an E. coli host. This was enabled by constructing an E. coli host strain (FTD250) devoid of the majority of structural genes involved in hydrogen metabolism, including the native hycABCDEFGHI and hyfABCDEFGHIJ-hyfR operons and the fdhF gene, known or predicted to be involved in the formate hydrogenlyase reaction. This allowed unambiguous characterisation of a cloned, recombinant version of P. atrosepticum FHL-2. The work with pTotal and its derivatives (Table 2) has clearly established that the enzyme can be produced and assembled at 37 °C and that the biosynthetic machineries for E. coli [NiFe] hydrogenases and E. coli molybdo-enzymes can facilitate maturation of the enzymes (Fig. 2).

P. atrosepticum formate dehydrogenase (FdhF) is bidirectional

In this work, the FHL-2 complex from P. atrosepticum was clearly shown to be able to contribute to H2 production under anaerobic fermentative conditions (the ‘forward’ reaction). However, unlike E. coli FHL-1, P. atrosepticum FHL-2 was unable to function in reverse as a hydrogen-dependent CO2 reductase, at least under the conditions tested here (Fig. 2). A series of genetic experiments were employed to begin to dissect the molecular basis of this unidirectionality.

The role of the P. atrosepticum formate dehydrogenase component was investigated. Indeed, sequence analysis of this protein, and genomic analysis of the organism, suggest it does not contain selenocysteine at its active site. This is interesting since substitution of the selenocysteine for a cysteine residue within the E. coli FdhF active site has been shown to significantly reduce its in vitro CO2 reduction activity to 19% of the native enzyme [34]. In our experiments, the selenium-free P. atrosepticum FdhF was shown to be readily reversible in vivo, but only when paired with the E. coli Hyd-3 enzyme (Fig. 5). The isolated P. atrosepticum FdhF was able to substitute for the E. coli enzyme in FHL-1 in both the H2 production and formate production directions, meaning the absence of selenocysteine is not regulating directionality of this component of FHL-2.

The interchangeability of the different species’ FdhF subunits was perhaps not entirely surprising. The P. atrosepticum FdhF shares 85% overall sequence similarity with the E. coli FHL-1 counterpart (Supplementary Figures S1-S10). Moreover, the interaction between E. coli FdhF and the remainder of the E. coli FHL-1 complex is known to be transient and weak and the proteins readily dissociated upon purification, especially under reducing conditions [32]. Structural studies have determined that E. coli FdhF interacts with E. coli HycB though hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Additionally, a single electrostatic interaction with Arg-122 of E. coli HycF was also noted. The data presented in this work would suggest that at least some of these interactions are conserved between FHL-1 and FHL-2. Lindenstrauß & Pinske (2019) have also demonstrated that the E. coli FdhF can form a functional complex with the Hyd-4 complex of T. guamensis, where the chimeric complex demonstrated the same level of hydrogen production as the native FHL-2 complex [23].

HydN is a functional subunit of the FHL-2 complex

The HydN protein is a small Fe-S protein with sequence identity to HyfA of FHL-2 and HycB of FHL-1 (Supplementary Figures S1-S10). Its possible role as a formate dehydrogenase small subunit has long been speculated upon, however the genetics and biochemistry of E. coli added confusion. In E. coli, hydN is encoded with a hydrogenase maturation gene hypF [35]. Deletion of the hydN gene in E. coli led to an slight reduction in dye-linked formate dehydrogenase activity [35] but overall FHL-1 activity was not significantly affected [36]. Indeed, E. coli FdhF can be purified in an active form without a small subunit [11] and HydN was not present in the E. coli FHL-1 cryo-EM structure [12]. The data presented in Figs. 4 and 5 show the functional importance of HydN and these data suggest that the cognate HydN studied here, along with FdhF, might have some influence in the directionality of the FHL-2 complex. Extra experimentation would be needed to explore this further, however, since the E. coli hydN gene is present in all of these experiments.

P. atrosepticum Hyd-4 is unidirectional

The unidirectionality of FHL-2 seems to stem for the most part from the Hyd-4 module, and most specifically the membrane arm. Deletion of the genes encoding the membrane arm from the native FHL-2 resulted in a loss in H2 evolution activity (Fig. 3). This is consistent with the behaviour of FHL-1 when its membrane arm is genetically removed [13]. More interestingly, it is shown here that the FHL-2 membrane arm, either in minimal HyfBC form (which is the equivalent to HycCD in FHL-1) or in extended HyfBCDEF form, can interact with the FHL-1 peripheral arm and restore H2 evolution activity (Fig. 8). However, it is clear that the reverse formate production activity is not restored in the same FHL-1/FHL-2 chimeras (Fig. 8). There, the presence of the full-length HyfBCDEF membrane arm reduces the ability of the enzyme to operate in reverse quite significantly (Fig. 8).

It should also be considered that changes in oxygen sensitivity could also contribute to these observations. No strict anaerobic precautions were put in place during the cell washing stage prior to anaerobic incubation with CO2/H2. While native FHL-1 survives this treatment, it could be that FHL-2, and/or the FHL-1/FHL-2 chimeras, are more sensitive to O2 attack.

The molecular basis of the interaction between the FHL-1 membrane arm and peripheral arm is now well understood. Structural data obtained for E. coli FHL-1 revealed the presence of a cluster of loops at the interface that are conserved across the complex I and complex I-like families, including membrane-bound hydrogenases [12]. Indeed, previous research has shown the disruption of glutamate residues within the loop that connects HycE with HycD can have significant negative effects on FHL activity [32, 37]. It should be considered that, in our chimeric FHL complexes, incorrect or unstable binding of peripheral arm to the membrane arm may be the basis of the unidirectionality observed. Indeed, further analysis into the native structure of FHL-2 will be required to effectively compare the loop-cluster structure and wider interaction interface.

Experiments focused on the hydrogenase catalytic subunits did not produce meaningful data (Figs. 6 and 7). This may not be altogether surprising when it is considered that these subunits are normally buried in the middle of a much larger complex with at least four other interacting partners to consider. Even cross-substitution of relatively conserved hydrogenase large and small subunits may not be straightforward. For example, a similar occurrence has been noted when attempting to form chimera of E. coli hydrogenase-1 and hydrogenase-2 [38]. To facilitate the assembly of chimeras between these two isoenzymes, a total of 13 residues required engineering at the large subunit – small subunit interface [39].

Many hydrogenases function bidirectionally and are capable of catalysing reactions reversibly [32, 39, 40]. Furthermore, Mo/W-dependent formate dehydrogenases are known to catalyse the oxidation of formate to CO2 reversibly [41]. However, bidirectionality is not uniform across these enzyme families. O2-tolerant [NiFe]-hydrogenases, such as E. coli Hyd-1, are generally thought to function unidirectionally in H2-uptake [42]. Several other [NiFe]-dependent hydrogenases have shown catalytic bias for hydrogen oxidation with little to no proton reduction activity [43–45]. Interestingly, Pyrococcus furiosus possesses a [NiFe] dependent membrane-bound hydrogenases that preferentially catalyses H2 production and displays very little H2 oxidation activity [46]. Outwith the O2-tolerance mechanism observed in some [NiFe] hydrogenases, and the properties of the metal centres and gas channels that are involved, the reversibility of metalloenzymes either in vitro or in vivo is difficult to predict.

Concluding remarks

In this work, the FHL-2 complex from P. atrosepticum was cloned and shown to be active in an E. coli host and capable of producing molecular hydrogen during fermentative growth. Furthermore, we have shown that this FHL-2 complex appears to function unidirectionally in favour of hydrogen production. The inability of FHL-2 to function in reverse appears to stem from subtle molecular differences in the Hyd-4 module and the presence of additional subunits in the membrane arm. There may also be some involvement with the HydN-containing formate dehydrogenase module. Indeed, this work identified HydN as an bona fide alternative formate dehydrogenase small subunit in FHL-2 and future work will focus on understanding the competing or complementary role of HydN with its homolog HyfA within the complex. Indeed, the molecular tools developed here will allow further characterisation of important aspects of FHL-2 activity, including the role the complex I-like membrane arm. Understanding how electron transport is coupled to proton translocation in membrane-bound hydrogenases has wider significance since complex-I-like enzymes are found in a broad range of microbes and eukaryotic organelles.
