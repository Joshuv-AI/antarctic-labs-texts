Antioxidant activities of lignin-derived volatile phenolics in sesame oil – Relative superiority of 4-vinylguaiacol and its interactions with endogenous antioxidants
Authors: Tian-Pei Zhao, Jing-Yu Chen, Yue-Hua Li, Chen-Xia Zhang, Yu-Xiang Ma, Zhao Qin, Xue-De Wang, Hua-Min Liu, Rui-Fu Cui, Lei Wang, Yong-Jun Xu, Hai-Ming Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically investigated the antioxidant activities of seven lignin-derived volatile phenolics in sesame oil. Using in vitro assays and a purified sesame oil (PSO) model, 4-vinylguaiacol exhibited the strongest antioxidant capacity, followed by 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-methylphenol, and guaiacol, all of which outperformed vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone. The former four compounds effectively enhanced PSO stability, as evidenced by prolonged oxidation induction time, increased thermal decomposition temperature, and suppressed rise of various oxidation indices during accelerated storage. Quantum chemical calculations supported that 4-vinylguaiacol had the strongest antioxidant activity, consistent with experimental ranking. Binary interaction analysis revealed antagonism between 4-vinylguaiacol and sesamol, indicating that these two compounds should not be combined in antioxidant formulations, whereas additive effects were observed with γ-tocopherol. This study provides a theoretical basis for the rational design of antioxidant formulations. As natural antioxidants, 4-vinylguaiacol and its combination with γ-tocopherol show promising application prospects in commercial edible oil systems.

Introduction

Vegetable oils with distinctive flavors derived from heat-processed oilseeds are widely consumed in China and other Asian countries, including those obtained from roasted sesame seeds (Arab et al., 2022), rapeseeds (Azadmard-Damirchi, Habibi-Nodeh, Hesari, Nemati, & Fathi Achachlouei, 2010), and flaxseeds (Yuan, Wei, Yang, & Zhou, 2025). These oils contain a certain number of volatile phenolic compounds, primarily derived from the thermal degradation of lignin during high-temperature roasting (Zhao et al., 2025a). Some of these volatile phenolics have been identified as key active components responsible for the smoky aroma in vegetable oils (Yin, Ma, Li, Wang, Liu, & Shi, 2021). Crucially, since these phenolics possess phenolic hydroxyl groups known to confer antioxidant activity (Gao, Chen, He, Shi, & Zeng, 2024), they may also affect lipid oxidative stability. Although these phenolics share structural similarities, differences in their substituent groups may result in variations in antioxidant potency. However, no systematic study has yet compared the antioxidant potency of these lignin-derived volatile phenolics.

Among various oilseeds, sesame seeds are widely consumed for their unique roasted flavor and abundant nutrients (Arab et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2023). The structure of lignin in sesame hulls has been well characterized (Zhao et al., 2025a). In our previous study (Zhao et al., 2025b), a total of seven lignin-derived volatile phenolics were identified in sesame oils prepared from sesame seeds roasted at different temperatures (180–260 °C), including guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, 4-methylphenol, vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone. Accordingly, these seven phenolics were selected as representative compounds to systematically investigate and compare their antioxidant behavior in sesame oil. Several studies have revealed the antioxidant potential of such phenolics. Guaiacol has been found to act as an antioxidant in linoleic acid-rich oils (Mikołajczak, Tańska, Ogrodowska, & Czaplicki, 2022). 4-Ethylguaiacol and vanillin have been reported to exhibit antioxidant activity both in vitro and in vivo (Zhao et al., 2017). 4-Vinylguaiacol has been identified as an antioxidant in emulsions (Shin, Jeong, Jia, Hong, & Lee, 2019; Terpinc, Polak, Segatin, Hanzlowsky, Ulrih, & Abramovic, 2011) and edible oils, as demonstrated in flaxseed oil (Mikołajczak, Pilarski, Gesinski, & Tańska, 2023), rapeseed oil, and olive oil (Tańska, Mikołajczak, & Konopka, 2018). 4-Vinylguaiacol has also been detected in beer, and the antioxidant activity of beer was positively correlated with its 4-vinylguaiacol content (Rahman et al., 2020). However, these reports are mostly fragmented investigations of individual phenolics in specific systems. They lack systematic comparisons of the relative antioxidant efficacy of these homologous phenolics under a unified framework, as well as their interactions with other endogenous antioxidants in oils. This has hindered the development of natural blended antioxidants for edible oils based on such compounds.

As a typical representative of roasted oilseed systems, sesame oil, with well-characterized endogenous antioxidants such as sesamol and γ-tocopherol (Arab et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023), provides an ideal model for analyzing interactions between lignin-derived phenolics and other intrinsic oil components. Furthermore, although the content of these seven lignin-derived volatile phenolics in sesame oil is limited, they form a unique phenolic profile. As a functional library of natural antioxidants, the intrinsic activity differences among these compounds and their interaction patterns with natural antioxidants such as sesamol and γ-tocopherol remain unclear. To fill this knowledge gap, the core objective of this study is to systematically determine the antioxidant activity ranking of these seven phenolics and their interaction patterns with key endogenous antioxidants in sesame oil. To achieve this and to accurately attribute observed effects directly to the added phenolics, a purified sesame oil (PSO) model stripped of native antioxidants, pigments, and oxidation products was used for all evaluations.

Therefore, this study was designed to: (1) comparatively evaluate the in vitro antioxidant capacities of the seven lignin-derived volatile phenolics (Fig. S1) and their effects on the oxidative stability of PSO, using sesamol as a positive control; (2) elucidate the underlying chemical reactivity of these phenolics via quantum chemical calculations; and (3) systematically establish their antioxidant efficacy ranking and, for the most active compound, explore its binary interactions with sesamol and γ-tocopherol. This work clarifies, for the first time, the antioxidant hierarchy and interaction patterns of these lignin-derived volatile phenolics in an oil matrix, providing novel insights for the development of natural antioxidant strategies.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

A commercially available refined sesame oil (Taibai, Takemoto Oil Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was selected for this study due to its low content of endogenous antioxidants, which was essential for our purification objectives. This oil was purchased via Taobao (http://www.taobao.com). Guaiacol (99%), 4-ethylguaiacol (99%), 4-vinylguaiacol (98%), 4-methylphenol (99%), vanillin (99%), acetovanillone (98%), isoacetovanillone (98%), and sesamol (98%) were obtained from Macklin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). γ-Tocopherol (98%) was supplied by Solarbio Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Silicic acid, activated charcoal, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline)-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS), and 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ) were purchased from Aladdin Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HPLC-grade methanol and n-hexane, as well as analytical-grade diethyl ether, chloroform, glacial acetic acid, isooctane, and other reagents, were obtained from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Analysis of antioxidant capacity of phenolics

DPPH• scavenging ability

First, methanol solutions of each phenolic compound were prepared at concentrations of 0.01–1.0 mg/mL. The DPPH• scavenging assay was performed according to Liu et al. (2020). A 200 μL sample was mixed with 3 mL of DPPH methanol solution (0.13 mmol/L) and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. The DPPH• scavenging rate was calculated using the following formula.(1)DPPH•scavenging rate%=1−As−A0Ac×100As is the absorbance of the sample mixture, A0 is the absorbance of the sample without DPPH solution, and Ac is the absorbance of the control.

ABTS•+ scavenging ability

The ABTS•+ scavenging assay was performed as reported by Xue et al. (2025). Before the experiment, the ABTS working solution was diluted with methanol to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. Then, 150 μL of sampl was mixed with 3 mL of diluted ABTS solution. After incubation at 25 °C for 10 min, absorbance was measured at 734 nm. The ABTS•+ scavenging rate was calculated using the same formula as for DPPH• scavenging assay.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

FRAP was determined according to Svestková, Balík, and Soural (2024). A 150 μL sample at 0.4 mg/mL was incubated with 3 mL of FRAP working solution at 37 °C for 60 min, and absorbance was measured at 593 nm. A standard curve (y = 0.0074x + 0.0009, r2 = 0.9998) was established using Fe2+ standard solutions (4–200 μmol/L). Results were expressed as mmol Fe2+ equivalents per gram of compound.

Determination of sesame oil composition

L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎ color values were measured using a CS-821 N spectrophotometric colorimeter (CHN Spec, Hangzhou, China). Tocopherol content was determined according to Yin et al. (2023). Briefly, 0.5 g of oil sample was dissolved and diluted to 10 mL with n-hexane. After mixing and filtration, the solution was injected into a Waters e2695 HPLC system (Waters, USA) equipped with an NH2 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) and a 2475 fluorescence detector. Separation was performed isocratically with n-hexane/isopropanol (99:1, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min and column temperature of 40 °C. Fluorescence detection was performed at excitation and emission wavelengths of 298 nm and 325 nm, respectively. Quantification was performed by external standard calibration. Sterols were extracted and enriched according to Arab et al. (2022) and analyzed by GC under the conditions reported in the literature. Quantification was performed using an internal standard method (1 mL of 2 mg/mL cholesterol solution). Lignans were extracted and analyzed by HPLC as described by Yin et al. (2023), with quantification by external standard method. Other basic indicators were determined as described in subsequent sections.

Preparation of PSO with removal of minor constituents

To eliminate interference from endogenous antioxidants and other minor components, sesame oil was purified using silicic acid-activated charcoal chromatography (Liu et al., 2020). Briefly, a C184605CR glass column (Synthware Glass Instruments Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was packed layer by layer with silicic acid, activated charcoal, and silicic acid, with each layer having a height of 20 cm. The column was wet-packed with the above filler using n-hexane. Sesame oil (500 mL) pre-mixed with an equal volume of n-hexane was loaded onto the column at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. n-Hexane was used as the eluent. After all the eluate was collected, the primary solvent was removed using a RE-52AA rotary vacuum concentrator (Shanghai Yarong Biochemical Instrument Factory, Shanghai, China) at 28 °C. Subsequently, vacuum freeze-drying (LGJ-25C, Foring Technology Development Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was performed for 48 h to remove residual solvent. The resulting stripped oil was designated as purified sesame oil (PSO). PSO was odorless, tasteless, and colorless. Key physicochemical indices of sesame oil before and after purification are listed in Table S1. Major antioxidants, including lignans (sesamol, sesamin, asarinin, and sesamolin), tocopherols, and sterols, were undetectable in PSO. The fatty acid composition remained within the typical range for sesame oil. Acid value (AV) and peroxide value (PV) showed no significant change, indicating that no further oxidation occurred during preparation. These results suggested that PSO was suitable for the simulated system in subsequent experiments due to the effective removal of endogenous antioxidants.

Preparation of PSO samples containing phenolic compounds for thermal oxidation testing

Each phenolic compound (guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, 4-methylphenol, vanillin, acetovanillone, isoacetovanillone, and sesamol) was individually mixed with PSO at 100 mg per 50 g PSO. Sesamol served as the positive control. The mixture was sonicated for 10 min at room temperature and vortexed for 5 min using a vortex shaker (YETO, vortex-2, China) to obtain stock solutions at 2000 mg/kg. These stock solutions were serially diluted with PSO to obtain final concentrations of 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 mg/kg. These samples were subjected to oxidation induction time (OIT), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and oven-accelerated storage tests. PSO without added phenolics was used as the blank control. The concentration range (50–1000 mg/kg) was selected for the following reasons. The primary aim of this study was not to simulate the natural trace-level contributions of these phenolics in sesame oil. Instead, using concentrations higher than their endogenous levels was necessary to achieve two key experimental goals: (1) to establish a distinct dose-response relationship between phenolic concentrations and the oxidative stability of PSO; and (2) to elicit measurable and comparable antioxidant effects, thereby enabling unambiguous differentiation of the antioxidant efficacy among the seven target phenolics. This concentration range is also supported by previous studies; for instance, 4-vinylguaiacol has been applied at 200–800 mg/kg to improve the oxidative stability of cold-pressed vegetable oils (Tańska et al., 2018).

Determination of OIT

OIT was determined using a Rancimat 743 instrument (Metrohm AG, Switzerland) following the method of Yin et al. (2023) with slight modifications. Briefly, an oil sample (3.0 ± 0.01 g) was heated at 110 °C in a sealed glass tube with an air flow of 20 L/h.

Thermal oxidation analysis

A Q5000 thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Instrument Corp., New Castle, DE, USA) and a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC214, Netzsch, Selb, Germany) were used to obtain TGA and DSC curves, respectively. Samples (10.0 ± 0.5 mg) containing 1000 mg/kg of each phenolic were placed in aluminum crucibles. TGA and DSC tests were performed in air (50 mL/min) at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 50 °C to 600 °C. Decomposition and oxidation temperatures of the samples were analyzed according to TGA and DSC curves, respectively (Gao et al., 2024).

Accelerated storage experiment

Accelerated storage was used to evaluate oil oxidative stability (Mikolajczak et al., 2023). PSO samples with 1000 mg/kg of each phenolic were stored in amber bottles at 63 °C in the dark. Samples were collected every 3 days over 15 days for chemical analysis.

Determination of AV, conjugated diene, and triene values

AV was determined according to AOCS official method Cd 5a-40 (AOCS, 2017). Conjugated diene (K232) and triene (K268) values were measured at 232 nm and 268 nm, respectively (Quek et al., 2023).

Determination of PV, ρ-anisidine, and total oxidation values

PV was determined by AOCS official method Cd 8–53 (AOCS, 2017). ρ-Anisidine value (ρ-AnV) and total oxidation value (TOTOX) were determined as described in a previous study (Meng et al., 2021). TOTOX was evaluated using the formula: TOTOX = 2 × PV (in meq/kg) + ρ-AnV.

Determination of fatty acid composition

Fatty acid composition was determined by GC-FID according to Yin et al. (2022). Methyl esterification of oil sample was performed using boron trifluoride. The extracts were filtered and analyzed for fatty acid composition using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890B, USA) equipped with an HP-88 column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm). Fatty acids were identified by comparing their retention times with those of standard methyl esters. Relative content of each fatty acid was determined by area normalization.

Quantum chemical calculations

The three-dimensional structures of the target phenolic compounds for quantum chemical calculations were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Calculations were performed with Gaussian 09, Revision D.01 software (Gaussian, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The B3LYP density functional theory (DFT) method and 6–311++G(d,p) basis set were used for geometrical structure optimization, electronic structure evaluation, and other related calculations of the phenolic compounds. Subsequently, molecular surface electrostatic potential, highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)-lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies, and conceptual density functional theory (CDFT) parameters were derived. Additionally, O—H bond dissociation enthalpy (BDE) and adiabatic ionization potential (AIP) were calculated at the same theory level, with the calculation formulas following those reported in the literature (Thbayh & Fiser, 2022). Multiwfn 3.6 and VMD software were used to analyze and visualize the obtained data.

Analysis of interaction types in PSO

Binary interactions between 4-vinylguaiacol and sesamol or γ-tocopherol on PSO oxidation were investigated according to Tang et al. (2023). Two 2000 mg/kg stock solutions were mixed and diluted according to the concentrations listed in Table S2. Interaction types between the two compounds were determined from OIT values. The synergistic index (SI) of the antioxidant combinations was calculated using the following equation.(2)SI=EABEA+EB−EA×EB/100EA, EB, and EAB are the OIT increments of antioxidant A, B, and their combination, respectively. SI is used to classify interaction effects: synergistic (>1), additive (=1), and antagonistic (<1).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics 23.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for multiple comparisons among group means. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Figures were plotted using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corp., USA).

Results and discussion

Antioxidant activities of phenolics in vitro

The in vitro antioxidant capacities of phenolics were assessed via DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP. The DPPH• scavenging activity of each phenolic is shown in Fig. 1A. The results showed that the DPPH• scavenging ability of most phenolics gradually increased with increasing concentration and then stabilized. At equivalent concentrations, 4-methylphenol, vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone exhibited significantly lower DPPH• scavenging ability than other phenolics, with scavenging rates ≤5% at 1.0 mg/mL. Guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, and 4-vinylguaiacol demonstrated efficacy comparable to sesamol, with DPPH• scavenging capacities ordered as: sesamol >4-ethylguaiacol >4-vinylguaiacol > guaiacol. In the ABTS•+ scavenging assay (Fig. 1B), the scavenging ability of all phenolics gradually increased with increasing concentration. Among them, sesamol, guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, and 4-methylphenol showed a faster increase and eventually stabilized, whereas acetovanillone, isoacetovanillone, 4-vinylguaiacol, and vanillin displayed weaker but steadily increasing activity. Overall, the ABTS•+ scavenging efficacy followed the order: sesamol > guaiacol >4-ethylguaiacol >4-methylphenol > isoacetovanillone > acetovanillone > vanillin ≈ 4-vinylguaiacol. In the FRAP assay (Fig. 1C), sesamol and guaiacol had significantly (p < 0.05) higher reducing power than the other phenolics, both exceeding 7.5 mmol Fe2+/g. These were followed by 4-ethylguaiacol and 4-vinylguaiacol, with values of 5.91 and 2.20 mmol Fe2+/g, respectively. 4-Methylphenol, acetovanillone, isoacetovanillone, and vanillin showed relatively low reducing power; in particular, vanillin showed the lowest activity (0.92 mmol Fe2+/g).Fig. 1DPPH• scavenging activity (A), ABTS•+ scavenging activity (B), and FRAP activity (C) of phenolics. Values are expressed as means ± SD (n = 3). Columns labeled with different lowercase letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).Fig. 1

Phenolics do not always exhibit consistent antioxidant activity across different antioxidant assays. Sesamol consistently showed superior antioxidant activity in all three methods, indicating that none of the volatile phenolic matched its efficacy. Among the seven volatile phenolics, 4-ethylguaiacol and 4-vinylguaiacol both showed moderate DPPH• scavenging but diverged in the ABTS•+ assay. This may be because 4-ethylguaiacol maintained moderate activity via the weak electron-donating effect of the para-ethyl group, whereas 4-vinylguaiacol was severely limited in ABTS•+ scavenging due to the hydrophobicity and conjugated electron-withdrawing effect of the vinyl group. Similarly, 4-methylphenol exhibited contrasting activity between DPPH and ABTS assays. This may be attributed to the weak electron-donating ability of the methyl group, which was insufficient to significantly lower the O—H BDE in the DPPH assay, thereby reducing the reaction rate by increasing the energy barrier for H+ transfer. In addition, the inability of the methyl group to form a conjugated system led to rapid dimerization and deactivation of the generated phenoxyl radicals, preventing sustained DPPH• scavenging. In contrast, the ABTS assay is dominated by an electron transfer mechanism (Liao et al., 2022) and is faster than the DPPH assay. The low oxidation potential and absence of steric hindrance of 4-methylphenol provided an advantage in this assay. Notably, vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone consistently performed poorly in all three assays due to their carbonyl groups (-CHO/-COCH₃). These strong electron-withdrawing groups elevate BDE and oxidation potential, hindering both hydrogen-atom transfer and electron transfer. Overall, differences in radical species, substrates, reaction systems, conditions, and mechanisms may explain the divergent in vitro assay results (Zhao et al., 2017). A comprehensive antioxidant evaluation therefore requires multiple analytical methods.

Effects of phenolics on the OIT of PSO

The effects of different phenolic additions (50–1000 mg/kg) on the OIT of PSO are shown in Fig. 2. The OIT of PSO was 1.58 h. All tested phenolics at various concentrations prolonged the OIT of PSO to varying degrees compared with the control, suggesting that they all possess antioxidant capacity to retard lipid oxidation. Moreover, OIT increased gradually with increasing phenolic concentration, indicating a positive correlation between antioxidant efficacy and concentration within the experimental range. Sesamol exhibited the strongest effect on OIT prolongation at all tested concentrations, likely due to its superior radical-scavenging ability. Among the seven volatile phenolics, 4-vinylguaiacol was particularly effective in extending the OIT of PSO across all additive levels. At the maximum concentration, it prolonged the OIT by 1.84 h, followed by 4-ethylguaiacol and 4-methylphenol (1.05 and 0.64 h, respectively). Acetovanillone, guaiacol, isoacetovanillone, and vanillin exerted relatively weaker effects but still prolonged OIT to some extent. The effect of 4-vinylguaiacol on improving the OIT of vegetable oils observed in this model system is consistent with previously reported results in other oil systems. For instance, Tańska et al. (2018) demonstrated that 4-vinylguaiacol (200–800 mg/kg) effectively extended the OIT of cold-pressed rapeseed oil, flaxseed oil, and extra virgin olive oil, with superior efficacy compared to 4-vinylsyringol.Fig. 2Effect of different concentrations of phenolic compounds on the OIT of PSO samples. Effects of phenolics on the thermal oxidation of PSO

TGA continuously monitors the weight change of oils caused by oxidation under programmed heating conditions to evaluate the oxidative stability of oils (Coni, Podestà, & Catone, 2004). According to the TGA curve (Fig. 3), PSO (Fig. 3A) began to decompose at 262.26 °C and entered a rapid decomposition phase at 321.41 °C due to volatilization of oxidation products (Zhao, Sun, Lü, & Li, 2020). Similar to the addition of sesamol (269.11 °C), the addition of guaiacol (292.43 °C), 4-ethylguaiacol (270.98 °C), 4-vinylguaiacol (266.74 °C), and 4-methylphenol (263.25 °C) also increased the initial decomposition temperature of PSO. This suggests that these phenolics can inhibit the oxidation of PSO (Gao et al., 2024). However, vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone failed to raise the initial decomposition temperature. DSC can also be used to assess the oxidative stability of oils (Gao et al., 2024; Tan, Man, Selamat, & Yusoff, 2002). As shown in Fig. S2, the oxidation onset temperatures of all oil samples ranged from 160.56 °C to 180.91 °C, consistent with reported values for edible oils (130–180 °C) (Oomah & Sitter, 2009). The initial oxidation temperature of PSO alone was 160.56 °C. After adding guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, 4-methylphenol, vanillin, acetovanillone, isoacetovanillone, and sesamol, the initial oxidation temperatures increased to 171.68 °C, 166.43 °C,169.84 °C, 165.95 °C, 167.58 °C, 166.81 °C, 168.10 °C, and 180.91 °C, respectively, confirming their ability to inhibit oxidation. Based on TGA and DSC results, all phenolics exhibit some ability to mitigate lipid oxidation, with vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone showing relatively weaker effects.Fig. 3TGA curves of PSO samples mixed with different phenolic compounds.Fig. 3

Effects of phenolics on AV, k232, and k268 during accelerated storage of PSO

AV, an indicator of free fatty acid content in oil, reflects the degree of hydrolytic rancidity (Meng et al., 2021). Fig. 4A shows the changes in AV of PSO during storage. All samples exhibited gradual AV increases with prolonged storage. The AV of the control group increased from 0.16 mg/g (day 0) to 0.52 mg/g (day 15). The addition of guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, 4-methylphenol, and sesamol mitigated the elevation in AV of PSO samples during storage, with their values reaching 0.48, 0.37, 0.32, 0.47, and 0.23 mg/g on day 15, respectively. Sesamol (the positive control) was most effective in inhibiting AV elevation, followed by 4-vinylguaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-methylphenol, and guaiacol. Mikołajczak et al. (2023) similarly reported that adding 800 mg/kg 4-vinylguaiacol to cold-pressed flaxseed oil reduced AV by over 40% after 10 days of storage. However, the addition of vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone promoted the increase in AV, with final values of 0.78, 0.64, and 0.67 mg/g, respectively, which were significantly higher than that of the control group (0.52 mg/g).Fig. 4AV (A), K232 (B), K268 (C), PV (D), ρ-AnV (E), and TOTOX (F) of PSO samples mixed with different phenolic compounds during accelerated storage. During lipid oxidation, unsaturated fatty acids form conjugated diene and conjugated triene structures, resulting in characteristic absorbance at 232 and 268 nm, respectively (Quek et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 4B and C, K232 and K268 values of all oil samples gradually increased with prolonged storage. In the control group, K232 and K268 increased from 1.94 and 1.89 (day 0) to 11.98 and 3.32 (day 15), respectively. The formation of conjugated dienes was significantly faster than that of conjugated trienes, consistent with previous reports (Arab et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2021; Mikołajczak et al., 2023). Sesamol was most effective in inhibiting double-bond alterations in unsaturated fatty acids among all treatments. Of the seven lignin-derived phenolics, 4-vinylguaiacol was effective in reducing the elevation of conjugated diene and triene values, with K232 and K268 reaching 9.28 and 3.09 on day 15, respectively. In contrast, a previous study reported that 4-vinylguaiacol promoted the increase in conjugated diene values during storage of cold-pressed flaxseed oil (Mikołajczak et al., 2023). This discrepancy may be due to the purification of sesame oil in this study, which eliminated pro-oxidative factors such as metal ions, free fatty acids, and hydroperoxides. This purification allows a clearer reflection of the intrinsic role of 4-vinylguaiacol in inhibiting lipid oxidation. However, guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-methylphenol, vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone had no significant inhibitory effect on the increase in conjugated diene and triene values of PSO, or even promoted their elevation.

Effects of phenolics on PV, ρ-AnV, and TOTOX during accelerated storage of PSO

PV and ρ-AnV reflect the contents of primary oxidation products (hydroperoxides) and secondary oxidation products (aldehydes, mainly 2-olefins and 2,4-diolefins) in vegetable oils, respectively (Harlina, Ma, Shahzad, & Khalifa, 2022). As shown in Fig. 4D and E, PV and ρ-AnV of all oil samples increased during storage. In the control group, PV increased from an initial 2.13 meq/kg to 163.22 meq/kg by day 15, while ρ-AnV increased from undetectable to 23.32 (p < 0.01). Sesamol showed excellent inhibition against the accumulation of PV and ρ-AnV. Among the seven phenolics, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, and 4-methylphenol consistently mitigated increases in PV and ρ-AnV during storage. By the end of accelerated storage, these compounds limited PV to 132.06, 64.61, and 146.36 meq/kg, and ρ-AnV to 8.79, 4.55, and 16.42, respectively (p < 0.05). In contrast, guaiacol, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone exerted weak inhibitory effects on PV and ρ-AnV. Vanillin showed almost no inhibition of PV and significantly (p < 0.01) increased ρ-AnV. This may be because the aromatic aldehyde structure of vanillin acts as a direct substrate for the anisidine reagent and interferes with the colorimetric reaction, rather than reflecting actual oxidative deterioration. TOTOX values integrate both primary and secondary oxidation products and thus serve as a comprehensive index for evaluating the overall oxidative degradation of PSO (Meng et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 4F, among the seven phenolics, compared with the control group (349.77), 4-ethylguaiacol (272.92), 4-vinylguaiacol (133.77), and 4-methylphenol (309.15) significantly (p < 0.01) reduced TOTOX values at the end of storage. These results further confirm the ability of these three phenolics to retard oil oxidation.

Effects of phenolics on fatty acid composition during accelerated storage of PSO

During accelerated storage, the fatty acid composition of PSO changed significantly due to lipid oxidation (Table 1). The content of saturated fatty acids (SFA) showed a continuous increasing trend, whereas unsaturated fatty acids (UFA) decreased gradually. After 15 days of storage, the SFA content in the control group increased from 13.89% to 14.09%, and the UFA content decreased from 85.65% to 83.68%. Notably, the reduction in linoleic acid (C18:2) reached 1.29%, which was significantly higher than that of oleic acid (C18:1, 0.67%), indicating that polyunsaturated fatty acids are more sensitive to oxidative stress. The observed changes in fatty acid composition were mainly attributed to the oxidation and cleavage of unsaturated bonds. Under accelerated storage conditions, UFA, especially polyunsaturated fatty acids, are prone to hydrogen abstraction and peroxidation reactions, leading to chain scission and consequent reduction in their relative content (Mikołajczak et al., 2023). Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed in the protective effects of different phenolic compounds on the fatty acid composition of PSO. Sesamol exhibited the strongest antioxidant activity, with the UFA content remaining at 84.81% after 15 days, which was significantly higher than that of the control group (83.68%). Meanwhile, the SFA content (13.94%) in the sesamol group showed no significant difference from the initial value, suggesting that sesamol effectively inhibited the oxidative loss of UFA and the relative accumulation of SFA. Following sesamol, 4-vinylguaiacol exhibited the second strongest protective effect, with a UFA retention rate of 84.28%, significantly higher than that of other lignin-derived phenolics. Both 4-ethylguaiacol and 4-methylphenol were able to delay the degradation of UFAs to varying degrees, with UFA contents at the end of storage standing at 83.92% and 83.91%, respectively. Phenolic compounds exert antioxidant effects mainly by donating hydrogen atoms or electrons to scavenge free radicals, thus protecting unsaturated fatty acids from oxidation and maintaining the stability of fatty acid composition in PSO. In contrast, guaiacol, vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone exhibited weaker protective effects; their UFA contents at the end of storage (83.67%, 83.33%, 83.65%, and 83.59%, respectively) were comparable to or lower than that of the control group (83.68%), indicating their limited ability to inhibit PSO oxidation.

PCA was used to visualize differences in the effects of each phenolic on oil quality indices at the end of storage (Fig. 5). The first two principal components (PC1 = 76.6%, PC2 = 12.7%) accounted for 89.3% of the total variance, effectively reflecting the influence of phenolic additives on oil stability. The score plot (Fig. 5A) revealed clear separation among different treatment groups, indicating that phenolics exerted distinct effects on the measured indices during storage. The loading plot (Fig. 5B) showed that C16:0, C18:0, SFA, ρ-AnV, AV, K268, K232, PV, and TOTOX clustered on the right side and contributed positively to PC1, indicating strong intercorrelation among these oxidation-related variables. In contrast, C18:1, C18:2, and UFA clustered on the left side, with interrelated trends. Notably, the vanillin group lay even closer to the positive oxidation-related variables along PC1 than the control, suggesting that vanillin promoted increases in these variables and exerted a pro-oxidant effect. Although the 63 °C accelerated storage test may involve reaction pathways differing from long-term ambient storage, this method remains highly efficient for comparing and ranking antioxidant efficacy. Quantum chemistry computations

Quantum chemical calculations can reliably predict the antioxidant capacity of phenolic compounds (Tang et al., 2023). By comparing computational results with experimental data on lipid inhibition, reliable structure-activity relationships can be established. Based on the above results, four lignin-derived volatile phenolics (guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, and 4-methylphenol) were selected for quantum chemical calculations due to their superior in vitro and in-oil antioxidant performance. Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) is a powerful tool for analyzing chemical reactivity and molecular recognition (Saloni, Kumari, Ranjan, & Chakraborty, 2022). MEP surfaces are color-coded to delineate areas with different electrostatic potentials. The distribution of charges can be used to determine molecular interactions and the nature of chemical bonds. Calculated at the B3LYP/6–311++G(d,p) level (Fig. 6, left), MEP plots revealed electron density distributions identifying sites for electrophilic attack, nucleophilic reactions, and hydrogen bonding. The most negatively charged sites (red regions) are mainly centered on oxygen atoms in the hydroxyl (-OH) group, indicating favorable sites for electrophilic attack. Positively charged sites (blue regions) correspond to nucleophilic reaction zones. These differently charged regions closely correlate with the physicochemical properties of molecules, endowing them with unique reactivity and interaction profiles essential to antioxidant function.Fig. 6Molecular electrostatic potential and frontier molecular orbital analysis of guaiacol (A), 4-ethylguaiacol (B), 4-vinylguaiacol (C), and 4-methylphenol (D).Fig. 6

Molecular electrostatic potential and frontier molecular orbital analysis of guaiacol (A), 4-ethylguaiacol (B), 4-vinylguaiacol (C), and 4-methylphenol (D).

Frontier molecular orbitals are used in quantum chemistry to characterize molecular reactivity, specifically referring to the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) (Nada et al., 2022). HOMO represents the electron-donating orbital, designated as H, while LUMO corresponds to the electron-accepting orbital, denoted as L. The energy difference ΔE (ELUMO minus EHOMO) represents the energy barrier a molecule must overcome to reach the transition state. A decrease in ΔE correlates with a low energy threshold for the transition state, indicating that the compound is less stable and more reactive (Xue et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 6 (right), the HOMO-LUMO plots and the energies of guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, and 4-methylphenol were characterized. In the HOMO-LUMO plots, green and red represent the negative and positive phases of the orbitals, respectively. The ΔE values for guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, and 4-methylphenol were 5.72995 eV, 5.614742 eV, 4.67197 eV, and 5.636984 eV, respectively. This quantitative difference in ΔE indicates a relative reactivity class, with 4-vinylguaiacol being the most reactive, followed by 4-ethylguaiacol and 4-methylphenol, while guaiacol is the least reactive. This finding was empirically supported by accelerated oxidation testing at 63 °C, where 4-vinylguaiacol demonstrated superior oxidation resistance.

CDFT plays an important role in elucidating molecular properties by transforming inherently difficult-to-measure chemical properties into quantifiable metrics with high precision. The core of this analysis lies in various key chemical parameters, including vertical ionization potential (VIP), vertical electron affinity (EA), Mulliken electronegativity, chemical potential, hardness, softness, electrophilicity index, and nucleophilicity index (Flores-Holguín, Frau, & Glossman-Mitnik, 2019). The CDFT indices of the phenolics are presented in Table S3. 4-Vinylguaiacol had the lowest VIP (7.1592 eV), indicating reduced stability of its valence electrons and enhanced chemical reactivity. Electronegativity refers to the ability of an atom or group to attract electrons in a chemical bond; high electronegativity indicates strong electron-absorbing capacity (Al Garadi et al., 2021). 4-Vinylguaiacol and 4-methylphenol exhibited high electronegativity, meaning they tended to absorb electrons in chemical reactions. Hardness and softness indicate the ability of a molecule to resist electronic perturbation and to accept electrons or undergo polarization, respectively (Rahman et al., 2020). Hard molecules have a large ΔE, while soft molecules have a small ΔE (Obi-Egbedi, Obot, El-Khaiary, Umoren, & Ebenso, 2011). Therefore, soft molecules are more reactive than hard molecules. As shown in Table S3, among the four phenolics, guaiacol had the highest hardness value (10.086 eV), indicating it was the hardest molecule with the weakest reactivity. 4-Vinylguaiacol had the highest softness (0.1171 eV−1), reflecting it was the softest molecule with the strongest reactivity. The electrophilicity index indicates the ability of a molecule to acquire electrons (Xue et al., 2025). A molecule with a high electrophilicity index is a better electrophilic reagent, while one with a low electrophilicity index is a better nucleophilic reagent (Rahman et al., 2020). Among the four phenolics, 4-vinylguaiacol and 4-ethylguaiacol had the highest and lowest electrophilicity indices, respectively, indicating they acted as effective electrophilic and nucleophilic reagents, respectively. Overall, 4-vinylguaiacol exhibits a more pronounced tendency to react with free radicals.

In addition to frontier molecular orbital analysis, the O—H bond BDE (Fig. S3) and AIP (Table S4) were calculated to further evaluate the radical-scavenging potential of the selected phenolics. 4-Vinylguaiacol exhibited the lowest BDE (3.2868 eV) among the four compounds, followed by 4-ethylguaiacol (3.3464 eV), 4-methylphenol (3.3724 eV), and guaiacol (3.4014 eV). A lower BDE indicates a weaker O—H bond and thus a higher tendency to donate a hydrogen atom to terminate free radical chain reactions (Thbayh & Fiser, 2022). Similarly, 4-vinylguaiacol showed the lowest AIP (6.1266 eV), reflecting its greater ease of electron donation via the single electron transfer (SET) pathway. These thermodynamic parameters are fully consistent with the experimental rankings observed in oil-based assays, confirming the superior antioxidant performance of 4-vinylguaiacol.

The superior antioxidant activity of 4-vinylguaiacol observed in the PSO model can be rationalized by comparing its structural features with those of other lignin-derived phenolics, in conjunction with the quantum chemical parameters calculated in this study. As inferred from the in vitro antioxidant assays, vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone showed poor antioxidant activity due to the presence of carbonyl groups, which may also account for their weak performance in the lipid system. Compared with guaiacol, the introduction of para-substituents in 4-vinylguaiacol and 4-ethylguaiacol can extend the conjugated system or provide electron-donating effects, both of which enhance radical stabilization. Notably, the vinyl group in 4-vinylguaiacol participates in resonance delocalization of the unpaired electron through conjugation with the aromatic ring and the double bond, thereby significantly lowering the BDE of the O—H bond. In contrast, the ethyl group in 4-ethylguaiacol exerts only a weak inductive electron-donating effect without extending conjugation, providing less stabilization to the phenoxyl radical. This distinction is clearly reflected in their calculated parameters: 4-vinylguaiacol exhibited the lowest BDE and AIP, the highest chemical softness, and the smallest ΔE, indicating the greatest molecular reactivity. In comparison, 4-ethylguaiacol showed higher BDE and a larger ΔE, consistent with its intermediate antioxidant efficacy. Overall, these results demonstrate that under hydrogen atom transfer (HAT)-dominant lipid oxidation conditions, the combination of a conjugating para-substituent with an electron-donating methoxy group in the ortho position (i.e., the guaiacol backbone) provides the optimal structural features for effective radical scavenging. It should be noted that the PSO used in this study was devoid of endogenous antioxidants but maintained its original fatty acid composition. Although the simplified model system differs from real commercial sesame oil, the fundamental oxidation behavior of the lipid matrix was not altered. The observed structure-activity relationships and antioxidant interactions are therefore universal and can be reasonably generalized to practical oil systems.

Binary interaction analysis

Both experimental and theoretical calculations indicated that 4-vinylguaiacol exhibited relatively superior antioxidant activity among volatile phenolic compounds.

For designing effective natural antioxidant systems, understanding the interactions between individual components is crucial. Therefore, a preliminary investigation was conducted into the binary interactions between 4-vinylguaiacol and two key endogenous antioxidants, sesamol and γ-tocopherol, to determine their combined effects on lipid oxidation (Fig. 7). As shown in Fig. 7A, the OIT of the 4-vinylguaiacol and sesamol combination increased with increasing total concentration (MC1-MC5) and decreased as the proportion of sesamol declined (MR1-MR5). However, the combination of the two exhibited significant antagonistic effects (SI = 0.53–0.75) in a concentration-dependent manner. For different proportion ratios of the two components, antagonism first intensified and then weakened as the proportion of 4-vinylguaiacol shifted from minority to majority relative to sesamol, with the strongest antagonism observed at an equal proportion. These results demonstrate that the concentration and ratio of antioxidants affect the degree of interaction (Liu et al., 2020). Similarly, the OIT of the 4-vinylguaiacol and γ-tocopherol combination increased with increasing total concentration (MC1-MC5) and decreased as the proportion of γ-tocopherol declined (MR1-MR5) (Fig. 7B). Notably, the combinations showed only slight antagonism (SI = 0.86–0.90) in cases with higher additions (MC4 and MC5) and higher proportions (MR4 and MR5) of 4-vinylguaiacol, while most combinations exhibited near-additive effects (SI = 0.95–1.00). The antagonistic interaction between 4-vinylguaiacol and sesamol can be explained by two plausible mechanisms (Liu et al., 2022): (i) a regeneration cycle, in which the more potent antioxidant (sesamol) preferentially reduces the phenoxyl radical of 4-vinylguaiacol, consuming its own reducing capacity and thereby decreasing the net antioxidant efficiency; or (ii) formation of less active intermolecular complexes between the two compounds, which would lower the overall radical-scavenging activity. Based on our experimental data—sesamol consistently exhibited significantly stronger antioxidant activity than 4-vinylguaiacol in all in vitro assays and in the PSO model—the regeneration hypothesis appears more plausible. As a more efficient hydrogen atom donor, sesamol can rapidly quench lipid peroxyl radicals. In the mixture, it may also reduce the 4-vinylguaiacol phenoxyl radical back to its parent phenol form (a regeneration cycle). This diversion of sesamol's reducing capacity away from direct lipid radical quenching would result in net antagonism. To definitively distinguish between regeneration and complex formation, however, techniques such as electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy, direct electrochemical measurements, or kinetic analyses are required; thus, this represents a priority for future research. For the mixture of 4-vinylguaiacol and γ-tocopherol, the predominantly additive effects suggest that the two compounds do not interfere with each other's radical-scavenging pathways. They likely operate independently or via complementary mechanisms within the oil matrix, with no significant cross-reaction that would lead to antagonism or synergism. Overall, the combination of 4-vinylguaiacol with γ-tocopherol was superior to its combination with sesamol. The former combination could serve as a potential antioxidant formulation for edible oils, although further ratio optimization is needed to maximize its protection.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the antioxidant hierarchy of seven lignin-derived volatile phenolics in a purified sesame oil model. Results showed that guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol, 4-vinylguaiacol, and 4-methylphenol exhibited significantly stronger overall antioxidant activity than vanillin, acetovanillone, and isoacetovanillone. Among these, 4-vinylguaiacol demonstrated relatively superior efficacy in retarding lipid oxidation, as reflected by its remarkable ability to prolong OIT, enhance thermal stability, and suppress the increase of key oxidation indices (e.g., AV, PV, ρ-AnV, TOTOX) as well as the degradation of UFA during accelerated storage. The antioxidant efficacy ranking in the lipid system was highly consistent with quantum chemical calculations. A series of parameters confirmed that 4-vinylguaiacol possessed the strongest intrinsic molecular reactivity and antioxidant activity. This consistency verified that intrinsic thermodynamic reactivity and electronic structure are important determinants of antioxidant performance in real oil matrices. Furthermore, binary interaction analysis revealed that the most active phenolic, 4-vinylguaiacol, exhibited antagonistic effects with sesamol, but near-additive effects with γ-tocopherol across most ratios and concentrations. In conclusion, this study provides a novel perspective for understanding the multifunctional roles of volatile flavor phenolics in edible oils. In particular, the relatively superior activity of 4-vinylguaiacol holds important prospective and guiding significance for its application as a novel natural antioxidant derived from food by-products (e.g., sesame hull lignin) in other oxidation-prone oil systems.

However, the concentration of phenolics used in this study was higher than their natural levels in sesame oil. Direct application at this concentration may pose food safety risks (Njimou et al., 2021; Yangui, Njimou, Cicci, Bravi, Abderrabba, & Chianese, 2017) and introduce pronounced smoky sensory characteristics (Zhao et al., 2025b). Nevertheless, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the differences in antioxidant capacity among seven homologous phenolics and to assess the application potential of the most effective ones. Future research should address the practical challenge of delivering an effective antioxidant dose of these relatively potent phenolics (particularly 4-vinylguaiacol) while considering their impact on final product safety and flavor profile. In this context, optimization and validation based on sensory thresholds in complex food matrices represent a promising approach for translating current theoretical research into practical applications. Further studies could also explore synergistic strategies with other natural antioxidants to reduce the required dosage while maintaining efficacy.
