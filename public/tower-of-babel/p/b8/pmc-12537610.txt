Preliminary investigation of the association between desmoglein-2 expression and patient prognosis following radical surgery for colon cancer
Authors: Yanqing Li, Xiaoxia Wang, Xin Wang, Xiangdong Jia, Meng Ren, Tianxiang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDesmoglein-2 (DSG2), a calcium-dependent transmembrane glycoprotein, serves as a critical mediator of intercellular adhesion by functioning as an integral component of desmosomal junctions. Aberrant expression of DSG2 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of a diverse array of malignancies. However, the expression patterns and functional significance of DSG2 in the context of colon cancer (CC) remain inadequately characterized. Consequently, this study was designed to comprehensively elucidate DSG2 expression and evaluate its potential prognostic utility in CC.MethodsTumor specimens from 104 CC patients and 77 samples of adjacent paraneoplastic tissue were procured, accompanied by the corresponding clinicopathological data from the contributing patients. Immunohistochemical analyses were performed on all samples using tissue microarrays. Variations in DSG2 expression between CC tissues and adjacent paraneoplastic tissues, along with the association between DSG2 expression and clinicopathological parameters of CC, were evaluated using the χ2 test. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was utilized for both univariate and multivariate analyses, while survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method. All statistical computations and graph plotting were executed using R software (version 4.2.0).ResultsDSG2 expression was significantly lower in CC tissues compared to peri-cancerous tissues (P < 0.05). DSG2 expression exhibited significant correlations with tumor differentiation (P = 0.041) and distant metastasis (P < 0.05). Univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that overall survival (OS) in CC patients was significantly associated with tumor size (HR = 1.649, 95% CI: 1.007–2.700, P = 0.047), lymph node metastasis (HR = 2.102, 95% CI: 1.284–3.440, P = 0.003), distant metastasis (HR = 2.459, 95% CI: 1.502–4.026, P < 0.001), and DSG2 expression (HR = 0.399, 95% CI: 0.237–0.672, P = 0.001). Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that lymph node metastasis (HR = 1.872, 95% CI: 1.136–3.085, P = 0.014), distant metastasis (HR = 1.756, 95% CI: 1.024–3.011, P = 0.041), and low DSG2 expression (HR = 0.529, 95% CI: 0.305–0.916, P = 0.023) are independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS).ConclusionDiminished DSG2 expression is correlated with reduced survival rates in CC patients, suggesting that DSG2 could serve as a potential prognostic biomarker for CC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-025-05006-5.

Background

Colon cancer (CC), ranking as the third most prevalent malignancy globally and the second primary cause of cancer-associated mortality, significantly endangers human health [1]. Recent data from authoritative online epidemiologic databases indicate that over 1.9 million new cases of CC were identified in 2020, with 900,000 deaths documented concurrently [2]. Age represents the predominant risk factor for CC; while its incidence remains low among individuals younger than 50, projections indicate a substantial increase in CC cases in China due to the rising median age of the population [3]. Despite the escalating incidence and mortality rates of CC and advancements in screening technologies, numerous patients continue to be diagnosed at advanced stages, exhibiting multiple symptoms like rectal bleeding or colonic obstruction [4]. Nevertheless, the repertoire of effective therapeutic targets for CC remains limited [5]. Consequently, alongside enhancements in surgical treatments and radiotherapy, it is imperative to identify new diagnostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets for CC.

DSG2, a calcium-binding transmembrane glycoprotein, is a member of the cadherin family. Located on chromosome 18q12.1, DSG2 plays a crucial role in the assembly of desmosomes [6, 7]. The extracellular domain of DSG2 facilitates cell–cell adhesion, and its cytoplasmic domain interacts with desmosomal plaque proteins, linking to the intermediate filament cytoskeleton inside the cell [8, 9]. Ongoing research has revealed that the biological functions of DSG2 extend well beyond simple mediation of intercellular connections. Numerous studies have demonstrated that DSG2 is implicated in cellular proliferation and apoptosis, significantly contributing to tumor progression [10]. Elevated expression of DSG2 has been documented in cancers such as ovarian [11], breast [12], non-small cell lung [13], pulmonary adenocarcinoma [14, 15], and hepatocellular carcinoma [16], and is strongly associated with poor prognosis. Conversely, low expression of DSG2 in gallbladder [17] and pancreatic [18] cancers has been linked to enhanced capabilities for tumor cell migration and invasion. Liu et al.’s [19] research indicates that DSG2 is essential for maintaining intestinal barrier function, with its dysfunction potentially leading to increased intestinal permeability and subsequent inflammatory bowel diseases. Individuals with inflammatory bowel diseases exhibit an elevated risk of developing CC relative to the general population [20]. Nonetheless, the expression and functional role of DSG2 in CC remains underexplored. This study seeks to elucidate the expression of DSG2 in CC and its prognostic implications.

Methods

Patients and materials

Cancer tissues from 104 CC patients, along with adjacent normal tissues from 77 cases (totaling 181 samples, Table S1), were collected at the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region People’s Hospital. These samples were derived from patients treated between 2010 and 2015, chosen according to well-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All specimens were fixed in formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Comprehensive clinicopathological data were collected, encompassing patient gender, age, tumor size, differentiation status, depth of invasion, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis. The cohort consisted of 49 males (47.11%) and 55 females (52.89%). The age range of the patients was 25 to 85 years, with a median age of 64; 51.92% (54 cases) were 64 years or younger, while 48.08% (50 cases) were older than 64. Regarding tumor size, 60 cases measured 5 cm or less (57.69%), and 44 cases exceeded 5 cm (42.31%). Histologically, 36 cases exhibited poor differentiation, while 68 displayed well differentiation (65.38%). Concerning serosal invasion, 15 cases exhibited no invasion (14.42%), while 89 cases demonstrated serosal penetration (85.58%). Lymph node metastasis was not detected in 64 cases (61.54%) and was evident in 40 cases (38.46%). Distant metastasis was absent in 70 cases (67.31%) and present in 34 cases (32.69%) (Table 1). The patients, predominantly of Han and Mongolian ethnicities, were residents of Inner Mongolia. None of the patients received radiotherapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, or immunotherapy before the surgery. All patients underwent elective surgery and received adjuvant chemotherapy (the Xelox regimen, oxaliplatin + capecitabine, for 8 cycles). Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from the initial biopsy confirming diagnosis to the time of death. Follow-up assessments were conducted via outpatient visits and telephone calls over a period of 90 months. This study was approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee for Human Research (202400901L). Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE guidelines (Table S2). Clinicopathologic features

Databases exploration

RNA sequencing data in Fragments Per Kilobase per Million (FPKM) format, along with the corresponding clinicopathological details for 480 colorectal cancer (CC) patients, were obtained from the TCGA-COAD project within The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The RNA sequencing data, initially in FPKM format, were systematically converted into transcripts per million (TPM) format to enhance data comparability. The TCGA database, which is publicly accessible, ensured that informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of data collection. The UALCAN platform (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) facilitates comprehensive analysis of cancer transcriptomes, protein expression, and patient survival data derived from the TCGA database, specifically to assess the expression of the DSG2 coding gene. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was employed to elucidate the functions of genes differentially expressed in association with DSG2 in colorectal cancer specimens, focusing on pathways enriched at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 and a nominal P value < 0.05.

Histopathology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and hematoxylin and Eosin staining

Tissues fixed in 10% formalin underwent sequential washing and dehydration through a graded alcohol series (70%, 80%, 95% I, 95% II, 100% I, 100% II). Tissue clearing involved xylene, initiating with a 30-min incubation in a 1:1 anhydrous alcohol and xylene mixture, followed by sequential 15-min exposures to xylene I and continued with xylene II until optimal clarity was attained. Cleared tissues were promptly transferred with forceps into pre-melted molten paraffin wax within a thermostat-controlled oven, ensuring efficient displacement of xylene. The molten wax was decanted into an embedding cassette; subsequently, wax-impregnated tissues were rapidly relocated into the cassette using pre-heated forceps, arranged for ideal sectioning, and allowed to cool and solidify. The solidified wax block was meticulously trimmed and secured onto a microtome. The microtome blade was precisely adjusted to a 10-degree angle, enabling the cutting of 5-µm-thick sections. Sections were carefully floated ribbons-down on water to eliminate any wrinkles. Pre-cleaned glass slides were coated with glycerol protein, then gently maneuvered beneath the flattened wax sections to lift them from the water. The wax sections were meticulously aligned on the slides, which were subsequently positioned on a staining rack and transferred to a thermostat-controlled oven to dry at 40 °C for 1 day. Routine hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was conducted, and subsequent verification confirmed that the majority of histological structures were preserved, rendering the samples satisfactory for analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Hematoxylin–eosin staining

Hematoxylin–eosin staining

Immunohistochemical staining for Desmoglein-2 (DSG2) was performed employing the EnVision™ Detection Kit, strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s protocols. The kit includes a positive control, while phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) substitutes the DSG2 antibody for negative control applications. All colon tissue samples were sectioned from standardized paraffin-embedded blocks of excised tissues. Sample sectioning sites were designated by a qualified pathologist. Sections measuring 5 µm in thickness were precisely cut from the paraffin blocks and mounted onto adhesive slides. The slides underwent dewaxing by immersion in xylene (three cycles, 10 min each), followed by a descending ethanol series (100%, 85%, 70%, each for 10 min) and subsequent rinsing in water. Slides were incubated in 0.3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 10 min to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by three successive PBS washes. Antigen retrieval was facilitated using an EDTA-based solution (heat-induced epitope retrieval) at 96 °C for 30 min. Slides were blocked with a 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution, precisely applied using an immunostaining pen. Sections were subsequently incubated with polyclonal rabbit anti-human DSG2 IgG, diluted 1:2000, at 37 °C for 2 h, then washed three times with PBS. Following this, sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary anti-rabbit antibody at 37 °C for 30 min and subsequently washed thrice in PBS. DAB substrate was applied to develop color, and sections were then counterstained using hematoxylin. Finally, slides were dehydrated, clarified in xylene, and securely mounted beneath neutral balsam.

Evaluation of IHC data

The immunohistochemical staining results were independently assessed by two pathologists who were blinded to the clinical and pathological details of the patients involved. Staining intensity was categorized as follows: no staining (0 points), weak staining (1 point), moderate staining (2 points), and strong staining (3 points). The percentage of positively stained cells was quantified as: less than 5% (0 points), 5% to 25% (1 point), 26% to 50% (2 points), 51% to 75% (3 points), and over 75% (4 points). The final DSG2 expression score for each specimen was determined by multiplying the staining intensity score by the percentage of stained cells score, with the resultant scores documented for subsequent analysis. Scores of 4 or greater were classified as high expression, whereas scores below this threshold were deemed low expression.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were executed utilizing SPSS version 20.0, whereas bioinformatics analyses and graphical representations were conducted using R software (version 4.2.0). The χ2 test was employed to ascertain the differences in DSG2 expression between colorectal cancer and adjacent normal tissues, and to evaluate the association between DSG2 expression and the clinical-pathological parameters of the disease. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to explore the impact of variables on outcomes. Survival curves were generated employing the Kaplan–Meier method, with the results subsequently validated via the Log-rank test. A P value of less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Expression of DSG2 gene in pan-cancer

RNA sequencing data from 33 cancer projects were retrieved and processed with the STAR aligner from TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Subsequently, data in transcripts per million (TPM) format were extracted. Statistical analyses were performed using the stats and car packages in R, contingent upon the data satisfying the required statistical criteria; absent these criteria, analyses were not conducted. Data visualization was achieved using the ggplot2 package in R (Fig. 2). Results demonstrated that the DSG2 gene exhibited high expression in various cancers, including bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), and others, indicating a pattern consistent across multiple tumor types. Conversely, reduced expression of DSG2 was noted in several cancers including breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), with statistical significance (P < 0.05).Fig. 2Differential expression of DSG2 in tumors and normal tissues of various human systems

Survival curves constructed using the Kaplan–Meier method (Fig. 10) and analyzed with the log-rank test revealed that Tumor Size (> 5 cm), lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and reduced DSG2 expression were linked to poorer OS (P < 0.05). Discussion

In this study, utilizing the TCGA dataset, we found that the DSG2 gene is significantly overexpressed in nine distinct types of human cancers, including bladder urothelial carcinoma, cervical squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, kidney chromophobe, lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, stomach adenocarcinoma, and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma. Conversely, we observed significant underexpression of the DSG2 gene in four cancer types: breast invasive carcinoma, colon adenocarcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, and rectal adenocarcinoma. Additionally, reduced expression of the DSG2 gene was noted in colorectal cancer tissue samples from the TCGA database, a finding that was corroborated by data from the UALCAN database.

Prior research has demonstrated the significant impact of inflammatory biomarkers on the prognosis of CC [23, 24]. Our findings suggest a potential association between DSG2 gene expression and tumor immune cell infiltration. Our research results show that the expression level of the DSG2 gene is positively correlated with central memory T cells (Tcm), T helper cells, and T helper cell 2 (Th2 cells). Tcm is a subtype derived from naive T cells after antigen activation, with a higher recall response in vivo and the ability to home to lymph nodes to receive antigen restimulation. Klebanoff et al. [25] found in their study that Tcm cells possess super anti-tumor capabilities. The CD62L+ expressed by them can more easily receive the antigen information presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in lymph nodes and get secondarily activated. Tcm plays an important role in regulating the innate and adaptive components of the immune system, making it a key player in cancer immunology. T helper cells play an important role in the development of humoral immunity by regulating the cellular responses that occur in the germinal center [26]. It is a distinct subpopulation of CD4+ cells, expressing a series of cell surface molecules, such as CXC-chemokine receptor 5 (CXCR5), co-receptor programmed cell death 1 (PD1), B cell lymphoma 6 (BCL-6), and inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS), and these molecules play important roles in the interaction with B cells [27]. As a subtype of T helper cells, Th2 cells secrete cytokines such as interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-5, and IL-10, which contribute to the production of antibodies and are related to humoral immune responses. T helper cells and Th2 cells are an important part of tumor immune surveillance. In mouse models and clinical studies, when their activities are weakened, the susceptibility and metastasis of cancer will be higher [28]. We observed that DSG2 expression levels positively correlate with central memory T cells (Tcm), T helper cells (Th), and T helper type 2 cells (Th2). Tcm cells, originating from naive T cells upon antigen activation, exhibit a robust in vivo recall response and can return to lymph nodes for re-stimulation by antigen-presenting cells (APC). Klebanoff et al. [25] identified that Tcm cells possess superior anti-tumor capabilities, primarily attributed to their expression of CD62L+, which facilitates their reactivation in lymph nodes upon interaction with APCs. Tcm cells play a critical role in modulating both the innate and adaptive components of the immune system, underscoring their pivotal importance in cancer immunology. T helper cells are essential for orchestrating cellular responses in germinal centers, playing a significant role in the development of humoral immunity [26]. They constitute a distinct subset of CD4+ cells, characterized by the expression of surface molecules like CXCR5, PD1, BCL-6, and ICOS, crucial for interactions with B cells [27]. Th2 cells, a subtype of T helper cells, secrete cytokines including interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-5, and IL-10, which are instrumental in antibody production and pivotal for humoral immune responses. The activity of T helper and Th2 cells, key components of tumor immune surveillance, is known to reduce the susceptibility and metastatic potential of cancers when diminished, as evidenced in mouse models and clinical studies [28]. Therefore, we hypothesize that low expression of DSG2 may play an important role in the immune escape mechanism of colon cancer cells, thereby promoting the growth and progression of colorectal cancer (CC). However, more in vitro experiments will be needed to verify this hypothesis in the future.

In this investigation, we employed the TCGA-COAD dataset to conduct a differential analysis of the raw gene expression matrix. GSEA demonstrated significant pathway enrichment for the low DSG2 expression group, notably in TP53 phosphorylation regulation, sumoylation of chromation organization proteins, sumoylation of ubiquitintlation proteins, the G1 phase of the cell cycle, mitotic telophase cytokinesis, and DNA methylation signaling pathways. TP53, an essential tumor suppressor, plays a crucial role in both the development and therapeutic strategies of malignancies [29]. While definitive conclusions regarding the role of TP53 phosphorylation in CC progression remain elusive, ongoing research, including studies by Wang and Sun [30], has identified high TP53 expression in malignancies, with TP53 mutations associated with poor prognoses across various cancers. Furthermore, several studies have corroborated that TP53 mutations can diminish anti-tumor immunity and attenuate the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies [31–33]. The low DSG2 expression group exhibited enriched differential gene expression in pathways associated with sumoylation of chromation organization proteins, the G1 phase of the cell cycle, and mitotic telophase cytokinesis, underscoring a critical role for DSG2 expression in regulating tumor cell proliferation. Moreover, human tumorigenesis is intricately linked with sumoylation of chromation organization proteins and its substrates. Sumoylation plays an essential role in the maintenance and self-renewal of cancer stem cells. Knockout of the SUMO activating enzyme E1 or the SUMO conjugating enzyme E2 has been shown to inhibit the self-maintenance and renewal of colorectal cancer cells [34]. Yang et al. [35] discovered that musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene homolog B (MAFB) facilitates CC tumorigenesis through regulation of the cell cycle. The ubiquitination process, critical for the regulation of cell proliferation, is intricately controlled by a spectrum of enzymes including E1, E2, E3, and DUBs. It dynamically modulates inflammation and various programmed cell death pathways [36]. Autophagy significantly contributes to human tumorigenesis, with BECN1 (beclin1) serving as a central regulator of this process, frequently found to be downregulated in a range of malignancies. CUL3 (cullin3), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, engages in interaction with BECN1 and facilitates its K48-linked ubiquitination, thereby accelerating tumor cell proliferation [37]. DNA methylation, a prevalent epigenetic mechanism, plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis, with alterations in gene methylation status linked to the onset, growth, and progression of diverse cancers [38, 39]. In conclusion, the role of DSG2 transcends its established function in cell adhesion, suggesting a broader and more intricate biological influence on the initiation and progression of CC.

Through immunohistochemical analysis, we observed reduced DSG2 protein expression in CC, findings that corroborate our bioinformatics analysis results. Our investigation into the relationship between DSG2 expression and the clinical-pathological features of CC patients demonstrated significant correlations with tumor differentiation and the presence of distant metastases. Survival curves and log-rank tests showed that patients with low DSG2 expression exhibited significantly poorer prognoses compared to those with high DSG2 expression. Additionally, multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed DSG2 as an independent prognostic factor for OS in CC patients. Collectively, these results support the potential of DSG2 as a prognostic biomarker for CC.

DSG2, first identified in colon tissue and also referred to as human desmoglein colon (HDGC), serves as a transmembrane protein that interacts with desmocollin (DSC) on the cell membrane to regulate intercellular adhesion. Among the four subtypes of desmoglein, DSG2 is the most prevalently expressed in various tissues [40]. Various cell adhesion molecules, particularly desmosomes, are implicated in carcinogenesis, with their expression and function regulated by multiple mechanisms [41]. Kundu et al. [42] observed that reduced desmosomal proteins can lead to decreased cell adhesion, thereby facilitating colorectal cancer metastasis. Ramani et al. [43] demonstrated a significant correlation between pancreatic tumor malignancy and low DSG2 expression, highlighting the critical role of DSG2 loss in pancreatic cancer invasion. Subsequent studies have confirmed low DSG2 expression in gastric cancer [44], squamous cell carcinoma [45], lung cancer [46], and prostate cancer [18]. Our findings further indicate that low DSG2 expression is associated with poor OS in CC patients. Reduced expression of the key desmosomal protein DSG2 may disrupt tumor adhesion functions, thereby promoting tumor cell invasion and migration. Conversely, Kamekura et al. [47] demonstrated that the absence of DSG2, through the inhibition of EGFR signaling, suppresses the proliferation of murine colorectal cancer cells, with DSG2 and desmocollin-2 (DSC2) exerting opposing effects on tumor proliferation. Desmosomes, with their complex junctional structures, are assembled from multiple proteins [48]. Variations in desmosomal protein expression may promote cancer development under specific conditions; however, the impact of DSG2 on carcinogenesis differs across various tumors, influenced by factors including tumor composition, protein expression levels, subcellular localization, and tissue-specific expression [49]. Moreover, in physiological mouse models possessing intact immune systems, tumors develop within the appropriate tissue-specific microenvironments. Consequently, findings from Kamekura et al. suggest that the downregulation of DSG2 expression may contribute to the inhibition of colorectal tumor progression in mice. These findings, which diverge from our own, may be influenced by the limitations inherent in these alternative carcinogenic models. In our study, colorectal cancer tissues collected directly from patients provide a more accurate reflection of the tumor’s true state. Yang et al. [50] analyzed 587 CC samples, 41 colitis tissues, and 114 adjacent cancerous tissues, along with corresponding clinicopathological data, revealing that DSG2 expression is significantly lower in CC than in adjacent non-tumor tissues and is an independent predictor of poor OS in CC patients. Low DSG2 expression correlates with poor survival in CC patients, indicating that DSG2 could serve as a prognostic biomarker for these patients, aligning with our study results. Moreover, our study has added more bioinformatics analysis results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, drawing on our experimental results, data analysis, and bioinformatics analysis, we have determined that low DSG2 expression correlates with poor survival outcomes in CC patients, indicating that DSG2 could potentially serve as a prognostic biomarker for CC. Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations, including a small sample size, a retrospective design, and missing molecular covariates (MSI, KRAS/BRAF) in the Cox regression multivariate analysis. Future studies should aim to validate these findings through multicenter, prospective, and large-scale research. This article presents a preliminary investigation into the correlation between Desmoglein-2 expression and the prognosis of patients undergoing radical surgery for colorectal cancer. A significant limitation of this study is the absence of validation experiments, attributed to insufficient funding. With increased research funding in the future, the authors intend to elucidate these observations further through additional experimental results.
