Challenges of the general practitioner’s role in integrated primary health care: a qualitative study of trainees in a reform-oriented network in Northern Italy
Authors: L Marchesi, A F Azzolini, E Tobaldini, A Corraro, L M Bracchitta, B D’Avanzo, A Nobili, A De Simoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntegrated primary health care (PHC) is essential for efficient, equitable healthcare, especially amid rising chronic conditions and an aging population. It ensures coordinated care across services, with GPs as key gatekeepers. Yet, European systems face obstacles like service fragmentation, workforce shortages, inadequate funding, digital barriers, inconsistent policies and poor integration across services.ObjectiveThis study explores the perspectives of trainee doctors in Northern Italy on the challenges of integrated PHC and suggests best practices to enhance integrated care and GP training.MethodsThis is a qualitative study using semi-structured remote interviews, analyzed using data-driven thematic analysis. Eligible participants were regional and university-based trainees.ResultsTwelve participants (7 F, 5 M, aged 25-49y, 8 regional and 4 university-based trainees) were recruited. All shared a good understanding of integrated PHC challenges, such as managing patients with chronic conditions and limitations of the digital infrastructure for interoperability across primary, secondary, community, and social care services. They noted that service fragmentation, increased patient access to online health information and remote consultations have shifted patient expectations and disrupted traditional primary care dynamics, making it more challenging for GPs to promote integrated care. Rural areas are facing shrinking local health services, lack of staff and reduced capacity to provide integrated care comparable to urban settings. Participants emphasized the importance of team-based care and the need for both bottom-up initiatives (e.g., self-initiated collaborations with local care providers) and top-down support mandated by policy, such as dedicated regular meetings during office hours and clinical governance initiatives. They shared examples of good practices that could be incorporated in future policies, such as on-call secondary care specialists available for phone consultation to prevent unnecessary referrals. Participants also highlighted the importance of quality standards in GP training and the need to develop competencies in effective team-based working.ConclusionsThis study highlights key challenges and actionable solutions proposed by GP trainees to improve integrated care and training, offering insights relevant to health systems beyond Italy. Although based on more engaged and reform-minded trainees, this calls for more research on this group of future primary care professionals, who may be key to informing interventions to improve integrated PHC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03420-4.Keywords: Health Service Fragmentation, Health system reform, Integrated Care, Interprofessional Collaboration, Primary Health Care, Rural Areas

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines integrated health services as the coordinated delivery of health promotion, disease prevention, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care across various levels and sites of care, tailored to the needs of individuals throughout their life course [1]. Primary health care (PHC) represents a comprehensive, whole-of-society approach that encompasses both primary care and essential public health functions as the foundation of integrated health services. It also involves multisectoral policies and actions and the active empowerment of people and communities [1]. Within this framework, primary care serves as the cornerstone, acting as the first point of contact and ensuring continuity and coordination across healthcare settings. Integrated PHC is particularly vital in addressing the growing prevalence of chronic diseases and the complexities of aging populations, as it fosters seamless collaboration between healthcare providers and enhances patient outcomes [2]. Thus, healthcare systems are increasingly recognizing integrated PHC as a cornerstone for becoming more efficient, equitable and patient-centred [3].

Despite its benefits, European healthcare systems face significant challenges in adopting integrated PHC. Fragmentation of services often results in poor coordination between primary care, hospitals, and social care providers [4] Workforce shortages exacerbate these issues, with several areas struggling to recruit and retain general practitioners (GPs) and other essential healthcare professionals [5]. Financial and structural barriers further hinder integration efforts, including insufficient funding models and misaligned incentives among stakeholders [6]. Additionally, limited adoption of digital health solutions impedes data sharing and communication across care settings [7]. Variability in policies and regulations across countries makes standardisation difficult, creating disparities in access to integrated care [8].

The role of GPs is evolving to meet the demands of integrated care systems. The European WONCA gave a clear definition of it “General practice / family medicine is an academic and scientific discipline, with its own educational content, research, evidence base and clinical activity, and a clinical specialty orientated to primary care”, and it stressed also the importance of team work, patients and community empowerment and focus on health problems in their physical, psychological, social, cultural and existential dimensions [9]. Based on the definition, training programs are increasingly focusing on equipping future GPs with skills such as interdisciplinary teamwork, care coordination, and digital health literacy [9]. However, progress remains uneven across Europe, with certain regions failing to update curricula to address the competencies required for integrated care delivery [9]. Addressing these gaps is critical for preparing GPs for their central role in managing complex patient needs within multidisciplinary teams.

In Italy, primary care operates within the Servizio Sanitario Nazionale (SSN), where GPs serve as gatekeepers to filter secondary and tertiary care provided by specialist consultants and Local Health Authorities manage primary care services under a regionally administered system [10]. Despite the structured organisation, integration between primary and secondary and tertiary care is limited. GPs are self-employed physicians and not employees such as doctors working in hospitals. [11]. Training requires six years of medical education followed by a three-year GP regional course (CFSMG), though this is not a university-based specialization and lacks a national standardization, academic integration, and structured curricula. Since the 1990s, there is also a university-based residency focused on PHC with a duration of 4 years, called Community and Primary Health Care Medicine (Scuola di specialità in Medicina di Comunità e delle Cure Primarie, SpecMCCP), which represents an academic approach to Primary Care training. According to current Italian regulations (D.I.M. 68/2015), completion of the SpecMCCP would theoretically entitle graduates to practice as GPs within the SSN, but the absence of an implementing decree prevents them from practising in this role, unless they also complete the CFSMG or obtain an equivalence [12].

MedicInRete (NetDoctors) – Training and Networking for Primary Care, coordinated by the Department of Health Policy at the Istituto di Ricerche Farmacologiche Mario Negri IRCCS [13], the Alliance for Primary Care Reform [14], and the PHC Now or Never Campaign [15] is a national initiative/project launched in Italy in 2023 to support the development of integrated PHC by actively involving the future medical workforce, i.e. both CFSMG and Spec MCCP trainees. The study was conducted as part of the MedicInRete, a national initiative that was developed as a space for the exchange of ideas and practices, enabling future GPs and MCCPs in training, or already practicing their profession, to meet (primarily online), share experiences, critically discuss the evolving role of general practice, and promote interdisciplinary and intersectoral projects to strengthen integrated PHC. Thus, this project fosters collaboration among (training) doctors in primary care to enhance care coordination and address local health challenges. Through discussion groups, educational modules and practice-oriented activities, participants explore the practical application of integrated care models in community settings. Given the wide scope of the initiative and their engagement in discussions about the challenges of primary care, the doctors involved in MedicInRete could offer valuable insights into the challenges GPs are facing in Integrated PHC.

This study investigates the evolving role of primary care physicians in integrated PHC, from the perspectives of GP trainee doctors and specialists of primary care. It examines how GPs in training are experiencing the increasing integration with a broader range of services including community, social, voluntary, and secondary care, while identifying the key determinants, barriers, and facilitators influencing successful integration. Additionally, this study seeks to highlight best practices for enhancing primary care to inform current policy makers and GP training services.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative study through semi-structured remote interviews. The transcripts were analyzed using qualitative Thematic Analysis to identify key themes [16].

Selection of participants

Eligible participants were CFSMG or SpecMCCP physicians enrolled in the MedicInRete programme from Northern Italy, which has a more advanced primary care and are more similar to each other compared to other regions in Italy [17]. There was no financial reward for taking part in MedicInRete, participation was on voluntary basis. Physicians who had completed their CFSMG or SpecMCCP training more than one year prior to the study were excluded.

The total number of participants to the MedicInRete programme was 147 (99 CFSMG and 48 SpecMCCP trainees), recruited from various Italian regions and years of training through email invitations from local NHS authorities. The number of trainees recruited to MedicInRete from the three target recruitment regions in Northern Italy was 40/147, comprising 6 CFSMG and 2 SpecMCCP from Lombardy, 13 CFSMG and 10 SpecMCCP from Emilia-Romagna, and 6 CFSMG and 3 SpecMCCP from Veneto, respectively.

The total number of CFSMG is hard to define due to lack of public data, but a likely estimation could be of around 5000 trainees in the whole nation. The total national number of SpecMCCP trainees is 101.

The study invitation was sent via email from the regions enrolled in the MedicInRete training project. Two follow-up invitations were sent at one-week intervals.

Semi structured interviews

We could not identify any non–study-specific, validated interview topic guides. The interview topic guide was therefore developed for this study, based on current scientific literature and themes emerged during the MedicInRete project meetings. It was initially piloted and refined with a sample of three participants enrolled in MedicInRete (2 SpecMCCP trainees, 1 CFSMG). As result, the flow of the interview questions was changed, to start with a comparison with previous historical models of GP work in primary care, mostly single handed.

Data collection

The interviews took place via the Microsoft Teams platform using the login credentials of the Mario Negri Institute, so that the recordings were automatically stored in the Institute secure server. The interviews were conducted between September and December 2024 by ADS, a UK based academic GP with about 20 years clinical experience in primary care and expertise in qualitative research.

Audio recordings of the interviews and focus group discussions were transcribed using the MS Teams automatic transcription, with manual corrections made where necessary. The interviews started with broad, open-ended questions, followed by more specific questions as necessary to clarify points and guide the discussion towards key themes.

The sample size aligns with standard qualitative health research studies and was based on the recommendations of Krueger et al. (2014) [18]. This number allowed for a diversity of viewpoints while ensuring that each participant had adequate time—estimated at approximately 1–2 h to fully articulate their perspectives.

Data analysis

Transcripts of the discussions were analysed using a Thematic Analysis [16], using Microsoft Excel files, which followed six key stages: repeated reading of the text to become familiar with the content; generation of initial codes, based on sematic analysis, by identifying the most elementary segments of raw data relevant to the research topic; identification of themes by grouping codes based on semantic proximity; review and reorganisation of themes; definition and naming of themes; and compilation of a report in which themes were presented as a coherent set and explicitly linked to the data.

A data-driven rather than a theory-driven approach was used. The coding process was conducted independently by two researchers (LM, AFA, with supervision from ADS), who then compared their preliminary results. The WHO definition of integrated care is intentionally broad, and we adopted this inclusive approach in the development of the topic guide. Integrated care has also been conceptualized across multiple dimensions: clinical, organisational, functional, and normative within an established framework [19]. Following an inductive, data-driven analytic approach, these dimensions were applied a posteriori to organise and map the emergent subthemes. Any discrepancies were explicitly discussed until a consensus was reached or a new code or theme was developed. After the initial consensus was reached between the two main authors, the results were then discussed with the whole group of researchers. After analysing all the interviews, the themes were divided into three main areas (The changing role of GPs in integrated care, System-level barriers and facilitators, Training and workforce implications) with a total of 47 macro topics and 123 subtopics.

LM and AFA are SpecMCCP trainees with 4 and 7 years of clinical experience, respectively, and 2 and 1 year of clinical research experience. They are part of one of the two GP training systems analyzed in the article. Their insider positioning informed the analytic process, shaping how the data were engaged with, interpreted, and contextualized. Rather than seeking to eliminate potential bias, the researchers approached their perspectives as a resource for interpretation, while remaining critically attentive to how their assumptions, experiences, and investments in the topic might influence meaning-making. Reflexive practices were embedded throughout the research process, including ongoing discussion and critical engagement with the data and interpretations. The analysis was further developed through discussions with ADS, whose involvement provided additional perspectives and contributed to the depth and rigour of the analytic process.

Any quotations included in publications arising from this study were anonymised to ensure that no individuals could be identified.

Ethical approval

All participants gave recorded oral consent to be recorded and transcript for the anonymous data collection. Individual written informed consent was also obtained from all participants, and this is stored on Sharepoint at the Mario Negri Institute.

Ethical approval for the study was received from the Territorial Ethics Committee Lombardy 6 (CET Lombardy 6) (reference: 37761/2024).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679).

The changing role of GPs in integrated care

Participants talked about the changes of the role of a contemporary GP compared to 30–50 years earlier. Participants noted that in the past the GP was a central and trusted figure in patients’ lives. However, some suggested that it was patients rather than GPs who have changed the most over time. Contemporary GPs’ central role in clinical management has gradually diminished, partly due to the increased accessibility of health information through the Internet and remote communications. Patients are more likely to form their own opinions about what they need, rather than placing their trust in the GP’s guidance.

“Today the patient arrives already informed and seeks confirmation (…) their will is to be referred to secondary care, while the push of the service is actually to devolve more and more care to the territory. I also agree that chronic diseases should be managed by primary health care” (P 6).

Many participants mentioned the fact that contemporary GPs have to take care of an increased number of patients suffering from chronic conditions as an important difference compared to the past. This shift requires a change in perspective regarding the GP’s role that should adopt a broader, more integrated approach with other community, social and secondary care services.

“The type of patient today is a chronic multi-morbid patient, with polypharmacy and increased life expectancy, and so this brings up many questions and many reflections on what our role is becoming. Also in my opinion, that [role] is to support healthy ageing, and so it is becoming more a medicine of complexity and fragility rather than a medicine of single pathology” (P4).

Chronic and multimorbid patients have many check-ups to do, and participants described GPs feeling lost in the complexity of the health care system. The role of a contemporary GP includes also supporting patients in navigating the complex network of care services.

“The patient also needs to be accompanied within a series of visits and pathways that sometimes also require a lot of energy, a lot of attention in meeting all deadlines, checks, follow up (…)this patient (…) needs the presence of a caregiver who is trained and is made part of the whole process” (P3).

Digital tools are valuable to help GPs in their ‘gate-keeping role’ and to keep track of all the check-ups patients do in primary and secondary care. In 2012 in Italy the Fascicolo Sanitario Elettronico (Electronic Health File, FSE) was launched with the goal of integrating patient data from both the secondary and the primary care system.

“The FSE can be accessed via the web and there is the GP information with the clinic opening hours, there are all the health reports uploaded, the health situation, the vaccinations certificates, in short, there is more or less a summary of what has been done within the hospital pathway of the local health authority” (P6).

Another key theme that emerged was the concern about health care in rural areas where staff shortages and geographic isolation make teamwork more challenging.

“Despite my health agency and my region is one of the top regions in terms of health and health equity, (…) despite the good will to put systems in place (…) then we are unable to provide the same level of equitable access to healthcare services available in other Italian cities. (…) When a choice was made years ago to close all the small garrisons and hospitals in small towns to create these large hubs [hospitals], the spokes [local services] became smaller and smaller and now we are realising a little bit that we have impoverished ourselves. There is nothing left there, from shops to hospitals to pharmacies, so people stay there alone. It was an institutional political choice” (P10).

SpecMCCP talked about their different job opportunities compared to regional based trainees.

“We cannot practise as general practitioners, but we have other jobs, e.g. in community hospitals, in intermediate facilities, in CdC (ed Case della Comunità, integrated GP practices) both at management level, especially at organisational level, and also with clinical components. I see myself more in this sector. Our speciality also includes two other outlets: palliative care, i.e. hospice and home palliative care (…) district director, directors of departments at management level. Here, however, I still see myself (…) in organisational and clinical roles at a territorial level, i.e. CdC, community hospitals and territorial operating centres. In short, primary care and territorial care, that’s it” (P8).

System-level barriers and facilitators

Participants identified various factors that either facilitated or hindered the development of more integrated PHC. The most frequently mentioned facilitators were the skill of working within teams, both face to face or remotely, and to deliver multidisciplinary care to patients.

“So I believe that a doctor can no longer work alone, but has to work with a multidisciplinary and interprofessional team, therefore involving medical colleagues, but also colleagues with other skills, so from nurses to social workers, psychologists and so on.” (P10).

Another sub-theme was the importance of cooperating with the district nurses called IFeC (Infermiere di Famiglia e di Comunità, Family and Community Nurse), a new district nurse figure dedicated to improve management of chronic and multimorbid patients at home and to promote health education. However, participants reported that IFeC are understaffed and often unable to perform the full range of activities for which they are trained. A GP trainee in Lombardy gives an overview of the situation in his district and says:

“We have district nurses who are understaffed (…) they are very few. They would also like to do more of what they are trained for, so prevention, health education and so on. But in fact, it has been imposed on them that they have to do three hours a week, so they have to do medications three hours a week, with patients who access through the family doctor or freely (…) with the two district nurses that there are in West town district I have a beautiful dialogue and relationship, and they help me a lot” (P4).

A crucial issue is the poor communication between primary and secondary care. Participants said that most likely reason is hospital doctors being understaffed and with excessive workloads.

“(…) there is no direct communication, but only through the discharge letter in which is written what the patient has done and what are the next appointments, but there is no direct discussion with the caregiver and the general practitioner” (P1).

“In my opinion, from a clinical perspective, patients are often primarily managed by secondary care, whereas from a social care perspective, they are more closely associated with primary care, as we maintain the connection with their family or community services. So maybe we know that patients should not be discharged without perhaps going through a community hospital (ed. intermediate care) (…) In my opinion, a direct dialogue is necessary to go beyond the clinical sphere, precisely, in those selected cases where we need to know whether a patient can go home and what kind of care he/she can have” (P6).

On the other hand, also GPs face excessive overload, particularly due to bureaucratic demands. This was identified as a significant barrier to integration since they are overwhelmed by many tasks, which limit the time and energy they can dedicate to collaborative work and patient-centred care.

“The biggest one is the bureaucracy (…) I have to make a form for anything, anything I have to ask for, e.g., a catheter change, I have to make a form.I don’t know how to ask for a blood sample, or even worse in the case of a very old patient with multiple pathological conditions with bed-wetting syndrome and incontinence I have to make a request every three months for this lady to have pads and I have to justify it (…) And I mean, nowadays these things are useless in my opinion, because if a person has a chronically stabilized pathological condition, it’s difficult that after three months he’s miraculously well and no longer needing the service. There is so much bureaucracy. I waste a lot of time with bureaucracy” (P6).

To overcome the missing communication between primary and secondary care, caused by the lack of time, a fundamental facilitator for meaningful integration is ensuring that primary care providers have a clear understanding of the available secondary care services, as well as the process required to access and integrate with them.

“I also have to be trained, to be continuously trained and updated on the status of services (…) there are meetings called core meetings (…) There are four or five of them a year, but they are meetings in which general practitioners are called to a compulsory refresher course that is often held by specialists and professionals who deal with a specific area of care (…) they don’t go there to explain diseases to you (…) but they explain their service, how it works, how to access it, how to contact them, what to do, what not to do. They are, in my opinion, just a bit too infrequent and not so structured” (P11).

A major facilitator of integrated PHC would be the implementation of interoperable software to enable communication between primary and secondary care, improving information sharing, referrals, and continuity. However, outdated IT infrastructure of the Italian NHS remains a major barrier, making digital modernization essential for effective integration.

“(…) often is more of a barrier than an asset because they are very cumbersome. Just to be able to produce a report, there are so many steps to do that take time away from the relationship with the patient and from the clinical examination; the IT tools should be a help and not a slowdown” (P10).

Another facilitator of integrated territorial care with the secondary one is the so-called PDTA (Pathways of Diagnostic and Therapeutic healthcare Assistance), which advises about the most appropriate treatment pathway for a specific pathology, considering the available scientific evidence and the resources of the territory. PDTAs are designed by multidisciplinary and inter-professional working groups, which also involve patients’ representatives.

“When these hospital-territory PDTAs were formed, in my opinion a great step forward was taken in this sense, in preventing these patients by reducing direct access to secondary-level diagnostics and hub centers [hospitals], as it used to be, there is a lot of work to be done. But surely the advent of the PDTAs, which have also provided not only a therapeutic, but a care pathway” (P8).

Another facilitator of integrated care mentioned by some participants is the “Specialist on Call” service, implemented in the Emilia Romagna region which allows GPs to consult directly with secondary care specialists for clinical advice and referral guidance.

“Specialists on-call can be contacted by the general practitioner, within certain time slots, on certain days, to get an opinion, or perhaps avoiding issuing a prescription or an inappropriate referral So, they provide a shared management of the patient” (P8).

Participants also described so-called Centre of Assistance and Urgency (CAU) recently implemented in the Emilia Romagna region, as another example of facilitating integrated care. These centres have been established with the idea of reducing overcrowding ER by treating patients with less severe but urgent medical needs. They are open 24/7, equipped with all the first level diagnostic tools, and have a direct communication channel with the hospital

“The CAU is really a little bit more integrated than the rest of the system, because in addition to contact with other specialists there is also the possibility of booking examinations and therefore i.e., if I see a true urgency or a true cardiological or ophthalmological emergency rather than having the patient go through the emergency room which generates stress, trauma and, as well as clogging up the whole emergency room, I have the possibility of giving the patient an appointment directly in dermatology and this, alas, the patients like it very much, rightly so” (P11).

Many obstacles towards the development of a more effective and integrated PHC system were identified. Firstly, participants mentioned the need for a mindset shift among professionals.

“There is very little self-criticism in our category in Italy and little desire to change too, so there is always a tendency to be critical of legislators and funding” (P5).

The current contractual framework was seen by some participants as another barrier to the integration of primary and secondary care. As GPs in Italy currently work as freelancers, some felt that this arrangement does not support the structural and organizational changes required for integrated care. There is ongoing political discussion about whether make GPs employees could facilitate better integration.

Training and workforce implications

Participants talked about future perspectives that they hoped could be implemented to make the Italian PHC better integrated with other services, stronger and face public health challenges.

They described how GPs need adequate structures to be able to properly work in teams. This includes policy frameworks that prioritize interprofessional collaboration, structural redesigns that encourage shared physical working environments, and funding to enable joint integrated care meetings during working hours.

“It is also just the care of the group. So those hours there can also be for instance two hours where we talk about more particular or more complex clinical cases and one hour where we stay together and try do team building” (P4).

More resilient and better integrated software systems are needed to strengthen the health care system and improve communication between primary and secondary care. Some participants suggested taking inspiration from other best practices around Europe:

“What would be needed would be a single provider. In the Netherlands, a colleague told me, there is a single software system. Both territorial and hospital care use the same computer system, they can also see what I write, I can also see what they write (…) Because even for the hospital care not having my point of view is a problem, so that’s a big challenge. So, to have intercommunication, or at least software that talks to each other and can integrate with each other” (P1).

To promote smoother communications between primary and secondary care, participants thought that FSE is a fundamental resource, they hoped for its improvement because it has a lot of potential but currently it is not fully exploited. At present, only specialists’ consultations, examination reports, and discharge letters are uploaded as PDFs, while GPs cannot upload their own notes. Key priorities include enabling GPs to upload their notes and creating a dedicated section summarizing the patient’s medical history and social background, to provide specialists with a clearer overview of the patient’s condition.

“(…) the FSE, I find it fantastic, and indeed it should be shared between all specialists and the general practitioner, so that they already have my [patients’] medical history, my information and can even copy-paste it without rewriting everything at the cardiology visit”. (P12)

Integration between the primary and secondary systems goes through more interconnected communication between GPs and hospital doctors as suggested earlier by the project of the Specialist on Call currently available in some areas of Emilia Romagna.

“It is essential to have the possibility of contacting hospital doctors and even to have a telephone within the ward that is used for GPs, where hospital staff know that if it rings it is a GP”. (P1)

Some participants talked about the importance of the continuity of care and the fact that GPs should be the ones holding the reins of the health situation of their own patients, they should keep contact with the specialists and help guide them through the services that are available. To be able to do that they need to be well integrated in the system.

“Perhaps something that would make sense is to have a referral centre, for example, within the CdCs with outpatient clinics for more high-frequency chronic diseases (…) So, there is really a need for continuity. Continuity with both the GP and the specialist, to maintain continuity of the relationship (…) You mentioned two things. The first thing, the same software system, so that everything goes into the same file and you don’t have to go searching. And also, the very physical proximity. So, this physical proximity that maybe the CdC are trying to implement.” (P1)

In other to manage chronic and multimorbid patients, GPs should be trained during the CFSMG to cooperate in multidisciplinary teams and to face the complexity of the chronic patients better. Some participants emphasised the importance of incorporating training on multidisciplinary teamwork and managing the complexity of chronic patients into GP education.

“The training (…) must be completely revised, (…) the needs of the patients have changed, the territory has changed, and so many things have changed, and so this way of doing medicine in such an approximate way, well, perhaps approximate is the term for general medicine in Italy, it is frustrating for patients, but it is also frustrating for doctors” (P5).

To work towards a better integrated system, participants stated that both bottom up and top-down actions are needed.

“Here, in the area where I live, they are, let’s say, very virtuous from above, because once a month they have what they call ‘nucleus meetings’ in which they divide the city into small districts and each nucleus director sees all his general practitioners to take stock of the situation and to talk about any critical issues, problems, needs, so there is a lot of attention (…) Yes, [they take place] during working hours and when there are these meetings here the entire company is obviously notified, which makes up for the lack of doctors who are on these courses. During these trainings the out-of-hours medical service is active to cover GPs that are not there” (P10).

In addition, GP trainees need to acquire knowledge (and training if necessary) about the health and social care services available in the area where they will be working after qualifying as GPs, to support better integration within the local healthcare system, recognising that direct local experience is ultimately the best approach.

“You only know the territory when you work there. There is no way of knowing what your territory has to offer as a health service if you don’t start working there, without understanding anything about it and gradually let’s say gaining experience in the field” (P3).

The CFSMG is governed by regional institutions, leading to significant variations in training across regions that does not provide a standardized curriculum for GP training nationwide. Many hoped for a national standardization of the training.

“We should talk about standardizing training, because here in Italy training for general practitioners is regional. Each region creates its professional class in a different way; we do not talk to each other between regions. With a national training, then there would be more standardization.” (P10).

To address all these challenges effectively, there should be a unified front to advocate for change. However, generational differences within the profession can hinder this effort, as highlighted by many participants.

“There is almost a split; young people think one way and old people another. The problem is that it is the older GPs who then go to the negotiating table”. (P2)

In fact, many participants addressed the older generation of GPs as less prone to accept the evolution of the role of the PHC professionals working in teams and being more integrated with hospital care

“I try not to generalize, but very often for the older generation of family doctors this [working in a more modern way] is more difficult. Not all of them, but a good part is. In fact, a good proportion of doctors still, for example, are not in a group and work individually.” (P2)

Many talked about changing the actual contract of GPs (ACN) because it is seen as a fundamental issue to change to be able to take more steps towards a better integrated health care. There is an ongoing debate on whether GPs should be employees of the NHS or remain freelancers

“ (…) Employment would certainly allow us to have more homogeneity because today, a doctor in general is free to organize him/herself as he/she believes, as he/she wants and it is up to his/her professionalism” (P5).

“Regarding the integration of the GPs certainly, going back to what we were saying before, in my opinion the contractual form somewhat generates this separation.” (P11).

It is important to note that not all supported a change from freelancers to employees, with some listing pros and cons of both scenarios

“On the one hand, freelancing gives you that flexibility in your work (…) the ability to self-manage. If one knows how to do it well, one works very well. On the other hand, it’s clear that you have expenses, you don’t have paid holidays, you don’t have sick leave, and so on the other hand, being an employee certainly has considerable advantages (…) So I think the truth lies somewhere in between and I still can’t say what is better as an employee or as a freelancer”. (P6)

Participants discussed also about the recent introduction of the CdC [21] as part of a primary care reform implemented in 2022, enabling GPs to work in multidisciplinary teams, with a key role in managing patients affected by chronic health conditions.

“A CdC where there are specialists, where there is equipment, basic diagnostic imaging, nurses, social workers, physiotherapists, where there is us (i.e., GPs), where there is an out-of-hours medical service during nights and on holidays, and where therefore the patient can access every day and draw on a pool of general practitioners who are not their own, but a group of 20–30, all sharing the same healthcare records and therefore patients always find someone available” (P5)

Interviewees stated that this reform promotes multidisciplinary and better integrated care. The main criticism is that this organization works in urban but not rural settings

“Since it’s something like that, obviously in so many extra-urban areas and disadvantaged even geographically, in Italy with the mountains, I wouldn’t know how it could be implemented”. (P5)

Rural areas are, in fact, facing shrinking local health services, lack of staff and reduced capacity to provide integrated care comparable to urban settings. Physical distance poses a significant barrier to effective multiprofessional teamwork. To overcome this challenge some participants suggested implementing shared technological infrastructures and digital solutions to facilitate communication and coordination across geographically dispersed teams.

“(…) a sort of group medicine that does not stay in one clinic. Everyone stays in their own outpatient clinic, and the doctors share their charts on a software” (P9).

Some participants mentioned telemedicine as a potential way to support integrated care, especially in rural areas. They saw it more as a future option to explore rather than something they currently feel equipped to implement. At the same time, they noted that predominantly remote work can be burdensome and less stimulating, as face-to-face interactions are always appreciated.

“They talk a lot about telemedicine, and it will certainly help those of us who are far away. But practicing medicine entirely at distance through telehealth can be a bit burdensome for the professional and not very stimulating, to be honest. Sometimes I really look forward to it, it is a bit of a celebration for me when someone comes for a joint visit.” (P12)

Furthermore, in rural areas, even more than in urban settings, there is greater need to strengthen the territorialization of care. Ensure that services are adapted to the specific needs and geography of the local population.

“they should give (…) the possibility for specialists (cardiologists, dieticians, ophthalmologists, etc.) to come once a month because we have a problem of distance to reduce waiting lists and in any case ensure that patients in this area are always treated locally” (P 9).

Although the dimensions of the Integrated care framework by Valentijn et al. 2013 [19] were not taken into account in the initial inductive analysis, all of them emerged naturally from participants’ accounts during the analytical process, highlighting the participants’ perception of the multifaceted challenges of GPs’ roles within integrated primary healthcare (see Table 2). From a clinical integration perspective, challenges emerged in relation to the increasing complexity of patient needs, with GPs managing growing numbers of individuals with multimorbidity while also acting as care navigators within fragmented systems. At the professional level, multidisciplinary team training and collaboration with health and social care professionals were recognized as essential facilitators to build cross-profession skills and effective teamwork. At the organizational level, inequalities in access, particularly in rural areas, alongside workforce shortages, limited district nursing capacity, and communication gaps between primary and secondary care were key barriers. Functional integration challenges were reflected in the need for more effective and interoperable digital systems, despite the progress made through electronic health records and shared software, as well as the importance of accessible and up-to-date service mapping. Finally, normative integration issues highlighted differences in professional roles, training pathways, and the need for a broader cultural shift towards collaborative, multidisciplinary working.

Discussion

Main findings

This study highlights the perspective of trainees on the integration of primary with secondary care and community and social services, identifying challenges, potential solutions and best practices. Participants showed a clear understanding of the multifaceted challenges surrounding the implementation of integrated primary care, across all its dimensions: clinical, professional, organizational, functional, and normative [19]. According to GP trainees, patients have changed due to the influence of online health information and digital technologies, leading to altered patient–GP dynamics and more transactional consultations that hinder integrated PHC. This relatively novel finding merits further research to explore how to optimise these new dynamics and inform GP training and potential future interventions. Facilitators of integrated care were identified at multiple levels. At the training level, there was strong support for the inclusion of organizational skills and team-working competencies as core components of the professional curriculum. While this has been noted previously (9), they emphasized the need for a top-down policy approach including the provision of physical spaces and protected time to enable team-based practice involving primary, community, social and secondary care services, as critical to achieving integration. Additionally, in rural areas where workforce shortages and geographic isolation make interprofessional teamwork particularly challenging, a proposed solution was the adoption of differentiated policy approaches that explicitly recognize and respond to the distinct organizational, social, and professional dynamics. Such tailored strategies could help mitigate inequities, support workforce retention, and foster more effective models of integrated care in underserved areas.

Participants shared practical examples of good practices that could be incorporated into future policies, such as secondary care specialists on-call available for phone consultations to prevent unnecessary referrals and the advantages of PDTA, mostly developed towards healthcare aspects.

Interestingly, the need to integrate the social dimension and to assess the social determinants of health emerged only tangentially. This may reflect the focus of their training curricula, which tended to concentrate almost exclusively on healthcare aspects. Another area that was scarcely addressed concerned the approaches and tools that could be employed for the assessment and management of the needs of local populations.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings align with those of Al-Bedaery et al. 2023 [9], which emphasizes the need for collaborative skills across primary and secondary care settings, interprofessional education, and a shift away from siloed training models toward approaches that prepare future GPs for integrated, team-based care in managing complexity and chronic conditions.

Results suggest that participants have a clear understanding of the meaning of integrated care as a positive attitude toward defragmentation of service provision inside health and social systems [22]. Integrated care emerged as the creation of systems that effectively connect primary, secondary, and tertiary care with the social sector, thereby fostering communication and continuity of services [23]. Participants’ views align with existing literature that links integrated care to patient-centered care and the principles of the quadruple aim: improving population health, enhancing patient experience, containing costs, and ensuring the well-being of healthcare professionals [23].

Participants described the diminishing central role of general practitioners (GPs) in patients’ lives, a role historically grounded on trust and continuity, which is now in crisis. They highlighted how patients themselves may have changed, particularly due to decreased continuity of care, widespread access to health information online, which have shifted expectations of the management of their care and altered traditional dynamics. This represents a novel finding beyond what is already known, as these aspects have only been partly described in the literature [24]. As a result, GPs may find their ‘integrated care role’ more challenging, especially as digitalization and the rise of transactional consultations have further affected the foundation of long-term, trust-based relationships [25]. These findings suggest that public expectations around the role of GPs could benefit from a shift toward recognizing them as trusted guides in navigating the complexities of integrated care systems and ensuring continuity especially for patients with chronic conditions. Strengthening the GP’s role as a coordinator and gatekeeper of health information, supported by a robust digital infrastructure that enables interoperability across primary, secondary, community, and social care, is viewed as essential to realizing integrated care. We found evidence that incentivization could be a welcome mechanism to support integrated care. This reflects the UK’s experience with the Quality and Outcomes Framework (QOF), a model that has been positively evaluated, particularly in areas related to early detection and management of long-term conditions, where delivery is supported by information technology and the wider primary care team [26].

Although videoconference-based consultations are an effective alternative to face-to-face consultations for many patients in primary care and mental health services [27], study participants did not mention this option as something they are currently trained or skilled to do, but rather as a potential future possibility.

They also highlighted that primarily working remotely can be challenging and less engaging, as people generally appreciate the connection that comes from face-to-face interactions. This omission is notable, particularly given the challenges faced by rural areas, where such an approach could offer significant benefits.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, participants were recruited from among motivated trainees who opted in the programme MedicInRete, which may have enhanced the quality and depth of insights shared, particularly regarding integrated care and interprofessional collaboration. Second, piloting of the topic guide helped refine both the structure and flow of the interviews, allowing the research team to explore complex themes in greater depth. Importantly, this study captures the perspectives of young primary care trainees, a group whose views are rarely included in discussions on health system reform, providing a valuable and often overlooked voice to the policy debate.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The first interview question addressed only the evolution of the GP’s role, not that of the MCCP specialist. This represents an intrinsic limitation, since although the university-based residency was established in the 1990s, the number of specialists has been increasing only in the past five years, making it impossible to ask about the evolution of the MCCP specialist’s role, but focussing on GP role only. It is important to emphasize that in Italy, the current university-based residency program provides standardized training with a strong focus on teamwork and an integrated approach to medicine, in line with recent rems aimed at building a more integrated healthcare system. However, for political reasons, this program does not allow specialists to practice as general practitioners, creating a unique situation in Europe where the training necessary to practice as a GP is either held by universities or by the health ministry.

Finally, the study recruited participants enrolled in MedicInRete, a programme focused on integrated care. This may represent a source of selection bias, potentially skewing findings toward more engaged and reform-oriented practitioners. In addition, most participants were based in Emilia-Romagna, one of Italy’s best-resourced and highest-performing regions in terms of health indicators. While this context may have facilitated more advanced reflections on integrated care, it may also limit the transferability of the findings to other settings. Further research involving a broader and more diverse sample of participants is therefore needed to confirm and extend these results.

Implications for future research

Further research should explore this group of future healthcare professionals delivering primary care, as they develop an understanding of population, systems, and training needs during their formative years. This could ultimately lead to the development of interventions to improve integrated PHC. Finally, citizen and patient participation in this context must be prioritized. Initiatives that seek to understand public expectations of GPs, and how patients perceive the role of general practice within an integrated system, are essential. Future research should explore these perspectives in depth to inform both integrated PHC policy and the design of training curricula aligned with patient-centred models of care.

Conclusions

This study highlights key implications for both practice and future research. There is a pressing need to address equity in healthcare and to explore innovative models for integrated PHC, through both bottom-up initiatives and policy-driven top-down approaches, tailored to the distinct contexts of urban and rural settings.

Embedding integrated PHC competencies early in clinical training may equip future practitioners with the skills needed to navigate complex care systems and work effectively within integrated teams.
