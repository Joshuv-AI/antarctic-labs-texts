Patient-derived organoids for precision oncology in colorectal cancer: from tumor modeling to clinical translation
Authors: Barbara Lupo, Irene Catalano, Livio Trusolino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precision oncology in colorectal cancer (CRC) has been profoundly informed by genomic and transcriptomic profiling, yet molecular stratification remains only partially predictive of therapeutic benefit. This reflects a broader challenge: moving from static molecular information to dynamic, adaptive, and time-resolved predictions of drug response for individual patients along their clinical history. In CRC, this challenge is compounded by extensive inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity, clonal evolution, chromosomal instability, phenotypic plasticity and tumor-microenvironment (TME) interactions, all of which reshape therapeutic vulnerabilities over time.Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as a functional platform to address this gap, with CRC providing one of the most fertile contexts for their development and application. By preserving key histopathological, molecular, and phenotypic features of parental tumors while remaining experimentally tractable, expandable, and amenable to pharmacological and genetic perturbation, PDOs add a functional layer to precision oncology in CRC. Living organoid biobanks and ex vivo screening studies have shown that PDOs can capture interpatient heterogeneity, uncover mechanisms of sensitivity and resistance, and, in selected contexts, mirror clinical responses to chemotherapy and targeted agents. The same experimental tractability has made these systems powerful tools for investigating tumor initiation and clonal evolution, while enabling exploration of cellular plasticity through the lens of dynamic cellular hierarchies. In parallel, next-generation CRC organoid systems incorporating stromal and immune components may extend functional testing to TME-dependent processes, including response to immunotherapy.In this review, we discuss the conceptual foundations of organoid technology and its emergence as a functional precision oncology platform in CRC. We examine how CRC organoids have advanced understanding of tumor initiation, evolution, chromosomal instability, and phenotypic plasticity; summarize their applications in drug discovery, therapy-response prediction, and resistance modeling; and highlight the development of next-generation multicellular organoid systems incorporating immune and stromal components. Finally, we address the major technical, biological, and clinical challenges that must be overcome to translate PDO-based approaches into robust and clinically actionable tools for precision medicine.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a global health burden, ranking as the third most diagnosed cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Clinical outcome is strongly stage-dependent: while localised disease is often curable, metastatic CRC (mCRC) has dismal prognosis, with 5-year survival rates below 15% [2].

At the genomic level, sporadic CRC arises through distinct but partially overlapping molecular trajectories. Most tumors (~ 85%) are microsatellite-stable and mismatch repair-proficient (MSS/pMMR), broadly overlapping with the non-hypermutated, chromosomal instability (CIN)-driven subtype [3, 4]. Within this group, APC loss and canonical Wnt activation are hallmark initiating events [3, 5]. A smaller subset (~ 15%) arises from defective mismatch repair (dMMR), resulting in microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) phenotype [6]. Although particularly sensitive to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), MSI-H/dMMR tumors represent only a minority of mCRC [7, 8], leaving most patients – predominantly those with MSS/pMMR disease – with limited benefit from current immunotherapy approaches [9].

Genomic profiling has improved molecular stratification in CRC. However, available biomarkers remain more effective for excluding patients unlikely to respond than for positively identifying those likely to benefit from therapy [10]. Anti-EGFR therapy exemplifies this limitation: while extended RAS and BRAF testing are central to molecular stratification in mCRC and help identify tumors intrinsically resistant to EGFR blockade [11–13], RAS wild-type status alone does not ensure therapeutic response, and even molecularly selected tumors may fail to benefit from anti-EGFR agents [14]. More recently, KRAS-directed therapies have expanded the targeted-treatment landscape in mCRC, particularly for KRASG12C mutant tumors [15]. However, translating these advances to more prevalent variants such as KRASG12D has proven more challenging, despite encouraging preclinical progress [16, 17]. Hence, even with growing enthusiasm around KRAS actionability, much of the CRC molecular landscape remains relatively recalcitrant to effective and durable targeted intervention.

This gap is even more evident for cytotoxic chemotherapy, which remains the therapeutic backbone for most patients with mCRC [11]. Regimens such as FOLFOX (5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin) and FOLFIRI (5-fluorouracil and irinotecan) are widely used, often combined with targeted agents [18]. However, treatment selection remains largely empirical, reflecting the absence of robust predictive biomarkers of chemotherapy response [19].

These limitations stem not only from interpatient genomic heterogeneity [20–22], but also from the dynamic and adaptive nature of tumor biology. Under environmental and therapeutic pressures, CRC evolves through spatial and temporal diversification, giving rise to distinct cellular subpopulations with distinct vulnerabilities [23–27]. Importantly, non-genetic mechanisms – including phenotypic plasticity, cell state transitions, and tumor-microenvironment interactions – play a central role in driving adaptation and treatment escape, and are not captured by tumor-intrinsic genomic profiling [28, 29].

Collectively, these observations highlight a fundamental bottleneck in precision oncology: converting static molecular information into dynamic, patient-specific predictions of therapeutic response. In this perspective, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) offer a particularly promising platform to address this gap, enabling the incorporation of a functional layer to complement molecular profiling and better inform therapeutic decision-making.

In this review, we offer a comprehensive overview of how organoid technology is reshaping functional precision medicine in CRC. We discuss the rationale for integrating genotype-based stratification with functional readouts, outline the development and application of organoid models in studying CRC tumorigenesis and evolution, and examine their potential for drug discovery, therapy-response prediction, resistance modeling, and multi-omic interrogation. Finally, we address the key technical and translational challenges that must be overcome to translate these platforms into robust and clinically actionable tools.

Premises and promise of organoid technology in CRC

Although organoids are now central to stem-cell biology and precision oncology, the principles of organoid culture long predate the widespread adoption of the term itself. Organoids are long-term expandable, self-organizing three-dimensional (3D) epithelial structures derived from stem cells and maintained in vitro by reconstituting essential niche signals within an extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffold [30]. The field traces its roots to pioneering 1980s studies showing that epithelial cells embedded in ECM can re-establish tissue-like architecture [31, 32]. Subsequent work extended these observations beyond epithelial morphogenesis, revealing that, under permissive 3D culture conditions, stem cells can self-organize and execute endogenous developmental programs, giving rise to organotypic structures that recapitulate key histological and functional features of native tissues [33, 34]. Together, these advances enabled robust organoid protocols from pluripotent and adult tissue-resident stem cells, establishing tractable models for developmental biology, regenerative medicine, and cancer research [35–41].

In the intestinal field, the identification of LGR5⁺ cells as bona fide epithelial stem cells provided the critical foundation for CRC organoid models [42]. In a landmark study, single LGR5⁺ cells were shown to generate self-organizing intestinal organoids that recapitulate crypt-villus architecture and multilineage differentiation, first in mice and later in human tissues, when supplied with defined niche factors within a laminin-rich ECM scaffold [37, 43].

Originally conceived as models of normal tissue homeostasis, organoids were poised to drive a transformative advance in cancer research. Tumors can, in many respects, be viewed as distorted versions of normal tissue hierarchies in which latent developmental and regenerative programs are opportunistically resuscitated, a concept first proposed by Pierce and colleagues in the 1970s and 1980s [44, 45] and later substantiated by studies showing that tumor cell states often mirror normal developmental hierarchies and lineage trajectories [46–48]. CRC exemplifies this principle, being driven by inappropriate activation of Wnt signaling, a core developmental pathway [49]. In this context, organoids provide a physiologically relevant framework to investigate how these programs are co-opted during tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic adaptation.

The first patient-derived CRC organoids were reported by Sato and colleagues in 2011 [43]. Unlike normal intestinal organoids, which remained strictly dependent on exogenous niche signals, CRC PDOs showed variable growth-factor requirements, reflecting progressive niche independence acquired during tumor evolution. Larger CRC organoid biobanks later confirmed that PDOs retain key histopathological and gene-expression features of parental tumors, with heterogeneous niche dependencies that parallel tumor progression [50].

These advances position PDOs alongside traditional CRC models, each characterized by distinct strengths and limitations. Conventional cancer cell lines offer scalability and tractability for large-scale genetic and pharmacological screens [51, 52], and in CRC they capture much of the disease’s molecular landscape [53]. However, they lack tumor architecture and are subject to selection biases imposed by prolonged adaptation to two-dimensional (2D) culture. In CRC, this issue is exemplified by the disproportionate reliance on dMMR lines, which can skew biological inference [54]. Primary cultures better preserve original tumor features, but their short lifespan and poor expandability constrain systematic experimentation, long-term assays, and longitudinal analyses [54].

Genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), by contrast, develop autochthonous tumors in immunocompetent hosts and remain essential for dissecting tumor-immune and microenvironmental interactions. Nonetheless, despite recent advances, traditional GEMMs are curbed by laborious multi-allelic engineering, limited scalability, and lower genetic diversity than human tumors [55, 56]. Patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) mitigate some of these constraints by recapitulating patient-specific tumor architecture and heterogeneity [57], and they have proven valuable for translational drug testing [58]. However, conventional PDXs are established in immunodeficient hosts and cannot faithfully model tumor-immune interactions. Moreover, they remain resource-intensive, relatively low-throughput, and susceptible to mouse stromal replacement and selective drift during propagation, all of which should be considered in translational settings [58–61]. Although humanized PDX models can help compensate for host immunodeficiency, they remain technically complex and do not fully recapitulate all aspects of human immunity, which constrains their broader routine use [62].

Within this landscape, PDOs combine key biological and practical strengths of existing CRC models. They retain patient-specific molecular and phenotypic features while remaining experimentally tractable, easily expandable, genetically manipulatable, and amenable to medium- and high-throughput pharmacological interrogation. These properties have positioned organoids at the center of functional precision oncology, supporting their integration with targeted genome engineering, single-cell and multi-omics profiling, biomarker discovery, and drug sensitivity testing [30, 63, 64]. More broadly, growing regulatory efforts to reduce reliance on animal testing have incorporated organoids within the expanding framework of New Approach Methodologies, human-relevant in vitro platforms intended to minimize the use of animal models in drug development and translational research [65, 66].

Mechanistic insight from CRC organoids for precision oncology

Therapeutic response in CRC is not solely dictated by its underlying genomic landscape, but rather emerges from the dynamic interplay among clonal evolution, cellular states, and selective pressures imposed by the microenvironment and therapeutic interventions. In this framework, tumors should be regarded as moving targets, continuously adapting through divergent evolutionary trajectories, compensatory signaling networks, and cell-state transitions that reshape therapeutic vulnerabilities over time. Although high-resolution molecular profiling technologies have profoundly advanced CRC characterization, static snapshots - even when exceptionally detailed - often fail to capture the temporal and adaptive nature of the disease. Precision oncology therefore requires experimental platforms able to monitor the dynamic processes underlying tumor initiation, clonal evolution, and cellular plasticity, enabling the identification not only of pre-existing oncogenic dependencies but also of those that emerge during progression or in response to treatment.

In this context, organoid models have emerged as powerful tools for the functional and longitudinal interrogation of tumor-intrinsic evolutionary and adaptive processes, owing to their ability to preserve selected features of tumor heterogeneity and plasticity. By enabling these dynamic processes to be monitored under controlled experimental conditions, organoids offer the opportunity not only to anticipate trajectories of therapeutic resistance, but also, potentially, to steer tumor evolutionary paths toward states of increased treatment vulnerability. However, these models capture only a subset of the selective pressures operating in vivo, as tumor evolution in patients is also shaped by complex interactions with the tumor microenvironment, the immune system, the microbiota, and systemic environmental factors. In the following sections, we discuss how organoid models are contributing to our understanding of CRC initiation and progression, as well as the mechanisms of cellular plasticity that sustain tumor adaptation and therapeutic resistance.

Cancer initiation and genetic evolution

Understanding how CRC initiates and evolves is central to precision oncology, as the temporal order and interaction of genetic alterations shape tumor heterogeneity, metastatic competence, immune evasion, and therapeutic vulnerabilities. Deciphering these evolutionary trajectories is therefore essential to identify clinically actionable dependencies, define the timing of their emergence, and ultimately determine how and when they can be most effectively therapeutically exploited.

Organoid technology has profoundly transformed the study of tumor initiation and evolution in CRC, owing in large part to its well-defined genetic architecture. In the early 1990s, Vogelstein and colleagues proposed a model describing a series of genetic alterations that drive CRC progression. In this adenoma-carcinoma sequence, the transition from benign adenoma to invasive carcinoma and metastatic disease occurs in a stepwise manner, with each stage associated with specific genetic events. This so-called “Vogelgram” conceptualized colorectal tumorigenesis as a succession of alterations affecting four major pathways: constitutive activation of Wnt signaling, activating mutations in downstream effectors of receptor tyrosine kinase signaling (such as BRAF or KRAS), and subsequent inactivation of the TGF-β and p53 pathways [49]. This model was initially validated in GEMMs, in which sequential perturbation of these pathways resulted in progressively advanced neoplastic lesions [67]. However, the marked genetic instability and heterogeneity of CRC have made it difficult to directly link individual genetic alterations to discrete stages of tumor progression. Moreover, available mouse models are limited in their capacity to recapitulate late-stage disease, as their lifespan often precludes progression to overt metastasis. These limitations highlighted the need for experimental systems capable of reconstructing tumor evolution in a controlled and human-relevant context, thereby enabling the identification of predictive biomarkers and therapeutic liabilities associated with distinct evolutionary states.

Early studies using CRC-derived organoids demonstrated that the mutational status of the canonical pathways described in the Vogelgram determines growth factor dependencies in vitro [50]. The convergence of a well-defined genetic framework of CRC progression with the ability to selectively culture organoids harboring specific genetic alterations through tailored media conditions established organoids as a tractable and highly informative platform for mechanistic studies of CRC initiation and evolution. This paradigm was further strengthened by the emergence of CRISPR-Cas genome-editing technologies, which enabled precise engineering of intestinal organoids and direct functional interrogation of genotype-to-phenotype relationships.

Two seminal studies by Matano et al. [68] and Drost et al. [63] in 2015 provided critical experimental validation of this approach. Drost and colleagues introduced targeted mutations in APC, TP53, KRAS, and SMAD4 into human intestinal stem cell (ISC)-derived organoids by CRISPR/Cas9, selecting defined mutant combinations through stepwise withdrawal of niche factors [63]. Upon xenotransplantation into immunodeficient mice, these engineered organoids enabled direct dissection of how specific oncogenic mutations contribute to histopathological features of invasive carcinoma. Furthermore, three-dimensional live-cell imaging revealed the emergence of chromosome segregation defects and the acquisition of extensive aneuploidy. Matano and colleagues similarly demonstrated that combinations of canonical CRC mutations were insufficient to fully recapitulate tumorigenic and metastatic behavior after xenotransplantation unless introduced into organoids with pre-existing CIN, highlighting the critical role of genomic context beyond individual driver events [68]. Subsequent work by Fumagalli and colleagues reinforced this conclusion by showing that aggressive and invasive phenotypes depend specifically on alterations affecting CIN-associated genes, including loss of SMAD family proteins [69]. Collectively, these studies established a key principle for precision oncology: clinically relevant tumor phenotypes may emerge not simply from isolated oncogenic events, but from broader genome-wide evolutionary states such as CIN. This may partly explain why tumors carrying similar driver mutations can nevertheless display markedly different clinical behavior and treatment responses.

These foundational studies stimulated considerable efforts to improve genome engineering in organoid systems. A base-editing optimized approach enabled efficient introduction of specific single-nucleotide variants, although restricted to a limited subset of nucleotide conversions [70]. Prime editing subsequently expanded the editable mutational spectrum in colonic organoids, albeit with modest efficiency and variable specificity [71]. More recently, a multiplex base-editing strategy leveraging Cas9 orthologs from different species enabled the simultaneous editing of multiple genes in a single step. By minimizing culture time and repeated clonal selection steps, this strategy reduced off-target accumulation compared with sequential approaches [72]. Complementary to these, Lorenzo-Martin and colleagues developed an ex vivo “mini-colon” system incorporating optogenetically controlled CRISPR-Cas9, thereby enabling spatiotemporal control over genome editing to investigate how the timing and topography of mutational events influence clonal expansion dynamics [73]. Although technically demanding and limited in throughput, this system illustrates the increasing sophistication of organoid-based models for studying tumor evolution.

Not all CRCs follow the canonical Vogelgram trajectory. Up to 30% of sporadic colorectal cancers develop through the serrated pathway [74], which constitutes a leading cause of interval cancers diagnosed within 60 months of a negative colonoscopy [75]. In contrast to CRCs arising through the conventional adenoma-carcinoma sequence, serrated CRCs are enriched for activating BRAFV600E mutations and are strongly associated with MSI and the CpG island methylator phenotype. Although the clinical and molecular characteristics of serrated CRC are well established, a comprehensive understanding of the genetic and signaling networks driving tumor initiation and progression remains incomplete [76]. Organoid models have recently provided important insights into this alternative evolutionary route. For example, colonic organoids derived from Villin-Cre; BrafV637E mice were treated with 4-hydroxytamoxifen to induce oncogenic BRAF kinase activity, thereby establishing an in vitro model of serrated colorectal tumorigenesis. Using this system, investigators sequentially disrupted Tgfbr2, Rnf43, Znrf3, p16Ink4a, and Mlh1 via CRISPR-Cas9 and performed orthotopic organoid transplantation to evaluate how cumulative genetic alterations influence tumorigenic potential [77]. In a separate study, organoids harboring BRAFV600E together with R-spondin 1 fusions generated flat serrated lesions in mice, whereas additional overexpression of Grem 1 (an inhibitor of the TGF-β signaling pathway) resulted in tumors exhibiting the histopathological features of human traditional serrated adenomas [78].

CRCs driven by mismatch repair defects harbor a distinct spectrum of driver mutations acquired through a different evolutionary route. Building on the observation that MLH1-deficient organoids recapitulate MSI-associated mutational signatures in short-term culture [79], Mizutani and colleagues modeled progression toward fully developed MSI CRC through simple growth factor-based selection of spontaneously arising oncogenic mutations, without deliberate genome engineering [80]. This approach was feasible precisely because the elevated mutation rate characteristic of MSI tumors accelerates the spontaneous acquisition of driver alterations within experimentally accessible timeframes.

Despite their power for controlled mechanistic studies, engineered organoid systems necessarily capture only a predefined fraction of the genetic history of CRC. By design, they impose a reductionist view of tumorigenesis that cannot fully reproduce the stochastic, genome-wide accumulation of mutations and copy number alterations that shape the unique evolutionary trajectory of individual tumors. In this regard, organoids have proven more powerful not as engineering platforms but as experimental systems for directly observing and quantifying endogenous cellular processes, providing a more faithful context compared to traditional 2D cell lines. Their critical advantage is the ability to generate clonal cultures from single cells, providing sufficient high-quality DNA for whole-genome sequencing while avoiding many of the amplification artifacts inherent to single-cell sequencing approaches. Blokzijl and colleagues exploited this strategy to sequence clonal organoids derived from multiple healthy human tissues, revealing that mutation burden increases linearly with age and is broadly comparable across tissues, while the underlying mutational profiles are tissue-specific and largely driven by endogenous processes [81]. Roerink and colleagues extended this approach to tumor-derived organoids, using multiregional clonal cultures to reconstruct phylogenetic relationships and quantify intratumor heterogeneity with unprecedented resolution [82, 83]. More recently, we adapted mutation accumulation experiments – classically conducted in lower organisms – to organoid cultures to directly measure mutation rates in individual MSS CRCs, providing an experimentally testable complement to computational inference from bulk sequencing data [84].

Organoids have likewise emerged as uniquely faithful systems for studying CIN. Notably, loss of normal tissue architecture in conventional 2D monolayers is itself sufficient to induce error-prone cell divisions, whereas these defects do not materialize in healthy 3D organoid cultures [85, 86]. Using live-cell imaging of patient-derived tumor organoids, Bolhaqueiro and colleagues demonstrated that CIN is widespread in CRC regardless of genetic background – including MSI status – and that CIN levels vary substantially even within individual tumors, thereby contributing to intratumor heterogeneity [86]. Building on this, the same group developed 3D Live-Seq, an approach combining live-cell imaging with whole-genome sequencing of imaged cells to reconstruct karyotype evolution across sequential cell generations [87]. This strategy revealed that CRC evolution follows both gradual and punctuated dynamics, with early acquisition of major chromosomal alterations followed by ongoing instability that fuels subclonal diversification. More recently, integration of copy-number variation and single-nucleotide variant profiles from single-cell whole-genome sequencing with viral lineage barcoding enabled the reconstruction of highly resolved evolutionary trees in long-term organoid evolution experiments spanning more than 100 cellular generations over approximately six months of culture. This multi-modal approach allowed the identification of recurrent chromosomal events together with their timing and order of acquisition during CRC evolution, demonstrating the power of organoid systems to model long-term tumor evolutionary trajectories under controlled conditions [88].

A major limitation shared by nearly all organoid models – whether employed as engineering or observational platforms – is the absence of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Buissant des Amorie et al. highlighted this limitation by generating multiregional organoids from phenotypically distinct regions of early-stage CRC tumors, including adenomatous regions, tumor cores, and invasive fronts [89]. Despite marked phenotypic divergence, these organoids harbored largely comparable driver mutations and karyotypic profiles, suggesting that cell-intrinsic genomic differences alone were insufficient to explain their distinct behaviors. Functional interrogation using tailored media further excluded cell-intrinsic traits as primary determinants, implicating microenvironmental cues as key determinants of phenotypic heterogeneity.

Mouse-derived CRC organoid models combined with syngeneic transplantation into immunocompetent hosts can partially address this limitation by preserving interactions with an intact TME [90]. In these systems, oncogenic alterations can be engineered in vitro or introduced in vivo using inducible genetic tools, and transplantation sites can be tailored to model both primary growth and metastatic dissemination. The host and stromal compartments can be genetically manipulated independently through lineage-specific Cre drivers, and engineered stromal cell populations can be co-implanted with tumor organoids in selected experimental settings [90]. Despite the technical challenges associated with efficient stromal engraftment, these models can help interrogate the respective contributions of tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental factors to tumor progression and therapeutic response. Tauriello et al. exemplified this approach by generating a biobank of mouse CRC organoids with diverse genetic backgrounds, orthotopically transplanted into syngeneic immunocompetent and immunodeficient hosts. With this procedure, they showed that TGF-β-activated stroma drives immune evasion in MSS CRC [91]. By combining pharmacological inhibition of TGF-β signaling, genetic ablation of Tgfbr2 in the host, and organoids carrying Smad4 loss-of-function mutations, the study dissected the respective contributions of tumor-intrinsic and stromal TGF-β signaling to immune exclusion. Nevertheless, species-specific differences in epithelial biology and immune responses remain important considerations when extrapolating these findings to human disease.

Altogether, organoid-based approaches have substantially advanced our understanding of CRC initiation and evolution, offering experimental access to processes – from mutation rate measurement to real-time karyotype dynamics – that were previously difficult to interrogate in physiologically relevant systems. Fully realizing the potential of these platforms, however, will require integrating increasingly sophisticated microenvironmental components and leveraging large patient-derived biobanks capable of capturing the genetic and phenotypic diversity of the disease.

Phenotypic plasticity

CRCs comprise heterogeneous tumor cell populations exhibiting distinct transcriptional and functional states, only a minority of which can be attributed to clonal genetic alterations [92]. Intratumoral heterogeneity therefore largely reflects non-genetic plasticity – the capacity of cancer cells to reversibly transition between phenotypic states without acquiring permanent genomic changes. Much of this diversity mirrors the hierarchical organization of the normal colonic epithelium, wherein LGR5-expressing crypt-base columnar cells sustain tissue homeostasis through continuous self-renewal and multilineage differentiation [42]. Early lineage-tracing experiments in mice demonstrated that this hierarchy is not rigid. Under conditions of tissue damage or inflammation, cells committed to the secretory lineage can regain stem-cell properties: DLL1-expressing secretory progenitors return to a stem-like state after crypt damage, while slow-cycling secretory precursors can re-enter the stem-cell compartment and contribute to epithelial regeneration [93, 94]. These observations challenged the notion of a strict unidirectional differentiation trajectory and established plasticity as an intrinsic property of the intestinal epithelium.

From the perspective of precision oncology, these findings have major implications: tumor behavior cannot be fully inferred from static molecular classifications alone, as cellular identity itself is dynamic and can be reshaped during disease progression and therapeutic intervention. The development of organoid technology provided a tractable platform to directly trace these phenotypic switches and extend these observations to cancer biology.

A first contribution of organoid systems in this field has been methodological but highly consequential. Since organoid-forming capacity is a functional readout of stem potential [43, 95], culturing defined cell populations ex vivo has been used to assess their stem-like properties – a strategy that 2D immortalized cell lines cannot support due to their restricted phenotypic composition. Using this approach in mouse models, Buczacki et al. demonstrated that label-retaining intestinal cells could regenerate LGR5+-containing organoids in culture [94], while Barriga et al. showed that MEX3A+/LGR5+ cells similarly formed organoids encompassing all intestinal lineages [96]. In human CRC samples, Ganesh and colleagues subsequently identified an L1CAM+ subpopulation with enhanced organoid-forming activity that is detected in both primary colorectal tumors and liver metastases [97]. More recently, Verhagen and colleagues used the same assay in mouse models to support a role for Paneth cell as an alternative cell of origin for intestinal tumorigenesis in the context of inflammation through dedifferentiation and acquisition of stem-like properties [98]. By functionally interrogating reversible stem-like states, organoid systems have therefore provided a framework for identifying tumor cell populations with enhanced regenerative and tumor-propagating potential, features that are highly relevant to therapeutic persistence and disease relapse.

Beyond these functional assays, organoid technology has been coupled with CRISPR-Cas9 knock-in reporters, suicide cassettes, lineage-tracing constructs, and barcode-based approaches to characterize tumor cell subpopulations and investigate their plastic properties during cancer initiation, metastasis, and drug tolerance. In a seminal study, De Sousa e Melo et al. showed that diphtheria toxin-mediated ablation of LGR5+ cells in xenotransplanted murine CRC organoids failed to durably suppress tumor growth, and that LGR5+ cells rapidly re-emerged upon toxin withdrawal. This provided direct evidence that non-LGR5 cells can re-acquire stem-cell identity [99]. Shimokawa et al. corroborated these findings in human CRC organoid xenografts engineered with a rainbow reporter inserted into the LGR5 locus to trace LGR5+ cell dynamics, together with inducible LGR5-iCaspase9-tdTomato construct enabling controlled LGR5+ cell ablation [100]. Following LGR5+ cell depletion, tumors initially shrank but eventually regrew in parallel with re-establishment of the LGR5+ compartment. Crucially, this process was fully recapitulated in organoids cultured in vitro, demonstrating that the plastic rescue of LGR5+ cells could occur independently of stromal input. By directing a lineage-tracing cassette to the KRT20 locus, the authors further showed that differentiated KRT20+ cells could revert to an LGR5+ tumor-initiating state, directly sustaining tumor regrowth. Fumagalli et al. extended this bidirectional plasticity to metastatic progression, demonstrating via intravital imaging of murine CRC organoid xenografts that most circulating tumor cells are LGR5−, yet seed liver metastases in which LGR5+ cells subsequently re-emerge in a niche-independent manner [101].

These early studies were largely centered on the binary distinction between LGR5+ and LGR5−subpopulations. In more recent years, the introduction of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dramatically expanded the resolution at which phenotypic heterogeneity could be examined. In this setting, organoids have served both as discovery platforms, amenable to high-resolution transcriptional characterization for the identification of novel functional states, and as mechanistic systems to experimentally interrogate states initially defined in patient samples. They have also proven particularly valuable for studying adaptive response to therapy, a domain that is difficult to investigate in clinical material in patients because longitudinal sampling of treatment-exposed tumors is logistically and ethically challenging. This has been especially informative for tumors that initially respond to treatment but eventually relapse following an early adaptive drug-tolerant phase.

In the context of therapy resistance, a live genetic-tracing system in xenotransplanted human CRC organoids – in which LGR5+ cells were stochastically labelled with fluorescent reporters to enable clonal tracking – identified a pre-existing quiescent LGR5+/p27+ subset that survived chemotherapy and re-entered the cell cycle upon treatment cessation, conferring a reversible drug-tolerant state dependent on adhesion to COL17A1 at the cell-matrix interface [102]. In parallel, Álvarez-Varela et al. combined scRNA-seq, lineage tracing, and suicide constructs in patient-derived CRC organoids. By doing so, they identified a latent LGR5+ population marked by high MEX3A expression that downregulates the Wnt/ISC gene program upon chemotherapy exposure and adopts a transient YAP-driven fetal progenitor-like state before reconstituting the tumor [103]. Using both human CRC samples and mouse models, Cañellas-Socias et al. identified a distinct EMP1-high residual population, termed high-relapse cells (HRCs), surrounded by α-SMA+ cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and capable of regenerating the LGR5+ cancer stem-cell compartment. Targeted CRISPR-mediated ablation of HRCs in mice prevented metastatic recurrence in xenotransplanted mice [104]. Ganesh and colleagues further demonstrated that L1CAM+ cells – enriched at invasive fronts, metastatic lesions, and post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy samples – arise, at least in part, through disruption of intercellular epithelial contacts, which relieves E-cadherin/REST-mediated transcriptional repression of L1CAM. These cells exhibited enhanced resistance to irinotecan-based therapy, and their selective depletion restored chemosensitivity [97]. Additional studies have shown that chemotherapy can induce the transition of LGR5+ cancer stem cells into an LGR5-negative state characterized by activation of fetal and regenerative intestinal gene programs [105, 106].

Despite the apparent diversity of these functional populations, a recurrent theme has emerged: many adaptive and drug-tolerant states converge on activation of oncofetal and regenerative transcriptional signatures. These states are fundamentally distinct from the canonical Wnt-dependent LGR5+ stem-cell program and become increasingly enriched as disease progresses. From a broader perspective, these studies collectively indicate that cancer cell plasticity is pervasive in CRC and that functional cellular identities are dynamic rather than fixed. This has direct therapeutic implications, since strategies targeting a single cell state are likely to be undermined by state transitions and phenotypic escape.

Attention has consequently shifted towards identifying the molecular determinants of plasticity itself, an area in which organoid models have again played a central role. Studies using patient-derived and genetically engineered organoid systems have shown that conversion from the classical Wnt-dependent LGR5+ state toward oncofetal and regenerative programs can be triggered by CAF-derived signals [89, 104, 107], chemotherapy exposure [103, 106], suboptimal niche conditions [103], and mechanical cues [108] (Fig. 1). A recent study revealed a pivotal role for oncogenic KRAS signaling in driving an oncofetal/regenerative transcriptional program while suppressing the classical LGR5/Wnt-dependent state. Consistent with this model, KRASG12D inhibitors shifted tumor populations toward an LGR5+ configuration, providing a rationale for combining KRAS inhibitors with LGR5-targeted therapies to therapeutically exploit CRC cell-state plasticity [109] (Fig. 1).

Using isogenic mouse organoids harboring sequential driver mutations, Mzoughi et al. demonstrated that the Retinoid X Receptor functions as a gatekeeper of oncofetal reprogramming during tumor initiation. Following APC loss, activation of YAP and AP1 was sufficient to sustain the plastic oncofetal state during tumor progression. Notably, the authors argued that the clinical significance of oncofetal and LGR5+ states in isolation may be constrained by their functional redundancy [111]. Interestingly, scRNA-seq of matched normal colon, primary CRC, and metastatic lesions revealed distinct patterns of tumor cell identity during disease progression: primary tumors predominantly retained an LGR5+ ISC-like phenotype, whereas metastases exhibited marked cellular plasticity [110]; in particular, metastatic cancer cells underwent reprogramming into a conserved fetal progenitor state, followed by non-canonical differentiation toward squamous- and neuroendocrine-like lineages [110]. Using matched PDOs, the study found that PROX1 is a marker of the fetal progenitor state and acts as a key repressor of non-intestinal lineage programs. Progressive loss of PROX1 during tumor evolution unleashes lineage infidelity enabling aberrant multilineage differentiation, largely in a cell-autonomous manner [110] (Fig. 1). Complementing these findings, Snippert and colleagues recently provided functional evidence using multiregional paired organoid models that, in early stage CRC, de novo acquisition of oncofetal cell states at the invasive front is instigated by trophocyte-like CAF populations within the TME [89] (Fig. 1). Together, these studies reposition phenotypic plasticity from a descriptive hallmark of CRC biology to a potentially actionable therapeutic vulnerability. By identifying the signaling pathways, transcriptional programs, and microenvironmental cues governing cell-state transitions, organoid models provide an opportunity to develop combinatorial therapeutic strategies aimed at constraining tumor adaptability and steering tumor evolution toward more tractable cellular configurations.

At the same time, the experimental tractability of organoids represents a double-edged property in this context. Culture conditions – including medium composition and organoid size – can per se alter the relative abundance of phenotypic subpopulations [112, 113]. Moreover, standard organoid culture protocols may fail to maintain fetal and regenerative cell states during prolonged passages, whereas chemically defined culture systems appear to better maintain these populations [114]. As a consequence, the phenotypic composition of organoid cultures reflects not only the biology of the originating tumor, but also the selective pressures imposed by in vitro culture conditions. Although this plasticity can be experimentally leveraged to generate controlled representations of specific cell states [103], it equally underscores the need for rigorous standardization to ensure that conclusions remain generalizable beyond a given culture system.

Patient-derived organoids as functional precision oncology platforms in colorectal cancer

A central tenet of precision oncology is that therapeutic strategies should be tailored to the molecular features of individual tumors [115]. In CRC, large-scale genomic and transcriptomic profiling has defined clinically relevant molecular subgroups [3, 116–119]. Nevertheless, these classifications remain largely correlative and translate into matched therapy for only a minority of patients [120], a gap compounded by the persistently high attrition rate of anticancer agents during clinical development [121].

In the quest for innovative, human-relevant cancer models, organoids stand out as particularly promising tools because they faithfully recapitulate the histoarchitectural organization, genetic landscape and phenotypic traits of their tumors of origin across diverse oncogenic contexts [122–127]. Notably, in selected experimental settings, subtype identity and drug response patterns can be broadly retained through iterative in vitro and in vivo passages [128], although culture-associated changes in clonal architecture or phenotypic composition may still occur [129]. This biological fidelity, combined with experimental versatility, has fueled the establishment of living organoid biobanks across multiple tumor types [123, 126, 130–132], providing clinically annotated repositories of sufficient scale to advance functional precision oncology.

In CRC, the establishment of organoid biobanks has been expedited by the high efficiency of organoid derivation procedures, which in some studies exceed 90% [50, 133]. These advances have enabled the generation of collections that encompass both primary tumors and metastatic lesions [134]. A seminal study by van de Wetering et al. [126] established the first CRC organoid biobank, integrating genomic profiling with high-throughput drug screening. Even within a relatively small cohort, this resource captured marked inter-patient variability in drug response and reproduced key biomarker-drug associations. Subsequent single-cell analyses showed pronounced intratumoral diversification within CRC organoids, with distinct subclones exhibiting divergent drug sensitivities [82], highlighting how phenotypic heterogeneity within individual tumors may compromise response prediction.

The widespread adoption of organoid biobanking spurred efforts to use ex vivo drug responses as functional biomarkers in CRC (Fig. 2). In a landmark study, mCRC PDOs exposed to clinically relevant compounds, including chemotherapies and targeted agents, exhibited response profiles that substantially reflected treatment outcomes in patients, with reported positive and negative predictive values of 88% and 100%, respectively [135]. Subsequent studies yielded more nuanced conclusions, reporting stronger concordance for irinotecan-based regimens than for oxaliplatin-containing therapies [136, 137]. Encouraging results also emerged in rectal cancer, where treatment-associated morbidity and the steep rise in disease incidence over the last decade make early identification of non-responders a pressing clinical priority [138–140]. In this heterogeneous landscape, the OPTIC study represents one of the strongest prospective validations of PDO-based treatment prediction. By implementing optimized workflows for organoid establishment and pharmacological testing, the study demonstrated that PDO-patient concordance can extend beyond radiological response to clinically more informative endpoints, including progression-free survival and overall survival, across both chemotherapy and targeted approaches [141, 142]. Overall, these studies support the potential of PDO-based assays for treatment-response prediction in CRC, while also underscoring that their performance is shaped by biological and technical variables, including cohort size, sample quality, culture conditions, predefined sensitivity cutoffs, and biopsy processing time. Interpretation of PDO drug-sensitivity data must also take into account the temporal disconnect between ex vivo assays, which typically measure response over a few days, and clinical treatment schedules, which often extend over several months and encompass ongoing tumor evolution. These observations emphasize the importance of optimized and standardized workflows, as illustrated by the OPTIC study [141], and highlight the need for broader prospective validation to strengthen the predictive value of PDO-based assays across treatment settings. Recently, automated organoid-generation procedures and microfluidic droplet-bioprinting platforms have emerged as promising strategies to improve scalability, reproducibility, turnaround times, and predictive performance of functional drug screening, thereby increasing compatibility of organoid-based testing with clinical decision-making timelines [143].

Beyond treatment response prediction, organoid models have been extensively used to interrogate tumor biology at multiple levels [144, 145] (Fig. 2). Alonso and colleagues, for example, used CRC organoids to model adaptation to KRAS inhibition, showing that genetically encoded resistance mechanisms coexist with non-genetic inflammatory and fetal-like states, and that disrupting adaptive reprogramming enhances KRAS inhibitor activity [146]. Organoid systems can also be applied to interrogate residual, therapy-responsive tumor states that are difficult to access clinically. In this context, we used matched PDX-PDO models to show that the anti-EGFR antibody cetuximab primes sensitive mCRC cells for apoptosis, and that BCL-XL blockade unleashes this primed state to convert EGFR blockade into a deeper cytotoxic response [147]. These studies exemplify how organoid platforms can identify transient adaptive liabilities that are not readily predictable from baseline molecular profiling alone (Fig. 2).

Although organoid-based approaches have proven informative in the context of targeted therapies, their use to dissect and predict responses to conventional chemotherapy remains comparatively more challenging. This limitation is particularly relevant in CRC, where cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment and where ineffective treatment can expose patients to unnecessary toxicity without meaningful benefit. Biomarker discovery in this setting remains fragmented, with several candidate determinants but no consolidated framework for patient stratification. In this context, using integrated PDX and PDO models alongside clinical validation in patients, we identified low expression of the RAD51 recombinase – which assists in repair of DNA double strand breaks – as a functional biomarker associated with impaired homologous recombination and a BRCAness-like phenotype predictive of chemotherapy sensitivity in mCRC [148]. Continued organoid-based discovery efforts may help generalize such mechanistic insights across chemotherapy contexts and translate them into clinically actionable models of chemosensitivity (Fig. 2).

At a broader level, the field has increasingly evolved toward multidimensional CRC platforms combining organoids with complementary model systems and molecular annotation strategies to capture tumor heterogeneity and drug response landscapes with greater resolution. The OncoTrack initiative represents a relevant example of this direction, integrating PDOs, PDXs, multi-omics profiling, and pharmacological testing to derive molecular predictors of therapeutic response, including a transcriptomic signature of cetuximab sensitivity that outperforms RAS/RAF mutational status alone [149]. The open-science resource XENTURION embraces a similar multidimensional strategy by combining matched PDX-PDO models with extensive molecular and pharmacological annotation. However, it is designed to map adaptive vulnerabilities rather than predict treatment response. Using a stepwise strategy integrating cetuximab-induced transcriptional rewiring, functional screening, and in vivo validation in XENTURION models, we identified adaptive co-dependencies that enhanced EGFR blockade responses, uncovering targetable modifiers of anti-EGFR sensitivity [150].

The increasing scale and depth of annotation has also established organoids as useful platforms for CRC drug discovery [151] (Fig. 2). Particularly valuable is the possibility of benchmarking antitumor activity against toxicity using matched normal organoids, an approach that can help uncover putative cancer-specific vulnerabilities despite the practical infeasibility of obtaining patient-matched normal tissues from multiple organs [145]. Even so, examples in which organoid-based discovery has directly led to new, clinically relevant therapeutic candidates remain uncommon. One notable exception is MCLA-158 (petosemtamab), identified through a bispecific antibody screen performed in CRC PDOs alongside matched normal counterparts. This agent elicits LGR5-dependent EGFR degradation selectively in cancer stem cells, outperforming cetuximab in KRAS-wild-type organoids while retaining activity in KRAS-mutant models, a setting typically refractory to conventional EGFR-targeted therapies [152]. In this case, organoid technology has been leveraged to select for biological features difficult to capture in conventional screens, including selective stem cell-state targeting and relative sparing of normal tissue. MCLA-158 is currently undergoing phase 1/2 clinical evaluation in advanced solid tumors, including mCRC as monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy (NCT03526835) [153].

An additional advantage of organoid technology lies in the possibility to incorporate early lesions and rare histological and molecular subtypes that are often underrepresented in conventional preclinical models [50, 128]. This is particularly relevant for emerging entities such as sporadic early-onset CRC (EOCRC), which shows increasing incidence and distinct clinicopathological features compared with late-onset disease, yet remains biologically poorly understood [140, 154, 155]. Recently, an EOCRC-enriched organoid biobank captured genetic profiles that are poorly represented in existing CRC models, including PTPRK-RSPO3 fusions associated with Wnt-dependent transcriptional features and preserved differentiation potential, supporting a rationale for differentiation-based therapeutic strategies [156]. Organoid platforms have also been extended to high-risk adenomas, allowing drug testing in premalignant contexts [157].

Collectively, PDO models spanning from normal epithelium to metastatic disease provide a valuable framework to study tumor evolution and stage-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities. Larger prospective biobanks and coordinated efforts are still needed to standardize workflows, broaden molecular representation, and support clinically integrated functional precision oncology studies.

The winding road of patient-derived CRC organoids from ex vivo drug testing to clinical decision-making

A major translational ambition of PDO technology is to move beyond drug-response modeling and become an actionable component of real-time clinical decision-making (Fig. 2). This concept is increasingly being explored across tumor types; however, its clinical implementation remains uneven, with prospective treatment-guiding studies still constrained by assay feasibility, the lack of standardized response-assessment metrics, and the challenge of delivering clinically relevant results within therapeutically feasible turnaround times [158].

CRC exemplifies this translational gap. In an illustrative case report, treatment selection guided by PDO drug-sensitivity testing was followed by a partial radiological response that enabled a second liver metastasectomy [159]. Yet, the broader clinical landscape remains sobering and difficult to interpret systematically. A manual survey of ClinicalTrials.gov records together with published prospective and interventional studies reveals that an increasing number of CRC trials now incorporate PDOs into their design. However, these studies show considerable heterogeneity in how PDOs are operationally integrated into clinical workflows. Across trials, PDOs are used to evaluate multiple therapeutic classes, predominantly cytotoxic chemotherapy and, to a lesser extent, targeted agents. In some cases, PDO-derived drug-sensitivity data become part of treatment discussions – often within multidisciplinary molecular tumor boards and alongside genomic profiling – to guide or support treatment selection. Yet, these studies are typically non-randomized, frequently retain physician discretion over final treatment allocation, and are therefore intrinsically limited in their ability to establish a causal impact of PDO-guided therapeutic strategies on clinical outcomes. In other settings, organoids serve primarily as exploratory, correlative, or feasibility tools aimed at investigating mechanisms of drug-response or identifying predictive biomarkers, rather than informing treatment decisions (Table 1).

Interventional/prospective studies incorporating CRC PDOs into clinical workflows

In the SENSOR trial, organoid establishment and pharmacological testing were completed within a 10-week timeframe, but only a minority of patients ultimately received PDO-guided therapy, largely because of low culture success rate and clinical deterioration during intervening standard-of-care treatment; also, no RECIST-defined objective responses were observed among treated patients [160]. Similarly, the APOLLO study demonstrated the feasibility of PDO profiling within an approximately 8-week workflow; however, treatment modifications informed by PDO results were exceptional, partly reflecting restricted access to off-label therapies and funding constraints; although one partial response was documented, the study remained underpowered to draw robust conclusions [137]. More recently, Jensen and colleagues reported one of the largest prospective interventional studies in refractory mCRC, achieving shorter turnaround times and delivering PDO-informed treatment to a larger subset of patients. Nonetheless, the observed benefit was mainly limited to short-term disease stabilization, while the absence of a comparative design limited assessment of true therapeutic efficacy [161]. Likewise, Martini and colleagues showed that PDO drug screening could inform selected treatment decisions in chemorefractory mCRC, but clinical benefit was again restricted to disease stabilization in a small number of patients, without clear evidence of tumor regression [162].

Randomized evidence remains scarce. Examples include the SYNCOPE trial (NCT04842006) in rectal cancer, which evaluates a precision strategy incorporating circulating tumor DNA analysis and organoid-guided adjuvant therapy; NCT05352165, comparing PDO-guided personalized neoadjuvant therapy with standard neoadjuvant treatment in locally advanced rectal cancer; and NCT05832398 in mCRC, directly comparing PDO-informed chemotherapy selection against standard-of-care regimens. Results from these trials are still pending.

Taken together, these experiences illustrate both the promise and the current limitations of organoid-based functional precision oncology. Although the conceptual rationale supporting PDO-guided therapeutic stratification is compelling, the path toward routine clinical implementation remains unsettled in CRC, as in other tumor contexts. Beyond technical feasibility, successful integration into clinical practice will ultimately depend on whether PDO-derived information can be generated reliably, returned within clinically compatible timelines, acted upon therapeutically, and translated into clinically meaningful benefit.

Next-generation CRC organoids beyond epithelial reductionism

In a seminal Cell Stem Cell Perspective published in 2013, Yoshiki Sasai, a pioneer in the field of 3D stem cell technology, emphasized that self-organizing culture models and conventional tissue engineering should not be viewed as mutually exclusive approaches, despite their conceptual difference [33]. Although originally formulated in the field of developmental biology, this perspective also frames the current evolution of organoid technology: a shift from reductionist epithelial self-organization toward increasingly complex multicellular structures that better approximate tissue-level architecture and function.

Classical epithelial organoids, by design, exclude stromal, immune, and vascular compartments and therefore do not capture the TME crosstalk that shapes both therapeutic response – particularly to immunotherapy – and metastatic dissemination [158, 163–165]. In this respect, the very feature that makes classical organoids experimentally powerful – their simplicity – also defines one of their main limitations and introduces a fundamental trade-off between tractability and biological complexity. This limitation is particularly relevant in CRC, where stromal-rich transcriptional states and immune contexture are markedly associated with clinical outcome [166]. The example of immunotherapy is particularly illustrative. ICB has transformed the treatment landscape for MSI-high/dMMR CRC, characterized by high immunogenicity and robust immune infiltration, whereas MSS/pMMR tumors remain largely resistant, and biomarkers beyond MSI/MMR status remain incompletely defined [8, 163, 167].

To overcome the epithelial reductionism of “nude” PDOs, next-generation systems – often referred to as “organoids 2.0” or “immune-organoids” [168] – increasingly incorporate immune and stromal components of the TME, generating more holistic models that better reproduce subtype identity, therapy resistance, and immunologic behavior than epithelial cultures alone [169, 170]. Operationally, two main strategies are deployed to generate immune-organoids: TME reconstitution and TME preservation (Fig. 3). TME reconstitution follows a “bottom-up” logic, whereby epithelial organoids are co-cultured with exogenous stromal or immune components, or alternatively re-aggregated with dissociated bulk tumor material into hybrid formats such as micro-organospheres (MOS) (Fig. 3). By contrast, TME preservation strategies prioritize tissue-level fidelity by culturing intact or partially digested tumor fragments, as in air-liquid interface (ALI) organoids or patient-derived organotypic tumor spheroids (PDOTS), thereby retaining endogenous immune and stromal components together with aspects of the native spatial organization [168] (Fig. 3). The rationale underlying these approaches is compelling, particularly because even humanized PDX models remain imperfect surrogates for patient-specific tumor-immune interactions owing to technical complexity, HLA-related constraints, and incomplete immune reconstitution [62].

Co-culture systems incorporating exogenous stromal and immune populations have rapidly expanded in complexity and scope. These platforms may include CAFs, endothelial cells, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), T cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and engineered immune effectors [169, 171–175] (Fig. 3). In CRC, a landmark study by Dijkstra et al. demonstrated that combining PDOs with autologous PBMCs from patients with dMMR CRC enabled selective enrichment of tumor-reactive T cells, which could be functionally tested for recognition and killing of matched tumor organoids [171]. This platform was later refined into a standardized two-week organoid-PBMC co-culture protocol incorporating functional readouts of T-cell activation, with potential relevance for antigen-agnostic T-cell discovery and adoptive T-cell strategies [176]. In the neoadjuvant setting, related approaches have been explored as functional correlates of immunotherapy response, with variable concordance between ex vivo T-cell-PDO reactivity and clinical outcomes [177]. Engineered immune effector platforms further extend this paradigm, enabling functional evaluation of CAR-mediated cytotoxicity in CRC organoids and supporting development of CAR-based strategies in solid tumors, where clinical translation remains challenging [178].

Beyond immune readouts, reconstituted co-culture systems have proven particularly informative when incorporating CAF populations. In CRC, single-cell profiling of PDO-CAF co-cultures showed that stromal cues can reshape drug response by promoting both chemoprotection and cell-state plasticity. For instance, PDOs otherwise sensitive to cytotoxic agents became protected from chemotherapy-induced apoptosis when cultured in the presence of CAFs [107]. In the same vein, an organoid-stroma biobank showed that CAF co-culture restores fidelity to consensus molecular subtypes (CMS), an established transcriptional classification system for CRC [118], and unmasks salient features associated with the CMS4 subtype, including resistance to EGFR and topoisomerase I inhibition [169, 179]. Related observations had already emerged from genetically reconstituted mouse tumor organoid of MSS-like CRC, in which aggressive phenotypes – including CMS4-related stromal enrichment and immune suppression – were largely absent in vitro but reappeared after transplantation, as tumor cells instructed a TGF-β-activated stromal program eliciting T-cell exclusion and ICB resistance [91]. This study contributed to challenging the rigid view of MSS CRC as uniformly immunologically inert and instead suggested that immunogenicity may be functionally “encrypted” by the TME. In this direction, immune-organoid co-cultures offer a way to identify and target tolerogenic stromal and immune programs that may unmask latent ICB sensitivity in otherwise resistant tumors [180].

Unlike reconstituted co-cultures, TME-preserving methods generate immune organoids retaining endogenous tissue architecture and native multicellular interactions. The ALI protocol was first applied to primary intestinal tissue, enabling long-term organoid cultures that preserved epithelial-mesenchymal organization and multilineage differentiation [181] (Fig. 3), and was later extended to gastrointestinal tumor modelling, where it recapitulated stepwise oncogenic transformation [182]. A landmark pan-cancer study showed that ALI PDOs preserve the parental tumor T-cell receptor repertoire and enable activation and expansion of tumor-specific TILs following ICB exposure within clinically relevant timelines [170]. More recently, CRC-specific ALI models have been deployed to test immunotherapy responses directly ex vivo, revealing local immune remodeling after PD-1 blockade, including expansion of regulatory T-cell populations [183]. CRC-specific ALI cultures remain however comparatively underdeveloped relative to co-culture systems.

Microfluidic technologies have further expanded the reach of next-generation organoid systems by improving automation, miniaturization, and throughput. Among these, MOS are generated from low-input tumor biopsies by droplet-emulsion microfluidics, yielding small tumor-stromal aggregates that retain key microenvironmental features and allow fully automated multiplexed drug and immunotherapy testing [168] (Fig. 3). In mCRC, Ding et al. reported MOS-based drug-response profiling within 14 days of biopsy acquisition, a timeframe compatible with clinical decision-making [184]. This platform has since been applied to automated high-throughput screening of targeted therapy combinations in the context of a registered clinical trial in patients with mCRC (NCT05189171) [185]. This system enabled simultaneous evaluation of multiple agents and dose ranges from limited biopsy material, with ex vivo dose-response profiles correlated with clinical outcomes. Complementing these findings, an independent MOS-based study reported highly reproducible chemotherapy response measurements and retrospective concordance with clinical outcome in CRC [186]. A related microfluidic alternative is represented by PDOTS, in which small tumor fragments are cultured in collagen-based devices (Fig. 3). Compared with MOS, PDOTS avoid full tissue dissociation and therefore better preserve native TME architecture [187]. Although CRC-specific PDOTS studies remain more scattered compared with other tumor types, available evidence supports their feasibility for individualized ex vivo assessment of immunotherapy response [188].

Overall, the historical evolution of intestinal organoid technology has not followed a simple trajectory from epithelial simplicity toward increasing multicellular complexity. Rather, from the outset, two coeval and complementary paradigms co-existed: a reductionist epithelial stem-cell model, emphasizing epithelial autonomy, exogenous niche-factor control, and experimental tractability; and a more organotypic approach aimed at preserving endogenous tissue architecture. This dichotomy later resurfaced in CRC modeling, where both systems proved capable of recapitulating various aspects of tumor biology. Accordingly, “next-generation” organoids reflect less a linear leap forward than a reorientation of the field toward different biological questions. The key challenge is therefore not merely to increase model complexity, but to align model architecture with the biological processes under investigation. Reductionist epithelial organoids and organotypic systems each offer distinct strengths and constraints, illuminating different aspects of stem-cell regulation, niche dependence, immune interactions, and CRC evolution. Ultimately, their value dwells precisely in their nature as models: tunable and experimentally accessible approximations of intestinal biology.

Concluding remarks and challenges

The integration of organoid technology into clinical decision-making for mCRC represents a promising but still evolving avenue of precision oncology. Proof-of-concept studies clearly indicate that organoid-derived data can inform therapeutic discussions and capture clinically relevant aspects of treatment response, but their use to guide regimen selection remains largely investigational and constrained by several biological and technical limitations. Consequently, the promise of PDO-based functional precision medicine in CRC remains aspirational, grounded mainly in preclinical and translational evidence, while prospective demonstrations of meaningful patient benefit remain limited.

One unresolved issue is the limited robustness of the current clinical evidence base. Most studies remain restricted to small cohorts, non-randomized designs, and anecdotal experiences. Standardized analytical pipelines, predefined response cutoffs, and harmonized decision algorithms will be essential to interpret PDO drug-screening results and translate them into reproducible therapeutic recommendations. Without such frameworks, treatment allocation remains vulnerable to discretionary decision-making, limiting causal attribution of clinical outcomes. Implementation will also require coordinated workflows streamlining organoid derivation, pharmacological assays, data interpretation, and treatment assignment at a clinically relevant pace. Adaptive trial designs may provide a flexible framework for real-time functional testing, but only if paired with broader access to off-label and tumor-agnostic therapies, since restricted drug accessibility has already emerged as a major limitation in PDO-guided efforts [137].

Standardization is also essential at the experimental level, both for clinically oriented studies and across the large retrospective, translational, and preclinical drug-screening literature on which much of the current evidence still rests. Inter-laboratory variability in culture conditions, assay readouts, and sensitivity thresholds can undermine the robustness of PDO drug-response measurements and may partly explain some of the inconsistent findings reported in observational studies [136, 137]. This is especially relevant when testing standard cytotoxic agents used in CRC, such as oxaliplatin, since even seemingly minor technical differences in medium composition – such as the inclusion or omission of N-acetylcysteine – may affect drug-response profiles [141, 142]. Similarly, intrinsic variability in division rates among PDOs may bias conventional drug-response metrics and introduce important artifacts when inferring sensitivity profiles, potentially confounding both biomarker discovery and mechanistic studies. Growth rate-corrected approaches have been proposed to mitigate this bias by accounting for differences in baseline proliferation and other assay-related factors that may affect division kinetics [189].

From a practical perspective, a major hurdle is the quality and quantity of starting tumor material. Although early CRC organoid studies reported remarkably high derivation rates under optimized conditions [50, 126, 127], clinical implementation often depends on small biopsy specimens that may be poorly cellular, poorly viable, or otherwise inadequate for successful organoid derivation and downstream pharmacological testing [135]. Tumor heterogeneity – both spatial and temporal – further compounds this problem, as single-biopsy-derived PDOs may not capture the full spectrum of clinically relevant subclones and resistance mechanisms. Sampling multiple tumor regions or performing longitudinal biopsies may help mitigate these biases, but this is hampered by limited tissue availability, procedural burden for patients, and the logistical and experimental complexity associated with establishing and screening multiple parallel organoid cultures from the same individual. Beyond sampling, assay resolution represents an additional consideration: bulk viability measurements may average out heterogeneous drug responses within the available PDO population. Accordingly, non-bulk measurements, including image-based phenotypic profiling and clonal organoid drug testing, can provide higher-resolution information on therapy-response heterogeneity [82, 190]. However, their incorporation into co-clinical workflows remains constrained by scalability, standardization, and practical feasibility.

Turnaround time represents another critical bottleneck. Microfluidic and miniaturized culture platforms can accelerate ex vivo drug testing by reducing input material and shortening assay workflows. However, whether these technologies can be standardized, scaled, and capably integrated into routine clinical workflows remains uncertain.

Current CRC organoid systems are also resource-intensive. Most protocols rely on media supplemented with complex combinations of growth factors and pathway-modulating agents, despite evidence that CRC organoids may be inherently less dependent on selected intestinal niche factors than normal colonic cultures. In practice, however, several widely adopted CRC organoid protocols still recommend the use of growth factor- and niche factor-rich media to ensure efficient establishment and expansion [126, 127, 136]. This has important implications not only for cost and scalability, but also for inter-laboratory reproducibility, and potentially drug-response readouts. In parallel, the use of animal-derived ECM constituents imposes additional costs and introduces further variability owing to their undefined composition, xenogeneic origin, and batch-to-batch heterogeneity. In this regard, the development of synthetic, animal-free matrix alternatives may improve standardization and facilitate scalable high-throughput workflows [191].

From a biological standpoint, and as outlined above, conventional CRC PDOs do not recapitulate TME complexity. This limitation is germane to CRC research, where therapeutic response may be shaped by stromal signaling, immune infiltration, microbiota-associated cues, and the metastatic niche [164, 192, 193]. This is especially consequential for immunotherapy testing, given that a major unmet goal remains extending immune sensitivity to MSS/pMMR tumors by enhancing tumor immunogenicity, reversing immune exclusion, and overcoming TME-mediated resistance [91, 163]. Such questions are difficult to address using epithelial-only PDO systems, whereas more complex TME-preserving or reconstituted organoid systems remain insufficiently standardized for routine clinical implementation.

In conclusion, PDO-based platforms offer a compelling route toward functional precision medicine in CRC. The field now faces a critical transition: moving from proof-of-concept and proof-of-feasibility toward proof-of-utility. Achieving this transition will likely depend less on accumulating additional descriptive evidence and more on rigorously designed prospective studies testing whether organoid-derived information can meaningfully change treatment allocation, spare patients unnecessary toxicity, and ultimately improve clinical outcomes.
