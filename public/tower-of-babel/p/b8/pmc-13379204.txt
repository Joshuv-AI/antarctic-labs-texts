Nontuberculous Mycobacteria in Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia: A National Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Ophir Bar-On, Saharon Less Elazari, Haim Ben Zvi, Karin Yaacoby-Bianu, Idit Raz, Ronen Bar-Yoseph, Michal Gur, Inbal Golan Tripto, Dvir Gatt, Huda Mussaffi, Moshe Heching, Dario Prais, Miri Dotan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe prevalence and clinical significance of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) in individuals with primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) remain unclear, with most data extrapolated from cystic fibrosis. This first national multicenter cohort study evaluating NTM in PCD patients aimed to determine the prevalence, species distribution, and clinical impact of NTM isolation.MethodsA retrospective, longitudinal study was conducted across four tertiary centers in Israel (2010–2024). Patients with confirmed PCD were evaluated for demographic, clinical, microbiological, and longitudinal lung function data. NTM species were classified by growth rate and pathogenicity. Each NTM-positive PCD patient was matched 1:1 to an NTM-negative PCD patient.ResultsOf 231 patients with PCD, 24 (10.4%) had ≥1 positive NTM culture. Most isolates were slow-growing species, predominantly Mycobacterium simiae. Rapid-growing species, mainly Mycobacterium abscessus, were identified in patients with significantly lower forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) at the time of NTM isolation when compared with those harboring slow-growing species (43.5% vs. 82.3%, p = 0.03). Chronic isolation occurred in 25% of NTM-positive individuals. Longitudinal analysis showed no significant association between NTM status and FEV1 decline. Intravenous antibiotic use was not higher in NTM-positive patients and did not increase following NTM isolation. Only one patient fulfilled the criteria for NTM pulmonary disease.ConclusionNTM isolation is not uncommon in PCD patients and is often intermittent, mostly by slow-growing species. NTM status was not associated with accelerated FEV1 decline, although rapid growers occurred predominantly in patients with poorer baseline lung function. Routine annual NTM surveillance appears reasonable to better determine prevalence and monitor outcomes.

Introduction

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a genetically heterogeneous disorder caused by dysfunctional cilia. Clinical manifestations include neonatal respiratory complications and laterality defects, but the hallmark feature is defective mucociliary clearance, leading to impaired airway defense, recurrent infections, and bronchiectasis [1–3].

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are ubiquitous acid-fast bacilli, broadly classified as slow or rapid growers [4]. Although exposure through inhalation or ingestion is common [5], pulmonary disease typically occurs in susceptible hosts with underlying structural lung diseases, such as cystic fibrosis (CF) and PCD [6]. NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is characterized by granulomatous airway inflammation, nodular bronchiectasis, and occasionally fibro-cavitary lesions [7].

Despite the BEAT-PCD (Better Experimental Approach to Treat PCD) consensus recommendations [8] for NTM surveillance at least annually, data on the prevalence and clinical significance of NTM in PCD patients are scarce and are often extrapolated from CF studies [6, 9, 10]. In CF, NTM prevalence reportedly ranges from 6 to 13% and is associated with older age and lung function decline [11–13], but differences in pathophysiology limit generalizability to PCD [14]. Our study aimed to address this knowledge gap by evaluating the prevalence and clinical impact of NTM isolation in PCD.

Methods

This national multicenter, retrospective, longitudinal, observational study included all patients with a confirmed or highly likely diagnosis of PCD followed at four specialized centers in Israel between 2010 and 2024. PCD diagnosis followed the European Respiratory Society (ERS) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines [15, 16], based on clinical criteria, including the screening PrImary CiliAry DyskinesiA Rule (PICADAR) score, nasal nitric oxide, and laboratory investigations (high-speed video microscopy and transmission electron microscopy). Genetic testing was performed in most cases.

Electronic medical records were reviewed to extract demographic and clinical information, including sex, age, respiratory symptoms at birth, laterality defects, congenital structural heart anomalies, chronic cough, chronic nasal symptoms, and recurrent otitis media. Lung function was assessed by spirometry, with forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) as the primary outcome, expressed as a percent predicted using Global Lung Initiative (GLI) reference equations; all tests met ATS standards [17, 18]. Available radiology was reviewed for bronchiectasis, nodules, or cavitary lesions, based on written reports from experienced thoracic radiologists at each center.

Microbiological data were extracted from expectorated sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage cultures. NTM species were classified according to growth rate, as slow- or rapid-growing [4], and according to pathogenic potential, as described for CF [19–21]. During the study period, and across different centers, microbiology reports varied for the Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), occasionally reporting MAC or specific subtype; therefore, our species distribution data reflect the nomenclature used in the original laboratory report.

Infection patterns were classified as “single” if an NTM species was isolated once during the observation period; “intermittent” if positive cultures occurred sporadically with ≥1 year between negative cultures; and “chronic” if recurrent isolation of the same species occurred in consecutive years. “Species replacement” was defined as isolation of different NTM species at subsequent time points. Application of the Leeds criteria [22] for chronic infection with Pseudomonas aeruginosa, commonly used in CF, was not feasible due to insufficient microbiological sampling.

For comparative analyses, PCD patients with ≥1 positive NTM culture were matched 1:1 to PCD controls without any history of NTM isolation. Matching was performed manually within each participating center to minimize center-related differences in surveillance practices and follow-up. Controls were selected based on the closest possible age to the case at the time of NTM isolation and, when possible, matched by sex. If more than one potential control met these criteria, one individual was selected at random. A 1:1 matching ratio was chosen to ensure comparability across participating centers, as several centers had relatively small PCD cohorts and could not reliably provide multiple appropriately matched controls. Matching by genetic variants was not performed due to the relatively small cohort size and the marked genetic heterogeneity of PCD. The primary outcome was NTM prevalence. Secondary outcomes included NTM species distribution, clinical and lung function characteristics of NTM-positive versus NTM-negative patients, and the association between NTM isolation and longitudinal decline in lung function (FEV1%pred).

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean, standard deviation (SD), and percentages. One-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc testing was used to compare lung function across NTM characteristics. Longitudinal lung function was analyzed using linear mixed effects models adjusted for age and repeated measures, to assess the number and proportion of positive cultures over time, with post hoc models examining specific study periods. Analyses were conducted using IBM-SPSS version 27, with a two-tailed significance level of p < 0.05. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the coordinating center (RMC-0453), and all participating sites obtained local ethics approval for data sharing.

Results

Study Population

Between 2010 and 2024, 231 patients with highly likely or confirmed diagnosis of PCD were followed at four participating tertiary centers in Israel. Of these, 24 patients (10.4%) had at least one positive NTM culture during the study period and were classified as NTM-positive. Each NTM-positive PCD patient was matched 1:1 by age and sex (when possible) to an NTM-negative control PCD patient from the same center (Table 1).

The two groups were comparable in neonatal respiratory symptoms, laterality defects, and other clinical manifestations, as well as PCD genetic variants. Chronic P. aeruginosa infection was significantly more frequent among NTM-positive individuals, 37.5% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.035.

Lung Function and Longitudinal Outcomes

To assess the longitudinal impact of NTM isolation on lung function, we applied a linear mixed model adjusting for age and repeated measures. Age-adjusted mean FEV1 was similar between NTM-negative patients (80.1 ± 2.1%) and NTM-positive patients (78.6 ± 2.0%), with a mean difference of 1.45%, p = 0.62, as shown in Figure 1. Age was independently associated with FEV1 (F = 18.1, p < 0.001), whereas NTM status (F = 0.40, p = 0.53) and time points over the 5-year interval (F = 0.36, p = 0.84) were not.

To evaluate the effect of NTM pathogenicity, FEV1%pred was compared between patients with low-moderate pathogenicity NTM species versus moderate-high/high pathogenicity species over 3 years of follow-up. Baseline FEV1 (1 year prior to first NTM isolation) did not differ between groups (85.6 ± 13.7% in the low-moderate group vs. 80.4 ± 16.0% in the moderate-high/high group, p = 0.25), and values remained similar through years 0–2 (all p > 0.45). By year 3, FEV1%pred was lower in the low-moderate group (70.0 ± 16.0%) compared with the moderate-high/high pathogenicity group (87.0 ± 8.7%), though not statistically significant (t = −2.11, p = 0.064).

To assess the effect of NTM growth rate, FEV1%pred was compared between patients with slow- versus rapid-growing NTM species. Slow-growing species included Mycobacterium chimaera, Mycobacterium kansasii, Mycobacterium simiae, Mycobacterium gordonae, Mycobacterium paragordonae, and MAC, comprising M. avium, Mycobacterium intracellulare, and M. chimaera. Rapid-growing species included Mycobacterium abscessus, Mycobacterium fortuitum, and Mycobacterium chelonae. At the time of NTM isolation, FEV1%pred was significantly higher with slow growers (82.3 ± 14.7%) than rapid growers (43.5 ± 23.3%; p = 0.03). Although the difference was not statistically significant at subsequent time points (all p > 0.07), a consistent trend toward lower lung function in patients with rapid growers, predominantly M. abscessus, persisted at years +1 and +2 (shown in Fig. 2). Intravenous (IV) antibiotic use was low and did not differ between NTM-positive and NTM-negative patients, nor before versus after NTM isolation.

Error bars represent standard deviations. FEV1, forced expiratory volume in 1 s; PCD, primary ciliary dyskinesia; NTM, nontuberculous mycobacteria.

NTM-related pulmonary disease, based on NTM-PD criteria, was diagnosed in only one patient (1/24, 4.16%) who had chronic M. intracellulare infection, subsequently complicated by chronic M. abscessus infection, requiring multiple prolonged courses of antibiotic therapy and ultimately lung transplantation. All other NTM-positive patients in our cohort did not progress to fulfill true NTM-PD criteria.

Distribution of NTM Species

Among the 24 NTM-positive patients, the most frequently isolated species was M. simiae (n = 11), followed by M. abscessus (n = 4). Members of the MAC were identified in eight patients, including M. avium (n = 3), M. intracellulare (n = 3), and unspecified MAC (n = 2). Other species included M. chelonae (n = 3), M. gordonae (n = 5), M. fortuitum (n = 2), M. chimaera (n = 1), M. paragordonae (n = 1), and M. kansasii (n = 1) (shown in Fig. 3). The median number of distinct NTM species isolated per patient was 1 (range, 1–7).

When classified by pathogenicity using categories previously described in CF, namely low pathogenicity (M. gordonae, M. paragordonae), moderate pathogenicity (M. simiae, M. kansasii, M. fortuitum), moderate-high pathogenicity (M. chelonae, MAC species), and high pathogenicity (M. abscessus), 58.3% (14/24) of patients harbored at least one moderately pathogenic species, 37.5% (9/24) had moderate-high pathogenic species, 16.7% (4/24) had high-pathogenicity species, and 20.8% (5/24) had low-pathogenicity species. Patients could contribute to more than one category.

Slow-growing NTMs predominated: 21 patients (88%) isolated at least one slow-growing species, while seven (29%) isolated rapid-growing species. Four patients (17%) demonstrated mixed isolation patterns over time; 17 patients (71%) isolated only slow-growing species, and three (13%) only rapid-growing species. Chronic P. aeruginosa infection was more prevalent in NTM-positive than NTM-negative patients (37.5% vs. 8.3%; p = 0.035) but did not differ between patients harboring rapid- versus slow-growing NTM species (42.9% vs. 35.3%; p = 0.73).

Microbiological Course and Persistence

Patterns of NTM isolation varied considerably over the study period. Chronic NTM isolation was observed in six patients (25%), most commonly involving M. intracellulare or M. abscessus. Intermittent isolation occurred in thirteen patients (54%), predominantly with M. simiae or other slow-growing species such as M. gordonae. Species replacement was identified in five patients (21%), typically reflecting a shift from M. simiae to members of the MAC or M. abscessus.

Radiological Findings

Chest CT scans were available for 19 (79%) NTM-positive patients and 22 (92%) NTM-negative control patients. The prevalence of bronchiectasis was similar between groups (84% vs. 86%, respectively, p = 1.00). Pulmonary nodules were infrequent and did not differ significantly between NTM-positive and control patients (5% vs. 14%; p = 0.61). No cavitary lesions were identified in either group.

Surveillance Intensity and Culture Yield

Over the 14-year period, both the number of NTM cultures performed and the number of positive cultures fluctuated, with no consistent annual trend (shown in Fig. 4). Several peaks occurred, most prominently after 2018, when local protocol changes in some centers favored more frequent sampling, reflected by parallel increases in total samples and positive NTM cultures.

Across all years, patients who ever had an NTM-positive culture underwent significantly more repeat NTM cultures than NTM-negative patients (4.7 ± 2.3 vs. 3.1 ± 1.6 cultures per patient, respectively, p = 0.021). Among NTM-positive patients, 78 isolates were identified, representing a mean of 3.3 ± 2.1 positive cultures per patient.

Discussion

In this first national multicenter, age- and sex-matched cohort of PCD patients, NTM was isolated in 10.4% of individuals. This prevalence aligns with the limited data available in the literature, which report rates of approximately 10–15%, ranging from 0% in children up to 29% in adults [2, 23–26], though small sample sizes and inconsistent culture practices limit generalizability. Importantly, many earlier studies obtained very few mycobacterial cultures, likely underestimating true NTM prevalence.

In CF, annual NTM screening is a standard practice [27], and the BEAT-PCD network has similarly recommended at least yearly NTM cultures [8]. Our findings support this approach, demonstrating that increased sampling frequency is associated with higher culture yield.

NTM isolation in our cohort was mostly intermittent and dominated by slow-growing species, particularly M. simiae, differing from CF where M. abscessus and M. avium predominate [11–13]. M. simiae is relatively common in Israel and the Middle East [28], and although often considered of low pathogenicity, it has been associated with clinically significant disease [29, 30].

To evaluate the clinical significance of NTM isolation in PCD patients, we matched NTM-positive and NTM-negative patients for age and sex whenever possible. Our analysis showed that NTM status was not associated with significant differences in FEV1%pred at baseline, nor during longitudinal follow-up. Age was the only independent predictor of lung function, consistent with the natural progression of PCD rather than NTM isolation or specific NTM subtype. These results indicate that in most cases, NTM in PCD represents an incidental colonization rather than a clinically relevant disease. Stable lung function among NTM-positive patients may partly reflect closer clinical follow-up and more frequent antibiotic treatment.

Nevertheless, species-specific patterns were evident; patients harboring rapid-growing, highly pathogenic mycobacteria, mainly M. abscessus, had significantly lower baseline FEV1%pred values at the time of NTM isolation compared to those harboring slow-growing NTM species, suggesting that rapid growers may preferentially infect patients with more advanced baseline structural lung disease. Although this difference was not statistically significant over time, the trend is similar to that observed in CF studies, where M. abscessus isolation is associated with steeper functional decline and poorer outcomes.

Only one individual in our cohort met full diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD [31] and required lung transplantation, indicating that severe disease, while uncommon, can still occur. Notably, this single case occurred within the overall cohort, which predominantly comprised patients with slow-growing, typically less pathogenic species. In contrast, among the patients harboring highly pathogenic rapid growers (predominantly M. abscessus), 1 of 4 developed NTM-PD, consistent with the greater virulence of these organisms. The distinction between baseline PCD clinical features versus symptoms originating from progression to NTM-PD can be challenging due to their overlapping nature; yet, in our patient, once clinical deterioration had occurred, severe systemic manifestations, including weight loss and persistent constitutional symptoms, were evident, as well as deteriorating lung function despite increased physiotherapy and standard antibiotic usage. Despite this severe individual case, overall, NTM isolation in our cohort was not associated with increased i.v. antibiotic requirements, either when comparing NTM-positive with NTM-negative patients, or when evaluating i.v. use before versus after NTM acquisition.

Our findings are consistent with prior work in PCD microbiome research, showing that multiple bacterial species coexist within the PCD airway environment. While Haemophilus influenzae is the most common airway PCD pathogen, isolated in up to 65% of patients [10, 32], P. aeruginosa colonizes roughly one-third, though chronic infection occurs in only a minority [25, 33]. Prior studies have demonstrated that P. aeruginosa colonization is associated with worse baseline FEV1 and more extensive bronchiectasis, but not with accelerated longitudinal FEV1 decline. This suggests it may serve more as a marker of disease severity rather than as a cause for disease progression. In our cohort, chronic P. aeruginosa co-infection was significantly more prevalent among NTM-positive than NTM-negative patients. However, within the NTM-positive group, the prevalence of chronic P. aeruginosa infection did not differ between patients harboring rapid-growing versus slow-growing NTM species. Together, these findings support the concept that underlying airway disease severity may predispose to both P. aeruginosa and NTM acquisition, rather than NTM determining co-infection patterns.

There are several key differences between NTM isolation in PCD versus CF. First, the spectrum of NTM species differs: M. simiae predominated in our PCD cohort, while it is uncommon in CF, where M. abscessus and M. avium are more frequently isolated. Second, the underlying pathophysiology of these two conditions differs, with PCD characterized by impaired ciliary clearance of well-hydrated mucus and relatively preserved epithelial integrity, while CF involves Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Regulator (CFTR) dysfunction leading to dehydrated mucus with secondary ciliary dysfunction along with dysfunctional airway epithelium [34–36]. These differences may alter bacterial adherence, persistence, and host inflammatory responses, potentially explaining why NTM-associated morbidity appears less pronounced in PCD than in CF.

Although the precise clinical significance of NTM in PCD remains to be determined, our findings support routine surveillance cultures at least annually, particularly in older patients, and in those with chronic P. aeruginosa infection. In many cases when NTMs are isolated in PCD patients, careful observation with repeat cultures and radiologic monitoring may be sufficient [21]. A key clinical implication of our study is the potential to avoid overtreatment of isolated NTM cultures, thereby reducing unnecessary exposure to prolonged multidrug regimens. Antimycobacterial therapy should be reserved for individuals who fulfill the ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA criteria for NTM-PD [31].

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design resulted in incomplete data, variable follow-up, and heterogeneity in NTM surveillance. Sampling intensity for NTM cultures varied between centers, across age groups, and over time; because increased testing is associated with higher detection rates, surveillance bias cannot be excluded, which may have influenced prevalence estimates and inferences regarding persistence. Documentation of sample collection type, i.e., spontaneous expectorated sputum, induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage, was not consistently available. The small number of NTM-positive cases restricted organism-specific subgroup analyses and may have led to misclassification of chronic NTM isolation in the setting of non-standardized sampling. In addition, the use of a 1:1 matched design, although chosen to maintain comparable surveillance practices across centers, may have limited statistical power to detect small differences between groups. Lung function assessment relied on spirometry and FEV1, while more sensitive markers such as lung clearance index or imaging may better capture early disease changes [37–39]. Residual confounding by underlying disease severity, including higher rates of P. aeruginosa among NTM-positive patients, may also have influenced outcomes. Nonetheless, given the rarity of NTM in PCD, this represents the largest multicenter study to date examining its prevalence and clinical associations using longitudinal follow-up.

In summary, NTM isolation occurs in approximately 10% of individuals with PCD in Israel, most often as intermittent colonization by slow-growing species such as M. simiae. Despite this relatively high prevalence, no significant association was found between NTM isolation and FEV1 decline, after controlling for age and sex. Rapid-growing species, particularly M. abscessus, were isolated predominantly in patients with lower baseline lung function but did not appear to drive accelerated lung function deterioration. These findings suggest that while NTM is an important member of the PCD airway microbiome, it probably rarely causes progressive disease. Routine surveillance cultures, performed at least annually, are recommended to identify the true prevalence of NTM in PCD patients, and further larger prospective studies are needed to better understand the clinical significance of NTM isolation in these patients.
