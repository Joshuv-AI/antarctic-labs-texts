Genomic selection of soybean (Glycine max) for genetic improvement of yield and seed composition in a breeding context
Authors: Mark J. Miller, Qijian Song, Zenglu Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic selection has been utilized for genetic improvement in both plant and animal breeding and is a favorable technique for quantitative trait development. Within this study, genomic selection was evaluated within a breeding program, using novel validation methods in addition to plant materials and data from a commercial soybean (Glycine max) breeding program. A total of 1501 inbred lines were used to test multiple genomic selection models for multiple traits. Validation included cross‐validation, inter‐environment, and empirical validation. The results indicated that the extended genomic best linear unbiased prediction (EGBLUP) model was the most effective model tested for yield, protein, and oil in cross‐validation with accuracies of 0.50, 0.68, and 0.64, respectively. Increasing marker number from 1000 to 3000 to 6000 single nucleotide polymorphism markers leads to statistically significant increases in accuracy. Cross‐environment predictions were statistically lower than cross‐validation with accuracies of 0.24, 0.54, and 0.42 for yield, protein, and oil, respectively, using the extended genomic BLUP model. Empirical validation, predicting the yield of 510 soybean lines, had a prediction accuracy of 0.34, with the inclusion of a maturity covariate leading to a notable increase in accuracy. Genomic selection identified high‐performance lines in inter‐environment predictions: 34% of lines within the upper quartile of yield, and 51% and 48% of the highest quartile protein and oil lines, respectively. Statistically similar results occurred comparing rankings in empirical validation and selection for advancements in yield trials. These results indicate that genomic selection is a useful tool for selection decisions.

INTRODUCTION

The value of soybean (Glycine max) is determined by multiple quantitative traits, including yield, protein, oil, and amino acid content. The variation of these quantitative traits is typically regulated by a large number of genes with small effects (Bernardo, 2008; Doebley, 2006). Therefore, improvement of quantitative traits using marker‐assisted selection is ineffective (Bernardo, 2008; Melchinger et al., 1998). This is an impediment to breeding efforts, as molecular selection tools allow for an acceleration of the breeding pipeline and an increased rate of genetic gain (Bonnecarrere et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2016). Genomic selection (GS), a genomics‐based selection tool, serves as an alternate to marker‐assisted selection, which is better suited to the prediction of quantitative traits.

GS utilizes genetic and phenotypic data from a known set of plant materials, the training set (TS), to estimate the phenotypic values of future plant materials, the prediction set (PS), based on their genetics. These associations are then used to generate a genomic estimated breeding value (GEBV) for each individual in the PS (Heffner et al., 2009; Meuwissen et al., 2001; Whittaker et al., 2000). GS methodologies commonly assume that the trait of interest is controlled by many genes with small effects. The assumption of quantitative genetic control allows GS models to predict complex, highly quantitative traits (Lorenz et al., 2011; Melchinger et al., 1998; Meuwissen et al., 2001).

The accuracy of GS is heavily determined by TS formation, specifically the genetic relatedness of the TS to the PS. The greatest GS accuracy has been observed in GS when the TS is large and highly related to the PS, leveraging the similarities of linkage disequilibrium within both populations (Norman et al., 2018). GS relies on the relation between genotypic data and phenotypic observations to accurately assess marker effects; therefore, predictions are more accurate if the TS and PS are genetically similar (Lorenz & Nice, 2017; Lorenz et al., 2011; Meuwissen et al., 2001; Stewart‐Brown et al., 2019). Zhu et al. (2021) found that a TS consisting of only full siblings of the PS could achieve significant prediction accuracies for soybean yield, between 0.5 and 0.7, even at a minimal TS size of 60 soybean genotypes. Addition of individuals genetically distant from the PS to the TS, by contrast, can lead to a reduction of TS accuracy as was shown in Lorenz and Smith (2015). In this experiment, the TS was tested at various sizes; prediction accuracy increased as related individuals were added, eventually reaching a plateau, then falling as unrelated genotypes were added to the TS. The size of the TS needed to reach a predictive accuracy plateau and the value of the said plateau depend on how related the added materials are to the PS (Stewart‐Brown et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2021). Additionally, when used over multiple breeding cycles, the specific TS of a breeding program will need to be updated periodically to account for changes in linkage disequilibrium within the PS (Gorjanc et al., 2018; Stewart‐Brown et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2009).

The amount of GS research specific to soybean is limited, with minimal utilization of the technique within public breeding programs. Within plant breeding, GS has been thoroughly researched in grains such as wheat, maize, and barley with soybean‐specific GS research limited in comparison (Stewart‐Brown et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2021). Studies on soybean GS within the literature primarily focused on GS with the TS drawn from diversity panels or small number of bi‐parental families. One of the larger studies of soybean yield GS utilized the SoyNAM population, a set of 40 populations which all share one common parent. The non‐common parents in the populations were lines identified to be high yielding in the region of the study, defined as a latitudinal of ca. 37°C to 40°C N (Diers et al., 2018). Using this population, Xavier et al. (2016b) examined multiple GS models via cross selection, finding a mean yield prediction accuracy of 0.41 over two field seasons when using the most effective model in the study (Diers et al., 2018; Xavier et al., 2016b). A later study of soybean yield GS using the SoyNAM population confirmed these results, with prediction accuracies between 0.2 and 0.36 using genomic selection models that accounted for genetic and environmental effects (Widener et al., 2021). These findings from the SoyNAM population indicate that soybean genomic selection can be a useful tool, and the results should be validated within a breeding program context (Ma et al., 2016; Xavier et al., 2016b).

Existing studies of soybean GS indicate that the technique has a strong predictive ability for yield and seed composition traits, which are the focus of soybean breeding programs. The primary utility of GS is for the prediction of yield, as seed composition traits can be measured via near infrared spectrometry with a high accuracy. While selecting for yield, GS for seed composition traits using the same sets of genetic data can be performed without additional resources (Duhnen et al., 2017; Stewart‐Brown et al., 2019; Xavier et al., 2016a). Stewart‐Brown et al. (2019) researched the predictive ability of RR‐BLUP for yield, protein, and oil utilizing multiple TS compositions. In all instances, the larger TS, consisting of 400 recombinant inbred lines, led to a significantly greater predictive ability for the three traits evaluated: yield, protein, and oil content. Smallwood et al. (2019) investigated the predictive ability of multiple genomic selection models for multiple agronomic traits in soybean using a single bi‐parental population evaluated at 2 years. Cross‐validation indicated a high predictive ability of 0.51, even for low heritability traits such as yield, though the prediction accuracy fell to 0.13 when predicting performance of the same bi‐parental populations in a different environment.

This study aims to fully investigate the potential of GS for yield and multiple seed composition traits within a soybean breeding program, using a set of plant materials developed and utilized for cultivar development. Multiple genomic selection models were evaluated via multiple evaluation methods with a large, multi‐year TS to predict yield, protein, oil, and amino acid content.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

Plant materials in this study were developed and evaluated within the University of Georgia soybean breeding program's yield trials. Maturity groups ranged from VI through VIII with all materials derived from improved breeding material and intended for commercial release in the Southern United States (Boerma et al., 2012, 2016; Li et al., 2020). Two sets of plant materials were used in this study: a core TS utilizing data from 2015 to 2021 multi‐location yield trials and a second set utilized for empirical validation.

The first set of plant materials, the core TS, consisted of 991 inbred lines evaluated in the University of Georgia advanced yield trials between 2015 and 2021. These plant materials served as the core TS within this research and primarily consisted of F5‐derived recombinant inbred lines (RILs). The advanced yield trials (AYTs) were conducted at three locations with three replicates per location within the southern growing region. Each plot consisted of four rows with a 4.9‐m row length and 76.2‐cm row spacing with a density of 27 seeds/m. All plots were end‐trimmed to a length of 3.7 m before harvest with only the middle two rows harvested for yield to reduce edge effects. Each trial followed a randomized complete block design and included two checks with similar maturity.

The second set of 510, primarily F5‐derived RILs, termed the empirical validation materials that were separate from those in the core TS, was utilized for the empirical evaluation of genomic selection. These materials were drawn from material identified for advancement based on visual selection within 2020 plant row experiments and then evaluated for yield and other agronomic traits in the 2021 University of Georgia preliminary yield trials (PYTs). These trials were grown at two locations with two replicates per location. Each plot consisted of two rows with a 4.9‐m row length and 76.2‐cm row spacing with a density of 27 seeds/m. Similar to the first set of materials, all plots were end‐trimmed to a length of 3.7 m prior to harvest to reduce edge effects.

Genotyping

DNA extraction was accomplished using two separate techniques. All plant materials evaluated from 2015 to 2019, a majority of the core TS, utilized a modified CTAB, chloroform method detailed in Keim (1988) for DNA extraction. Bulked, lyophilized leaf tissues were collected from ∼15 plants of each genotype. DNA from plant materials evaluated in 2020 and 2021, part of the core TS and the empirical validation materials, was extracted utilizing ground seed tissue bulked from ∼40 soybean seeds in a 96‐well plate format. The samples were randomly selected to check DNA quality and quantity via gel electrophoresis and fluorimeter DNA quantification with a minimum desirable concentration of 100 μg/mL.

The SoySNP6k iSelect BeadChip array containing 6000 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) was utilized for all materials in the core TS. The chip was developed using ∼18,000 accessions in the USDA Soybean Germplasm Collection (Song et al., 2020). Following genotyping, SNP alleles were determined using the GenomeStudio software (Illumina) with manual corrections based on visualization. The SNP markers were coded as 2, 1, or 0, corresponding to the number of alleles from the nucleotide variant first in alphabetical order for the said marker. SNP markers within our data set with a minor allele frequency of <0.06 and/or >20% missing data were removed from analysis, leaving 3900 polymorphic markers for further analysis. Missing SNP marker alleles were imputed using the Beagle software (Browning et al., 2018).

The SoySNP3k iSelect BeadChip array (unpublished data) was used to genotype the empirical validation materials. The SoySNP3k markers consist of 3000 SNPs that are a subset of those from the SoySNP6k marker set. SNP allele calls, imputation, and data filtration were accomplished with the SoySNP3k data using the same programs and parameters as in the SoySNP6k data, leaving 1981 polymorphic markers for further analysis.

A final subset of markers was the SoySNP1k marker set, which consisted of 1245 SNPs, a subset of those included in the SoySNP6k iSelect Beadchip. While the former two marker sets are available with an associated Illiumina genotyping platform, a platform specific to SoySNP1k is not yet publicly available. This subset of markers was utilized in validation, but genotyping was not carried out using this array on any plant materials. Marker reads for this assay were drawn from the SoySNP6k genotyping results for the core TS and from the SoySNP3k genotyping results for the empirical validation materials. This represented the lowest marker density utilized in the study, with 1098 markers remaining after data filtration under the parameters described above.

Phenotypes and phenotypic data analysis

All plant materials within this study were phenotyped for multiple traits including yield, plant height, lodging, maturity, protein, oil, cysteine (Cys), and methionine (Met) contents. Yield was calculated based on the weight as measured by the combine during harvest and adjusted to 13% moisture. Maturity was recorded as the days from planting to the date when R8 development was observed in 95% of the plants in a plot (Fehr et al., 1971). Protein, oil, Cys, and Met contents were measured on approximately 200 whole seeds of each RIL using the Perten DA 7250 analyzer (PerkinElmer Inc.). The NIR spectroscopy was calibrated by the manufacturer using thousands of samples with known seed composition. Seed composition was reported on a dry matter basis for all reps and locations. Both Met and Cys contents were normalized to protein content for analysis by dividing the measured content of each amino acid by the total measured protein content for each sample, with the final value represented as a percentage of protein content. Furthermore, the sum of Cys and Met was reported as a separate trait for analysis.

Best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) values for each trait were calculated via mixed models, which accounted for relevant effects such as location, year, maturity, and genotype effects. For yield, the following model was used: y=μ+E+M+G+EG, where y denotes the phenotype of interest; M is maturity measured in number of days from planting; E is the environmental effect, defined as a location × year combination; G is the genotypic effect; and E(G) is the genotype by environment interaction. G and E(G) were treated as random effects, while all other factors were treated as fixed effects. For all other traits, BLUPs were calculated using a model of the form: y=μ+E+G+EG.

Histograms and residual plots were used to check for outliers in the raw phenotypic data, as well as the BLUP values, with any severe outliers removed. Calculation was performed using the lme4 package in R (Bates et al., 2015; R Core Team, 2020).

The broad‐sense heritability for all traits (H 2) was calculated for plant materials in the core TS. The lines within the AYTs that make up the core TS differ every year, leading to calculation of heritability on a yearly basis. H 2 was determined on an entry mean basis for each trait using variance components from the calculation of BLUP values in following equation: H2=σ2G/σ2G+σ2G×Ee+σ2Rer, where σ2G, σ2G×E, and σ2R are the variance of genotypes, the genotype by environment interactions, and residuals, respectively; e is the number of environments for the test; and r is the number of replications.

Genomic prediction models

Four GS models were used within this study. Three of these four models—RR‐BLUP (Whittaker et al., 2000), Bayesian B (BayesB; Meuwissen et al., 2001), and Bayesian ridge regression (BayesRR; Pérez et al., 2010)—have been thoroughly studied within GS literature. BayesB and RR‐BLUP were among the first proposed genomic prediction models and have remained effective in current research on genomic selection, in multiple studies in a variety of plant species (Heslot et al., 2012; Lorenz et al., 2011; Meuwissen et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2018; Whittaker et al., 2000). BayesRR is an alternate Bayesian method that utilizes a shrinkage factor similar to RR‐BLUP and has been used previously in GS studies on soybean grain yield (Duhnen et al., 2017; Pérez et al., 2010). RR‐BLUP was executed using the RR‐BLUP package in R (Endelman, 2011), and BayesB and BayesRR were performed using the bWGR package in R (R Core Team, 2020; Xavier et al., 2020).

The additional model assessed in this study was extended genomic BLUP (EGBLUP). This model adds an additional matrix of marker effects that estimate additive by additive epistatic effects. This model has been previously utilized in animal breeding research, with limited use in soybean and wheat (Duhnen et al., 2017; Raffo et al., 2022; Su et al., 2012). The overall equation is of the form: y=Xβ+Zaua+Zquq+e, where Za is an n × n genetic relationship matrix, ua is an n × 1 vector of additive genetic effects, uq is an n × 1 vector of additive by additive epistatic effects, and Zq is an n × n modified kinship matrix intended to estimate the effect of additive by additive epistasis on the trait of interest. Zq is a Hadamard product of Za and Za; therefore, each value of Zq is the square of the value at the same coordinate of Za (Henderson, 1985; Jarquin et al., 2014; Su et al., 2012). The rationale and derivation of Zq is well detailed by Jarquin et al. (2014). All calculations for this model were performed using the EMMREML R package (Akdemir & Godfrey, 2015).

In addition to the four models presented here, each model was also tested with and without a maturity covariate when predicting yield. Prior research has demonstrated that the inclusion of correlated traits as covariates in GS can lead to greatly improved prediction accuracies, especially when predicting in unknown environments and can serve as a parameter to evaluate genotype by environment effects (Jarquin et al., 2020; Michel et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2019). Furthermore, maturity is highly heritable, correlated with yield, and often evaluated early in the breeding pipeline for all candidate RILs (Ortel et al., 2020). Therefore, maturity data could be included as a covariate in yield genomic prediction within a standard breeding pipeline. The modified formula is of the form: GEBVf,i=GEBVi+miτ, where GEBV f,i represents the final genetically estimated breeding value of individual i, mi indicates the days to maturity calculated for that same individual, and τ is the effect of maturity as calculated when generating BLUP values for yield.

Validation

All models were assessed in a series of validations for yield and seed composition traits including protein, oil, and Cys and Met contents. These three forms of validation—cross, experimental, and empirical—were chosen as they differ in how closely they resemble the conditions GS would be used under. Cross‐validation is one of the more common forms of validation, forming the TS and PS randomly from all plant materials. The latter two methods, experimental and empirical validation, define their TS and PS more strictly by evaluating plant materials in specific groupings determined by year, in the case of experimental validation, or a singular set of advancement candidates in a breeding program, as in empirical validation. Cross‐validations randomly select TS and PS are therefore the least similar to a breeding scenario, while experimental validation more resembles a breeding scenario.

Cross‐validation

All combinations of trait, model, and marker density were assessed via 50 instances of fivefold cross‐validation utilizing plant materials from the core TS. Prediction accuracy was equal to the Pearson correlation between the true phenotypic values and the predicted values for the masked portion of the TS. This method is the standard means of prediction accuracy validation for research relating to genomic selection (Islam et al., 2020; Smallwood et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018). A Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used to determine the statistical differences between model and marker density combinations for a given trait.

Experimental validation

Under this validation method traits, models, and marker, densities were assessed in conditions more similar to those of a breeding program using the core TS. All plant materials evaluated during the same year are used as the PS, with their phenotypic values hidden from the model. All other plant materials were used as a TS. For example, 169 lines were evaluated in the 2021 AYTs. The PS therefore would consist of those 169 RILs, and the TS would consist of the 822 remaining lines evaluated from 2015 to 2020. PS sizes ranged from 133 to 186 RILs across experimental validation. This process was carried out for every year of the evaluation within the core TS starting with 2015 and continuing until 2021. The reduced relatedness of the TS to PS will likely reduce the prediction accuracy of experimental validation compared to cross‐validation (Jarquin et al., 2020; Lorenz & Smith, 2015; Zhu et al., 2021). Mean accuracy across all years was used to determine the prediction accuracy. In addition, within each year, the 25% highest performing lines were identified on the basis of their BLUP values, calculated using field phenotypes, for a given trait with the same done for the 25% lowest performing lines. When predicting each year, the proportion of the 25% highest performing lines by phenotypic evaluation ranked among the 25th percentile by GS was recorded. The same was repeated for the 25% lowest performing lines. Statistical differences between model and marker density combinations were evaluated using a Tukey HSD test.

Empirical validation

Empirical validation was a practical application of genomic selection within a plant breeding program. Within this validation method, the core TS acted as the TS, and the empirical validation materials acted as the PS. Only markers from the SoySNP3k marker set, which were used to genotype the empirical validation materials, and the SoySNP1k marker set, using marker data gathered from SoySNP6k genotyping for the core training set and SoySNP3k for the empirical validation materials, were used. This is the largest scale validation of those used in this study with a TS and PS of 991 and 510 genotypes, respectively. Yield was the only trait predicted through this method using models that included or excluded maturity covariates. As the contents of the PS and the TS were fixed, this validation method featured no randomization or replication for RR‐BLUP or EGBLUP. For each of these models, validation was performed twice, with and without maturity covariates. The probabilistic nature of Bayesian genomic prediction models meant that when evaluating the same PS, using the same TS, the values predicted by a Bayesian model will differ each time when a test is run (Beaumont & Rannala, 2004; Heslot et al., 2012). Therefore, validation was performed 30 times for BayesB and BayesRR with or without a maturity covariate, respectively. Prediction accuracy was determined as the correlation between the GEBVs determined by the GS models and the results of the 2021 PYT for the 510 inbred lines. After determining the most effective GS model for these lines, all lines within the extended validation materials were ranked according to their GEBVs as estimated by the said GS model. Of these 510 lines, a subset was selected for advancement to AYTs. The selection decision was made independent of this study's evaluations. The GEBV ranking of these lines, determined via empirical evaluation, was assessed to determine if the lines selected for advancement were predominantly among those highly ranked by GS or if there was no correlation between advancement and empirical validation ranking.

RESULTS

Heritability

The broad sense heritability was calculated on an entry mean basis yearly for all traits as the genotypes included within each year of validation differed, with the exception of the check varieties used. For yield, the heritability ranged from 0.11 to 0.47 on a yearly basis, with a mean value of 0.29 and all seed composition traits had considerably higher heritability by mean and on a year by year basis relative to yield. Protein and oil content had an average broad sense heritability of 0.82 and 0.87, respectively, which was greater than the amino acids included in the study. Cys and Met had mean heritabilities of 0.69 and 0.67 individually and 0.71 when combined together. Broad sense heritability did not fall below 0.5 during any year for the seed composition traits (protein, oil, Met, and Cys) included in this study (Table S1).

Cross‐validation

Cross‐validation is the standard method used to assess the accuracy of genomic selection. Of the four models tested, EGBLUP gave the highest prediction accuracies for each of the traits investigated when using the SoySNP6k marker set: yield (0.50), protein (0.68), oil (0.64), Cys (0.49), Met (0.60), and Cys + Met (0.55) (Table 1). According to a Tukey HSD analysis (alpha = 0.05), EGBLUP had a statistically higher prediction accuracy than other models tested in this study. In regard to the relative accuracy among traits, greater predictive accuracies were observed in traits with greater heritability, which is consistent with understanding of GS. The only exception to this trend was Cys, which had a mean prediction accuracy near equal to yield, both with and without a maturity covariate (Figure 1).

Marker density also had a noted impact on predictive accuracy. For all models and seed composition traits, an increase in the number of SNP markers used improved prediction accuracy, though not always to a statistically significant degree. For EGBLUP, the accuracies for yield were 0.5, 0.49, and 0.48 utilizing the SoySNP6k, SoySNP3k, and SoySNP1k marker sets, respectively. In a paired t‐test comparison of the yield, prediction accuracies using these marker sets all were found to be statistically different from one another (p < 0.001). For example, the prediction accuracy of protein and oil, 0.67 and 0.62, respectively, via EGBLUP using the SoySNP3k marker did not statistically differ from the prediction accuracies found using the soySNP1k marker set. The SoySNP6k marker set did lead to a statistically increased prediction accuracy over the SoySNP3k marker set for protein and oil, with prediction accuracies of 0.68 and 0.64, respectively. For the amino acid seed composition traits, increased marker densities generally lead to increased prediction accuracies, though marker sets more often came to statistically similar results when using EGBLUP. For Met the SoySNP3k marker set (0.58) had statistically similar results to the SoySNP1k (0.57) marker set. Cys prediction accuracies were statistically similar between the SoySNP6k (0.49) and SoySNP3k (0.48) marker sets with both having an accuracy statistically above that found when using the SoySNP1k marker set (0.47). For the prediction of Cys and Met combined, the relationship between marker sets was identical to that observed in yield cross‐validation. Within cross‐validation, the addition of a maturity covariate to yield predictions, leading to a prediction accuracy of 0.49 for EGBLUP 6k, did not lead to a statistically significant increase in mean prediction accuracy over yield predictions without an included maturity covariate, which was 0.5 for EGBLUP 6k (Table 1).

Experimental validation

Experimental validation was performed for each year from 2015 to 2021 for all traits and marker sets (Figure 2). As in cross‐validation, increasing the number of markers raised prediction accuracy, though these increases were not found to be statistically significant. Yield with a maturity covariate had a mean prediction accuracy of 0.24 when utilizing EGBLUP using the SoySNP6k marker set. The addition of the maturity covariate led to a ∼25% increase in prediction accuracy for yield, which increased from 0.19 to 0.24. Oil and Cys were best predicted by BayesB when using the SoySNP6k marker set with a prediction accuracy of 0.43 and 0.21. All other seed composition traits (protein, Met, and Cys + Met) were predicted best by EGBLUP when using the soySNP6k marker set with mean prediction accuracies of 0.54, 0.37, and 0.31, respectively. The SoySNP3k marker set accuracies using EGBLUP were 0.23, 0.53, 0.4, 0.18, 0.35, and 0.29 for yield with a maturity covariate, protein, oil, Cys, Met, and Cys + Met, respectively. Prediction accuracies when using the SoySNP1k marker set with EGBLUP were slightly decreased for all traits with yield using a maturity covariate, oil, Cys, Met, and Cys + Met having prediction accuracies of 0.19, 0.39, 0.16, 0.33, and 0.26, respectively. Protein did not see a decrease in accuracy moving from SoySNP3k to SoySNP1k. For all GS models and traits tested, except Cys, the prediction accuracies for cross‐validation were statistically above those of experimental validation. For Cys, there was not a statistically significant difference in the results between experimental and cross‐validation (Table 2).

An analysis of variance was carried out for all traits to determine the effects of year, the GS model, marker set, and the interaction between GS models with the marker set in relation to prediction accuracy. For yield, all three primary factors (model, markers, and year) were found to be statistically significant, while the interaction between marker set and the GS model was not statistically significant. Of these significant factors, year had the greatest absolute difference in effects, ranging from 0.21 to 0.56, relative to model, whose factor coefficients all were of ∼0.04 in magnitude, and markers whose factor coefficients were 0.05 and 0.03 under SoySNP3k and SoySNP6k, respectively. However, when including a maturity covariate for yield predictions, the GS model was no longer a significant factor. The same was found for protein and oil experimental validation, with marker set and year of evaluation as the two significant factors, with larger coefficients found for year as a factor relative to marker set. All three primary factors were significant in the prediction of Met, with the same relative magnitude of coefficients among factors seen in yield, with year having the greatest magnitude, followed by markers and model. For predictions of Cys, only year of evaluation and marker set were significant factors, with the same relative ranking of coefficient effects among factors as observed in protein and oil. An analysis of variance of Cys + Met found that the year of evaluation was the only significant factor in GS.

Empirical validation

The final form of evaluation was empirical validation. Under this form of validation, 510 plant rows selected for advancement to PYTs, replicated trials in two locations, were genotyped using the SoySNP3k marker set. These 510 RILs were then assessed via GS to determine their GEBVs, which were later compared to yield collected in the replicated yield trials. Unlike in the other validation tests, there was no variance in the TS or PS as the 510 lines in the empirical validation materials were the PS, and all other plant materials in this study were the training set. In all instances, the standard deviations of prediction accuracy for either Bayesian model were less than or equal to 0.01 over the 30 replications. The highest yield prediction accuracy was achieved using EGBLUP with the SoySNP3k marker set, 0.34 with a maturity covariate and 0.19 without a maturity covariate. When utilizing maturity as a covariate, accuracies rose for all models, with an increase from 0.22 to 0.28 for BayesB, 0.15 to 0.32 for BayesRR, and 0.15 to 0.3 for RR‐BLUP when using the SoySNP3k marker set (Table 3). The use of a smaller marker set, SoySNP1k, and a maturity covariate led to a reduction in prediction accuracy to 0.24 for BayesB, 0.28 for BayesRR, 0.27 for EGBLUP, and 0.26 for RR‐BLUP.

Prediction of high and low performance genotypes

GS's ability to identify genotypes with high and low trait values was tested for all traits in both experimental and empirical validations. In experimental validation, each combination of GS model and marker set was tested for how well it could identify the 25% of lines with the highest and lowest trait values for each trait. When predicting yield with maturity as a covariate, EGBLUP correctly identified 34% of lines within the 25th percentile and 40% of lines below the 75th percentile. In identification of the upper and lower quartiles, accuracy was statistically above 0.25 for all marker densities (p < 0.01). Similar results were found for the identification of high and low performance lines for seed composition traits as well. Marker set, however, did not have a statistically significant effect, with results when using the SoySNP6k marker set not statistically differing from those found when using the SoySNP3k or SoySNP1k marker set (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

The use of multiple forms of validation and marker sets led to notable differences in relative and absolute predictive accuracy among the genomic prediction models. The results of this research should better inform breeders on the capabilities of genomic selection in a breeding context and in what situations it should be deployed.

Comparison of validation methods

The findings of this study indicate that the prediction accuracies found via cross‐validation for yield and the other traits included in this study are statistically different from those that GS would achieve in a plant breeding context. In cross‐validation, both the TS and PS are selected at random; therefore, lines from the same pedigree could be within both the TS and the PS by chance, resulting in a higher level of relatedness between the two sets of plant materials and an increased GS accuracy. In contrast, experimental validation treats each AYT as a PS, one at a time. Therefore, relatedness is minimized between the TS and PS. Within this study, an average of 90% of RILs within the PS were full siblings of one or more lines in the TS during cross‐validation. This is a statistically greater level of relatedness than in experimental validation, where on average 13% of RILs in the PS were full sibling of materials in the TS. The impact of relatedness between the TS and VS on prediction accuracy is well documented and can have a greater effect than TS size and model choice (Lorenz & Smith, 2015; Smallwood et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2021). In assessments of whether to utilize yield GS in a breeding program, validation methods that predict across environments and populations are therefore preferable. These validations more closely resemble a breeding context, whereas cross‐validation can lead to a higher level of relatedness between the TS and PS than would be observed in a breeding scenario.

Among the GS models evaluated in this study, EGBLUP had the statistically highest prediction accuracies. In cross‐validation, for all traits, EGBLUP gave the greatest prediction accuracy, exceeding all other models in this study at all marker sets. EGBLUP most closely resembles GBLUP but utilizes an additional matrix representing additive by additive epistatic effects, suggesting that the additive by additive epistatic effects for the traits in this study are non‐negligible. The findings are similar to those of Duhnen et al. (2017) where EGBLUP was used to predict soybean seed yield and seed protein content, providing greater prediction accuracies for said traits compared to other models such as RR‐BLUP.

Furthermore, the addition of maturity as a covariate had varying effects on prediction of yield using GS. Within cross‐validation, the prediction of yield with or without a maturity covariate was statistically similar. For both empirical and experimental validations, however, the use of a covariate led to increases in prediction accuracy, compared to equivalent models that did not include a maturity covariate. These latter validation methods, experimental and empirical validation, predict the GEBVs of a VS evaluated in environments where none of the materials in the TS have been tested. The maturity covariate therefore provides an additional yield correlated parameter for the GS model to assess environment and genotype by environment effects leading to more accurate predictions. The maturity covariate is unimpactful in cross‐validation as the TS will contain closely related materials to the PS, which were tested in the same conditions as the PS and are thus subject to similar genotype by environment effects as it is a highly similar genotype being tested in the same environment.

Prediction of high and low performance genotypes

The primary goals of GS within the soybean pipeline are to identify high performing lines for advancement or identify poorly performing inbred lines for removal. Thus, another means of evaluating GS was what proportion of high and low performing lines could be identified within experimental validation. For yield, the mean proportion of high and low performing lines identified was 0.34 and 0.41, respectively. In both instances, this level of identification was statistically above 0.25, which would occur if lines were selected at random. Between the identification of high‐performance and low‐performance plant materials, identification of low performance materials, 0.41, was statistically above that of high‐performance materials, 0.34. Therefore, GS is better suited to identifying poorly performing plant materials relative to high performing ones and thus would be best utilized to remove lines during the plant row stage, though it will still provide an advantage in the selection of superior candidates for advancement.

An additional validation for GS was empirical validation. Under this validation type, 510 plant rows, the empirical validation materials, were ranked according to yield GEBVs as determined via GS using the core TS as the TS. In comparing these GEBVs to results of later replicated yield trials, the prediction accuracy of EGBLUP model using the SoySNP6k marker panel and maturity covariate was 0.34. Of these 510 RILs, 72 were selected for advancement into AYTs. Of these 72, 35% were within the 25th percentile identified by the EBGLUP model using the SoySNP6k marker set, a value within the α = 0.05 confidence interval found in experimental validation's identification of high yield plant materials using the same GS parameters. A confidence interval of the same GS model in cross‐validation ranged from 0.49 to 0.5, indicating the mean accuracy achieved by EGBLUP using a maturity covariate and the SoySNP6k marker set via cross‐validation differed significantly from that found via empirical validation. As the results found in experimental validation are statistically similar to those found in empirical validation, experimental validation provides a better estimation of the mean GS prediction accuracy for yield than cross‐validation does. Furthermore, as EGBLUP SoySNP6k ranked 60% of the 72 lines selected in the upper half of yield GEBVs among the 510 lines in the empirical validation materials, it can act as a valuable tool in advancement decisions prior to large scale, replicated yield trials and could possibly replace some of preliminary yield trials in breeding programs, reducing overall breeding cycle time (Watson et al., 2019).

Impact of marker sets and genomic selection model on prediction accuracy

Within this study, three marker sets—SoySNP6k, SoySNP3k, and SoySNP1k—were evaluated, and each differed in their marker number consisting of 6k, 3k, and 1k markers, respectively. The prior two of marker sets, SoySNP6k and SoySNP3k, are available on an Illumina SNP array‐based platform for genotyping in soybean research (Song et al., 2013, 2020). The SoySNP1k Illumina array utilizes a subset of 1250 markers from the SoySNP3k marker set but is currently not publicly available. The lower density marker set was created to reduce the costs associated with genotyping, as they allow more samples to be genotyped with the same amount of resources. The number of breeding lines that will need to be genotyped could number in hundreds or thousands, even after initial phenotypic evaluation by a breeder. Therefore, it is of great concern to determine what the minimal required markers are to achieve sufficiently reliable estimations of yield in GS.

Overall marker set does have a statistical impact on prediction accuracy in cross‐validation, though the relative change in accuracy is minimal. For example, in cross‐validation of yield, there was a reduction in mean prediction accuracy moving from the 6k marker set (0.5) to the 1k marker set (0.47) when using EGBLUP or a reduction to 0.49 if using 3k marker set, and each of these results were statistically different from one another. By contrast, in experimental validation, the reduction of marker density did not result in a statistically significant reduction in prediction accuracy, nor was the ability to identify low and high yield plant materials affected in a statistically significant way by the marker set used. The prediction accuracy of 3k in empirical validation (0.34) using an EGBLUP with a maturity covariate was 25% higher than that of the same model using the 1k marker set (0.27). Additionally, empirical validation using the 3k marker set identified more lines selected for advancement in the upper quartile (35%) compared to the 1k marker set (26%).

In consideration of the all validation results, the SoySNP3k marker set appears the most preferable, maintaining desirable levels of accuracy at a reduced capital cost. As found through empirical validation, the use of the SoySNP3k marker set leads to notably higher prediction accuracy than the SoySNP1k marker set in breeding conditions. Therefore, the use of the 3k marker set is preferable over the 1k marker set. The accuracy of the SoySNP3k marker set was only slightly below that of the SoySNP6k marker set in cross and experimental validation, and these differences were significant only in cross‐validation. Therefore, the use of the 3k marker set over the 6k marker set is preferable as the reduced genotyping costs from using 3k will allow for more plant materials to be genotyped within soybean breeding programs, with only a small, though statistically significant reduction in prediction accuracy. In some instances, this decrease in accuracy due to reduced marker density could be overcome by the increased TS size made possible by a lower cost marker panel, though this will depend on the relative costs of marker assays and the genetic composition of the TS (Zhu et al., 2021).

One of the limitations of this study was the amount of TS and PS compositions possible for experimental and empirical validation. One of the most beneficial aspects of cross‐validation is that it allows a set of plant materials to be randomized into a practically unlimited number of TS and PS combinations for testing. By contrast, the TS and PS configurations possible in experimental validation are limited by the number of field seasons covered in the study, as each one will act as one PS. Empirical validation can only be carried out once for each set of plant materials evaluated for advancement. The limited amount of TS and PS configuration that could be tested in empirical and experimental validation made statistical evaluation and comparison difficult, but was sufficient to evaluate GS parameters and determine which factors impacted prediction accuracy.

Deployment of GS in soybean breeding

Within a breeding program, GS will exist as a selection component, augmenting currently existing techniques and methodologies. The most useful place for the deployment of GS would be the evaluation and selection of plant rows or single plants which will be used for growing plant rows. After a breeder visually selects the plants or inspects the plant rows, GS could then be used to evaluate the selected plant materials and aid the breeder in making an advancement selection decision. The plant breeder's visual inspection removes materials that are undesirable on the basis of visual traits and reduces the number of plant materials that will need to be genotyped, conserving resources and ensuring genotyping can be completed quickly enough for GS to be included in advancement decisions. The candidates for genomic selection will need to be genotyped using a high throughput workflow to ensure genetic data are available in a timely manner such that GS can be used prior to planning for the next season. The model for GS would be an EGBLUP GS model with a maturity covariate using the 3k marker set in the presence of resource constraints. The yield and seed composition GEBVs generated by GS would be considered along with markers for other significant traits such as soybean root‐knot or cyst nematode resistance in making advancement decisions. This advancement decision could be made on the basis of a set of minimum parameters for seed composition and resistance traits and suitably high yield GEBVs, or with a suitably weighted index considering multiple traits (Lenz et al., 2020; Rapp et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2018).

One of the novel aspects of this study was its use of plant materials and validation conditions that would exist in a soybean cultivar development program. The SoyNAM population is one of the more thoroughly documented and researched soybean populations in regard to soybean GS (Howard & Jarquin, 2019; Persa et al., 2020; Xavier et al., 2016b). Persa et al. (2020) utilized the SoyNAM population to evaluate a GS model that included components to account for genotype by environment and family by environment interactions with genomic BLUP marker evaluation (Endelman, 2011; Persa et al., 2020). Validation consisted of cross‐validation using the SoySNP6k marker panel. When utilizing a model which accounted for genetic, environmental, and genotype by environment effects, an accuracy of 0.46 was achieved. Cross‐validation using EGBLUP with the SoySNP6k marker set on breeding program materials achieved a prediction accuracy of 0.5, with an associated confidence interval wholly above 0.46. Thus, the GS method investigated in this study exceeded the prediction accuracy found in the SoyNAM population in a comparable study of soybean grain yield (Persa et al., 2020).

CONCLUSION

Within this experiment, GS was evaluated under various contexts to determine which factors impacted GS prediction accuracy and which GS models were preferable for yield and seed composition traits. Across all traits evaluated, EGBLUP gave the best prediction among the GS models tested in cross‐validation and equaled or exceeded the other models tested for experimental validation. The estimations of prediction accuracies provided by each validation method differed to a statistically significant degree between cross and experimental validation for yield with the choice of GS model and marker density significantly impacting prediction accuracy to a statistically significant degree. Increasing the marker number led to statistically significant, but minor (∼0.02 from SoySNP1k to SoySNP6k) increases in prediction accuracy in cross‐validation. GS can be deployed following visual and phenotypic evaluation of the breeder at the plant row stage in order to reduce the number of lines. The EGBLUP model with a maturity covariate is most preferable for the prediction of yield, while the EGBLUP model without a maturity covariate is preferable for all other seed composition traits included in this study (protein, oil, Cys, Met, Cys + Met). In regard to genotyping, the SoySNP3k marker set is preferable as it allows for a more efficient use of resources for genotyping in comparison to SoySNP6k and was shown to maintain desirable levels of accuracy.
