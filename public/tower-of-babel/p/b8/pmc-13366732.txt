Exploring the relationships between multisite musculoskeletal pain, pain characteristics, and physical function: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Wren Burton, Yan Ma, Cynthia R Long, Jacob McCarey, Robert Vining, Brad Manor, Jeffrey M Hausdorff, Matthew H Kowalski, Peter M Wayne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMultisite musculoskeletal pain is highly prevalent and negatively impacts physical function through several potential mechanisms. However, it remains unclear how the number of affected pain regions relates to function. This study explored associations between different aspects of pain (global pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, pain interference) and physical function in individuals with multisite pain.MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis included 101 participants aged 18–85 years. Multisite pain was assessed across eight anatomic regions, using binary questions, with scores based on total affected sites. A composite function score was derived via principal component analysis from self-reported physical function, objective gait speed, and clinician-assessed dynamic balance. Hierarchical multivariable linear regression models examined the associations between number of pain regions and composite function, adjusting for age, sex and BMI, with sequential addition of global pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference.ResultsParticipants (aged 40 ± 17.6; 69% women) reported a median of 2 (IQR 1–3) pain regions. After adjusting for demographics, a greater number of reported pain regions correlated with worse composite physical function (ß: -0.16; 95% CI (-0.31 to -0.01); R² = 0.520, ΔR² = 0.205). Global pain intensity explained additional variance in physical function (ß: -0.20; 95% CI -0.30 to -0.10);R² = 0.589, ΔR² = 0.069), whereas catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference contributed minimally.ConclusionsGreater number of pain regions was associated with worse composite function, and global pain intensity explained additional variance in this relationship. The independent contributions of catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference to composite function were minimal when examined alongside this cumulative pain burden, though findings should be interpreted with caution and replicated in larger samples, with longitudinal designs to clarify the directionality of these relationships. Results are consistent with biopsychosocial understandings of multisite pain in which cumulative pain burden, psychological factors, and functional outcomes are interconnected through overlapping pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09990-9.Keywords: Chronic pain, Pain measurement, Physical function performance, Catastrophization

Background

Multisite musculoskeletal pain, defined as pain occurring in multiple body regions, has a prevalence of up to 81% in the general adult population, and higher estimates in those over the age of 65 [1–3]. The presence of multisite pain is associated with reduced physical and mental health, lower quality of life, and increased pain intensity [4]. Longitudinal data demonstrate that baseline multisite pain precedes and predicts functional decline in aging adults [5–7]. Multisite musculoskeletal pain can lead to a cascade of negative health outcomes including long-term disability, diminished work capacity, higher rates of healthcare utilization, and increased fall risk [1, 8]. It may further compromise functional independence through alterations in gait [9], potentially mediated by cognitive and emotional processes where pain acts as a mental distraction complicating motor function [10]. Multisite musculoskeletal pain frequently co-occurs within a broader network of chronic overlapping pain conditions that share common psychosocial and central pain processing mechanisms, which may collectively contribute to functional impairment beyond what any single pain condition accounts for [11, 12].

Pain interference is a construct that characterizes the relationship between pain and function in daily activities [13]. Higher pain interference is associated with lower function and mobility [8, 14, 15]. Pain-related cognitive-behavioral factors, such as kinesiophobia (fear of physical movement due to a perceived vulnerability to pain or reinjury) can further complicate physical function [16, 17]. Kinesiophobia is prevalent across various musculoskeletal pain conditions and is associated with increased pain intensity and proprioceptive errors, as well as reductions in physical activity and functional performance [18, 19]. Pain catastrophizing, characterized by an exaggerated negative mental mindset during actual or anticipated pain, is also associated with increased pain intensity, pain interference, and decreased physical health [20, 21]. Pain catastrophizing can exacerbate kinesiophobia, amplifying pain interference, and creating a self-perpetuating cycle that impairs physical function [22, 23].

Elevated baseline kinesiophobia and pain catastrophizing scores exceeding normative values are associated with longitudinal outcomes in patients with chronic spine pain, including slower improvements in disability and worse mental health outcomes [20, 24]. While fear-avoidance and biopsychosocial frameworks have advanced the understanding of how these constructs relate to pain and disability, most prior work has examined these relationships in single-site or condition-specific pain populations [17, 25, 26]. Less is known about how the number of affected pain regions, as a marker of pain burden, relates to physical function when assessed using both objective performance-based and self-reported measures [16, 27]. The relative contributions of pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference to functional outcomes have rarely been examined simultaneously and sequentially alongside functional data, creating opportunities for disentangling their independent effects.

In this exploratory cross-sectional analysis we used data from a previously conducted study to: (1) descriptively compare global pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, pain interference, and composite function in individuals with musculoskeletal pain in a variety of body regions; (2) assess if there is an association between the number of anatomic pain regions and a composite measure of physical function; and (3) explore the patterns between global pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference through their associations with composite function.

Methods

Study design and research participants

This exploratory analysis utilized data from a data repository in a previously completed cross-sectional study assessing gait performance in individuals with and without chronic neck pain at the Osher Clinical Center for Integrative Health based at Brigham and Women’s Hospital. Reporting adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE; Supplementary File 1) [28]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Protocol #: 2019P002325). Detailed information regarding the study design and recruitment methods has been published [29].

A convenience sample of eligible participants was recruited. Eligible individuals were aged 18–85 years, fluent in English, and free of conditions such as neurological or musculoskeletal disorders impacting walking ability, balance impairments, or recent cervical spine surgery. Multisite pain was measured using an adapted version of the pain assessment used in the Women’s Health and Aging Study, for which participants are asked about the presence of musculoskeletal pain in eight different anatomic regions (neck, upper back, shoulders, wrists/hands, low back, hips, knees, and feet/ankle) [30]. A summary numeric score for the number of pain sites was created based on the sum of participants’ answers to binary questions about each location. For this analysis, we approached the number of pain sites in two ways. First, the variable was categorized into four groups based on the total number of self-reported pain regions (no pain, one pain site, two pain sites, three or more pain sites) for descriptive statistics of participant characteristics. Then the variable was used as a continuous variable from 0 to 7 pain regions for subsequent analyses.

Instruments and outcome measures

Collected demographic information included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), race, ethnicity, and educational level. This analysis focused on previously unexamined measures from the prior study [29], including number of pain regions, global pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference reported by participants. We also collected physical function and gait performance data.

All instruments and outcome measures have shown to be valid and reliable and have been successfully employed in prior and ongoing research. Data from self-report surveys and balance testing results were collected using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools hosted at Partners Healthcare, while gait performance data were obtained using the Zeno™ Walkway system (ProtoKinetics LLC, Havertown, Pennsylvania, United States of America).

Global pain intensity was recorded based on a numerical rating scale where patients were asked to rate their average pain levels throughout their entire body over the past 7 days on a scale from zero (representing no pain) to ten (worst pain imaginable) [31]. The numeric rating scale demonstrates good to excellent test-retest reliability in musculoskeletal pain populations and has shown superior psychometric properties compared to other single-item pain intensity measures [32, 33].

Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS-29) was used to measure physical, mental, and social well-being. Individual domains are scored from 1 to 5, and raw scores for PROMIS-29 physical function and pain interference were extracted and converted to T-scores. The T-score metric is based on United States census data and is normalized to a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 [34–36]. Higher T-scores reflect a greater degree of the construct being measured, though the clinical interpretation varies by construct (e.g., a higher physical function T-score indicates better function; higher pain interference T-score indicates more interference). The PROMIS-29 demonstrates good to excellent psychometric properties in populations with musculoskeletal pain conditions [37–39].

Gait speed was assessed using the Zeno™ Walkway and ProtoKinetics Movement Analysis Software. Data was collected by having participants walk across the 20-foot gait walkway once under each of four conditions: quiet walking at a self-selected preferred speed, and three dual-task conditions involving cognitive or head movement challenges [29]. For this study, gait speed from the quiet walking condition (preferred walking speed) was extracted as a key outcome and reported in meters per second.

Dynamic Gait Index (DGI) was used to assess gait, balance, and fall risk. The DGI tests the ability of an individual to maintain walking balance while responding to different task demands through eight dynamic conditions (walking on a level surface, changing speeds, head turning horizontally and vertically, walking and turning 180 degrees to stop, stepping over and around obstacles, as well as ascending and descending stairs) [40, 41]. Each condition was scored by a trained clinician from 0 to 3, with 3 indicating normal performance and 0 indicating severe impairment, for a maximum possible cumulative score of 24. DGI has demonstrated high reliability and validity in clinical populations [42].

Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK) is a 17-item scale which was used to assess fear of movement. Higher scores indicate a higher level of kinesiophobia, with a maximum score of 68 [43]. Scores above 37 are considered to indicate clinically relevant levels of kinesiophobia [44]. The TSK has shown to be valid and reliable in pain populations [45–47].

The Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) is a validated 13-item instrument used to measure rumination, magnification, and helplessness related to pain, with higher scores (up to 52) indicating a higher level of catastrophic thinking, with total scores above 20 considered reflective of a clinically relevant level of catastrophizing [48–51]. The PCS has demonstrated excellent internal consistency and reliability [51].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (version 4.3.2; R Core Team 2021). Demographic and clinical characteristics (including age, sex, height, weight, BMI, race, ethnicity, and educational level) were summarized by pain distribution categories (no pain, one pain site, two pain sites, three or more pain sites). Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, with group differences assessed using chi-square tests. For smaller cell counts (expected count < 5), Fisher’s exact test (extended for RxC tables) was employed. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, and group differences assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for variables with equal variance and Welch’s ANOVA for variables with unequal variance.

Data were evaluated for missingness, distributional assumptions, and detection of outliers, and examined for linearity using scatterplot matrices. Homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene’s test. Multicollinearity was assessed via correlation matrices, with correlations exceeding 0.70 flagged for further investigation. Spearman’s correlations were computed among all included outcomes to characterize their interrelationships prior to regression analysis (Fig. 1).

Spearman’s correlation matrix for all included outcome measures

To derive a composite function measure, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were assessed among three selected performance variables: PROMIS-29 physical function, gait speed, and DGI. These variables provide complementary aspects of function, including patient-reported function capabilities, clinically assessed DGI and objective performance-based measures of mobility, respectively. Principal component analysis was performed on these three variables. Data were standardized to ensure equal weighting of each variable. The first principal component (PC1), which explained most of the shared variance, was extracted as a composite function score. This score was centered (mean = 0) for use in subsequent analyses, where positive values indicate better than average function and negative values indicate worse than average function.

Hierarchical multivariable linear regressions examined the association of explanatory variables and composite function. Variables were entered in sequential blocks: Model 1 included demographic covariates (age, sex, BMI); Model 2 added the number of pain regions; and Model 3 added global pain intensity. To examine the independent contribution of pain catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference, we conducted additional analyses testing each factor individually Models 3a-3c, respectively. The order of the explanatory variables in Models 3a-3c was based on theoretical models of pain in which catastrophizing represents a cognitive predisposition that influences fear-based responses (kinesiophobia), which in turn contribute to pain interference as a facilitator of reduced physical functioning [52, 53]. Continuous variables were mean-centered prior to analysis to facilitate interpretation. For each model, beta coefficients (ß) with 95% confidence intervals, R2 values, and R2 change statistics were reported. As the outcome variable is the standardized composite score (mean = 0), ß reflect the change in composite function associated with a one-unit change in each explanatory variable at its mean value. Model assumptions were evaluated at each step using the Global Validation of Linear Model Assumptions framework [54] and ‘gvlma’ package in R, which provides simultaneous assessment of linear model specifications, including skewness, kurtosis, link function, and heteroscedasticity. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to assess multicollinearity.

Given that prior analyses with this data demonstrated age-stratified differences in gait speed and variability [29], a sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine whether age moderated the relationship between the number of pain regions and composite function. Interaction terms (age x pain count) were tested in both Models 2 and 3 using ANOVA.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the 101 participants included are reported in Table 1, stratified by grouping of pain distribution. The overall mean cohort age was 40.2±17.6 years, and predominately female (69%). The median (interquartile range (IQR)) number of pain regions was 2 (1 to 3). Demographic and clinical characteristics were generally comparable across the four pain distribution groups. No differences were observed in age, sex, BMI, race, or education level. Differences were noted in ethnicity, with a higher proportion of Hispanic individuals in the “No Pain” group compared to all the other pain groups. Pain intensity increased across pain distribution groups, with mean scores increasing from 0.14 in the no pain group to 4.97 in the 3 + pain regions group. Scores for pain interference, catastrophizing, and functional measures (physical function, dynamic gait index, gait speed, and composite function) demonstrated a similar graded pattern, with outcomes worsening as the pain distribution increased. Kinesiophobia scores plateaued beyond the single pain site group (35.13, 35.85, and 36.84 for the 1, 2, and 3 + pain sites groups, respectively).

Spearman’s correlations among all included outcomes are presented in Fig. 1. Pain intensity demonstrated strong positive correlations with pain interference (r = 0.82) and number of pain sites (r = 0.77), and negative correlations with PROMIS-29 physical function (r = -0.72) and DGI (r = -0.46). Catastrophizing and kinesiophobia showed moderate correlations with pain intensity (r = 0.68 and r = 0.57, respectively) and with each other (r = 0.62). The observed correlation patterns supported the use of hierarchical regression with sequential entry of outcomes to examine their independent contribution to composite function.

Results from Spearman’s correlations (Fig. 1) for the principal component analysis indicated moderate correlations, 0.28–0.45, between the selected variables, suggesting enough relation to form a meaningful composite without redundancy [55, 56]. Principal component analysis was performed to create a composite function measure using PROMIS-29 physical function subscale, preferred gait speed, and DGI scores. The first principal component (PC1) explained 61% of the variance, with loadings of 0.56 for PROMIS-29 physical function, 0.56 for preferred gait speed, and 0.61 for DGI. These comparable loadings support the use of PC1 as a robust composite function score.

Hierarchical multivariable linear regression models are presented in Tables 2 and 3. All model assumptions were satisfied across analyses, and variance inflation factors confirmed no multicollinearity (all variance inflation factors < 5). Demographic factors (Model 1) explained 31% of the variance in composite function (R2 = 0.32). The addition of number of pain regions entered in Model 2 substantially increased the explained variance to 52% (ΔR2 = 0.21). In Model 3, pain intensity contributed an additional 7% of explained variance (ΔR2 = 0.07). Pain catastrophizing (Model 3a) and kinesiophobia (Model 3b) independently contributed 0.5% additional variance when added to Model 3, while pain interference (Model 3c) contributed 1.6% additional variance. When all three measures were included as explanatory variables, they collectively contributed 2% additional variance. Results from sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplementary File 2.

Discussion

This exploratory cross-sectional analysis addressed 3 aims. In the first aim, demographic factors (age, sex, BMI, race) were observed as being similar across pain distribution groups. Descriptively, a graded pattern was observed in which pain in more body regions corresponded with higher levels of catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference, alongside reductions in composite function. In hierarchical regression, demographic factors explained 31% of the variance in composite function, with older age, higher BMI, and female sex were associated with lower physical function. Results demonstrate complex functional burdens associated with multisite pain, extending prior research documenting the adverse effects of both multisite and widespread pain on patient-reported outcomes, though these pain conditions are defined and operationalized differently across the literature [8, 13, 57]. This aligns with longitudinal and cohort studies showing that individuals with multisite or widespread musculoskeletal pain report poorer physical function and quality of life and are at higher risk for persistent disability [16, 58]. Findings are consistent with a biopsychosocial understanding of multisite pain, in which biological factors such as pain burden and intensity, psychological factors such as catastrophizing and kinesiophobia, and functional outcomes are interconnected through multiple overlapping pathways [59, 60].

In our second aim, the number of pain regions had a substantial association with composite function even after adjusting for demographic factors. Specifically, individuals with pain in a greater number of body regions exhibited lower physical function as the number of pain regions increased. However, this association was attenuated when global pain intensity was added to the model, suggesting that global pain intensity may capture much of the functional impact of multisite pain in cross-sectional data. Consistent with prior research, pain intensity appears to be a key concurrent indicator of functional status, often explaining a substantial proportion of variance in disability and quality-of-life outcomes in chronic musculoskeletal pain [61, 62]. While the number of body regions affected by pain contributed to explaining composite function, the intensity of pain experienced may be a more proximal indicator of current functional capacity [63–66]. Despite the worsening of function across pain distribution groups, mean DGI scores across all groups exceeded the established fall risk threshold of 19, suggesting that functional impairment in this sample may not have reached clinically severe levels [67]. The number of pain sites and global pain intensity together may represent a cumulative pain burden that accounts for a substantial proportion of variance in functional outcomes, which may in turn contextualize the apparently limited independent contributions of catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference.

In the third aim, when examined alongside this cumulative pain burden, the contributions of catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference to composite function were minimal. These findings were inconsistent with fear-avoidance and psychological flexibility models, in which pain catastrophizing and kinesiophobia are central cognitive-affective responses that foster avoidance behaviors, physical deconditioning, and disability over time [16, 61]. Cross-sectional study designs cannot capture the temporal dynamics through which factors such as catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference may mediate the relationship between pain and function. While global pain intensity may serve as a more proximal indicator of current functional capacity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference may exert their influence through longer-term neurological adaptations leading to central sensitization or behavioral patterns, such as activity avoidance and physical deconditioning, that accumulate over time and may not be as readily detectable in single-timepoint analyses [63–66, 68–70]. Mean kinesiophobia and pain catastrophizing scores in this sample fell below established clinical cut-offs [44, 51], indicating that this was not a population with clinically elevated levels of either construct, which may partially account for their limited explanatory value. In contrast to the graded pattern observed for catastrophizing, the plateau in kinesiophobia scores across pain distribution groups may further contribute to its limited independent contribution. The limited independent contributions of these constructs may also partly reflect their shared variance with pain intensity, as evidenced by moderate to strong intercorrelations (Fig. 1).

In contrast to our findings, multiple studies in chronic musculoskeletal pain populations have linked higher baseline catastrophizing and kinesiophobia with greater subsequent pain, disability, and lower quality of life, even after adjusting for clinical covariates [16, 62, 71]. Prospective and mediation studies indicate that catastrophizing and kinesiophobia often exert their influence on function indirectly, mediating the relationship between pain intensity and both self-reported and performance-based physical functioning [61, 72]. Reductions in catastrophizing and kinesiophobia during rehabilitation have been shown to predict subsequent improvements in pain and disability, supporting their role as dynamic treatment targets rather than just static correlates [72].

Global pain intensity demonstrated a strong association with composite function, contributing substantially to the explained variance in this exploratory analysis. This finding is particularly noteworthy given that current clinical practice guidelines for chronic musculoskeletal pain emphasize the assessment of psychosocial factors and prioritizing functional improvement and participation over pain elimination as primary treatment goals [73–77]. Within this context, the present results suggest that because the two separate constructs of pain intensity and functional capacity are related, there is clear value in routinely assessing both to help inform management decisions and care goals. Given that our cross-sectional study design could not assess potential mechanisms underlying the relationships between pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, pain interference, and composite function, future research should investigate if and how different types of interventions prevent functional decline and break cycles of pain-related fear and avoidance in individuals with multisite musculoskeletal pain. Some interventions, such as pain education, exercise, group medical visits, and multidisciplinary pain management programs, have shown benefit in individuals with single-site pain, but have yet to be evaluated in multisite pain contexts [24, 78–82].

The patterns observed in our analysis reinforce the need to examine overlapping biopsychosocial processes that contribute to and prolong pain and disability [83–85]. While global pain intensity was consistently negatively associated with composite function in our cross-sectional analysis, multisite pain likely arises from and influences multiple interconnected domains. Prior research with these data has noted age-stratified differences in gait speed and variability [29], however, formal sensitivity analyses in this study found no evidence that age moderated the association between the number of pain regions and composite function. Future longitudinal studies with broader age distributions are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of age-dependent effects, including formal mediation analyses to test whether catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference mediate relationships between pain characteristics and function over time. Such research should also investigate how other factors such as depression, anxiety, and socioeconomic status, may relate to pain distribution and function [59, 70, 86, 87].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its alignment with recent calls for more holistic, whole-person metrics of health that extend beyond single indicators of function [84]. The use of principal component analysis to create a composite function measure allowed integration of self-reported physical function, objective gait speed, and clinician-assessed dynamic balance, providing a multidimensional representation of function. However, this composite measure was derived from a single sample without a formal validation cohort, thus future research is needed to validate this measure. The hierarchical multivariable linear regression modeling approach allowed for systematic examination of the relative contributions of the number of pain regions and additional constructs to composite function, enabling characterization of their independent contributions and identification of the consistent association between global pain intensity and composite function.

While the presence of pain at each anatomic region was assessed using binary questions and summed to create a continuous count variable, this approach does not capture regional pain duration, or underlying mechanisms, and assumes equal weighting across body regions, which may introduce misclassification and limit construct validity. The use of a single global pain intensity score, while providing a general estimate of perceived pain burden, does not account for regional variability in pain intensity or fluctuations over time. Although region-specific intensity ratings were available, the global measure was selected given the near-identical distributions of the two measures and to avoid the interpretive challenges introduced by summing intensity scores across participants with differing numbers of pain sites. Several included outcome measures relied on self-report, and as such, the potential for shared method bias cannot be excluded. Additionally, regression coefficients are reported in unstandardized form, which limits direct comparison of effect sizes across explanatory variables measured on different scales. While the standardized composite outcome partially mitigates this concern, future confirmatory studies should consider reporting standardized coefficients to facilitate more direct comparisons of the relative contributions of pain intensity, catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference to functional outcomes. These factors should be considered when interpreting the observed associations, and future studies should explore more nuanced approaches to characterizing multisite pain burden and intensity.

This was an exploratory cross-sectional analysis utilizing data from a previously completed study, which limits the generalizability of findings and causal inferences that can be made about the examined relationships. The observed associations cannot establish temporal precedence or directionality. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether pain intensity catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, or pain interference precede functional decline in the context of multisite pain or emerge as consequences of increasing pain burden. No formal sample size calculation was conducted for this secondary analysis, and the sample size may have limited statistical power and the stability of regression estimates, which may reflect insufficient power to detect small effects rather than true independence from functional outcomes. Additionally, the study population was relatively young, and findings may not generalize to older adults in whom age-related changes in pain processing and functional ability may alter these relationships. A further limitation is that the interrelationships among pain intensity and psychological variables were not formally examined within the regression framework, precluding conclusions about mediation or shared pathways. While the correlation heatmap provides descriptive context, future studies employing mediation or structural equation modeling are needed to clarify the pathways through which multisite pain influences function.

Conclusions

In adults with varying pain presentations, pain in a greater number of body regions was associated with worse composite function, and global pain intensity explained additional variance in this relationship. The independent contributions of catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and pain interference to composite function were minimal when examined alongside this cumulative pain burden, though these findings should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional design and modest sample size. Results are consistent with a biopsychosocial understanding of multisite pain in which cumulative pain burden, psychological factors, and functional outcomes are interconnected through overlapping pathways. Replication in larger samples is warranted, and longitudinal designs are needed to clarify the directionality and potential mediating relationships among multisite pain, global pain intensity, and functional outcomes.
