Phenology and floral morph variation suggest non-classical distyly in the Sisrè berry plant [Synsepalum dulcificum (Schumach. & Thonn.) Daniell, Sapotaceae]
Authors: Dèdéou A. Tchokponhoué, Mario R. Vigninou, Sognigbé N’Danikou, Chantal Lokossou, Nicodème V. Fassinou Hotegni, Ebenezer Ewedje, Alice Muchugi, Daniel Nyadanu, Brice Sinsin, Enoch G. Achigan-Dako
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding floral biology and phenological variation patterns in plant species is crucial for hybridization and genetic improvement. In Synsepalum dulcificum (Sisrè berry plant), the lack of such knowledge hinders proper mastering of the pollination ecology of this species, with drawbacks in terms of cross success. A total of 100 S. dulcificum juveniles from two sites and 250 flowers were selected, monitored for two years, and assessed for phenological phase durations, synchronization pattern, flower abortion and floral morphometrics. Data were analyzed by combining generalized linear models with binomial and Poisson error structures, t-test, Mann-Whitney test and circular statistics.ResultsOur results revealed that S. dulcificum is a non-classical distylous species with an isoplethy (equal frequency of floral morphs) marked by all individual plants unexpectedly exhibiting both S- and L- floral morphs. Abortion was as high as 86%, culminated at the phases of style and petal wilting (26%) and fructification onset (28.7%), and varied from year 1 (93.7%) to year 2 (79.8%) (χ2 = 23.63, df = 1, p < 0.001, Cohen’ d effect size = 0.42). The length of the phenological phases varied across (85 days for year 1 vs 86 days for year 2) and within the year (85 days for dry season vs 91 days for rainy season), with an average fruiting cycle of 87 ± 2 days. Flowering and fruiting were highly synchronous across sites with a marked seasonality whose extent was site- and phenophase- specific (Flowering: Rayleigh Z = 0.0534, p = 0.001 for site 1 and Rayleigh Z = 0.2213, p < 0.001 for site 2; Fruiting: Rayleigh Z = 0.4672, p < 0.001 for site 1 and Rayleigh Z = 0.4502, p < 0.001 for site 2). January and April represent peak periods for most of the phenological events in the species.ConclusionThese findings suggest a need to redefine individual-level morph classification in distylous species and explore the biological drivers of high abortion rates in S. dulcificum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07320-0.

Background

Documenting the spatial configuration of floral components is critical for determining the most appropriate mating system in plant species. For centuries, scientists assumed that plant species were essentially characterized by flowers with continuous variation in the relative positions of their sexual organs. These species are known to be homostylous and are predominantly self-pollinated [1]. In contrast, heterostylous species exhibit floral polymorphisms under genetic influence with either two (distyly) or three (tristyly) distinct floral morphologies (floral morphs). Distylous species are composed of L-morph and S-morph plants [1], usually in equal frequency (isoplethy) [2]. While L-morph plants produce flowers with longer styles than stamens, S-morph plants produce flowers with styles shorter than stamens. In tristylous species, Eckert and Barrett [3] reported that L-morph plants are characterized by flowers with long styles and anthers at low and medium heights, whereas S-morph plants are composed of flowers with short styles combined with anthers at medium and high positions. For the third morph, i.e., the M-morph, its plants are characterized by a middle-positioned stigma surrounded by both high and low anthers. A comparative study by Costa et al. [4] revealed that L-morphs present an approach-herkogamous phenotype and are better suited to promote pollen transfer between plants and intramorphs via cross-pollination than are the M- and S-morphs. This reciprocal herkogamy observed in distylous and tristylyous species promotes cross-pollination [5], reduces pollen waste and therefore increases male fitness. Additionally, the self-incompatibility of most heterostylous species reduces inbreeding depression and thus promotes female fitness [6].

Heterostylous species are present on every continent except Antarctica and can easily grow in all types of habitats [7]. Most of them are perennial species, with few being annual. In the tropics, most heterostylous species are woody and represented by shrubs and small trees. As of 1979, heterostyly was recorded in only 24 families of plants, with speculation suggesting a very low probability of detecting it in additional families [8]. Despite this prediction, heterostyly has been reported in additional families, and a recent review by Barrett [2] indicated that at least 28 families exhibit this floral polymorphism, which unlocked the restrictive limitation of the botanical scope of this phenomenon. In this work, we speculated that Synsepalum dulcificum (Schumach & Thonn.) Daniell, a shrub species native to West Africa [9], exhibits heterostyly, a characteristic that has never been reported in Sapotaceae.

In common usage, berries refer to small, pulpy, and often colourful fruits that are typically sweet or tart in taste. They are usually consumed fresh and are often used in processed foods such as desserts, jams, and smoothies [10–12]. Synsepalum dulcificum (also known as the Sisrè berry plant) is a promising shrub species whose fruit can change sour taste into a sweet taste. This property is due to miraculin, which is a glycoprotein present in the fruit pulp [13]. People in West Africa have been using Sisrè berries not only to sweeten their drinks, such as acidic palm wine [14] and choukoutou (local drinks in Benin, Togo and Burkina Faso) but also to facilitate the consumption of food such as porridge, gari (a cassava-based food) and unripe orange fruits. Tchokponhoué et al. [15] reported 56 different medicinal applications, including the treatment of anaemia, haemorrhoids, hypertension, malaria and cancer, using plant parts such as the bark, leaves, fruits, roots, seeds and twigs. The species is also a highly important commodity, with the kilogram of powder from the pulp estimated at United States Dollar (USD) 2,500 (https://www.miraclefruitfarm.com/shop).

Despite its economic, medicinal and nutritional potential and increasing demand for fruits, Synsepalum dulcificum has faced several constraints. Our empirical observations revealed a high rate of floral abortion during open pollination, whereas Xingwei et al. [16] reported a fruit set as low as 5%, which together led to a low fruit yield in this species. Nutrient deficiency, variations in environmental parameters and water supply may also increase the floral abortion rate in Synsepalum dulcificum. For Kokubun et al. [17], floral abortion can also result from a malfunction of floral structures such as the pistil and stamen. For example, in cocoa, floral morphology affects flower fertilization success [18]. This study revealed that splay staminode-style flowers are the least efficiently pollinated and the most exposed to floral abortion compared with converging and parallel staminode-style flowers. According to Barrett and Richards [1], the diversity in floral morphology, such as distyly or tristyly, can also reduce the interaction between reproductive organs and lead to low pollen transfer. They also noted that heterostylous species promote compatible pollination between floral morphs and reduce pollen wastage.

Individual phenology observations revealed that diverse flower and fruit development stages can be found at the same time in Sisrè berry plants [16]. Unfortunately, failure in synchronization of flower receptivity and pollen maturity leads to non-random cross-fertilization and consequently to a low level of diversity [19]. This also promotes floral abortion and self-fertilization. As revealed by Mutasa-Göttgens and Hedden [20] and Frimpong-Anin et al. [18], temperature conditions represent inductive conditions that regulate blooming in trees. In a parallel study carried out by Nguemo et al. [21] on Vitellaria paradoxa, it was shown that the phenological behaviors of the species vary according to agroecological zones and environments. These findings suggest that floral phenology may also vary under various environmental conditions in Synsepalum dulcificum. Additionally, the duration of transitions between phenological stages is highly dependent on external factors such as water supply and organic nutrient management [22].

Although previous findings related to phenological variations provided valuable information for better understanding the floral development patterns of the Sisrè berry plant, these findings are still incomplete. For example, the phenological phases (phenophases) as well as their lengths reported in Tchokponhoué et al. [23] were only based on the first lifetime flowering episode in the considered juveniles, and whether these parameters stand at a later developmental stage of the plants needs to be clarified. In addition, no proper reports of floral piece morphometrics exist in this species, and such a situation could not help demarcate floral morphs, if any, in the species. Although empirical observations pinpoint massive abortion in Synsepalum dulcificum, proper quantification of its extent and of the most concerning floral phenophases is still lacking in this species.

This study was then initiated (i) to assess the extent of individual phenological development synchronization, ii) to gauge floral phenological phase variation across years and seasons, iii) to decipher the prominence of flower shedding across floral phenological phases and iv) to unravel any potential floral morph variation. Since Synsepalum dulcificum is naturally confined to the Guineo-Congolian region of Benin, we hypothesized that the individual species will then exhibit high synchrony in their phenological phases. We address four research questions in this study. First, how synchronous are phenological changes in Synsepalum dulcificum? We would expect high synchrony since several phenological stages can be found at the same time on different individuals. However, the degree of synchronization may also vary significantly between phenological stages. Second, is the floral phenological variation in Synsepalum dulcificum season-specific? Here, we anticipate the possibility of fine-tuning the floral developmental stages previously reported in the species by Xingwei et al. [16] and, with the hypothesis that the floral phenological phase length in Synsepalum dulcificum is affected by the seasonal variation. Third, how did floral organ shedding evolve throughout the phenological stages? We hypothesized that the initial floral development stages presented a lower percentage of floral abortion than did the advanced stages. Fourth, is Synsepalum dulcificum a heterostylous species, and how is it expressed? Responding to these research questions will help provide key guidance for the efficient development of breeding populations of this species.

Materials and methods

Experimental sites

The study was conducted from January 2021 to December 2022 at two different experimental sites, both of which are located in the Guineo-Congolian region of Benin. Sites 1 and 2 were located at the Genetics, Biotechnology and Seed Science Unit (GBioS) (N 6° 25’1.7, E 2° 20’22.66) and the Laboratory of Applied Ecology (LEA) (N 6° 25’1.66, E 2° 20’22.34) of the University of Abomey-Calavi (UAC), respectively. Site 1 was a horticultural production site with no surrounding natural vegetation, unlike site 2, which was a fodder production site surrounded by natural vegetation made up of trees such as neem [Azadirachta indica A. Juss.], Gmelina (Gmelina arborea Roxb. ex Sm.) and kassod trees [Senna occidentalis (L.) Link], and herbs such as coat buttons (Tridax procumbens L.) and Caribbean stylo [Stylosanthes amata (L.) Taub.]. Both sites had ferrallitic soil types [23]. During the first year of the experiment, the monthly average rainfall, temperature and relative humidity were 3.61 mm, 26.74 °C and 85.3%, respectively. During the second year, the average values of rainfall, temperature and relative humidity were 3.3 mm, 26.42 °C and 84.68%, respectively.

Experimental system

Two separate experiments were conducted. Experiment 1 was set to assess plant phenological variation, synchronization, and seasonality patterns, and made use of 100 S. dulcificum juveniles from two sites including 50 juveniles on site 1 and 50 on site2. Experiment 2 was conducted only on Site 1 and focused on 250 flowers randomly selected on 25 out of the 50 plants considered in experiment 1. Only 25 plants were considered for experiment 2 to ensure a consistent tracking and quality collecting of all target parameters. This second experiment examined plant and floral phenological variation, floral morphometrics and abortion patterns.

Experiment 1: phenological variation and synchronization

The planting material was composed of 100 juveniles of Synsepalum dulcificum (50 from each experimental site) established in a staggered square arrangement (quincunx) spaced 1 m × 1 m apart on site 1, resulting in a density of 10,000 seedlings/ha, and a density of 2 m × 2 m on site 2, resulting in 2500 seedlings/ha. The juveniles selected on site 1 were established in January 2015, whereas the juveniles from site 2 were established in September 2017. Seeds used to raise juveniles at both sites were collected from the Toffo district in the Republic of Benin, but on different trees (tree 1: 06°55′12″N, 002°16′12″E for site 1 juveniles; tree 2: 06°55′09.5″N, 002°16′23.3″E for site 2 juveniles). Voucher specimens from trees 1 and 2 were deposited at the Benin National Herbarium, located at University of Abomey-Calavi; identified by the Herbarium taxonomist (Prof Paul Yedomonhan) and deposited under the accessions names GBS-SYD-002 and GBS-SYD-010, respectively. All the juveniles started flowering 18–24 months after sowing. The site 1 juveniles were six years old at the time of the experiment, whereas those at site 2 were three years old. The plants were fertilized every six months with the application of ½ kg of compost per plant. The plants were watered every week at a rate of 11 L per plant, and weeding was carried out every four months. These plants were tracked for phenological development at the individual level.

Experiment 2: floral phenological variation and morphometrics

For this experiment, twenty-five of the fifty plants previously selected at site 1 were randomly chosen. A total of 250 flowers (10 flowers on each of the 25 plants) were randomly used on the selected plants to estimate the floral abortion percentage and assess the phenological variation pattern in floral development. For this purpose, ten newly formed whitish buds were tagged on each juvenile of the 25 juveniles and subjected to daily monitoring. The detailed phenological stages presented in this study were built from the previous description provided by Tchokponhoué et al. [23] and Xingwei et al. [16], in which seven different basic phenological stages were indicated in the species, including budding, flowering, flower bloom, fructification onset, fruit physiological maturity, ripening onset, and full ripening.

Data collection

Experiment 1

The occurrence date of each plant phenological phase, including budding, flowering, fruit enlargement and fruit ripening, was recorded throughout weekly observations from January 2021 to December 2021.

For the measurement of flower morphometric parameters, two randomly tagged whitish buds on each of the 25 individuals (for a total of 50 buds) were tracked until the anthesis stage. Then, the 50 developed flowers (2 buds that gave rise to 2 flowers on each of the 25 plants) were removed, placed in labelled petri dishes and moved to the laboratory. Morphological parameters, including flower length, flower width, style length, petal length, petal width, pedicel length, filament length, ovary length, and ovary width, were measured via an IP67 Alpa digital calliper, and the number of anthers on each anther was directly counted.

To identify floral morphs in the species, the relative lengths of the style and stamen were determined through differences in size and relative positioning. Flowers with longer styles than stamens were separated from flowers with shorter styles than stamens. The predominant floral morph was determined through percentage calculation, and the floral morph ratio was therefore determined.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted in the R environment [24].

Phenological synchronization

According to Staggemeier et al. [25], circular statistics are more appropriate for calculating phenological parameters and comparing plant phenology across environments. On this basis, our raw dataset containing phenology event occurrence dates was first transformed to the day of the year (DOY) via the yday() function of “lubridate” package [26] and then to radians for circular statistical purposes. For this transformation, the first day of the year was set to 01/01/2021. The distributions across the environment of the two most functionally important phenological stages (flowering and fruiting) in the species were subsequently separately estimated via the fitting of a von Mises kernel density function. Prior to that, we calculated the overlap coefficients between the two estimated distributions via the “Overlap” package [27]. The coefficient of overlap (∆) ranges from 0 (no synchrony or no overlap) to 1 (complete synchrony or complete overlap). Three types of coefficients of overlap exist, including ∆1 (used for estimating a pairwise synchronization level and appropriate for small populations with less than 50 observations, each), ∆4 (a global estimator to assess the overall synchronization level in populations with more than 75 observations each) and ∆5 (suitable for assessing how the phenological rhythm at the individual level affects the duration of a whole population’s phenological period extent) [27, 28]. While ∆1 and ∆4 were largely used [29–31], ∆5 was rather classified as non-recommended because of its instability [27]. Here, since a total of 4,356 and 1,551 observations were conducted on flowering and fruiting phases, respectively, on 100 individuals, ∆4 was considered the best overlap coefficient to use. The phenological seasonality pattern was checked for all the phenological events via the Rayleigh test, where the significance of the test (p < 0.05) indicated high seasonality; otherwise, the phenological distribution was uniform throughout the year [32]. Circular representations were performed via the “circular” package [33].

Phenological phase duration and floral abortion

The length of specific phenophases (dependant variables) expressed in days count classically come as count data, which intrinsically incorporates a systematic heteroscedasticity linked to a variance that increases linearly with the mean [34]. This violation of the constant variance assumption implies the use of a generalized linear model (with a Poisson error distribution) more suitable to model this parameter [35]. Consequently, a generalized linear model (glm) with a Poisson error structure was fitted to assess how seasonal variation affects the length of phenophases (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J and K). As for the floral abortion, the dependant variable (abortion percentage) being a proportion data also carries a form of heteroscedasticity, which resulted from a variance that is an inverted U-shaped function of the mean. Then, a glm with a binomial error structure was fitted to elucidate how the abortion percentage changed across observation years and across phenological stages. Where relevant, the effect sizes for the significance of floral abortion percentages variation were computed using the ES.h () function of the “pwr” package [36].

Flower morphometrics

Prior to the analysis, we performed a Shapiro‒Wilk test to check the normality of the data. All the data were normally distributed except for the flower width and style length. When the data were normally distributed, a t test was performed, whereas in the opposite situation, a Mann‒Whitney test was performed to determine the morphological differences between the flower morphotypes. The results are presented as the means ± standard errors (SE). Where relevant, the effect sizes of the significances of flower morphometrics variation were computed using the function hedges_g () of the “Effectsize” package [37]. The Hedges’ g effect size statistic was chosen (e.g., compared for instance with cohen’ d statistic) to account for any potential small- sample related bias.

Fine-tuning the phenological phases length

The fruit production cycle in Synsepalum dulcificum lasted 87 ± 2 days, whereas flowers reached anthesis after 39 ± 4 days following the appearance of whitish buds. All the phenological phase lengths varied significantly across seasons (p < 0.001) and within seasons (dry season of 2021, p < 0.01; rainy season of 2021, p < 0.01; dry season of 2022, p < 0.01). The longest fruit production cycle was observed during the rainy season (91 days). The transition duration from brown bud to flowering onset was significantly greater in the rainy season than in the dry season and contributed the most to its long fruit production cycle. Floral abortion percentage in the Sisrè berry plant

Overall, a high percentage of abortion (86.79 ± 6.95%) was observed in Synsepalum dulcificum. The abortion percentage was greater (93.7%) during the first observation year than during the second observation year (79.8%) (χ2 = 23.6, df = 1, p < 0.001, Cohen’ d effect size = 0.42) (Fig. 5).

Flowers with L-morph represented 84% of the studied flowers in Synsepalum dulcificum. Flower length (t = −0.60, df = 3.76, p = 0.58) and width (t = −0.60, df = 3.98, p = 0.97), pedicel length (t = 2.08, df = 3.55, p = 0.11) and ovary width (t = −0.79, df = 3.36, p = 0.47) in general did not differ between the two morphs, whereas petal length (t = −2.8, df = 3.78, p = 0.05) and style length (W = 144.5, p = 0.06) differed only marginally between the two morphs (Table 2). In opposite, petal width (t = −5.54, df = 4.98, p = 0.002, Hedges’ g effect size = −1.73, 95% CI: [−2.78, −0.66]) and filament length (t = −3.43, df = 4.86, p = 0.01, Hedges’ g effect size = −1.1, 95% CI: [−2.12, −0.06]) were significantly greater in S-morph than in L-morph flowers, whereas the ovaries of L-morph flowers were significantly longer (t = 5.30, df = 4.63, p value = 0.003, Hedges’ g effect size = 1.77, 95% CI: [0.70, 2.83]) than were those of S-morph flowers (Table 2). All these Hedges’ g values pointed to a large effect size of the difference observed in floral morphometrics of the two morphs, thus strengthening the conclusion of distyly in the species.

Discussion

Phenological distribution and synchrony

Uncovering knowledge related to phenological synchrony within a given species is crucial for understanding reproduction patterns. This study points to high synchrony in phenophases across the observation sites. The high synchrony may be explained by the relative similarity of environmental conditions, as suggested by the climatic data presented in the methodology for both sites, which are all confined to a unique environment, the Guineo‒Congolian phytogeographical zone of Benin. As reported in Vitellaria paradoxa by Nguemo et al. [21], significant environmental variation between sites may alter phenological timing. The high synchrony observed during flowering promotes high pollen availability, which is normally presumed to result in high fruiting onset, as observed in avocado (Persea americana L.) by Lovatt [38]. However, unfortunately, only an overall fruit set of approximately 14% was observed in this study. Although low, the percentage of fruit set recorded in this study represented nearly 200% fruit set increase compared with the 5% reported by Xingwei et al. [16] in their study in Malaysia. This difference is not surprising, as the lower average temperature in Malaysia (25.4 °C) may have impeded key processes such as pollen germination and pollen tube growth, which are crucial for an optimal fruit set [39]. This non-negligeable difference may also be due to the difference in genotypes tested in both studies, which are certainly not of the same genetic background. Our findings also reflected a consistency in seasonality detection from sites-merged to site-specific analysis, which is indicative of the robustness of the conclusions concerning the occurrence of phenological events in this species. In this study, flowering peaked in April, which corresponds to the beginning of the wet period of the rainy season in Benin. This strengthens the crucial role that watering or irrigation plays in species development and production [23].

Floral and fruiting phenological phase length responses to seasonal variation

Using six-year-old juveniles, the whole fruit production cycle of Sisrè berry plants was set to 83–91 days. A study on the same individuals by Tchokponhoué et al. [23] at a younger age (two-year-old juveniles) reported a cycle length of up to 155 days. This is indicative of the influence of ontogeny on plant production cycle length, with older Sisrè berry plant stands having a faster production cycle, the most striking reduction in length being observed in (i) the transition phases from whitish bud to style and petal elongation compared with the report of Tchokponhoué et al. [23] and (ii) the transition from anthesis to fruit ripening compared with the results of Xingwei et al. [16], who also used less aged plants. Indeed, older or mature plants, owing to their dense foliage and large leaf area, have greater photosynthetic activity [40]. As a result, they produce significantly greater amounts of sugars, which are the primary products of photosynthesis and are needed in the regulation of numerous metabolic and developmental processes, such as flowering and fruiting [41]. The high availability of sugars, especially sucrose, actually helps reduce flowering and fruiting time by acting as an inducing signal [42].

In addition to water and nutrient supplies [23], seasonal variation also affects the phenological phase length in S. dulcificum, as indicated by our results, and thus corroborates findings of Singh and Kushwaha [43] in tropical tree species, who reported equally shorter cycle lengths during the dry season than during the rainy season. Temperature has a significant effect on the growth cycle, and flowering time at high temperatures has been shown to shorten the duration of the fruit production cycle in perennial trees [44]. In contrast, the low temperature observed during the rainy season negatively affects pollen tube growth, whereas relatively high rainfall disturbs pollinator activity, thus affecting pollen transfer and increasing the length of the cycle [45].

Abortion rate in the Sisrè berry plant

Previous estimates of flower and fruit abortion percentages in the species indicated a range of 79.2–100% [16], an interval in which these study values fit well. Moreover, we better understood the variation in the abortion rate among the various stages that resulted in highly significant flower and fruit drop from anthesis to physiological maturity. More specifically, phenological phases such as the browning of the style and petal and the onset of fructification were the most sensitive phases. The browning of the style and petal is a physiological stage that indicates the end of the fertilization process in flowers. The observation of a high abortion rate at this stage suggests that fertilization was not successful. According to Hedhly et al. [40], pollen germination and growth, which are critical for fertilization and fruit setting, are highly influenced by climatic conditions. This suggests that a high fluctuation in temperature during the last pre-fertilization hours may be detrimental to fruit set in the species, thus opening the room to investigate the tolerable thermal amplitude variation for successful fruit set in the species. Abortion events observed after fertilization are likely related to non-genetic effects such as competition for space, nutrients, and sunlight exposure [16, 46]. As shown by our results, flowering and fruit set are highly synchronous in Sisrè berry plants, while the species is known to bear a large number of each organ. This may create a double burden that results from both intra- and inter-organs competition for nutrient mobilization, which ultimately leads to the high reduction in yje carried over flowers and fruits. This finding corroborated the observations of Dutta et al. [47] in Citrus spp., who reported that fruit and flower drop are greater in species with a high flowering intensity. Several other factors, such as water and nutrient deficiency, weather adversities and the absence of prunning, can also cause significant flower and fruit drops in fruit tree crops [48].

Floral morph variation in the Sisrè berry plant

This study reported for the first time distyly in S. dulcificum, a tropical shrub species, and thus expanded to at least 29 the number of taxonomic families in which heterostyly is now present [2, 49], a new phenomenon reported in the Sapotaceae family. These findings support the claims that woody heterostylous in the tropics are mainly shrubs and small trees [1] and that heterostyly is more common in actinomorphic flowers [8], a flower type exhibited in S. dulcificum [50]. Almost all heterostylous species so far reported have a limited number of stamens, with common figures of 2, 4, 5, 6, 10 and 12, with the distylous S-morph exhibiting a larger petal than the L-morph [8]. Our observations in S. dulcificum corroborated all these predictions in heterostylous species.

In a classical distyly situation, an individual presents only a specific floral morph [51], either the S-morph or the L-morph. Strikingly, all the S. dulcificum plants investigated presented both morphs. This co-occurrence of morphs is, to our knowledge, a novel observation in heterostylous systems.

On the basis of this unique observation, we propose that a single distylous species individual be characterized as either an S-morph individual (individual producing only a floral S-morph), an L-morph individual (individual producing only a floral L-morph), an LS-morph individual (individual producing both floral morphs but with a dominance of floral L-morph) or an SL-morph individual (individual producing both morphs but with a dominance of floral S-morph).

Using this suggested classification, Synsepalum dulcificum can then be characterized as exclusively producing LS morph individuals. Because each LS individual presents both floral morphs, this non-classical distyly leads to an isoplethy in any individual of the population, reflecting the situation of a population at equilibrium [52]. Despite this equilibrium of the investigated population, the floral morph production in the species was predominantly oriented to the L-morph, which has the functional implication to promote cross-pollination in the species [53]. The culmination of L-morph prominence observed in the species with a high abortion percentage seemed to indirectly echo a detrimental effect of cross-pollination in the species. Hence, mechanistically investigating the impact of various mating systems on fruit set in this species is necessary. Because previous reports in this species also argued for the occurrence of autogamy [23], which is known to be promoted by the S-morph in heterostylous species, in-depth investigations to disentangle the contribution of each morph to fruit set in the species are needed to provide a concise view of the pollination landscape in the species. The configuration of floral structures can negatively affect flower fertilization [18], with the malfunction of floral structures such as pistils and stamens caused mostly by environmental conditions as one of the most important sources of floral abortion [17]. Style exposure in the dominant floral L-morph exposes the flowers of this species to an increased abortion percentage. In terms of implications, the LS-morph individuals of S. dulcificum could promote both autogamy and allogamy, thus diversifying breeding strategies specifically the breeding populations development and types of variety to release. Such morphs could also improve the species plasticity, hence enhancing its persistence in new environments and therefore its conservation.

Our findings on the flower morphometrics clearly indicate a size variation between the S- and L-morphs’ petal, filament and ovary which are supported by large effect sizes. However, the intrinsic prominence of the L-morph over the S-morph in the species created an imbalance in the sample size considered in assessing the floral morphometric variation. Although the effect size estimate (Hedges’ g) used was robust enough to correct for such an imbalance [54] we anticipate on the necessity to conduct repetitive tracking of the S-morph to balance both morphs sample size in further floral morphometric analysis.

Conclusion

This study highlighted the distylous nature of the Sisrè berry plant and pointed to the systematic isoplethy of the natural population of this species. We propose a new characterization chart for distylous individuals composed of four modalities, with all Synsepalum dulcificum individuals being of the LS morph. An abortion rate of nearly 86% was observed in this species, thus questioning the advantage of the prominence of the floral L-morph known to promote cross -pollination against the S-morph known to favour self-pollination. We showed that the phases extending from style and petal browning to fruiting onset were the phases most affected by abortion in this species and stressed the importance of experimentally testing the influence of various mating systems and various morphs on fruit set in this species. Our findings paved the way for:

i)exploring the implications of the distyly nature of the species for productivity and fruit quality in the species, and.ii)experimentally testing the fruit set response of diverse floral morphs to various mating systems.
