Prognostic accuracy of mid-regional proadrenomedullin in patients with sepsis – A longitudinal observational study
Authors: Akshita Makkar, H. L. Khushboo, Mukesh Bairwa, Ashish Sharma, Bela Goyal, Ravi Kant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Prognostication in sepsis remains challenging as conventional scores and biomarkers, including procalcitonin (PCT) and lactate, show limited predictive strength when used alone. This study evaluated the ability of mid-regional proadrenomedullin (MR-proADM) to predict 14-day mortality in patients with sepsis, intending to improve early risk stratification.Methods:A prospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in North India over 18 months. One hundred and twenty patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and ward with sepsis were enrolled. On admission, serum MR-proADM, PCT, and lactate were measured, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores were calculated. Prognostic accuracy was determined using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.Results:The mean age of the participants was 45.9 ± 16.1 years, with 54.2% male. Nonsurvivors (n = 56) required ICU care more frequently (69.6%) and ventilator support (55.4%). MR-proADM levels were significantly higher in nonsurvivors (2.48 ± 1.93 nmol/L) than survivors (1.43 ± 0.78 nmol/L, P < 0.001). MR-proADM achieved an area under ROC of 0.767, which was comparable to SOFA (0.712), lower than lactate (0.807), and superior to PCT (0.693) and APACHE II (0.699). MR-proADM >5.75 nmol/L was uniformly associated with mortality.Conclusion:MR-proADM is a reliable adjunctive prognostic biomarker for sepsis and may enhance early risk stratification when used alongside established scores and biomarkers.

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated host response to infection, continues to be a leading cause of mortality reporting 11 million deaths globally.[12]

Traditional severity scores like Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) due to their reliance on laboratory parameters has limited their practical use in emergency settings.[3] Among biomarkers, lactate above 4 mmol/L and poor clearance rates predicts the adverse outcomes.[4] The updated sepsis guidelines define septic shock as the use of vasopressors to maintain mean arterial pressure ≥65 mmHg with lactate >2 mmol/L in the absence of hypovolemia.[5] Procalcitonin (PCT) released during bacterial infections also differentiates sepsis from noninfectious systemic inflammation.[6]

Adrenomedullin (ADM), a vasodilator and anti-inflammatory peptide, elevates during critical illness.[78] ADM behaves as a “hormokine,” acting hormonally in healthy states and cytokine during inflammatory stress.[9] Direct ADM measurement is technically challenging due to rapid degradation and protein binding. Mid-Regional Proadrenomedullin (MR-proADM), a stable fragment, has emerged as a surrogate marker reflecting ADM activity.[10] Elevated MR-proADM has been observed in sepsis, pneumonia, heart failure, and kidney disease with levels about ten times higher in septic patients with SIRS.[111213]

Research has frequently investigated the scores and biomarkers for sepsis and their correlation but far fewer have examined their capacity to predict mortality, that too short term-14 days mortality owing to rapidly progressing nature of sepsis. Furthermore, due to very less data on Indian population with differences in epidemiology, health infrastructure and comorbidity burden, patients may differ, necessitating population-specific validation.

METHODS

Study design and patient selection

This study, designed as a prospective observational cohort, was carried out in the department of internal medicine at a tertiary care hospital in North India, across 18 months. Assuming an area under receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) of 0.65 for MR-proADM, with 95% confidence and 80% power, and allowing 10% attrition, the sample size was estimated at 114, rounded to 120. Adults (≥18 years) meeting sepsis criteria as per the 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SOFA ≥2) were enrolled from the intensive care unit (ICU) and ward settings after informed consent. Exclusion criteria included chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, chronic heart failure, thyroid malignancy, cardiogenic shock, trauma, burn, and anaphylaxis. All eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were enrolled consecutively from ICU and ward settings to minimize the selection bias. Daily screening of admissions was performed, and no convenience sampling was used. Predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied uniformly to all screened patients to ensure representativeness and reduce the sampling bias.

On admission, demographics, comorbidities, and clinical characteristics were recorded. Blood was drawn from either the radial or femoral artery. For disease severity, SOFA and APACHE II scores were calculated at baseline. Blood sample was divided such that it was processed for lactate by arterial blood gas and PCT was measured using rapid test kit-Radiometer POCT model AQT90 FLEX-an immunochromatographic assay, with testing performed within 15 min of sample collection in EDTA vial according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Another whole blood vial was withdrawn when patients first fulfilled Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria (suspected or confirmed infection with SOFA score ≥2) and was centrifuzed, serum extracted and stored in refrigerator at-20 C for MR-proADM. The samples were obtained as the part of the initial diagnostic evaluation, generally before or during the early phase of therapeutic interventions such as antibiotic administration and hemodynamic resuscitation whenever feasible. Patients were followed for 14 days and classified as survivors or nonsurvivors. 2 elabscience ELISA kits were used sequentially to run 60 samples per slot along with controls (sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit manufactured by Elabscience Biotechnology (Houston, TX, USA). The assay has a detection range of 0.93–59.52 nmol/L and an analytical sensitivity of 0.558 nmol/L. The reported intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation are both <10%, indicating acceptable reproducibility of the assay. Optical density was measured at 450 nm and concentrations were calculated using a standard calibration curve. Standard treatment guidelines were followed throughout the study. The aim was to assess the prognostic value of baseline MR-proADM levels at the time of sepsis recognition, which reflects the patient’s physiological status before significant treatment effects occur.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was 14-day all-cause mortality among patients diagnosed with sepsis. The 14-day timeframe was selected to capture early mortality related to the acute phase of sepsis and to evaluate the prognostic value of admission biomarkers for early risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

The secondary outcomes included the association of MR-proADM levels with disease severity scores (SOFA and APACHE II), comparison of its prognostic performance with other biomarkers (lactate and PCT), and its relationship with important clinical outcomes such as ICU admission, vasopressor requirement, ventilator support, and duration of hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were summarized as the mean ± standard deviation and compared using either independent t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U-test. At the same time, categorical variables were assessed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Prognostic factor regression analysis was used for outcomes, with Proadrenomedullin as the predictor and age, SOFA, APACHE, PCT and lactate as the covariates. Binary logistic regression was performed to see the association of MR ProADM with the outcomes after adjusting for relevant covariates. ROC curve analysis was applied for MR-proADM, lactate, PCT, SOFA, and APACHE II. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was adopted. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 25 and proper STROBE guidelines are followed for study reporting.

RESULTS

A total of 120 patients were enrolled, including 64 survivors and 56 nonsurvivors. Nonsurvivors were significantly older (50.70 ± 14.27 vs. 44.60 ± 15.20 years, P = 0.003). Gender distribution did not differ significantly (survivors: 46.9% male, 53.1% female; non-survivors: 62.5% male, 37.5% female, P = 0.160) [Table 1]. Respiratory infections were the most common source (47% in survivors vs. 72.9% in nonsurvivors, P < 0.001). Genitourinary, CNS, bloodstream, and gastrointestinal infections followed. Diabetes mellitus was the most frequent comorbidity in both survivors and nonsurvivors (32.8% and 50.0%), with no significant difference between groups. Chronic lung disease (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, and bronchiectasis) was more common in nonsurvivors (23.2% vs. 4.7%, P = 0.008).

DISCUSSION

Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and supportive care in sepsis, mortality continues to be alarmingly high due to masquerading features. Timely recognition and risk stratification remains a cornerstone in sepsis management, facilitating appropriate triage and timely intervention.[14] Research has frequently investigated the correlation between sepsis severity and clinical scores or biomarkers, but far fewer have examined their capacity to predict mortality. The current work aimed to evaluate MR-proADM as a prognostic tool for 14-day mortality in sepsis and to compare its performance with SOFA, APACHE II, lactate, and PCT.

Most studies evaluating MR-proADM have been conducted in Western or high-income settings with the lack of robust Indian data. The epidemiology, health infrastructure, and comorbidity burden in Indian patients may differ, necessitating population-specific validation.

The demographic profile of our study cohort, comprising 120 patients, highlighted several noteworthy trends. Age emerged as a statistically significant determinant of outcome, with non-survivors exhibiting a higher mean age (50.70 ± 14.27 years) compared to survivors (44.60 ± 15.20 years; P = 0.003) in line with Banerjee et al. showing aging, due to its association with immunosenescence, reduced physiological reserve and increased comorbidities is risk factor for adverse outcomes in sepsis.[15] Correlating with MR ProADM, the mean age (Years) in the MR Proadrenomedullin ≤0.7 nmol/L group was 40.10 versus 46.42 in >0.7 nmol/L (P = 0.245) although nonsignificant is consistent with finding in study by Odermatt et al. on role of MR Proadm in predicting 10 years all-cause mortality with significant age difference among survivor and non survivors (41 years vs. 73 years).[16]

With regard to comorbidities, diabetes mellitus was the most frequently observed, affecting 34.20% of the study population with the prevalence of 50.0% in nonsurvivors and 32.8% in survivors. This aligns with the findings of study by Jiang et al. which shows high blood sugar were associated with poor outcome.[17] Interestingly, chronic lung disease was significantly more prevalent among nonsurvivors (23.2%) compared to survivors (4.7%), with a P value of 0.008. This finding underlines the susceptibility of individuals with compromised respiratory reserves to poor outcomes in septic states, likely due to impaired pulmonary defenses and reduced resilience against inflammatory injury supported by the study done by Chen et al.[18] In our study, among all recognized systems, respiratory infections are the most common and statistically significant source in both (47.0%) in survivors and (72.9%) in nonsurvivors, respectively (P < 0.001). Several prior studies focused on 28-day or ICU mortality but lacked statistical focus on shorter endpoints like 14-day mortality, which can be more relevant for early triage and clinical decision-making. The central finding of our study was the significant elevation of MR-proADM levels in nonsurvivors (2.48 ± 1.93 nmol/L) relative to survivors (1.43 ± 0.78 nmol/L; P < 0.001). The prognostic capability of MR-proADM was validated by ROC curve analysis, which yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.767 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.683–0.850) surpassing the predictive capacity of both PCT (AUC 0.693) and creatinine (AUC 0.588) along with traditional scoring system like SOFA and APACHE II. This aligns with the results of the previous studies done by lundberg et al. and caironi et al. on 30-day mortality associated with MR-proADM.[1920] Similar is reported by Spoto et al. that MR-proADM levels ≥3.15 nmol/L were associated with nonsurvivors, while levels ≥2.0 nmol/L correlated with a SOFA score ≥2.[21] Similarly, in one meta-analysis, 24 studies included 6730 adult patients (1208 nonsurvivors and 5522 survivors) I from which in adult cohort, pooled mean difference between nonsurvivors and survivors of MR-proADM was 2.55 mmol/L (95% CI: 1.95–3.15) with higher values in patients with septic shock (4.25 mmol/L; 95% CI, 2.23–6.26 mmol/L) than in patients with sepsis (1.77 mmol/L; 95% CI: 1.11–2.44 mmol/L).[7]

Although the difference in vasopressor use between survivors (26.6%) and nonsurvivors (30.4%) was not statistically significant (P = 0.645), this parameter still reflected a trend toward greater illness severity in nonsurvivors aligning with caironi et al. findings.[7] Caironi et al.(Chest 2017) reported that admission bio-ADM stratified 30-day mortality and predicted need for hemodynamic support (Youden-derived thresholds ~70-108 pg/mL; ORs for vasopressor/CRRT and mortality were significant), supporting the concordant prognostic signal across ADM assays, even though absolute concentrations cannot be directly compared because of different analytes and assay units.[20]

In our cohort, 1.69 nmol/L is the best cut-off of MR-proADM cut-off for predicting 14-day mortality with 70% sensitivity and 72% specificity and mean SOFA score is significantly higher among nonsurvivors (10.43 ± 3.27) than survivors (8.02 ± 2.92), P < 0.001. This finding is comparable with Haag et al. (TRIAGE study) where 1.75 nmol/L is the best cutoff of MR-ProADM for 30-day mortality prediction, with 81% sensitivity and 75% specificity and combining it with the SOFA score enhanced its predictive accuracy, improving the AUC from 0.81 to 0.87 and enabling better risk stratification.[22] Similarly, APACHE II scores followed a similar trend, being markedly elevated in nonsurvivors (23.71 ± 7.29) versus survivors (18.59 ± 7.12, P < 0.001).

Quantitatively, our findings align with prior MR-proADM studies summarized by Angeletti et al. (2015, APMIS 123:736–749), where AUROC values for sepsis mortality prediction ranged from 0.70 to 0.85 and optimal cut-offs between 1.8 and 2.5 nmol/L compared to AUROC was 0.767 (95% CI 0.683–0.850) with an optimal threshold of 1.69 nmol/L (sensitivity 70% and specificity 72%) in our study. The mean MR-proADM concentration difference between survivors and nonsurvivors (1.43 ± 0.78 vs. 2.48 ± 1.93 nmol/L) in our study matches the magnitude reported in Western cohorts (≈1–3 nmol/L gap). While our AUROC was slightly lower than the 0.81–0.83 reported in larger European studies, this difference is likely attributable to the inclusion of ward patients and the use of a 14-day endpoint instead of 28-day mortality.[23] 0.767 AUROC in our study also falls within the performance range reported for bio-ADM in ICU sepsis cohorts (AUCs ≈ 0.76–0.83) in Caironi et al. (Chest 2017).

Nonsurvivors had a mean PCT level of 22.3 ng/mL compared to 5.95 ng/mL in survivors, with 19 ng/mL identified as the optimal cut-off for predicting 14-day mortality (AUC 0.693). This result is consistent with prior evidence supporting PCT as both a diagnostic and prognostic marker in sepsis. This is supported by Jafari et al. and Demirdal et al. where PCT range of 5 to >16 ng/ml is associated with 5.7% to 79% mortality rate and >10 ng/ml is significantly associated with severe bacterial infections or septic shock and mortality.[2425] Tang et al. also found that PCT levels >8.77 ng/mL had a positive predictive value of 0.96 for Gram-negative septic shock, which corresponds closely with the elevated levels seen in our nonsurvivor group.[26] Thus, our findings reinforce PCT’s role not only as an indicator of bacterial sepsis but also as a robust predictor of mortality, supporting its use in triage and early therapeutic decisions.

Serum lactate also showed a strong correlation with outcome. Nonsurvivors had a mean lactate level of 3.40 mmol/L versus 1.85 mmol/L in survivors, a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001), with an AUC of 0.807 for mortality prediction. Patients with lactate >4 mmol/L accounted for 28.6% of nonsurvivors, whereas 60.9% of survivors had lactate ≤2 mmol/L. In the study by Shapiro et al., patients with lactate levels ≥4.0 mmol/L had a mortality rate of 28.4%, significantly higher than those with lower levels.[27] A study by Villar et al. showed the most predictive cut off of mortality at levels of ≥4.0 mmol/L.[28] Our findings, where nonsurvivors had mean lactate levels >3.0 mmol/L, closely mirror these prior observations. Notably, even in the absence of overt hypotension or shock, elevated lactate in our cohort was associated with significantly worse outcomes, which supports its utility as an early prognostic marker, in line with the Surviving Sepsis Campaign.

There was a gap in understanding how MR-proADM levels correlate with hospital resource utilization, such as ICU care needs, ventilator need, and duration of hospitalization. Our study addressed this by showing patients with higher MR-proADM (>2.35 nmol/L) values were more likely to require ICU care (P < 0.001) in 69.9% of non survivors, and prolonged hospital stay was observed in 59.4% of survivors (P = 0.065). These associations support the findings by Spoto et al. who reported that MR-proADM levels ≥3.33 ng/mL were predictive of ICU admissions.[29]

Our results are consistent with previous studies, reaffirming the prognostic significance of lactate, PCT, and MR-proADM in sepsis. Nevertheless, the distinct demographic features of the Indian cohort – characterized by a younger population and differing comorbidity patterns – highlight the need for region-specific validation.

Limitations

First, the sample size was relatively modest, which may limit the statistical power and precision of the estimates. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings or populations. Third, as an observational study, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded despite standardized data collection and predefined inclusion criteria. In addition, MR-proADM levels were measured only at admission, and serial measurements were not performed, which could potentially provide further insight into disease progression and treatment response. Finally, external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is necessary to confirm the prognostic utility of MR-proADM in diverse patient populations.

From a practical perspective, the implementation of MR-proADM testing in routine clinical practice requires consideration of cost, availability, and laboratory infrastructure. While MR-proADM assays are commercially available and can be performed using standard ELISA-based platforms, their availability may still be limited in many healthcare centers, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. However, as biomarker-based risk stratification gains increasing clinical relevance, broader availability and cost reduction may improve feasibility in the future. In the current study, MR-proADM demonstrated prognostic performance comparable to established clinical scores, suggesting that it may serve as a useful adjunct biomarker when available.

CONCLUSION

Despite advances in critical care, sepsis remains a major cause of death worldwide, with the burden being particularly severe in countries such as India due to delayed recognition and resource limitations. This study assessed 120 ward and ICU patients and found that MR-proADM levels at admission were significantly elevated in nonsurvivors (P < 0.001) and correlated closely with SOFA, APACHE II, lactate, and PCT. While clinical scores remain essential for assessing organ dysfunction, MR-proADM may provide additional pathophysiological insight into endothelial dysfunction and disease severity in sepsis. Its practicality, affordability, and strong prognostic accuracy highlight MR-proADM as a valuable adjunctive tool alongside conventional severity scores rather than a standalone prognostic tool for early mortality risk assessment in resource-constrained healthcare environments. Larger multicenter studies are needed to further validate its role in routine clinical practice.
