Collective Response and Navigation Dynamics of Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense under Rotating Magnetic Fields
Authors: Danny Villanueva, Alicia G Gubieda, Alfredo García‐Arribas, Ana Abad‐Diaz‐De‐Cerio, Jorge Feuchtwanger, David de Cos, M Luisa Fdez‐Gubieda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnetotactic bacteria (MTB) are aquatic microorganisms that biomineralize magnetic nanoparticles called magnetosomes, organizing them into chains that enable navigation along geomagnetic field lines. Their self‐propulsion, magnetic responsiveness, and preference for low‐oxygen environments make them promising candidates as biohybrid microrobots for biomedical and environmental applications. However, controlling large populations of MTB simultaneously remains a significant challenge. This study analyzes over 30 000 trajectories of Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense (MSR‐1) under rotating magnetic fields (RMF) of varying strengths (0 − 1000 µT) and frequencies (0.1−0.5Hz) to characterize individual and collective motility behaviors. Trajectories shift from rectilinear to circular with increasing field strength, while collective alignment emerges above 250 µT. At 1000 µT and 0.1Hz, up to 29.3% of bacteria align in the NorthSeeker (NS) direction and 23% in the South‐Seeker (SS) direction. Angular dispersion decreases from ≈ 38.2° to ≈14.7° with increasing field strength, whereas higher RMF frequencies significantly reduce alignment. Swimming velocity remains stable across most conditions, showing a robust bimodal distribution centered near 21.5 and 45 µm s−1, with a deviation only under highest tested RMF condition. These findings reveal a collective dynamic dependent on the frequency and strength of the magnetic field and highlight that the individual response cannot be straightforwardly translated to collective dynamics.Keywords: collective motion, Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense (MSR‐1), magnetotactic bacteria, North‐seeker, South‐seeker, rotating magnetic field (RMF)

Introduction

Magnetotactic bacteria (MTB) are unique self‐propelled aquatic microorganisms capable of aligning passively and navigating actively along the geomagnetic field lines, a behavior known as magnetotaxis.[ 1 ] This ability allows MTB to efficiently locate and remain within their optimal microaerobic or anaerobic environments, typically found in oxic–anoxic transition zones (OATZs). In addition to responding to magnetic fields, MTB exhibit aerotaxis, moving along oxygen gradients to maintain favorable conditions for survival.

The key to their magnetotactic behavior lies in specialized organelles called magnetosomes. These organelles are composed of a ferrimagnetic core, either magnetite (Fe3O4) or greigite(Fe3S4), enveloped within a lipid bilayer membrane. The morphology of the magnetosomes is remarkably diverse, including cube‐octahedral, elongated prismatic and bullet‐shaped forms, with sizes ranging from 35 to 120 nm.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] These dimensions ensure that the magnetic core remains stable at room temperature and retains a single magnetic domain. The arrangement of magnetosomes into chains maximizes their magnetic efficiency, allowing MTB to effectively behave as biological compasses.[ 1 ]

MTB's response to magnetic fields makes these microorganisms highly promising for use as bio‐nanorobots. The combination of remote‐guidance potential, self‐propulsion, and natural affinity for hypoxic environments are often found in tumor regions places MTB among the most advanced candidates for targeted biomedical applications.[ 2 ] This unique set of features opens novel possibilities in therapies such as magnetic hyperthermia,[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] providing localized heating to treat cancer; as well as in drug transport and targeted delivery,[ 8 , 9 ] allowing precise administration of therapeutic agents through magnetic control.

To fully harness the capabilities of MTB, achieving precise control over their motility is essential, and one of the greatest challenges in guiding MTB is understanding their collective response. Various factors influence this behavior, for example: environment,[ 10 ] bacterial concentration,[ 11 ] oxygen gradients,[ 12 ] magnetic field configuration,[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] flagella morphology,[ 16 ] etc. Addressing these factors is key to advancing applications that rely on coordinated MTB dynamics.

This study focuses on investigating the dynamics of magnetotactic bacteria under rotating magnetic fields (RMF). While the motility of MTB under varying magnetic fields has been extensively studied both theoretically and experimentally, these works have predominantly focused on characterizing individual bacteria motion.[ 14 , 17 ] However, their dynamic collective response to time‐varying magnetic fields remains unclear, particularly when considering thousands of bacteria trajectories.

To investigate the motility of MTB and gain a deeper understanding of their behavior, we examined the collective response of MTB to a rotating magnetic field (RMF) configuration, which allows for a precise control over the turning and directional motion of MTB, enabling the selection of specific trajectories within microfluidic devices. Furthermore, under certain RMF conditions, MTB can be induced to perform localized rotations without a net displacement,[ 18 ] positioning RMF as a potential tool for concentrating MTB in specific locations. Recent studies also suggest that combining RMF with other magnetic field configurations enhances MTB infiltration and movement through biological systems,[ 19 ] expanding their potential in biomedical applications. These considerations justify the interest of studying the collective motion of MTB under rotating magnetic fields.

In this work, the strain Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense (MSR‐1) is used as a motility agent. MSR‐1 is one of the most studied MTB because it is easy to cultivate, which allows its genetic and physiological properties to be well‐characterized.[ 20 ] MSR‐1 biomineralize magnetosomes composed of cube‐octahedral magnetite with an average size of ≈ 40 − 45 nm. Furthermore, MSR‐1 organizes these magnetosomes in a single helical chain composed of around 25 magnetosomes along the main axis of the bacterium.[ 21 ] This arrangement optimizes their magnetic dipole moment along the longitudinal axis of the cell and facilitates efficient navigation under external magnetic fields.

MSR‐1 is characterized by a spiral morphology, with a length of 4 µm and a width of 0.5 µm. Furthermore, like Magnetospirillum magneticum, they are bipolar (or amphitrichous) bacteria, presenting a single flagellum at each end of the body. As a result, they are able to develop versatile motion patterns such as run‐stop, run‐tumble, run‐deviation, run‐reverse, and oscillatory motion among others, enabling the bacteria to adapt their trajectories in response to external cues such as oxygen gradients and magnetic fields.[ 12 , 16 , 22 ] This adaptability is critical in their natural habitats, where they align with geomagnetic fields to locate optimal growth conditions near the oxic–anoxic interface. Although the wild MSR‐1 species present north‐ seeking polarity, the cultured strain presents a lack of swimming polarity behavior, as it can navigate parallel or antiparallel to the direction of the applied magnetic field, thus exhibiting both north‐seeking (NS) and south‐seeking (SS) navigation modes, respectively.

This work explores the collective response of Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense (MSR‐1) under rotating magnetic fields (RMF) and is organized as follows. In Section 2 we present the morphological characterization of the bacteria, including cell dimensions and magnetosome per cell obtained via transmission electron microscopy. In Section 3, we describe the experimental setup developed to apply RMF with controlled strength and frequency, along with the custom software used for segmentation and tracking of bacteria from optical microscopy videos. Sections 4 and 5 provide a qualitative analysis of MSR‐1 both at the individual and collective motility under varying RMF strengths and frequencies, respectively. In Section 6, we quantify aligned bacterial populations, evaluating the proportion of North‐Seeker and South‐Seeker cells and their angular dispersion. In Section 7, we study the velocity distributions of the entire bacterial population, analyzing how they are affected under different RMF conditions. Together, these sections build a comprehensive understanding of MSR‐1 dynamics under rotating magnetic stimulation.

Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense (MSR‐1), Transmission Electron Microscopy Characterization

To ensure the robustness and reproducibility of our study on the response of Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense (MSR‐1) to rotating magnetic fields (RMF), all experiments were conducted using three independent bacterial cultures. In the qualitative part of the manuscript, we present results from a single culture to highlight individual and collective navigation trends. For the quantitative analyses, averaged values from all three biological replicates are reported. Detailed results from each replicate are provided in the Supporting Information (Pages 1–4), demonstrating the consistency and reliability of the experimental findings.

A comprehensive understanding of the response of MSR‐1 to RMF requires detailed knowledge of the morphological and magnetic characteristics of the bacterial population. Experimental Setup and Image Processing Methodology

In order to explore the collective response of the MSR‐1 strain under a rotating magnetic field (RMF), the following experimental system was set up, see Figure 2a. It consists of a three‐Helmholtz coil (three‐axis) configuration to control the magnetic field, an optical microscopy system to record the magnetotactic bacteria, and a microfluidic channel to control the environmental conditions and contain the bacteria during the recording time.

a) Schematic representation of the experimental setup, showing the three‐Helmholtz coil system arranged along the three principal axes (x, y, z), the microfluidic device containing a bacterial suspension and the objective lens used for all motility experiments, representing the microscope system. b) Representation of the magnetic field vector ,B⃗, and its angle, θ B, in the plane (x, y) following a counterclockwise rotation around the z‐axis. The flow direction remains constant along the negative y direction. c) Experimental non‐motile MSR‐1 trajectories colored according to their recording times between t = 0 to 25 s. d) Representation of MSR‐1 bacteria at two consecutive time points, ti and t i + 1​, used to define the bacteria navigation direction, θ b, the angle of the magnetic field, θ B, and navigation velocity, v⃗. e) Illustration of the axial polarity behavior of MSR‐1, where North‐seeking and South‐seeking bacteria swim following the magnetic field orientation and its opposite orientation, respectively. The external magnetic field, MSR‐1 swimming velocity and magnetic moment were denoted by B⃗, v⃗ and m⃗, respectively.

We used a cell concentration of 108 cells mL−1, which allowed for the observation of ≈ 500 − 900 individual bacteria within the microscope's field of view (625 µm × 501 µm) at any time. With a recording time of 25 s, this configuration resulted in the successful capture of up to 30 000 MSR‐1 trajectories in some cases (for more details, see Experimental Section/Methods). Additionally, all MSR‐1 bacterial suspensions contained both motile and non‐motile bacteria, being the last one less than 5% of total population. While both groups were detected and tracked, their trajectories were analyzed separately for different purposes. Motile MSR‐1 trajectories were used to assess the effects of the applied RMF on bacterial navigation, while non‐motile ones were used to characterize the flow velocity within the microchannel.

The three‐Helmholtz‐coil configuration enables full control of the applied magnetic field in all possible directions. All the parameters of the applied magnetic field, such as its strength, B, direction, θ B, and frequency, f, were controlled through a custom‐made program.

We cancelled the geomagnetic field (≈ 50 µT) and restricted our working region to the (x, y) focal plane of the microscope, with the x‐axis oriented perpendicular to both the flow and the microchannel walls, as shown in Figure 2a,b. The field along the z‐direction was cancelled, thus exclusively conditioning bacterial motion within the focal plane. This allowed the observation periods of the trajectories to be maximized.

We evaluated the effects of the RMF on the navigation of MSR‐1 by varying the frequency, f, and strength, B, of the applied magnetic field. The experiments were conducted using all combinations of frequencies f = 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5 Hz with magnetic field strengths B = 0, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 µT. These values were selected based on preliminary observations, which revealed different patterns in the collective navigation behavior of MSR‐1: at constant frequency, bacterial alignment increased with magnetic field strength. In turn, under a constant field strength, increasing frequencies resulted in diminished alignment, and, depending on the frequency, collective alignment could be completely disrupted, even when the field strengths were much stronger than the Earth's magnetic field.

MSR‐1 suspensions were placed in linear microchannels, as shown in Figure 2a, measuring 100 µm in height and 1 mm in width, allowing precise control over environmental conditions. All motility experiments were performed at the mid‐height of the microchannel, 50 µm above the bottom surface. This positioning minimized interactions with surfaces, avoiding effects such as amplified thermal fluctuations,[ 23 ] hydrodynamic coupling‐induced circular motion[ 24 , 25 , 26 ] and other surface‐related hydrodynamic interactions. As a result, we minimized any potential undesired effects and recorded exclusively the response of MSR‐1 to the fluid environment and the external RMF.

The flow conditions in the microchannels were characterized using non‐motile MTB as flow probes. Figure 2c shows their experimental trajectories under the flow, colored according to their recording time from t = 0 to 25 s. As trajectories begin and end at different timestamps, depending on the time interval each bacterium remains in the field of view, the color of each trajectory may differ. Notably, long‐duration trajectories display a clear color gradient along their path, representing the temporal progression.

Non‐motile bacteria exhibit an erratic motion due to Brownian motion, combined with passive displacement caused by the drag flow. Detecting these motion patterns allows us to distinguish them from motile MSR‐1. Stable flow velocities of 6 − 8 µm s−1 were maintained, corresponding to a low Reynolds number (Re = 1.5 × 10−3), thus confirming laminar flow (for more details, see the Supporting Information, Pages 5–8).

The navigation of the bacteria under different RMF configurations was recorded with an inverted microscope at 20 frames per second, which allowed sufficient temporal resolution to capture the full diversity of MSR‐1 navigational patterns. To perform motility studies, we developed a program (called BacMov) for the automatic segmentation, detection, and tracking of motile and non‐motile magnetotactic bacteria in the recorded footage. This software extracts several properties of individual bacteria, including the position of their centroid (geometric center of the bacterium), which we use to reconstruct trajectories and calculate swimming velocities, as shown in Figure 2d. Using advanced algorithms for the detection and tracking of bacteria, BacMov has allowed us to explore the collective dynamics of MSR‐1 under rotating magnetic fields, offering new insights into their navigation and motility behaviors (for further detail, see the Supporting Information, Pages 9–12).

According to the reference frame defined in Figure 2d, we establish the navigation direction of the bacteria, θ b, as the angle formed between the displacement vector—connecting the centroid of a bacterium at consecutive time points—and the x‐axis of the chosen coordinate system. All motility experiments were designed to maintain a consistent flow direction, thereby maintaining the same navigation angle for nonotile bacteria, approximately at θ b = 270°, corresponding to the negative y‐axis direction, see Figure 2b.

Using motile MSR‐1, we identify and distinguish two bacteria swimming modes, those swimming in the direction of the magnetic field, north‐seeking mode (NS), and those in the opposite direction, south‐seeking mode (SN), as shown in Figure 2e. In both cases, the magnetic moment remains aligned parallel to the applied magnetic field, corresponding to a state of minimum magnetostatic energy. This distinction is dynamic rather than inherent to each bacterium, as they switch between the NS and SS navigation modes, enabled by to the axial polarity of MSR‐1.

Collective Motility Behavior Under Different Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF) Strengths

In this section, we analyze the motion of motile MSR‐1 bacteria under a RMF at a fixed frequency of f = 0.1 Hz for different magnetic field strengths,B = 0, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 µT. The influence of the RMF is evaluated at both the local and global levels: individually, by examining the most representative trajectories, and collectively, by assessing population trends in relation to navigation direction and time.

Figure 3 shows representative trajectories of the MSR‐1 strain under an RMF with strengths of B = 0, 250 and 1000 µT, at a frequency of f = 0.1 Hz. Plots corresponding to the rest of the applied magnetic fields are presented in the Supporting Information. Again, bacterial trajectories are color‐coded according to the recording times at which they enter and exit the field of view. Short‐duration trajectories appear as quasi‐monochromatic lines, while long‐duration ones exhibit a color gradient reflecting their temporal progression.

Representative trajectories recorded under an RMF at a constant frequency, f = 0.1 Hz, and different magnetic field strengths a) B = 0 µT, b) B = 250 µT, and c) B = 1000 µT. Trajectories are colored based on the recording time, from t = 0 to 25 s, as indicated by the colour bar. In the inset zoomed regions, distinct motility patterns are highlighted alongside the trajectory plots.

At B = 0 µT, the strain MSR‐1 apparently moves without any preferred direction, following rectilinear trajectories in all directions, as shown in Figure 3a. Closed circular displacements are rarely observed. This behavior reflects the bacteria surface exploration strategy: without a guiding reference, they favor straight‐line trajectories to cover a larger area. In contrast, closed circular movements would limit their exploration efficiency. The same behavior is observed for B = 100 µT, with rectilinear trajectories being predominant (see Figure S8a of the Supporting Information).

Under a magnetic field of B = 250 µT, as shown in Figure 3b, the presence of curvilinear trajectories becomes noticeable. However, rectilinear trajectories remain predominant, suggesting the magnetic field is not strong enough to fully orient MSR‐1 motion. Some long‐duration trajectories, such as the one highlighted in Figure 3c, display a color gradient that reflects their temporal progression, providing a clear visual example of how bacterial motion evolves over time. Increasing field strength does not appear to reduce the diversity of navigation patterns, such as run tumble and zigzag motions, appear more distinctly compared to lower magnetic field strengths.

For higher magnetic field strengths, B = 500 µT (Figure S8b, Supporting Information) and 1000 µT (Figure 3c), curvilinear trajectories become increasingly dominant over rectilinear ones. Additionally, increasing field strength does not appear to reduce the diversity of navigation patterns, as reverse motion, zigzag trajectories, and other behaviors remain observable.

Furthermore, under these conditions, we can distinguish two bacteria swimming modalities: those swimming in the direction of the field, NS, and those swimming in the opposite direction, SS. The presence of a flagellum at each cell pole facilitates the transition between these two modes. This dual behavior is common in laboratory cultures unless a prior selection is made, as reported in other works.[ 12 ]

The collective motion tendency was studied using 2D bacterial population histograms as a function of the navigation direction, θ b, and the recording time, from, t = 0 to 25 s. Such population‐based analyses allow us to visualize trends in bacterial alignment under RMF and also situate our observations within broader studies of bioinspired magnetic control and magnetic materials characterization.[ 11 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] Figure 4 provides a representative example using B = 1000 µT and f = 0.1 Hz, showing, a 3D view of a bacteria population histogram, its 2D projection and the direction of the RMF, θ B, over time. This combined visualization enables direct comparison between bacterial orientation and the applied RMF. Each bin corresponds to a population of bacteria within a specific range of navigation directions (5.7°) and experimental time (0.3 s) intervals, with the color bar ranges from white to dark blue to indicate the population of each bin. Both θ b and θ B share the same reference frame and the same angular domain values, [0, 360°], as shown in Figure 2d. In this type of representation, if MSR‐1 bacteria respond to the periodic action of the RMF, periodic patterns are expected to emerge in the 2D population histograms as θ b follows θ B, which evolves linearly over time with a slope equal to 2πf, as defined in Equation (2), and illustrated in Figure 4b,c.

Three‐panel visualization of MSR‐1 population dynamics under a rotating magnetic field with a strength B = 1000 µT at f = 0.1 Hz. a) 3D view and b) 2D projection of bacterial population histogram as a function of navigation direction θ b and recording time t. c) Applied magnetic field direction as a function of time.

The bacterial navigation pattern, θ b, as a function of time, t, closely follows the angular progression of the RMF, θ B. At each time, both NS and SS trajectories are observed, corresponding to motion parallel and antiparallel to the magnetic field. For example, at t = 0 s, bacteria are found navigating at θ b = 0°, NS, and 180°, SS, as shown in Figure 4b.

Figure 5 shows 2D bacterial population histograms at constant frequency, f = 0.1 Hz, for varying magnetic field strengths between B = 0 and 1000 µT. As shown in Figure 5a, for B = 0 µT, motile bacterial population exhibits a uniform distribution in the histogram. MSR‐1 population displays random motion despite the presence of a flow with a velocity of ∼ 7 µm s−1, with bacteria swimming in all possible directions over time. This observation is consistent with the previous trajectory analysis and indicates that the MSR‐1 strain relies on an external magnetic field to establish a defined navigational route.

2D bacterial population histograms as a function of navigation direction, θ b, and recording time, t, for a RMF of 0.1 Hz and different magnetic field strengths: a) B = 0 µT, b) B = 100 µT, c) B = 250 µT, d) B = 500 µT, and e) B = 1000 µT. MSR‐1 swimming polarities NS and SS were marked with red and green arrows in (c), as reference. The color bar indicates the bacterial population in each bin of the histogram.

Motion randomness is broken when a RMF is applied, as shown in Figure 5b. At a low magnetic field strength, such as B = 100 µT, an emerging pattern can be observed, with a slight concentration of the bacterial population between 180° and 360°. However, this pattern remains poorly defined, suggesting that a stronger magnetic field is required to visualize collective motion.

Figure 5c shows that at higher RMF strengths, such as B = 250 µT, a percentage of motile bacterial population move in an organized manner under the influence of the RMF, describing a linear pattern in the 2D bacterial population histogram. However, this organized motion is not maintained by all bacteria, since a significant fraction appears unaligned with the field and contributes to the background signal in the histogram.

To visualize the effects of RMF, Figure 5c was marked with red and green arrows to indicate NS and SS swimming navigation modes. Note that the red line specifically represents the direction of the rotating magnetic field. There is a clear overlap between the applied magnetic field orientation and navigation pattern lines, suggesting a short‐time response to the magnetic field direction change.

As shown in Figure 5d,e, for higher magnetic field strengths, B ≥ 500 µT, narrow and well‐defined line patterns emerge over a constant white background population. Magnetic field strengths of this magnitude can overcome the inherent stochasticity and variability in populations, ensuring synchronized motion and establishing a clearly defined collective navigation path aligned with the field orientation.

In summary, a collective response of motile MSR‐1 bacteria is observed only for RMF strengths of 250 µT or higher, far exceeding the geomagnetic field. Under these conditions, three distinct swimming behaviors can be distinguished. We identify bacteria that, at times, move outside of the route defined by the applied RMF. In addition, we identify bacteria migrating both parallel and antiparallel to the RMF direction, following the NS and SS navigation modes, respectively. As pointed out above, it is important to highlight that these navigation modes are dynamic, since individual bacteria switch between the NS and SS modes. This transition occurs through various motion strategies, such as run‐reverse, run‐tumble and others, as shown in Figure 3. This result motivates the next section, where we focus on how varying the frequency of the RMF affects the collective behavior of MSR‐1.

Collective Motility Behavior Under Different Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF) Frequencies

Many questions arise regarding the generality of previous results at higher RMF frequencies. For instance, one of the most relevant studies on this topic, developed by Erglis et al. 2007,[ 14 ] evaluates the effects of a rotating magnetic field on the MSR‐1 species. The study investigates how the bacteria trajectories change with increasing frequency while maintaining a constant field strength. At individual scale, it observes that bacteria follow the magnetic field even at frequencies as high as f = 0.62 Hz for RMF strengths of H = 10 Oe (equivalent to B = 1000 µT).

In this section, we evaluate the generality of the bacteria response to RMF using different frequencies but at a collective scale, analyzing up to 30 000 motile bacteria trajectories per experiment in some cases. Some of the most representative motions are shown in Figure 6 , in which a RMF of B = 1000 µT is applied at different frequency values, f = 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 Hz.

Representative trajectories recorded under a RMF with a constant magnetic field strength of B = 1000 µT at frequencies: a) f = 0.1 Hz, b) f = 0.2 Hz, and c) f = 0.5 Hz. The trajectories are colored based on their recording time, from t = 0 to 25 s, as indicated by the color bar. In the inset zooms, distinct motility patterns are highlighted alongside the trajectory plots.

At low frequencies, f = 0.1 and 0.2 Hz, as shown in Figure 6a,b, respectively, MSR‐1 motile bacteria predominantly exhibit circular trajectories, suggesting a response to magnetic torque. This behavior is consistent for both swimming modes, NS and SS, with occasional transitions between them. However, at higher frequencies, f = 0.5 Hz, as shown in Figure 6c, trajectories become predominantly rectilinear. This finding suggests that the bacteria have surpassed a threshold, whereby they are no longer capable of synchronizing their navigation with the RMF.

The potential for modelling the navigation of MTB under a RMF has been explored in some research studies to date. These developments are based on the balance between magnetic torque and viscous torque, and predict two scenarios with respect to a critical frequency, fc [ 14 , 30 ]: (f < fc ) synchronous regime, where bacteria follow the magnetic field describing circular trajectories, and (f > fc ) non‐synchronous regime, where bacteria alternate their navigation between aligning and misaligning with the RMF.

This theoretical framework accurately predicts experimentally observed behavior at low frequencies, such as f=0.1 and 0.2Hz, where we primarily observe circular trajectories consistent with the synchronous regime. Likewise, the characteristic paths detected at f=0.5Hz, such as peanut‐shaped loops, and others, are also predicted by Erglis’ model in the asynchronous regime, above the critical frequency. However, these trajectories are only observed in isolated instances. Instead, most bacteria exhibit rectilinear motion in all directions, even under strong fields such as B = 1000 µT. This suggests that, at f=0.5Hz, asynchronous behavior is restricted to specific cases, highlighting both the complexity of collective bacterial motion and the limitations of current theoretical models.

The collective motion behavior was analyzed using 2D bacterial population histograms plotted as a function of navigation direction, θ b, and recording time, t. Figure 7 shows these results for a constant magnetic field strength of B = 1000 µT, at frequencies f = 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 Hz.

2D bacterial population histograms as a function of navigation direction, θ b, and recording time, t, for RMF at constant magnetic field strength of B = 1000 µT and frequencies: a) f = 0.1 Hz, b) f = 0.2 Hz, and c) f = 0.5 Hz. MSR‐1 swimming polarity NS and SS were marked with red and green arrows in (a), as reference. The color bar indicates the bacterial population in each bin of the histogram.

As shown in Figure 7a,b, the characteristic line patterns are still observed. As expected, more lines are observed at f = 0.2 Hz, and they are spaced closer together with a higher slope due to the reduced periodicity associated with increasing the frequency (Equation (2)). In contrast, at higher frequencies, f = 0.5 Hz, a threshold has been surpassed. The characteristic RMF pattern lines observed at lower frequencies vanish, as shown in Figure 7c. This result indicates that the MSR‐1 strain is unable to synchronize its navigation with f = 0.5 Hz.

At lower magnetic field strengths, we found that for f = 0.2 Hz, the bacterial response is similar to that observed at f = 0.1 Hz, requiring at least B = 250 µT to produce the characteristic line patterns indicative of collective behavior, as shown in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). Similarly, no collective response is observed at f = 0.5 Hz, at any magnetic field strength tested in the present study, as shown in Figure S11 (Supporting Information).

Aligned Population Under Rotating Magnetic Fields (RMF)

In this section, we quantify the effects of the RMF in terms of the angular dispersion and aligned population around the field direction. This information can be derived from the bacterial population histograms presented above, wherein the line‐shaped patterns represent the alignment of bacteria, and the line thickness reflects their angular dispersion around the field direction. However, factors such as the slope of the pattern lines, periodicity, and truncation at 0 and 360° boundaries, complicated fitting procedures to extract angular dispersion and aligned population values.

To simplify the problem, the experimental magnetic field direction, θ B, was subtracted from all motile bacteria navigation directions, θ b, at each time point, thus obtaining instantaneous angular dispersion, Δθ = θ B − θ b, and reducing the complex multiline pattern to two straight lines. By subtracting the contribution of the RMF direction, bacteria that align with the field are expected to exhibit relative motion centered around Δθ = 0 and 180°, corresponding to navigation in the NS and SS modes, respectively.

Figure 8 shows the instantaneous angular dispersions for different RMF strengths and frequencies, highlighting their influence on bacterial navigation. For clarity, the data correspond to a single representative replicate. Additional results from all three independent replicates are provided in the Supporting Information. Two distributions centered around Δθ = 0 and 180° are observed over a uniform background for f = 0.1 and 0.2 Hz, indicating the coexistence of NS and SS navigation modes, as shown in Figures 8a,b, respectively.

Normalized instantaneous angular dispersion (Δθ) histograms under RMF at frequencies: a) f = 0.1 Hz, b) f = 0.2 Hz, and c) f = 0.5 Hz for different magnetic field strengths B = 250, 500, and 1000 µT. The solid line in (a) and (b) is the fitted function of two Gaussians obtained by nonlinear least squares. The shaded region around Δθ = 0 and Δθ = 180° in (a) and (b) marks an angular interval of ± 25° used to estimate the population of bacteria aligned with the RMF. The correlation coefficients for all Gaussian fittings are consistently greater than 0.98 (R > 0.98). All data shown correspond to a single representative replicate.

For the highest frequency applied, f = 0.5 Hz, the probability density is constant for all Δθ values, which clearly indicates that no collective synchronization of the bacteria with the RMF at any magnetic field strengths is taking place at this frequency, as shown in Figure 8c. These results indicate that frequency is a critical parameter, as it can suppress collective navigation even at magnetic field strengths much higher than the geomagnetic field, such as B = 1000 µT.

For f = 0.1 and 0.2 Hz, the instantaneous angular dispersion, Δθ, was fitted through nonlinear least squares fitting to two Gaussian functions centered around Δθ = 0° and Δθ = 180°. The standard deviation of each Gaussian, reflecting the dispersion of the bacterial population around the RMF, was extracted for each biological replicate. The average standard deviations, 〈σ NS〉 and 〈σ SS〉, obtained across the three biological replicates for all combinations of B and f, are presented in Table 1 . These parameters quantify the degree of bacterial alignment with the RMF. The main conclusion that can be drawn is that the standard deviation decreases with higher magnetic field strengths and smaller frequencies.

The bacterial populations maximized at higher field strength and lower RMF frequencies. In contrast, the lowest populations, 〈P NS〉 = 17.2% for NS and 〈P SS〉 = 15% for SS, were found at B = 250 µT and f = 0.2 Hz. These results reveal that the NS navigation mode dominates over SS, this preference is probably correlated with the natural NS polarity of the MSR‐1 strain.

These experimental observations highlight that the collective response of the bacterial population cannot be directly inferred from single‐cell behavior, which calls for a closer examination of the factors underlying the differences between individual and collective dynamics. The distinction can be understood by considering the intrinsic heterogeneity of the bacterial population. While theoretical models of MTB motion often assume idealized bacteria with uniform physical parameters, in practice each cell exhibits variability in length, width, and number of magnetosomes, as shown by the distributions in Figure 1. These morphological differences translate into a distribution of magnetic moments and, consequently, a distribution of critical frequencies across the population. As the rotation frequency of the external field increases, more bacteria surpass their individual critical frequencies and lose alignment, leading to a progressive decrease in the fraction of aligned cells. This explains, for example, why at f = 0.5 Hz even under strong fields (B = 1000 µT), a substantial fraction of bacteria swim rectilinearly rather than following the rotating field. In addition, our results show that the critical frequency is not a fixed parameter but depends on the applied magnetic field strength: at a constant rotation frequency, stronger fields increase the proportion of bacteria that remain aligned.

On the other hand, one phenomenon that the theoretical framework does not capture is the predominance of rectilinear trajectories observed when the critical frequency is exceeded, instead of the predicted characteristic navigation patterns. This behavior may be related to the bacteria's biological needs: if the external field rotates too fast to be followed, bacteria may revert to a navigation mode analogous to that observed in the absence of a magnetic field. In this scenario, straight‐line trajectories could represent an efficient strategy to explore larger areas in search of nutrients. Together, these considerations explain why the collective behavior cannot be straightforwardly extrapolated from the response of a single bacterium but instead emerges from the interplay between individual variability, field conditions, and biological constraints

Swimming Velocity Under Rotating Magnetic Fields (RMF)

After analyzing the impact of RMF on bacterial population, we focus on their influence on bacterial velocity, a subject that remains poorly studied. The only work identified in the bibliography regarding magnetotactic bacteria under RMF was conducted by Pan et al.[ 31 ] who investigated the species Magnetotactic coccus (MYC‐1). This study observes that swimming velocity decreases with the RMF strength. Specifically, at constant frequency of f = 0.5 Hz, bacterial velocity decreases from 220 µm s−1 to 100 µm s−1 as the magnetic field strength increases from B = 0.1 mT (100 µT) to 1.5 mT (1500 µT).

This strong dependence between the applied RMF and navigation velocity has also been observed in certain non‐biological propulsion systems, such as synthetic magnetic microswimmers. In these systems, the velocity strongly depends on f, increasing with frequency until reaching a threshold fc , beyond which it drops.[ 32 ] Furthermore, in these systems, additional factors such as the shape and size of the micro‐swimmers become relevant, thereby introducing further complexity.

Inspired by these studies, we evaluated the swimming velocity of motile MSR‐1 under all previously discussed RMF conditions. Instantaneous velocities were calculated as the distance between bacterial centroids in consecutive frames divided by the recording time interval, as shown in Figure 2d. The mean velocity for each trajectory was then determined from the average of these velocities.

Representative histograms of the average swimming velocities at RMF strength of B = 250 and 1000 µT for frequencies f = 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5 Hz, are shown in Figure 9 . These histograms correspond to a single representative replicate; all data from the three independent replicates, including additional velocity distributions, are provided in the Supporting Information. For all the RMF tested while the characteristic velocity, ⟨v¯2⟩, shows a slight shift toward lower values with increasing RMF frequency, regardless of the RMF strength. The most representative case is observed for B = 1000 µT, presented in Figure 9d–f, where a notable shift from v¯2=47μms−1 to 36.7 µm s−1 takes place when the frequency is increased from f = 0.1 Hz to f = 0.5 Hz. Additionally, the ratio of the height of the peaks, averaged across the three independent biological replicates, is similar for most RMF strengths and frequencies, 〈h 1/h 2〉 ≈ 1.3, except for the highest applied magnetic field and frequency (1000 µT and 0.5 Hz), where 〈h 1/h 2〉 = 2.0. This suggests that the bimodal‐velocity behavior could be affected under certain magnetic field conditions, although further studies at stronger magnetic fields are needed to confirm this observation.

The origin of the bimodal swimming velocity distribution observed in MSR‐1 remains unresolved. Based on our results, several extrinsic factors can be ruled out. First, the shape of the swimming trajectories—whether circular or rectilinear—does not explain the bimodality, as both velocity modes persist even when one trajectory type clearly dominates. Even under strong RMF conditions, such as B = 1000 µT, where collective behavior leads to predominantly circular (f = 0.1 , 0.2 Hz) or straight paths (f = 0.5 Hz), the velocity distribution remains clearly bimodal, as we showed in Figure 9 and Table 2. A deviation occurs at f=0.5Hz, where the higher‐velocity peak (v¯2) is reduced, as shown in Figure 9f however, keeping the bimodality.

Second, the velocity distribution does not correlate with magnetic polarity, NS or SS, since the bimodality is present even in the absence of a magnetic field. Similarly, variations in magnetosome content are not responsible, as bimodality is observed even without RMF stimulation. These findings collectively suggest that the phenomenon is not driven by external magnetic conditions or by trajectory geometry.

Additionally, previous studies report contrasting observations depending on environmental conditions. For example, Popp et al.[ 12 ] observed a unimodal velocity distribution centered around 41.6µm s−1 under strictly controlled in the absence of oxygen gradients. In contrast, Lefevre et al.[ 33 ] reported a unimodal low‐velocity distribution near 20µm s−1 in the oxic–anoxic transition zone. Since an oxic–anoxic zone inherently involves an oxygen gradient, the bimodality in velocities cannot simply arise from the coexistence of both gradient‐free and gradient‐containing environments within the same local conditions. Altogether, these results suggest that the bimodal velocity pattern arises from intrinsic features of MSR‐1 motility.

The consistent presence of two distinct velocity modes suggests that MSR‐1 actively employs this bimodality to diversify its motility patterns. The coexistence of two distinct velocity modes enables richer navigation dynamics, facilitating a broad spectrum of swimming behaviors. As illustrated in Figures 3 and 6, transitions between NS and SS modes are often accompanied by abrupt changes in velocity depending on the new direction or swimming state. This flexibility likely enhances the bacterium's ability to adapt its navigation in complex environments and may be particularly advantageous under dynamic external stimuli, such as RMF. Further investigation is needed to assess how increasing RMF frequencies and strengths might influence this behavior and to identify whether consistent trends emerge under such conditions.

The persistence of a bimodal velocity distribution across all RMF conditions in MSR‐1 prompted us to compare its motility behavior with other magnetotactic strains. Notably, unlike MSR‐1, the MYC‐1 strain exhibits swimming velocities that are highly sensitive to magnetic field strength. For example, at a constant frequency of f=0.5Hz, MYC‐1 cells reduce their velocity from 220 µm s−1 to 100µm s−1 as the magnetic field increases from 0.1mT to 1.5mT.[ 28 ] In contrast, MSR‐1 swimming velocities remain largely consistent across field strengths and frequencies, with only a noticeable shift observed at the highest strength and frequency tested (B = 1000 µT, f = 0.5 Hz). As shown previously, stronger magnetic fields increase alignment efficiency without substantially affecting the bimodal distribution.

This contrast likely arises from fundamental differences in biological and structural properties between the strains. MSR‐1 and MYC‐1 differ in morphology—spirillum‐shaped versus coccoid—and in magnetosome organization, which together influence the balance of magnetic and viscous torques acting on the cells. These characteristics play a critical role in how each species navigates under external magnetic fields. As such, strain selection becomes a key factor in the design of microrobotic platforms for biomedical applications, particularly those requiring precise and reliable navigation, such as targeted drug delivery or tumor infiltration.

Further studies are necessary to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving these contrasting velocity responses to RMFs, which will help optimize the use of magnetotactic bacteria in biomedical applications.

Conclusions

We have conducted a systematic study to explore the individual and collective motility of MSR‐1 under varying RMF conditions. At the individual level, bacterial trajectories gradually transition from diverse motility patterns to predominantly circular motion as the magnetic field strength increases, particularly at low frequencies. At the population level, collective alignment emerges above B = 250 µT, and around 50% of bacteria (NS and SS combined) align with the field at B = 1000µT and f=0.1Hz. This alignment weakens with increasing frequency, revealing a frequency‐dependent disruption of the collective motion. Concurrently, angular dispersion around the RMF direction decreases with field strength, indicating tighter directional control under stronger fields.

Additionally, we found a bimodal velocity distribution typically centered around v¯1∼21.5μms−1 and v¯2∼45μms−1, that remains largely stable under varying RMF conditions. However, at the highest tested field strength and frequency (B = 1000 µT, f = 0.5 Hz), a noticeable shift of the second peak, ⟨v¯2⟩, toward lower velocities suggests that certain RMF parameters can influence the mean velocity. Further investigation under stronger magnetic fields and higher frequencies is needed to reach a clear conclusion.

Finally, our findings reveal that individual bacterial responses under rotating magnetic fields do not straightforwardly translate to predictable collective dynamics. In particular, frequency appears to play a more disruptive role than field strength in suppressing collective motion. While alignment increases with field strength, high frequencies compromise synchrony among bacterial populations. Our analysis further indicates that this discrepancy arises from the intrinsic heterogeneity of the population: variations in bacterial size and magnetosome content led to a distribution of magnetic moments and, consequently, of critical frequencies. As a result, collective dynamics cannot be characterized by a single critical frequency but instead emerge from the interplay between these distributions and the applied magnetic field.

More broadly, the ability of MSR‐1 to spontaneously switch between north‐seeking (NS) and south‐seeking (SS) modes—even under strong magnetic fields—reveals the complex nature of its motility. Understanding and characterizing this dynamic behavior is essential for the development of bacteria‐based navigation systems.

We believe this study offers valuable insights that could drive future advancements in magnetic control, not only of magnetotactic bacteria but also of active matter, micro‐ and nanoscale robotics, and biohybrid systems. These findings are particularly relevant for biomedical applications such as targeted drug delivery, magnetic hyperthermia, and microscale robotics, where precise motion and spatial control are critical.

Experimental Section

Bacteria Culture

The Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense strain MSR‐1 cells were cultured at 28 °C in flask standard medium (10 × 10−3 m HEPES, 735 × 10−6 m KH2PO4, 600 × 10−6 m of MgSO4 · 7H2O, 4 × 10−3 m of NaNO3, 0.01% (wt/vol) of yeast extract, 0.3% (wt/vol) of soybean peptone, 0.3% (wt/vol) of sodium pyruvate, and 100 × 10−6 m of Fe (III)‐citrate). MSR‐1 bacteria were collected after 48 hours to ensure the presence of well‐formed magnetosome chains. For motility experiments, suspensions were prepared with cell concentration around ≈ 108 cell mL−1; 1% of lacked magnetosomes. All cultured samples contained two well‐defined bacteria groups: a) motile bacteria, capable of self‐propulsion, and b) nonmotile bacteria, which were exclusively carried by the flow. However, the proportion of nonmotile bacteria was always minimal compared to motile ones, less than 5%. To minimize any contribution from nonmotile bacteria, all motility analyses presented in this work were performed exclusively using trajectories of active bacteria. Trajectories of nonmotile bacteria were used solely to estimate and verify that the background flow remained stable under the different applied magnetic field conditions.

Microscopy and Magnetic Field Setup

An inverted optical microscope (Zeiss Axio‐vert a1) with a 20× magnification objective (Zeiss Plan‐Apochromat 20x/0.8), equipped with a high‐resolution CCD camera (Zeiss Axiocam) was used to visualize and record high quality images of the M. gryphiswaldense strain. Images were captured at a frame rate of 20 s−1 with high‐resolution color and a size of 2752 × 2208 pixels (covering625 µm × 501 µm). The microscope was mounted inside a Three‐Axis Helmholtz coil system, forming a magnetotaxia platform for the control and guidance of MTB. Coils were controlled by independent power sources, allowing the application of time‐varying magnetic fields with precise regulation of both strength and frequency. Calibration of the three‐axis system was performed using a high‐precision three‐axis Gauss probe, allowing for precise compensation of the Earth's magnetic field and suppression of residual spurious magnetic fields within the laboratory environment, thereby establishing well‐defined zero‐field conditions for subsequent measurements.

The magnetotaxis system is fully computerized, providing control, recording, and synchronization between the applied field and optical microscope recordings. Motility experiments were performed under an RMF, with field strengths B = 0 − 1000 µT and frequencies f = 0.1 − 0.5 Hz.

Transmission Electron Microscopy Characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on unstained MSR‐1 cells adsorbed onto 300‐mesh carbon‐coated copper grids. TEM images were acquired using a JEOL JEM‐1400 Plus electron microscope operating at 120 kV.

The cell length (L cell) was determined by measuring the major axial axis of each segmented bacterium. This axial line was obtained via skeletonization process,[ 34 ] ensuring a continuous path that connects both ends of the cell. The cell width (W cell) was measured orthogonally at the midpoint of the axial axis, corresponding to the distance between the lateral edges of the segmented cell.

Motility Experiments

A commercial microfluidic device was employed from Ibidi, slides labeled asµ‐Slide VI0.1, with a linear channel 100 µm high, 1 mm wide and 17 mm long. In all motility experiments, 100 µL of bacteria suspensions were used to ensure that the entire channel was virtually full. The microchannels were filled at a slow and gradual rate, as sudden injections would result in the formation of bubbles.

Although phenomena such as the appearance of oxygen gradients and evaporation of the medium are noticeable after more than 12 hours after filling, 2 hours were chosen as the maximum working period per microchannel, in order to ensure always working with fresh suspensions.

Motility experiments were performed using three independent MSR‐1 bacterial culture replicates to verify the reproducibility of the analysis. The full results from each replicate are provided in the Supporting Information, Pages 1‐4, whereas the manuscript reports only the averaged values of the key parameters, including population dispersion around the magnetic field direction, the fraction of aligned bacteria, and swimming velocities.

Image Analysis

The recorded images were subjected to a rigorous image processing procedure, which involved automatic segmentation, detection, and tracking of bacteria using dedicated MATLAB‐based programs tailored to each processing stage. The segmentation of bacteria was performed separately for each channel (R, G, and B) and consisted of two phases: (a) creation of a segmentation mask and (b) fine segmentation based on bacteria pixel intensity. In the first phase, all bacteria in each frame, along with their surrounding regions, were detected using tubular structure detection algorithms such as the Frangi Vesselness filter. This approach was followed to eliminate background signals, minimize artefacts caused by shadows around the bacteria, and separate closely adjacent bacteria.

The recordings captured bacteria both within the focal plane, appearing as dark (black) features, and bacteria located slightly out of the focal plane, which manifested as bright (white) features.

In the second phase, these two bacterial populations were segregated to optimize detection performance, as simultaneous processing could compromise accurate segmentation. Bacteria within the segmentation mask were classified based on pixel intensity thresholds, enabling reliable differentiation between in‐focus and out‐of‐focus groups.

Standard image processing algorithms were then applied to segment all bacteria. Finally, a logical operation was performed on the binary images obtained from each channel (R, G, and B) to generate a fully segmented video. This channel‐based segmentation approach improved the overall results, as certain bacteria were better defined in specific channels.

The detection and identification of bacteria were performed using the BlobAnalysis algorithm implemented in MATLAB, which provides information about all bacteria segmented in the previous phase. This information includes the coordinates of their centroids, which are obtained as the geometric center of the bacterium, as well as orientation angle, area, perimeter and the major and minor axes of an ellipse fitted to each of them. Centroids are used to identify the location coordinates of bacteria, thus enabling the reconstruction of their trajectories. This process enabled the calculation of their swimming velocity, magnitude and direction.

Our bacteria tracking algorithm uses this information to link bacteria from frame i to frame i + 1, thus identifying each of them as the same single bacterium in two different frames. The linking algorithm we developed follows a structured workflow, which includes predicting the position of bacteria in frame i + 1 using a Kalman filter, updating linked and unlinked bacteria through the Hungarian assignment algorithm,[ 35 , 36 ] and creating a new track if a bacterium detected in a given frame has not been linked to any bacteria from previous frames.

The recorded trajectories were filtered to eliminate potential errors in trajectory association. Before data processing, comprehensive visual inspections were conducted to ensure the accuracy and integrity of the trajectories.

Statistical Analysis

Bacteria were initially characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to obtain distributions of cell length, width, and the number of magnetosomes per cell. For these TEM‐based measurements, mean and standard deviation of the distribution are reported as (mean ± SD), with the number of cells analyzed per measurement (N).

All experiments on bacterial motility were conducted using three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Between N tracks = 18 000 and 30 000 bacterial trajectories were analyzed for each condition of applied magnetic field. For analyses of angular dispersion (〈σ NS〉,〈σ NS〉), aligned bacterial populations (〈P NS〉,〈P SS〉), and characteristic velocities (⟨v¯1⟩,⟨v¯2⟩), each replicate was processed individually, and key parameters were obtained from the respective measurements. In the case of velocity distributions, a two‐component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) was fitted to each replicate to extract the characteristic velocities and standard deviations. Final values reported in the manuscript correspond to the mean across the three replicates, with the associated standard error of the mean (SEM), and are expressed as (mean ± SEM). Since the main goal of this study is to evaluate trends rather than to test formal hypotheses, no inferential statistical tests were performed. All data analyses were carried out using MATLAB R2024b.
