Emerging multisystem disease mechanisms of e-cigarette vaping on human health
Authors: Md Mohaimenul Islam Tareq, Sreejayan Nair, Guanglong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) have quickly grown from a niche smoking cessation design to a ubiquitous nicotine-delivery device, causing ever-growing debate concerning their safety and long-term health outcomes. This review critically summarizes the available evidence from current literature on the development of e-cigarette technologies, nicotine pharmacodynamics, and systemic biological effects, especially pulmonary, cardiovascular, and neurological outcomes. Although noncombustible, e-cigarette aerosols contain hazardous compounds such as nicotine, metallic particulates, and aldehyde-reactive species, which can interact with the endothelial surfaces, immune effector cells, and neuronal networks, leading to oxidative stress, inflammation, and tissue remodeling. Clinical and preclinical evidence shows that e-cigarette vaping is linked to acute and chronic pulmonary injury, endothelial dysfunction, suppressed neurodevelopment, and behavioral dysregulation in adolescents. Additionally, the so-called nicotine-free and flavored products often contain unreported toxicants and do not have standardized safety tests. The consumption trends among young people and the vulnerable have been on the increase due to unsubstantiated assumptions about its controversial harm-reduction effects and overly zealous advertising campaigns. This review will also discuss controversial issues of the harm-reduction paradigm, gaps in regulatory control, and future research areas, such as longitudinal cohort studies. An evidence-based, multidisciplinary framework is firmly needed to guide innovation, policy, and clinical practice and, as such, make sure that responses to public health are aligned with sound scientific knowledge.Keywords: E-cigarette, Nicotine, Aerosol, Toxicant, Comparative Harm Assessment, Cardiovascular Diseases, Pulmonary Dysfunction, and Neurological Disorders

Introduction

Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes), also known as vapes, are electronic devices that facilitate the release of aerosols as a method of delivering nicotine, flavoring agents, and an extensive assortment of ancillary chemical substances for inhalation [1]. The major route of exposure involves inhalation of aerosols that usually contain nicotine, volatile organic compounds, carbonyls, metals, and particulate matters, which can be taken up in systemic circulation through the pulmonary vasculature to the central nervous and cardiovascular systems [2]. It is interesting to note that nicotine, which is the major addictive substance in e-cigarettes, has multiple organ system effects [3]. Airway inflammation, oxidative stress, and early pathological evidence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are linked to pulmonary exposure to aerosolized particles [4]. Nicotine affects neurodevelopment, changes synaptic plasticity, and predisposes the nervous system to mood disorders in the central nervous system especially during adolescence [5]. Exposure of the heart to nicotine increases heart rate, causes vasoconstriction, and stimulates atherogenesis, thus increasing the chances of developing hypertension and ischemic heart disease [6], [7].

The increased attractiveness of vaping, particularly among young people, is commonly driven by perceptions of lower harm, advertising, and the appeal of flavorful products [8]. Regulatory controls are highly heterogeneous: in some jurisdictions, flavored e-liquids are banned, or advertising must be regulated, but in others, enforcing these regulations is difficult [9]. The lack of consistent regulatory policies makes it difficult to communicate information on human health and to monitor products, especially given the frequent appearance of new devices and chemical additives in the market [10], [11]. Although the use of e-cigarettes is growing, longitudinal research has been the limiting factor on the long-term health impacts of vaping [12]. The current toxicological and clinical data are mainly based on short-term exposure studies or the extrapolation of combustible tobacco studies [13]. Specifically, the information regarding the association of exposure to e-cigarettes with neurological and cardiovascular outcomes is scarce and disjointed [14]. The lack of longitudinal data hinders the development of universal health policies [15], [16].

One of the main controversies is whether e-cigarettes may be viewed as a harm-reduction aid for adult smokers or as a means of nicotine addiction among young people [17], [18]. In addition, there exists a scientific debate on the comparative toxicity of vaping and traditional smoking, particularly concerning brain maturation and cardiopulmonary health [19], [20]. According to recent animal and human research, e-cigarette aerosols can have apoptotic effects on endothelial function and neurotransmitter signaling, and can induce immune responses on their own independent of conventional combustion by-products [21], [22]. Moreover, even the so-called nicotine-free products have some very small amounts of nicotine or other harmful chemicals, and this fact undermines the assumption of safety in non-nicotine vapes [23], [24].

The current review summarizes existing data on the pathophysiological impact of e-cigarettes and nicotine use on the lungs, brain, and heart. It aims to elucidate mechanistic knowledge of organ-specific toxicity and societal health impact among the at-risk population, especially young people, and to outline research priorities for long-term longitudinal and mechanistic research.

Evolution and composition of e-cigarettes

The current state of e-cigarettes has been changing rapidly since the first ones were commercially introduced into the market at the beginning of the 2000s [16]. As a substitutive nicotine delivery device compared to traditional cigarettes [15], e-cigarettes have evolved to several generations comprising cig-a-like, vape pens, mods, and pod-based products [25]. The new generations include alterations in design, heating systems, and the development of e-liquids to improve the delivery of nicotine and user experience [16], [26]. Unstandardized chemical content also makes the new generations of disposable vapes distinct from regulatory and toxicological issues [27], [28]. Most e-cigarette devices consist of three main elements, namely, a power source (a battery), a heating coil, and a reservoir for e-liquid [29]. The typical contents of the e-liquids are propylene glycol (PG), vegetable glycerin (VG), flavoring chemicals, and nicotine at different strengths [30]. More recent analytical investigations have demonstrated that there are other contaminants, such as heavy metals (e.g., nickel, lead), volatile organic compounds, and aldehydes, some of which are known respiratory and cardiovascular risk factors [25], [31], [32]. Heating temperatures are also significantly different, resulting in aerosol particle size and concentration profiles inconsistent [33], [34].

Toxicological profiles of nicotine-free and flavored vaping products

Although promoted as nicotine free, a significant percentage of electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) have been reported to have small trace amounts of nicotine as well as other inhaled contaminants. Analytical reviews of these products have been done periodically, and the products have been found to contain nicotine, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and heavy metals (including lead, nickel, and chromium), which in most instances can be ascribed to leaching of heating ingredients or cross-contamination during the production process [35], [36]. Temperature-dependent properties of e-cigarette devices affect the emissions of toxicants. At high power, the thermal degradation rate of solvents such as PG and VG is thermally increased, producing more toxic aldehydes such as formaldehyde and acetaldehyde [37]. Inappropriate labelling and a high level of batch-to-batch variability in disposable devices also contribute to the misuse of these products, which counter the claims of safety with respect to non-nicotine vapes [38].

Certain flavoring additives, such as those listed as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by mouth, are uncharacterized in aerosol form. Diketones (like diacetyl), with a buttery or creamy taste have been linked to bronchiolitis and cilia dysfunction in the epithelial layer at low concentrations [39]. Recent toxicogenomic data show that cinnamon, mint, and fruit-related e-liquid may activate oxidative stress pathways and mitochondrial dysfunction of airway epithelial cells [40]. Moreover, most e-liquids in the commercial market are not tested regarding inhalation-specific toxicology, therefore, regulatory gaps still exist regarding quality control and ingredient disclosure [41]. Nevertheless, these findings further underly the importance of understanding the risk profile of e-cigarette products, especially labeled as nicotine-free and flavored e-cigarettes.

Systemic effects and pharmacological properties of nicotine

Nicotine, as the major psychoactive ingredient of many e-cigarette preparations, is a strong agonist of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChR) [42]. After inhalation, nicotine is quickly absorbed via pulmonary alveoli and enters the systemic circulation, reaching peak plasma concentrations within minutes, like many combustible tobacco products [43]. Nicotine and its metabolic components pass through the blood-brain barrier and regulate the release of several neurotransmitters (such as dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin) to impose addictive effects [44], [45]. At the systemic level, it increases heart rate, elevates blood pressure, and induces vasoconstriction, which are mediated by the sympathetic nervous system [46]. Neuroadaptation, dependence, and downstream cardiovascular and immune changes are the consequences of repeated nicotine exposure [44]. Toxicological effects of heating products of e-cigarettes

E-cigarette vaping does not create smoke through the combustion of tobacco as in cigarette smoking. Instead, it produces aerosols by heating the e-liquid to 200–300 °C. Even though this mode of vaping generates less production of carcinogens compared to cigarette smoking, there is still a considerable number of toxicants that come from the aerosolization process. PG/VG undergoes thermal degradation to form formaldehyde, acrolein, and acetaldehyde, all of which are associated with airway and vascular damage [23]. Further toxicological studies have suggested that some flavored e-liquids and high-voltage vaporizers have the potential to induce oxidative stress and damage to DNA equivalent to conventional cigarette smoking, especially when used regularly or at high voltage levels [47], [48]. Moreover, preclinical models have shown that vapors of flavored e-juice cause inflammatory changes and oxidative damage in respiratory epithelial cells [49]. Although e-cigarettes eliminate tobacco burning, thermal aerosolization of e-liquid produces reactive carbonyl species and other harmful by-products undermining the integrity of the airways and blood vessels. These toxicological studies prove that non-combustion vaping is not necessarily inert [50]. Furthermore, e-cigarettes have the ability to deliver measurable quantities of nicotine and other toxic substances in aerosols. Biological effects of nicotine and aerosols

Many biological systems are affected by exposure to e-cigarettes. In Endothelial cells, short-term exposure to e-cigarettes suppresses nitric oxide production, increases oxidative stress and inflammation causing vessel constriction and vascular dysfunction [46], [57]. In the brain, nicotine binds to nAChRs, modifies neurotransmission across important brain areas including the prefrontal cortex and ventral tegmental area. These effects are particularly harmful to young people, as they impair synaptic pruning, reduce neuroplasticity, and increase the incidences of anxiety and depression [58]. Aerosol of e-cigarettes also weakens the immune system, by suppressing the activity of macrophages, deteriorating the mucociliary clearance, and modifying the cytokine signaling, which can make the body more susceptible to respiratory infections and aggravate inflammatory respiratory diseases [23], [59].

Pulmonary effects

E-cigarette use has been associated with a continuum of pulmonary toxicities, including both acute injuries, such as e-cigarette vaping associated lung injury (EVALI), and chronic respiratory changes like the early stages of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). As shown in Fig. 3, e-cigarette aerosol exposure alters epithelial and immune function in the lungs. These changes induce oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and other chronic pulmonary diseases. Some pathological agents, including vitamin E acetate, have been detected in acute injury as diffuse alveolar damage and foam macrophages in the bronchoalveolar lavage of affected persons [60]. Epithelial disruption and dysfunction of the barrier between airway and the underlying basement tissue occur due to chronic exposure to e-cigarette aerosols, mainly by downregulation of tight junction proteins, such as Zonula Occludens-1 (ZO-1), resulting in occlusion allowing paracellular leakage and inflammation [23], [61]. Experimental models also show the presence of alveolar apoptosis and mitochondrial stress after prolonged exposure [62]. Proteomic studies in humans reveal that there are molecular signatures of oxidative injury and innate immune activation [63]. Immune cell behavior is also altered by vaping. Alveolar macrophages are characterized by weakened phagocytosis and the expression of inflammatory cytokines, especially IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α [64], [65]. These immune dysregulations correlate with increased susceptibility to bacterial and viral infections, as demonstrated in murine models of respiratory pathogen infection [66]. Moreover, exposure to e-cigarette vaping aggravates allergic reactions of the airways with increased Th2 cytokine release and eosinophilia presence in airways in sensitized animals [67]. Taking together, these experimental findings suggest that even without combustion products, e-cigarettes are not harmless and can have a detrimental effect on pulmonary health (Table 2).

Cardiovascular effects

The use of e-cigarettes has become increasingly linked with a wide range of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, which is propelled by non-nicotine as well as nicotine components. Fig. 4 summarizes the major cardiovascular effects of e-cigarette exposure, including hemodynamic changes, endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and thrombotic risks. Acute exposure increases heart rate and blood pressure due to its impact on the activity of the sympathetic nervous system. The hemodynamic response is identical to that of conventional cigarette smoking, even with e-vaping in the absence of nicotine [71], [72]. These responses are coupled with elevated circulating catecholamines and systemic oxidative stress, resulting in abnormal vascular activity and increased endothelial and vascular tension. In both chronic and acute exposure conditions, endothelial dysfunction is an essential antecedent to atherosclerosis, and is characterized by a reduction in flow-mediated dilation and depletion in nitric oxide bioavailability [42], [73]. In vitro and in vivo animal studies also indicate that e-cigarette aerosols induce endothelial apoptosis, an increase in adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, and induce ROS generation leading to increased vascular inflammation [74].

Cardiovascular effects of e-cigarette exposure. E-cigarette aerosols cause acute changes in blood pressure and heart rate, worsen endothelial function due to oxidative stress, and enhance vascular inflammation. Chronic e-cigarette exposure induces platelet activation and predisposes to thrombosis, which is involved in atherogenesis, myocardial infarction, and stroke in dual users.

E-cigarette exposure also increases the thrombogenic potential. Short duration of exposure to aerosols is linked to platelet aggregation and thrombus formation in murine models with impact from the pro-thrombotic vape constituents [75]. Among humans, habitual e-cigarette users are found to have an increased biomarker of platelet activation, arterial constriction, and systemic inflammation like that of cigarette smokers [76]. Although there is limited evidence on long-term epidemiological data, initial cross-sectional research has shown that there is a strong correlation between the use of e-cigarettes and the risk of myocardial infarction, especially in dual users who also smoke combustible tobacco [76], [77]. These studies question the unsubstantiated assumption that transitioning from tabaco smoking to e-cigarette vaping reduces cardiovascular risk and its health impact considering the massive popularity among people with a history of underlying cardiometabolic issues. Neurological effects

E-cigarettes deliver nicotine levels that are high enough to cross the blood-brain barrier in a few seconds. Nicotine binds to nAChRs and acts on critical neural pathways in learning, memory, and reward. As shown in Fig. 5, the neurological effects of nicotine exposure include altered synaptic plasticity, disrupted neuronal development, and adverse cognitive and behavioral outcomes. Nicotine enhances synaptic plasticity by stimulating nAChRs, glutamatergic transmission, and dopaminergic release, particularly in the mesolimbic pathway [83]. Both reinforcement and addiction are associated with this neuropharmacological modulation, and in the adolescent brain the effect is highly enhanced. When compared to their adult counterparts, adolescents are more vulnerable to remodeling of brain architecture under the influence of nicotine due to the high level of neural plasticity and expression of nAChRs. Animal experiments suggest that nicotine exposure at key neurodevelopmental stages changes synaptic pruning and dendritic complexity, especially in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus [45]. Such neuroanatomical alterations have been linked to cognitive impairment, attention deficit, and increased anxiety and depressive behaviors of rodents and human adolescents. New neuroimaging studies in adolescent e-cigarette smokers also support these findings with reduced functional connectivity between executive control areas and increased amygdala responsiveness [84].

Nicotine and the regulation of nAChR-associated pathogenesis in the brain following e-cigarette vaping. Nicotine binds to nAChRs in specific regions of the brain to impair neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity. These interactions are associated with impaired cognition, emotion regulation, and neurodevelopment, especially among adolescents.

E-cigarettes have been associated with behavioral hazards of mood disorders, attention dysregulation, and working memory failure. The longitudinal cohort studies indicate that symptoms that are similar to ADHD and generalized anxiety are more likely to be exhibited by adolescents who vape regularly despite the correction of confounding factors like socioeconomic status and the use of polysubstance [39]. It is assumed that these results may reflect disruption of dopaminergic systems, including the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and nucleus accumbens (NAc), which are associated with motivational salience and reward anticipation. Studies using animal models show that chronic nicotine exposure causes changes in dopamine transporter (DAT) expression and results in reduced sensitivity to rewards with time, which then causes the compulsive-like drug-seeking behavior [85]. In addition, other ingredients (PG and flavoring aldehydes) with nicotine-free vaping also cause neural damages [86]. Taken together as summarized in Table 4, these results imply that the developing brain is especially prone to e-cigarette aerosol-induced neurotoxicity, which is of great concern to the neural behavior of adolescents.

The ongoing debate on the beneficial effects of e-cigarette vaping

Although a growing body of empirical data is available to confirm the pernicious biological impact of e-cigarette vaping, there is still a substantial literature claiming that e-cigarette can serve a harm-reduction approach when used by adult smokers as a substitute for combustible tobacco [93], [94], [95]. Compared to conventional cigarettes, e-cigarettes do not involve the burning of cigarettes and are associated with a significant decrease in carbon monoxide, tar, and many polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon emissions, and hence a drop in exposure to many well-known carcinogenic and cardiovascular toxic substances. There are also clinical studies that involve adult smokers who switch fully to using e-cigarettes that show improvements in respiratory symptoms, similar reduction of exhaled carbon monoxide, and a partial recovery of vascular endothelial functions in comparison to further smoking of cigarettes [96], [97]. Pharmacologically, the nicotine intake through e-cigarettes can ameliorate the withdrawal and craving symptoms among nicotine-dependent smokers and serve as a nicotine-replacement modality among some adult smokers who fail the traditional cessation programs [98], [99], [100]. Most importantly, these potential benefits seem to depend on the full replacement of combustible tobacco because dual use of cigarettes and e-cigarettes does not, when compared, bring about similar reductions in toxicant exposure and can continue to pose cardiovascular and pulmonary risk [76], [101], [102]. There is no empirical basis to a harm-reduction argument in support of adolescents, never-smokers, or pregnant women, in which the risks of neurodevelopment and cardiometabolic disorders outweigh any potential benefit. Altogether, the current evidence suggests that e-cigarettes can potentially offer a comparative risk reduction to a very limited group of adult smokers and, at the same time, pose significant public health risks due to youth and dual users. Based on these competing effects, it is essential to differentiate between individual-level harm reduction and population-level harm amplification in assessing the net health effect of electronic cigarettes [103], [104], [105].

Future research directions

Future studies should focus on large-scale and long-term cohort research to clarify the long-term impact of e-cigarette use in various populations. Recent data mainly focus on short-term or cross-sectional studies, which restrain extrapolation on disease progression and cumulative toxicity [69]. Longitudinal data are particularly required to investigate the connections between e-cigarette vaping and chronic cardiovascular, pulmonary, and neurodevelopmental disorders in different user groups, including the dual users. Also, studies are needed on the standardization of exposure procedures, product categorization, and biomarker measurements to warrant reproducibility and gross comparability [106]. With the fast-changing device design and e-liquid formulation, any future studies must take into consideration the variability in heating parameters, flavor additives, and user behavior [107].

To better understand the underlying mechanisms, multi-omics techniques, such as transcriptomics, metabolomics, and epigenetics should be incorporated to develop molecular imaging methods. These methodological modalities can provide early cellular reaction and exposure-specific molecular fingerprints for e-cigarette-induced injury. Special attention should be given to prenatal and pediatric populations, which are under-represented in the existing data, and may be more biologically susceptible to aerosolized harmful substances [108], [109]. There is an urgent need to conduct experimental research and registries that would have systematically recorded the effects of in-utero exposure and early developmental stages. At the same time, e-cigarette technology should provide innovative control on emissions, safety of biomaterials, and product labeling. A regulatory system driven by mechanistic data, not consumer trends, will be critical in designing safer alternatives and reducing the initiations in youths [110], [111].

Conclusion

E-cigarettes are one of the rapidly emerging types of inhalation devices with diversified chemical compounds and delivery patterns, which challenge existing toxicology and clinical paradigms. Although originally intended as a harm-reduction option to the harmful use of combustible tobacco, recent evidence highlights the risk of the disruption of pulmonary epithelial integrity, cardiovascular homeostasis, and neurodevelopmental processes, especially in adolescents and the prenatal populations. This notion has been proven to be controversial in the scientific community. Major mechanistic injuries include oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, neurotransmission dysregulation, and immune modulation that are affected through nicotine-dependent and independent mechanisms. Although the use has been widespread, particularly among youth populations, regulatory framework and clinical diagnosis are still limited due to lack of longitudinal data, underrepresentation of children’s cohorts, and disparities in product formulation and labeling. These gaps in knowledge should be addressed in future studies by focusing on rigorous and long-term cohort studies, a combination of multi-omics and novel imaging modalities, and real-time toxicologic studies. In parallel, innovations in e-cigarette design and the regulatory health policies must focus on emission control, proper labeling, and protection of youth initiations. To reduce the threats of health issues and create safe and science-based nicotine delivery alternatives, a unified and evidence-based regulatory approach is required.
