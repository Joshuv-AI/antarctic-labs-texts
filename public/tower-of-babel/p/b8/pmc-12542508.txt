Cephalomannine as in vitro and in vivo anticancer agent in mesothelioma cells: mechanisms of action
Authors: Federica Morani, Irene Dell’Anno, Antonio Daga, Stefano Doccini, Luisa Bisceglia, Federica Gemignani, Stefano Landi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn our previous drug screening, cephalomannine, fludarabine, and crenolanib were among the most active compounds against permanent cell lines of Pleural Mesothelioma (PM).MethodsHere, firstly we assessed the efficacy of these compounds on PM-xenograft NOD-SCID mice and evaluated the overall survival (OS) as major endpoint. Then, we evaluated in vitro the underlying mechanisms involving CPM (at 0.1 µM) as anticancer agent on REN (derived from PM) and MeT-5A, a mesothelial cell line often used as a non-malignant model of mesothelial cells. We measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR), the intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and the protein level of BAX and Bcl2, as proxy for apoptosis. Moreover, we measured IL-18 levels, as marker of the inflammasome pathway activation.ResultsWhile no effects were found for fludarabine (40, 100, or 200 mg/kg body weight) and crenolanib (15 mg/kg body weight), for cephalomannine (CPM, 2 mg/kg body weight) we observed a prolonged median OS in the treated group (75 days) vs vehicle control group (55 days; P = 0.0021). We found an increase in ROS production and a reduction in OCR in the REN cell line, compared to MeT-5A. We also noticed an activation of apoptosis, but not of inflammation.ConclusionsThese results confirm the effectiveness of CPM on PM, suggesting novel targets and pathways for investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14902-6.Keywords: Pleural mesothelioma, Cephalomannine, Drug screening, Oxidative stress, Apoptosis

Introduction

In a recent study, we screened 1170 FDA-approved drugs on five pleural mesothelioma (PM) cell lines (Mero-14, Mero-25, IST-Mes2, NCI-H28, and MSTO-211H) and one non-malignant SV40-immortalized mesothelial cell line (MeT-5A) [1, 2]. Fludarabine and cephalomannine (CPM) emerged as highly toxic compounds, exhibiting cytotoxic effects in both permanent and patient-derived primary cell lines [1, 2]. Additionally, considering the significance of PDGFRB in PM tumorigenesis [2], we demonstrated that the in vitro inhibition of PDGFRB, using its specific inhibitor crenolanib, led to decreased proliferation, colony formation, migration, and increased apoptotic rates in PM cell lines [2]. Based on these results, the present study aimed to investigate the impact of fludarabine, crenolanib, and CPM on PM tumour growth in a PM-xenografted mouse model. Pseudo-orthotopic inoculation of human PM REN cells into NOD-SCID mice revealed that CPM was the only drug capable of significantly extending overall survival (OS).

CPM, also known as taxol B, is a taxol analog of paclitaxel (PTX) extracted from yew plants [3]. As known from literature, the main mechanism of action of taxanes consists in the ability to affect the microtubule dynamics by promoting their polymerization and stabilization in living cells [4]. This alteration induces mitotic arrest, ultimately leading to cell death [5]. Specifically, taxanes exhibit their antitumor effect through the inhibition of microtubule dynamics [6], resulting in a reduced number of unattached kinetochores [7]. This mechanism triggers the mitotic checkpoint, preventing chromosome missegregation and culminating in cell cycle arrest at metaphase [8].

However, additional anticancer activities of taxanes have been suggested [9]. A recent study demonstrated that PTX induces an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and an overexpression of the genes and proteins associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress in osteosarcoma cells [10]. Furthermore, in mammary gland tumour cells, PTX led to a down-expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and an over-expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX [11–14]. These changes imply mitochondrial damage, potentially linked to altered cellular respiration, ending with apoptosis through the disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential [15, 16]. Additionally, a recent study highlighted the dysregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. The NLRP3 inflammasome, a component of the innate immune system, triggers the release of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1ß and IL-18. However, the effect of PTX on NLRP3 inflammasome remains unclear [17].

In this study, we were not aimed to prove the ability of CPM in affecting microtubules dynamics once more, since this mechanism has been well-established for taxanes. Instead, our focus was on investigating and potentially confirming the other mechanisms described for PTX but not reported for CPM, especially in the context of PM cells. The discovery of novel drugs and mechanisms effective against PM is crucial and could be beneficial, as currently, the global incidence is still high (age-standardized rates of 0.30 per 100,000 persons) [18], and the only available chemotherapy options consist of platinum-based compounds combined with pemetrexed [19–21].

Methods

Mice and in vivo experiments

All in vivo experiments were performed on six-week-old female Non-Obese Diabetic Severe Combined Immunodeficient (NOD-SCID) mice from ENVIGO (San Pietro al Natisone, Udine, Italy), in accordance with the National Regulation on Animal Research Resources and approved by the Review Board of the IRCCS Policlinico San Martino, Genoa, Italy, and Italian Ministry of Health (n° 462/2020-PR). Twenty mice were pseudo-orthotopically inoculated by intra-peritoneal (i.p.) injections of 5 × 106 luciferase transduced REN cells in 0.5 mL of DMEM medium, as reported in our previous work [22]. Mice were monitored for disease symptoms every other day (starting from one week after tumour challenge) by visual inspection and sacrificed by CO2 asphyxiation at Human End Points to prevent or alleviate pain and/or distress. Mice were placed in a transparent 10 L euthanasia chamber and fluxed with medical grade compressed CO2 at flow rate 5 L/minute for 5 min to induce rapid unconsciousness followed by death, as indicated by AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals 2020 Edition (https://www.avma.org/KB/Policies/Documents/euthanasia.pdf). I.P. engraftment was preferred to the subcutaneous one at it better mimics the natural cancer microenvironment, despite hampering the quality of the imaging. In any case, the main endpoint of the study was the overall survival, and the images were used only as qualitative and not for quantitative evidence. Since the tumour in the peritoneum grows in a disseminated manner, forming small semi-adherent nodules that are impossible to detect by palpation, a maximal tumour size to determine the stop of the experiment and the animal suppression cannot be established. According to our Ethic Committee, the luminescence is employed as an alternative, so that any luminescence emission exceeding 109 RLU will be a reason for terminating the experiment and euthanizing the animal. For the following experiments, the maximal tumour size (i.e. luminescence) was not exceeded. However, we could not quantify the acquired signals, as we observed the presence of hemorrhages preventing precise measurements. So, the reported figure (Fig. 2) should only be considered as a rough representation of disease progression/reduction following CPM treatment.

The drugs were firstly dissolved in 10% DMSO (Euroclone S.p.A., Milan, Italy; purity > 99.9%) and then crenolanib and fludarabine were diluted in saline 1% glycerol formal while CPM was diluted in PBS/2% BSA immediately before i.p. injection. All substances were from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). The solubility and treatment pattern for each drug is summarized in Table 1. The three drug structures are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Untreated animals were i.p. dosed with the same volume of saline 1% glycerol formal or PBS/2%BSA. We administered all drugs by i.p. to increase the local concentration of each compound around the tumour site. Mice were then evaluated by BLI for tumour take rate and dimension. Tumour growth was analysed by IVIS acquisitions. Anti-neoplastic drug efficacy was evaluated by comparing the OS from the time of inoculation of the mesothelioma cells. In vivo doses were determined based on a combination of data from various publicly available sources and pre-pilot tests conducted on a small scale. The study design is outlined in Table 1. For the in vitro doses, selection was guided by the extended experiments performed in a previous study [1, 2].

Mesothelioma cell lines

In this work, we employed commercially available immortalized cell lines: the malignant REN, and the non-malignant MeT-5A. MeT-5A was purchased from ATCC, REN was kindly provided Dr. Laura Moro, Universita` del Piemonte Orientale ‘A. Avogadro’, Novara, (Italy). Met‐5A cells were grown in Medium 199 supplemented with 3.3 nM EGF, 400 nM hydrocortisone, and 870 nM zinc‐free bovine insulin (all from Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA). REN cells were grown in DMEM (all from Euroclone S.p.A., Milan, Italy). Both cell lines were maintained in medium supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated FBS, 2 mM L‐glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin (all from Euroclone S.p.A., Milan, Italy). All cell lines were routinely passaged every 1–2 weeks. Cells were grown at 37 °C and 5% CO2. All treatments were performed by using DMSO (Euroclone S.p.A., Milan, Italy; purity > 99.9%) as vehicle at 5%.

Oxygen consumption rate measurements

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured in MeT-5A and REN cells using an XFe24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Cells were plated in XF 24-well cell culture microplates at a density of 100,000 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were treated with CPM or vehicle (DMSO) at 0.1 μM for 24 h before starting the experiment. Plates preparation and injection strategy adopted for OCR measurements followed the standard procedure for the Mito Stress Test, as previously reported [23, 24]. Measurements of endogenous respiration were performed with DMEM high glucose without sodium bicarbonate, supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate 2 mM glutamine, and adjusted at pH 7.4. After baseline measurements, OCR was analyzed by the sequential injection of 1 mM of oligomycin, 2 mM of carbonyl-cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP) and 0.5 mM of rotenone plus antimycin A (all chemicals were from Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Data were expressed as pmol of O2/min normalized post-assay by the fluorescence of Hoechst 33,342 as function of the cell number. The cells are stained during the last set of mix and measurement steps in the XF assay to reduce technical error during manual administration of the dye. (XF Data Normalization by the Agilent Seahorse XF Imaging and Normalization System – Seahorse guidelines and Application notes—https://www.agilent.com/cs/library/applications/application-normalization-technical-guidelines-cell-analysis-5994-0022en-agilent.pdf.pdf) Statistics was assessed by multiple unpaired t tests with Bonferroni’s correction.

Determination of reactive oxygen species

For the evaluation of intracellular ROS production, the ROS detection assay kit (BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were pre-incubated with CPM or vehicle (DMSO) at 0.1 μM for 24 h before starting the experiment. Cells were analyzed on FLUOstar Optima plate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany) at a wavelengths Ex/Em: 495/529 nm, and the difference in ROS levels between treated and untreated condition were expressed as relative fluorescent units (RFU) after background subtraction. Statistical significance was calculated using unpaired t test with Welch’s correction.

Western blot assay

Cells were collected at confluence after 72 h of treatment with 0.1 μM of CPM or vehicle (DMSO). For protein extraction protocol, electrophoresis, western blot assay and detection method, please refer to our previous publication [25]. The specific primary antibody employed were: anti-BAX rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:4000); anti- Bcl2 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:3000); anti-IL18 mouse monoclonal antibody (1:1000), all from Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA. A rabbit polyclonal antibody specific for β-tubulin (1:8000; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) was used as an index of homogenate protein loading in the lanes. Secondary antibodies anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:10,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) and anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:20,000; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) were added for 1 h at room temperature and used for signal detection.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (Microsoft Inc, Redmond, WA, USA) and Statgraphics Centurion XVI (version 16.1.11; StatPoint Technologies Inc., Warrenton, VA, USA) were used to carry out the statistical analyses. The statistical threshold of significance was set at 0.05. OS curves were estimated for each mice group using the Kaplan–Meier method and the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test was employed to compare the curves.

Results

In vivo evaluation of fludarabine, crenolanib, and cephalomannine

Fludarabine was evaluated at three different dosages, chosen based on a literature review: 200 mg/Kg, 100 mg/Kg and 40 mg/Kg. The treatment patterns for each dosage are summarized in Table 1. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were not significant for all dosages, showing similar OS compared to the control group (Log-rank, Mantel-Cox test: 200 mg/Kg – P = 0,6176; 100 mg/Kg – P = 0,2899; 40 mg/Kg—P = 0,1341) (Fig. 1A-C). Similarly, the assessment of crenolanib at 15 mg/Kg (Table 1) did not result in significant differences in survival compared to the control (Log-rank, Mantel-Cox test: P = 0,2906) (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, fludarabine at 40 mg/Kg was further assessed in combination with crenolanib at 15 mg/Kg (Table 1). As shown in Fig. 1, the combination did not induce any statistically significant change in OS (Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test: –P = 0,6158) (Fig. 1E). CPM was evaluated at 2 mg/Kg body weight, 1 dose/die, for 5 consecutive days, for 2 consecutive weeks (Table 1). Notably, the Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated a statistically significant increase in OS for the treated group (median 75 days) compared to the vehicle control group (55 days; Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test, P = 0,0021) (Fig. 1F).

In summary, CPM resulted in the only cytotoxic compound with activity in the PM-xenografted mouse model considered in this study. Representative images of tumour dimension and localization before and after CPM treatment, acquired by the in vivo imaging system IVIS, are shown in Fig. 2. This result prompted further investigation into potential biological activities on PM cells, building on previous studies on taxols in other types of cancer.

Initially, we investigated the oxidative status of PM cells treated with CPM, resulting in a significant increase in ROS production in malignant cells compared to non-malignant ones, as shown in Fig. 3.

Western blot studies were performed to assess CPM’s ability to induce apoptosis. Representative blots for BAX and Bcl2 protein expression are presented in Fig. 5A and C, with their densitometric quantification shown in Fig. 5B and D, respectively. In our study, CPM treatment led to a significant upregulation of BAX and downregulation of Bcl2 in the malignant cell line, indicating the occurrence of apoptosis. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 5A-D, CPM moderately altered the BAX/Bcl-2 ratio in MeT-5A. In summary, CPM significantly induced ROS production, caused alterations in the respiratory chain, and elicited apoptosis. Furthermore, we investigated IL-18 as a marker of the activation of the inflammasome pathway in MeT-5A and REN cells. As shown in Fig. 5E and F, IL-18 protein expression remained unchanged following CPM treatment, indicating that CPM has no effect on the regulation of this proinflammatory cytokine in these cells.

Discussion

In this study, we present the outcomes of treatments with fludarabine, crenolanib, and CPM in an PM-xenografted mouse model. These compounds were identified based on our prior in vitro studies, departing from a screening of a large library of approved drugs [1, 2]. In this specific in vivo model, only CPM demonstrated the ability to significantly extend the OS of mice. CPM, as taxol analogue, differs from PTX only in the amide portion, with a tigloylamide substituent at the C3’ position instead of benzamide [26]. PTX, derived from yew trees, is renowned as one of the most effective antineoplastic drugs and has demonstrated efficacy across a broad spectrum of tumors [3, 27]. Despite some limitations of the present study (small number of doses, limited available data on the pharmacokinetic of CPM, modest extension of the OS of mice) a key strength of the study lies in the similarity of CPM's chemical properties to those of PTX, which is already approved for human use. This could dramatically cut costs and time for testing on human patients, especially in the context of lethal diseases such as PM. As a matter of fact, CPM has shown promising anticancer activity in various preclinical models, including glioblastoma [28] and lung cancer [29].

Various medications containing PTX as the active substance have been approved by authorities for treating several types of cancers [30–34], including mesothelioma [35–40] (ID NCT05449366. ClinicalTrials.gov.). However, the antitumor efficacy of PTX is compromised by limitations such as its known non-linear pharmacokinetics [41] and the low solubility, leading to poor oral bioavailability [42]. Additionally, multidrug resistance can develop in cancer cells due to increased P-glycoprotein efflux [43, 44]. Moreover, around 70–85% of patients treated with PTX may experience hypersensitivity reactions, inflammation, and other important side effects [41, 45–48], especially in women [49]. Limited alternatives exist for patients who cannot tolerate PTX [34].

To overcome these effects, numerous new taxanes have been synthesized [50–53] and several, like BAY59-8862 [54], BMS-275183 [55], and MAC-321 [53], have progressed to clinical trials, reaching phase-II (refer to clinicaltrial.gov for more details). The focus of these developments has been on modifying the substituents at C3’ of the C13 side chain [53–55]. Interestingly, these structural modifications made the new taxanes effective against tumor cells resistant to PTX, sometimes exhibiting improved solubility, oral bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics [27, 56]. Additionally, the P450-oxidative catabolism of the different taxanes has been shown to vary significantly based on their slightly modified C3’ substituents [57, 58]. Interestingly, it has been demonstrated that the metabolism of CPM differs from that of PTX. While C6alpha of CPM and PTX still serves as a common hydroxylation site, the major hydroxylation site for CPM shifts to C4″ in the side chain, with CYP3A4 (instead of CYP2C8) becoming the primary metabolizing enzyme [26]. Given the very close structural similarities, it is not surprising that CPM demonstrates antitumor activity comparable to that of PTX in various cancer cell lines. Indeed, it has proven to be especially effective against leukemia, breast cancer, neuroblastoma, and glial neoplasia [5, 59–62]. In a drug screening approach similar to ours, Qiao and Kondo in 2018 found that CPM inhibited cell proliferation and induced autophagy in glioblastoma cells at a low concentration (IC50 < 100 nM), identifying CPM as one of the top candidate anti-glioblastoma drugs [28]. The anticancer activity of taxanes is well established, primarily attributed to their stabilizing effect of the microtubules of the kinetochore. These molecules inhibit microtubule assembly/disassembly, leading to mitotic arrest and cell death just after 3–5 min of a treatment with a dose of 10 µM [5, 6]. However, in our study, we investigated whether additional biological activities described for taxanes in various types of cancer could also be observed in PM cells. Our results supported the idea that taxanes exhibit multiple effects against cancer cells, extending beyond their well-known activity on the kinetochore. Indeed, our findings reveal that CPM induces increased oxidative stress in REN cells, consistent with observations for other taxanes [10]. In particular, we found a relative increase in the production of ROS after 24 h treatment with CPM 0.1 µM in REN cells of about + 85%, that is remarkably similar (+ 80%) to that observed at the same dose and time for PTX in the CHMm cell line, derived from a pleural effusion of a metastatic mammary cancer [11]. This oxidative stress leads to the alteration of the respiratory chain and the induction of apoptosis. REN cells treated with CPM showed a reduced OCR, particularly evident after FCCP injection, reflecting a deficient SRC and a reduced ATP production. According to our data, only a slightly reduced, however not statistically significant, proton leak was measured. ROS production may decrease because not enough electrons flow through the chain and the"leakage"for superoxide production doesn’t occur significantly. However, a chronic reduction in mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) activity can lead to oxidative stress through other mechanisms such as NADH accumulation or the activity of enzymes such as the cytoplasmic oxidases.

We acknowledge that another potential limitation of the present study was the not complete investigation of all the possible molecular pathways triggering apoptosis and all the possible effects occurring in mitochondrion. However, this was behind the scope of our study, as we were aiming to investigate whether CPM could reproduce, on PM cells, the effects observed in other cancer cell lines for PTX. Indeed, we replicated these findings accomplishing the established goals. In fact, in our experiments, CPM at 0.1 µM for 72 h of exposures produced alterations in BAX and Bcl-2 also reported for PTX in other malignant (i.e. A2780 ovarian carcinoma, SHEP and SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells and MCF-7 breast carcinoma) and non-malignant (mouse embryonic fibroblasts MEFs) cell models, when the same doses and exposure times were employed [63, 64]. The in vivo results are of paramount importance, considering the lack of knowledge about the behavior of CPM in vivo, both in general and particularly in PM. Additional in vivo experiments could be designed to gather details on the pharmacokinetic and dynamic profile of CPM, offering valuable insights for future advancements in this field toward human application. It is expected that most of the pharmacokinetic parameters of CPM are similar to PTX, as differences are predicted to be linked to a different biotransformation provided mostly by CYP3A4 (on the former) and CYP2C8 (on the latter) while adsorption, distribution, and excretion are expected to be similar. The different catabolism is likely to affect the plasma stability of CPM, however this parameter is also affected by how taxols are administered (either as micelles, nanoparticles or pure formulation, as reviewed in [28, 41]). It should be also considered that the intraperitoneal administration is absorbed from the digestive tract and subsequently reaches the liver in a process that could lead to a more rapid metabolization. In case the treatments were performed intrapleurally it could be expected that the rate of entry into the systemic circulation is slower producing a higher local concentration. However, we preferred the intraperitoneal injection for avoiding the high risk of side effects with possible high rate of death animals.

In summary, since CPM replicates the same activities of PTX but it presents different catabolism, it is conceivable that, with the appropriate formulation, it could be one candidate taxol to be used as alternative for patients who do not tolerate the toxicity of PTX.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study, along with previous research, suggests that CPM could be a promising anticancer agent, especially in the context of PM. Despite encouraging results in some preclinical models, clinical trials on CPM are still needed to evaluate its safety and efficacy in humans and to assess whether it could be employed as an alternative when cancer cells develop resistance and/or patients experience side effects under PTX therapies.
