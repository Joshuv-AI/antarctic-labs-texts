Multimodal contrastive prognostication framework for early neurological outcome prediction in post-cardiac arrest patients
Authors: Akhil Kasturi, Ashley R. Proctor, Ali Vosoughi, Chloe T. Zhang, Nathan Hadjiyski, Sydney V. Palka, Jenna Gonillo Davis, Lisa M. Cardamone, Samantha Helmy, Jeronimo Cardona, Thomas W. Johnson, Yang Gu, Mark A. Marinescu, Olga Selioutski, Regine Choe, Imad R. Khan, Axel Wismüller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) remains a critical public health challenge with mortality rates close to 90%. Current prognostication methods commonly analyze data of individual modalities separately and delay assessment until 72 hours post-arrest, creating a critical gap in early decision-making. Here, we introduce contrastive language and image reasoning with masked autoencoders (CLAIR), a novel multimodal framework integrating head computed tomography (CT) imaging with non-imaging clinical patient information through a cross-attention mechanism and contrastive learning approach to predict cerebral performance category (CPC) score in patients after cardiac arrest. In a retrospective study of 208 patients, we evaluated CLAIR against CT-based imaging-only assessment, as well as clinical evaluation by two experienced ICU neurologists. Our method achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.94 (CI: 0.90-0.97) when trained on a combination of multiplanar CT reconstructions and non-imaging clinical data, significantly outperforming CT scan-based imaging-only methods (AUC-ROC: 0.80, CI: 0.74-0.86) with statistical significance (p = 0.03). In a structured evaluation, the clinicians suggested that CLAIR assisted assessments resulted in fewer prognostic errors than non-assisted evaluations. Further, we demonstrate the applicability of our approach for early neurologic outcome prediction using CT scans obtained within the first 24 hours post-arrest (median acquisition time: 3.1 hours). Our results suggest that CLAIR can contribute value as a clinical assistive tool aiming at reliable early prognostication for post-cardiac arrest patients, potentially enabling more timely clinical decision-making, family counseling, and resource allocation.

Introduction

Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) remains a critical public health challenge, affecting over 600,000 individuals annually in the United States with mortality rates approaching 90%1. Despite recent advances in resuscitation science, meaningful improvements in outcomes remain elusive. Following return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), most survivors remain comatose for prolonged periods, sometimes indefinitely, due to evolving hypoxic-ischemic brain injury2. Recent conventional and deep learning-based studies on post-cardiac arrest prognostication employ various individual predictors, including neurological examination findings such as pupillary reflexes3 and motor responses3, electrophysiological measurements like electroencephalography (EEG) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP)4–7, serum biomarkers like neuron-specific enolase (NSE)8, and neuroimaging findings from Computed Tomography (CT)9,10 and diffusion-weighted (DW) Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)11. However, these approaches typically analyze each modality independently rather than integrating them into a comprehensive multimodal predictive system that could leverage their collective prognostic value.

Current prognostication methods face significant limitations that hamper timely and accurate neurological assessment after cardiac arrest. Recent studies suggest that no individual predictor achieves absolute certainty when forecasting poor neurological outcome12, leading to potential diagnostic uncertainty or false predictions when used in isolation12. Clinical examinations lack reliability due to confounding effects of sedation and neuromuscular blockade3,12, while electrophysiological tests suffer from methodological inconsistencies and often lack standardized interpretation6,12. Serum biomarkers like NSE have shown wide variability limiting their standalone prognostic value despite their theoretical advantages12. Neuroimaging studies frequently involve two methods for the quantification of anoxic brain injury after SCA to predict the neurological outcome, the CT-based gray-white ratio (GWR) and diffusion weighted imaging MRI-based apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC)13,14. These quantitative measures have not gained widespread clinical acceptance, primarily because GWR relies on manual measurement, which can vary based on CT scanner characteristics. Furthermore, MRI access is often limited during short-staffed periods such as nights or weekends. This equipment and personnel-intensive imaging modality frequently impedes timely image acquisition in acutely brain-injured patients, leading many institutions to prioritize CT as the imaging modality of choice. These limitations create a critical neurodiagnostic gap that obscures a potentially intervenable moment in the early hours after cardiac arrest. Additionally, the current practice of delaying prognostication until at least 72 hours15 post-ROSC creates a critical gap during the early post-arrest period when clinical decisions regarding resource allocation and therapy intensification are most impactful.

Although these limitations highlight the need for a multimodal assessment methodology for early neurological prognostication that can inform time-sensitive clinical decisions while maintaining prognostic accuracy, surprisingly little scientific effort has been devoted to developing multimodal machine learning approaches to cope with this challenge, even though both US and European guidelines on assessing disorders of consciousness recommend a multimodal assessment (MMA) that combines behavioral, neuroimaging, and electrophysiological metrics16. To address this urgent need, we introduce the Contrastive Language and Image Reasoning with Masked Autoencoder (CLAIR) model for early neurological prognostication in post-cardiac arrest patients. Our overall goal for conducting this work was to demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed CLAIR multimodal framework by investigating its performance for early neurological prognostication in post-cardiac arrest patients within a retrospective study encompassing clinical and imaging data from 208 patients. Further, we aimed to validate whether integrating head CT imaging with clinical parameters enables accurate outcome prediction within 24 hours of ROSC, rather than requiring the conventional 72-hour waiting period, thereby providing clinicians with a reliable assistive tool for earlier therapeutic decision-making, family counseling, and resource allocation.

Results

Patient Characteristics. A total of 240 adult patients (age \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥ 18) were collected from the University of Rochester Medical Center. Of those, 32 patients who withdrew from life-sustaining treatment solely due to non-neurologic reasons were excluded. As shown in Fig. 1, the remaining 208 patients received 306 primary CT scans, and every scan’s axial, sagittal, and coronal reconstruction was obtained in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) file format. The median age was 59 years (Interquartile Range (IQR) 49–68 years), among these patients were 37% females. Patient data stratified by neurological outcome (overall, good, poor) and data split (Training, Testing, Validation) is summarized in Table 1, demonstrating comparable key patient demographic and clinical biomarkers information in each group.

In the cohort, the median time from cardiac arrest to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) across all patients was 15 minutes (IQR 9.5–27 minutes). The good outcome group demonstrated a median ROSC time of 10 minutes compared to 20 minutes in the poor outcome group, representing a 10-minute difference between groups. Moreover, 62% of the overall cohort underwent therapeutic hypothermia to 33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}∘C.

From the entire dataset of 208 patients (306 scans), 133 patients were used to construct and train the model. These provided a total of 369 multiplanar CT scan reconstructions from 123 initial examinations and 324 reconstructions from 108 follow-up examinations.

We restricted our test and validation sets exclusively to the axial CT scan reconstructions obtained within the first 24 hours following cardiac arrest (initial scans) to ensure clinically realistic assessment conditions and early applicability of our prognostic model for post-ROSC decision-making (Fig. 1). All splits were performed at the patient level with no subject overlap between training, validation, and test sets. The validation set comprised 30 axial CT scans (15 patients with good neurological outcomes and 15 with poor outcomes), with a median post-ROSC acquisition time of 4.3 hours (IQR, 2.3–19.2). The test set included 45 axial CT scan reconstructions (22 good and 23 poor outcomes) with a median post-ROSC acquisition time of 3.2 hours (IQR, 2–7.7).

To improve neurological prognostication and produce a quantifiable prediction coefficient in post-cardiac arrest patients, we developed and evaluated CLAIR, a transformer-based multimodal framework integrating multi-view computed tomography (CT) scans (axial, coronal, sagittal) with demographic, clinical, and neurodiagnostic data. We assessed its performance using five-fold cross-validation and compared it against a Video Masked Auto Encoder Model (VideoMAE)17 fine-tuned on the current dataset.

We evaluated CLAIR using two sets of clinical information; CLAIR-I was trained and evaluated using multi-view CT and early clinical, demographic, and neuro-diagnostic data (Clinical Set I), and CLAIR-II used multi-view CT and comprehensive clinical data collected throughout hospitalization of a patient (Clinical Set II). For comparison, VideoMAE-Ax trained on axial CT scans and VideoMAE-ACS trained on Axial, coronal and sagittal CT scans were used.

The integration of clinical data significantly enhanced the performance of the CLAIR methods compared to the VideoMAE-based methods (Table 2, Fig. 2). VideoMAE-Ax and VideoMAE-ACS demonstrated moderate predictive capabilities, with AUC-ROC values of 0.79 and 0.80, respectively. In contrast, CLAIR-II exhibited improved performance, achieving an AUC-ROC of 0.92 and demonstrating the highest sensitivity (0.91). Notably, CLAIR-I achieved the best overall performance, with an AUC-ROC of 0.94, along with the highest specificity (0.91) and positive predictive value (PPV) (0.91). Both CLAIR models demonstrated an accuracy of 0.89, significantly surpassing the performance of VideoMAE models (0.72–0.73.72.73) (Table 2).

In this study, we developed CLAIR, a multimodal deep-learning framework aimed at early neurological prognostication for patients after cardiac arrest (CA). We validated our method’s efficiency through two evaluation approaches. In the first evaluation, we performed a five-fold cross-validation to assess model performance and compared it against current state-of-the-art methods. In the second evaluation, we compared the model performance against two clinicians on a smaller test set with 20 cases. The findings indicated that clinicians, when supported by CLAIR, exhibited fewer prognostic errors in comparison to their performance without AI assistance. However, given the limited sample size, these results should be interpreted as preliminary, and validation in larger, multi-center cohorts is needed to definitively validate the model’s clinical utility. Traditional clinical guidelines recommend delaying definitive neurological prognostication for a minimum of 72 hours post-return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) to mitigate potential confounding variables15,18. However, this 72-hour interval could be critical for reducing the likelihood of false-positive prognoses as early prognosis can significantly aid clinicians and families in making informed decisions19.. Recent studies in machine learning for neurological prognostication have primarily relied on neuroimaging techniques such as CT scans and MRIs to predict the neurological outcomes of a patient post-arrest. However, accurate neurological prognostication should consist of a comprehensive evaluation including patient demographics, clinical examinations, electrophysiological data, and serum biomarkers along with imaging findings15,20. While some studies have used various data types, such as laboratory results and neuroimaging in isolation19,21–25, these methods often show limitations, highlighting the need for a multimodal approach for more accurate and timely neurological outcome predictions for CA patients.

Individual clinical predictors have limited capacity to forecast outcomes on their own15. For example, demographic factors like age and gender are generally considered low quality predictors15. In contrast, time to ROSC, initial heart rhythm, and electrophysiological metrics such as somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) and electroencephalograms (EEG) are regarded as predictors of moderate quality15,20. High quality predictors include assessments like the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and neuron-specific enolase (NSE) values20. Based on this set of predictors, we integrated all features into the CLAIR framework, allowing the model to benefit from their complementary prognostic strengths. By unifying these predictors within our CLAIR architecture, we were able to enhance the contextual understanding of our model for each patient and yield more accurate predictions of these neurological outcomes. In a five-fold cross-validation on a test set of 45 patients, CLAIR demonstrated a significant improvement in area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.94 ± 0.03) compared to models with individual predictors.

We have evaluated two configurations of CLAIR and Video-MAE to evaluate the best model performance. Our results demonstrate that integrating clinical variables with imaging yields a consistently higher discriminative performance, with statistical significance (p = 0.031). This improvement highlights the value of multimodal integration, where imaging and clinical features together provide a more balanced and context-aware prediction.

Additionally, to examine our method’s clinical utility we have compared our model’s performance with two clinicians over a structured series of tests. The evaluation suggested that both clinicians, when assisted with CLAIR, demonstrated fewer prediction errors compared to the performance without the AI assistance. Interestingly, clinicians performed relatively better when restricted to early clinical data alone. This may have been due to conflicting results from various diagnostic tests, including NSE, EEG, and SSEP, all of which are available to clinicians at least 24 hours after admission. Scenarios where moderate- and high-quality prognostic indicators conflict each other present dilemmas for clinicians, and decision-making heuristics in these cases are poorly studied. CLAIR may be able to provide important prognostic clarity in these patients.

A review of two cases where Clinician I, assisted by CLAIR, made incorrect predictions revealed discrepancies between expected and actual outcomes. In patient 3,both the clinician and model suggested a good outcome, but the ground truth indicated a poor outcome due to the patient’s early withdrawal from life support. A subsequent review of the chart suggested a potential ’false withdrawal’ based on inaccurate neuroprognostication from clinicians or interpretation from the patient’s family member, indicating that the model’s initial prediction may have been accurate. This patient highlights an important inherent limitation of the model: the clinical outcome CLAIR is trained to predict can be influenced by bias from clinicians and legally authorized representatives (LAR). Most poor outcomes in our dataset were due to Withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy (WOLST), which is ultimately decided by the patient’s LAR and is representative of the main cause of death after cardiac arrest26. LAR decisions may be influenced by factors outside the scope of our model, including race and socioeconomic status27. Clinicians play a major role in this decision and may be influenced by implicit bias and uncertainty in interpreting discordant diagnostic data28. Ultimately, these subjective factors introduce the risk of self-fulfilling prophecies influencing machine learning algorithms predicting outcomes after cardiac arrest29. Future studies of prediction models such as ours should include larger subsets of patients with discordant diagnostic data and WOLST decisions to improve predictive ability.

In the case of Patient 4, both Clinician I and Clinician II, as well as the CLAIR model, predicted a favorable neurological outcome. However, the ground truth label indicated a poor outcome (CPC 5). Upon reviewing the patient’s medical records, we discovered that the patient had awoken after cardiac arrest to a good neurological outcome, but then experienced a second unrelated cardiac arrest and died before discharge. Due to the patient’s neurological function after the second arrest, the CPC score was assigned as 5. This highlights that unforeseen factors that can impact patient outcomes beyond the immediate clinical assessments. However, the AI-assisted performance for clinician I (Test 9) and clinician II (Test 10) was notably effective and accurate in comparison to the other tests (Test 1 - 8), suggesting the model’s ability to aid clinicians and residents for prognosis.

Our study has several limitations. First, the relatively modest retrospective sample size of 208 patients may limit the generalizability of our findings. While this cohort was sufficient for initial model development and validation, the limited sample size restricts our ability to perform a comprehensive clinical evaluation to assess the model’s clinical utility. Larger multicenter studies are essential to better validate our model’s clinical utility and establish its performance across diverse patient populations and clinical settings. Second, the predictions made by the CLAIR model rely heavily on the quality of ground-truth labels, which can be influenced by subjective interpretations and variability in clinical assessments, potentially introducing biases in model training and validation. Third, our retrospective study contains a very low number of CPC 3 cases, which has limited our model’s performance in these instances. Patients who classify as CPC 3 (severely neurologically disabled but conscious) upon discharge represent a complex, understudied subpopulation with potential for improvement after hospitalization and should be further studied with larger datasets.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multimodal deep learning framework to integrate axial head CT scans with their coronal and sagittal reconstructions along with clinical patient information through cross-attention mechanisms for early neurological prognostication within 24 hours of post-cardiac arrest.

Conclusion

This study presents a vision-language model designed for the prognostication of post-cardiac arrest patients through joint reasoning using both clinical and imaging data. Our findings indicate that this approach significantly enhances predictive accuracy compared to methods relying solely on CT scans for early neurological prognostication within 24 hours after the arrest. Additionally, our evaluation suggests that this method could serve as an effective assistive tool for clinicians and residents, in aiding them make more informed judgments during prognosis. Although our results demonstrate strength within our cohort, validation across multiple institutions and more diverse demographic samples is essential to ensure broader applicability of our method.

Methodology

Study design and data collection

This single-center observational retrospective study was approved by the Research Subjects Review Board (RSRB 00004678) at the University of Rochester Medical Center. The RSRB waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the minimal risk, retrospective design and use of de-identified data. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

The study was performed using data from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest for patients admitted to Strong Memorial Hospital (SMH), University of Rochester Medical Center, Rochester, NY, from January 2020 to December 2022. The study population comprised adult patients (age \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥ 18 years) hospitalized at SMH with a diagnosis of cardiac arrest during admission. Exclusion criteria included patients who underwent early withdrawal of care due to non-neurological reasons.

Patient demographics, cardiac arrest characteristics, laboratory values, and neurological exam data on hospital arrival and throughout admission were collected via comprehensive chart review. Neurological outcomes at discharge were determined from discharge summaries and physical and occupational therapy notes.

Outcome assessment

The Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) was used to quantify neurological outcomes, ranging from 1 (good cerebral performance) to 5 (brain death), and dichotomized to good (CPC 1–3) or poor (CPC 4–5) for analysis30. Withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy (WOLST) was categorized as CPC 5, and reasons for withdrawal were ascertained from documented discussions between clinicians and patients’ medical decision-makers. WOLST was characterized as medically driven if reasons were primarily influenced by somatic factors (refractory multi-organ failure or shock) versus neurologically driven if the decision was influenced primarily by the severity of brain injury. A neurointensivist (Dr. Imad Khan) reviewed each WOLST decision to adjudicate the primary reasons driving each decision.

CT image acquisition and processing

Non-contrast CT head scans were acquired using a Philips iCT 256 scanner with 2.5 mm slice thickness. From the dataset of 208 patients with 306 primary CT scans, axial, sagittal, and coronal reconstructions were obtained in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format for each scan.

We developed Contrastive Language and Image Reasoning with Masked Autoencoders (CLAIR), a multimodal vision-language framework designed for early neurological outcome prediction in post-cardiac arrest patients (Fig. 3). The architecture integrates multi-view head CT scans with corresponding clinical information through joint reasoning using four interconnected components: (1) 3D CT feature extraction with the help of a vision encoder (Fig. 3a), (2) text feature extraction and multimodal projection using clinical data (Fig. 3b), (3) neurological outcome prediction through a prognostic classifier (Fig. 3c), and (4) an adaptive loss function combining contrastive and classification objectives (Fig. 3d).

Vision encoder

For the CT feature extraction (Fig. 3a), we employ a Video Masked Autoencoder (VideoMAE)17 based encoder to extract rich spatiotemporal representations from the patient’s head CT volumes. VideoMAE is a self-supervised framework pre-trained on large-scale video datasets (e.g., Kinetics-40031). The processing workflow begins by partitioning each CT slice into non-overlapping 16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×16 pixel patches, which are then embedded using combined spatial-temporal patch embeddings to generate a sequence of feature tokens (I1, I2, I3,..., In), as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Positional encodings across both spatial and temporal dimensions are added to preserve the original ordering and slice relationships within the CT volume. The model adopts a “tube” masking strategy, where random masks are applied across CT slices, creating a challenging reconstruction task that encourages the vision encoder to learn meaningful 3D neuroanatomical structures and subtle pathological patterns indicative of neurological injury following cardiac arrest.

Text encoder

The structured clinical data (Fig. 3b) are processed using BioClinicalBERT32, a BERT-based Transformer architecture that converts patient information into rich semantic representations. BioClinicalBERT is built upon BioBERT33 (pre-trained on PubMed biomedical literature) and further pre-trained on clinical text and discharge summaries from MIMIC-III dataset34. This domain-specific pre-training enables the model to capture nuanced semantic relationships within clinical data, such as patient demographics, laboratory values, and diagnostic information.

Our approach transforms each patient’s diagnostic and demographic information into structured natural language sequences (Fig. 3b). Key clinical variables such as age, sex, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), initial cardiac rhythm, neuron-specific enolase (NSE) levels, time to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), and neurodiagnostic test outcomes (e.g., EEG and SSEP) are converted into semantically coherent sentences that preserve clinical context and relationships.

The resulting clinical sentences are tokenized using byte pair encoding with standard BERT formatting including, start-of-sequence (CLS) and end-of-sequence (SEP) tokens32,35. These tokenized sequences are subsequently processed using the pre-trained BioClinicalBERT encoder to generate dense text feature vectors (T) that capture clinical semantic information for downstream multimodal projection.

Cross attention decoder

To integrate the CT and clinical text features, we employ a transformer-based decoder block composed of stacked cross-attention36 layers (Fig. 3b). The cross-attention architecture in vision-language models (VLMs) was first introduced in Flamingo37 and later adopted by several recent VLMs38,39. As shown in the vision-language projection block of Fig. 3b, the image feature vectors I from the vision encoder and the text feature vectors T from the text encoder are integrated together through multimodal projection using stacked cross-attention blocks.

Image-text contrastive loss

To complement the binary classification and encourage tighter alignment between the imaging and clinical feature spaces, we compute a symmetric image-text contrastive loss42 by aligning the image features I from the vision encoder with the multimodal embeddings TI from the decoder in a shared latent space. This contrastive loss function encourages corresponding image-text pairs to exhibit high similarity while maximizing the distance between unrelated pairs.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and categorical variables as number of patients with percentages. Patient demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between groups using appropriate statistical tests based on data type and distribution. Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2 test was used for categorical variables. Model performance comparisons were evaluated using a one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test to assess directional improvements in AUC. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant; any p-value below 0.001 is reported as < 0.001. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.10.

Software and implementation

All deep learning models were developed and trained using Python 3.1044 with PyTorch 2.0.145 on the BlueHive computing cluster (Center for Integrated Research Computing) at the University of Rochester, using a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. CT scans in DICOM format were processed using pydicom 2.4.346. Statistical analyses, including Mann-Whitney U tests, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2 tests, and one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, were performed using SciPy 1.13.047.
