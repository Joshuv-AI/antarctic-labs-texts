Development and validation of a triglyceride-glucose index-based nomogram for predicting risk in STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI
Authors: Jinyong Huang, Junyi Zhang, Linjie Li, Meiyan Chen, Yongle Li, Xiangdong Yu, Shaozhuang Dong, Qing Wang, Jun Chen, Qing Yang, Shaopeng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a widely recognized surrogate marker of insulin resistance, has been extensively studied in the context of cardiovascular diseases. This study aimed to develop and validate a nomogram that predicts the prognosis of patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), utilizing the TyG index alongside readily available clinical data.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 1022 STEMI patients, divided into a training cohort (n = 715) and a validation cohort (n = 307). Potential risk factors were identified, and a predictive nomogram was constructed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-penalized Cox regression. Model performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration plots, Harrell’s C-index, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe nomogram incorporated five predictors: residual SYNTAX score (rSS), number of affected coronary vessels (VDn), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), TyG index, and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP). Harrell’s C-index values of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.70–0.75) in the training cohort and 0.80 (95% CI: 0.76–0.83) in the validation cohort demonstrated strong discrimination. The area under the curve (AUC) for 1-year and 2-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) predictions were 0.77 and 0.77 in the training cohort, and 0.86 and 0.85 in the validation cohort, highlighting excellent predictive accuracy. Subgroup analysis confirmed consistent model performance across age groups (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years), with AUC values ranging from 0.74 to 0.87. Calibration plots demonstrated excellent agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, and DCA highlighted the model’s clinical utility by quantifying net benefits across a range of threshold probabilities.ConclusionsThis study developed and validated a novel predictive nomogram that integrates the TyG index with key inflammatory (hsCRP), anatomical (rSS and VDn), and functional (BNP) markers to stratify MACE risk in STEMI patients after PCI. The model demonstrated robust predictive performance and clinical utility, offering a practical tool for precise risk stratification and facilitating personalized treatment strategies to improve long-term outcomes.Keywords: ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), Triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), Prediction nomogram

Background

Although mortality rates for coronary artery disease (CAD) have declined over the past four decades, it remains a leading cause of death, responsible for nearly one-third of all deaths in individuals over 35 years old [1]. Acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), a severe form of CAD, is associated with high rates of mortality and hospitalization [2]. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the most effective treatment for restoring blood flow in STEMI patients. However, despite emergency PCI, the 10-year cumulative all-cause mortality rate remains as high as 23.8% [3, 4]. To reduce the risk of future cardiovascular disease (CVD) events in high-risk individuals, tailored strategies are implemented to manage modifiable risk factors. Timely initiation of personalized therapy and effective risk factor management are critically important in clinical practice [5]. Identifying modifiable or persistent risk factors following stent implantation is crucial for improving the prognosis of high-risk STEMI patients.

Currently, widely used risk scoring systems such as GRACE and TIMI offer effective tools for assessing short- and long-term risks in STEMI patients [6–8]. However, these models primarily rely on clinical variables, such as age, cardiac function, and electrocardiogram findings, and do not fully incorporate metabolic-related biomarkers, which are essential for predicting long-term outcomes in STEMI patients. Given the growing recognition of the link between metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes, insulin resistance) and cardiovascular diseases, incorporating metabolic markers into risk prediction has become a key research focus.

Research indicates that insulin resistance (IR) contributes to various physiological disturbances, including endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, smooth muscle cell proliferation, increased inflammation, and excessive endothelin-1 production [9]. Recently, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has gained attention as an alternative marker of insulin resistance. The TyG index, calculated from fasting triglyceride (TG) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels, offers high clinical feasibility. Studies have demonstrated that the TyG index is closely associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and outperforms the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) in predicting the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases [10, 11]. In STEMI patients, the TyG index is strongly associated with cardiovascular events and can effectively predict prognosis [12].

However, a single predictive marker is insufficient for comprehensive risk assessment. Integrating multiple clinical features has become a growing trend to enhance predictive accuracy [13]. Previous studies have highlighted the prognostic value of factors such as residual SYNTAX score (rSS), the number of affected coronary vessels (VDn), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) in STEMI patients [14–17]. This study aims to develop a comprehensive predictive model integrating the TyG index and other clinical variables to assess the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after PCI in STEMI patients using a nomogram. This innovative model incorporates metabolic markers, coronary artery anatomical characteristics, and clinical variables, addressing gaps in existing models and providing precise support for personalized treatment in STEMI patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

A retrospective analysis of admission data was conducted for consecutive STEMI patients at Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, utilizing the Hospital Inpatient System database. The study period spanned from August 2018 to March 2023. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older and had a hospital-confirmed diagnosis of STEMI. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were assessed through in-hospital records and telephone follow-up. Of the 1,262 initial candidates, 240 participants were excluded based on predefined exclusion criteria: (1) patients who did not undergo coronary angiography (CAG) or received only CAG (n = 30), (2) individuals with severe hepatic or renal disease or active infection (n = 15), (3) patients with a history of myocardial infarction, PCI, or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) (n = 113), (4) participants with incomplete clinical data (n = 16), and (5) participants lost to follow-up (n = 66). A total of 1,022 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Patient grouping

This study aimed to develop a nomogram for predicting the prognosis of STEMI patients by integrating the TyG index with other routine clinical variables. To assess the model's stability and predictive accuracy, 1,022 patients were randomly assigned to training and validation cohorts. The training cohort was used to develop the model, while the validation cohort was employed to assess its internal validation. Patients were allocated using a random number generator to ensure comparable baseline characteristics across cohorts. The training cohort comprised 70% of the patients (n = 715), and the validation cohort comprised 30% (n = 307) (Fig. 1).

In machine learning and statistical modeling, dividing datasets into training and validation sets is essential for evaluating model performance and stability. A 70:30 split is a commonly used approach, ensuring adequate training data while retaining sufficient validation data to evaluate generalizability. Allocating 70% of the data for training ensures an adequate sample size for model fitting, while reserving 30% for validation effectively evaluates internal validation, and predictive accuracy. This distribution strikes a balance between model training stability and the assessment of generalizability, making it a widely adopted practice in high-impact medical research [18, 19].

Data collection

Data were retrieved and processed from inpatient and outpatient medical records. Baseline information collected for each patient included demographic data, clinical history, hematological parameters, echocardiographic findings, and PCI details. Demographic data included sex, age, height, weight (used to calculate body mass index [BMI]), heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and smoking status. Clinical history included diagnoses of diabetes, hypertension, and stroke. Hematological parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), hemoglobin (HGB), serum albumin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), hs-CRP, serum creatinine (Scr), BNP, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). The TyG index was calculated as the natural logarithm of [fasting TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL) / 2]. Echocardiographic measurements included left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). PCI-related data encompassed the surgical approach, number of stents, infarct-related artery (IRA), VDn, and implanted stents. The SYNergy between PCI with TAXUS and Cardiac Surgery (SYNTAX) score and rSS were calculated based on coronary artery lesion characteristics before and after the procedure. Data on prescribed medications were also recorded.

Clinical endpoint

Follow-up data were obtained through hospital records and telephone interviews conducted by experienced clinicians with patients and their families. Standardized computerized case report forms were used to ensure thorough data collection. The primary clinical endpoint of this study was the occurrence of MACE during the follow-up period after PCI. MACE was defined as a composite of all-cause death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, repeat revascularization, in-stent restenosis, heart failure, stroke, or transient cerebral ischemia.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2). Participants were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts in a 70:30 ratio using the R function “createDataPartition” to balance outcome events across the cohorts. The training cohort was used for variable selection and model construction, while the validation cohort was utilized to validate the model’s performance.

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, while skewed variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR, 25th–75th percentiles). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare continuous variables, while chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were applied to compare categorical variables.

The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model was employed to identify key clinical variables associated with the prognosis of STEMI patients. A primary advantage of LASSO is its ability to effectively handle high-dimensional data [20]. By incorporating a regularization parameter, LASSO automatically eliminates redundant features, thereby minimizing the risk of overfitting. In this study, LASSO regression was employed to select variables significantly associated with patient prognosis and to construct a nomogram. The choice of LASSO regression was based on three main considerations: (1) its ability to perform robust variable selection when dealing with a large number of predictors, ensuring both model stability and interpretability, which is particularly important in retrospective studies; (2) its effectiveness in preventing overfitting by controlling model complexity through regularization; and (3) its interpretability, as it automatically identifies and selects important variables, enhancing the clinical applicability of the resulting model [21]. To develop the LASSO model, data from the training cohort were used, and internal validation was performed using the validation cohort. The predictive accuracy of the model was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), with values closer to 1 indicating superior performance. Clinical variables were selected based on established associations with cardiovascular prognosis, such as age, sex, blood glucose levels, and lipid profiles. Variables demonstrating statistical significance were further refined using LASSO regression. Cross-validation was employed to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ) for the LASSO model.

The predictive ability of the nomogram model was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), while calibration curves were plotted to assess the concordance between predicted and observed outcomes in both training and validation cohorts [22]. To further evaluate the clinical applicability of the model, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted. Furthermore, a post-hoc subgroup analysis stratified by age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years) was performed to assess the model’s predictive performance across different age groups using time-dependent ROC curves in both cohorts.

A risk score was calculated for each patient cohort based on the nomogram, enabling the classification of patients into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted to compare survival probabilities between high-risk and low-risk groups stratified by the median risk score derived from the Cox proportional hazards model. Survival curves were generated, and differences between the groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics comparison between patients with and without MACE

Between August 2018 and March 2023, a study conducted at Tianjin Medical University General Hospital identified 1,262 STEMI patients, of whom 1,022 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. Over a median follow-up period of 477 days (IQR: 74–715), 477 of the 1,022 patients (46.7%) experienced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). The baseline characteristics of patients with and without MACE were compared (Table S1). Patients with MACE exhibited poorer metabolic profiles, characterized by elevated fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and hsCRP levels, as well as a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus. They demonstrated more severe coronary artery disease, as reflected by higher SYNTAX and rSS, along with a greater prevalence of multivessel disease. Additionally, lower left ventricular ejection fraction, reduced serum albumin, and elevated serum creatinine levels were observed in the MACE group. Notable differences in medication usage were observed, with higher rates of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in the MACE group. Furthermore, the follow-up duration was significantly shorter in the MACE group compared to those without MACE.

Refinement of predictive variables and construction of a nomogram model

The ‘glmnet’ package was used to perform LASSO-penalized Cox regression analysis on the training cohort to identify the most significant risk predictors [23]. Based on the results of the LASSO Cox regression analysis, a multivariate Cox regression model was constructed to predict patient outcomes [24]. Forty-seven candidate variables were subjected to LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation to identify the most informative predictors. The LASSO regression model identified five potential predictors of MACE in the training cohort based on nonzero coefficients: rSS, VDn, hsCRP, TyG index, and BNP (Fig. 2a and b). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further confirmed these five clinical variables as independent risk factors for MACE. These five independent risk factors were subsequently used to develop a nomogram in the training cohort for predicting 1-year and 2-year MACE (Fig. 3).

This nomogram integrates rSS (residual SYNTAX score), VDn (number of affected coronary vessels), hsCRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein), TyG index (triglyceride-glucose index), and BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide) to predict the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)

Model performance evaluation

The nomogram exhibited a C-index of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.70–0.75) in the training cohort and 0.80 (95% CI: 0.76–0.83) in the validation cohort. Time-dependent ROC curve analysis demonstrated AUC values of 0.77 (sensitivity: 0.83, specificity: 0.60, Youden index: 0.43) and 0.77 (sensitivity: 0.78, specificity: 0.65, Youden index: 0.43) for 1-year and 2-year MACE, respectively, in the training cohort (Fig. 4a). In the validation cohort, the corresponding AUC values were 0.86 (sensitivity: 0.74, specificity: 0.87, Youden index: 0.61) and 0.85 (sensitivity: 0.79, specificity: 0.85, Youden index: 0.64) for 1-year and 2-year MACE, respectively (Fig. 4b). Calibration plots assessed the concordance between predicted and observed probabilities for 1- and 2-year MACE risks (Fig. 4c and d). Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the nomogram provided net benefits across a broad range of threshold probabilities, underscoring its clinical utility (Fig. 5a and d). The prediction nomogram demonstrated excellent performance based on Harrell’s C-index, ROC curve analysis, calibration plots, and DCA.

Subgroup analysis

Given the study population’s relatively advanced median age of 66 years, an additional subgroup analysis was performed, stratifying by age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years), to assess the model’s predictive performance for MACE. Time-dependent ROC curve analysis revealed significant differences in AUC values between age subgroups in both the training and validation cohorts, highlighting the model’s ability to accurately predict outcomes across different age groups. In the training cohort, the AUC values for the < 65 years subgroup were 0.74 (1-year) and 0.76 (2-year), whereas for the ≥ 65 years subgroup, the AUC values remained consistently at 0.79 for both 1-year and 2-year predictions (Fig. S1a and S1b). Similarly, in the validation cohort, the AUC values for the < 65 years subgroup were 0.86 (1-year) and 0.87 (2-year), whereas the ≥ 65 years subgroup exhibited AUC values of 0.85 (1-year) and 0.82 (2-year) (Fig. S1c and S1d). These findings underscore the robustness and applicability of the model across age-stratified subpopulations.

Discussion

The incidence of STEMI has been rising alongside an aging population, contributing to increased hospitalizations and elevated mortality rates among patients with CAD [25]. However, a lack of reliable biomarkers to predict complications and prognoses in STEMI patients impedes the identification of patients who may benefit from aggressive post-PCI treatment. The pathophysiology of STEMI involves multiple factors related to coagulation, inflammation, and metabolism, necessitating timely intervention to minimize damage and improve outcomes. Mathematical models incorporating multiple markers are increasingly utilized in medicine, and recent observational studies have developed risk nomograms based on routine clinical characteristics to assess MACE risk in ACS patients [26, 27]. In this study, we developed a predictive nomogram integrating the TyG index, coronary angiography data (VDn and rSS), and inflammatory and cardiac function biomarkers (hsCRP and BNP) to estimate the risk of MACE in STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI. The model demonstrated robust predictive ability in both the training and validation cohorts, as evaluated by Harrell’s C-index, AUC values, calibration curves, and DCA, offering clinicians valuable insights for assessing patients’ long-term prognosis. Subgroup analysis further validated the nomogram’s consistent predictive performance across age groups (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years), with AUC values ranging from 0.74 to 0.87 for 1-year and 2-year MACE. These findings reinforce the model’s generalizability and its potential for clinical application across diverse patient populations. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to integrate the TyG index with coronary angiographic data in constructing a predictive model for MACE in STEMI patients. Notably, this predictive model delivers a more comprehensive and individualized risk stratification, which is crucial for optimizing clinical management and treatment strategies.

The TyG index has gained recognition as a straightforward yet potent marker of insulin resistance, combining two key biochemical indicators: triglycerides and fasting glucose [28]. Unlike standalone markers such as TG or cholesterol (CHOL), which have limited predictive value for coronary heart disease risk, the TyG index integrates metabolic information, providing a more comprehensive assessment of cardiovascular risk. Recent studies by Colantoni et al. and Tao et al. highlight its advantages over traditional lipid markers, such as CHOL or LDL-C, in predicting adverse cardiovascular events [28, 29]. Additionally, compared to other insulin resistance markers such as HOMA-IR, the TyG index offers greater robustness and clinical applicability in STEMI patients. The TyG index has been extensively studied as a predictor in various cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, including CAD, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and stroke [28, 30–34]. Notably, elevated TyG index levels have been independently linked to increased incidences of ASCVD and stroke [35]. In the context of NSTE-ACS patients treated with PCI, the TyG index was strongly correlated with the risk of MACE [36].

Our study builds upon existing evidence supporting the TyG index as a reliable marker of insulin resistance. Consistent with the findings of Zeng et al., our results demonstrate the independent predictive value of the TyG index for long-term outcomes in STEMI patients undergoing PCI [37]. Notably, our model extends previous research by incorporating coronary anatomical complexity, including rSS and VDn, which aligns with studies by Altekin et al. emphasizing the prognostic significance of residual lesion burden [38]. Furthermore, the inclusion of inflammatory biomarkers such as hsCRP adds incremental predictive value, consistent with the findings of Reinstadler et al. [39]. In contrast to studies relying solely on metabolic or anatomical markers, our model integrates metabolic, anatomical, and inflammatory features, significantly enhancing predictive accuracy and clinical applicability, thereby offering a more comprehensive risk prediction for STEMI patients. While left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and age are well-established predictors of adverse cardiovascular events, they were not included in our model due to their lack of statistical significance in the LASSO regression analysis. Variability in LVEF measurements due to imaging techniques and operator differences may reduce its predictive reliability [40, 41]. Similarly, while age is an important risk factor, its impact is often mediated by other clinical variables, such as comorbidities and disease severity, which are already captured by our model [42, 43]. The variables included in our model were selected based on their higher incremental predictive value, in line with contemporary predictive modeling strategies [22, 44]. These choices contributed to the robust performance of our model in risk stratification.

The inclusion of coronary artery lesion complexity, assessed using VDn and rSS scores, aligns with recent studies emphasizing the pivotal role of coronary anatomy in predicting clinical outcomes. Research by Altekin et al. demonstrated that the rSS score independently predicts mortality and MACE in STEMI patients following PCI [38]. Similarly, Burgess et al. found that an rSS > 8 is associated with increased rates of cardiac death and recurrent myocardial infarction [45]. Our study supports these findings and emphasizes the importance of incorporating coronary anatomy into risk stratification, reinforcing the understanding that coronary lesion complexity is crucial in determining the long-term prognosis of STEMI patients. Compared to the TyG index alone, the inclusion of coronary artery lesion complexity offers a more comprehensive representation of the patient’s cardiovascular pathology, making it an indispensable component of our model.

Inflammation plays a central role in the pathophysiology of STEMI, with hsCRP serving as a well-established marker of systemic inflammation. Previous research has shown that elevated hsCRP levels are associated with unfavorable long-term outcomes in STEMI patients. Our findings align with those of Reinstadler et al., who demonstrated that hsCRP levels ≥ 3 mg/L independently predict long-term cardiovascular outcomes [39]. Furthermore, Li et al. demonstrated that elevated admission hsCRP levels independently associate with impaired microvascular reperfusion and higher rates of adverse cardiovascular events following PCI [46]. Our study extends this evidence by demonstrating that incorporating hsCRP with other clinical variables, such as the TyG index and coronary lesion complexity, adds incremental predictive value, enabling a more refined and nuanced risk stratification.

BNP is widely acknowledged as a prognostic marker for both heart failure and overall cardiovascular risk. In STEMI patients, elevated BNP levels are indicative of heart failure and are associated with adverse long-term outcomes. Our findings align with studies by Jarai et al. and Qin et al., which demonstrated that elevated BNP levels independently predict poor long-term outcomes, including higher rates of cardiovascular mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events in STEMI patients following revascularization [47, 48]. Although BNP was not analyzed as a standalone predictor in our model, its inclusion, alongside other clinical variables, enhances the model’s predictive accuracy and clinical relevance.

Compared to existing models such as GRACE and TIMI, which primarily rely on clinical parameters like age, heart rate, and cardiac biomarkers [7, 49], this model integrates metabolic markers (TyG index), anatomical factors (rSS and VDn), inflammatory indicators (hsCRP), and cardiac function markers (BNP), offering a more comprehensive and nuanced approach to risk stratification. While SYNTAX-based models account for coronary lesion complexity [50], they often omit metabolic and inflammatory factors, despite the well-established significance of these factors in long-term risk assessment [36]. The nomogram demonstrated robust predictive performance, with a C-index of 0.73 in the training cohort and 0.80 in the validation cohort, highlighting the value of an integrated model for personalized risk assessment and optimization of treatment strategies in high-risk STEMI patients.

The nomogram developed in this study provides an accessible and practical tool for evaluating the risk of MACE in STEMI patients following PCI. By integrating key clinical, anatomical, and inflammatory biomarkers, the model enables precise risk stratification and personalized treatment, thereby facilitating informed clinical decision-making and efficient resource allocation, especially in settings with limited access to advanced imaging or laboratory tests. Unlike existing prediction tools, which typically rely on limited variables such as age, sex, and ejection fraction, our model offers a more comprehensive assessment by incorporating functional, metabolic, and anatomical markers. This tool can guide more aggressive interventions for high-risk patients while reducing the risk of overtreatment for low-risk patients. Further validation in diverse clinical settings and integration into electronic health record systems will be crucial for broader applicability and for enhancing clinical decision-making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, there is a potential for selection bias, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. The inclusion criteria, data collection, and outcome assessment were dependent on existing medical records, introducing the possibility of inconsistencies or missing data. Key variables, such as medication adherence, lifestyle changes, and dynamic clinical events during follow-up, were not fully captured, potentially affecting the accuracy and completeness of the results. Second, the relatively short follow-up period, which primarily focused on 1-year and 2-year MACE risk, represents a critical but limited timeframe for assessing major adverse events in STEMI patients. The lack of data on outcomes beyond 2 years restricts the evaluation of the model’s long-term predictive performance. The absence of extended follow-up data limits the ability to validate the model’s performance over time. Future studies with longer follow-up periods, such as 5 or 10 years, are necessary to assess the nomogram’s predictive ability for chronic risk stratification and explore dynamic predictors. Third, while LASSO regression effectively minimized overfitting by selecting the most predictive variables, there remains a risk of overfitting the model to the specific dataset used. This limitation underscores the importance of external validation in diverse, multicenter cohorts to ensure the robustness and reproducibility of the nomogram across broader clinical settings. Fourth, the exclusion of certain patient subgroups, such as those with prior myocardial infarctions or revascularization procedures, aimed to create a more homogeneous study population and enhance model performance. However, this approach may limit the applicability of the model to real-world clinical scenarios where recurrent events are common. Future research should include these subgroups to improve the nomogram’s generalizability and utility. Fifth, we acknowledge that the median age of the study population was 66 years, representing an older cohort, which may limit the model’s applicability to younger patients. Additionally, certain atypical STEMI cases were not included in this study. Future research will aim to expand the study population to encompass a broader age range and include atypical STEMI presentations, ensuring the model’s applicability across more diverse and representative clinical scenarios. Finally, although the model integrates key predictors—including the TyG index, rSS, VDn, hsCRP, and BNP—certain potentially significant variables, such as smoking status, cholesterol levels, and kidney function, were excluded. While their effects may have been indirectly captured by other predictors, their omission may limit the model’s applicability in specific patient populations or healthcare settings. Further studies incorporating these variables may refine the model and enhance its clinical relevance.

In summary, despite these limitations, the nomogram provides a novel and practical tool for predicting MACE in STEMI patients following PCI. Prospective, multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods and broader patient populations are essential to validate and further optimize the model for widespread clinical adoption.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated a predictive nomogram that integrates metabolic (TyG index), anatomical (rSS and VDn), inflammatory (hsCRP), and functional (BNP) markers to assess the risk of MACE in STEMI patients following PCI. The nomogram demonstrated robust predictive performance and clinical utility, facilitating more precise risk stratification and enabling personalized treatment strategies. By incorporating a diverse range of clinical variables, this model offers a comprehensive and innovative approach to optimizing the long-term management of STEMI patients. Further multicenter studies are needed to validate its applicability across broader patient populations.
