Stepped rehabilitation of people with persistent shoulder pain in New Zealand (StePS-NZ): a randomised clinical trial protocol
Authors: Gisela Sole, Margie Olds, Ricky Bell, Christina Douglas, Ari Samaranayaka, Trudy Sullivan, Jean-Sébastien Roy, François Desmeules, Nicola Swain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRotator cuff-related shoulder pain (RCRSP) is a common musculoskeletal disorder, with prevalence increasing with age. In Aotearoa New Zealand, many people with RCRSP are compelled to seek physiotherapy privately as hospital outpatient clinics have long waiting lists, or go without care. We test the efficacy of Stepped Care, a new approach to RCRSP primary care, to use resources as efficiently as possible. This study aims to (i) define the effectiveness of a Stepped Care Model for persistent RCRSP compared to usual, pragmatic physiotherapy; (ii) explore patients’ and the physiotherapists’ perspectives and experiences of the interventions and research processes; and (iii) compare the cost-effectiveness of the Stepped Care Model with pragmatic physiotherapy.MethodsThis pragmatic, two-armed parallel-group randomised clinical trial will enrol 200 adults with RCRSP, who are randomised to receive either Usual Physiotherapy or Stepped Care. In the Stepped Care group, all patients receive two 1-h sessions focussing on patient education. After 6 weeks, those with persistent symptoms receive pragmatic physiotherapy, while those whose symptoms have resolved will receive no further intervention. The Usual Physiotherapy group receives up to 10 sessions of pragmatic care over a 3-month period. The primary outcome is upper limb function and severity of symptoms using the QuickDASH at 3 months. Secondary outcomes includes pain severity, pain-related fear, pain self-efficacy and health-related quality of life, assessed at baseline, 6 weeks, 3, 6, and 12 months. Outcome measures in continuous scale, including QuickDASH, will be analysed to estimate the difference in the measure between two physiotherapy treatments, while the measures in categorical/binary scales will be analysed to estimate the differences of proportions with favourable outcomes. Confidence intervals will be used to make noninferiority interpretations. A cost-effectiveness analysis will be conducted at the individual level and reported from a societal perspective.DiscussionIf the findings from this study support a stepped care approach to treatment for RCRSP, it could lead to more treatment being available at lower cost to both individuals and the health system.Trial registrationUniversal Trial Number: U1111-1298–4087. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSKD) were globally ranked first, contributing towards 12% of total years lost due to disability [1]. The prevalence rate of MSKD is higher in females and increases with age in both sexes [2]. In the Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ) context, direct access physiotherapy (not requiring a referral from a medical doctor) forms part of the primary care system for most MSKDs. Health care for MSKD may be covered by the Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC, NZ’s national taxpayer-funded no-claim injury compensation system) if the patient reports an injury or accident as the cause of symptom onset, yet in many cases, patients pay a surcharge for physiotherapy, incurring costs for every session. As ACC does not cover claims for atraumatic MSKD, patients with such conditions access private physiotherapy at their own cost, seek appointments in the Te Whatu Ora-Health NZ (Health NZ) orthopaedic out-patient clinics or with general practitioners (GPs), or do not access health care.

Disorders of the shoulder girdle are one of the most common types of MKSD [3]. Around 50% of cases of shoulder pain resolve within a few weeks; for the remaining cases, symptoms may be unresolved for three or more years [4, 5]. Around 50–70% of primary care patients with shoulder pain have rotator cuff-related shoulder pain (RCRSP) [6], an umbrella term that includes conditions previously termed subacromial pain impingement syndrome, rotator cuff tendinopathy, and symptomatic partial or full thickness rotator cuff tears [7]. The main mechanism is now proposed to be intrinsic physiological overload of the tendons [8, 9] and inflammatory changes at the tissue level [10]. The symptoms are influenced by multiple factors, including nervous system sensitisation to movement and a lowered pain threshold [10, 11], psychosocial factors (depression, anxiety, stress, poor sleep) [12, 13], and health-related comorbidities [8, 14]. The disorder is costly in terms of personal suffering [15], health, and work-related expense [3].

Besides the personal financial burden for attending private physiotherapy (ACC surcharge for traumatic conditions or full fees for non-traumatic conditions), other barriers for health care exist. In the Health NZ system, long waiting lists occur for non-critical MSKD physiotherapy care, anecdotally of between 3 and 11 months, compounded by staff shortages [16]. Further, statistics provided by ACC (2020) indicate that Māori and Pasifika (indigenous people of New Zealand and of the Pacific Islands respectively) are under-represented in ‘soft tissue’ shoulder claims. Those statistics support a recent clinical audit of patients accessing two shoulder-specialist physiotherapy clinics [17], collectively suggesting a continuing disparity in health care access for shoulder disorders. Lower access for indigenous and other patient groups may be due to unaffordability of treatment, transport costs, time off work, unawareness, or low trust in the health system [18]. Government-led initiatives currently explore clinical prototypes to reduce hospital specialist waiting lists and to improve health equity for people with MSKD [19]. New approaches are also needed at primary care level to improve timely and equitable access for people with MSKD, including shoulder pain.

Physiotherapy interventions for RCRSP use an individualised, pragmatic approach of patient education, exercise and physical activity prescription, and manual therapy to the shoulder girdle and spine [20]. These interventions usually require frequent visits over several weeks, adding indirect costs of travel and time off work or from other commitments. To address challenges for access, physiotherapy services need to be more adaptable. A care pathway that first offers patient education and explores pain self-management, providing additional physiotherapy interventions only to those whose symptoms remain unresolved, as in a Stepped Care Model [21], may reduce waiting lists, potentially offering more timely appointments and potentially decreasing costs.

In a Stepped Care Model [21], patients receive sessions that focus on improving their understanding of their condition, self-management skills, and healthy lifestyles, offering those with on-going pain and disability pragmatic physiotherapy. What we do not know is whether a Stepped Care model is as effective as immediate usual, pragmatic physiotherapy, whether it is acceptable for users in the NZ context, or if it is cost-effective.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of a Stepped Care Model for persistent RCRSP compared to usual, pragmatic physiotherapy. The second objective is to explore patients’ and physiotherapists’ perspectives and experiences of the interventions and research processes. The third objective is to compare the cost-effectiveness of the Stepped Care Model with usual physiotherapy. We hypothesise that providing patient advice/education first in a Stepped Care Model and usual physiotherapy only to patients with unresolved symptoms, will be as effective as usual physiotherapy, and more cost effective.

Trial design {8}

The Stepped Care for Persistent Shoulder Pain (StePS-NZ) trial is a multi-centre non-inferiority, pragmatic randomised clinical trial (RCT) with 2-arm parallel groups and 1:1 allocation ratio to compare a stepped physiotherapy care to usual physiotherapy for people with RCRSP. We include a quantitative and qualitative process evaluation and an economic evaluation. The protocol conforms to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [22, 23]. We define the interventions for each trial arm using the Template for intervention description and replication (TIDier) framework [24].

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Study setting {9}

Four private physiotherapy practices in four geographically diverse NZ areas provide the physical screening and interventions: two in the North Island (a specialist-shoulder clinic in Auckland and a rurally-based private practice in the Waikato) and two in the South Island (a Christchurch private community-based practice and a Dunedin university-based clinic). Regular contact and support are provided by the project manager to ensure that study fidelity is maintained.

For the purpose of this trial, we define RCRSP clinically as pain in the proximal lateral aspect of the upper arm, with or without reduced muscle strength during shoulder elevation or external rotation, and a painful active range of motion [26]. At least three of five clinical tests should be painful: painful arc (pain during active elevation) [27], pain or weakness during resisted isometric external rotation [27], Jobe/empty can test [28], Hawkins test [29], and the Neer test [30]. We do not confirm the diagnosis of RCRSP with ultrasound imaging as that is not usual practice for physiotherapists in the NZ context.

Eleven physiotherapists across the four clinics provide the intervention. The same physiotherapists provide care for both groups at each clinic to minimise potential confounders of the patient-therapist relationship (the therapeutic alliance) [32] and the physiotherapist’s clinical experience on patient outcomes. To allow generalisability to the NZ physiotherapy workforce, physiotherapists of varying experience are included, from early graduate (≤ 3 years post-graduation) to experienced (> 10 years) clinicians.

Recruitment {15}

We are recruiting participants via advertising on social media, GP, and physiotherapy practices. Hard copy advertisements are posted on community noticeboards (such as at supermarkets), and in staff rooms of local supermarkets and hardware stores. Based on the findings of our feasibility study [33], we overestimate that 18 months will be sufficient to recruit 200 patients across the 4 centres. We are using preferential sampling to ensure we have representative numbers for Māori and Pasifika.

Advertisements include a QR code to a University of Otago webpage with brief information about the study and an attached participant information sheet. The webpage links to an online questionnaire relating to inclusion criteria (i) and (ii) and exclusion criteria (i) and (ii). Those who meet these criteria enter their name and contact details in the online questionnaire or can send an email to a study-specific email address (accessed by the project manager and principal investigator) or phone the project manager. The project manager follows up with an email or phone call to answer any questions relating to the study. When a respondent who passes the initial online screen confirms continued interest, their details are referred to the closest provider clinic to attend a physical screen [for inclusion criteria (iii) and exclusion criterion (iii) and (iv)] by a physiotherapist trained by the research team about the study processes. The process and outcome of the physical screening appointment are noted by the physiotherapist on a REDCap survey, accessible by the project manager.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

The physiotherapist informs respondents at the end of the screening session about their eligibility and provides answers for any remaining questions prior to the participant providing written or online (via REDCap) informed consent. If the respondent is still undecided about participating at the end of the screening appointment, they will be given a few days to make their decision, followed up by a phone call by the project manager. If they agree, they are sent a link to the REDCap consent form for completion. Once the participant has completed the REDCap consent form, the project manager receives an automated email alert.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Participants’ consent to the collection and use of their data, including in future research within NZ and overseas.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Eligible and consented participants are block randomised on a 1:1 ratio to one of the two groups, stratified by centres and ethnicity (Māori/Pasifika versus all others). The Dunedin-based project manager generated the allocation sequence in REDCap under supervision of the biostatistician (AS) prior to the start of recruitment. Once the project manager has received the REDCap email alert of a consented participant, they allocate participants to the intervention groups centrally, and informs the relevant clinic via their respective practice management software. The participant is considered entered to the study once allocated to a group. The clinic administrator then contacts the participant to arrange the first physiotherapy session.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

REDCap’s sequence concealment is used for treatment allocation. That function prevents the prediction of any of the allocations prior to actual allocation and cannot be modified once the allocation is finalised ensuring allocation concealment and preventing undue bias in group allocations [34].

Implementation {16c}

The project manager generated the allocation sequence, enrolls participants and assigns them to interventions. After assignment, the clinical practice takes over management of the patient.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

We compare outcomes of a Stepped Care physiotherapy intervention with that of usual, pragmatic physiotherapy.

Intervention description {11a}

Both groups receive individualised physiotherapy over a maximum of 3 months and have access to a NZ-based website for people with shoulder pain (www.shoulderpain.org.nz). Principles of a person-centred approach are used [35]. Please see the TIDieR checklist for more details (Supplementary material).

Group 1: Usual physiotherapy intervention includes up to 10 sessions over the 3-month period, as per our previous feasibility trial [33]. The first two sessions (up to 1 h) entail a full clinical assessment as per the physiotherapist’s usual practice, and initiation of the intervention. Thereafter the sessions are ½ h each. Individualised physiotherapy can include manual therapy to the shoulder and spine, based on pragmatic clinical decision making [33, 36–38]. A graded exercise programme starts with low intensity, pain-free exercises, focusing on movement quality, often defined as ‘motor control’ exercises [39]. Exercises are progressed to moderate-intensity exercises based on individual assessments. Defined individual pain tolerance levels guide repetitions/loads and progressions. General physical activity is included, such as walking, stationary bike, running, or gym training. Patient education is integrated across the physiotherapy sessions, tailored to the individual participant. That may include information about the condition, basic pain physiology, pain self-management (including activity modification), the role of exercise and physical activity for the management of pain, and lifestyle factors (physical activity, weight management, sleep, stress management/relaxation) [33]. The frequency of the sessions and discharge from physiotherapy (if prior to 10 sessions or 3 months) are determined collaboratively by the participant and the physiotherapist.

Group 2: Stepped Care intervention includes two initial physiotherapy sessions of the patient education and self-management programme. The first session is 1 h long and includes a ~ 20-min clinical assessment of the participant’s pain, goals and preferences. Patient education relates to their condition/injury, basic pain neuroscience, pain self-management (including activity modification) and lifestyle factors (including relaxation, sleep management) [33, 40]. A general physical activity is prescribed. A general physical activity programme can be as effective as specific shoulder exercises for people with shoulder pain [39] and follows contemporary understanding for management of persistent pain [41]. The second session is scheduled 2–3 weeks later and ranges between ½ and 1 h, depending on the physiotherapist’s clinical judgement. During that session, the physiotherapist reviews the participant’s status, self-management ability and physical activity programme, and explores person-specific long-term self-management strategies for shoulder health.

At 6-week follow-up, those with resolved symptoms (abbreviated Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand questionnaire, QuickDASH, ≤ 11.5, expected to be 40–45% patients of this group [39]) have no further physiotherapy (Group 2a). Those with unresolved symptoms (QuickDASH > 11.5) receive follow-up interventions by the physiotherapist as for Group 1, with up to 8 sessions over 6 weeks (total maximum: 10 sessions, Group 2b).

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participants of the Stepped Care group are asked to report unchanging or intolerable pain during the first 6 weeks (that is, prior to offering usual physiotherapy for those with remaining QuickDASH > 11.5). Such participants are offered usual, pragmatic physiotherapy earlier. Participants whose symptoms are resolved at 6 weeks (QuickDASH ≤ 11.5) but have a flare-up during the next 6 weeks that they are concerned about, can access pragmatic physiotherapy up to 3 months following the first session, and thus become part of Group 2b.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Training of physiotherapists

The physiotherapists and project manager attended a 1-day face-to-face training course delivered by 3 research team members (RB, NS, GS). Five further sessions were held via Zoom, ranging between 1 and 2 h. Training included (1) principles for health conversations and patient engagement, reflective listening [42], motivational interviewing and coaching skills [43–45]; (2) cultural responsiveness for physiotherapists for the NZ context; (3) use of education resources and principles of behaviour change and self-management [46]; (4) manual therapy, and exercise and physical activity prescription as recommended by clinical practice guidelines; and (5) trial-specific clinical notes documentation and data management [47].

Participants’ compliance with the intervention

Physiotherapists monitor participants’ compliance during follow-up sessions, and participants are asked to complete weekly diaries about their physical activity and exercise completions on the mobile App, MyCap (REDCap).

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Participants are asked not to seek care for the same condition from other physiotherapists, chiropractors or osteopaths prior to discharge from the physiotherapy care provided by this study. They can seek GP consultations or be referred to the GP by the study physiotherapist for their shoulder pain which are documented in the clinical patient notes or for participants to add to their diary. If the physiotherapist or the GP considers that imaging (e.g. ultrasound) or a cortisone injection is required during the 3-month period, that will be noted in the clinical notes. They may continue to take pain medication (over the counter or prescribed) and may seek Rongoā Māori (traditional interventions), documenting those in their diary.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

If participants are deemed to require treatments beyond the trial, they will be offered physiotherapy in the provider clinic or referred to a clinic of the participant’s preference. Such treatments will be self-funded, or a claim to the ACC will be submitted, if relevant. Participants will be asked to report such treatment, as well as any further health care access for their shoulder pain (for example, imaging, medication, referral to orthopaedic surgeons) in the follow-up questionnaires.

Sample size {14}

The study’s aim is to assess the equivalence in two treatment arms (Usual Physiotherapy Group and Stepped Care Group). An equivalence test comparing mean QuickDASH scores in 2 groups, using two one-sided equal variance test requires 82 participants in each group to achieve 90% power at a 5% significance level when the actual difference between two population means is 5, standard deviation is 13 in each group [56], and the equivalence limit is ± 11. Scaling this up to allow for adjusting covariates and to account for drop-outs, we will recruit 100 participants per group.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Physiotherapists cannot be blinded to treatment allocation. Blinding is not possible for the principal investigator (GS) and the project manager who are responsible for coordinating the study, contacting potential participants and physiotherapists, and data collection and monitoring. Participants are informed that they are in one of two groups, that both groups receive high-value, individualised care and that follow-up sessions are varied in number and frequency of sessions. The participants are not informed whether they are allocated to ‘usual care’ or ‘experimental’ groups, thus are blinded to the group allocation. All primary and secondary outcomes measures are self-reported by the participants via REDCap thus not influenced by the knowledge of group allocation. The participants of the Stepped Care Group are informed that eligibility for usual, pragmatic physiotherapy sessions in the second half of the 3-month period relies on the outcomes of the 6-week follow-up assessment. The specific outcome (QuickDASH) is not clarified with the participants to minimise risk of participants inflating their results to improve eligibility for further treatments. The biostatistician and the health economist are blinded to the treatment allocation until the final analysis stage where noninferiority is assessed.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Unblinding the participant and/or biostatistician will be required when a participant has a severe unforeseen adverse reaction to the allocated treatment or has indications for medical treatment that cannot be offered in this trial. This makes it possible to take appropriate measures such as crossing the patient to the other treatment group or referring for an appropriate care provider outside the trial.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

REDCap is used to record the patient-reported outcome measures. Participants complete digital diaries (on REDCap MyCap mobile App) on home exercises, physical activity, pain medication use, adverse responses (e.g. increased pain) and direct pain-related costs (e.g. GP attendance, diagnostic tests, pain medication) and indirect costs (e.g. transport to sessions, child-care, home help, time off work). Hard copy diaries are provided for participants who prefer that mode. Scheduled REDCap emails are sent to participants for follow-up completion of outcome measures. The clinical assessments and treatments are recorded by the physiotherapist with the clinic’s patient management system. Referrals are documented, and the patients will report any concomitant treatments received during the trial (e.g. pain medication or injection therapy) in the diaries.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

The project manager emails the participant a few days ahead of an assessment timepoint, prompting them that they will receive a link to the REDCap questionnaires. Scheduled reminders for completing assessments are sent via REDCap. As much outcome data as possible is collected from participants who withdraw or deviate from the protocol, including reasons for withdrawal. Participants are offered a NZ$30 voucher following the screening assessment and, following the 12-month timepoint, NZ$15 for each completed follow-up assessment as an acknowledgement for their contribution. Those who are interviewed for the qualitative study receive a NZ$40 voucher.

Data management {19}

De-identified electronic data (including clinical patient records) will be kept on secured shared drive available only to the researchers and the project manager. Only selected members of the research team will have access to the participants’ details (principal investigator, project manager and those leading the qualitative studies). The project manager and principal investigator will prepare the downloaded Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for statistical analyses. The two intervention groups will be allocated a random code to maintain biostatistician blinding until the final stage of the analysis where unblinding is necessary for interpretations of results in terms of noninferiority.

All files, including a log linking the participant ID with their identities, will be transferred and archived in the University of Otago’s high-capacity central file storage (HCS) system stored for at least 10 years. As per guidelines of the NZ National Ethics Advisory Committee all data relating to participants identifying as Māori will be also stored in a folder reserved for Māori in the HCS [71]. The funder confirmed that an external data monitoring committee is not needed for this trial.

Confidentiality {27}

The project manager and the principal investigator signed non-disclosure agreements with each clinic to protect patient and practice confidentiality. Participants are assigned a study identification code (ID) once allocated to a group. Data from the REDCap questionnaires will be downloaded to a Microsoft ® Excel spreadsheet, coded to the participant and kept confidential and de-identified. Only the primary investigator and the project manager have access to personal information about potential and enrolled participants. Physiotherapy clinical documentation are stored in the respective physiotherapy clinics as part of their usual patient data management system. The principal investigator and the project manager have access to the clinical notes, upload relevant baseline patient reported outcome assessment to the specific participant’s folder to be included as part of the physiotherapist’s clinical assessment as usual, and download and de-identify completed clinical documentation of all assessments and treatments at completion of the individual’s intervention.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Statistical analysis

We will use descriptive statistics and frequentist quantitative measures at each measurement time point to explore relationships of outcomes with patient characteristics and interventions. We will use a repeated measures version of two one-sided test approach to assess equivalence of outcome(s) in the groups (Group 1, Usual Physiotherapy and Group 2, Stepped Care), considering possible clustering by clinic. Outcome measures in continuous scale, including QuickDASH, will be analysed to estimate the difference in the measure between two types of physiotherapy treatments, while the measures in categorical/binary scales will be analysed to estimate the differences of proportions with favourable outcomes between treatments. Confidence intervals will be used to make noninferiority interpretations. Principles underlying intention-to-treat analysis will be followed depending on the level of attrition and treatment crossover. This includes deciding a way of handling missing data due to item missingness and lost to follow-up (see ‘Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}’). Per-protocol analysis (restricted to participants who adhered to the allocated interventions, namely those who attended randomised physiotherapy until formal discontinuation) will be undertaken as secondary analyses.

Economic analysis

A cost-effectiveness analysis will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis at the individual level and reported from a societal perspective. The outcome measures will be QuickDASH scores and Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) [72]. To calculate QALYs, each participant’s health profile (i.e. their self-reported health on the five dimensions and levels of the EQ-5D) will be converted to a utility value using the NZ EQ-5D-5L social value set [72]. Direct medical costs will include physiotherapy costs (gained from the physiotherapy clinical notes), and dispensed pharmaceutical costs, diagnostic imaging, GP, physician and orthopaedic surgery costs (as reported by the participant at the 6-week, 3-, 6- and 12-month timepoints). Direct and indirect shoulder-related costs incurred by participants, such as transport costs, over-the-counter medication, carer support and time off work will be included based on participant self-report. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (QuickDASH) and cost-utility ratios (QALYs) will be calculated; specifically, the incremental cost ratios for QuickDASH and QALYs for the Stepped Care group (including both sub-arms) compared to the Usual Physiotherapy group. Bootstrapping will be used to generate cost-effectiveness acceptability curves which estimate the probability the intervention is cost-effective across a range of willingness-to-pay thresholds. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the sensitivity of the results to variation in key parameters.

Phase III: process evaluation and monitoring

Process evaluation will include monitoring treatment fidelity and protocol adherence, and a qualitative investigation to explore potential explanations for trial outcomes, to understand implementation challenges, and to explore patients’ and physiotherapists’ recommendations to improve usability and acceptability of a Stepped Care model.

As the usual physiotherapy group includes pragmatic, individual interventions, these are not prospectively prescribed. To describe the provided interventions, the project manager will code treatments documented in the clinical notes, for example the type of manual therapy, prescribed exercises and physical activity, per session [33]. The number of patient entries of exercises, physical activity, pain medication and other health care visits will also be coded by the project manager and entered into a Microsoft ® Excel spreadsheet [33].

Treatment fidelity will be monitored as follows:

The project manager has regular contact via email or telephonically with the clinics. On completion of participants’ interventions, the clinics’ administrators will be asked to upload clinical documentation to a secure shared drive of the University of Otago. The project manager will monitor clinical documentation and provide feedback to the physiotherapist, for example, if more treatment details need to be recorded or if clarification is required.

Each physiotherapist is asked (with the patient’s consent) to audio-record the two Stepped Care self-management sessions and usual physiotherapy sessions during 2-week blocks at the start, mid-way and before the study completion (a total of 6 weeks) [73]. A random sample of the recordings will be assessed by Swain and the project manager to assess fidelity to the plan with a focus on the delivery of the Stepped Care sessions and overall clinician-patient interactions. If needed, they will discuss strategies to maintain fidelity with the clinician to ensure that the same guidelines are followed throughout the study. Two combined refresher sessions will be held via Zoom for the physiotherapists to discuss treatment fidelity, challenges and solutions.

Patient adherence to home-based exercise and physical activity programme will be defined by expressing the recorded frequency as a percentage of the total number of sessions that should be performed.

Interim analyses {21b}

An interim analysis will be undertaken when half of the participants have been recruited. This is to determine whether the research question can be answered early and will include assessing adverse effects and justifying continuation of the research.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

Statistical sub-group analyses will be undertaken where sufficient data are available, for example for ethnic- or site-specific analyses. We will also undertake two qualitative studies, for the participants and the physiotherapists respectively.

We will invite 21 participants to semi-structured individual interviews (via videoconferencing, Zoom), 14 for the Stepped Care (7 who went on to further treatment and 7 who did not) and 7 for the Usual Physiotherapy arm. Following completion of the 3-month follow-up assessment, participants will receive an automated email that includes a participant information sheet for this qualitative study and a link to the REDCap interview-specific consent form. Purposive sampling will be used to include participants from all four Clinics, across ethnicities (Māori and Pasifika versus all other ethnicities). The interviews will explore motivation to participate in the study, experience and usefulness of the intervention for each arm/sub-arm and of the patient education resources, and use (and reasons) of health care for shoulder pain external to the trial. Their suggestions will be sought to improve usability and application of the interventions, specifically those who were allocated to the Stepped Care group if found that approach is found beneficial.

Focus groups will be held with all provider physiotherapists (n = 11) following intervention completion. We will explore their experiences of delivering the interventions across the trial arms. We will also explore their views of possible wider role and implementation of a Stepped Care model for individuals with persistent shoulder pain.

We will develop a semi-structured interview guide with open-ended questions for the respective qualitative studies. The questions will be based on current knowledge about experiences with the interventions, and on our previous feasibility study [74]. Swain and/or a research student will undertake the interviews which will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriber, and anonymised. Participants will be given the opportunity to correct their transcripts. They will also be sent a summary of themes after the analysis for any feedback.

We will use Interpretive Description to capture themes within subjective perceptions and experiences and generate knowledge for the clinical context [75]. The first three transcripts will be analysed and developed into a broad initial thematic framework. Independent parallel coding will be conducted to verify trustworthiness and robustness of data analysis.

Participant data and physiotherapist data will be analysed independently and reported. Following this some attempt will be made to combine the themes from both into a higher-order analysis.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Missingness is expected due to item missingness or lost to follow-up. The intention-to-treat analysis will include all the participants allocated to treatments. Item missingness will be handled according to recommendations of developers of the specific outcome measures. The details of handling item missingness in other measures and missingness due to loss to follow-up will depend on the extent and the pattern of missingness observed in data. Those handling methods can include complete case analysis (for example, in the case of per-protocol analysis if the missingness is very small), or either single or multiple imputation in other situations, with a combination of both if such missingness observed in multiple variables.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant-level data and statistical code {31c}

De-identified participant-level data will be made available to other researchers on reasonable request to the study principal investigator, after the final manuscript has been published. Such data may also be added to data from other sources to form larger datasets.

Discussion

MSKDs are increasing, come at high personal, economic and health costs and are often associated with at least one health comorbidity, such as cardiovascular disease, mental health problems, diabetes or obesity [77, 78]. Physiotherapy waiting lists for people with MSKD can worsen health outcomes and have higher health costs than for those who receive timely physiotherapy care appointments [79], in part, as RCRSP can progress to persistent pain and disability. With publicly funded health care systems increasingly constrained as evident by waiting lists, more efficient and accessible primary care models or pathways are needed. A Stepped Care model may address waiting lists. In this trial, we explore the hypothesis that providing a Stepped Care model that initially focuses on patient’s understanding of their condition, pain self-management strategies and lifestyle factors is not inferior compared to usual, pragmatic physiotherapy, specifically for individuals with RCRSP. The trial is placed in the NZ private physiotherapy practice context that has a critical role in primary care delivery for MSKDs and includes one rural and three urban clinics. Exploring participants’ and the provider physiotherapists’ experiences may inform the value of implementing such intervention for other MSKD. Using a pragmatic rather than a prescriptive physiotherapy intervention enhances external validity to the NZ clinical context and may enhance acceptability by patients and clinicians. An economic evaluation will compare the outcomes and direct and indirect costs between the Stepped Care model and usual, pragmatic physiotherapy. If such a model provides greater flexibility, convenience and affordability, timely access to care may be improved for patients that historically have not been able to access such services (including Māori and Pasifika). About 50% of individuals seen in primary care for shoulder pain [4], and those with persistent pain are likely to be responsible for most of the shoulder-related health care and economic costs [3]. If the outcomes of the Stepped Care Model are not inferior to those of usual, pragmatic physiotherapy, appointments may become available sooner for more patients, with the possibility of decreasing the risk of persistent symptoms and disability [79], and decreasing the burden of care provided by the national health system.
