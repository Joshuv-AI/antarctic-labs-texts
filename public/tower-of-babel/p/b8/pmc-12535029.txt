Genome-wide identification and expression analysis of the GARP superfamily in response to abiotic stresses in alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.)
Authors: Yajing Wu, He Zhu, Siqi Wang, Hao Liu, Qingchuan Yang, Xue Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a vital forage legume, faces significant productivity limitations from abiotic stresses. The GARP superfamily, characterized by a conserved B-motif domain, is crucial for regulating plant growth, development, and stress reponses. Despite extensive studies in model plants, the systematic characterization of GARP genes in alfalfa remains unexplored.ResultsIn this study, a total of 51 GARP genes (referred to as MsGARPs) were identified from the alfalfa reference genome (Zhongmu No. 1) and renamed on the basis of their chromosomal distribution (MsARR-B1 to MsARR-B20 and MsGLK1 to MsGLK31). These MsGARP proteins were phylogenetically classified into 5 subfamilies, ARR-B, GLK, NIGT1/HRS1/HHO, PHR1/PHL1 and KAN.​ Gene structure and conserved motif analyses revealed motif 1 (B-motif) being universally conserved across all MsGARP members. Collinearity analysis detected 5 tandem and 7 segmental duplication events. Transcriptomic data demonstrated tissue-specific expression of MsARR-B20 and widespread expression of 32 MsGARP genes across various tissues. Further analysis revealed that all MsGLK genes responded to drought, salt, and cold stress. Additionally, MsGLK6, MsGLK17, MsGLK21 and MsGLK23 exhibited significant differential expression in response to phosphate stress in both roots and shoots of alfalfa, with their expression induced under phosphate deficiency, highlighting their pivotal roles in adaptation to phosphorus starvation in alfalfa.​ConclusionThe study provides the first systematic characterization of the MsGARP superfamily in alfalfa, elucidating their evolutionary dynamics and stress-responsive profiles. Background

Transcription factors (TFs) regulate various signal transduction pathways and play important roles in plant growth and development [1]. The GARP (G: Golden2, AR: ARR-B, P: Psr1) TFs are plant-specific and possess a conserved DNA-binding domain known as the B-motif. This motif consists of approximately 60 amino acids, characterized by the sequence signature (SHLQ (K/M) (Y/F)), and forms a three-dimensional structure containing a helix-turn-helix (HTH) motif [2]. The B-motif and its specific sequence are crucial for distinguishing GARPs from MYB or MYB-related proteins [2]. Although the MYB domain contains a highly similar sequence (SHAQK(Y/F) F), leading to frequent misclassification of GARP proteins as MYB proteins, a key difference is that the B-motif contains only one tryptophan (W) residue, whereas the MYB domain contains three residues [2–4]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, 56 GARP genes have been identified, including type-B authentic response regulators (ARR-B) and GOLDEN2-like (G2-like, GLK) TFs [3, 5]. While the GARP superfamily has been identified in species such as Oryza sativa, Gossypium hirsutum, Camellia sinensis, and Brassica napus, it has not yet been systematically identified in alfalfa, and the functions and regulatory mechanisms of MsGARP TFs remain to be elucidated.

GARP TFs have been reported to be involved in plant development, nutrition, hormone signaling, circadian clock oscillations, as well as resistance to pathogens and various abiotic stresses [6–10]. GLK proteins are essential for plant photosynthesis, governing chloroplast development and photosynthetic gene expression [6]. For instance, gkl1 gkl2 double mutants exhibit reduced accumulation of chlorophyll precursors, leading to an albino phenotype in Arabidopsis [6, 11, 12]. ARR-B TFs serve as critical regulators of the cytokinin (CK) pathway [13, 14]. The arr10 12 18 triple mutants display defective female gametophyte development in Arabidopsis [15], while CRISPR/Cas9-edited osrrs mutants in rice show delayed heading date but enhanced yield [16]. KAN subfamily members primarily modulate the development of various plant organs [4]. In Arabidopsis, KAN1, KAN2, and KAN3 are involved in outer integument development [17], and kan1 kan2, kan1-11, and kan2 kan5 double mutants exhibit finger-like projections on leaf abaxial surfaces, with the latter two showing leaf margin defects [18, 19]. The NIGT1/HRS1/HHO and PHR1/PHL1 subfamilies are pivotal for regulating plant nutrient uptake and utilization [20–24]. The first nutrient response factor identified was Chlamydomonas phosphorus-stress response 1 (Psr1), whose mutants fail to increase phosphate uptake under Pi starvation, accompanied by reduced photosynthetic activity and growth rate [25, 26]. In Arabidopsis and Oryza sativa, PHR1 acts as a key regulator of Pi homeostasis, interacting with SPX proteins to mediate low-P stress responses [27–30]. Under Pi-sufficient conditions, the PHR1/SPX complex remains inactive, whereas low-P stress triggers the release of PHR transcription factors, which bind to the PHR1-binding sequence (P1BS) cis-element to induce Pi uptake-related genes and activate the Pi starvation response [30, 31]. Notably, nitrate-induced NIGT1 proteins suppress SPX gene expression, relieving SPX-mediated inhibition of PHR1 and activating the PHR1-dependent PSR pathway, forming an NIGT1-SPX-PHR1 regulatory cascade for nitrogen-phosphorus balance [32]. In addition, GLK1 also exerts positive effects on the phosphate starvation response (PSR) [33]. Moreover, GARP TFs also participate in other abiotic stress responses. For example, rice osnigt1 mutants exhibit enhanced salt tolerance, and a similar phenotype is observed in Medicago truncatula mthho3 mutants [34, 35]. Additionally, in Oryza sativa, overexpression of ZmGLKs promotes rapid stomatal closure under drought stress [36]. These findings collectively highlight the versatile roles of GARP TFs in plant adaptation to diverse environmental challenges.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is one of the earliest cultivated and most widely grown perennial leguminous forages globally, valued for its high protein content, low fiber, and palatability as livestock feed [37]. Its deep root system not only enhances its capacity to sustain relatively high yields but also plays a pivotal role in soil improvement and erosion prevention [38, 39]. However, escalating global warming and ecological degradation have subjected alfalfa to severe abiotic stresses during growth and development [40]. Therefore, identifying key functional genes via modern biotechnological approaches and breeding improved varieties through molecular breeding techniques is critical for increasing alfalfa quality, improving productivity, and accelerating the development of dairy and forage industries [41, 42]. In recent years, the alfalfa genome has been assembled, which is a breakthrough for alfalfa breeding [43–45]. Given that GARP proteins have been extensively demonstrated to play crucial roles in mediating responses to abiotic stresses across diverse plant species, investigating the functional relevance of GARP family members in Medicago sativa is therefore of substantial significance.

In this study, comprehensive characterization of the GARP superfamily in Medicago sativa was conducted using genome-wide screening, phylogenetic tree construction, gene structure and motif analysis, and collinearity analysis. Additionally, RNA-Seq analysis of MsGARP genes was performed across different tissues and under various abiotic stresses. These results provide a reference for subsequent studies on the functions and regulatory mechanisms of MsGARPs in Medicago sativa under abiotic stress.

Results

Identification of GAPR genes in the Medicago sativa genome

A total of 51 MsGARP genes were identified in the genome of Medicago sativa cultivar ‘Zhongmu No. 1’ through Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiling and conserved domain analysis (Table S1). These 51 genes were systematically renamed as MsARR-B1 to MsARR-B20 and MsGLK1 to MsGLK31 based on their chromosomal locations and subfamily classification in the previous study [46].

Table S2 detailed the characteristics of the identified MsGARP genes, including gene ID, chromosomal location, coding sequence (CDS) and protein lengths, molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), and predicted subcellular localization. Within the MsGARP superfamily, MsARR-B20 contained the longest CDS (3642 bp), while MsGLK11 had the shortest CDS (354 bp). Correspondingly, their encoded proteins ranged from 118 to 1214 amino acids (aa) in length and from 13.61 to 134.88 kDa in MW. The pI values spanned from 4.76 (MsGLK4) to 9.85 (MsGLK11), with 25 proteins acidic (pI < 6.5), 8 neutral (6.5 ≤ pI ≤ 7.5), 18 alkaline (pI > 7.5). Furthermore, all MsGARP proteins were predicted to localize to the nucleus, except for MsGLK27, which was predicted to reside in the chloroplasts.

Among the 51 MsGARP genes, chromosome 1 (chr1) contained the greatest number (9 genes), specifically MsARR-B1, MsARR-B2, and MsGLK1 to MsGLK7. In contrast, chr6 had the fewest genes (MsGLK24 to MsGLK26) with no MsARR-Bs (Fig. 1).

Phylogenetic analysis of MsGARP proteins

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships and functional characteristics of MsGARP proteins, multiple sequence alignment was performed using the full-length amino acid sequences of the 51 MsGARP and 56 AtGARP proteins (Figure S1). A neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed with MEGA software. As shown in Fig. 2, the 51 MsGARP proteins clustered into five subfamilies: ARR-B, GLK, PHR1/PHL1, NIGT1/HRS1/HHO, and KAN, consistent with previous reports [35]. These proteins exhibited uneven subfamily distribution, with the ARR-B subfamily containing the largest group (20 members), followed by PHR1/PHL1 (13 members) and KAN (10 members), while GLK and NIGT1/HRS1/HHO each had 4 members.

Analysis of gene structure and conserved motifs of MsGARP members in Medicago sativa

To elucidate the structural characteristics of MsGARP members in Medicago sativa, conserved motif analysis of 51 MsGARP proteins was conducted using the MEME online platform (Fig. 3). Motif 1 contains the conserved B-motif and was present in all 51 MsGARP proteins. Within the ARR-B subfamily, motif 1, motif 2, motif 4, and motif 6 exhibited complete conservation, whereas motif 3 was absent specifically in MsARR-B9 and motif 5 was lacking in MsARR-B6 and MsARR-B9. Notably, motif 8 occurred exclusively in five ARR-B members (MsARR-B6, MsARR-B7, MsARR-B8, MsARR-B11, and MsARR-B12), while motif 7, motif 9, and motif 10 were unique to MsARR-B13, MsARR-B14, and MsARR-B15 (Fig. 3A). For GLK proteins, motif 1 was 100% conserved, motif 2 was present in 93.55% (29 of 31) of members, and motif 3 occurred in 32.26% (10 of 31) of members (Fig. 3B). Notably, motif 8 was exclusively identified in the NIGT1/HRS1/HHO subfamily, while motif 3 was exclusively present in the PHR1/PHL1 subfamily, demonstrating subfamily-specific conservation during GARP evolution. Furthermore, motif 9 was uniquely retained in five KAN subfamily members that share a common evolutionary origin.

Phylogenetic tree, gene structure, and motifs of GARP superfamily members in alfalfa. A Gene structure and motifs of the ARR-B subfamily. B Gene structure and motifs of the GLK subfamily

Gene structure analysis revealed extensive variation in exon number. The results show that most MsGARPs have five exons (41.18%) or six exons (23.53%), Interestingly, MsGLK16 and MsGLK28 consist of only one exon, while MsARR-B20 and MsGLK27 contain 12 and 13 exons, respectively.

Gene duplication events and collinearity analysis of GARP genes in Medicago sativa

Analysis of gene duplication events among MsGARP genes revealed a higher frequency of tandem duplications in the MsARR-B subfamily compared to the MsGLK subfamily. As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 5 tandem duplication events were identified, involving 13 MsGARP genes, such as the tandem duplication events MsARR-B1/MsARR-B2 and MsGLK2/MsGLK3 located on chr1 and another tandem duplication event MsARR-B6/MsARR-B7/MsARR-B8/MsARR-B9 located on chr4. In addition, 7 segmental duplication events, involving 14 MsGARP genes, were detected across multiple alfalfa chromosomes (Fig. 4, Table S3). For example, a segmental duplication event was found between MsARR-B1 on chr1 and MsARR-B5 on chr3. These findings demonstrate that gene duplication events significantly contributed to the phylogeny and evolution of the MsGARP superfamily.

Gene density and abundance on chromosomes are depicted in the innermost and middle circles, respectively. The scale on the right denotes gene density and abundance

To illustrate the evolutionary conservation of GARP genes, collinearity maps between Medicago sativa and three model plants (Arabidopsis thaliana, Glycine max and Medicago truncatula) were constructed (Fig. 5). Notably, eight MsGARPs were collinear with all three species, and MsGLK22 and MsGLK29 were collinear with both Glycine max and Medicago truncatula. Meanwhile, 22, 72 and 39 collinear gene pairs were present in Arabidopsis thaliana, Glycine max and Medicago truncatula, respectively. These results indicate that Glycine max and Medicago truncatula are more closely related to Medicago sativa than Arabidopsis thaliana is, which is probably because they all belong to the legume family.

Cis-acting element analysis of MsGARPs

To further investigate the potential functions of the MsGARP genes, cis-acting elements within the promoter regions were analyzed using the PlantCARE database. These cis-acting elements were classified into four groups: light response, phytohormones, growth and development, and abiotic stresses. Notably, light-responsive elements were widely distributed across all members of the MsGARP superfamily (Table S4). The latter three categories were selected for further analysis. As shown in Fig. 6, the abscisic acid response element (ABRE) was absent in 6 genes, whereas methyl jasmonate (MeJa) response elements were identified in 43 MsGARPs, with only 3 genes containing flavonoid response elements. Additionally, abiotic stress response elements were present in more members. In particular, MsGLK27 contained 13 low-temperature response elements, suggesting that it may play a critical regulatory role under low-temperature stress.

Expression of MsGARPs in different tissues of Medicago sativa

MsGARP expression profiles across various alfalfa tissues, including elongated stems, pre-elongation stems, roots, leaves, flowers, and nodules, were obtained from public databases. As shown in Figs. 7, 12 MsGARP genes were not expressed in any of these 6 tissues, suggesting potential expression under stress conditions or in specific unexamined tissues. The remaining 39 genes exhibited varying expression levels across these tissues. Notably, MsARR-B20 expression was specific to elongated stems, while MsARR-B17 and MsGLK9 were expressed only in elongated stems and nodules. MsARR-B9 was also expressed in only 2 tissues, roots and elongated stems. In contrast, MsGLK2 and MsGLK17 were expressed in all tissues except roots, and MsGLK11 was expressed in all tissues except flowers. A total of 32 genes were expressed in all six tissues, with MsGLK13 and MsGLK14 showing particularly high expression in leaves and nodules, respectively. These results indicate that MsGARPs likely play crucial roles in diverse tissues and developmental stages of alfalfa.

Expression of MsGARPs under salt, drought and cold stresses

To investigate the response patterns of MsGARP genes to salt, drought, and cold stress, we analyzed their expression profiles using publicly available RNA-Seq data from NCBI databases. As shown in Figs. 8A-C, all MsGLK genes responded to salt, drought, and cold stress. In contrast, MsARR-B genes exhibited distinct expression patterns. Integration of the three expression profiles revealed that MsARR-B11–MsARR-B14 showed no response to short-term exposure to any stress, suggesting these genes may not be directly involved in stress-response pathways or could function at later stages or in specific tissues under these experimental conditions. MsARR-B6 displayed differential expression trends under salt and cold stress but remained unresponsive to drought within the 24-hour treatment period. MsARR-B7 exhibited a transient down-regulation followed by an increase and subsequent decrease specifically under salt stress. MsARR-B15 responded exclusively to cold stress, showing a significant up-regulation at 24 h post-treatment. In conclusion, the expression levels of MsGARP genes exhibited a notable alterations in response to salt, drought, and cold stresses.

To validate the reliability of the RNA-Seq data, we selected three MsGARP genes exhibiting responses to all three stresses for qRT-PCR analysis, with primer sequences provided in Table S5. As shown in Fig. 9A-C, under salt stress, the expression level of MsGLK4 initially increased followed by a decrease, remaining significantly lower than control (CK) at both 12 and 24 h post-treatment. MsGLK6 expression reached the lowest level at 24 h post-treatment, while MsGLK30 significantly increased within 1 h post-treatment (Fig. 9A). Under drought stress, MsGLK4 expression increased during the early treatment phase but slightly declined later. In contrast, MsGLK6 expression displayed an initial decrease followed by an upward trend (Fig. 9B). Under cold stress, the expression patterns of both MsGLK4 and MsGLK30 featured an initial decrease followed by an increase. Conversely, MsGLK6 expression rose sharply within 1 h before gradually declining thereafter (Fig. 9C). The qRT-PCR results were consistent with the transcriptome data, thus validating the reliability of the RNA-Seq analysis.

Expression of MsGARP genes in root and shoot under phosphate stress

Inorganic phosphate (Pi) represents the primary form through which plants acquire phosphorus (P) from the environment. Numerous studies have demonstrated GARP genes as key regulators involved in plant Pi acquisition and utilization. In this study, alfalfa (Zhongmu No. 1) plants were subjected to Pi-deficient (− P) and Pi-sufficient (+ P) treatments, and transcriptome sequencing was performed on root and shoot tissues to obtain the expression profiles of MsGARP genes. As shown in Figs. 10A, 5 MsARR-B genes and 30 MsGLK genes were involved in the response to Pi-deficiency, exhibiting distinct expression patterns in shoots and roots. To clarify the differential expression patterns of MsGARP genes in roots and shoots under the two conditions, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using thresholds of P ≤ 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1. Figure 10B shows that 9 MsGARP genes were significantly differentially expressed in roots between Pi-deficient and Pi-sufficient conditions, among which 8 genes were up-regulated and only MsGLK14 was down-regulated. Figure 10C shows that 8 MsGARP genes in shoots exhibited significant differential expression in response to Pi-deficiency, and MsGLK10 and MsGLK16 were induced under Pi-sufficient conditions. Integrative analysis identified MsGLK6, MsGLK17, MsGLK21, and MsGLK23 showed significant differential expression in response to Pi-deficiency in both shoots and roots of alfalfa (Fig. 10D). Notably, all 4 genes were induced under Pi-deficient conditions, suggesting that they may play vital roles in the low phosphate stress response of alfalfa. In addition, the qRT-PCR results of these 4 genes were consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 11), which confirmed the reliability of the RNA-Seq analysis.

Discussion

The GARP gene family, including ARR-B and G2-like transcription factors, plays a crucial role in plant growth, development, and the regulation of various stress responses. The GARP gene family has been studied in several plant species, such as 56 GARPs in Arabidopsis thaliana, 35 GARPs in Spirodela polyrhiza, 146 GARPs in Brassica napus, and 69 GARPs in Camellia sinensis. However, systematic identification of GARP genes in alfalfa remains lacking [46–48]. In this study, using the alfalfa genome (Zhongmu No. 1) as the reference, 51 MsGARP genes were identified and comprehensively analyzed. Previous studies have highlighted that the B motif is a multifunctional domain specific for DNA binding and contains nuclear localization signals [2]. Consistent with previous findings [46], our subcellular localization prediction revealed that 98% of MsGARP proteins are localized in the nucleus, with only one MsGARP protein (MsGLK27) localized in the chloroplast. Additionally, significant variations were observed in the protein sequences of MsGARPs. The longest protein sequence (MsARR-B20) is nearly 10 times the length of the shortest one (MsGLK11). The distribution of MsGARP genes across chromosomes is uneven, with most genes concentrated on chr1, chr4, and chr7, while only 4 genes were present on chr6.

Gene duplication events, including tandem and segmental duplications, facilitate the emergence of new traits or functions in plants, and such events are more prevalent in plants than in other eukaryotes [49, 50]. In the MsGARP gene family, a total of 5 tandem duplication events and 7 segmental duplication events were identified, suggesting that segmental duplication primarily drives the evolution of MsGARP genes. Based on the uneven chromosomal distribution of MsGARP genes, it was found that chr1 and chr7 harbor the most tandem duplications, with 2 events each, while a single tandem duplication event on chr4 includes 4 genes, indicating that the distribution of tandem duplications is consistent with that of MsGARP genes. Furthermore, to explore the evolutionary relationships of GARP genes between alfalfa and Arabidopsis thaliana, Glycine max, and Medicago truncatula, collinearity analysis was performed. The results revealed that alfalfa shares more homologous gene pairs with Glycine max and Medicago truncatula than with Arabidopsis thaliana, indicating that GARP genes are more conserved among leguminous plants.

Phylogenetic trees not only illustrate the evolutionary relationships between genes but also, when combined with the analysis of cis-acting elements in gene promoter regions and transcriptome data, can effectively reflect their potential functions and physiological activities. The GARP gene family was divided into five subfamilies in alfalfa, including ARR-B, GLK, NIGT1/HRS1/HHO, PHR1/PHL1, and KAN, which is consistent with previous studies [35]. For the GLK subfamily, inhibition of AtGLK1 and AtGLK2 expression in Arabidopsis thaliana has been shown to reduce photosynthesis, cause leaf yellowing, and decrease chlorophyll content [11]. Interestingly, MsGLK13 in alfalfa has a close evolutionary relationship with AtGLK1 and AtGLK2. Transcriptome data further revealed that MsGLK13 is expressed at significantly higher levels in leaves than in other tissues and contains 10 light-responsive elements, suggesting that it likely performs similar functions related to photosynthesis and chlorophyll regulation.

In the ARR-B subfamily, studies on Arabidopsis have shown that the arr10 arr12 double mutant exhibits inhibited root elongation [51], while the arr1 arr10 arr12 triple mutant shows weaker growth [52], suggesting that these three genes have functional redundancy and are involved in the authentic histidine kinases (AHKs)-dependent phosphorelay signaling pathway regulating cytokinin responses [53]. Notably, MsARR-B5, MsARR-B16, and MsARR-B17 in alfalfa exhibit a close evolutionary relationship with ARR1, ARR10, and ARR12. Transcriptome analysis revealed that the expression of MsARR-B5 is affected by drought stress, and its promoter contains a drought-responsive element (MBS) and three defense and stress-responsive elements. MsARR-B16 also contains four MBS elements, and its expression level significantly decreased compared with that at 0 h after drought stress. This indicates that these genes may not only be involved in regulating the growth and development of Medicago sativa, but also play important roles in responding to drought stress.

Transcriptomic analysis of MsGARP genes across different tissues in alfalfa showed that MsGLK14 is expressed in all six tested tissues, with particularly high expression in nodules, stems, flowers, and roots. MsGLK14 belongs to the KAN subfamily and shares the highest homology with AtKAN4. KAN genes in Arabidopsis have been extensively studied and are mostly associated with plant organ development, implying that MsGLK14 may play a key role in regulating alfalfa development.

NIGT1/HRS1/HHO and PHR1/PHL1 subfamilies members are primarily associated with nitrogen and phosphorus signaling pathways [20–24]. Transcriptome data under Pi-sufficient and Pi-deficient conditions showed that all MsGARP genes in these two subfamilies respond to phosphate availability. Among them, MsGLK6 and MsGLK23, which are from the NIGT1/HRS1/HHO subfamily, both exhibited significant differential expression in whole alfalfa plants under Pi–sufficiency and Pi–deficiency treatments. Phylogenetic analysis indicates that MsGLK6 and MsGLK23 are homologous to Arabidopsis thaliana HHO2 and HHO3, have been extensively documented to function in phosphorus metabolism. For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana, overexpression of NIGT1.2 (HHO2) enhances Pi uptake, whereas the nigt1.1 nigt1.2 double mutant shows reduced Pi absorption under Pi starvation while displaying increased NO3 uptake, an adaptive mechanism that maintains plant nitrogen-phosphorus balance during Pi starvation [24]. Based on these, MsGLK6 and MsGLK23 are hypothesized to act as key regulators in the phosphorus signaling network of alfalfa, potentially mediating conserved mechanisms for P homeostasis and stress adaptation. Notably, MsGLK6 also exhibited significant expression dynamics under salt and drought stresses (Fig. 9). This aligns with previous findings in Medicago truncatula, where MtHHO3 acts as a negative regulator of salt stress [35], suggesting a conserved and multifaceted role for this gene across legumes. In addition, studies have shown that HRS1 expression is inhibited under NaCl stress, and its chimeric repressor enhances salt tolerance by reducing ion leakage, a phenomenon also observed in rice [34]. Furthermore, analysis of cis-acting elements revealed that MsGLK27, which belongs to the PHR1/PHL1 subfamily, contains 13 low-temperature response elements. Transcriptome data under cold stress showed that the expression level of MsGLK27 was significantly increased compared with that at 0 h. This suggests that MsGLK27 may be a key factor in the cold stress regulatory pathway of Medicago sativa.

Alfalfa is an economically important forage crop, providing high nutritional value for livestock. However, it is frequently exposed to various biotic and abiotic stresses due to environmental degradation and human activities. In this study, we comprehensively characterized the MsGARP gene family in alfalfa and analyzed the expression patterns of its members under abiotic stresses (salt, drought, cold, and phosphorus deficiency). In the future, functional verification of these MsGARP genes using transgenic or gene-editing techniques will be essential for breeding new alfalfa varieties through modern biotechnology.

Materials and methods

Identification of MsGARPs

The Medicago sativa (Zhongmu No. 1) genome was obtained from the Alfalfa Genome Project [35] (https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Medicago_sativa_genome_and_annotation_files/12623960). Genomic data for Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max were downloaded from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org), Medica Go Genome (http://www.medicagogenome.org), and SoyBase (https://www.soybase.org), respectively. Using Arabidopsis thaliana protein sequences as queries, Blastp analysis was performed with TBtools v2.119 (E-value >1e⁻⁵) [54]. Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) of GARP domains (PF00249, PF00072, PF14379) were acquired from Pfam (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org) to filter redundant sequences. Candidate MsGARPs were validated using Conserved Domain Database of NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd). Physicochemical properties were analyzed via ExPASy (https://www.expasy.org), while subcellular localization was predicted using Cell-PLoc 2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2) and WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp).

Phylogenetic and structural characterization of MsGARPs

Protein sequences of MsGARP and AtGARP were aligned in MEGA 11.0 [55]. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was subsequently constructed with 1000 bootstrap replicates [51]. Gene structures of MsGARPs were extracted from the alfalfa reference genome GFF annotation files. Conserved protein motifs were identified using MEME (https://meme-suite.org), with a maximum of 10 motifs characterized. All results were integrated and visualized using TBtools V2.119 [54].

Chromosomal localization, gene duplication and collinearity analysis of MsGARPs

Chromosomal locations of MsGARPs were mapped using the GFF annotation files from the alfalfa genome and visualized with TBtools V2.119 [50]. Tandem duplication events were defined as two or more genes located within 200 kb on the same chromosome. Segmental duplication refers to DNA fragments (>1 kb) located on non-homologous chromosomes or within non-adjacent regions of the same chromosome, exhibiting >50% sequence similarity, with each fragment containing ≥ 1 homologous gene [56]. Collinearity analysis of MsGARP genes within alfalfa and with Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max was performed using MCScanX [57].

Analysis of the cis-acting elements

The 2000 bp promoter regions upstream of MsGARP genes were extracted using TBtools. These sequences were analyzed for cis-acting elements via PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) and results were visualized using TBtools v2.119 [54].

Analysis of MsGARP expression profiles using RNA-seq data

RNA-seq data for MsGARP genes from six alfalfa tissues (SRP055547) and responses to cold, drought, and salt stress (SRR7091780-SRR7091794 and SRR7160313-SRR7160357) were downloaded from the NCBI database [58, 59]. SAM files were generated by mapping against the reference genome of alfalfa (Zhongmu No. 1). Transcriptomic data were quantified using FeatureCounts software, and gene expression levels were evaluated based on FPKM values. Results were visualized using TBtools v2.119.

Plant materials growth conditions and treatments

Alfalfa (Zhongmu No. 1) seeds were surface-sterilized, placed in petri dishes containing moistened filter paper, and germinated for seven days in an artificial climate incubator under controlled conditions (16/8 h light/dark photoperiod, 24 °C/22°C day/night temperature, 70–80% relative humidity). Seedlings were grown hydroponically for four weeks in 1/2 Hoagland’s solution (Table S6) before stress treatments were initiated, with salt stress applied using 250 mM NaCl, drought stress with 400 mM mannitol, and cold stress at 4 °C. Salt/drought samples (leaves) were collected at 0 h (CK), 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h; for cold stress, samples were collected at 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 24 h, and 48 h. Three replicates of three leaves each per time point were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. For phosphorus (P) treatment, alfalfa stem cuttings were rooted in vermiculite. Uniform seedlings were acclimated in 500 µmol/L Pi solution for 3 weeks, then transferred to either Pi-sufficient (500 µmol/L Pi, +P) or Pi-deficient (0 µmol/L Pi, − P) solutions (Table S7). After 3 weeks, whole roots and shoots were sampled for transcriptome sequencing.

Total RNA was extracted from roots and leaves of Pi-sufficient and Pi-deficient plants. Qualified RNA was used to prepare cDNA libraries, which were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. Raw reads were processed to remove adapters, poly-N sequences, and low-quality bases, yielding clean reads. These clean reads were aligned to the alfalfa reference genome (Zhongmu No. 1) using HISAT2 (v2.0.5). Gene expression was quantified with featureCounts (v1.5.0-p3) and normalized using FPKM. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those satisfying both adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 and |log2(FoldChange)| ≥ 1.

RNA extraction and expression pattern analysis by qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from all samples and reverse-transcribed into cDNA following Wang et al. [35]. qRT-PCR assays were performed using the CFX96 system (Bio-Rad) with SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). Each gene was analyzed in triplicate using primers listed in Table S5. Expression levels were calculated via the 2-∆∆CT method and normalized using the MsActin housekeeping gene.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test at P < 0.05, with data presented as mean ± SD. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and TBtools v2.119.

Conclusions

In this study, 51 MsGARP genes were identified from the alfalfa reference genome (Zhongmu No. 1) and renamed based on their chromosomal distribution and subfamily classification. These genes were phylogenetically categorized into five subfamilies: ARR-B, GLK, NIGT1/HRS1/HHO, PHR1/PHL1, and KAN, with the ARR-B subfamily being the largest. Conserved motif analysis revealed subfamily-specific conserved motifs, implying functional analogy within each subfamily. Cis-acting element analysis demonstrated that MsGARP promoters contain abundant stress-responsive and hormone-related elements, supporting their involvement in regulating plant growth, development, and abiotic stress responses. Collinearity analysis identified 5 groups of tandem duplication events and 7 pairs of segmental duplication events, which likely contributed to the expansion and evolutionary diversification of the MsGARP superfamily. Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct expression patterns, with MsARR-B20 showed specific expression in elongated stems, while 32 MsGARPs were ubiquitously expressed across six tissues, indicating their roles in diverse developmental processes. Abiotic stress transcriptomic data indicated that 13 MsARR-B genes and all MsGLK genes are involved in responses to salt, drought, and cold stresses, whereas MsARR-B11 to MsARR-B14 did not respond to these three stresses. Three selected genes were validated by qRT-PCR, confirming the reliability of the transcriptomic data. Phosphate stress transcriptomic data demonstrated that MsGLK6, MsGLK17, MsGLK21 and MsGLK23 exhibit significant differential expression in response to phosphate deficiency, with their expression being induced under Pi-deficient conditions in both roots and shoots of alfalfa. In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive characterization of the MsGARP gene family, offering new insights into future functional investigations of MsGARP genes and potential applications in molecular breeding.
