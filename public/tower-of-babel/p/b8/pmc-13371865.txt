Purple Urine Bag Syndrome in a 26-Year-Old Woman Living with HIV and Metastatic Spinal Cord Compression: A Case Report
Authors: Vincent Wasswa, James Nelson Okema, Kim Andrew Otto, Kiragga Denis Lwembawo, Maria Gabriella Nampiinga, Irene Birungi, Michael Ndyomugabe, Ivaan Pitua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPurple urine bag syndrome (PUBS) is a rare complication of urinary catheterization in which urine, catheter and collection bag develop a characteristic purple discoloration from bacterial metabolism of dietary tryptophan to indigo and indirubin. It is reported almost exclusively in elderly, chronically catheterized women, with very few reports from sub-Saharan Africa.Case PresentationA 26-year-old para 2+0 woman, six months postpartum and living with HIV (CD4 = 194 cells/µL), presented with a two-month history of progressive lower-limb weakness, urinary and fecal incontinence and chronic constipation, with three days of fever and loin pain, having been catheterized three times in the preceding four weeks. She was febrile and paraplegic with a T12 sensory level. Empirical ceftriaxone was started for presumed urosepsis; two days later the urine and bag turned purple. Urine culture grew Escherichia coli at >105 CFU/mL, multidrug-resistant but sensitive to piperacillin/tazobactam. Therapy was escalated and the catheter exchanged; the urine cleared within 24 hours with symptomatic improvement. MRI showed a lytic T10/T11 lesion with cord compression and hepatic and pulmonary nodules consistent with metastatic disease; tumor markers were within normal limits, narrowing but not excluding the primary.DiscussionRecognized PUBS risk factors include prolonged catheterization, female sex, neurogenic bladder, immobility, chronic constipation and renal disease, occurring mostly in elderly women. Our patient was young but had several of these factors, plus multidrug-resistant bacteriuria and HIV-related immunosuppression that may have increased her susceptibility to infection. Cumulative risk-factor burden, rather than age alone, appears to drive the syndrome; empirical ceftriaxone failed, reflecting rising regional cephalosporin resistance.ConclusionPUBS is a visible indicator of catheter-associated urinary tract infection that can occur outside the typical elderly demographic when risk factors converge and should prompt urgent culture, catheter exchange and culture-directed antibiotics to prevent progression to urosepsis: to our knowledge, the first reported case from Uganda.

Introduction

Purple urine bag syndrome (PUBS) is an uncommon complication of urinary catheterization in which the urine, catheter tubing and drainage bag develop a purple discoloration.1 The pigment arises from bacterial metabolism of dietary tryptophan: gut bacteria convert tryptophan to indole, which is sulphated in the liver to indoxyl sulphate; in alkaline urine, urinary bacteria expressing indoxyl sulphatase and phosphatase cleave indoxyl sulphate to indoxyl, which then oxidizes to indigo (blue) and indirubin (red), staining the plastic surfaces purple.2,3

Reported prevalence ranges from approximately 8% to 16% in chronically catheterized populations, with a strong female and elderly preponderance; the mean age at presentation in pooled literature is 75.6 ± 12.8 years.4,5 Established risk factors include prolonged indwelling catheterization, female sex, neurogenic bladder, immobility, chronic constipation, alkaline urine, renal disease and high dietary tryptophan load.3,6 Although alkaline urine is considered the classic enabling condition, since indoxyl sulphatase and phosphatase activity is favored at higher pH, isolated cases have also been reported in mildly acidic urine (pH 6.0–6.5), suggesting that the syndrome is not strictly pH-dependent and may occur under suboptimal biochemical conditions when bacterial enzyme activity and pigment load are sufficiently high.7 Causative organisms include Providencia stuartii, Providencia rettgeri, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Proteus species and Enterococcus species.8,9 HIV itself has not been established as a direct risk factor for PUBS; rather, HIV-related immunosuppression may indirectly increase susceptibility to urinary tract infection and colonization by resistant uropathogens, compounding the effect of the better-established risk factors above.

PUBS remains rare in the African literature. Isolated cases have been described from South Africa, Cameroon (the first Central African case), Burkina Faso and Nigeria.10–12 To our knowledge, no case has previously been published from Uganda or East Africa. We report a 26-year-old woman living with HIV who developed PUBS during admission for urosepsis on a background of metastatic spinal cord compression, recurrent catheterization, immobility and chronic constipation. At 26 years she is among the youngest patients with PUBS reported globally and, to our knowledge, the youngest from sub-Saharan Africa, complementing the few reports of PUBS in young adults from elsewhere.11,13 This case is reported in line with the SCARE 2023 criteria.14

Case Presentation

Patient Information and History

A 26-year-old woman, para 2+0 and six months postpartum, was referred from a regional referral hospital in western Uganda to a national referral center in Kampala for further evaluation and management.

She had been living with HIV for four years and was adherent to first-line antiretroviral therapy (tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, lamivudine, and dolutegravir; TLD). Her most recent CD4 count was 194 cells/μL.

Two months before presentation she developed symmetric, progressive lower-limb weakness. Over the following month she experienced urinary retention with overflow incontinence and worsening constipation, requiring urethral catheterization on three separate occasions over four weeks. Three days before referral she developed fever, loin pain, and vomiting.

Pre-referral management comprised intramuscular tramadol 100 mg and oral aceclofenac 100 mg, with insertion of a new urinary catheter; no antibiotics were given. She had no known drug allergies, was a non-smoker, and did not drink alcohol. There was no family history of malignancy.

Clinical Findings

On admission she was febrile (axillary temperature 38.1°C), markedly pale, but not jaundiced. Oxygen saturation was 96% on room air, blood pressure 104/69 mmHg, pulse 118 beats per minute, and random capillary blood glucose 120 mg/dL. She was fully conscious and oriented. Neurological examination revealed flaccid paraplegia with a sensory level at T12, localized tenderness over the lower thoracic spine without an obvious gibbus, hyperreflexia in the lower limbs, normal tone, absent clonus and down-going plantar reflexes. The urinary catheter, in situ since the previous day, drained foul-smelling but initially clear urine into a colorless bag.

Diagnostic Assessment

A full blood count revealed a neutrophilic leukocytosis (white-cell count 14.97 × 109/L; reference 4–12), absolute neutrophil count 12.65 × 109/L (1.5–7), hemoglobin 4.6 g/dL and platelet count 281 × 109/L. She received three units of whole blood. Urinalysis showed yellow, cloudy urine, leukocyte esterase ++, nitrite positive, pH 6.0, specific gravity 1.015, with 2–5 pus cells per high-power field and many bacteria; protein, blood, glucose, ketones and bilirubin were negative. Mid-stream urine culture grew Escherichia coli >105 CFU/mL, resistant to cefazolin, cefuroxime, cefepime, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin and trimethoprim/sulphamethoxazole, and sensitive to imipenem, ertapenem, piperacillin/tazobactam, amikacin, gentamicin, nitrofurantoin and tigecycline. Renal function was initially deranged (blood urea nitrogen 77 mg/dL, creatinine 0.63 mg/dL) but normalized after fluid resuscitation (blood urea nitrogen 19 mg/dL, creatinine 0.25 mg/dL). Renal ultrasound was unremarkable. Blood smear was negative for malaria parasites.

Thoraco-lumbar MRI demonstrated a heterogeneously enhancing, non-circumscribed extradural-extramedullary lesion involving the body and posterior elements of T10, with left-sided extradural extension causing severe compression of the spinal cord and left exit-nerve root at the T10/T11 intervertebral foramen, associated with a peri-vertebral collection at the same level findings most consistent with a neoplastic process and differential diagnosis of spine metastases. An additional circumscribed T2-hyperintense lesion was noted at the T11/T12 intervertebral disc, also suspicious for metastatic involvement (Figure 1A). Chest imaging revealed homogeneously enhancing nodular masses in the right lung (largest measuring 1.39 × 1.37 cm), together with multiple nodular masses bilaterally, most likely representing lung metastases (Figure 1B). Collectively, these findings are consistent with metastatic disease of undetermined primary. To narrow the differential for the primary tumor, serum tumor markers were obtained at the Uganda Cancer Institute on day 8 of admission. CA-125 was 27.2 U/mL (reference 0–35), CA 19–9 was 4.10 U/mL (reference 0–39) and CEA was 0.54 ng/mL (reference 0–6.50); all three were within normal limits. Therapeutic Intervention

Initial management comprised fluid resuscitation, antipyretics, transfusion of three units of whole blood and empirical intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g once daily. Because the indwelling catheter had been changed on the day of referral, it was not exchanged at admission. When urine-culture results became available on day 3 and confirmed multidrug-resistant E. coli, therapy was escalated to piperacillin/tazobactam 4.5 g intravenously every 8 hours (renal function had normalized, so renal dose adjustment was not required) and the urinary catheter and bag were exchanged simultaneously. The purple discoloration of the urine and collection bag observed on day 2 is illustrated in Figure 2. Bowel-care measures and laxatives were started for chronic constipation. Follow-Up and Outcomes

Within 24 hours of antibiotic escalation and catheter exchange, the urine in the new collection bag was clear and amber (Figure 3). Fever resolved, the leukocytosis trended downward and the patient described marked symptomatic improvement from the urinary tract infection. She completed a 10-day course of piperacillin/tazobactam without recurrence of the purple discoloration. The paraplegia was unchanged at the time of writing, and oncological work-up continued; identification of the primary tumor was ongoing.Figure 3Clear amber urine in a new collection bag, 24 hours after catheter exchange and the start of culture-guided piperacillin/tazobactam therapy.A collection bag filled with clear amber urine, marked with volume measurements.

Patient Perspective

The patient and her caregivers were initially alarmed by the unexpected purple discoloration of the urine bag. After a structured explanation of PUBS as a benign visual sign of a treatable urinary infection, she expressed relief and engaged actively with the care plan, including bowel care, catheter hygiene and the wider oncology work-up. She emphasized that being given a clear name and explanation for what she had seen reduced her anxiety considerably.

Discussion

PUBS is a rare but distinctive manifestation of urinary tract infection, with a reported prevalence of 8–16% among chronically catheterized patients and a strong predilection for elderly women.4,5 The mean age at presentation in pooled literature is approximately 75 years, and most published cases occur in nursing-home or long-term-care settings.3 Our patient, at 26 years, sits at the extreme young end of this distribution, comparable to the few young-adult cases reported, such as a 22-year-old woman with neurogenic bladder due to caudal regression syndrome from India and a young Burkinabé patient with uraemia.11,13 These young-adult cases share a common pathway distinct from the typical elderly presentation: rather than a single dominant factor such as advanced age or long-term institutionalization, each is characterized by a structural cause of neurogenic bladder (caudal regression syndrome, and in our patient, metastatic cord compression) combined with a systemic predisposing condition (uraemia, or HIV-related immunosuppression in our patient). This pattern contrasts with a recently reported elderly chronically catheterized woman in whom PUBS occurred on a background of alkaline urine and was managed conservatively, without antibiotics, once culture confirmed asymptomatic colonization.16 Taken together, these cases suggest that age itself is not the operative variable; rather, the determinant is whether structural urinary stasis and a systemic predisposing factor coexist with a UTI requiring active treatment, as in our patient, or with asymptomatic bacteriuria amenable to observation, as in the elderly comparator case. This case shows that PUBS can be driven by the cumulative weight of risk factors rather than by chronological age.

The pathogenesis is now well characterized. Dietary tryptophan is deaminated by gut bacteria to indole, which is absorbed, hydroxylated and sulphated in the liver to indoxyl sulphate, and renally excreted. Urinary bacteria producing indoxyl sulphatase and phosphatase, most commonly Providencia spp., Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Proteus spp. and Enterococcus spp., cleave indoxyl sulphate to indoxyl, which oxidizes in alkaline urine to indigo (blue) and indirubin (red). Our patient’s urinalysis recorded a pH of 6.0, a mildly acidic value that sits outside the classically described alkaline range for PUBS. Most published series report alkaline urine in over 90% of cases, reflecting the pH-dependence of indoxyl sulphatase and phosphatase activity, but a minority of cases, including with urinary pH in the 6.0–6.5 range, have been documented.3,7 The mechanism by which discoloration occurs despite suboptimal pH is not fully established, but plausible explanations include a sufficiently high bacterial enzyme burden or pigment precursor load to overcome reduced catalytic efficiency at lower pH, localized pH gradients within the catheter and bag that differ from the single voided-sample measurement, and possible enzymatic activity from co-colonizing organisms not captured on routine culture. Our patient’s heavy bacteriuria (>105 CFU/mL) and the fulminant, single-strain nature of her infection may have provided enzyme and substrate concentrations sufficient to produce visible pigment despite the acidic milieu. This observation reinforces that a measured urinary pH outside the classic alkaline range should not be used to argue against a PUBS diagnosis when the clinical and visual picture is otherwise characteristic.

Our patient illustrates that multiple low-grade risk factors can substitute for advanced age. Recurrent short-interval catheterization (three catheters in four weeks) provided a repeated nidus for bacterial colonization. Neurogenic bladder secondary to T10/T11 cord compression caused urinary stasis and incomplete drainage. Chronic constipation increased intestinal transit time, augmenting bacterial tryptophan metabolism and indole production. Immobility and prolonged bed-rest amplified both stasis and constipation. Immunosuppression from HIV (CD4 = 194 cells/µL) likely facilitated colonization by a multidrug-resistant E. coli strain. The clustering of these factors is well documented individually,5,6,8 but their simultaneous occurrence in a young patient is unusual.

A clinically important feature of this case is the disconnect between empirical and culture-directed therapy. The patient appeared to deteriorate biochemically (manifesting as overt PUBS) despite intravenous ceftriaxone, the standard empirical agent for urosepsis at our institution. Culture revealed an extended-spectrum cephalosporin-resistant E. coli, consistent with the rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant uropathogens in sub-Saharan Africa. Resolution of both the systemic features of urosepsis and the purple discoloration followed escalation to piperacillin/tazobactam and physical removal of the colonized catheter and bag, as has been reported elsewhere.5,9 Two practical points follow: empirical antibiotics in catheter-associated urinary tract infection must be informed by local antibiograms, and catheter exchange is an indispensable adjunct, since antibiotics alone do not eradicate biofilm-associated organisms.

The diagnostic context here added a second layer of complexity. The same patient presented with metastatic spinal cord compression of undetermined primary. The normal CA-125, CA 19–9 and CEA reduced the pre-test probability of ovarian, pancreato-biliary and colorectal primaries respectively, but these markers have limited sensitivity for early-stage disease and do not exclude breast, lung, lymphoma or sarcoma origins.15 The PUBS therefore served two clinical purposes. First, it was an early visual signal that the patient harbored a resistant uropathogen requiring escalation. Second, it acted as a marker of cumulative immunological and structural vulnerability in a patient already undergoing investigation for occult malignancy. In low-resource settings where access to tumor markers, advanced imaging and culture is delayed, such bedside signals carry disproportionate diagnostic weight.

Regionally, this case has further importance. PUBS remains under-reported in Africa, with isolated cases described from South Africa, Cameroon, Burkina Faso and Nigeria, and none previously from Uganda or East Africa.10–12 In settings where culture services are limited and where stigma surrounding HIV may delay presentation, the purple bag can act as an early clinical signal that prompts targeted investigation and earlier antimicrobial stewardship. In asymptomatic catheterized patients, by contrast, PUBS is largely benign, and management can focus on catheter hygiene, treatment of constipation and reassurance rather than antibiotics.8 Distinguishing symptomatic complicated infection, as in our patient, from asymptomatic bacteriuria with PUBS is essential to avoid both under- and over-treatment.

Strengths of this report include adherence to the SCARE 2023 reporting framework,14 photographic documentation of both the discoloration and its resolution, full microbiological characterization and integration of a tumor-marker panel that informs the wider oncological work-up. Limitations include the absence of speciation by polymerase chain reaction for indole-sulphatase-producing genes, the fact that urine pH was measured on the admission urinalysis rather than re-measured at the moment the discoloration was noted on day 2, and the unresolved primary tumor at the time of writing, which limits long-term prognostic statements.

Conclusion

Purple urine bag syndrome is, first and foremost, a visible clinical indicator that a catheterized patient harbors a urinary tract infection requiring active management; it can arise outside the typical elderly demographic when multiple risk factors converge, as in our patient. Regardless of the patient’s age, the purple bag should be treated as a prompt for urgent urine culture, catheter exchange and culture-directed antibiotic therapy, which together restore both the color of the urine and the patient’s clinical trajectory.
