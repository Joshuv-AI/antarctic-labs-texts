Patient perspectives on the use of artificial intelligence to support treatment decision making in renal cancer: findings from the KATY project
Authors: Christina Golna, Chara Tzavara, Pavlos Golnas, Aikaterini Nikitara, Maria Nomikou, George Kapetanakis, Emilia Daghir – Wojtkowiak, Katarzyna Barud, Syed Zulkifil Haider Shah, Alexander Laird, Javier Alfaro, Fabio Massimo Zanzotto, Maria Gazouli, Kyriakos Souliotis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial-Intelligence (AI) empowered tools are increasingly being assessed for treatment selection in cancer. Patient perceptions on their use will be critical for uptake in clinical practice. We investigated patient willingness to endorse an AI-empowered Personalized Medicine (PM) system (the EU Commission funded “KATY”) to support treatment selection in renal cancer and mapped factors that impact on their perspectives.MethodsThis was a non-interventional, cross-sectional study piloted in Greece through the umbrella Hellenic Cancer Federation (ELLOK). Data were collected through anonymized electronic questionnaires between May and September 2024. Patients were recruited directly by ELLOK (convenience sampling) and provided their full consent prior to enrolling in the study. Results were collected by ELLOK and analysed using SPSS statistical software (version 27.0). For the comparison of proportions, chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used. Multiple logistic regression models were used to investigate the association between patient characteristics and willingness to endorse use of the KATY system. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.Results84 patients participated in the study. Most (89.3%) felt comfortable with an AI-empowered system supporting physicians with renal cancer treatment selection. Over 50% were willing or extremely willing to endorse the use of a tool with the characteristics of the KATY system. Willingness to endorse was significantly lower in participants who were 65 + years old and significantly higher in participants who were employed/self-employed. Factors that impacted on willingness to endorse were, in order of importance, the system’s contribution to treatment selection accuracy, cost and speed, followed by whether the system impacted on the human aspect of care, on the traceability of responsibility for treatment selection and on data privacy and security. Importance attributed to treatment selection cost was significantly associated with disease stage (p = 0.017), whereas treatment selection speed was significantly associated with time from diagnosis (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.82–0.98, p = 0.013). Time from diagnosis was also significantly associated with privacy and data security, with patients diagnosed more recently attributing significantly greater importance to this aspect (OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.86 ─ 0.99, p = 0.047).ConclusionsThis survey sheds light into patient attitudes and practices towards AI-powered systems to support treatment decision in renal cancer. It can, therefore, contribute to the wider discussion on ways to enhance patient understanding and potential endorsement of such systems, particularly in life-threatening or severely debilitating conditions such as cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03457-6.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Renal cancer, Decision making support, Patient perceptions, Health policy

Introduction

AI-empowered Personalized Medicine promises to proactively identify highly individualized, tailor-made, precise care strategies that can target complex health challenges at the specific patient level. To realize the vision of “personalized treatment” it is imperative to first, gather, process, and learn how to reach such conclusions from massive amounts of data, and second, gain healthcare professionals’ and patients’ trust in their use. Both of the latter need to ultimately endorse the value of personalized treatment support so as to integrate this strategy into daily clinical practice.

More specifically, for AI-empowered Personalized Medicine to be a game changer, clinicians need to use it to make everyday decisions. Building trust in AI-assisted systems is a constantly evolving process rooted in education and clinician-machine teaming. However, in this whole engineered-based process, it is critical to ensure the patients’ perspective, including their personal values, lifestyle, or emotional and specific health context, is accounted for. This will assist physicians with leveraging AI-assisted systems, bridge the gap between technical optimization and human-centered care, and ensure treatment decisions made by a doctor with the use of AI can be optimally and seamlessly implemented by and with the patient [1].

In renal cell carcinoma (RCC), choosing the optimal therapy which will provide the best treatment response at the patient-specific level is currently the biggest unmet need. To address this need, project KATY (Knowledge At the Tips of your Fingers), funded by the H2020-EU.3.1. - SOCIETAL CHALLENGES - Health, demographic change and well-being programme under a call “AI for Genomics and Personalized Medicine”, leverages the wealth of available clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) data stored in public repositories and then uses AI-assisted tools to compare across all therapeutic options. These tools include (i) the KATY Holistic Neural Network (KATYHoNN) model and (ii) the Distributed Knowledge Graph. The project aims to implement different AI-assisted strategies to enhance the contribution of -omics in personalized medicine in renal cancer.

The introduction of such an AI-powered physician decision support system in clinical practice would hinge on the acceptance of not only physicians but also patients. User perceptions, experience and acceptance have been shown a critical priority in implementing clinical decision support systems [2]. As patients become increasingly empowered to participate in decision making about their health care options [3] and there is a trend towards “health democracy” or “democracy in health” [4], understanding patients’ awareness of and attitudes towards AI-powered applications, including for health and more specifically for treatment decision support, will be critical in successfully launching such systems.

Therefore, and within the remit of the KATY project, it was agreed early on that the project team would need to gauge patient views on the innovation that KATY offers as well as ensure this innovation is endorsed by patients on care. This would greatly boost the system’s patient-centeredness, which has been shown to positively contribute to both adherence to treatment [5, 6], better treatment outcomes [7] and improved patient-physician relationship [8].

In addition, it was agreed that patient views should be sought through an institutional representation body, such as a Patient Association and / or a Federation of Patient Associations. Involving patients in decision making through institutional representation bodies not only adds a critical layer of collaborative action to health care projects, but also further empowers patients and their representatives to become active participants to the general discussion about the future of health care, on the basis of their actual needs and expectations [9]. Public and patient involvement (PPI) has also been considered increasingly important in health policy decision-making at the health system level (macro-level), such as in system-wide health service planning, resource allocation or priority setting. It is understood to contribute to health care being more responsive to patient and population needs and the health care system becoming more democratic [10]. Existing evidence regarding PPI in macro-level health policy decisions suggests that it improves the quality of and access to health care. Furthermore, it increases user responsiveness and satisfaction with the health care services provided [11, 12].

Based on the above, and to probe into patient perspectives and willingness to endorse an AI-empowered system with the KATY features, we conducted a perspectives and willingness survey amongst renal cancer patients, initially in Greece, through the umbrella Hellenic Cancer Federation (ELLOK). The Hellenic Cancer Federation (ELLOK) is a patient-led non-profit organization with 49 Cancer Patient Associations as members, representing various cancers and regions of the country, with a comprehensive understanding of the diverse landscape of cancer care in Greece. Additional study objectives were to record barriers / concerns of patients that impact on their willingness, and to correlate these with sociodemographic and cancer diagnosis information.

Methods

This was a non-interventional, cross-sectional study conducted between May and September 2024. Data were collected through an online questionnaire that was distributed to patients directly by ELLOK. The study recruited adult patients with a self-reported renal cancer diagnosis who at the time of study were members of/ affiliated with / or friends of a Cancer Patient Association in Greece that was affiliated with ELLOK. Dissemination was the sole responsibility and decision of ELLOK (convenience sampling). A subject was considered eligible for inclusion in this study, only if all the following criteria applied: adults aged ≥ 18 years old, able and willing to sign the informed consent form, able to read and write in Greek, self-reported renal cancer diagnosis (all stages).

We developed a novel, custom questionnaire (see Supplement) to assess patient willingness to endorse a diagnosis and treatment paradigm as the one proposed by the KATY system. We also sought to correlate this willingness with patient sociodemographic, cancer diagnosis, level of familiarity with and acceptance of AI-empowered tools in general and in health in particular, as well as specific aspects of the KATY system. The questionnaire consisted of three sections: Sect. 1 recorded basic anonymized participant demographics, in ranges supporting aggregation of data, wherever possible and appropriate, (age – ranges, place of residence – urban, semi-urban, rural, educational level, marital status, income level - ranges) and renal cancer diagnosis details. Section 2 recorded background use of and perceptions towards technology, including health information tracing applications. Section 3 provided an upfront detailed information sheet that introduced the respondent to the KATY characteristics and described its basic features, to ensure a shared understanding of the topic. It, then, recorded patient willingness to endorse its use by the treating physician relative to the following aspects: privacy and data security, accuracy, speed, cost, and human aspect of care. Other barriers / considerations, such as transparency in decision making were also explored as elements with a potential impact. The questionnaire was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Health Policy Institute and reviewed by the KATY legal, ethical and privacy advisors (University of Vienna, Department of Innovation and Digitalisation in Law, Vienna) as well as ELLOK Board of Directors prior to its finalization.

Time from renal cancer diagnosis was presented as mean, median, standard deviation and interquartile range. The rest of the variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies. For the comparison of proportions chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used. If the normality assumption was satisfied for the comparison of means between two groups, Student’s t-tests were used. Mann-Whitney test was applied for the comparison of continuous variables between two groups when the distribution was deviated from normal [13]. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were adopted to investigate the association between demographic and clinical characteristics (i.e. stage, time from diagnosis), and willingness and barriers to endorsing use of the KATY platform, from which odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals emerged. Multiple logistic regression was conducted with stepwise method with pentry=0.05 and premoval=0.10. Models’ diagnostics were evaluated using the Hosmer and Lemeshow statistic [14]. All reported p values were two-tailed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software (version 27.0).

Results

84 renal cancer patients participated in the survey. Their demographic and cancer diagnosis characteristics are summarized in Table 1, whereas their self-reported use of technology, including AI-empowered applications, is presented in Table 2. Table 3 summarizes participant self-reported awareness as well as willingness to endorse use of AI to support treatment decision in healthcare. Further, over 50% of respondents would be readily or extremely willing to accept use of an AI-supported platform with the characteristics of the KATY system to support choice of treatment, with less than 15% being unwilling or extremely unwilling. Willingness to endorse use of such a system was significantly greater in participants who were 55–64 years old compared to participants who were 65 + years old (OR = 3.80; 95% CI: 1.33–10.90); p = 0.013) and significantly higher in participants who were employed/ self-employed (OR = 3.33; 95% CI: 1.29–8.63); p = 0.013) (Table 4).

Respondents also assigned a relative importance to the factors that impacted on their willingness to endorse the use of a platform with the KATY system characteristics. Treatment selection decision accuracy was rated as an important/ very important factor by 90% of the sample, followed by treatment selection decision cost (86.3%) and speed (85.9%). Privacy and data security was considered an important/ very important factor by 66.3% of respondents (Table 5). Multiple logistic regression analyses revealed that decision cost was significantly associated with disease stage (p = 0.017). The percentage of patients attributing importance to decision cost was significantly lower in patients in Stage I disease (72.2%), whereas patients in stage II/ III or IV had 8.08 times greater probability to consider decision cost as important/ extremely important compared to patients in Stage I (OR = 8.08, 95% CI 1.40–46.66, p = 0.020). Further, decision speed was significantly associated with time from diagnosis (OR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.98, p = 0.013), and patients who rated decision speed as important/ extremely important were diagnosed significantly more recently. In addition, maintaining the human aspect of care was significantly associated with patients’ educational level, i.e. patients with a college degree (adult higher education)/ bachelor’s degree/ master’s or PhD degree had a 4.58 times greater probability to rate human aspect of care as important/ extremely important (OR = 4.58, 95%CI 1.01–22.31, p = 0.049). Finally, although in univariate analyses, privacy and data security was found to be significantly associated with time from diagnosis (p = 0.013) and disease stage (p = 0.016) (Table 6), after multiple logistic regression, it emerged that only time from diagnosis was significantly associated with the importance of privacy and data security, and patients who considered this aspect important/ extremely important were diagnosed significantly more recently (OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.86 ─ 0.99, p = 0.047) (Table 7).

Discussion

This survey mapped perceptions of renal cancer patients and their willingness to endorse the use of an AI – empowered treatment decision support tool with the features of the KATY system, as well as barriers and concerns that shape these perceptions. Our study confirmed that most renal cancer patients would be willing or extremely willing to endorse the use of such a platform, if it were managed / overseen by a physician. It also highlighted that almost 1 in 3 patients would be neutral towards its use, allowing room for and indicating a potential for more targeted and in-depth educational and knowledge building activities that may address any unresolved concerns or contextual ambivalence and offer patients an opportunity to better understand and potentially strengthen confidence in the use of such tools. Our findings are in line with previous literature on the importance attached to the physician maintaining responsibility for treatment decisions [15], as AI could support rather than replace clinicians and optimize collaborative intelligence [16].

Willingness to endorse use of a platform with the KATY characteristics was significantly lower in participants who were 65 + years old. This is in line with consistent references in the literature [17] of the impact of age on the adoption of technology or AI-powered solutions. Age is seen as a persistent barrier, as older adults are considered more sceptic, and less confident in endorsing AI supported tools, as they may lack critical skills and knowledge to be able to understand, evaluate and use AI [18].

Willingness was also significantly higher in participants who were employed/ self-employed. This is a finding that may indicate a broader correlation between an active lifestyle (i.e., being in employment) and the level of acceptance of AI-empowered tools to support cancer diagnosis and treatment selection. As cancer survivors often deal with reduced employment post‑treatment and strive to return to work to improve quality of life [19], patients in active employment may be more eager to optimize use of all available resources to ensure optimal treatment selection, to ensure swifter return to work and less risk of long-term unemployment [20].

Willingness was assessed against specific barriers and concerns that have been reported in the literature in the past. These included privacy, transparency/uncertainty, human element of care, social justice, cost for patient and healthcare system [21], lack of trust in data privacy, patient safety, technological maturity, and the possibility of full automation [22], perception of less customization and personalization of AI supported tools [23], preference for human contact and discretion over accuracy and speed [24], acceptability, comfortability, informed consent, privacy and security [25] and the need for physicians to have the final responsibility for treatment decisions [15, 16].

Irrespective of levels of willingness to endorse the system, participants were overwhelmingly concerned about the accuracy of such an AI-powered decision support tool (relative importance 90.0%). This is in line with previous literature on patient perspectives on the use of AI for screening [26] and reflects literature on the importance of perceived trust in the application by users (i.e. healthcare professionals) themselves.

Further, patients attached a high relative importance to decision cost (86.3%) and decision speed (85.9%). Importance allocated to decision cost was significantly correlated with disease stage, with patients at later stages (II, III and IV) being more sensitive towards this factor than those patients diagnosed with stage I cancer without progression. This finding is in line with National Cancer Institute’s patient information on the relationship between suffering from advanced or recurrent cancer and a higher risk of financial cancer toxicity [27], i.e., an overwhelming financial burden, distress, and hardship linked to the high costs of cancer care. With regards to decision speed, time from diagnosis impacted on relative importance attached to this factor. For patients newly diagnosed with cancer, there is a profound stress that comes with the diagnosis [28] and a reported agony regarding the speed with which services may be provided and outcomes achieved, which adjusts itself as time living with the condition increases.

Educational level impacted on the importance attached to human aspect of care. This factor reflected preference for a diagnosis and treatment choice decision to be managed by a human rather than an AI-supported tool, and is highlighted in previous literature [29, 30] as of critical importance. The importance of symbiosis between humans and AI has emerged as a dominant theme in the literature on AI [26], which consistently underlines that patients may be open to AI use, but only if it sustains the human physician-patient relationship. In our survey, human aspect of care was rated below decision accuracy, cost and speed and was significantly correlated with patients’ educational level, with those patients that had attained a higher educational level attaching a greater relative importance to it. This may be explained by the fact that patients with higher educational attainment tend to have greater health literacy and more active engagement in health care, which in turn is associated with higher expectations of shared decision-making, information sharing, and person-centered care from their physicians [31–33]. In addition, patients with a higher educational level may be more sceptical of the importance and accuracy of technology than those with a lower educational state. This is mentioned in the literature as an “informed skepticism”, because more educated users are aware of limitations, transparency issues, and risks [34].

Concerns reported elsewhere in the literature [35, 36], such as the traceability of responsibility for decision were less important overall to respondents. The latter appeared more focused on the “positive” aspects of their care, such as accuracy, speed and cost of decisions that may optimize their care, rather than seek to pre-empt allocation of “blame” for any potential suboptimal decision making.

Finally, with regards to privacy concerns, which is consistently highlighted in the literature as one of the most pressing barriers to effective and well accepted use of AI decision support tools [37], our participants rated this factor as the least important to them (66.3% relative importance), with patients recently diagnosed being statistically significantly more probable to attribute high importance to this factor. This finding, which has been reported elsewhere [38], may be attributed to the diagnosis being recent and the patient still grappling with how and when to communicate their health status to family, work or social circles. They may, therefore, be extremely sensitive to any data privacy-related aspects of care. As time passes, and the patient settles into both the diagnosis and the management of their condition, concerns about privacy breaches may abate and the patient focuses on the outcomes of their care. Studies note that patients who are several months past diagnosis commonly report lower distress regarding accessing clinical information and demonstrate increased engagement with their care plan and outcomes rather than preoccupation with privacy or concealment of their disease [39]. The latter finding is critical in terms of the overall discussion [35] around AI-supported tools in health care decision making and may provide additional insights into what is important to the patient.

Overall, our survey highlights that in debilitating and potentially life-threatening conditions such as cancer, the patient may be more prepared and eager to endorse their use, if they measurably contribute to enhanced decision accuracy, cost and speed. All other concerns follow and may be addressed through targeted actions towards patients, particularly in collaboration with their Patient Associations.

Limitations

The study’s findings should be interpreted allowing for several methodological considerations. Data were collected using a self-report questionnaire, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. The survey was piloted in Greece through ELLOK to ensure patient concerns and perspectives are input into the development and launch phase of the KATY system. As such, results related to cost and speed may be conditioned by personal patient experiences with Greek healthcare system specifics regarding funding and access to care. Nonetheless, the survey is replicable in any European country, through a patient association or an umbrella federation, and as such can provide insights into similarities and differences in perceptions and practices of patients in different healthcare systems.

Although recruitment was open nationwide, the final sample consisted of 84 participants. The sample size was relatively small but likely reflects the voluntary nature of participation and the absence of incentives. Recruitment was conducted through the national Federation of Cancer Patient Associations, which may have introduced selection bias. Individuals affiliated with patient associations are typically more engaged in disease management and more informed. Consequently, patients who are less engaged with healthcare services or not affiliated with patient organizations may be under-represented. Future studies would benefit from combining multiple recruitment strategies and larger samples to enhance greater representativeness. Finally, this survey reflects patient views. It is importance to ensure physician and institutional buy-in before AI-empowered healthcare decision making can be fully adopted.

Conclusion

This survey probes into patient readiness to endorse use of AI-powered tools to support treatment decision in renal cancer and the factors that shape this willingness. The survey highlighted the importance attached to specific elements of a system that would bear the characteristics of KATY, such as, in order or importance, decision accuracy, decision cost and decision speed, followed by the human aspect of care, traceability of responsibility for decision and privacy and data concerns. Our survey provides a contextual background and a relative patient ranking of aspects related to use of AI empowered decision support tools, to help inform the development and launch of the KATY system. Going forward, it would benefit from further in-depth qualitative research to explore how patients understand, negotiate and prioritize these aspects in practice, particularly in relation to ethical concerns that may not be fully captured through survey-based methods.
