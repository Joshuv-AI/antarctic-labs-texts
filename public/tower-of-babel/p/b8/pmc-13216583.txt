Impact performance and probabilistic reliability of natural fiber-reinforced standard and high-strength concrete
Authors: Ajila Hexil S, Karthikeyan Kothandapani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of sustainable high-performance concrete has increasingly emphasized the incorporation of natural fibers to improve mechanical and impact resistance. This study presents a comprehensive and novel investigation of multiple natural fibers, coconut (0.5–1.5%), flax (0.1–0.5%), jute (0.15–0.55%), and bamboo, hemp, and kenaf (0.25–1.25%), evaluated in two concrete grades (M25 and M80). In addition to experimental assessment, probabilistic reliability modeling was integrated to characterize the stochastic nature of impact behavior in natural fiber-reinforced concrete (NFRC). Repeated impact testing in accordance with ACI 544-2R demonstrated that coconut fiber at 1–1.25% provided the highest impact resistance, increasing failure counts by 65% in M25 and 83% in M80 relative to the control concrete. Kenaf (0.75–1%) and bamboo (0.5–1%) exhibited moderate improvements of up to 20%, whereas jute, flax, and hemp produced comparatively modest gains of 5–10%. Despite substantial improvements in impact resistance, compressive strength remained comparable to that of the control concrete. Ductility indices and post-cracking ratios revealed distinct post-peak deformation mechanisms governed by fiber type, including pull-out, rupture, and interfacial slip. To further quantify performance differences, a grade–fiber synergy analysis was proposed to evaluate the interaction between concrete strength and fiber efficiency across grades. The probabilistic characterization of impact resistance was performed using Weibull statistics, supplemented by bootstrap resampling and Bayesian uncertainty analysis, enabling assessment of reliability and parameter stability. The results establish a reliability-based framework for optimizing NFRC formulations to improve structural resilience under dynamic loading.

Introduction

Developing impact-resistant concrete systems has become a critical priority in modern structural engineering, as infrastructure is increasingly exposed to dynamic hazards such as accidental impacts, debris strikes, and seismic-induced collisions. Unlike static loading, impact events generate highly localized damage, rapid stress-wave propagation, and complex fracture mechanisms that require specialized evaluation techniques. In this context, drop-weight and low-velocity impact tests remain reliable experimental methods for characterizing crack initiation thresholds, energy absorption capacity, and post-fracture toughness of brittle cementitious materials1.

Natural fibers are increasingly used as sustainable reinforcement materials in cement-based composites due to their ability to enhance crack resistance through improved fiber–matrix interaction2. The mechanical performance of natural fiber-reinforced concrete mainly depends on fiber type and dosage, which influence post-cracking behavior and impact energy absorption capacity3. Numerous studies report that natural fibers enhance energy dissipation, ductility, and post-peak behavior in both cementitious and geopolymer matrices while reducing environmental impact4. They also contribute to strength enhancement and improved microstructural integrity in high-strength concrete by providing effective crack-bridging action5. Improvements in impact resistance are primarily attributed to fiber pull-out, fibrillation, crack-bridging action, and interfacial friction, which delay crack propagation and dissipate energy6. Lignocellulosic plant fibers such as hemp, coconut (coir), jute, flax, bamboo, and kenaf possess favorable tensile strength, surface roughness, and hierarchical microstructure, enabling effective multi-scale reinforcement within concrete matrices7.

Experimental investigations have demonstrated measurable performance gains in natural fiber–reinforced concretes. Hemp fiber–reinforced foam concrete optimized via Taguchi methods showed improvements of 15.76% in energy absorption and 23.58% in matrix resilience8. Coir fibers at 0.5–1.0% volume fraction increased compressive strength by 8–15% and effectively suppressed crack growth9. Eco-efficient concretes incorporating agricultural waste and natural fibers achieved strength gains of 15–25% while maintaining lightweight characteristics10. Kenaf fibers improved flexural and micromechanical properties of high-strength cement composites by 10–20%, depending on treatment and dosage11. Modified jute fibers enhanced compressive strength by 20% and splitting tensile strength by 12% in recycled aggregate systems12. Plant fibers have also demonstrated benefits in high-strength and geopolymer concretes. Their inclusion improves crack resistance and sustained load-carrying capacity compared with plain concrete13. In geopolymer matrices, natural fibers increased flexural strength by 15–22%, indicating improved fiber–matrix compatibility14. Bamboo fibers increased fracture energy by 298.4% in lightweight aggregate concretes15 and improved compressive and tensile strength through enhanced interfacial bonding16, while bamboo-reinforced UHPC exhibited 12–19% gains in flexural strength17. Kenaf-reinforced geopolymer concrete showed reduced sorptivity (14–28%) and improved durability under cyclic exposure18. Flax fiber–reinforced recycled aggregate concretes demonstrated enhanced strain-rate sensitivity and dynamic modulus under impact loading19. Thin structural systems also benefit from natural fiber reinforcement. Sisal fiber ferrocement slabs exhibited approximately 35% improvement in impact energy absorption due to effective crack bridging20, and broader reviews confirm consistent impact resistance enhancements in fiber-reinforced composites21. Banana fiber self-compacting concrete achieved 10–22% improvement in impact energy absorption at 1.0–1.5% fiber content22. Multi-layer hemp–sisal geopolymer concretes exhibited synergistic behavior, improving compressive strength by 12–18% and flexural strength by 20–28%23.

Coconut fiber–reinforced concrete has been extensively investigated, with studies reporting up to a twofold increase in impact resistance compared to control specimens24. This improvement is primarily attributed to enhanced crack-bridging and energy absorption mechanisms under dynamic loading25. Additionally, the inclusion of coconut fibers delays crack propagation and improves post-cracking behavior in cement-based composites26. From a sustainability perspective, natural fibers can provide mechanical performance comparable to that of synthetic fibers while offering a substantially lower environmental impact27. Coconut fiber addition has also been shown to reduce water permeability and enhance durability at optimal contents up to 0.6%28. Insights from hybrid natural fiber polymer composites further clarify impact mechanisms relevant to cementitious systems. Impact performance depends strongly on fiber volume fraction and hybrid configuration, with mechanisms such as pull-out and rupture observed in sago palm–carbon systems29, stiffness–impact trade-offs in recycled cotton/PET hybrids30, filler-induced debonding in jute–banana–eggshell composites31, and damage redistribution in bamboo–banana–cotton sandwich panels32. Pistachio shell–filled PMMA composites demonstrated improved impact strength at moderate filler contents and finer particle sizes, whereas higher fractions and coarser particles promoted agglomeration and stress concentration, reducing toughness33. These findings highlight the sensitivity of impact resistance to fiber architecture and interfacial quality. Fiber orientation and processing-induced damage further influence impact behavior. Drilling-induced delamination in kenaf–glass laminates has been linked to feed-rate sensitivity and stress concentration34. Conversely, unidirectional fiber alignment in banana–snake plant hybrids enhanced impact resistance and reduced water uptake35. Hybridization strategies have also shown benefits, including improved impact strength in jute–snake plant systems36, stiffness gains in jute–banana twine composites37, and enhanced energy absorption in jute–Rosa hybrida hybrids despite reduced tensile strength38. Natural fibers additionally offer thermal insulation benefits due to intrinsic porosity and lumen structure, as demonstrated in sisal–glass gypsum composites39 and numerical studies on natural fiber–reinforced polymers40. Durability remains a critical consideration. Hydrothermal aging studies indicate that moisture uptake and fiber–matrix compatibility strongly influence long-term performance, with accelerated debonding observed in sisal epoxy systems compared to glass fiber composites41.

Matrix modification with supplementary cementitious materials, such as fly ash and silica fume, further enhances performance. These materials improve particle packing, refine pore structure, and increase compressive strength by approximately 18–25%42. Fly ash–silica fume systems also reduce shrinkage and improve long-term fiber stability43. Hemp fiber incorporation in fly ash–based high-strength concrete improves tensile strength and reduces brittleness44,45. Hybrid systems combining natural and synthetic fibers, such as coconut and polypropylene, have demonstrated improvements in impact resistance of up to 40%46. Multiple-fiber studies confirm that fiber type and combination significantly affect mechanical performance and water absorption47. Most investigations identify an optimal natural fiber dosage range of 0.5–1.5% by weight to balance strength, impact resistance, and durability48, with jute-reinforced concretes exhibiting sustained toughness under durability cycles49.

Despite extensive evidence supporting the mechanical and sustainability benefits of natural fiber–reinforced concretes, their behavior under standardized low-velocity drop-weight impact loading remains comparatively underexplored. Existing studies predominantly emphasize static or quasi-static performance, or report impact resistance using global indicators such as absorbed energy and total blow count, offering limited insight into failure progression and performance variability. Moreover, the combined influence of fiber type, volume fraction, and concrete strength grade on impact behavior and probabilistic reliability has not been systematically examined, particularly for eco-efficient systems incorporating supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash and silica fume.

To address these gaps, the present study evaluates the impact resistance of natural fiber–reinforced concrete incorporating fly ash and silica fume under repeated low-velocity drop-weight loading. Six fibers, coconut, jute, bamboo, kenaf, flax, and hemp are examined in both normal-strength (M25) and high-strength (M80) matrices. The study systematically assesses the influence of fiber type and volume fraction on: (i) blows to crack initiation and ultimate failure; (ii) impact energy absorption and post-cracking ductility; and (iii) failure mode evolution.

Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on single-fiber systems and deterministic performance metrics, this work integrates multi-fiber, multi-grade evaluation with reliability-based modelling. A novel Grade–Fiber Synergy Index (GFSI) is proposed to quantify fiber–matrix compatibility across different strength grades, enabling direct cross-grade comparison. The statistical significance of fiber type and dosage effects is assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), providing quantitative insight into their relative contributions to impact performance. Beyond deterministic evaluation, Weibull-based probabilistic models, supported by bootstrap resampling and Bayesian inference, are employed to characterize impact reliability and associated uncertainties at both crack-initiation and ultimate-failure stages. By integrating material design, impact response, failure mechanisms, and probabilistic assessment, this study establishes a unified and reliability-oriented framework for evaluating sustainable natural fiber–reinforced concrete systems under impact loading.

Concrete mix proportions

The concrete mixes were proportioned in accordance with IS 10262:201963 and IS 456:200064 for durability and material requirements. Two grades, M25 and M80, were designed to evaluate matrix-fiber interaction under impact loading. Aggregates were selected in accordance with IS 383:201663. The M25 mix was designed for moderate exposure with a water–cement ratio of 0.39 and incorporated 25% fly ash and 5% silica fume to enhance particle packing and reduce porosity. The M80 mix was proportioned in accordance with Annexes A and G of IS 10262:2019, with a low water–cement ratio of 0.27, containing 15% fly ash and 10% silica fume to improve matrix densification and refine the interfacial transition zone (ITZ). A polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer was used to maintain workability, particularly for the low w/c M80 mixes. Natural fibers were incorporated at volume fractions based on geometry and workability considerations. Coconut fibers (0.5–1.5%), flax (0.1–0.5%), jute (0.15–0.55%), and bamboo, hemp, and kenaf (0.25–1.25%) by volume of the binder were selected in line with prior studies13,65–67 to balance mechanical enhancement and workability. Fibers were added in dry condition, and their moisture content was determined by oven-drying at 105 °C to constant mass. The absorbed water was deducted from the mixing water to maintain the target water–cement ratio.

Specimen preparation and curing

Three 100 × 100 × 100 mm cubes were cast for each mix to determine compressive strength in accordance with IS 516:202168, providing triplicate measurements for statistical reliability. For drop-weight impact testing as per ACI 544.2R-8969, six cylindrical specimens (150 mm diameter × 63.5 mm height) were prepared per mix to account for variability in natural fiber orientation and distribution. All specimens were mixed uniformly, proportioned accurately, and compacted properly. After casting, specimens were demolded after 24 h and water-cured at 27 ± 2 °C until the test age. Testing procedures

Compressive strength test

Compressive strength was determined on 100 × 100 × 100 mm cube specimens using a 3000 kN capacity Compression Testing Machine (CTM), as shown in Fig. 4a, in accordance with IS 516:2021. The specimens were loaded at a constant stress rate of 0.23 MPa/s until failure. The average of three specimens was reported as the compressive strength of each mix.Fig. 4Test set-up for (a) compressive strength testing and (b) drop-weight impact testing.

Test set-up for (a) compressive strength testing and (b) drop-weight impact testing.

Drop weight impact test

Repeated drop-weight impact tests were conducted using the ACI 544 apparatus shown in Fig. 4b. A 4.54 kg hammer was repeatedly dropped from a height of 457 mm onto a 63.5 mm diameter steel ball positioned centrally on the concrete disc specimen (150 mm diameter × 63.5 mm thickness). The specimen was placed within a rigid frame with a 162 mm spacing between opposing lugs. The number of blows required to produce the first visible crack was recorded as the initial cracking impact count (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{N}}_{\mathrm{ic}}$$\end{document}), while the number of blows causing ultimate failure was recorded as the final cracking impact count (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{N}}_{\mathrm{fc}}$$\end{document}).

Results and discussion

Compressive strength of NFRC

The compressive strength results (Fig. 5) indicate that natural fiber incorporation produces a characteristic optimum response in both M25 and M80 concretes. The control mixes achieved 34.83 MPa and 93.80 MPa for M25 and M80, respectively. Coconut (CF) and kenaf (KF) fibers exhibited the most stable compressive response. For M25, KF-1 (33.92 MPa) and CF-1.25 (34.09 MPa) maintained strengths within 3% of the control. For M80, CF-1.25, and KF-0.75, the maximum compressive stress reached 89.93 MPa, corresponding to less than a 5% reduction relative to the control, confirming that moderate fiber incorporation does not significantly compromise compressive capacity. Similar improvements in kenaf fiber-reinforced concrete were observed by Elsaid et al.53 and Lam et al.54, who attributed strength stability to effective crack arrest and mechanical interlocking within the matrix.Fig. 5Compressive strength of G1 (M25) and G2 (M80) natural fiber reinforced concrete.

Compressive strength of G1 (M25) and G2 (M80) natural fiber reinforced concrete.

Jute and flax fibers demonstrated moderate improvement at 0.5% and 1.0% volume fractions in both concrete grades. However, at 1.5%, a consistent reduction in compressive strength was observed, indicating that excessive fiber content disrupts matrix continuity and increases internal voids. Hemp fiber mixes showed behavior comparable to jute and flax, with optimal dispersion at ≤ 1.0%, improving microcrack resistance, but higher dosage leading to fiber agglomeration and ITZ weakening. Bamboo fiber mixes exhibited greater sensitivity beyond 1.0%, with pronounced strength reduction at 1.5%, likely due to higher water absorption and elastic mismatch within the dense cementitious matrix. These trends are consistent with Zhang et al.55 and Yan et al.12, as well as recent bamboo fiber investigations15–17, which report that fiber dispersion efficiency and interfacial bonding govern compressive performance.

Impact performance of NFRC

Initial impact—crack initiation resistance

The number of blows required to initiate the first visible crack reflects the concrete matrix’s resistance to the onset of microcracking under repeated impact loading. The variation in crack-initiation resistance of NFRC M25 and M80 grades is shown in Fig. 6, which indicates that the inclusion of natural fibers improves resistance to localized tensile stress development within the cementitious matrix. Coconut fiber exhibited the highest crack initiation resistance in both grades. In M25, CF-1.25 achieved 104 blows (≈65% increase), while in M80, it reached 187 blows at 1%. The superior performance can be attributed to the higher elongation capacity and rough surface morphology of coconut fibers, which enhance mechanical interlocking and improve stress redistribution within the cement matrix. Similar mechanisms were reported by Ali et al.24 and Wang et al.50. Kenaf and hemp fibers demonstrated optimal performance at 0.75%, with kenaf reaching 96 and 103 blows, and hemp achieving 91 and 98 blows in M25 and M80, respectively. At these intermediate dosages, efficient fiber dispersion promotes microcrack bridging and delays crack coalescence. However, higher contents reduce matrix continuity and interfacial bonding efficiency, consistent with observations by Elsaid et al.53 and Alani et al.45. Jute and flax fibers showed moderate enhancement within 0.35–1.0%, followed by a decline at higher dosages. Failure mode characterization under impact

The present investigation revealed that plain concrete specimens of both M25 and M80 grades predominantly exhibited brittle failure characterized by three to four major radial cracks extending from the point of impact toward the specimen edge, accompanied by localized cone formation and partial fragmentation. As illustrated in Fig. 10, the control specimens display straight and widely spaced crack trajectories with minimal secondary branching, indicating sudden tensile splitting and rapid loss of structural integrity. In contrast, fiber-reinforced specimens (Fig. 11) exhibited a distinctly modified failure morphology. The incorporation of natural fibers transformed the failure mode from dominant radial splitting to a more distributed cracking pattern characterized by multiple fine radial cracks, enhanced crack branching, and reduced crack opening widths. Instead of a few major fractures, the impact zone developed a network of closely spaced cracks, indicating effective crack bridging across crack planes. Similar transitions from brittle fracture to distributed cracking in natural fiber-reinforced concrete under impact loading have been documented in previous studies50,51. The observed crack dispersion is attributed to fiber bridging and pull-out mechanisms, which interrupt crack continuity and delay crack coalescence. Coconut and hemp fiber-reinforced mixes showed extensive crack subdivision and improved surface integrity, consistent with earlier findings that ductile natural fibers promote pull-out governed failure and enhance post-impact stability24,45,50. Microstructural characterization

SEM observations (Figs. 15a, b) confirm that post-impact response is governed by the interaction between fiber tensile capacity and fiber–matrix interfacial resistance. Under impact loading, radially propagating microcracks interact with embedded fibers, and energy dissipation occurs primarily through pull-out, rupture, or interfacial debonding depending on fiber morphology and bond strength.

In M25, coconut and kenaf fibers predominantly exhibit slip-controlled behavior characterized by partial pull-out, surface abrasion, and crack deflection, indicating progressive interfacial debonding and frictional sliding. Bamboo and jute show greater evidence of fiber rupture and localized matrix splitting, suggesting stress concentration around stiff inclusions and limited sliding capacity. Flax shows early interfacial separation, with aligned microcracks in the ITZ, indicating weak shear transfer and reduced sustained bridging. Hemp displays strong embedment with cohesive matrix cracking and crack diversion, consistent with effective stress redistribution prior to rupture.

In M80, the higher matrix stiffness intensifies stress transfer at the interface. Coconut and kenaf maintain observable pull-out with matrix abrasion, confirming continued energy dissipation through sliding despite the dense matrix. Jute and bamboo exhibit more frequent rupture and circumferential microcracking, reflecting limited post-peak deformation. Flax again shows premature debonding, while hemp acts as a stiff bridge that restrains crack opening until critical stress is reached, followed by abrupt rupture or interface splitting. Overall, the transition from M25 to M80 narrows the fracture process zone and shifts the dominant mechanism from progressive pull-out toward rupture-controlled failure in fibers with limited deformability.

Synergy analysis

Grade–fiber synergy analysis

The effectiveness of fiber reinforcement in concrete depends not only on intrinsic fiber properties but also on the characteristics of the surrounding cementitious matrix. To investigate grade-dependent behavior, a Grade–Fiber Synergy Analysis was conducted by comparing the impact resistance of fiber-reinforced concrete in normal-strength (M25) and high-strength (M80) matrices. To quantify the relative efficiency of each fiber across different grades, a Grade–Fiber Synergy Index (GFSI) was introduced. The index was calculated using the failure impact resistance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{N}}_{\mathrm{fc}}$$\end{document}) at the optimum fiber dosage for each fiber type, as expressed in Eq. (5):5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GFSI=\frac{\mathrm{\%} increase in {\mathrm{N}}_{\mathrm{fc}} of M80 relative to its control}{\mathrm{\%} increase in {\mathrm{N}}_{\mathrm{fc}} of M25 relative to its control}$$\end{document}

The respective control mixes for M25 and M80 were used as reference states to isolate the contribution of fiber reinforcement. A GFSI greater than unity indicates performance amplification in high-strength concrete, whereas a value less than unity suggests relatively greater effectiveness in normal-strength concrete. The underlying rationale is associated with matrix densification. High-strength concrete possesses lower porosity, a denser interfacial transition zone (ITZ), and improved confinement. These characteristics enhance interfacial stress transfer and increase resistance to fiber pull-out, thereby promoting more effective crack-bridging under impact loading. However, improved matrix properties translate into enhanced performance only when the fiber can mobilize the increased interfacial stresses. Failure impact resistance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{N}}_{\mathrm{fc}}$$\end{document}) was selected as the governing parameter because it represents the cumulative post-cracking impact resistance sustained up to failure, incorporating the combined contributions of crack bridging, pull-out, and fiber rupture mechanisms. The GFSI framework provides a rational basis for evaluating fiber–matrix compatibility and supports performance-oriented material selection.

Grade-dependent fiber performance

Figure 16 presents the Grade–Fiber Synergy Index (GFSI) values for all investigated natural fibers, clearly demonstrating that fiber effectiveness under impact loading is strongly dependent on concrete grade. Bamboo fiber exhibits the highest GFSI value (2.17), indicating pronounced performance amplification in M80 concrete. This behavior suggests that bamboo fibers effectively utilize the improved confinement and enhanced interfacial bonding provided by the high-strength matrix. Under impact loading, the denser microstructure promotes stronger stress transfer across the fiber–matrix interface, enabling sustained crack bridging and greater resistance prior to rupture or resisted pull-out.Fig. 16Grade fiber synergy index for NFRC.

Grade fiber synergy index for NFRC.

Jute (1.81), kenaf (1.63), and hemp (1.47) also demonstrate GFSI values greater than unity, confirming preferential performance in M80 concrete. The enhanced synergy of these fibers may be attributed to increased frictional resistance during pull-out and improved stress redistribution within the stiffer matrix. In high-strength concrete, reduced porosity and a more compact interfacial transition zone (ITZ) limit premature debonding, allowing these fibers to contribute more effectively to crack restraint before failure.

Coconut fiber (1.27) shows a moderately balanced response, indicating relatively stable reinforcing efficiency across both grades. This suggests that its crack-bridging behavior is less sensitive to matrix densification and that similar pull-out mechanisms govern its performance in both M25 and M80 concretes. In contrast, flax fiber exhibits a significantly lower GFSI value (0.24), indicating limited enhancement in high-strength concrete. This reduced synergy may be associated with weaker interfacial interaction or premature debonding under increased confinement, which restricts effective stress transfer and sustained crack bridging.

In high-strength (M80) concrete, the denser matrix enhances confinement and interfacial stress transfer. For fibers with adequate tensile capacity and surface interaction, this shifts the dominant mechanism toward rupture-controlled behavior or strongly resisted pull-out. Crack propagation becomes more constrained, and the fracture process zone narrows, resulting in improved macroscopic impact resistance.

Implications of grade–fiber synergy

The results confirm that grade-dependent fiber performance must be interpreted in terms of fiber–matrix compatibility. While increasing concrete grade produces a denser matrix with reduced porosity and improved interfacial bonding, superior impact performance is achieved only when the fiber has sufficient tensile capacity and interfacial strength to exploit these matrix improvements. Enhanced stress transfer and crack-bridging mechanisms amplify performance, as observed for bamboo, jute, kenaf, and hemp in M80 concrete. Fibers unable to sustain higher interfacial stresses, such as flax, show limited synergy, while coconut fiber exhibits stable behavior across grades. The GFSI therefore serves as a performance-based selection tool for identifying fibers best suited to specific concrete grades in impact-resistant applications such as industrial floors, precast panels, protective slabs, and pavements, enabling rational optimization of fiber–matrix systems.

Probabilistic modelling of impact resistance

While nested ANOVA establishes statistical significance and quantifies the relative contribution of each factor, it does not directly evaluate the probability of failure under repeated impact loading. Therefore, a probabilistic modelling approach is adopted in the following section to assess the reliability and survival probability of the tested mixes.

Weibull parameter estimation

The two-parameter Weibull distribution70 is characterized by (i) the shape parameter, β (Weibull modulus), and (ii) the scale parameter, η (characteristic life). Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) is employed to derive these parameters for each fiber type under both first-crack and failure-crack impact energies. The Weibull probability plots for both the M25 and M80 natural fiber–reinforced concrete mixtures exhibit strong linearity in the ln(x) versus ln(ln(1/(1–F))) domain, confirming that the impact responses follow a two-parameter Weibull distribution with high predictive reliability.

Uncertainty assessment of Weibull predictions

The Weibull parameters obtained from maximum likelihood estimation provide representative point estimates of impact resistance, but the inherently stochastic nature of crack initiation and propagation under repeated impacts introduces statistical uncertainty into these estimates. This uncertainty, arising from matrix heterogeneity, variations in fiber dispersion, and random crack paths, directly affects the reliability functions derived from the Weibull model. To quantify this effect and evaluate the robustness of the predicted impact reliability, two complementary strategies were adopted: (i) a frequentist confidence‑interval analysis using non‑parametric bootstrap resampling, and (ii) a Bayesian posterior analysis based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation. These methods provide interval bounds for the Weibull shape parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} and scale parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document}, as described in Eqs. Engineering implications of probabilistic impact resistance analysis

The combined Weibull, bootstrap, and Bayesian analyses enable translation of experimental impact performance into reliability-based material selection criteria for impact-resistant concrete design. The Weibull scale parameter (η), representing the characteristic impact resistance, was highest for coconut fiber (CF) in both M25 and M80 concretes at crack initiation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{ic}$$\end{document}) and failure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{fc}$$\end{document}) stages. With η values ranging approximately from 94 to 102 in M25 and 198–210 in M80, CF-reinforced concrete exhibits superior energy absorption capacity and enhanced resistance to progressive crack propagation. This performance indicates its suitability for structural elements subjected to severe or repeated impact loading, where serious damage tolerance and sustained post-cracking load-carrying capacity are essential. In contrast, bamboo fiber (BF) and jute fiber (JF) demonstrate comparatively higher Weibull shape parameters (β), particularly in M80 concrete (β ≈ 13–16), indicating reduced statistical dispersion in impact response. Higher β values correspond to more uniform crack initiation and failure behavior, which is advantageous in reliability-oriented structural design. Accordingly, these fibers may be preferred in applications such as industrial flooring systems, protective slabs, and precast impact-resistant panels, where performance consistency and controlled damage evolution are prioritized over maximum energy absorption. Kenaf fiber (KF) exhibits intermediate η and β values across both strength grades, reflecting a balanced combination of impact resistance and reliability. This behavior suggests its applicability in structural components subjected to moderate impact demands, where a compromise between toughness and predictability is desirable. Flax fiber (FF), although associated with relatively lower η values, demonstrates acceptable β in M25 concrete, indicating predictable but comparatively lower impact capacity suitable for non-critical impact-prone elements. Hemp fiber (HF) exhibits lower β values and wider bootstrap confidence intervals and Bayesian credible bounds, indicating greater variability in crack initiation and failure response. While HF inclusion improves impact resistance relative to plain concrete, the observed uncertainty suggests that higher design safety factors may be necessary when incorporating hemp-reinforced mixes in impact-sensitive structural applications.

Comparative analysis with previous studies

Previous studies as listed in Table 11, investigated natural fiber dosages ranging from 0.25–2% (coconut) and 0.5–3% (jute, sisal, coir, and flax), as well as hybrid systems, reporting enhanced impact resistance and energy absorption at optimal dosages but strength reduction at higher contents due to fiber agglomeration and increased porosity. The present study employed coconut fibers (0.5–1.5%), flax (0.1–0.5%), jute (0.15–0.55%), and bamboo, hemp, and kenaf (0.25–1.25%), demonstrating similar impact-enhancement trends, with optimum performance at specific dosages and degradation beyond them. However, the impact test methodologies varied across studies, limiting direct quantitative comparison of results and highlighting the need for standardized reliability-based evaluation. Impact resistance further exhibits inherent stochastic variability due to material heterogeneity, crack development, and fiber–matrix interactions, which deterministic approaches in earlier studies do not capture. To address this, the present work incorporated Weibull-based reliability modelling, bootstrap analysis, and Bayesian inference, enabling probabilistic characterization of impact behavior and parameter uncertainty (R2 = 0.88–0.96). The reliability results reveal stable performance at optimal dosages and quantified variability across specimens, providing deeper insight into impact resistance beyond mean strength values.
