Introduction

Non-invasive measurement of muscle and joint loading is currently not possible. Instrumented implants have been used to directly measure the loads transmitted by various joints, including the hip [1], knee [2], and spine [3]. While these studies have provided valuable information on the functional performance of reconstructed joints, the results may not accurately reflect the loading incurred by intact joints in daily life.

Anatomical imaging has been used to obtain an indicative measure of joint loading: cartilage strain. DeFrate and colleagues combined magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and three-dimensional (3D) surface models of cartilage and bone to estimate cartilage deformation at the knee [4]. Tibiofemoral cartilage thickness was measured in healthy young adults before and after a 20-minute walking exercise and the change in cartilage thickness then used to evaluate tibial and femoral cartilage strains. Mean compressive strains in tibial cartilage were significantly higher than those in femoral cartilage, implying that tibial cartilage through its predisposition to higher joint load may be more vulnerable to the development of knee osteoarthritis (OA). The main limitation of this method is that cartilage deformation is measured at two different time points, before and after an activity, thus cumulative cartilage strain rather than instantaneous strain is assessed. More direct measures of joint loading such as the time histories of surface contact pressures and internal tensile and shear strains are challenging to obtain using imaging techniques alone.

Computational modelling can be used to evaluate muscle and joint contact loading non-invasively. A model of the musculoskeletal system is comprised of a model of the skeleton, a model of muscle-tendon paths, and a model of muscle-tendon actuation. These models may be combined with biomechanical data obtained from human motion experiments to determine muscle and joint loading in vivo.

This mini-review examines some of the most recent developments in computational modelling of knee contact mechanics and suggests possible avenues for future improvements. We focus on in vivo musculoskeletal modelling studies and do not consider cadaver-based methods, which are also commonly used to describe and explain the effects of muscle forces on joint loading at the knee. The review draws on key publications in the last 10 years that highlight the application of rigid-body and finite-element modelling techniques to the study of knee contact mechanics. These publications were identified through a search in PubMed using the keywords “surrogate knee model”, “elastic foundation knee model”, and “finite element musculoskeletal knee model”.

Computational modelling of the knee

The knee is the most intensively studied joint in the body. A recent review by Erdemir et al. [5] noted that the number of publications on modelling and simulation of knee biomechanics has grown from a baseline of nearly zero in the 1970′s to over 1000 publications annually in 2018, with a total of ∼11,000 papers published in the last 50 years. The complexity of knee models varies greatly, from the most elementary one-degree-of-freedom (1-DOF) hinge with only flexion-extension represented [6] to the most detailed 12-DOF model where all possible movements of the femur, tibia, and patella are included [7,8]. Aside from differences in the anatomical complexity of these models, different methods are used to simulate joint contact mechanics. These approaches range from assuming that the articular surfaces are perfectly rigid to more realistic calculations of cartilage and meniscal deformation in the presence of muscle action.

Rigid-body (non-deformable) knee models

Full-body musculoskeletal models used to simulate the biomechanics of human gait usually represent the knee as a simple 1-DOF hinge [6]. This decision may be justified if the intended purpose of the model is to understand how individual muscles coordinate whole-body movement in activities like running and jumping [9]. If, however, the purpose is to understand how muscles induce strains in the knee ligaments, cartilage, and menisci, then a more detailed 3D model with deformable articular surfaces and soft tissues would be necessary [7,8,10] (see Fig. 1).

Elastic foundation knee models

Rigid-body musculoskeletal models often simulate deformable joint contact using the “bed of springs”method from elastic foundation theory [7,8,11,12]. The spatial arrangement of these springs is determined by the geometry of the contacting surface and each spring responds independently to the load applied to it. When modelling the healthy knee, bone and cartilage geometries are segmented from computed tomography (CT) and MR images and transformed into triangulated polygon meshes [7,8]. Articular contact load is determined by calculating the penetration depth of the vertices within the intersecting bodies' geometric meshes. CT and MRI can produce subject-specific geometries of the articular surfaces and soft tissues; however, the architectural and material properties of these structures are difficult to obtain in vivo, thus model parameters are usually based on data derived from anatomical dissections and mechanical testing performed on cadaver specimens. This issue poses a significant barrier to the application of computational modelling in clinical medicine, for example, in planning patient-specific joint reconstructive surgeries and designing exercise-based therapies for rehabilitation.

Modelling and simulation of deformable joint contact can be theoretically challenging and computationally burdensome. Lin et al. [11] found that it took 32 hours of CPU time (90 minutes per time step) to calculate muscle and knee contact forces simultaneously for one gait cycle using a rigid-body musculoskeletal model with deformable joint contact. Much of this expense is owed to the time taken to detect contact between the surfaces of articular cartilage at each time step during a simulation [12]. Surrogate contact modelling offers a more efficient approach as it eliminates repeated evaluations of surface contact detection by using a statistical model created from the response of a deformable-body-contact model [13]. Replacing a 12-DOF elastic foundation knee model with an equivalent surrogate contact model, Lin et al. [11] found that it took 42 minutes (2 minutes per time step) to calculate muscle and knee contact forces simultaneously for one gait cycle. The accuracy of the surrogate model can be enhanced by using a non-uniform regression-based sampling method, which facilitates the use of substantially larger volumes of training data compared to interpolation-based sampling methods [14]. Surrogate models greatly reduce the time taken to perform simulations of articular contact, yet there is a significant initial investment in computational time. Creating a single surrogate model of the knee can take up to 10 hours of CPU time [11], suggesting that this approach may not be feasible for clinical studies involving large populations.

Finite-element knee models

The finite element method has been used extensively to analyze the effects of mechanical loading on bone, muscle, and cartilage health [5]. Here, each structure is discretized into a series of interconnected elements and material properties are assigned to each element to describe its mechanical behaviour. Bone displacements, muscle forces, and articular contact forces are given as inputs to the model and soft tissue deformations are found using, for example, linear elastic theory. One of the main advantages of a finite element model is that each structure can be represented with much greater fidelity than is possible using a rigid-body musculoskeletal model. For example, cartilage and the menisci can be meshed with multiple layers, and depth-dependent variations in collagen fibril orientation may be included in a simulation of fully deformable joint contact. This level of detail allows contact areas, surface pressures, and internal stresses and strains in cartilage and meniscus to be evaluated, enabling more realistic analyses of the interactions between muscle forces and joint loading.

Finite element models of the knee have been used to determine ligament, cartilage, and meniscal strains during dynamic activity [15]. Because the time taken to calculate muscle forces within a finite-element framework can be prohibitive, muscle-force optimization is usually performed using a rigid-body musculoskeletal model and the results used as boundary conditions for a subsequent finite-element simulation of contact mechanics [10]. The main objection to this sequential modelling approach is that the calculations of muscle and joint loading are performed separately, ignoring the reality that estimates of muscle-tendon lengths and hence muscle forces are inextricably linked to cartilage deformation.

Hume et al. [16] calculated muscle and joint contact forces synchronously in a finite element model by simulating maximum isometric contractions of the knee muscles. They found that cartilage deformation had a significant effect on muscle moment arms and the calculated values of muscle forces, supporting the contention that a sequential modelling approach may not adequately capture the interactions between muscle action and joint loading. These authors later combined a finite element model of the lower limb with optimization theory to compute muscle and knee contact forces concurrently during gait [17]. Calculation of muscle and joint loading took ∼30 hours of CPU time even though the optimization problems was solved at only a few discrete time points during the stance phase.

Future advances in modelling knee biomechanics

Static and dynamic optimization techniques can be used to estimate muscle and joint contact forces in vivo. The static method solves a separate optimization problem at each instant during an activity whereas the dynamic method optimizes a cost function over an entire cycle of the activity. Lin et al. [18] combined dynamic optimization theory with a full-body musculoskeletal model to calculate muscle and knee contact forces during gait (Fig. 2). Dynamic optimization was used to drive the model toward in vivo measurements of joint motion and ground forces while muscle metabolic energy consumption (cost of transport) was minimized over one gait cycle. Variants of this “data-tracking” method have been used in conjunction with rigid-body musculoskeletal models to calculate muscle and knee contact loading during gait [7,8,12]. Only one study to our knowledge has performed muscle-driven forward simulations of movement using a finite-element musculoskeletal model of the lower limb with deformable joint contact [19]. Navacchia et al. [19] developed proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers to track in vivo measurements of joint motion while muscle forces were calculated using a recruitment strategy that favored the activation of muscles with larger volumes. Simulations of the single-stance phase of walking took ∼3.5 hours of CPU time, much less than the days and even weeks needed when finite-element modelling and optimization theory are used in tandem [20]. The results of this study demonstrate the feasibility of calculating joint motion, muscle forces, and knee contact mechanics using a single finite-element model; however, foot-ground contact was not included in the model and the heuristic approach used to calculate muscle forces may not be generalizable to other activities (e.g., running and jumping). Nonetheless, future efforts should build on these promising results by including a model of foot-ground contact and exploring more efficient techniques such as direct collocation to perform muscle-driven simulations of movement in the context of a finite-element model. Direct collocation discretizes the state variables (i.e., muscle activations, muscle lengths, and muscle forces) over time and allows optimization-based simulations to be performed more efficiently by eliminating the need to integrate the equations of motion [21].

Workflow used to perform data-tracking dynamic optimization simulations of whole-body movement. Muscle excitation signals are input to a rigid-body musculoskeletal model with a model of foot-ground contact and deformable knee contact. The muscle excitations are input to a muscle-tendon model to calculate tendon forces, which then operate on the full-body musculoskeletal model to produce time histories of joint motion—joint accelerations (q¨), joint velocities (q˙) and joint angular displacements (q)—and ground forces. The model-predicted joint motion and ground forces are compared to corresponding in vivo experimental data, and a data-tracking dynamic optimization algorithm is then used to drive the model toward the in vivo measurements of joint motion and ground forces. Model-predicted knee kinematics are input to the deformable knee contact model to calculate knee-ligament and articular contact forces. Note that muscle and knee contact forces are calculated concurrently in this scheme and that cartilage deformation is simulated using the bed of springs method from elastic foundation theory [7,8].

Predictive simulations of movement are ideally suited to exploring the effects of changes in musculoskeletal anatomy on muscle and joint function. Model simulations that predict functional biomechanics may find powerful applications in orthopedics and sports medicine. One important advantage of predictive modelling is that a relatively unambiguous cost function may be formulated to model the goal of the activity, for example, minimize metabolic energy consumption or maximize running speed. In contrast, the data-tracking approach must hypothesize a cost function even when the goal of the activity may be ambiguous, as is the case for example when humans walk at speeds slower and faster than the preferred (self-selected) speed. Lin et al. [11] observed differences in knee contact forces when using two different effort-minimization cost functions. This discrepancy most likely arose from different muscle recruitment strategies chosen by the optimization algorithm and manifested as different levels of co-contraction used by the knee muscles included in the model. A few studies have combined rigid-body musculoskeletal modelling with dynamic optimization theory to perform predictive simulations of movement but none to our knowledge has combined a predictive simulation approach with finite-element modelling. Using a full-body musculoskeletal model, Lin and Pandy [22] performed predictive simulations of maximum-speed sprinting to study the effects of changes in muscle architecture on sprint performance. Predictive simulations of movement are rare because they are computationally more expensive than the conventional data-tracking approach illustrated in Fig. 2. However, the computational algorithms needed to solve these problems are now available and future efforts ought to explore the feasibility of coupling finite element modelling with predictive simulations to investigate how changes in musculoskeletal anatomy affect knee contact mechanics.

Conclusions

Simulations of knee contact mechanics range from an assumption of perfectly rigid articular surfaces to more realistic simulations of cartilage and meniscal deformation. Muscle and joint contact forces can be calculated synchronously at a reasonable computational cost using rigid-body elastic foundation models, but analogous simulations with deformable finite-element models can be unwieldly. Future work should explore the use of efficient numerical methods such as direct collocation for calculating muscle and joint contact forces simultaneously in the framework of a finite element musculoskeletal model. Finite-element modelling coupled with dynamic optimization theory will enable predictive simulations of movements so that the effects of changes in musculoskeletal anatomy on knee contact mechanics can be studied more systematically.
