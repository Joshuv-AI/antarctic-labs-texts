Assessing the scope and reporting quality of published health economic evaluation studies in Jordan: a systematic literature review
Authors: Lama Sheikha, Rasha M Arabyat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJordan faces growing healthcare challenges driven by limited resources, rising healthcare expenditures, and an increasing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Economic evaluations are essential for informing resource allocation and supporting health technology assessment (HTA). To date, however, no systematic review has summarized the scope and reporting quality of published full health economic evaluation studies (HEEs) in Jordan.ObjectivesThis study aimed to systematically identify, describe, and assess the reporting quality of published full HEES conducted in Jordan using PRISMA.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar databases was conducted up to November 2025. Eligible studies were full economic evaluation studies (cost-minimization, cost-effectiveness, cost-utility, or cost–benefit analyses) performed in Jordan and published in peer-reviewed journals. Data were extracted on study characteristics, economic analytical details, and outcomes. Adherence to reporting guidelines was appraised using CHEERS 2022 checklist.ResultsOut of 546 records screened, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria. The earliest full HEEs appeared in 2014, with research activity increasing after 2020, coinciding with the governmental efforts to activate the role of economic evaluations at national institutions. Most studies were cost-effectiveness analyses (n = 8, 53.3%), followed by cost–utility (n = 4, 26.7%) and cost–benefit analyses (n = 3, 20%). Most studies utilized a model-based design, predominantly adopting a healthcare system or payer perspective and focusing on direct medical costs. Non-communicable diseases, particularly neoplasms, were the most frequently studied disease areas. The median CHEERS score was 22.5 (IQR: 21 to 23) out of 28, indicating fair reporting quality, with frequent gaps in specifying comparators in study titles, analysis plans, and model validation.ConclusionsThe volume of health economics evaluations in Jordan is small but rapidly evolving showing overall fair reporting quality sustained by a high standard of classical reporting. Identified gaps in some reporting items reflect slow integration of new requirements rather than core methodological flaws. The current published research is structurally fragmented, biased toward pharmaceuticals, lacks standardized cost-effectiveness thresholds and localized empirical data. To effectively support Jordan’s transition toward Universal Health Coverage, the evolving national HTA framework must shift from a reactive to proactive institutionalized mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-026-00802-3.Keywords: Systematic Literature Review, Health Economic Evaluation, PRISMA, Jordan, Cost-Effectiveness Analysis, Cost–Benefit Analysis, Cost Utility Analysis

Introduction

The global demand for healthcare services is escalating, driven by rapid technological innovation, rising challenges from an aging population, and rising patient awareness and expectations. These expanding needs outpace the limited available funding. Consequently, optimizing resource allocation is no longer optional; it has become a critical global necessity.

To address this need, Health Technology Assessment (HTA) and health economic evaluations have emerged as evidence-based tools, that balance clinical efficacy and economic value to determine whether a new health technology offers a good value for money at an acceptable cost to the health system [1]. This is achieved by comparative analysis of the costs and outcomes of an intervention against its alternatives [2]. Using these economic evaluations to inform a transparent decision making can protect healthcare budgets, ensure that the most beneficial therapies are prioritized, and guide sustainable reimbursement policies where any inappropriate implementation of decisions can lead to substantial societal and financial costs. While developed nations recognized this value decades ago since 1970 s, the developing countries still face difficulties in creating well established systems that translate the economic evidence into an effective policy [3]. More importantly, global economic models cannot simply be transferred into Lower Middle-Income Countries (LMIC) settings as they rely on more stable and robust local data that do not align with the realities of developing countries. Consequently, there is an urgent need for localized economic evaluations that reflect local resource constraints, distinct epidemiological patterns, and unique systemic hurdles.

Jordan provides an example of a unique healthcare system that uniquely balances a reputation as a leading regional hub for advanced oncology and tertiary care [4] with domestic infrastructure that operates under severe fiscal strains. Historically, Jordan has absorbed successive waves of geopolitical shocks, resulting in long term hosting of massive refugee populations. According to World Bank data, approximately 66% of the country's total population growth since 2000 occurred after 2011, following the regional refugee influxes. This rapid demographic expansion has radically shifted national health strategies and exposed critical infrastructure gaps. By 2024, the total population reached 11.5 million [5]. Simultaneously, economic growth slowed considerably, with GDP growth declining from 8.2% in 2007 to 1.8% in 2019, and remaining low at 2.5% in 2024 [5]. These demographic pressures are compounded by a highly fragmented healthcare system that is divided between the Ministry of Health (MOH), Royal Medical Services (RMS), university hospitals, and private sector. As a LMIC, Jordan’s health expenditures challenges are clearly reflected in the 2022 National Health Accounts (NHA) report [6], from which all subsequent metrics are derived; health expenditure accounts for 7.74% of GDP with pharmaceuticals representing a major cost driver, accounting for 2.3% of GDP, with a per capita pharmaceutical expenditure of 70.36%. Sources of healthcare financing is dominated by the private sector (50.6%), followed by the public sector (44.1%) and donor contributions (5.3%). Out-of-pocket (OOP) spending is considered high, comprising 35.9% of health expenditures in 2022.

In a resource-scarce economy, simply expanding the national healthcare budget is not feasible. Consequently, Jordan has committed to restructuring its health system step by step to guide cost-effective resource allocation. The integration of health economic evaluations (HEEs) can be traced through key policy milestones, starting formally in 2012 when the MOH mandated economic evaluations as a prerequisite for introducing new drugs into the Jordanian pharmaceutical market [7]. To support this, the Jordan Food and Drug Administration (JFDA) required the inclusion of two pharmacoeconomists within its pricing committee according the “Drug and Pharmacy Law No 12 of 2013” to critically appraise the submitted economic evidence where needed [8], though both initiatives yielded limited policy impact due to the absence of official and standardized evaluation guidelines [7]. Institutional efforts soon shifted to the facility level in 2013 when the King Hussein Cancer Center (KHCC) established its Drug Policy and Technology Assessment Unit, which successfully expanded into a specialized program in 2016 [9]. Recognizing the need for unified governance, a national HTA committee was formed in 2018 to develop a comprehensive implementation roadmap and national HTA guidelines [7]. In late 2019 the Jordanian RMS established an independent HTA department as well [7].

While these coordinated governance movements affected the number of published health economic evaluations in Jordan over the last decade, a critical vulnerability remains; little is systematically known about their scope or reporting quality. Poorly designed HEEs pose a significant policy risk, as they can misguide policymakers within an already fiscally constrained system.

To the best of our knowledge, no study has systematically evaluated the domestic landscape of health economic evaluations in Jordan. This systematic review addresses this critical gap by identifying, describing, and critically appraising full HEEs conducted up to 2025. Specifically, it maps trends in publication volume, characterizes variations in willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds, and evaluates overall reporting quality to provide a rigorous roadmap for future research in the Kingdom.

Methods

This systematic review adhered to established reporting guidelines, using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10] and the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) to guide the analysis and synthesis of health economic data [11].

Systematic search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in October 2025 using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar to identify published HEEs conducted in Jordan. The search strategy was conceptually built on two pillars: (1) Full Health Economic Evaluation (HEE) and (2) Jordan. We combined subject headings (Mesh) and free text terms (e.g., pharmacoeconomic, economic model, and health econom*) with various Boolean operators and truncations to refine the search without any restriction on year of publication. We also manually reviewed the reference lists of the included articles to identify additional relevant publications and this process continued until no new articles were found. Full details regarding the search strings used in PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Eligibility criteria

We included only original, full HEEs published in peer-reviewed journals and available in English or Arabic. Inclusion required the study to be geographically relevant, meaning it was conducted in Jordan or utilized Jordan-specific data. We strictly excluded any paper that did not meet the full economic evaluation criteria, such as purely clinical studies lacking an economic component, descriptive cost studies (like cost-of-illness or cost-savings analyses), or simplified Cost-Minimization Analyses (CMAs) lacking key methodological elements. Furthermore, non-original research (e.g., conference proceedings, letters), grey literature (e.g., MOH reports, theses), and studies without local relevance (e.g., multiple-country comparisons or those authored exclusively by non-Jordanian affiliates) were excluded. We contacted corresponding authors to obtain initially unavailable full-text articles.

Screening and data extraction

The study selection and data extraction were performed by two independent reviewers.

Selection of articles proceeded in two stages: initially by screening titles and abstracts, followed by a detailed evaluation of potentially eligible full-text articles against all pre-defined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements regarding inclusion, exclusion, or full-text availability were resolved through consensus between the two reviewers.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using a standardized form adapted from a previous systematic review by Gavaza et al. [12]. The form was structured into two main sections. The first captured general study information, including author and year of publication, journal metrics, authors’ affiliations, and the type of health technology evaluated (encompassing pharmaceuticals, vaccines, medical devices, procedures, public health programs, and health services or system interventions). The second section focused on methodological and analytical details of the economic evaluation, such as the economic evaluation type, perspective, cost categories, discounting, primary outcomes, study design, funding source, and key cost-effectiveness conclusions (e.g., the specific economic decision rules used to establish cost effectiveness). The form was piloted on a subset of included studies to ensure clarity and consistency before full data extraction. In cases where a study had published corrections, the updated version was used for both data extraction and quality assessment. The complete data extraction form is available in Supplementary Table 1.

Reporting quality and transparency

The reporting transparency and completeness of the identified full economic evaluations was assessed using (CHEERS) 2022 checklist [11]. Developed by ISPOR Task Force, the checklist comprises 28 items organized across seven domains designed to ensure optimal study reporting [11]. Adherence was quantified using a scoring system adapted from Alefan et al. [13]: one point was awarded for complete reporting, 0.5 points for partially complete, and zero for incomplete items. A total score was calculated out of 28 points, and studies were subsequently classified into categories of reporting completeness: acceptable (≥ 23), fair (16–22), or unacceptable (≤ 15) [13]. This approach was adopted to allow for a structured, quantitative appraisal of reporting quality that will reflect ultimately on clarity, quality and credibility of research outcomes with minimized reporting bias [14].

Results

Study selection

The database search initially yielded 546 records. After removing duplicates, 533 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 40 were retrieved for full-text review. A total of 25 articles were excluded for the following reasons: not a full economic evaluation (n = 6), lack of geographic relevance (n = 6), out of scope or irrelevant (n = 5), no full text available (n = 4), non-peer-reviewed or non-original research (n = 2), or duplicate (n = 2). Ultimately, 15 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final review. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

General characteristics of included studies

The earliest full economic evaluation identified in this review was published in 2014 [15], followed by a relatively sparse publication volume between 2014 and 2018, averaging just a single publication per active year. Research activity increased thereafter, with a significant majority of the included studies (n = 12, 80%) published after 2020, with five studies were published in 2025 alone [16–20], representing one-third (33.3%) of all included publications. The chronological trend of the included studies illustrated in Fig. 2.

Chronological distribution and publication volume of full economic evaluations in Jordan (2014–2025)

All primary authors have rooted expertise in the health sciences. Most (n = 11; 73.3%) held backgrounds in pharmacy or clinical pharmacy [16–18, 20–27], followed by medicine (n = 3, 20%) [15, 28, 29], and nutrition (n = 1, 6.7%) [19]. First authors were predominantly affiliated with academic institutions (n = 12; 80%), while a smaller proportion were linked to healthcare institutions (n = 3; 20%) [17, 24, 25]. Co-authorship also noted to include contributions from industry and professional consultancy (n = 3; 20%) [17, 20, 29] and government (n = 1, 6.7%) [17]. International collaboration was evident in over half of the studies (n = 8; 53.3%), most frequently involving co-authors from the United States (n = 3; 20%) [21, 24, 29] alongside sporadic contributions from Australia [15], Egypt [17], Malaysia [19], Hungary [17], KSA [20] and Qatar [22].

Most identified studies were published in Health and Clinical Sciences journals (n = 6; 40%), followed by Pharmaceutical and Chemical Sciences (n = 5; 33.3%) and Economics, Finance, and Policy Modeling journals (n = 4; 26.7%). Journals rank was generally high: 12 studies (80%) appeared in Q1 or Q2 journals [16, 18, 19, 21–29], two in a Q3 journal (13.3%) [15, 20], none in Q4, and one lacked ranking information [17] as it was not listed on the SCImago Journal Rank (SJR) website [30].

The studies covered a range of health technologies and interventions. Nearly half of the studies (n = 8; 53.3%) focused on pharmaceuticals [15, 18, 20, 21, 24–27], three (20%) evaluated healthcare system interventions/services, particularly pharmacist-led programs [16, 22, 23], and the remaining studies examined vaccines (n = 2; 13.3%) [19, 29] and clinical procedures (n = 2; 13.3%) [17, 28].

Economic characteristics of the included papers

The economic data and analysis were characterized by a predominance cost-effectiveness analyses (CEA) (n = 8, 53.3%) [15, 18–21, 23, 26, 27], followed by cost–utility analyses (CUA) (n = 4, 26.7%) [24, 25, 28, 29] and cost–benefit analyses (CBA) (n = 3, 20%) [16, 17, 22]. Most evaluations (n = 11; 73.3%) were model-based, the majority used standard decision analytic frameworks, with six studies employed Markov models (54.5%) [18, 20, 23–26] and two employed decision tree models (18.2%) [19, 27]. Notably, the choice of modeling technique aligned with the clinical nature of the disease pathway whether it is chronic or recurrent versus acute in its time horizon. The remaining three studies utilized specialized modeling tailored to their specific research objective: one applied an age-structured epidemiologic dynamic model [29] to capture the disease spread at the population level depending on age, another used an ex-ante financial benefit–cost analysis model [17] to evaluate long term financial returns on investment, and one constructed a partitioned survival model [21] to simulate patient transitions between health states in critical care setting. The other 4 remaining studies out of 15 were non-model based. These consist of one trial-based study in the form of randomized controlled trial (RCT) (6.7%) [22], alongside two retrospective cohort studies (13.3%) [15, 28] and one prospective interventional study (6.7%) [16].

Nearly all studies (n = 14; 93.3%) reported their perspective, except one evaluation didn’t state it explicitly [28]. The most frequently adopted types were the healthcare system perspective (n = 5, 33.3%) [15, 16, 21, 22, 24] and the payer perspective (n = 5, 33.3%) [18, 20, 25–27], collectively accounting for two-thirds of the reviewed literature. Broader economic perspectives were less frequent, with only one study adopting a societal perspective (6.7%) [29] and another utilizing a mixed societal and payer approach (6.7%) [17]. Provider perspective was used in two studies (13.3%) [19, 23].

There was a notable consistency between the costs included in the evaluations and their declared perspective; all the twelve studies that used a healthcare system or public payer perspective (mainly for the MOH; reflecting its dual role as both healthcare provider and payer within the Jordanian setting) strictly confined their scope to direct medical costs. For the study from a societal perspective, both direct medical and indirect costs were captured. While the study adopting a dual societal and healthcare system viewpoint accounted for direct medical (incurred by the healthcare facility and the patient) and non-medical costs (incurred by the international patients and their traveling companions), omitting indirect costs.

The parameters sourcing strategies revealed distinct patterns between effectiveness and cost data types. For clinical effectiveness inputs, the majority of studies (n = 7, 46.7%) relied on secondary data extracted from published literature and RCTs [18, 20, 24–27, 29], whereas four studies (26.7%) used primary patient level medical records and interviews [15, 16, 21, 22]. The remaining utilized hybrid sources [17, 19, 23, 28]. Conversely, sourcing cost data was more fragmented. Only 2 studies (13.3%) relied entirely on secondary national price tariffs [18, 26], and 4 relied strictly on primary billing databases (26.7%) [16, 21, 22, 28]. The majority of evaluations (n = 9, 60%) combined hybrid sources, among these, 4 studies (44.4%) leveraged primary and secondary data [15, 20, 27, 29], while five (55.6%) integrated expert opinions to map Jordanian resource utilization [17, 19, 23–25].

The decision rules used to establish cost-effectiveness varied across the included studies. Standard willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds were applied as the following; three studies relied on macroeconomic benchmarks using the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria of one to three times the national Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita [20, 21, 29], while two oncology-focused evaluations (13.3%) utilized an institution-specific threshold calibrated to the King Hussein Cancer Center (KHCC) budget ($42,000–$56,000 per QALY) [24, 25]. Furthermore, three studies (20%) incorporated an opportunity-cost framework (the Woods et al. model [31]) [23], with two applying it as conservative part of a dual-threshold framework against a generous (1–3 × GDP) threshold [18, 26]. The remaining evaluations bypassed WTP thresholds entirely: three (20%) established cost-effectiveness based on clinical and cost dominance rules [15, 19, 27], three (20%) utilized monetary Cost–Benefit Analysis metrics (Net Benefit or Benefit-to-Cost Ratio) [16, 17, 22], and one paper (6.7%) did not explicitly define a formal economic decision framework [28].

Reporting quality assessment of the included studies

The completeness of reporting among the included studies, assessed using the CHEERS 2022 checklist, yielded a median CHEERS score of 22.5 (IQR: 21 to 23) out of 28, with scores ranging from 17 to 24. Overall, 13 studies (86.7%) scored 21 or higher across the entire period (2014–2025). No clear upward trend observed in CHEERS scores corresponding directly to the year of publication, but two extremes of the data partially support the trend with the lowest score [17] was recorded in an older study from 2015. And the highest score [24] was recorded in a newer study from 2025.

Only one of the 15 studies explicitly reported adherence to the CHEERS checklist [19]. Notable reporting gaps were identified across several items. For the title, which should specify both the type of economic evaluation and the comparator, 7 studies (46.7%) omitted the comparator, resulting in partial scores (0.5). Regarding the health economic analysis plan (HEAP) item, only the trial-based study with a registered protocol reported this item [22]. Items characterizing heterogeneity, distributional effects, and engagement with patients or other stakeholders were not addressed in most of the studies, even among studies claiming adherence to CHEERS. Furthermore, five studies (33.3%) received partial scores (0.5) for “analytics and assumptions,” mainly due to a lack of model validation.

Sensitivity analysis (SA) was conducted in 14 studies (93.3%). Of these, nine (60%) applied both deterministic SA (DSA) and probabilistic SA (PSA) [16–18, 22–26, 29], while three (20%) used DSA only [15, 19, 27], and two (13.3%) used PSA alone [20, 21]. A time horizon longer than one year was used in nine studies (all but one of these applied discounting with rates typically ranging from 3% to 4.75%) [17, 18, 20, 23–26, 29]. One study overall did not report using a discount rate [28]. Regarding conflict of interest, 13 studies (86.7%) explicitly declared no conflicts, one disclosed a conflict (6.7%) [29], and one failed to provide a statement (6.7%) [15].

Discussion

The relatively low volume of identified studies is consistent with findings from comparable countries, both regionally, as noted in Saudi Arabia [32, 33], and globally among countries within the lower middle income (LMIC) group, including Bangladesh [34], Vietnam[35] and Nigeria [36].

The primary finding of this review is a notable lack of standardization across local economic evaluations, characterized by highly heterogenous outcome measures that hinder cross-study comparisons. Cost-Effectiveness Analyses (CEAs) are the most common studies used, relying on intermediate, disease-specific metrics; while clinically relevant, these cannot be compared across different therapeutic areas. Conversely, Cost-Utility Analyses (CUAs) are underutilized, reflecting an underrepresentation of generic health metrics like Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs). However, the recent development of a Jordan-specific EQ-5D-3L value set provides a vital tool to incorporate localized utility estimates into future HTA submissions [37]. Similarly Cost–Benefit Analyses (CBAs) are limited due to methodological constraints and a lack of local willingness-to-pay thresholds [8]. This non-standardization creates a significant policy barrier: when attempting to prioritize resource allocation across diverse interventions, policymakers cannot directly compare different Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) outcome measures. Furthermore, existing evaluations lack adopting a broader societal perspective, which ultimately underrepresents the true socioeconomic value of healthcare interventions. It is worth mentioning though that international economic evaluation guidelines are divided on study perspectives, with some mandating a broad societal lens and others focusing strictly on institutional payer expenditures [38]. We believe that Jordan's unique position must be considered. Given the heavily burdened nature of the Jordanian healthcare system and its competing structural priorities, establishing a healthcare system perspective as an initial mandatory baseline is a pragmatic first step, then broadening this scope to a societal lens can be introduced as institutional guidelines solidify and local economic expertise expands.

Some key patterns were revealed through our analysis. First, there was a dominant use of decision analytic models that rely on clinical efficacy parameters extrapolated from multinational RCTs conducted in high-income settings. While this reliance on secondary or mixed data sources stems from a lack of local evidence, it introduces profound generalizability challenges. Failing to account for unique Jordanian clinical realities, such as biologic variations, adherence behaviors, and local resource constraints, can risk overestimating real-world effectiveness and underestimating budget impacts [38]. More importantly, a lack of systematic pooling introduces heterogeneity and parameter uncertainty, compromising external validity [1]. On the other hand, cost data sourcing is highly fragmented. This inconsistency mirrors a healthcare system lacking centralized, transparent cost-accounting databases, forcing researchers to hybridize data collection and use expert opinions to map out resource utilization introducing subjectivity and recall bias into the models.

Second, the current body of research demonstrates a disproportionate focus on pharmaceuticals, with no studies addressing medical devices, diagnostics, or preventive interventions. While this emphasis may be partially justified given that pharmaceutical expenditure accounts for approximately 29.8% of total health expenditure [6], highlighting the need for greater health economic evaluation (HEE) in this area, the imbalance nevertheless suggests that local health economics research is primarily driven by regulatory and market considerations rather than population health needs. Current policy frameworks appear to have effectively institutionalized economic evaluations for pharmaceuticals, while other important healthcare sectors remain largely overlooked, leaving critical public health domains unexamined. This misalignment disconnects the academic output and systemic needs, even though the included studies do align with national burdens by primarily targeting non-communicable diseases (NCDs), particularly neoplasms, which account for over 76% of total deaths in Jordan [39]. However, as Jordan transitions toward Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and strives to fulfill the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), resource prioritization must extend beyond pharmaceuticals. To bridge this gap, Jordan's evolving national HTA framework must shift from a reactive regulatory mechanism into a proactive strategic body, and governance bodies should establish institutional funding mechanisms and explicit appraisal pathways that incentivize economic research into preventative medicine, diagnostics, and overlooked public health sectors [39, 40].

Furthermore, most studies focused exclusively on direct medical costs, a finding consistent with the limited availability of data on indirect costs (e.g., absenteeism, presenteeism, productivity loss and premature mortality) in Jordan. While evaluating indirect costs is increasingly important in HTA, this limitation is acknowledged as a global issue due to the subjective and exhaustive methods often required for robust estimation [41]. The local literature, however, shows robust adherence to uncertainty reporting requirements. The vast majority of included studies systematically executed both Deterministic (DSA) and Probabilistic Sensitivity Analyses (PSA), with some including scenario modeling as part of DSA. This high rate of dual-sensitivity analysis demonstrates a strong methodological foundation for managing parameter uncertainty, even though the lack of standardized metrics and broad perspectives slightly restricts the global interpretability of the pooled evidence base.

How cost-effectiveness is defined in Jordan is considered a critical challenge. Evaluations alternate arbitrarily between the traditional WHO macro-level criteria (utilizing 1–3 times the per capita GDP), opportunity cost thresholds, and absolute financial metrics like positive Net Benefit (NB > 0). Varied threshold benchmarks create profound decision ambiguity for Jordanian policymakers. Because the chosen threshold is considered to determine the value for money, using different baselines means similar interventions can yield completely opposing policy recommendations [42]. While GDP-based benchmarks remain common in low- and middle-income settings, Marseille et al. [43] argue that these automated, "one-size-fits-all" thresholds serve as blunt decision-making tools. By setting an artificially low bar for value, GDP metrics can make inefficient interventions appear cost-effective while obscuring true opportunity costs and actual population’s WTP, and inadequately appraise the affordability. This can risk distorting priority setting in health systems like Jordan. To address this, Marseille et al. [43] suggest alternatives such as "league tables" that rank interventions by their ICERs until the budget is exhausted (though this can disadvantage certain life-saving treatments) or using supply-side opportunity cost thresholds to capture real-world financial friction. Ultimately, local health authorities must be encouraged to develop context-specific national thresholds that integrate budget impact analyses alongside standard cost-effectiveness data to guide future Jordanian HTA recommendations [40].

The included studies demonstrated robust fair reporting quality across the entire timeline. It was observed that most studies maintained reasonably high standard regardless of the publication year, suggesting that quality is more dependent on the individual research team's adherence to best practices rather than chronological evolution of the field. Our assessment identified four specific, commonly missed items that possibly decreased the total scores: Health Economic Analysis Plan (Item 4), Distributional Effects (Item 19), Patient Engagement (Item 21), and the Effect of Engagement (Item 25). When these items were excluded from the total score, the adjusted median CHEERS score (22.25/24) (IQR: 21.0 to 22.75) closely mirrors the original median score (22.5/28). This indicates that the underlying reporting quality is robust and highly standardized, with an overall compliance rate exceeding 80%, and reveals that the reporting deficits in Jordanian literature are not scattered or random, but rather systemic. A near-identical median and IQR suggests that HEE in Jordan generally adhere strictly to basic reporting structures. Addressing these four specific systemic gaps represents a clear, actionable target for improving Jordan's HTA output to meet absolute gold-standard international transparency requirements. Nevertheless, key areas for methodological advancement are still required, inadequate reporting of Analytics and Assumptions (Item 17) and Heterogeneity (Item 18), signal a need for greater transparency and detail regarding model validation in future studies.

To align Jordanian HEEs with global best practices, research must proactively address the current neglect of equity issues, distributional effects, and patient engagement. We encourage researchers to adopt these elements to enhance the ethical and societal relevance of their evaluations. To facilitate this advancement and integrate real-world data, research partnerships require expanding beyond the academic contribution to include private sector and governmental healthcare professionals.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, despite a comprehensive and systematic search strategy, some relevant published studies may have been unintentionally missed or excluded. Second, our search was limited to peer-reviewed journal articles, potentially excluding other relevant sources such as government reports or pharmaceutical company publications or even not published articles yet. Third, the use of a scoring system to assess study reporting quality may introduce subjectivity, as different evaluators might assign slightly different scores. We minimized this risk by achieving consensus between the two independent reviewers. Finally, due to heterogeneity across the included studies with respect to study perspectives, designs, time horizons and outcomes we were unable to use inferential analytical approaches or explore correlations. Consequently, our synthesis was limited to a descriptive narrative as this was a methodologically rigorous option to pool such diverse data.

Conclusion

Economic evaluations in Jordan show a promising trajectory, despite the relatively small number of published studies. Overall, the content, quality and standard of reporting are reasonably well established. On average, studies demonstrated fair completeness in reporting, with most deficiencies attributable to newly added items in the 2022 CHEERS checklist rather than major methodological flaws. Strengthening the local technical capacity in health economics and establishing clear national guidelines and WTP threshold may further increase the number of high-quality economic evaluations, supporting more informed resource allocation and improving healthcare decision-making in the region.
