Integration of HMW-GS and retrotransposon marker LTR14 for selection of wheat genotypes combining baking quality and resistance to take-all disease
Authors: Mozhgan Gholizadeh Vazvani, Roohallah Saberi Riseh, Hossein Dashti, Mohammad Jaffaraghaei, Evelin Loit-Harro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImproving bread wheat genotypes that combine durable resistance to take-all disease with high yield and favorable baking quality is an important objective in wheat breeding programs.ResultsIn this study, 97 bread wheat genotypes were selected from an initial collection of 1000 accessions based on previous phenotypic evaluations to ensure broad agronomic and quality-related diversity. In addition, three control cultivars (Roshan, Kavir, and Sardari) were included in the evaluation. These genotypes and control cultivars were evaluated for high molecular weight glutenin subunit (HMW-GS) composition, agronomic traits, disease severity (DS), and baking quality (BQ). SDS-PAGE analysis identified 11 HMW-GS, among which 5 + 10 (Glu-D1), 2* (Glu-A1), and 17 + 18 (Glu-B1) were associated with superior baking quality. The retrotransposon-based marker LTR14, previously associated with reduced disease severity, was excised, purified, and sequenced (NCBI GenBank accession number PX660489.1). Sequence analysis revealed partial similarity to γ-gliadin- and storage protein activator-related sequences. Correlation analysis showed a significant negative association between LTR14 and DS (r = –0.405, p < 0.05) and a positive association with lignin content (r = 0.310, p < 0.05). Heatmap clustering divided the genotypes into six groups, among which clusters 3 and 6 exhibited favorable combinations of low disease severity, high baking quality, desirable yield components, and the presence of LTR14 and favorable HMW-GS alleles. Principal component analysis explained 76.2% of the total variation, with PC2 positively associated with baking quality and grain yield traits and negatively associated with disease severity. Using the TOPSIS-based similarity index to the best conditions (SIB ≥ 0.75), nine genotypes were identified as promising candidates with favorable combinations of disease resistance, baking quality, and agronomic performance.ConclusionsThe results provide preliminary sequence-based characterization of the LTR14 retrotransposon-derived marker associated with reduced disease severity and suggest possible relationships with storage protein-related genomic regions. The integrative multi-trait framework applied in this study provides a preliminary strategy for identifying promising wheat genotypes for marker-assisted breeding programs targeting disease resistance, grain quality, and agronomic performance. However, it is important to emphasize that the study was conducted under a single environmental condition and sequence characterization was based on only one resistant genotype. Therefore, the observed associations must be considered preliminary, and substantial further multi-environment validation and broader sequencing analyses are essential to confirm the stability and broader applicability of these findings before any practical recommendations can be made.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09527-1.

Background

Wheat breeding programs worldwide have historically prioritized increasing grain yield to meet the growing demand for food security. Grain quality has often been regarded as a secondary objective in conventional wheat improvement strategies. However, in recent years, grain quality has gained substantial attention due to increased market demands and greater emphasis on end-use performance. The definition of wheat quality varies among stakeholders across the wheat value chain, including farmers, millers, bakers, processors, and consumers. From an agronomic standpoint, an ideal wheat cultivar is characterized by high yield potential combined with stable resistance to diseases and pests, as well as tolerance to abiotic stresses [1]. The development of wheat cultivars combining multiple disease resistance with superior grain quality represents a major objective of modern breeding programs. Breeders aim to develop elite genotypes that integrate durable resistance to diverse pathogens, high end-use quality, and resilience to environmental stresses within a single genetic background. Marker-assisted selection (MAS) has become an efficient and strategic tool for the simultaneous improvement of multiple traits [2]. The pyramiding of favorable alleles associated with superior agronomic and physiological performance—facilitated by advanced genotyping platforms and precise phenotyping approaches—can significantly accelerate genetic gain for yield. Importantly, disease resistance is agronomically valuable only when it is combined with high grain yield within the same genotype, ensuring both productivity and stability under disease pressure [3].

In modern plant breeding, selecting genotypes with superior multi-trait profiles requires integrative approaches to analyze complex datasets [4]. Multivariate statistical methods such as principal component analysis (PCA), heatmap clustering, and multi-criteria decision-making tools like the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) have emerged as powerful tools to facilitate genotype ranking [5–9]. These methods allow simultaneous consideration of traits related to quality, yield, and resistance, providing breeders with a more holistic framework for selection compared to single-trait approaches. PCA enables dimensionality reduction and highlights patterns of variation among traits, facilitating the identification of genotypes with superior performance across multiple agronomic and quality-related traits [10, 11]. These multivariate approaches are now widely used as indirect but effective selection tools in molecular breeding and have proven particularly useful when working with limited phenotypic replication or complex trait interactions [12]. In recent years, retrotransposon-based molecular markers, particularly iPBS (Inter-Primer Binding Site) markers, have played a significant role in assessing genetic diversity, population structure, and genetic relationships among plant genotypes. In a study conducted on pumpkin genotypes (Lagenaria siceraria), iPBS markers revealed varying levels of genetic diversity among populations by generating highly polymorphic profiles and grouping genotypes into distinct clusters. This provided valuable information for plant breeding programs and genetic resource management. Furthermore, population structure analysis using these markers can facilitate the selection of suitable parents for the improvement of desirable traits. iPBS markers are considered a powerful and reliable tool for germplasm evaluation in diverse plant species due to their wide distribution across the genome and their ability to generate reproducible and distinguishable banding patterns [13–15].

Wheat grain proteins are classified into four groups based on solubility. Albumins and globulins are soluble in water and saline solutions and are metabolically active. In contrast, the other two groups, which include glutenins (glutelins) and gliadins (prolamins), are insoluble and storage in nature [16, 17]. In wheat, glutenins and gliadins are categorized under the glutelin and prolamin fractions, respectively [16].

Wheat glutenins are classified based on molecular weight into high molecular weight glutenin subunits (HMW-GS) and low molecular weight glutenin subunits (LMW-GS). The HMW-GS are encoded by the Glu-1 loci located on the long arms of the homoeologous group 1 chromosomes, with each locus comprising two genes encoding the X- and Y-type subunits [18]. The X- and Y-type genes encode subunits that migrate slowly and more rapidly, respectively, on SDS-PAGE [19, 20]. Based on their N-terminal sequences, LMW subunits are further categorized into three types: LMW-m, LMW-s, and LMW-i, beginning with methionine, serine, and isoleucine residues, respectively. The allelic composition of HMW-GS in a cultivar or genotype has a significant impact on end-use quality. Gliadins are small monomeric proteins comprising alpha, beta, gamma, and omega subgroups, collectively constituting about 50% of the prolamin fraction [21]. The distribution of both HMW-GS and LMW-GS is strongly associated with baking quality. Although HMW-GS constitute only about 10% of the total glutenin fraction in wheat grain protein, and LMW-GS represent a larger portion of the glutenin fraction, HMW-GS nonetheless exert a greater influence on bread-making quality [22].

A correlation analysis was performed between Glu-A1 and Glu-B1 alleles and 24 agronomic traits, including coleoptile length, protein content, autumn growth habit, overwintering, spring re-growth, preharvest sprouting, plant height, lodging, hectoliter weight, heading, leaf area index, Septoria leaf blotch, angle of top leaves, common bunt, color of leaves, take-all, visual soil cover, yield in take-all nursery, visual thriftiness of plants, eyespot, yield, 1000 kernel weight, sedimentation value, and starch content. A strong positive correlation was found between Glu-B1 alleles and winter hardiness (overwintering), and a moderate correlation was observed between Glu-A1 alleles and resistance to powdery mildew [23]. Genotypes carrying specific Glu-B1 alleles (particularly GluB1b) were associated with increased resistance to take-all disease (caused by Gaeumannomyces tritici), and this higher resistance was significantly related to increased grain yield. In contrast, resistance to powdery mildew was significantly associated with allelic variation at the Glu-A1 locus rather than Glu-B1 [23]. In another study involving 15 bread wheat genotypes and commercial cultivars, the presence of null and 7 + 8 (Glu-B1) alleles was associated with resistance to take-all disease. Additionally, combinations of alleles such as 2 (Glu-A1), 7 + 9 (Glu-B1), and 5 + 10 (Glu-D1) were implicated in conferring resistance, which may be attributed to gene interactions at different loci and the polygenic nature of resistance to take-all disease [24]. In a previous investigation, 100 bread wheat genotypes and commercial cultivars were evaluated for resistance to take-all disease, and the association between resistance and molecular markers—including SSRs, retrotransposon markers (long terminal repeat), and wheat-rye chromosomal translocations—was analyzed through multivariate regression. Each band amplified by the molecular markers on 100 wheat genotypes was treated as a separate locus. Furthermore, loci associated with enhanced grain yield (including xwmc388-1, xbarc197-6, and xbarc244-4) and yield components (xbarc249-2, xbarc244-4, xwmc388-14, LTR14, xbarc233-4, xwmc388-18, and xbarc232-2) were identified. Previous studies have suggested that locus 14 of a long terminal repeat (LTR14) retrotransposon marker may be associated with take-all resistance, increased root lignin content, and potential effects on yield components. This locus may serve as an informative marker in future marker-assisted selection studies. Moreover, superior genotypes were identified based on both take-all resistance and high yield [9].

Previous molecular analyses of diverse wheat germplasm collections indicated that variation at retrotransposon-based molecular markers, particularly locus LTR14, may contribute to differences in resistance to take-all disease. These analyses were supported by greenhouse screening, field evaluations, and molecular genotyping, which together highlighted the potential role of transposable element–related loci in disease resistance mechanisms [9]. While previous studies have established an association between the LTR14 retrotransposon locus and take-all resistance in bread wheat, the functional relationship between this locus, root lignin content, and end-use quality remains largely unexplored. Specifically, it is not yet known whether allelic variation at LTR14 may co-locate with genes influencing baking quality, or if sequence variation at this locus could provide new insights into its proximity to quality-related loci.

To address this gap, the present study was designed to characterize the LTR14 locus in a resistant bread wheat genotype and to investigate its potential associations with HMW-GS alleles, baking quality, agronomic traits, and take-all disease severity within a diverse wheat panel. This integrative approach aimed to identify genotypes with favorable combinations of end-use quality, disease resistance, and yield-related traits, providing a hypothesis-driven framework for marker-assisted selection in wheat breeding programs.

In this research, given the importance of HMW-GS in baking quality, the objectives were to: (1) assess the allelic diversity of HMW-GS in bread wheat genotypes by SDS-PAGE analysis; (2) evaluate the potential association between a take-all resistance-associated marker (LTR14) and quality-related HMW-GS alleles; and (3) identify genotypes combining favorable bread-making quality, resistance to take-all disease, and high grain yield.

Materials and methods

Genetic resources

The genetic material consisted of 97 bread wheat genotypes selected from an initial pool of 1000 accessions maintained in the gene bank of Vali-e-Asr University of Rafsanjan, Iran. These accessions originated from diverse regions within Iran as well as several international sources, representing a broad range of agro-ecological backgrounds. The pre-selection from 1000 to 100 genotypes was based on multi-trait field evaluations of agronomic performance and diversity in yield and yield-component traits (e.g., grain number, 1000-kernel weight, plant height) as well as differences in reaction to take-all disease in field and greenhouse conditions. Previous studies have shown that selecting genotypes with contrasting performance and disease responses enhances genetic diversity in association analyses [25–29]. The three control cultivars (Roshan, Sardari, and Kavir) were included due to their distinct agronomic characteristics and relevance to local production systems. Roshan is a widely grown spring bread wheat known for relatively good tolerance to drought and salinity stress; Kavir has been documented as tolerant to salinity and drought and is often used as a stress-tolerant check in genetic studies; Sardari is a traditional local cultivar with contrasting agronomic performance, expanding the range of phenotypic responses in this study [30–32]. Prior to the present study, each selected genotype was advanced through single-spike selection to ensure genetic uniformity and subsequently maintained in the gene bank of Vali-e-Asr University of Rafsanjan, Iran.

Evaluation of genetic resources for resistance to take-all disease under greenhouse conditions

Wheat genotypes (97 bread wheat genotypes and Roshan, Sardari, and Kavir cultivars) were selected for phenotypic screening of resistance to take-all disease. Four surface‑sterilized seeds were sown per experimental unit (pot), as described below.

Experimental site and conditions

Experiments were conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, 70% relative humidity, and a 16-h photoperiod. The soil used was sandy loam (EC = 1.75 mS cm⁻1, pH 7.8), which was autoclaved at 121 °C for 1 h on three consecutive occasions.

Experimental design

The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design with three replicates per genotype. Each pot contained 200 g of soil (dry weight) and was considered as one experimental unit.

Fungal inoculum preparation

The pathogenic fungus Gaeumannomyces tritici was employed, previously validated in wheat germplasm screening and confirmed by molecular markers [9, 29]. This fungus was molecularly confirmed based on specific DNA markers designed by Freeman et al. (2005) [9, 33]. Millet was used as a substrate due to its high colonization capacity and uniform propagules distribution. A mixture of 100 g cooked millet seeds and 100 g moist sand was placed in 500-mL flasks and autoclaved twice at 120 °C for 20 min. The fungus was first cultured on PDA for 10 days at 25 °C. Actively growing 1-cm mycelia plugs were transferred to each flask and incubated at 20–25 °C for 15 days. The flasks were then maintained for an additional 15 days at 20–28 °C under natural and fluorescent light, with periodic shaking to ensure homogeneous fungal growth. Prior to use, inoculum homogeneity was visually confirmed, and flasks were stored at 4 °C until application [29].

Data collection and phenotyping

Ten days after sowing, the soil near the plant crown was inoculated with the millet–sand–fungus mixture at 0.5% (w/w) of wet soil weight and covered with moist sand. All collected data were analyzed using appropriate statistical methods. The experiment followed a completely randomized design, and ANOVA was conducted to detect significant differences among genotypes. Post-hoc comparisons were performed where applicable.

Molecular genotyping and marker information

Molecular genotyping of the wheat genotypes was conducted in a previous study using SSR markers, retrotransposon marker (long terminal repeat), and markers linked to wheat–rye chromosomal translocations [9]. Based on association analyses, locus 14 of a retrotransposon-based marker (LTR14) showed a significant negative association with take-all disease severity and was retained as a significant predictor in stepwise regression analysis [9]. Therefore, this locus (LTR14) was selected for further isolation, sequencing, and molecular characterization in the present study.

Locus isolation and sequencing

In a previous study, a retrotransposon-based marker amplified a distinct DNA fragment (~ 450 bp) in resistant genotypes, which was absent or different in susceptible ones, showing a highly significant association with take-all resistance and root lignin content [9]. The fragment corresponding to the LTR14 locus was excised from the polyacrylamide gel using the crush-and-soak method [38] and re-amplified using the LTR14-specific primers: F: GGTGTGTCCGGGGCGTTACA and R: CGGGAGCCCATTCGAAC. PCR reactions (20 μL) included 10 μL master mix (10X buffer, MgCl2, dNTPs 10 mM, Taq polymerase 5 U/μL), 6 μL sterile ddH2O, 1 μL of each marker, and 2μL genomic DNA (30 ng/μL). Negative controls without DNA were included in each run to check for contamination. Thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 55 °C for 60 s, 72 °C for 2 min, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The resulting PCR product (~ 450 bp) was visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel (Fig. 2; the uncropped gel image is provided in Supplementary file S1). Sanger sequencing was performed in both directions (bidirectional reads) by Gene Fanavaran Company (Tehran, Iran). Raw sequencing data were processed using MEGA7 software. Low-quality or ambiguous regions at the 5' and 3' ends of the sequence, likely due to sequencing technology limitations or residual primer sequences, were trimmed. This data cleaning process yielded a final consensus sequence of 276bp, which was subsequently used for downstream bioinformatics analyses, including homology searches via BLAST. The final sequence was deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under accession number PX660489.1. Only a single genotype (1528) was sequenced; therefore, the sequence-based interpretations presented in this study should be considered preliminary. However, genotype 1528 represents a nearly resistant line that has previously been utilized as a parental genotype in bulk segregant analysis, diallel analysis, and generation mean analysis studies [39–41]. Analysis of HMW-GS

Extraction of wheat seed storage protein

For each genotype and cultivar, a single seed was selected, and after removing the embryo, it was ground into powder and transferred to a 2 mL microtube. To each sample, 800 µL of extraction buffer (containing Tris buffer with pH 6.8, sodium dodecyl sulfate, Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250, mercaptoethanol, and glycerol) was added and vortexed for 1 h. The samples were incubated in a water bath at 90–95 °C for 10 min, then cooled, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new microtube and stored at −20 °C [42, 43].

Wheat seed storage protein electrophoresis using SDS-PAGE

For electrophoresis, an acrylamide gel was used, with a glycine-sodium dodecyl sulfate-Tris solution as the electrolyte buffer, Tris buffers with pH 8.8 and 6.8, a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution, ammonium persulfate, and TEMED. The top gel was prepared with a 4% concentration, and the bottom gel with a 10% concentration. After completing the electrophoresis (2 h for the top gel at 30 V and 9 h for the bottom gel at 80 V), each gel was placed in a washing solution (ethanol 50% V/V and acetic acid 10% V/V) for 1 h. Then, to fix the protein bands, a methanol-acetic acid solution was used as a fixing solution for 24 h. To visualize the protein bands, Coomassie brilliant blue R-250 was used. Each gel was placed in the staining solution for 3 to 4 h on a shaker. After this time, the gel was washed twice with distilled water. To remove excess stain between the bands, a de-staining solution consisting of 50% methanol and 10% acetic acid was used. The duration of treatment with this solution continued until the gel became clear and the protein bands were visible. After this time, the gel was washed twice with distilled water. Upon completion of the de-staining process, the gels were photographed using a camera [44–47].

Statistical analyses

Scoring of HMW glutenin subunits was performed according to the method of Payne and Lawrence [42]. Scoring was done using images of the HMW glutenin gels. Additionally, since the effect of favorable alleles on baking quality is additive, researchers developed a simple scoring method, assigning numerical values to each HMW glutenin subunit based on its impact on sedimentation volume with sodium dodecyl sulfate [42]. Therefore, the overall quality of a cultivar is estimated by identifying the HMW-GS and calculating the corresponding score [48, 49]. Statistical analyses were performed using MINITAB version 19, NTSYSpc, ClustVis, and GenAlEx software packages. Before statistical analyses, data normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Among the measured traits, NGSp showed deviation from normal distribution and was log₁₀-transformed, while GWR was square-root transformed to improve normality and stabilize variance. The remaining traits met normality assumptions and were analyzed without transformation.

All statistical analyses were performed using MINITAB version 19. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess differences among genotypes for quantitative traits. Mean comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test at the 0.05 probability level. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated among all variables (11 alleles and 8 phenotypic traits). To control for inflated Type I error due to multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (p < 0.05). The FDR correction was applied across all pairwise correlation tests (n = 171 comparisons). Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to summarize variation patterns and identify major contributing traits. Cluster analysis was performed to classify genotypes based on overall similarity. Stepwise regression analysis was used to determine the most important predictor traits influencing key target variables. In addition, chi-square analysis was conducted for categorical variables to examine the distribution of glutenin alleles among genotypes.

Heatmap construction

To visualize the combined distribution of molecular markers, baking quality, and agronomic traits among wheat genotypes, a heatmap was generated using ClustVis (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/) [50]. The input data included three types of variables: (1) binary values (0 and 1) representing the presence/absence of HMW-GS alleles and the LTR14 retrotransposon marker, (2) continuous variables corresponding to agronomic traits such as DS, SL, WGSp, and others, and (3) baking quality scores derived from glutenin subunit composition.

Hierarchical clustering was applied to the rows (genotypes) using Manhattan distance and the average linkage method. Manhattan distance was used for hierarchical clustering because it is equivalent to Hamming distance for binary molecular markers and does not assume interval-scale behavior for categorical variables. Although Gower distance is a general metric for mixed-type data, for binary and nominal variables it reduces to a simple mismatch score highly similar to Manhattan distance. Row scaling was set to none (no scaling) to avoid distortion of the original trait values, given the mixture of binary and continuous variables. Columns (traits and markers) were not clustered and were displayed in their original order. The tree ordering for rows was set to “tightest cluster first” to enhance visual grouping.

The heatmap allowed identification of genotypes with favorable combinations of alleles, agronomic traits, and disease severity. This approach provided a clear overview of multi-trait profiles among the 100 wheat genotypes, supporting the selection of top-performing entries for further evaluation in breeding programs.

Distance metric and clustering justification

Our dataset contained a combination of (i) binary molecular markers (presence/absence of HMW‑GS alleles and LTR14 retrotransposon), (ii) continuous agronomic traits (e.g., DS, SL, and WGSp), and (iii) categorical baking-quality scores. Because these variables are non‑continuous, heterogeneous, and include discretized binary components, we selected the Manhattan distance as the primary dissimilarity measure. Manhattan distance evaluates feature-wise absolute differences and is well suited for mixed and non‑normally distributed variables; for binary markers it is equivalent to counting mismatches, which provides an interpretable measure of genetic dissimilarity. Hierarchical clustering was performed using average linkage, which is less sensitive to outliers than single linkage and avoids overly compact clusters produced by complete linkage. This combination has been widely applied to heterogeneous biological datasets and provides balanced grouping across mixed data types.

Molecular analysis and phylogenetic tree construction

The retrotransposon-based marker LTR14, previously associated with reduced take-all disease severity and agronomic traits, was sequenced in the resistant wheat genotype 1528. The obtained nucleotide sequence was edited using MEGA7 and saved in FASTA format.

To investigate sequence similarity with storage protein-related loci, the sequence was subjected to BLASTn analysis against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) nucleotide database. A total of 17 highly similar hits were identified. Eight of these hits corresponded to annotated protein-coding genes, including γ-gliadin, LMW glutenin, and other seed storage protein-related sequences, whereas the remaining hits represented BAC clones, genome assemblies, or scaffold sequences lacking functional annotation. Only the eight annotated sequences were included in the phylogenetic analysis to improve comparability among biologically characterized sequences.

Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the MAFFT algorithm through the NGPhylogeny.fr platform. Poorly aligned regions were removed using BMGE to improve alignment quality. The final alignment length corresponded to the amplified fragment size (276 bp). Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the PhyML algorithm under the GTR (General Time Reversible) nucleotide substitution model with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was visualized using TreeDyn and exported in SVG/PNG and Newick formats.

Due to the relatively short fragment length (276 bp), bootstrap values and phylogenetic relationships were interpreted cautiously and used primarily to support general clustering patterns and sequence similarity. The phylogenetic analysis suggested partial similarity between LTR14 and several storage protein-related sequences, including γ-gliadin and storage protein activator genes; however, these observations should be considered preliminary and exploratory rather than definitive evidence of functional or evolutionary relationships.

Multi-trait genotypic selection using the TOPSIS method

In this study, the TOPSIS method based on the multi-criteria decision making (MCDM) model was employed to select the best genotype. The final TOPSIS score, referred to as the similarity index to the best conditions (SIB index), was calculated for each genotype. This index ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater proximity to the ideal performance across all traits. The steps to calculate the SIB index are as follows [9, 51–56]:

The present study was designed as an initial integrative assessment combining field-based agronomic evaluation and greenhouse-based disease screening to identify associations among disease response, baking quality, agronomic traits, and molecular markers within a genetically diverse wheat panel.

To prioritize promising genotypes, SIB scores obtained from TOPSIS analysis were used as a basis for comparative ranking. The TOPSIS approach integrated disease severity (DS), baking quality (BQ), and key agronomic traits into a unified multi-trait performance index. Genotypes were ranked according to their SIB values, with higher scores indicating greater similarity to the desirable multi-trait profile within the evaluated environment. Although SIB ≥ 0.70 was used as a reference point for interpretative purposes, genotype ranking rather than a strict threshold served as the primary basis for selection. Sensitivity analysis using alternative thresholds (≥ 0.65 and ≥ 0.75) indicated relative consistency among the highest-ranked genotypes within this dataset. Therefore, genotypes with consistently high SIB values were considered promising candidates for further evaluation under additional environments and growing seasons.

Phenotypic evaluation

Phenotypic response of wheat genotypes to take-all disease

Hierarchical clustering was performed using Euclidean distances and Ward’s method, with all variables standardized using z-scores to ensure comparability across traits. Based on this analysis, the genotypes were divided into three distinct clusters at a distance level of 82.33, each exhibiting unique phenotypic characteristics. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on ANOVA, which showed significant differences among the groups for the measured traits (p < 0.05).

Cluster 3 (Resistant genotypes): This cluster contains 16 genotypes and represents the most resistant group. It exhibits the highest root dry weight (0.279 g) and lignin content (13.485%), with significantly lower disease severity (7.625%) compared to the other clusters (p < 0.05). These genotypes have strong root systems and favorable cell wall compositions, suggesting enhanced physiological defense against take-all disease.

Cluster 1 (Susceptible genotypes): This cluster contains 40 genotypes and represents the most susceptible group. Disease severity is extremely high (87.858%), while root dry weight (0.113 g) and lignin content (5.055%) are the lowest among all clusters. These genotypes have weak roots and reduced cell wall components, highlighting their vulnerability to take-all infection.

Cluster 2 (Moderately resistant genotypes): This intermediate cluster contains 44 genotypes, with root dry weight (0.144 g) and lignin content (11.350%) higher than Cluster 1 but lower than Cluster 3. Disease severity is at an intermediate level (43.643%). This group shows relative resistance and considerable intracluster diversity, bridging the contrast between the extremes.

Intermediate-yield clusters and trait trade-offs

Cluster 6 was characterized by relatively high NGSp (53.758) and WGSp (2.179 g) but exhibited the lowest TKW (35.340 g), indicating a trade-off between grain number and grain size.

Cluster 4 displayed an intermediate profile, with moderate SL (8.985 cm) and TKW (39.918 g), but lower overall GWR (103.742 g). These clusters suggest potential differences in yield-component relationships among genotypes.

Low-yielding clusters

Cluster 1 showed intermediate NGSp (43.145) and WGSp (1.895 g) despite favorable SL (12.063 cm), reflecting moderate yield performance.

Cluster 3, despite having the highest TKW (50.194 g), showed the lowest NGSp (29.931) and WGSp (1.492 g), again indicating a compensatory relationship between grain size and grain number.

Overall, SL, NGSp, WGSp, and GWR were identified as the most discriminating traits contributing to genotype differentiation. The clustering pattern revealed substantial phenotypic diversity and suggested potential interrelationships among yield-related traits.

The result of sequencing

The amplified fragment (LTR14) from the resistant genotype 1528 was isolated, purified, and sequenced. The resulting sequence was deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under accession number PX660489.1. This sequence was used as an initial molecular characterization of the LTR14 marker-associated region and provides a basis for future comparative analyses across wheat genotypes.

Because retrotransposon-associated regions frequently contain repetitive sequences, these similarities were interpreted cautiously as indicative of partial nucleotide similarity rather than definitive functional homology. Although equal weights were assigned to all traits in the calculation of the SIB index, baking quality (BQ) represented an aggregate trait derived from the combined contribution of several quality-related alleles. In contrast, disease severity (DS) represented a single variable and showed no significant relationship with SIB, resulting in a comparatively smaller contribution to the index.

Nevertheless, resistance-related variation may have been indirectly reflected in the SIB index. Lignin content, which was not directly included in the SIB calculation, showed significant correlations with both the resistance-associated locus LTR14 (r = 0.310, p < 0.05) and DS (r = –0.608, p < 0.05) (Table 5), and also exhibited a positive association with SIB. Discussion

Based on the clustering results of genotypes according to agronomic traits and disease response, substantial genetic diversity was observed among the evaluated wheat genotypes. Overall, root dry weight and lignin content emerged as key traits differentiating resistant from susceptible genotypes. The clear separation between extreme clusters underscores the potential of these traits as physiological markers for resistance.

From a plant breeding perspective, Cluster 5 and, to some extent, Cluster 2 may serve as promising genetic resources for the simultaneous improvement of yield components. Conversely, clusters exhibiting specific trait limitations could be strategically utilized as parental lines in targeted crossing programs aimed at trait complementation and recombination.

In addition to the observed phenotypic diversity in agronomic performance and disease response, considerable allelic variation was also detected in HMW-GS, which are key determinants of end-use quality in wheat. The Glu-1 loci, located on the long arms of group 1 chromosomes in hexaploid wheat, exhibit multiple allelism and play major roles in determining baking quality. Previous studies indicate that the alleles at each of these loci have specific quality values that contribute to improved end-use quality. Among them, alleles 2 (Glu-A1), 17 + 18 (Glu-B1), and 5 + 10 (Glu-D1) are considered to have the highest quality scores within their respective loci. These alleles are distributed on the A, B, and D genomes of wheat, and their simultaneous presence in a genotype results in superior baking quality, with a maximum quality score of 10 [57]. The allelic composition of HMW glutenin subunits in each wheat genotype is a key determinant of baking quality. For example, wheat cultivars carrying the 5 + 10 allele at the Glu-D1 locus generally exhibit stronger dough properties compared to those carrying the 2 + 12 allele [58]. Furthermore, transgenic wheat lines overexpressing subunits 5 and 10 showed increased levels of polymeric proteins and improved baking quality [59]. The influence of HMW glutenins on dough strength is more pronounced than that of LMW glutenins, as HMW-GS are more actively involved in forming large glutenin polymers. All HMW-GS contain cysteine residues at both ends of the polypeptide chain, whereas LMW-GS lack cysteine residues at the N-terminal region. This structural difference influences the size and configuration of the resulting gluten polymers. HMW-GS act as chain extenders and contribute to the formation of a disulfide-bonded glutenin backbone within the gluten network. Hydrogen bonding via glutamine side chains also plays a significant role in stabilizing the gluten structure. In most cases, HMW-GS form inter- and intra-molecular disulfide bonds, which influence the secondary structure of gluten and ultimately impact dough quality [57]. In the current study, 49% of the evaluated germplasm carried the 5 + 10 allele at the Glu-D1 locus, suggesting that these genotypes could serve as promising sources for improving baking quality. This subunit is strongly associated with superior baking quality performance. Because the BQ score was derived from HMW-GS allelic composition, associations between specific alleles and BQ values should be interpreted cautiously, as part of the relationship may reflect the structure of the scoring system itself. Therefore, these results should be considered supportive of established allele-quality relationships rather than independent validation.

Previous studies have shown that HMW-GS can account for 18% to 55% of the variation in gluten strength and extensibility, whereas low molecular weight glutenin subunits contribute about 20% [60]. This variation is mainly attributed to the presence of favorable alleles such as 1 and 2* at the Glu-A1 locus on chromosome 1 A (genome A), 17 + 18 at the Glu-B1 locus on chromosome 1B, and 5 + 10 at the Glu-D1 locus on chromosome 1D, which significantly influence gluten strength [61, 62].

Investigating allelic variation in HMW-GS has remained a major focus in wheat quality improvement research over the past decades. Some allelic combinations have a greater impact on dough elasticity, strength, and loaf volume compared to others. Among the three Glu-1 loci, Glu-D1 has been consistently reported to exert the most significant influence on dough and baking properties, followed by Glu-B1 and Glu-A1. Moreover, several studies have demonstrated that both Glu-D1 and Glu-B1 alleles have substantial effects on dough quality, whereas the effect of Glu-A1 alleles depends on the presence of other Glu-1 subunits [57]. The high allelic diversity observed at the Glu-B1 locus provides a valuable opportunity for wheat breeders to enhance end-use quality [63]. This diversity enables the selection of favorable alleles, the development of novel allelic combinations with synergistic effects, and the identification of rare or underutilized alleles potentially linked to quality or resistance traits. The availability of such variation within Glu-B1 contributes significantly to improving dough properties [18, 19], broadening the genetic base, and achieving multi-trait improvement through targeted breeding strategies.

In a previous study, lines carrying Glu-B1 alleles exhibited greater resistance to take-all disease and also demonstrated higher yield performance [23]. In another study involving 15 bread wheat genotypes and commercial cultivars, the presence of Null and 7 + 8 alleles was associated with resistance to take-all disease. Allelic combinations such as 2*, 7 + 9, and 5 + 10 also contributed to resistance, which may be attributed to gene interactions at different loci and the polygenic nature of resistance to take-all disease [24]. Additionally, the diversity of 362 Ukrainian winter wheat varieties was assessed at storage protein loci (Gli-A1, Gli-B1, Gli-D1, Glu-A1, Glu-B1, Glu-D1, Gli-A3, Gli-B5, and Gli-A6) using markers linked to the Lr34/Yr18/Pm38/Sr57/Bdv1 gene cluster, which confers resistance to multiple biotrophic pathogens. Significant differences were observed in the frequency of glutenin alleles associated with disease resistance genes [64].

The phylogenetic analysis of the LTR14 sequence provided preliminary evidence of sequence similarity and phylogenetic proximity with genes encoding γ-gliadin and a storage protein, both involved in endosperm development and protein quality. However, because this analysis was based on a short fragment (276 bp) obtained from a single genotype, these relationships should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory rather than definitive.

In the phylogenetic tree, LTR14 clustered alongside MG560140.1 (γ-gliadin) and FM242576.1 (storage protein activator), suggesting possible sequence similarity with genomic regions related to γ-gliadin and storage protein activator genes. Similar findings have been reported where transposable elements and retrotransposons were shown to integrate near regulatory or coding regions of seed storage protein genes [65, 66]. Retrotransposon insertions can modulate gene expression, influence chromatin structure, or serve as alternative promoters [67]. In this study, the presence of LTR14 was positively correlated with the 7 + 8 allele and with lignin content, and negatively correlated with disease severity (indicating increased resistance). Although correlation and chi-square analyses did not show a direct association between LTR14 and alleles enhancing baking quality, BLAST and phylogenetic analyses suggested sequence similarity between LTR14 and γ-gliadin and storage protein-related sequences. Together, these results suggest a potential dual role of LTR14 in both defense and quality-related traits, which remains a hypothesis requiring further validation. A major limitation of the present study is that the LTR14 sequence characterization was performed using only a single resistant genotype (1528). Consequently, the current sequence data do not allow comprehensive assessment of allelic diversity, sequence conservation, or evolutionary variation of the LTR14 locus across wheat germplasm. On the other hand, retrotransposon-associated loci are often characterized by high sequence variability, copy number variation, and dynamic genomic distribution across wheat genomes. Therefore, sequence similarity observed for a single LTR14 fragment should not be interpreted as representative of the full diversity or evolutionary behavior of this retrotransposon family in wheat germplasm. Broader sequencing across diverse resistant and susceptible genotypes will be necessary to clarify the conservation and biological significance of the LTR14-associated region. Therefore, the phylogenetic and sequence similarity analyses presented here should be considered preliminary and exploratory. Additional sequencing of resistant and susceptible genotypes from diverse genetic backgrounds will be necessary to validate the observed associations and determine the broader biological relevance of the LTR14 locus.

Moreover, by identifying associations between specific alleles and baking quality scores, MAS can be applied to identify and utilize high-quality genotypes. The observed genetic diversity within this germplasm represents a valuable genetic reservoir that can be exploited in wheat breeding programs aimed at improving end-use quality. These loci can serve as indicators of the relationship between molecular markers and baking quality, although they are not precise predictors of gene alleles. The γ-gliadin and LMW glutenin genes are well-documented seed storage proteins directly associated with wheat baking quality. The presence of LTR14 within the same clade suggests that this retrotransposon-based marker may be linked to functional regions influencing grain quality traits. These gene fragments may represent potential candidates associated with resistance to take-all disease. It has been demonstrated that a gene belonging to the prolamin family, encoding a protein with gliadin domains, plays a role in defense against Fusarium graminearum. Wheat avenin-like proteins (TaALPs) are storage proteins that belong to the prolamin superfamily. In a comprehensive study, the genomic features of ALP-encoding genes in bread wheat were characterized. In silico analysis confirmed the presence of key peptides in TaALPs that are active in plant immune responses. Pathogenesis-related nucleotide motifs were also identified in the putative promoter regions of TaALP genes. Gene expression studies of TaALPs and pathogen resistance-related genes were conducted under control and F. graminearum infection conditions. The results indicated that TaALP and NMT genes were upregulated following pathogen inoculation. Furthermore, TaALP gene expression was localized to the embryo layer, aleurone, and sub-aleurone cells. Allelic variation in two ALP genes on chromosome 7 A was significantly associated with Fusarium disease symptoms, and higher expression of one ALP gene on 7 A was indicative of Fusarium resistance. This study confirmed that a class of wheat storage proteins or gluten-related proteins is involved in biochemical processes related to disease resistance. Given the abundance of these proteins in wheat grains and their essential functions, the findings offer valuable implications for breeding wheat for disease resistance [68]. In addition, several alleles at glutenin subunit loci in wheat and its relatives have been associated with disease resistance genes. For example, the leaf rust resistance genes Lr21 and Lr42, and the stem rust resistance gene Sr33 from Aegilops tauschii, are located within approximately 5% recombination distance from the Gli-D1 locus [69–71]. The yellow rust resistance gene Yr10 is mapped 5% recombination distance from Gli-B1 [72]. The powdery mildew resistance gene Pm3 is associated with Gli-A1 and neighboring loci (Gli-A5 and Glu-A3), with the Pm3g allele located approximately 5 cM from Gli-A5 [73]. The Lr10 leaf rust resistance gene was mapped 9.2 cM from Gli-A1 [74]. The 1BL/1RS translocation from Secale cereale (rye) cultivar Petkus, the most widespread alien introgression in common wheat, harbors resistance genes Pm8, Sr31, Lr26, and Yr9 [64]. Moreover, a study on 362 Ukrainian winter wheat varieties examined the diversity at storage protein loci (Gli-A1, Gli-B1, Gli-D1, Glu-A1, Glu-B1, Glu-D1, Gli-A3, Gli-B5, and Gli-A6) and their association with resistance genes such as Lr34/Yr18/Pm38/Sr57/Bdv1, which confer resistance to multiple biotrophic pathogens. A significant variation in the frequency of glutenin alleles associated with disease resistance genes was observed [64]. LTR14 may represent a statistically associated marker with potential utility for preliminary marker-assisted selection of genotypes with reduced disease severity and favorable quality-related traits. Sequencing of the LTR14 locus provides additional information on its genomic context, suggesting possible linkage with defense- and quality-related regions, highlighting its potential as a marker for multi-trait selection in wheat breeding.

The multivariate analyses conducted in this study (PCA, heatmap clustering, SIB based on TOPSIS model) proved effective in identifying wheat genotypes with combined superiority for baking quality, disease severity, and grain yield. Genotypes located in the favorable quadrants of the PCA biplot and clustered near desirable traits and alleles (such as 5 + 10, 2*, and LTR14) in the heatmap exhibited consistently superior agronomic profiles. These results highlight the potential of multivariate tools as indirect selection strategies in wheat breeding programs, complementing traditional phenotypic and molecular selection approaches. Similar strategies have been employed in recent studies to dissect complex trait interactions and to guide marker-assisted selection based on genotype–trait associations [10]. As previously reported by Mohammadi and Prasanna [12], PCA enables the identification of genotypes expressing high cumulative variation across multiple traits, which is particularly valuable when direct phenotypic evaluation is resource-intensive.

The multi-trait selection using the TOPSIS approach provided an integrative assessment of genotypic performance across baking quality, disease severity, and grain yield traits. Based on the most stringent threshold (SIB ≥ 0.75), nine genotypes (1528, 48, 862, 1518, 631, 1429, 1879, 3802, and 1510) were identified as promising candidates, exhibiting low disease severity, high baking quality, and favorable yield components. The integration of molecular and phenotypic data in the TOPSIS framework, combined with sensitivity checks, provides a robust approach to identify genotypes that consistently perform well across multiple traits and reduces the risk of overfitting selection decisions to a single dataset. These genotypes were also among the top performers in heatmap and PCA analyses, indicating consistent favorable trait profiles across multiple statistical frameworks. This congruence confirms that the integration of molecular markers with quantitative trait data via SIB provides a robust and statistically validated tool for genotype selection in wheat breeding. The significant negative correlation observed between the LTR14 marker and DS (p < 0.05) suggests a potential association between the marker and reduced take-all disease severity. The lack of a significant correlation with the SIB index, a composite measure incorporating bread-making quality, disease severity, and grain yield, may indicate that while LTR14 directly influences disease resistance, its impact on other components of the SIB index is less pronounced, or that the interplay of these traits within the SIB calculation masks a direct association with LTR14. Factors such as population structure and environmental effects could play a role in these complex relationships, warranting further investigation in more diverse populations and conditions. Furthermore, the fact that SIB values distinguished lines with elite multi-trait combinations, while other methods (e.g., correlation or regression) showed limited resolution, highlights the value of decision-support techniques such as TOPSIS in advanced selection strategies. Because the present study was conducted in a single environment and growing season, the observed genotype performance and trait associations may have been influenced by environmental conditions specific to Rafsanjan, Iran. Therefore, additional multi-location and multi-year evaluations will be necessary to assess the stability of the identified associations and potential genotype × environment interactions. Nevertheless, the studied wheat panel represented broad agro-ecological diversity and was selected from a larger collection previously evaluated under both field and greenhouse conditions.

The present study supports that integrating multivariate analysis with molecular markers can effectively guide the selection of elite wheat lines for dual-purpose improvement, namely enhancing both end-use quality and disease resistance. The allelic diversity present at these loci can be utilized for cultivar identification and establishing phylogenetic relationships.

Limitations and future perspectives

The present study has several important limitations that significantly affect the robustness and generalizability of the conclusions.

First, and most critically, sequence characterization of the LTR14 locus was performed using only a single resistant genotype (1528). This single-genotype approach substantially limits our ability to assess allelic diversity, sequence conservation, or evolutionary variation at this locus across wheat germplasm. Consequently, the sequence similarity observed between LTR14 and storage protein-related genes (γ-gliadin and storage protein activator) must be interpreted with extreme caution and should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive or representative of the broader LTR14 diversity in wheat.

Second, all phenotypic evaluations were conducted in a single environment (Rafsanjan, Iran) during one growing season (2021–2022). This single-environment design severely restricts the generalizability of the identified genotype–trait associations and does not account for potential genotype × environment interactions, which are known to significantly influence both disease resistance and baking quality traits in wheat.

Third, while the integrative multi-trait framework (PCA, heatmap, TOPSIS) proved useful for identifying promising genotypes within this dataset, the selection decisions based on these analyses remain environment-specific and require validation under diverse agro-ecological conditions.

Fourth, the identified associations between the LTR14 marker and agronomic traits were derived from correlation and regression analyses within a diverse germplasm panel. Although these statistical approaches are appropriate for hypothesis generation, they do not establish causation, and the observed associations may be influenced by population structure or linkage disequilibrium with other unlinked loci.

Importantly, however, we have initiated validation studies using an F9 recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from a cross between the resistant genotype 1528 (carrying the LTR14 band) and the susceptible genotype 164 (lacking this band). This population provides a valuable resource for:

Assessing co-segregation of the LTR14 marker with take-all resistance;

Validating the stability of the observed marker–trait associations across different genetic backgrounds;

Estimating the heritability and genetic control of resistance conferred by the LTR14-associated region.

Preliminary greenhouse screening of this F9 population has shown promising co-segregation patterns, and multi-environment field trials are currently underway to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

The allelic composition at the Glu-1 loci, particularly the presence of 2* (Glu-A1), 17 + 18 (Glu-B1), and 5 + 10 (Glu-D1), was associated with higher baking quality scores among the studied wheat genotypes. In contrast, genotypes carrying the null and 2 + 12 alleles generally exhibited lower baking quality performance. The retrotransposon-based marker LTR14 (NCBI GenBank accession number PX660489.1) showed statistically significant associations with reduced take-all disease severity and several agronomic traits, suggesting its potential utility in identifying genotypes with favorable resistance and performance characteristics.

Sequence analysis of the LTR14 fragment from resistant genotype 1528 revealed similarity to storage protein-related sequences, while association analyses indicated co-occurrence between LTR14 and favorable HMW-GS alleles in several genotypes. Genotypes such as 1528, 48, 2156, and 1879 combined favorable glutenin alleles, low disease severity, desirable baking quality, and relatively strong agronomic performance, highlighting their potential value for multi-trait wheat breeding programs.

Overall, the integration of molecular markers, multivariate analyses (PCA, heatmap, and TOPSIS), and trait-based selection provided a preliminary framework for identifying promising wheat genotypes with favorable end-use quality and reduced disease severity. However, it is important to emphasize that the study was conducted in a single environment and sequence characterization was based on a single resistant genotype; therefore, the observed associations must be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Further multi-environment evaluations and broader sequencing across resistant and susceptible genotypes are essential to validate the stability and broader applicability of these findings before these genotypes can be recommended for breeding programs.
