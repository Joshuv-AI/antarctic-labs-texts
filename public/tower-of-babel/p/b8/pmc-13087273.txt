Corn silk ameliorates fatty liver disease by modulating the gut microbiota and metabolites in the serum and liver
Authors: Lin Ding, Shan Ren, Chuangang Zang, Long Zheng, Liyan Jiang, Hao Guo, Wenqing Yang, Hong Guan, Jicheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorn silk, the stigma of the female flower of maize (Zea mays L.), is a medicinal and food-based homologous natural product. Previous studies have highlighted the lipid-modulating and hepatoprotective properties of corn silk; however, its effects on fatty liver disease remain unclear. C57BL/6J mice were fed an HFD and orally administered CSE. Hepatic histopathology, systemic inflammatory markers, and metabolic profiles were assessed. Gut microbiota and metabolomic analyses were integrated to identify the key regulatory axis. CSE administration significantly improved hepatic steatosis, reduced circulating lipid levels, and suppressed systemic and hepatic inflammation. Microbiota analysis revealed that CSE restructured the intestinal ecology by fostering a “competing guild” dominated by Akkermansia, which was negatively correlated with several pathobionts. Metabolomic profiling indicated that CSE significantly modulated pentose-glucuronate interconversion and primary bile acid biosynthesis. Notably, the enrichment of taurine and activation of the bile acid-FXR signaling axis were identified as pivotal mechanisms for enhancing fatty acid β-oxidation and restoring redox balance via the glutathione pathway. Our findings demonstrate that CSE effectively ameliorates FLD by modulating the gut microbiota-metabolite-liver axis. These results position CSE as a promising prebiotic and functional food candidate for the prevention and treatment of metabolic liver diseases.Keywords: Corn silk, Fatty liver disease, Gut microbiota, Metabolomic, AkkermansiaSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Diseases, Drug discovery, Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

Fatty liver disease (FLD) has emerged as a predominant global health challenge, representing the most prevalent chronic hepatic disorder worldwide. Within the broad spectrum of FLD, Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD) stands out due to its complex, multifaceted pathogenesis, which is increasingly understood through the "multiple-hit" hypothesis. This framework posits that the disease originates from a primary “hit”—characterized by excessive intrahepatic lipid accumulation typically driven by a high-fat diet or systemic insulin resistance. This initial metabolic insult subsequently sensitizes the liver to a cascade of secondary "hits," including oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and the aberrant release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These synergistic factors collectively orchestrate the pathological progression from simple steatosis to Non-Alcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH), which may further culminate in irreversible fibrosis or cirrhosis1,2. At present, effective pharmacological interventions for FLD are scarce, highlighting the critical demand for innovative pharmaceutical research and complementary treatment approaches. Herbal-based therapies, which offer a rich repository of bioactive compounds from natural sources, have emerged as compelling strategies for discovering novel therapeutic agents against FLD3,4. Specifically, these natural products exhibit protective effects against FLD-related complications, such as hepatitis and cirrhosis, by regulating lipid metabolism and attenuating fibrogenesis5,6. Building on this, the modulation of the gut-liver axis through the interplay between natural products and gut microbiota has emerged as a pivotal therapeutic strategy7,8.

Corn silk (CS), the stigmas of the female flowers of Zea mays L., is a classic representative of "Food and Medicine Homology." It has been utilized as a traditional medicinal herb and a functional dietary component across various nations, including China, the United States, and Korea. In 2012, China’s Ministry of Health officially categorized CS as a safe long-term dietary resource. As highlighted in recent research on food-derived resources, CS belongs to a critical category of natural products that alleviate metabolic syndromes through their multi-target functional components9. Historically, records such as "Dian Nan Ben Cao" document its efficacy in promoting diuresis, clearing hepatic-gallbladder damp-heat, and managing “Xiao Ke” syndrome (comparable to modern diabetes). Modern phytochemical profiling reveals that CS is rich in bioactive constituents, including polysaccharides, flavonoids, phenolic acids, and phytosterols, which contribute to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and lipid-lowering properties10–14. Previous experimental evidence indicates that aqueous CS extracts can effectively modulate lipid metabolism in high-fat diet-fed murine models by normalizing hepatic and serum cholesterol concentrations15–17.

This research explores the therapeutic efficacy of CS extract against FLD induced by a high-fat diet (HFD), with a particular emphasis on variations in blood and liver metabolites alongside the composition of the gut microbiota. A comprehensive pharmacological assessment was performed in HFD-induced FLD murine model. Metabolomic profiling combined with 16S rRNA sequencing techniques was applied to clarify the pathways by which CS alleviates FLD progression. This investigation provides new insights into the preventive mechanisms of CS in FLD pathogenesis.

Experimental section

Materials and reagents

The control diet was formulated with 10% fat, 20% protein, and 70% carbohydrates, whereas the HFD comprised 60% fat, 20% protein, and 20% carbohydrates (Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd., China). The specific composition of the HFD is summarized in Table 1. Corn silk extract preparation involved obtaining raw materials from Qiqihar Medical University’s Science and Technology Achievement Transformation Center. Approximately 20 kg of dehydrated CS was subjected to hot aqueous extraction (1:15 mass/volume ratio) via a vacuum reflux system (Chengdong Medicine Machine Instrument Factory, Wenzhou, China). Three consecutive 60-minute extraction cycles were performed, followed by solution concentration and subsequent spray-drying (GEA, Düsseldorf, Germany) to yield CS extract (CSE) with 4.74% extraction efficiency. Phytochemical characterization revealed that the extract contained 34.42% total glucose (phenol‒sulfuric method), 16.63% polysaccharides, 26.34% protein (Kjeldahl analysis), 4.07% flavonoids (aluminum chloride colorimetry), and 10.17% phenolic compounds (Folin‒Ciocalteu assay). The chemical profile of the CSE used in this study has been previously characterized by our group using UHPLC-ESI-QTOF-MS18. The major bioactive constituents were identified as ferulic acid, caffeic acid, protocatechuic acid, vanillic aldehyde, vanillic acid, salicylic acid, luteolin, and apigenin. These identified compounds, particularly the phenolic acids and flavonoids, provide the phytochemical basis for the biological activities observed in the present study.

Animal studies and dosage information

All animal procedures were performed following applicable ethical guidelines and received approval from the Institutional Animal Care Committee at Qiqihar Medical University (QMU-IACC-2022-88). The reporting of all animal procedures in this study fully conforms to the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0. Male C57BL/6 mice (16–20 g) with specific pathogen-free status were obtained from Changchun Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). The animals were maintained in a regulated environment with the temperature maintained at 20–22°C, a relative humidity of 45 ± 5%, and controlled 12-hour photoperiod cycles. Following a 7-day adaptation period with free access to standard chow, the subjects were randomly divided into three experimental groups (n = 8) using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The control group (labeled Ctrl) received a control diet alongside purified water (10 mL/kg), the FLD induction group (labeled HFD) was provided a high-fat diet alongside purified water (10 mL/kg), and the treatment group was provided a high-fat diet supplemented orally with CSE (0.8 g/kg), which is equivalent to a human dose of 87.9 mg/kg. The dosage of CSE (0.8 g/kg) utilized in this study was determined based on our group’s previously established research17. Mice were fed an HFD for 8 weeks to establish the FLD model, while receiving daily oral gavage of CSE throughout the induction period. Throughout the 8-week experimental phase, all the animals had continuous access to their respective dietary regimens and water. Body mass and nutritional intake were monitored weekly. Following an overnight fast, the mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation. Whole blood samples were then collected via cardiac puncture, after which the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation while still under deep anesthesia. Other biological samples were immediately harvested thereafter. The blood samples were allowed to coagulate at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4 °C to isolate the serum. The resulting serum was then aliquoted and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. Hepatic tissues were weighed, with portions preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathological evaluation, and the remaining samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for cryopreservation at −80°C. Fecal matter was systematically collected for subsequent analytical procedures.

Measurement of serological indicators

Serum concentrations of TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, ALT, and AST were assessed with an automated biochemical analyzer (Olympus AU 600 analyzer, Hamburg, Germany). The determination of insulin, IL-6, and TNF-α concentrations in both hepatic tissues and serum samples was performed through commercially available ELISA kits supplied by Shanghai Jianglai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Liver histological analyses

Liver specimens were immersed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for fixation, subsequently dehydrated and sectioned into paraffin blocks. These tissue samples were then subjected to a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining protocol to enable microscopic examination of the hepatic architecture. For lipid content analysis, cryosectioned liver samples were processed with Oil Red O staining solution to visualize lipid deposition. Histomorphological observations were conducted under 400x magnification via a CX31 light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), with digital image acquisition performed for documentation purposes.

Gut microbiota analysis

Genomic DNA isolation, library preparation, and high-throughput sequencing were carried out in accordance with established protocols from the Realbio Genomics Institute (Shanghai, China). PCR amplification of the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of microbial 16S rRNA genes was conducted with the dual-index primers 341 F (5’-CCTACGGGRSGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACVVGGGTATCTAATC-3’). Following quantification, amplicons were normalized and combined in equimolar ratios prior to sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., CA, USA). Adapter trimming was performed with cutadapt implemented in QIIME 2 (v2022.2). Cleaned sequences were processed through quality filtering, noise reduction, read assembly, and chimera elimination before they were subjected to taxonomic assignment through alignment with the Silva reference database (v138). The downstream analytical procedures utilized the generated taxonomic abundance matrix for comprehensive microbial community characterization.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Untargeted metabolomics aims to detect and identify a wide range of metabolites present in biological samples extensively, facilitating novel biomarker discovery and metabolic pathway exploration. Untargeted metabolic profiling was carried out via the XploreMET system (Metabo-Profile, Shanghai, China). Compound identification involved matching both chromatographic retention parameters and molecular fragmentation patterns against authenticated compounds in the JiaLib biochemical repository through the platform’s integrated analytical suite.

For the preparation of plasma samples, lipid components were extracted through a freeze‒thaw cycle followed by centrifugal separation. The isolated lipid fraction was homogenized with a deuterated internal standard and vortexed in a precooled methanol-chloroform solution (3:1 volumetric ratio). Subsequent centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 minutes at 4 °C enabled the collection of 200 μL aliquots of the organic phase in chromatographic vials. Solvent elimination was achieved through nitrogen evaporation, with residual material lyophilized prior to undergoing a 60-minute derivatization at 60 °C using the BSTFA reagent. Chromatographic separation was achieved through an Agilent 7890B GC system coupled to a high-resolution time‒of‒flight mass spectrometer (Leco Pegasus HT) operated in EI+ mode at 70 eV. Full-scan acquisition was used to monitor ions from m/z 50-−500 with a 5-spectra/sec acquisition rate. Metabolite identification was performed via XploreMET software (v3.0) through retention index alignment and spectral matching against the JiaLib reference library (2022 edition).

Liver samples were cryopreserved in a dry ice‒ethanol mixture prior to processing. Tissue aliquots (approximately 50 mg) were precisely measured and transferred to microcentrifuge tubes containing 25 mg of zirconium oxide beads (precooled), 10 µL of reference standard solution, and 50 µL of ice-cold 50% methanol for mechanical homogenization via a BB24 homogenizer (Next Advance, Inc., Averill Park, NY, USA). Following homogenization, the samples were centrifuged (14,000 × g, 20 minutes, 4 °C), and the clarified supernatant was subsequently collected for analysis. Organic solvent removal was achieved through sequential processing: chloroform evaporation using a CentriVap vacuum concentrator followed by lyophilization with a FreeZone freeze dryer equipped with specialized drying accessories.

Chemical derivatization and automated sample introduction were performed via a Gerstel MPS2 dual-head robotic workstation (Gerstel, Mühlheim, Germany). Analytical characterization was performed with an Agilent 7890B gas chromatography system coupled with a high-resolution time-of-flight mass spectrometer. The separation system utilized an Rxi-5 ms capillary column (30 m × 250 µm, 0.25 µm phase thickness; Restek Corporation, Bellefonte, PA, USA) with helium carrier gas maintained at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The thermal parameters included 270 °C injector/transfer line temperatures and a 220 °C ionization source temperature. Full-scan mass spectral data (m/z 50-−500) were acquired via electron impact ionization at 70 eV excitation energy.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data reflecting murine physiological and biochemical characteristics are expressed as the means ± SDs. Intergroup comparisons were evaluated through one-way ANOVA with subsequent Bonferroni’s post hoc analysis for multiple comparisons, where significant differences that reached the p < 0.05 threshold were deemed significant. All computational processes, including data processing and visualization, were executed through GraphPad Prism 8.0, which served dual purposes for both statistical computation and graphical presentation.

For gut microbiota assessment, normally distributed variables were analyzed with independent samples t tests, whereas nonparametric data were analyzed with the Mann‒Whitney U test. Rank sum tests were implemented to evaluate the alpha diversity metrics. Statistical comparisons between two experimental groups utilized the wilcox.test function in R, whereas multigroup analyses employed the kruskal.test methodology. Microbial community structure visualization was achieved through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) performed with R’s ade4 package, enabling multidimensional data pattern exploration.

Metabolomics investigations have employed two distinct categories of statistical approaches: 1) multivariate methods such as principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), along with machine learning techniques such as random forests and support vector machines; and 2) univariate techniques such as Student’s t test, the Mann‒Whitney‒Wilcoxon U test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and various correlation assessment methods.

Results

Effects of CSE on the physiological and biochemical parameters of FLD mice

Compared with those in the Ctrl group, the serum concentrations of TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C in the HFD group were notably greater (p < 0.05), indicating substantial dysregulation of lipid homeostasis. Histopathological evaluation through Oil Red O and H&E staining demonstrated substantial lipid droplet deposition in the hepatic tissues of model animals, validating successful FLD model induction. Subsequent therapeutic administration of CSE was implemented to evaluate its hepatoprotective potential. As shown in Fig. 1A, CSE administration effectively curbed HFD-associated weight gain throughout the experimental duration. Compared with the controls, the HFD cohort presented significantly greater body mass, whereas CSE-treated subjects presented substantial weight moderation. Following the 8-week therapeutic regimen, terminal body mass measurements revealed statistically significant reductions in the CSE group versus their HFD counterparts (Fig. 1B) (p < 0.05). Comparative analysis of nutritional intake revealed no intergroup variations in daily food consumption (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, CSE treatment significantly reduced the liver weight in FLD rats (Fig. 1D). The CSE protocol significantly attenuated the total body mass, incremental weight changes, and hepatic tissue mass parameters.

Effects of CSE on physiological parameters of FLD mice. (A) Body weight. (B) Final weight. (C) Food intake. (D) Liver weight. Data were presented as mean ± SEM (n = 8) **p < 0.01; *p < 0.05 versus the HFD group.

To evaluate the impact of CSE on lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses in HFD-fed mice, biochemical analyses of serum TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, AST, ALT, IL-6, TNF-α, and insulin concentrations were conducted (Fig. 2). Compared with the model cohort, the CSE-treated group presented marked reductions in TG, TC, LDL-C, and IL-6 concentrations (p < 0.05). Regarding liver function markers, serum ALT and AST activities showed no marked variations between groups. In contrast, CSE intervention significantly suppressed the levels of hepatic inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) and insulin (Fig. 3A-C). These findings indicate that CSE has dual therapeutic effects on mitigating hepatic inflammatory responses and enhancing lipid homeostasis in FLD models.

To assess the protective effect of CSE on hepatic morphology, histological analysis was performed using H&E and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 3D, E). H&E staining (400×) of the liver sections from the HFD cohort revealed severe macrovesicular steatosis characterized by large lipid droplets in hepatocytes (Fig. 3D, black arrows). Conversely, the CSE intervention group displayed a significant reduction in the size and number of intracellular lipid vacuoles. These morphological observations were further corroborated by Oil Red O staining (200×), where the HFD group exhibited dense, intensely red-stained intracellular lipid droplets. Consistent with the H&E results, CSE administration effectively attenuated the positive staining area, indicating a substantial reduction in neutral lipid accumulation.

Effect of CSE on the gut microbiome of FLD mice

Emerging research emphasizes gut microbiota regulation as a promising approach to mitigate FLD development. To investigate the potential effects of CSE on the intestinal microbial profiles of HFD-fed mice, fecal microbial characterization was conducted through genomic sequencing. Compared with the control, the HFD markedly diminished microbial diversity, as measured by the Chao1 index (a measure of species richness), which was effectively restored through CSE intervention. The treatment had no substantial effect on the HFD-induced increases in the Shannon and Simpson diversity metrics (Fig. 4A-C). UniFrac-based principal coordinate analysis demonstrated that CSE administration substantially modified the gut microbial architecture in HFD-fed subjects, resulting in clustering patterns that were more closely aligned with those of healthy controls (Fig. 4D). Phylum-level analysis revealed that HFD consumption decreased Bacteroidetes and Verrucomicrobia populations while increasing Firmicutes, Campylobacterota, and Deferribacteres proportions, with CSE particularly increasing Verrucomicrobia representation (Fig. 4E). The distribution of bacterial taxa at the genus level is presented in Fig. 4F. LEfSe analysis revealed significantly different bacteria at various taxonomic levels among the three groups (Fig. 4G), with color-coded nodes representing microbes that play pivotal roles in each group. The differentially abundant genera between the HFD and CSE groups included Akkermansia, Anaerotignum, Clostridium sensu stricto, Dysosmobacter, Ihubacter, Limosilactobacillus, Romboutsia, Saccharibacteria_genera_incertae_sedis, and Schaedlerella. CSE significantly increased Akkermansia abundance and decreased the abundance of other genera relative to those in the HFD group (Fig. 4H).

Effect of CSE on the serum metabolomic profiles of FLD mice

To investigate the metabolic pathways through which CSE alleviates FLD, untargeted metabolomics analysis of mouse serum was conducted. PLS-DA and OPLS-DA revealed clear separation among the Ctrl, HFD, and CSE groups, indicating significant differences in the serum metabolic profiles (Fig. 5A-C). The identified metabolites were categorized into organic acids, amino acids, carbohydrates, amines, fatty acids (FAs), steroids, alcohols, nucleotides, indoles, pyridines, lipids, lactones, amides, and vitamins (Fig. 5D). The major differentially abundant metabolites included amino acids, carbohydrates, FAs, indoles, nucleotides, and organic acids (Fig. 5E).

(E) Heatmap of major differential metabolites. (F) Volcano plot of Univariate Statistics. (G) Heatmap of differential metabolites. (H) Pathway analysis bubble plot. (I) Pathway analysis barplot.

Univariate analysis revealed distinct metabolic variations distinguishing the HFD and CSE cohorts. These variations were visually represented through a volcano plot, where crimson data points denoted metabolites elevated in the CSE cohort relative to HFD controls, whereas azure markers highlighted reduced compounds. Notable increases were observed in multiple metabolites, including epsilon-caprolactam, dihydroxyacetone, xylulose, fructose, ribitol, xylitol, N-acetylmannosamine, glycolic acid, and sorbitol, after CSE administration. In contrast, glutaric acid emerged as the sole metabolite demonstrating decreased levels among the identified differential compounds (Fig. 5F-G).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis additionally revealed the metabolic networks affected by CSE exposure. The results demonstrated that CSE potentially influences biological processes through multiple interconnected pathways, including pentose/glucuronate interconversion systems, amino-nucleotide sugar metabolic regulation, glyoxylate‒dicarboxylate balance mechanisms, glycerolipid homeostasis pathways, starch‒sucrose conversion processes, and fructose‒mannose metabolic cycles (Fig. 5H‒I).

Effect of CSE on the hepatic metabolomic profiles of FLD mice

To further explore the effects of CSE on hepatic metabolism in FLD mice, untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed on liver tissue. The PLS-DA results revealed distinct clustering among the Ctrl, HFD, and CSE groups, indicating significant differences in hepatic metabolic profiles (Fig. 6A). A total of 170 metabolites were identified in liver tissue and categorized into 24 groups, including amino acids, carbohydrates, organic acids, fatty acids, peptides, nucleotides, vitamins, amines, steroids, inorganic oxides, alcohols, lipids, and indoles (Fig. 6B).

Spearman correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore the potential relationships between key bacterial genera and FLD-associated indicators (Fig. 7). The abundance of Akkermansia was negatively correlated with body weight and hepatic TNF-α, serum TG, and serum IL-6 levels in FLD mice, whereas most other differential genera presented positive correlations with these FLD markers. In the analysis of the serum metabolites and FLD indicators, the serum IL-6 and hepatic insulin levels showed the strongest associations with the differentially abundant metabolites. Specifically, the abundances of glycolic acid, N-acetylmannosamine, xylitol, and xylulose were negatively correlated with the serum IL-6 concentration (p < 0.05), whereas the levels of glycolic acid and ribitol were negatively correlated with the hepatic insulin concentration (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the epsilon-caprolactam concentration was negatively correlated with the serum TC, LDL-C, and hepatic TNF-α concentrations (p < 0.05). Among the differential hepatic metabolites, hypotaurine was positively correlated with body weight, hepatic TNF-α, serum TC, serum LDL-C, and hepatic IL-6 (p < 0.05). Taurine levels were positively correlated with body weight, serum IL-6, and hepatic IL-6 levels (p < 0.05), whereas adenine levels were positively correlated with serum TC and LDL-C levels (p < 0.05). Additionally, the abundance of kynurenine was positively correlated with the serum IL-6 and hepatic TNF-α levels (p < 0.05). In contrast, glutamic acid abundance was negatively correlated with body weight, serum IL-6, and hepatic insulin levels (p < 0.05), whereas glutathione abundance was negatively correlated with body weight and serum IL-6 levels (p < 0.05).

(C) Correlation analysis between hepatic metabolites and FLD indicators. (D) Correlation between the serum differential metabolites and key bacterial genera. In-depth examination of the associations between serum and liver metabolite variations and pivotal microbial taxa revealed that Akkermansia exhibited inverse relationships with numerous bacterial genera. Significantly, serum components, including epsilon-caprolactam, glycolic acid, xylitol, and sorbitol, along with hepatic glutamic acid and glutathione, were positively associated with Akkermansia abundance (p < 0.05). Considering Akkermansia’s inverse correlation with FLD severity indicators, these biochemical compounds might exert protective effects against pathological development. Consequently, we propose that intestinal Akkermansia populations, combined with identified serum metabolites (epsilon-caprolactam, glycolate, xylitol, and sorbitol) and hepatic components (glutamic acid and glutathione), may function as critical biochemical intermediaries in the therapeutic mechanisms of CSE.

Discussion

Despite the documented bioactive properties of CS in metabolic regulation, its specific efficacy and underlying mechanisms in FLD have not been fully elucidated12,19–23. Our study demonstrates that CSE effectively ameliorates hepatic lipid accumulation and metabolic dysfunction in HFD-induced murine models. Specifically, we observed that CSE-mediated improvements are closely associated with the restoration of the microbiota-metabolite-disease axis. These findings provide a novel perspective on utilizing CS as a functional food for hepatic health.

In this study, CSE treatment effectively restored lipid homeostasis and suppressed systemic and hepatic inflammation in HFD-fed mice. While hepatic TG accumulation primarily stems from non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA) uptake and accelerated de novo lipogenesis (DNL), our findings suggest that CSE targets these pathways to recalibrate intrahepatic TG balance24. Notably, the observed reduction in hepatic insulin levels by CSE indicates an attenuation of insulin-driven lipogenesis, a critical factor given that disrupted insulin signaling often fuels DNL in FLD25. Furthermore, our results align with the ‘multiple parallel hits’ hypothesis, where CSE appears to intercept the progression from simple steatosis to hepatitis by modulating the inflammatory cascade26,27. By reducing proinflammatory mediators, CSE addresses the temporal co-occurrence of lipid deposition and inflammatory activation, reinforcing anti-inflammatory intervention as a potent strategy for managing metabolic liver dysfunction28.

The gut microbiome is a pivotal regulator of metabolic homeostasis, and its dysbiosis is intrinsically linked to the progression of FLD29. In this study, CSE administration significantly reshaped the microbial landscape, characterized by a marked enrichment of Verrucomicrobia, particularly the genus Akkermansia (Fig. 4). As a next-generation probiotic, Akkermansia plays a multi-faceted role in mitigating liver steatosis, potentially through the activation of intestinal FXR signaling, restoration of the mucosal barrier, and modulation of hepatic macrophage polarization30–33.

Our results support the ‘competing guilds’ model, which suggests that dietary interventions drive the stratification of microbial communities into antagonistic functional clusters34. We observed that CSE-induced Akkermansia expansion was negatively correlated with eight detrimental genera, including Clostridium sensu stricto and Romboutsia (Fig. 7), aligning with established patterns of microbial competition35–37. This suggests that CSE-derived polysaccharides act as selective prebiotics, fostering a beneficial guild centered on Akkermansia that suppresses pathobionts. This microbial restructuring likely serves as a primary mechanism by which CSE alleviates HFD-induced metabolic stress, positioning CSE as a potent prebiotic candidate for hepatic health.

Metabolomic profiling revealed that CSE induces significant metabolic rewiring in both serum and hepatic tissues. Specifically, the modulation of pentose and glucuronate interconversions serves as a metabolic hallmark of CSE intervention, which has been previously linked to reduced lipid accumulation and improved steatosis38,39. Beyond carbohydrate metabolism, a core finding of our study is the significant regulation of taurine and primary bile acid biosynthesis.

As critical signaling molecules, bile acids regulate lipid and glucose homeostasis by activating the FXR. CSE-induced enrichment of taurine—a key conjugate for bile acid synthesis—likely facilitates the activation of the intestinal FXR-FGF15 signaling axis, thereby suppressing hepatic lipogenesis and enhancing fatty acid β-oxidation40,41. This finding is particularly significant when integrated with our microbiota data, as Akkermansia is a known modulator of bile acid metabolism.

Furthermore, CSE appears to bolster the hepatic antioxidant defense system. The observed increase in glutamic acid and glutathione suggests that CSE mitigates ROS-driven metabolic reprogramming42–45. By replenishing the glutathione pool, CSE intercepts the ‘multiple hits’ that drive the transition from simple steatosis to more severe liver injury. Collectively, these metabolic shifts demonstrate that CSE acts as a multi-target modulator, restoring hepatic health through the integrated regulation of bile acid signaling and redox balance.

Limitations and future research

Despite these insights, certain limitations remain. First, while we identified the involvement of the FXR-FGF15 axis, future studies employing FXR-deficient models are necessary to confirm the indispensable role of this pathway. Second, although CSE demonstrates robust efficacy as a crude extract, further fractionation is required to pinpoint the specific bioactive polysaccharides or metabolites responsible for these effects. Future studies should also incorporate a positive control to quantitatively compare the potency of CSE with conventional therapies. Finally, clinical trials are warranted to validate the translational potential of CSE in human patients with metabolic dysfunction.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that CSE effectively ameliorates HFD-induced MASLD by restructuring the gut microbiota and reprogramming hepatic metabolism. Our findings highlight that CSE fosters a beneficial “competing guild” dominated by Akkermansia, which synergistically interacts with the bile acid-FXR signaling axis and glutathione-mediated antioxidant pathways to restore lipid homeostasis. These results provide a mechanistic foundation for utilizing corn silk as a functional food or prebiotic intervention for metabolic health.
