The forgotten population: Early‐onset Alzheimer's in people with Down syndrome
Authors: Michelle Cristina Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent decades, Alzheimer's disease has increasingly been discussed through narratives of prevention, resilience, and delayed onset. For individuals with Down syndrome, however, Alzheimer's disease is not a distant possibility, but an almost predictable consequence of increased longevity. The substantial rise in life expectancy in this population represents one of the greatest achievements of modern medicine. At the same time, it has exposed a profound biological vulnerability, as most individuals with Down syndrome will develop Alzheimer's disease‐related neuropathology decades earlier than the general population. Although Down syndrome constitutes the most common genetic cause of Alzheimer's disease, these individuals remain largely excluded from prevention strategies, clinical trials, and global dementia agendas. This Viewpoint argues that such persistent invisibility does not reflect a lack of scientific relevance, but rather a systemic failure to align biological certainty with ethical responsibility. By highlighting the unique trajectory of Alzheimer's disease in Down syndrome, as well as the social, familial, and caregiving implications of early cognitive decline, this article calls for a necessary shift in perspective: from longevity celebrated as an endpoint to longevity recognized as an obligation to ensure inclusion, dignity, and meaningful support. If efforts to confront Alzheimer's disease are to be genuine, they must begin by including those who have lived with its burden the longest, often in silence.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid precursor protein, biomarkers, dementia prevention, down syndrome, ethics in research, inclusion

In recent decades, discussions surrounding Alzheimer's disease have become almost synonymous with hope. Studies on diet, physical activity, and cognitive training continue to multiply, each promising to delay what feels inevitable. Yet there is one group for whom Alzheimer's disease is not a distant possibility, it is an almost predetermined consequence. These are individuals with Down syndrome, a population now living longer than ever, yet still largely invisible in the global conversation on dementia. The increase in life expectancy among people with Down syndrome stands as one of modern medicine's great achievements. In the 1950s, few lived beyond the age of 25; today, in many countries, life expectancy exceeds 60 years. 1 This progress, however, has come with a biological cost. Up to 90% of adults with Down syndrome develop the neuropathological hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease after the age of 40, and approximately 70% go on to develop clinical symptoms consistent with Alzheimer's disease, related dementia before the age of 60. 2 , 3 The explanation lies in chromosome 21, which carries the APP gene (amyloid precursor protein). The presence of three copies of this gene results in increased β‐amyloid production from early life, favoring abnormal accumulation and premature neurodegeneration. 3 Evidence from the Alzheimer's Biomarker Consortium‐Down Syndrome (ABC‐DS) indicates that β‐amyloid and tau deposition may begin as early as the third decade of life, 4 a period during which many individuals with Down syndrome are studying, working, and socially active. Long before clinical symptoms emerge, mitochondrial dysfunction, persistent neuroinflammation, and alterations in cholinergic and dopaminergic systems are already evident, creating a biological environment conducive to neuronal decline. 5 This singular timeline holds considerable potential for understanding the origins and progression of Alzheimer's disease, yet it continues to occupy a marginal place in the global research agenda. It is important to acknowledge that progress is being made. Initiatives such as the NIH INCLUDE Project 6 and the Trial‐Ready Cohort‐Down Syndrome 7 , 8 have expanded the infrastructure required to include individuals with Down syndrome in observational studies and clinical trials, while longitudinal consortia have refined biomarker‐based approaches for prevention and treatment research. Even so, access remains uneven, and the broader prevention discourse rarely engages meaningfully with families living with intellectual disability or with the systems responsible for supporting them. While large prevention initiatives, such as the FINGER and SMARRT studies, demonstrate how lifestyle interventions may preserve cognitive function, little has been done to adapt these strategies, in a systematic way, to people with intellectual disabilities. 9 , 10 Many face persistent barriers: limited access to structured physical activity programs, a lack of tailored public policies, and recurrent exclusion from research opportunities. Recommendations regarding physical activity, diet, and cognitive engagement seldom account for their lived realities, rendering the prevention narrative incomplete and, in many respects, unfair. Moreover, living better cannot be reduced to preserving cognition alone. For families, Alzheimer's disease in Down syndrome profoundly reshapes daily life: care demands increase, stress accumulates, communication becomes more complex, and social support often erodes. There is both space and urgency for programs that reduce caregiver burden, strengthen psychosocial support, safely adapt meaningful activities as abilities change, and guide diagnosis disclosure and future planning in ways that preserve dignity rather than compromise it. Down syndrome represents the most common genetic cause of Alzheimer's disease, 3 , 5 and research that includes this population generates knowledge relevant to early‐onset Alzheimer's disease, with benefits extending to other groups. Yet scientific visibility continues to lag behind biological certainty. Each additional decade of life gained by this population is not only a triumph of medicine, but also a missed opportunity to understand the disease before typical aging obscures its mechanisms. To overlook this group is to forgo one of the most informative natural windows into Alzheimer's disease. Living longer should mean living better. For many people with Down syndrome, however, longevity has arrived alongside a new form of forgetting, not neurological, but scientific. If we are truly committed to confronting Alzheimer's disease, we must begin by including those who have been living with it the longest, often in silence.
