A Whole Exome Sequencing and Targeted Gene Panel Approach to Identify Rare Etiologies of Pediatric Nephrotic Syndrome in Oman
Authors: Mohamed S Al Riyami, Intisar Al Alawi, Anisa Al Maskari, Badria Al Gaithi, Naifain Al Kalbani, Suliman Al Saidi, Joshua Pillai, Matthew Sampson, Zainab Al Hashmi, John A Sayer
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Nephrotic syndrome is the most common glomerular disorder in both children and adults, defined by heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, and generalized edema.1 It is classified into steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome and steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS).2 SRNS is genetically diverse and can occur as part of syndromic diseases or present as isolated proteinuria or nephrotic syndrome.S1–S5 Whole exome sequencing is a powerful tool for identifying monogenic causes of SRNS and the majority of cases may be solved with variants in NPHS1 and NPHS2.3,4 We describe the identification of additional genetic variants in Omani children with SRNS beyond NPHS1 and NPHS2 genetic variants. A full description is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

The study included 20 pediatric patients with SRNS with various histological appearances (Figure 1). The genetic analysis identified a variety of variants in genes associated with nephrotic syndrome (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2) and some with extra-kidney manifestations (Supplementary Table S3). This included biallelic variants in 9 autosomal recessive genes (WDR4, CD2AP, DGKE, SMARCAL1, CD151, PLCE1, MYO1E, LAMB2, NUP93) which were consistent with the clinical phenotype and pattern of inheritance (Figure 1). We also identified monoallelic variants in 3 autosomal dominant genes (LMX1B, TRPC6, and WT1) which were consistent with clinical phenotypes and pattern of inheritance (Figure 1). Molecular modelling of alleles provided data on variant pathogenicity (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).

(b) Distribution of autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant genetic causes of SRNS.

At the most recent follow-up, 20% of patients preserved kidney function, 5% had chronic kidney disease stage II, whereas 50% had reached kidney failure (6 treated with peritoneal dialysis, 2 treated with hemodialysis, and 2 treated with kidney transplantation). The cohort had significant mortality with 25% of patients dying within the follow-up period. The mean follow-up duration was 4.75 years.

This study highlights the substantial genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity underlying SRNS in Omani children, a known highly consanguineous population, beyond variants in NPHS1 and NPHS2. Early molecular diagnosis of SRNS is vital and has important implications for prognosis, family counselling, surveillance for associated complications, and the future implementation of precision and gene-targeted therapies in SRNS.
