Altering the carbohydrate-binding specificity of the legume lectin FRIL through structure-guided engineering
Authors: Yo-Min Liu, Hong Thuy Vy Nguyen, Xiaorui Chen, Md. Shahed-Al-Mahmud, Ting-Hua Chen, Kuo-Shiang Liao, Jennifer M. Lo, Tzu-Chun Kan, Chien-Tai Ren, Che Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
FRIL is a legume lectin from the hyacinth bean that has broad-spectrum antiviral activity. A distinctive trait of FRIL among similar mannose/glucose-specific legume lectins is that FRIL shows specificity for complex type N-glycans. We postulate that an extended binding site on FRIL facilitates this ligand selectivity. Here, we show legume lectin carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) loop B is the main determinant of complex versus high-mannose N-glycan specificity in FRIL and Concanavalin A (ConA), respectively. First, we find that the inactive precursors of recombinant FRIL (rFRIL) and proConA (rproConA) can be activated via deglycosylation. Secondly, the cryo-EM structures of inactive apo rFRIL, active FRIL in complex with Galβ1,4-(Fucα1,3-)GlcNAcβ1,2-Man tetrasaccharide, and active rFRIL in complex with Mannose9GlcNAc2 (Man9) N-glycan are determined, and residues H102 and Y101 on loop B are identified as crucial for complex glycan recognition. Finally, we swapped loop B residues 101 and 102 alongside loop C residue 145 on FRIL to their structural equivalent on ConA, resulting in a FRIL mutant that binds exclusively to high mannose N-glycans. Taken together, we have established a process of activating recombinant FRIL and related lectins through deglycosylation, and demonstrated the crucial role that loop B residues play in establishing oligosaccharide specificity.

Introduction

Legume lectins represent one of the largest families of lectins in nature, with over a hundred members displaying a wide range of glycan specificities. They are categorized as L-type lectins, which extend to other structurally similar lectins found in species outside the Fabaceae family. An array of biomedical properties has been reported in legume lectins, including anti-microbial, anti-cancer and immunomodulation1. Moreover, due to their ability to recognize core structural motifs on N-glycans that antibodies are less-equipped to distinguish, legume lectins remain power tools in the realm of glycan identification, glycoprotein purification, and characterization of cell surface glycoconjugates2. The legume lectin Flt3 Receptor Interacting Lectin (FRIL) from hyacinth bean (Lablab purpureus), also known as Dolichos lablab lectin, was found to preserve stem cells in suspension culture3,4, mitigate tumor neoangiogenesis5, and possess anti-influenza, anti-HSV and anti-SARS-CoV-2 properties6,7.

Legume lectins are produced as single-chained glycoprotein precursors and undergo extensive post-translational modifications to attain glycan-binding activity8,9. In the case of the prototypical legume lectin ConA (Supplementary Fig. 1), a “circular permutation” process occurs where the glycosylated linker connecting α and β subunits of a pro-lectin is excised by the vacuole enzyme asparagine endopeptidase (AEP), and the N- and C-terminals of the two chains are then joined together in a transpeptidation event catalyzed by the same enzyme10. For FRIL (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1), only one AEP cutting site exists on the glycosylated linker, and a single cut is made near the β subunit. The still-attached (and still-glycosylated) linker is then differentially digested by other carboxypeptidases in the vacuole, resulting in four variants of the α subunit3,11. Unlike ConA, circular permutation does not occur in FRIL, resulting in a “two-chain” lectin. Both FRIL and ConA belong to a group of D-mannose/D-glucose-specific legume lectins12,13. The structures of FRIL and ConA in complex with a trimannose has been determined previously, and residues that make up FRIL and ConA’s monosaccharide binding site are largely conserved14,15. This includes 6 residues distributed among four loops that make up FRIL and ConA’s CRD: Asp86 (FRIL numbering, ConA Asp208 due to circular permutation) on loop A, Gly103 and Gly104 (ConA Gly227 and Arg228) on loop B, Asn144 (ConA Asn14) on loop C, Gln229 and Asp230 (ConA Leu99 and Tyr100) on loop D. Furthermore, Tyr142 (ConA Tyr12) forms CH-pi stacking with mannose, and H102 (ConA Thr226) forms water bridges with the O2 of mannose16. Despite similar monosaccharide preferences, FRIL and ConA’s binding affinity to N-glycans differ significantly. ConA binds strongly to oligomannose N-glycans while FRIL prefers complex N-glycans6,17. This difference may be attributed to variations in their extended binding sites, which are residues outside the conserved monosaccharide binding site that stabilize larger oligosaccharides16. However, as far as we know, no structure of ConA or FRIL binding to more complex oligosaccharides (such as N-glycans) have been resolved, and FRIL’s extended binding site remains unknown.

Changes in the binding specificity of lectins can have far-reaching consequences. For example, a fundamental basis of inter-species transmission of influenza virus is mutations on the viral attachment protein hemagglutinin (HA), which functions as a lectin interacting with host cell sialic acid glycans. Tremendous efforts have been made to understand the HA mutations needed to shift from avian-type to human-type glycan structures (terminal 2,3-linked sialic acid to 2,6-linked sialic acid, respectively)18, and lectin-glycan interactions on biologically relevant glycans in general19. Legume lectins present promising candidates to explore these interactions, as they consist of a large family of structurally homologous proteins with diverse glycan recognition, including D-mannose/D-glucose, D-galactose/N-acetyl-D-galactosamine (GalNAc) and L-fucose16. However, progress has been hampered by difficulty in obtaining active recombinant legume lectins from two-chain legume lectins and a lack of comprehensive knowledge of extended binding site interactions in these proteins.

We therefore set out to determine the structure of FRIL in complex with a tetrasaccharide representative of complex type N-glycans, and create a recombinant version of FRIL for mutagenesis analysis on the interacting residues identified in our structure. During the course of the alanine mutagenesis, we unexpectedly found two mutants that did not exhibit a loss of binding, but a loss of glycan specificity. Structural comparison between FRIL and ConA showed that the position these two mutants occupy on loop B was important in differentiating between different N-glycans. Taking this a step further, we then attempted to swap residues on loop B in order to switch glycan specificity. The overall goal is to gain structural insight into the diverse glycan recognition properties of legume lectins.

Results

Production of bioactive recombinant FRIL and recombinant proConA, and activation by deglycosylation

In order to explore the lectin-glycan interactions between FRIL and its ligand, we first attempt to overcome the challenge of producing recombinant two-chain and circular permutated legume lectins, which has been a major obstacle in the study of these agents for years9. Previously, we had struggled to express recombinant FRIL in Escherichia coli BL21 and Pichia pastoris, but this resulted in poor expression levels and misfolding6. In this study, we expressed recombinant FRIL (rFRIL) residues 1-253, with a C-terminal His6-tag and Brisbane HA signal peptide (Supplementary Data 1), in HEK 293-EBNA (293E) mammalian cell suspension culture. The 293E expression system yielded approximately 4 ~ 5 mg of secreted glycosylated rFRIL protein per liter of culture medium after Ni-NTA purification. SEC-MALS (Supplementary Fig. 2g) suggests that our rFRIL proteins formed tetramers similar to native FRIL (nFRIL) purified from Lablab purpureus seeds, indicating proper folding. However, these recombinant lectins showed very little glycan binding activity in hemagglutination assay (glycosylated rFRIL 0.17 HAU/μg compared to nFRIL 2.67 HAU/μg, Supplementary Fig. 2j) or lectin binding ELISA with glycoprotein coatings (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 2i).

To find out why our glycosylated rFRIL has no activity, we employed cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) to take a closer look at our expressed protein in its apo form, without the addition of carbohydrate ligands (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4 and Supplementary Table 1). The glycosylated rFRIL is revealed to be in a tetrameric structure with each monomer exhibiting the typical jellyroll motif of nFRIL and other legume lectins, indicating correct folding of the recombinant protein. But surprisingly, an extra density map was observed in the rFRIL’s CRD despite no ligand provided (Supplementary Fig. 4a). This density map allowed us to model three saccharides of GlcNAcβ1,2-Manα1,6-Man, in which a disaccharide GlcNAcβ1,2-Man is more confidently positioned with a clear density map (Supplementary Fig. 4a). This unexpected ligand observed in a rFRIL is most likely from the self-glycan on the inter-subunit linker, which further supports Sheldon et al’s20 hypothesis that the glycosylated linker between a legume lectin’s α and β subunits acts as a self-neutralizing ligand for the lectin’s CRD.

We attempted three strategies to release the inactive FRIL from its self-ligand: mutational removal of N-glycosites on the linker, proteolytic digestion of the linker, and enzymatic deglycosylation of linker N-glycans. In the first strategy N116/117Q, N123Q and N116/117/123Q (QQQ) mutants were created, but we observed decreased expression levels and significant retention of protein inside the cell, pointing to a lack of proper protein folding (Supplementary Fig. 2k). For proteolytic digestion we used trypsin to cleave the linker at Lys128 instead of naturally-occurring AEP cleavage at Asn113 or Asn116, but this failed to activate the lectin (Supplementary Fig. 2h–j). Finally, following enzymatic deglycosylation treatment with PNGase F (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2h), we were able to obtain active rFRIL with similar hemagglutination (Supplementary Fig. 2j) and ELISA activity (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 2f) as native FRIL. This indicates that deglycosylation is responsible for FRIL activation.

After acquiring bioactive rFRIL, we wondered whether this process could be applied to other legume lectins. To test this out, we expressed recombinant proConcanavalin A (rproConA) in 293E mammalian cell suspension culture (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Expressed rproConA had a comparable yield as rFRIL, in the 4 ~ 5 mg/L range, with very little initial activity. Deglycosylation via PNGase F treatment activated rproConA in a similar way as rFRIL (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). However, without AEP-mediated circular permutation and stabilization of dimer-dimer interface contacts in mature ConA, the bioactive rproConA remained a dimer rather than a tetramer at neutral pH, exhibiting a lower molecular weight and lowered hemagglutination activity (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). At acidic pH, rproConA reverted to a tetramer, consistent with previous reports21. Nevertheless, we have established a reliable and consistent method for the production of two-chain and circular permutated legume lectins using a mammalian expression system followed by an enzymatic deglycosylation treatment, allowing further exploration into FRIL’s interactions with complex type N-glycans.

Identification of FRIL’s extended binding site residues

FRIL is reported to be a glucose/mannose lectin that binds to complex type N-glycans6,12. Previously solved crystal structure of FRIL in complex with trimannose14 (M3M6M, 1QMO) only shows density for a single mannose in the CRD with no extended binding site interactions, but oligosaccharides more representative of complex glycans have not been attempted. Therefore, we solved the X-ray crystal and cryo-EM structures of native FRIL complexed with a Galβ1,4-(Fucα1,3-)GlcNAcβ1,2-Man tetrasaccharide (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Tables 1, 2), an antennary Lewis X branch that is found on complex N-glycans22. Overall, our X-ray crystal and cryo-EM structures are consistent with the previously solved tetrameric structure of the native FRIL protein. In the crystal structure, there are sixteen monomers in one asymmetric unit, forming four tetramers (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Each monomer contains a single bound ligand in an identical binding mode, with the Fo-Fc map showing an unambiguous density for each atom of the ligand (Supplementary Fig. 5c–f). The crystal structure also showed a high consistency with the cryo-EM structure of the tetrasaccharide-bound nFRIL at the resolution of 2.4 Å (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 1). The tetrasaccharide ligand interacts with the FRIL protein mainly via hydrogen bonds in all structures. The core mannose (Man) in the tetrasaccharide adopts a similar conformation with previous solved monosaccharide bound nFRIL’s structure14, involving previously-known interactions with monosaccharide binding residues Asp86 (Asp208 in ConA numbering), Asn144 (ConA Asn14), Gly104 (ConA Arg228), Tyr142 (ConA Tyr12), and backbone interactions with residues Gln229 (ConA Leu99) and Asp230 (ConA Tyr100) (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 6b). However, in our tetrasaccharide bound FRIL structure, additional extended binding site interactions with N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and fucose (Fuc) were revealed. The Oγ of Ser45 contacts both the GlcNAc amide oxygen and the Fuc 2-OH; His102 forms hydrogen bonds with the Fuc 2-OH and GlcNAc 3-OH via its imidazole Nε2. His102 also forms hydrogen bonds with the GlcNAc 6-OH via its backbone carbonyl oxygen and amine nitrogen mediated by a water molecule. Our tetrasaccharide-bound structures suggest that all residues interacting with the β1,2-linked mannose in FRIL’s monosaccharide binding site fall within the four classical loops responsible for the architecture of legume lectin CRD (Asp86 on loop A, Gly103 and Gly104 on loop B, Tyr142, Tyr144 and Tyr147 on loop C, Gly228, Gln229 and Asp230 on loop D). Extended binding site interactions include another two residues (His102 and Tyr101) on loop B adjacent to the monosaccharide binding site, and Ser45 which falls outside the four classical loops (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 6a). To verify our structural observations, we performed alanine mutagenesis on Asp86, Gly103, Gly104, Tyr142, Asn144, Tyr147 and Asp230 of FRIL’s monosaccharide binding site, and Ser45, Tyr101, His102 of FRIL’s extended binding site (Supplementary Fig. 6c). These mutant’s N-glycan binding ability was analyzed by performing lectin binding ELISA with the glycoprotein A/California/07/2009 hemagglutinin (Cali09 HA), which contains both complex and high mannose N-glycans (Fig. 2c). Results show a loss in FRIL’s glycan binding ability when monosaccharide binding site residues Asp86, Asn144, Tyr147 and Tyr142 were replaced with alanine. G104A and D230Y mutants remain active due to those residues having backbone interactions with mannose (Fig. 2a). Of the residues that comprise FRIL’s extended binding site, S45A showed no binding on Cali09 HA ELISA, but interestingly, Y101A (EC50 7.08 ng/ml) and H102A (EC50 3.64 ng/ml) had slightly increased binding compared to wild type control (EC50 9.16 ng/ml), despite presumably losing the Tyr101 CH-pi interaction with galactose and His102 hydrogen bonds with GlcNAc and fucose.

Structural-based alignment shows that most other legume lectins have small hydrophilic residues in the Tyr101 His102 positions (ConA Ser225 Thr226) (Supplementary Fig. 6a). This raises the possibility that by replacing the bulky side chains of tyrosine and histidine with a smaller alanine, we had somehow altered rFRIL’s binding specificity instead of negating it. To test this hypothesis, we created five Cali09 HA proteins with different N-glycans, including a “fully glycosylated” (FG) HA with mostly complex type but also some high mannose N-glycans, an endoglycosidase H (EndoH) digested “complex-only” HA (CX) HA, a kifunensine-treated Man9-only (M9) HA, Mannose5GlcNAc2 (M5) HA from GnTI-/- cells, and an HA where each glycosite has only one GlcNAc remaining, dubbed “mono-GlcNAc” HA (MG) (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). These HAs were then coated onto plates for ELISA detection by Y101A and H102A mutants (Fig. 2e). As expected, rFRIL wild type (rFRIL-wt) displayed the strongest binding towards FG and CX-glycosylated HAs (EC50 7.4 ng/ml and 16.7 ng/ml, respectively), with relatively low affinity towards M5 and M9 (EC50 3218 ng/ml and 229.7 ng/ml, respectively). In contrast, Y101A and H102A displayed similarly strong binding towards FG, CX and M9 (EC50 of 6.8, 15.8 and 5.9 ng/ml in Y101A, 3.6, 7.1 and 3.4 ng/ml in H102A), indicating the lectin itself is active, but binding specificity for complex glycans has been lost. This result is confirmed by the glycan array (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Data 2), where Y101A and H102A showed a strong preference towards high mannose type glycans. Therefore, we have identified residues in FRIL’s extended binding site responsible for the lectin’s interactions with complex type N-glycans. Two of these residues, Tyr101 and His102, appear crucial for FRIL’s glycan binding specificity and warrant further investigation.

Structure-guided conversion of rFRIL’s specificity towards oligomannose glycans

Due to the surprising loss of glycan binding specificity observed in rFRIL mutants Y101A and H102A, a more thorough exploration of FRIL’s extended binding site was performed. First, we examined the cryo-EM structure of rFRIL-wt in complex with a Man9 N-glycan (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). Density for the entire Man9 ligand could not be fully visualized in rFRIL-wt’s CRD, but extra clear density map at the ligand site with a resolution of 2.4 Å allowed us to build two α1,3 linked mannoses in (Manα1,3-Man, Supplementary Fig. 4b). Not seeing the entire oligomannose ligand in our cryo-EM structure is expected, as rFRIL-wt has comparably weak binding towards Man9 but strong binding to complex N-glycans (Fig. 2e). The two mannoses we see likely correspond to the α1,3 branching points on either D1 or D2 arm of Man9. The non-reducing end of the primary mannose binds to FRIL in an almost identical conformation as the mannose present in our rFRIL-wt-tetrasaccharide structure, suggesting that FRIL’s Glc/Man-recognizing monosaccharide binding site binds to the same mannose position on both complex type and high mannose N-glycans. With a monosaccharide bind site recognizing the same sugar on both oligosaccharides, glycan specificity is likely determined by the extended binding site. Indeed, in contrast to our tetrasaccharide-bound FRIL structures where GlcNAc, Gal and Fuc come into contact with loop B residues 101-104, the density map for Man9 complexed with rFRIL-wt shows no further α1,2 mannose extensions of the D1/D2 branch beyond the core α1,3-linked mannose (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). A sequence alignment of FRIL loop B (101 ~ 104, ConA 223 ~ 228) with the loop B of other related legume lectins (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 6a) reveals that Tyr101-His102 on FRIL loop B is replaced by Ser223-Gly224-Ser225-X226, with X being a small side chain such as threonine (in ConA, high mannose specificity), glycine (in Dioclea grandiflora lectin DGL, high mannose specificity17), or alanine (in Platypodium elegans lectin PELa, asymmetrical complex type specificity23). From this alignment, we speculate that we can shift FRIL’s specificity away from complex N-glycans and towards high mannose N-glycans by conducting mutagenesis on loop B. First, we introduced single mutations H102T, Y101S and the insertion of a glycine and serine residue between 101 and 102 (101insGS102) to make the peptide chain longer. The binding affinity of these mutants was then analyzed by lectin binding ELISA with differently glycosylated HAs (FG, CX, M9 and M5) (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 3). Y101S and 101insGS102 showed increased binding towards M9 and M5 HAs without losing their affinity to CX HA, while H102T had decreased binding across the board. Next, we combined these three mutants to create 101insGS-H102T and Y101S-insGS-H102T mutants, which retained their binding towards M9 and M5 while experiencing a 5-fold decrease in their CX affinity (EC50 5.04 ng/ml in Y101S to 25.59 ng/ml in Y101S-insGS-H102T). This puts our rFRIL mutants in the vicinity of rproConA, but still does not completely negate binding to CX glycans. Therefore, we introduced a P145T mutation (ConA Thr15) that in previous reports corresponds to ConA’s extended binding site in trimannoside complexes14,15. Adding P145T to our triple mutant to create P145T Y101SinsGSH102T decreased binding to CX by another 16-fold (EC50 25.59 ng/ml to 398.1 ng/ml), while affinity towards Man9 was retained (EC50 2.63 ng/ml to 3.19 ng/ml) (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 3). We re-named this final mutant, with a 24-fold decrease in binding to CX glycans and a 72-fold increase in Man9 glycan compared to rFRIL-wt, as oligomannose-specific FRIL (FRIL-OMS). Bio-layer interferometry using FG, CX and M9 HAs as ligand confirms that FRIL-OMS has decreased response and larger Kd to CX HA compared to rFRIL-wt, and an increased response and smaller Kd to M9 HA (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 8a). Carbohydrate competition lectin binding ELISA also shows that FRIL-OMS but not rFRIL-wt was inhibited by yeast mannan (IC50 0.07 mg/ml), an oligomannose polysaccharide containing α1,3, α1,6 and α1,2-linked mannose (Supplementary Fig. 8b).

Consequently, we have engineered a FRIL mutant (FRIL-OMS) that specifically recognizes oligomannose rather than its native ligand of complex type N-glycan, by replacing FRIL residues responsible for complex type binding with their structural equivalents on related, high mannose binding lectins.

Structural and anti-viral activity of FRIL-OMS compared to rFRIL-wt

To confirm that FRIL-OMS preferentially binds to oligomannose and the biological implications that this entails, we first determined the cryo-EM structure of the mutant in complex with Man9 (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). We found that the mutation FRIL-OMS did not affect the protein tetramerization or the overall structure (RMSD ~ 0.28 Å from the wild type). A clear extra density was observed at the active site that allows us to model three mannose inside, Manα1,2-Manα1,3-Man (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Consistent with previous findings, the middle mannose in our FRIL-OMS structure has almost identical conformation with the core mannose in either the monosaccharide-bound, oligomannose-bound, or the tetrasaccharide-bound FRIL wild type. The only structural difference observed between FRIL-OMS and wildtype is a displacement of ~ 5 Å in loop B at the mutation site (Figs. 4b, 5). The mutation of rFRIL-wt’s Tyr101-His102 (YH) to Ser101-Gly101-Ser101-Thr102 (SGST) in FRIL-OMS resulted in a longer loop B, closing off a “gap” in the outer rim of FRIL’s binding pocket that Galβ1,4-(Fucα1,3-)GlcNAcβ1,2-Man tetrasaccharide originally passed through and interacted with (Fig. 5). Instead, the extra density for glycans was observed downwards by the SGST bulge in the Man9-bound FRIL-OMS (Supplementary Fig. 4d). An extra α1,2 mannose was built in, showing interactions with Thr102, Ser45 and Gln229 (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 4d). Taken together, two original interactions between rFRIL-wt and the tetrasaccharide ligand were broken (His102 with fucose and GlcNAc, Tyr101 with galactose), and three new interactions between FRIL-OMS and its Man9 ligand were formed (Thr102, Ser45, Gln229 with the third α1,2 mannose). Previous reports indicate FRIL possess anti-influenza activity6. Therefore, we were curious if FRIL-OMS’s antiviral spectrum differs from its rFRIL-wt progenitor. FG, CX, M9 and MG-decorated Cali09 influenza virus (X181) were prepared as previously described6, and subjected to lectin blotting and microneutralization assay. On lectin blot (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), rFRIL-wt exhibited strong binding towards the HA protein on FG and CX viruses, but only weak binding to M9. In contrast, FRIL-OMS binds strongly to M9 HAs and has negligible binding to FG and CX. With the same FG and M9-decorated viruses, in a microneutralization assay (Fig. 4d), rFRIL-wt neutralizes FG but not M9 virus, while FRIL-OMS neutralizes M9 but not FG virus. The mutant H102A, which had previously shown a broadened glycan specificity including both complex type and mannose 9 glycans, was able to bind and neutralize both FG and M9 viruses, while rproConA also recognizes both FG and M9 but showed preference towards M9-decorated viruses (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d).

Finally, we compared the neutralization profiles of rFRIL-wt and FRIL-OMS against a panel of influenza viruses with differing density of oligomannose N-glycans on their HAs (Fig. 4e). The egg-grown Cali09-like (H1N1) vaccine strain X181 has 6 N-glycosylation sites per HA monomer, four of which are mostly complex type and two (104 and 304) mostly high mannose24, but lack glycans on top of the HA head. Egg-grown A/New Caledonia/20/1999 (NC99, H1N1 pre-pandemic) has 8 N-glycosylation sites, two of which (142 and 177) are on top of the HA head and are mostly oligomannose25. A/Victoria/361/2011 (Vic11, H3N2 strain) HA has 11 glycosylation sites, three of which (133, 165 and 285) are high mannose on the HA head, creating a “high-mannose patch”26. Results show that while rFRIL-wt has relatively similar neutralization titers across all three strains (IC50 Cali09 0.23 μg/ml or 2.02 nM, NC99 0.37 μg/ml or 3.25 nM, Vic11 0.78 μg/ml or 6.85 nM), FRIL-OMS performed the best against Vic11 (IC50 4.10 μg/ml or 36 nM). This neutralization profile is in line with other known oligomannose binding antiviral lectins, such as Griffithsin27, H84T Banlec28 and Galanthus nivalis lectin (GNA)29 (Supplementary Fig. 9g). A concern we noticed previously6 was that ConA was cytotoxic to MDCK cells at higher concentrations, which shows up as false negatives in microneutralization assay. In our current study, neither rFRIL-wt nor FRIL-OMS caused cytotoxicity up to 500 μg/ml (Supplementary Fig. 9e). rproConA does show some cell death, but not as severe as mature ConA, possibly due to being a dimer rather than a tetramer.

In summary, a comparison between FRIL-OMS and rFRIL-wt structures (Fig. 5) reveals that in FRIL-OMS, the removal of tyrosine and histidine residues from the 101 and 102 positions breaks two interactions with galactose and fucose on our tetrasaccharide, and the elongation of loop B causes a clash between galactose and the binding site. In contrast to tetrasaccharide, the elongation of loop B on FRIL-OMS displaces Man9 downward, where an additional α1,2-linked mannose on the D1 branch of Man9 forms new interactions with the threonine present at the 102 position and Ser45, which originally interacted with GlcNAc on the tetrasaccharide. The effects of this shifted glycan specificity can also be reflected in FRIL-OMS’s biological activity against influenza viruses, with FRIL-OMS showing a starkly different binding and neutralization profile than its wild-type progenitor. These results show we have an engineered FRIL-OMS which completely shifts its binding specify from Lewis X containing N-glycan to high-mannose type N-glycans.

Discussion

Sheldon et al proposed that the N-linked oligosaccharide on the ConA precursor serves as a self-neutralizing ligand20. However, Ramis et al proposed that the glycan played a part in pH-dependent plant vacuolar refolding of ConA, and deglycosylation alone is necessary but insufficient for proConA activation30. In our current study, we observed that both recombinant proConA and recombinant FRIL are produced in an inactive form, which can then be activated through enzymatic deglycosylation alone. Furthermore, self-liganding of glycosylated FRIL was observed in our cryo-EM structure of apo rFRIL, which supports Sheldon et al’s hypothesis. Nevertheless, mutagenesis of N-glycosylation sites on FRIL’s linker caused protein retention and misfolding, indicating that linker glycans may serve a dual function: ensuring proper protein folding by engagement with molecular chaperones, and serving as a self-ligand to inactivate the folded lectin before it is transported to the vacuole. A limitation to our hypothesis is that recombinant lectins used in our study were produced in mammalian cells, which are adorned with high mannose or complex N-glycans. While this is consistent with the high mannose linker glycan found on native proConA30, native FRIL comes with plant-based pauci-mannose11. We speculate that pauci-mannose acts as a much weaker self-ligand, so that a single cut at linker position Asn113 is enough to activate native FRIL without needing complete deglycosylation. This is supported by our observation that complete removal of the native FRIL linker only improved binding slightly (Supplementary Fig. 2h, i).

Traditionally, production of recombinant ConA in prokaryotic expression systems involves protein refolding from inclusion bodies9,31, and as far as we know, mutagenesis has only been done on “one-chain” legume lectins, such as peanut lectin32 and EcorL33. Recently, bioactive recombinant ConA was successfully expressed using an A. tumefaciens-mediated plant expression system in lettuce34. Nonis et al expressed both proConA and AEP in E. coli and induced circular permutation events in vitro, creating mature recombinant ConA for structural analysis21. In this study, we obtained rproConA and rFRIL using a simple and robust method of mammalian cell expression followed by PNGase F deglycosylation. Our bioactive dimeric rproConA had similar glycan binding activity as natural mature ConA. However, without AEP-mediated circular permutation, the elongated C-terminus of rproConA likely interferes with the formation of a dimer-of-dimers configuration seen in mature ConA21, meaning our active proConA remained a dimer rather than a tetramer under neutral pH.

Commonly-used strategies for shifting glycan specificity of lectins include structure-based design35, random mutation36 and mimicking nature18. Engineering a novel specificity for a lectin requires not only enhancing the affinity for the target glycan but also eliminating affinity to the original ligand35. In our study, broadening FRIL’s carbohydrate recognition to include both complex (original ligand) and high mannose (target ligand) N-glycans can be achieved with a single residue change, either H102A or Y101S. However, eliminating FRIL’s binding towards its original ligand involves “mimicking nature”: in our case, mimicking loop B amino acid sequences from the structurally-related but high-mannose specific ConA. Osterne et al.37 have performed in silico simulations between ConA and Man9, and found that Thr168, Gly224 and Arg228 (FRIL Ser45 and 101 ~ 104 loop B, respectively) likely interact with the terminal mannose of the α1-3 branch of Man9, while Thr15, Ser21 and Arg33 (FRIL Pro145, Asn151 and Arg163, respectively) interact with the α1-6 branch. Our current findings seemingly support their simulation data, as when FRIL loop B (FRIL 101 ~ 104, ConA 223 ~ 228) and Pro145 (ConA Thr15) were replaced by homologous residues present on ConA, they and Ser45 (ConA Thr168) formed new interactions with Man9.

Legume lectins are comprised of four glycan-interacting loops on top of a beta jellyroll scaffold, yet this family of lectins recognizes a diverse spectrum of glycan motifs. Lectins have been utilized to detect and differentiate between structural motifs of various N-glycans38,39, and the possibility of swapping loops to switch glycan recognition raises tantalizing prospects in glycan array and biomarker screening40. Furthermore, human L-type lectins, such as ERGIC-53, are structurally similar to FRIL41. It is not out of the realm of possibility to speculate that key residues on FRIL’s and ConA’s extended binding site gleaned in this study could one day be transferred to human lectins, “humanizing” these powerful antiviral and anti-cancer agents with far-ranging biomedical applications.

Methods

Ethical statement

Chicken embryos used in this study were euthanized before 75% total incubation period (Day 16), and are therefore not subject to Academia Sinica Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval.

Biosafety considerations

Experiments using influenza viruses were approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of Academia Sinica (protocol no. BSF0420-00004402) and conducted by trained personnel in a Biosafety Level 2 laboratory.

Plasmid construction and protein expression

Codon-optimized FRIL, proConA and Cali09 HA sequences were synthesized by Genomics, Taiwan, with an N-terminus Brisbane-HA signal peptide and a C-terminus 6xHis tag. FRIL and Cali09 were cloned into a pTT expression plasmid (National Research Council of Canada) while proConA was cloned into pcDNA3.1 (Thermo Fisher). Primer sequences are provided as Supplementary Data 4. Plasmids were transiently transfected into early log-phase (1.6 ~ 2.2 × 106 cells/ml) HEK293 EBNA (ATCC CRL-10852) or HEK293S (ATCC CRL-3022 GnTI-, for M5 HA) cells with PEI (polyethylenimine, linear, MW25,000, Polysciences Inc.), in a ratio of 2 mg plasmid 4 mg PEI per 1 liter cell. For M9 and MG HA, kifunensine (Sigma Aldrich) is added to a final concentration of 1 mg/L. Transfected cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 8% CO2 with orbital shaking at 120 rpm for 5 ~ 6 days in Freestyle 293 medium (Thermo-Fisher Scientific) with 0.5% fetal bovine serum (Thermo-Fisher Scientific). After 5 ~ 6 days of expression, the supernatant is collected by centrifugation at 10543 rcf.

Recombinant proteins were purified with Ni-NTA (GE Healthcare) beads. Beads were added to the harvested supernatant, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h on an orbital shaker. The slurry is poured into an open column, and Ni-NTA beads are then washed twice with 10 column volumes of PBS, followed by 10 column volumes of 30 mM imidazole twice, 50 mM imidazole once, before the His-tagged proteins are eluted with 5 column volumes of 150 mM imidazole twice. The 150 mM imidazole eluate is buffer exchanged three times with PBS on a 50 kDa spin filter (Amicon).

For rFRIL and proConA, the eluate is then subjected to 4 h 37 °C PNGase F treatment at a ratio of 40 μg of PNGase F per 1 mg lectin. Some precipitation of the lectin is expected, especially for mutants. After PNGase F cleavage, the 36 kDa enzyme and our 100 kDa lectin are separated with a 50 kDa spin filter and 4 ~ 5 times washing. Do not use dextran or other polysaccharide-based size-exclusion chromatography during proConA and FRIL’s purification process.

For Cali09 HA, the PBS buffer-exchanged eluate undergoes a final polishing step by gel filtration. For MG and CX HA, endoglycosidase H cleavage happens before the polishing step, at a ratio of 1 mg HA to 100 μg Endo H for 1 h in a 37 °C waterbath. Afterwards, the HAs are loaded onto a Superdex 200 size exclusion column (GE Healthcare), and the HA trimer peak is collected around 210 kDa.

Native FRIL purification from bean

Lablab purpureus beans were purchased from a traditional medicine store in Taiwan and ground into fine powder using a blender (Blendtec). PBS was added to bean powder at a 10:1 ratio, and placed on an inverting mixer at 4 °C overnight for protein extraction. Next day, the mixture is centrifuged at 10000 g 25 min to remove bean powder. 20% w/w of ammonium sulfate is added to the supernatant and mixed at room temperature for 1 h. The solution is centrifuged for 12000 g 30 min, and the pellet is discarded. Another 40% w/w of ammonium sulfate is added to the supernatant for a total of 60% ammonium sulfate, and the solution is mixed at room temperature for 1 h to precipitate nFRIL. After centrifugation at 12000 g for 30 min, the supernatant is discarded, and the pellet is re-suspended in PBS containing 1 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM MgCl2.

1 g mannose beads (Sigma-Aldrich) is added per 20 ml re-suspended pellet, and the mixture is incubated overnight at 4 °C on an orbital shaker. The next day, the bead slurry is poured into an open column. The beads are then washed twice with 10 column volumes of PBS containing 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2. nFRIL is eluted from mannose beads with 200 mM of methyl-D-α-mannopyranoside (Sigma Aldrich), and the eluate is then dialyzed twice with PBS containing 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2, pH 8.

Ion exchange is used as the final polishing step for nFRIL purification. 1 ml of Unosphere Q beads (BioRad) is placed inside an open column, and the dialyzed nFRIL solution is passed through 3 ~ 4 times. nFRIL is found in the flow-through, while non-specific proteins bind to Unosphere Q beads. The flow-through with nFRIL is then concentrated by a 50 kDa spin column (Amicon).

FRIL mutagenesis

Either the KOD polymerase kit (Sigma-Aldrich) or the Q5 Site-directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England Biolabs) was used to conduct mutagenesis, according to their respective protocols. Primers were synthesized by Genomics, Taiwan, and all mutants were sequenced for confirmation. Primer sequences are provided as Supplementary Data 4. Expression and purification of mutants followed the same protocol as rFRIL.

Influenza virus production and purification

10,000-fold diluted A/California/7/2009-like virus (X181, vaccine strain) was inoculated into the allantoic cavity of 9-day-old fertilized chicken eggs (Animal Health Research Institute) at 100 μl/egg. The M9 and MG viruses were also injected with 0.2 mg/ml kifunensine. The injection hole was sealed with superglue (3M), and eggs were incubated for 48 h in a 35 °C incubator before being placed in 4 °C for another 24 h. Influenza viruses were harvested by collecting the allantoic fluid. The virus is then concentrated 10 to 20-fold using a 100 kDa spin filter. For MG viruses, endoglycosidase H was added to a final concentration of 20 μg/ml at this step, and allowed to cleave overnight at 4 °C. Finally, the virus stocks are filtered through a 0.22 micron bottletop filter.

To purify virus particles for the lectin blot, 25%, 30%, 40%, 50% and 55% (w/w) sucrose solutions were prepared in PBS, and 5 ml of each solution was sequentially loaded onto a 25 × 89 centrifugal tube (Beckman Colter), creating a 25 ~ 55% sucrose gradient. 10 ~ 14 ml of concentrated allantoic fluid is layered on top of the tube, and the tube is centrifuged at 20000 rpm (72100 rcf) for 15 h (Optima L-90K, Beckman Colter) on a SW-28 swing bucket rotor. The next day, virus particles were harvested in fractions of 1 ml starting from the top of the tube, and a hemagglutination assay on each fraction was conducted under a biosafety cabinet. Fractions with the highest hemagglutination titers are pooled and desucrosed by buffer exchange using a 100 kDa spin filter (Amicon).

Lectin binding ELISA and lectin blotting

In lectin binding ELISA, 2 μg/ml of HA, Spike or fetuin glycoprotein in PBS were coated onto an ELISA plate at 50 μl/well (0.1 μg/well) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the ELISA plate is blocked by 10% bovine serum albumin (Bioman) at 100 μl/well for 1 h at room temperature. Blocking solution is removed, and rproConA, nFRIL, rFRIL or FRIL mutants are loaded onto the first well of the plate at 10 μg/ml 146 μl/well, and 100 μl dilution buffer (PBS with 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2 and 1% bovine serum albumin) is loaded into subsequent wells. 0.5 log10 serial dilution is conducted by transferring 46 μl of the sample down to the last well. The plate is then incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Afterwards, the lectins are removed, and the plate is washed twice with PBS-T (PBS with 0.05% Tween 20) containing 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2. 1:2500 rabbit polyclonal anti-FRIL or anti-ConA antibody (LTK laboratories) in PBS-T containing 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2 is applied to the plate at 100 μl/well. This primary antibody is incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The plate is washed three times, and 1:5000 anti-rabbit HRP (Jackson Immuno-Research) is applied to the plate at 100 μl/well. After another hour at room temperature, the plate is washed three times. 50 μl/well of KPL SureBlue (Seracare) substrate is added to each well, and the reaction is stopped with 50 μl/well of 1 M H2SO4. OD450 is measured with Cytation5 (Biotek). Of note, calcium must be present in all buffers at each step beyond lectin application. Skim milk or any blocking solution that contains glycoproteins or sugars cannot be used.

In yeast mannan and methyl-D-α-mannopyranoside carbohydrate competition lectin binding ELISA, yeast mannan from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Sigma Aldrich) was prepared at a concentration of 360 mg/ml in PBS (1 mM CaCl2, MgCl2), and methyl-D-α-mannopyranoside (Sigma Aldrich) was prepared at a concentration of 2 M. These sugars were serially diluted on a U-bottomed 96-well plate, and an equal volume of 2 μg/ml rFRIL-wt, FRIL-OMS or rproConA were added to each well for a final concentration of 1 μg/ml lectin in all wells This mixture is incubated at room temperature for 15 mins before being transferred, well-to-well, to an ELISA plate coated with Cali09 HA glycoprotein and blocked by 10% BSA. The lectin-binding ELISA then proceeds as outlined in the previous section.

In lectin blotting, purified intact Cali09 virus particles adorned with different glycans were lysed using SDS loading dye (no DTT) and 100 °C boiling for 10 min. The lysed viruses were then loaded onto a 4 ~ 20% gradient gel (BioRad). Viral proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane using the semi-dry method (Trans-Blot SD, BioRad), and blocking was performed using 10% bovine serum albumin overnight at 4 °C on an orbital shaker. The next day, either rFRIL-wt, FRIL-OMS, or rproConA was added to the membrane at 2 μg/ml in PBS containing 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2. The lectins were then allowed to bind to glycoproteins on the lysed virus for one hour with orbital shaking. Afterwards, the lectins are removed, and the membrane is washed twice with PBS-T containing 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2. 1:2500 rabbit polyclonal anti-FRIL or anti-ConA antibody in PBS-T with 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2 is applied to the membrane. The primary antibody is incubated for 1 h with orbital shaking at room temperature. Then, the plate is washed three times, and 1:5000 anti-rabbit HRP is applied to the membrane. After another hour of shaking at room temperature, the membrane is washed three times, and Clarity ECL substrate (BioRad) is applied. Chemiluminescence is visualized using iBright 1500 (Invitrogen). After visualization, the membrane is stripped using Restore Stripping Buffer (Thermo Scientific), and blocking is applied once more. 1:2500 rabbit polyclonal anti-NP antibody (LTK Laboratories) is applied to the membrane, followed by 1:5000 anti-rabbit HRP, and influenza NP protein is visualized as a loading control. Unprocessed and uncropped blots are available in source data files.

Microneutralization assay

TCID50 is first determined for the egg-grown third passage (P3) virus stocks of A/California/7/2009-like (X181), A/New Caledonia/20/1999-like (A/Taiwan/223/01) and A/Victoria/361/2011-like (IVR-165) influenza vaccine strains. 146 μl of the virus stock is diluted 1000-fold and loaded into the first well of a 96-well tissue culture plate, and 100 μl of virus dilution medium (DMEM with 25 mM HEPES, penicillin-streptomycin, 0.2% BSA) is loaded into subsequent wells. Serially dilute the virus stock down to the 11th well by transferring 46 μl from the previous well to the next one (0.5 log10 dilution), for a 103 to 108 test. Plates were placed in a 37 °C 5% CO2 incubator for 1 h to mimic microneutralization conditions, and 100 μl/well of 1.5 × 105 cells/ml MDCK cells (ATCC) in virus dilution medium was added. Incubate the plate for 18 ~ 20 h in a 37 °C 5% CO2 humidified incubator, or until CPE can be seen but before cell death and detachment. The plate is washed once with PBS, then fixed and permeated with ice-cold 50% methanol 50% acetone. After drying the plate, 100 μl/well 10% skim milk 1% BSA in PBS is applied to the plate for 1 h blocking. 100 μl/well 1:2500 rabbit polyclonal anti-NP antibody (LTK laboratories) in 10% blocking solution (1% skim milk, 0.1% BSA in PBS with 0.05% tween 20) is added to the plate, and allowed to incubate at room temperature for another hour. Afterwards, the plate is washed 3 times with PBS-T, and 100 μl/well 1:5000 anti-rabbit HRP (Jackson Immuno-Research) is added. After another hour of incubation, the plate is washed three times, and 50 μl/well of KPL SureBlue (Seracare) substrate is applied. Reaction is stopped with 50 μl/well of 1 M H2SO4. OD450 is measured with Cytation5 (Biotek). TCID50/ml is calculated as the first serially-diluted concentration whose OD450 is above twice that of negative control wells.

For microneutralization assay, 73 μl of 50 μg/ml rFRIL-wt, FRIL-OMS, Griffithsin (NIH AIDS reagent program), H84T Banlec (kind gift from Dr Markovitz’s lab) and Galanthus nivalis agglutinin (GNA, Sigma Aldrich) were added to the first well (5 μg/ml for Griffithsin against NC99 Vic11, to keep its slope within the dilution range), and 50 μl/well of virus dilution medium is added to subsequent wells. 23 μl is sequentially diluted down from the first well in a 0.5 log10 serial dilution. 15 TCID50/50 μl of virus is then added to each well. The plate is incubated at 37 °C 5% CO2 1 h for virus neutralization, then 100 μl/well of 1.5 × 105 cells/ml MDCK cells (ATCC) in virus dilution medium is added. Subsequent steps are the same as the TCID50 test. Microneutralization % is calculated as {[(positive control – negative control) – (test well – negative control)]/(positive control – negative control)} x 100%.

Glycan array analysis

All glycans used in the array analysis are prepared by the modular synthesis method42. Glycans were dissolved in printing buffer (300 mM phosphate buffer, 0.005% Triton, pH 8.5) in 100 μM concentration. Microarrays were printed (BioDot; Cartesian Technologies) by a robotic pin (SMP3; TeleChem International) with the deposition of ∼0.6 nL of various solutions from 96-well plates onto NHS-coated glass slides (Nexterion H slide; SCHOTT North America). Printed slides were allowed to react in an atmosphere of 80% humidity for one hour, followed by desiccation overnight. The prepared slides were stored in a humidity-controlled dry box before the binding assay.

Glycan microarray slides with 169 different glycans were blocked for 1 h with blocking buffer (Superblock, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then washed with PBST (PBS buffer, 0.05% Tween 20).10 µg/ml His-rFRIL was applied onto the array and incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and then washed with PBST to remove unbound rFRIL. Anti-His antibodies (Alexa Fluor® 647 anti-His, Biolegend) were applied (1:300 dilution, PBST + BSA) for another incubation at room temperature for 1 h, and then washed with PBST. Microarray slides were spun dry prior to scanning with a GenePix 4300 A reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California) at OD 635 nm and analyzed with GenePix Pro 7.0 software (Molecular Devices).

Hemagglutination assay

Turkey red blood cells (tRBC, Jianrong) were washed three times with PBS and diluted to 0.5%. Add 50 μl PBS into each well of a V-bottomed 96-well plate, then add 50 μl of the lectin or virus sample to the first well for a total of 100 μl. Serially dilute 50 μl of the sample down to the last well, then add 50 μl of the 0.5% tRBC to each well (for a total of 100 μl). Allow the tRBC to settle for 30 min to 1 h.

Biolayer interferometry

FG, CX and M9 HAs were labeled with biotin using N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester (EZ-link-LC-LC-Biotin, Thermo Scientific) at a 1-to-5 molar ratio at 4 °C overnight in PBS pH 7.4. Excess NHS-biotin was removed by size exclusion chromatography. Biolayer interferometry was performed on an Octet HTX system (Sartorius) using Octet SA Biosensors (Sartorius). 100 nM of biotinylated HA were loaded onto sensors for 300 s, followed by a baseline of 120 s, association 600 s and dissociation 600 s. A two-fold serial dilution of various lectins starting from 200 nM were used as analyte (in PBS-T with 0.01% BSA, 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2), with a single reference and no probe regeneration. Results were analyzed with Octet Data Analysis software (Sartorius) using a 2:1 heterogeneous ligand model.

SEC-MALS

Size exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering was performed by running the purified protein samples through an ENrich™ SEC 650 10 × 300 column (BioRad) on an Akta FPLC (GE) connected to a three-angle light-scattering detector (mini-DAWN TREOS) and a refractive index detector (Optilab T-rEX. Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, California). Data analysis was done with ASTRA.

MTT cytotoxicity assay

MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] (Invitrogen) was dissolved as a 12 mM solution in PBS. MDCK cells were grown to 80% confluence on a 96-well plate, and the medium was replaced with 100 µL/well of serially diluted lectins in culture medium (DMEM without FBS) for 20 h incubation. 10 µL of MTT solution was added to each well before incubating for another 4 h. 100 µL/well of MTT solvent (10 mM HCl, 10% SDS) was added, and the plate was incubated overnight before absorbance at 570 nm was read to assess cell survival.

Crystallization, X-ray diffraction and structural refinement

Purified nFRIL was concentrated to 8 mg/mL and mixed with the tetrasaccharide at a final concentration of 10 mM. The synthesis and validation of the Galβ1,4-(Fucα1,3-)GlcNAcβ1,2-Man Lewis X tetrasaccharide is shown in the source data. Hanging-drop vapor diffusion crystallization was set at 20 °C, and a single crystal appeared on day 3 in the condition containing 0.2 M ammonium fluoride, pH 6.2, 20% PEG 3350. Crystals were harvested without the addition of cryoprotectant, and diffraction datasets were collected at National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC) beamline TPS05A, in the wavelength of 1.0000 Å and temperature 100 K. Data was processed in iMosflm43; Molecular replacement was performed by Phenix Phaser-MR44 using the monomer structure of PDB 1QMO14 as the searching ensemble. The 3D structure of Lewis X tetrasaccharide and all other figure-related glycans were encoded with CSDB glycoscience for building and optimizing in elBOW. The solved structure was further refined in Phenix.refine44 (used NCS) with manual adjustments done in WinCoot45 until R/Rfree converged and all geometry statistics were satisfactory. Structural figures were prepared with PyMOL46 and ChimeraX UCSF47.

Negative stain transmission electron microscopy

Purified nFRIL or rFRIL protein was diluted to 20 μg/ml in PBS before being applied onto glow-discharged carbon-coated 400 mesh CF400-CU grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 1 min at room temperature. Excessive protein solutions were blotted with filter paper, and grids were washed 3 times in water prior to staining with 1% uranyl acetate for 60 s at room temperature. Data was collected using a FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 120 keV and a magnification of 29 K that resulted in a pixel size of 3.13 Å at the specimen plane. 3D reconstruction was processed by cisTEM48.

Cryo-EM sample preparation, data harvesting and processing and structural refinement

Purified nFRIL, rFRIL or FRIL-OMS lectins (0.06–0.08 mg/ml in 1/10 PBS buffer) were either mixed with the synthetic Galβ1,4-(Fucα1,3-)GlcNAcβ1,2-Man tetrasaccharide (10 mM), the Man9GlcNAc2 (10 mM) oligosaccharide, or present in its apo state. The Man9GlcNAc2 oligosaccharide is prepared by the modular synthesis method42. 3-4 μl of the sample was applied onto a graphene-oxide-coated Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 Cu 200 holey carbon grid mounted in a Mark IV Vitrobot (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equilibrated at 4 °C with 100% humidity. Grids were blotted at force 0 for 3 s, waiting 10 s. Data was collected on a Titan Krios G3 at 300 kV (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a Gatan K3 detector and the Gif Quantum energy filter with 20 eV slit width. Movies were acquired in EPU (Thermo Fisher Scientific, v2.10) at two exposures per hole. Total electron dose was ~50 e−/A2 and fractionated into 50 frames. The movies were collected at the magnification of 105 K (for apo rFRIL and nFRIL-tetrasaccharide) and 130 K (for rFRIL-OMS-Man9 and FRIL-wt-Man9) in the defocus range from −1.2 to −2.0 μm. Movies were motion corrected using MotionCorr249. The micrographs were CTF-corrected, particles were picked and processed in relion 3.050 (for rFRIL apo and nFRIL+tetrasaccharide data sets) or CryoSPARC ver 451 (for nFRI-wt +Man9 and rFRIL-OMS +Man9 data sets). The D2 symmetry was applied, and the resolution of the final volume was determined by gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) cutoff at 0.143. PDB 1QMO14 was used for model fitting, and after the first cryo-EM structure was solved, the latter was used for model fitting on all subsequent datasets. The building of glycans and inter-subunit loops was done in Coot45. Geometry statistics were evaluated using Phenix real-space refinement44.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard error unless otherwise specified. Curves were fitted by variable-slope nonlinear regression for EC50 or IC50 values using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Sequence alignment was performed using DALI52.
