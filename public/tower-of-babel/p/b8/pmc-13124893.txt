Factors associated with central venous port implantation during breast cancer perioperative chemotherapy: a Japanese database study
Authors: Mari Abe, Kojiro Morita, Takaaki Konishi, Katsuhiko Nara, Yuya Kimura, Kiyohide Fushimi, Hideo Yasunaga, Gojiro Nakagami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVascular access guidelines in Western countries recommend the central venous (CV) administration of cytotoxic chemotherapy agents to reduce complications such as extravasation. However, in Japan, peripheral venous administration is common. This study aimed to clarify the current status of using CV ports in Japan and its associated patient and institutional factors in breast cancer cases.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the Diagnosis Procedure Combination data documented between 2013 and 2022 from 238 hospitals. Participants were patients with breast cancer who underwent mastectomy and perioperative chemotherapy. Data on patient demographics, anticancer drugs used, and hospital characteristics were collected from the inpatient and outpatient episodes. Factors associated with CV port implantation were examined using logistic regression analysis.ResultsAmong 45,960 patients, 113 (0.25%) underwent CV port implantation, mostly postoperatively and at trunk sites. Factors negatively associated with CV port use included partial mastectomy (Odds ratio, 0.62 [95% confidence interval,0.41–0.92]) (vs. total mastectomy), use of only irritant drugs (0.43[0.21–0.90]) (vs. vesicants), and treatment at medium-volume hospitals (0.34 [0.16–0.73]) (vs. low-volume hospitals), whereas use of both vesicant and irritant drugs or nonvesicant drugs, and high volume hospital did not show statistical significance.ConclusionCV ports were used in only 0.25% of breast cancer surgery cases, suggesting highly selective utilization. Their implantation may be influenced not only by clinical factors but also by institutional characteristics and practice patterns, highlighting the need for the context-specific use of CV ports.Keywords: Central venous port, Chemotherapy, Mastectomy

Introduction

Breast cancer prevalence has been increasing worldwide, including Japan [1]. In early-stage breast cancer, intravenous vesicant chemotherapy is often administered perioperatively as the primary treatment [2]. Extravasation is the most serious local adverse event of this therapy [3] and can result in phlebitis, ulceration and necrosis, which may occasionally require surgical intervention [4, 5]. Such complications may lead to treatment interruption and increased clinical workload and healthcare costs related to vascular access management. Thus, guidelines in Western countries recommend central venous (CV) administration to prevent damage to the peripheral vessel wall [6–8]. Specifically, using CV ports leads to a lower incidence of complications, including extravasation [9]. These ports also relieve patients from difficulties in securing venous access and provide high satisfaction [10].

In Japan, chemotherapy with vesicants is commonly administered via peripheral intravenous catheters [11], given that national guidelines provide only weak recommendations for CV access during repeated chemotherapy, regardless of the drug type [12]. Consequently, CV port use depends on the individual physician’s judgment and the available medical resources. Furthermore, medical human resources, including radiologists and breast surgeons, in Japan are unevenly distributed across regions and among institutions [13, 14], potentially causing heterogeneity in care.

However, the practice patterns and background characteristics regarding CV port use in Japan remain largely unknown. Understanding real-world use is crucial to bridging the gap between Japanese and Western clinical practices. Identifying patient- and institution-level factors associated with CV port implantation may support evidence-based decision-making and contribute to the development of clearer guidelines tailored to the Japanese clinical context. This study aimed to examine the prevalence of CV port implantations, the types of drugs administered through these ports, and the factors associated with their use.

Patients and methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used the Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) database [15]. This database contains nationwide inpatient administrative claims and discharge abstract data, including unique hospital identifiers, patient demographic characteristics, diagnoses recorded with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, drugs administered daily and the corresponding procedures, and clinical data. Although outpatient data are generally not included in the DPC database, some hospitals voluntarily provide outpatient records. The DPC database covers approximately 50% of all acute-care hospitals and 90% of all tertiary-level emergency care hospitals in Japan. These recorded diagnoses were reported to have high specificity or positive predictive value, while the procedure codes have high specificity and sensitivity [16]. The study was limited to 238 hospitals that provide both inpatient and outpatient data and services, given that CV port implantation can be provided as well among outpatients. Information on pre- and postoperative chemotherapy was obtained from both inpatient and outpatient data.

We also collected data from the 2017 Annual Report for Functions of Medical Institutions, which provides comprehensive details and statistics regarding hospitals [17]. Japan’s Medical Care Act requires all acute-care hospitals to accurately report their data. Unique identifiers for hospitals were utilized to link the databases.

Study period

We reviewed data documented from April 1, 2013, to March 31, 2022, and included those of patients who underwent mastectomy between April 1, 2014, and March 31, 2021. The perioperative period covered 1 year before and 1 year after surgery, during which chemotherapy data were collected from both inpatient and outpatient episodes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We specifically included patients who received perioperative intravenous chemotherapy within 1 year before or after mastectomy for breast cancer (ICD-10: C50, malignant neoplasm of the breast). For patients who underwent multiple mastectomies during the study period, only the earliest surgery was considered to ensure that each patient was counted only once. Conversely, we excluded patients who were treated at hospitals that lacked data in the Annual Report for Functions of Medical Institutions.

Outcomes

The outcome was CV port implantation within 1 year before or after breast cancer surgery. This time frame was selected to capture CV port implantations related to both preoperative and postoperative chemotherapies.

Patient and hospital factors

Participant characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and tumor classification at admission, were collected. We also collected participants’ treatment information, including operative sites (total, partial, and unspecified), axillary dissection, preoperative chemotherapy, tissue-damaging agent type, and radiation. In line with the National Health Service England and Management of chemotherapy extravasation: ESMO–EONS clinical practice guidelines, tissue-damaging agents were categorized into vesicant, irritant, both vesicant and irritant, and nonvesicant (i.e., neither vesicant nor irritant) [18, 19]. Patients were classified according to whether they had received at least one administration of a vesicant or irritant agent, because no risk threshold has been established based on administration doses. A patient who underwent both partial and total mastectomies in one hospitalization was categorized into total mastectomy.

Hospital characteristics included academic hospital status, tertiary-level emergency care facility, hospital bed capacity, rurality, and hospital volume. Rurality was assigned to each hospital according to its location within secondary medical areas, which are defined under Japan’s Medical Care Act as regional units that promote cooperation among healthcare, medical care, and welfare [20]. Data on secondary medical areas were linked with the DPC data to classify rurality by population size and density. To calculate the hospital volume for each hospital, we divided the total number of breast cancer surgeries performed between 2014 and 2021 during the years when the hospital participated in the DPC system by the number of those participation years.

Data analysis

The patient and hospital factors were compared between patients with and without CV port implantation (CV-port and non–CV-port groups). The factors associated with CV port implantation were identified using logistic regression analysis. Independent variables included age, BMI (> 25.0 kg/m2), operative procedure, axillary dissection, preoperative chemotherapy, tissue-damaging agent type, and hospital volume. We employed the forced entry method for selecting clinically relevant variables to limit the number of independent variables to approximately one per 10 outcome events [21].

Missing data were handled by conducting multiple imputation using the chained equations method with the “mi impute chained” command in Stata, generating 20 imputed datasets and obtaining results according to Rubin’s rules [22]. For imputation, the following variables were used: age, sex, BMI, operative procedure, axillary dissection, operative side, preoperative chemotherapy, tissue-damaging agent type, radiation therapy, academic hospital status, tertiary-level emergency care facility, hospital bed capacity, hospital volume, and rurality. To adjust for intracluster correlation within hospitals, we fitted a generalized estimating equation with the logistic regression model.

The significance level was set at 5%. All statistical data were analyzed using the statistical software Stata/MP 18 (8-core) (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical consideration

Owing to the anonymity of the database used in this research, informed consent acquisition was waived. Furthermore, the Ethics Committee of The University of Tokyo School of Medicine approved this study (Ethical Review Approval Number: 3501-[5]).

Results

We identified 45,960 eligible patients who underwent surgery for breast cancer and received chemotherapy within 1 year (Fig. 1). Their mean age was 56.6 years, and females represented 99.6% of the overall study population. Overall, only 113 (0.25%) patients had a CV port implanted within 1 year before or after breast cancer surgery. CV ports were inserted postoperatively in 82 (72.6%) patients and at trunk sites in 90 patients (79.6%) (data not shown).

Discussion

Using the database from acute care hospitals in Japan, this study revealed that only 0.25% of patients with breast cancer surgery and perioperative chemotherapy underwent CV port implantation. Both vesicant and irritant agents were more frequently administered among patients with CV ports than among those without. Factors such as partial mastectomy, use of only irritant drugs, and medium-volume hospitals were negatively associated with CV port implantation.

A large international study reported that approximately 50% of cytotoxic chemotherapy administrations were delivered via CV access [3]. In our study, only 0.25% of the patients received CV port implantation: specifically, over 90% of them received chemotherapy with cytotoxic agents, and patients with more advanced T-stage were more likely to receive a CV port. Clinicians would have appropriately selected CV port implantation for patients with greater disease burden. Although direct comparison is difficult, the proportion of CV port implantations was remarkably lower in Japan than in other countries. One possible reason is that CV access is not strongly recommended in the Japanese clinical guidelines [12]. The lack of skilled personnel and technical resources may have also prevented the routine clinical practice of CV port implantation [23]. Although CV port implantation involves higher upfront costs than peripheral venous access, the cost is less likely to affect the port indication than the patient risk and treatment requirements [24]. Because low utilization may increase the risk of complications such as extravasation injuries, elevated patient burden, and increased healthcare costs, investigating the factors underlying CV port implantation in Japan is highly relevant for clinical practice and health services. This pattern may also reflect limited consideration of long-term vessel health [25]. Furthermore, more than 70% of CV ports were implanted postoperatively. Intensive preoperative chemotherapy administered via peripheral veins may impair the peripheral vessel condition and make venous access more difficult at the time of surgery or thereafter. Furthermore, patients with breast cancer often have long survival [1]; consequently, repeated peripheral administration, particularly after axillary surgery, may increase cumulative vascular injury [8, 26]. Short- and long-term vessel preservation (i.e., vessel health) may not be fully considered in routine clinical practice in Japan.

Traditionally, most CV ports have been implanted at trunk sites, such as the anterior chest [27]. However, recurrence may be difficult to detect in anterior chest implantation. Furthermore, given that scars from mastectomy can impact women’s quality of life, many may hesitate to have additional scars on the chest from procedures such as reconstruction or contralateral reduction mammoplasty [28–30]. Similarly, some patients could be reluctant to acquire scars caused by CV port implantation on the chest. The upper arm is another site for CV port implantation. Compared with the anterior-chest implantation, it requires shorter operative time and fewer local anesthetics and enables prolonged use with comparable or fewer complications, thereby achieving higher levels of patient satisfaction [31–33]. With the growing adoption of upper-arm implantation techniques, the proposal of alternative CV port implantation sites by healthcare professionals, including nurses, could contribute to increased CV port implantation in patients with breast cancer.

In our study, 98.5% of patients who underwent intravenous chemotherapy received cytotoxic agents (irritants and/or vesicants), most likely via peripheral veins, owing to the short administration time and outpatient setting of breast cancer chemotherapy. However, even the brief peripheral administration of cytotoxic drugs can cause vascular endothelial damage, causing complications such as thrombosis, edema, and delayed-onset induration [8]. These complications can reduce the number of viable venous access sites and increase the risk of extravasation. Moreover, approximately half of the patients underwent axillary lymph node dissection, and most patients were female. These two factors are well-known risk factors for difficult venous access and extravasation [34, 35]. Additionally, breast cancer itself has been associated with a higher risk of induration at infusion sites [26]. Considering this background, CV port implantation could be a potentially safer and more reliable option for chemotherapy in patients with breast cancer, especially those who may face venous access challenges.

In the multivariable analysis, several factors, including undergoing partial mastectomy, receiving only irritant drugs, and being treated in a medium-volume hospital, were negatively associated with CV port implantation. These findings suggest multiple potential explanations. First, variations in how institutions classify and evaluate the risks associated with irritant drugs may explain the lower proportion of CV port use among patients receiving only irritant agents; that is, institutions may prioritize vesicant classification over other treatment-related burdens when determining the indication for CV port implantation. Second, patients opting for partial mastectomy to preserve their feminine identity [28] may be more hesitant to acquire an additional chest scar from the CV port. Third, the availability of specialized personnel and the level of procedural experience may vary between medium-volume hospitals, leading to inconsistent decisions regarding CV port implantation. Resources in these hospitals may be insufficient to support CV port implantation. Although these observations are based on univariable analysis, academic hospital status did not differ between groups, while hospital size was statistically associated with CV port implantation. This suggests that differences in institutional capacity may partially explain the negative association observed with medium-volume hospitals. Therefore, CV port implantation may be influenced not only by patient-related factors but also by institutional constraints and variations in practice patterns.

This study has several limitations. First, although we adjusted for key clinically relevant baseline characteristics, only a few CV port implantation events (n = 113) were observed. As the limited number of events may have widened some confidence intervals, the results should be interpreted with caution, particularly regarding the stability of the estimated odds ratios. Second, some baseline characteristics were not available in the current database. Unmeasured factors that may have influenced CV port implantation decisions include baseline peripheral vein condition, prior history of difficult venous access, patient preference, institutional policies and resources (e.g., trained personnel and assistive devices), and the actual route of chemotherapy administration following port implantation. Third, procedural data may vary because of differences in data entry practices across hospitals; for example, some hospitals may simplify or underreport outpatient surgical records. Fourth, since the current results were derived from only 238 hospitals that provide DPC data and outpatient claims, the selection bias may decrease generalizability for small- to medium-sized community hospitals or facilities that mainly provide outpatient chemotherapy without inpatient services. Nevertheless, we consider that the extremely low proportion in relatively large hospitals warrants attention and further evaluation. Fifth, patient transfers between hospitals during treatment may result in duplicate entries. However, given that perioperative chemotherapy is generally provided at the same hospital where the surgery was performed, the influence of this factor on the findings is likely negligible. Finally, the DPC data did not specify the purpose of performing CV implantation. Thus, we focused on patients who received chemotherapy and CV port implantation within a year before or after surgery to reasonably attribute the implantation to chemotherapy use.

Despite these limitations, this study offers novel insights into the proportion of and factors associated with CV port implantation among patients with breast cancer from acute care hospitals in Japan. These findings serve as a foundation for evidence-based decision-making and highlight the potential need for clearer guidelines for CV port use tailored to the Japanese clinical context.

Conclusion

In this study, only 0.25% of patients who underwent mastectomy for breast cancer in Japan received CV port implantation. Partial mastectomy, use of only irritant drugs, and treatment at medium-volume hospitals were negatively associated with CV port use. Thus, CV ports may have been used very selectively in Japan, with decision-making regarding venous access influenced not only by clinical need but also by variability in institutional factors or practice patterns.
