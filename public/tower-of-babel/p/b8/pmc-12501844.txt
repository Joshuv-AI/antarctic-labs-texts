Severe Undiagnosed Congenital Hypothyroidism Due to Missed Newborn Screening
Authors: Maggie Zhang, Amirbahador Golchin, Julia Donner, Ming Yeh Lee, Hilary Seeley, Kara D Meister
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the case of a 5-month-old female infant with undiagnosed congenital hypothyroidism (CH), presenting with failure to thrive (FTT), hypotonia, developmental delay, and severe constipation. Born full-term as an elective home birth, she had not undergone newborn screening (NBS). Evaluation revealed a markedly elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level and undetectable free thyroxine (T4). Treatment with levothyroxine was initiated at a conservative dosage of 37.5 mcg (7.5 mcg/kg/day) and titrated over time daily to avoid cardiac adverse effects. Nutritional support via nasogastric tube (NG) tube was also initiated due to poor oral intake. Free T4 normalized after 2 weeks, and TSH reached the normal range after 5 weeks, with the goal of maintaining T4 levels on the higher end of normal and TSH on the lower end of normal. By 4 months after the initial admittance, the patient demonstrated significant improvement in weight, progress in occupational therapy and physical therapy, and developmental milestones. This case highlights the significance of verifying NBS in infants with classic signs of CH, especially those born outside of hospital systems, and underscores the value of multidisciplinary collaboration—including social work—in optimizing patient outcome.Keywords: congenital hypothyroidism, pediatrics, neonatal

Introduction

Congenital hypothyroidism (CH), defined as a condition of thyroid hormone deficiency, is one of the most common and preventable causes of intellectual disability [1, 2]. The incidence of CH among neonates ranges from 1 in 2500 to 1 in 4000, with variation accounted for by geographic regions, ethnicity, and fluctuations in screening cutoffs and detection methods [3, 4]. Primary CH is characterized by the underproduction of thyroid hormone by the thyroid gland, due to thyroid dyshormonogenesis or most commonly, thyroid dysgenesis. Temporary CH can be caused by iodine deficiency or maternal thyroid blocking antibodies. Central CH is rare, caused by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) deficiency and hypothalamic or pituitary dysfunction, and is often associated with other pituitary hormone deficiencies.[1, 5]

Though most newborns with CH show no signs, it is typically detected within days after birth, with more than 98% of children born in the United States (most states report participation >99.9%) undergoing newborn screening (NBS), a program that has been monumental in early detection and prevention of congenital diseases [6-9]. Delayed diagnosis can lead to numerous adverse effects, including intellectual disability, poor motor coordination, muscular hypotonia, and diminished attention span, with risks increasing with greater delay of diagnosis [2, 7]. Here, we report an infant with delayed diagnosis of CH requiring treatment with levothyroxine and supplemental nutrition via a nasogastric (NG) tube, resulting in a positive outcome.

Case Presentation

A 5-month-old female infant, born full-term as an elective home birth, presented in consultation to the pediatric otolaryngology clinic for a weak cry, dysphagia, and failure to thrive (FTT). Birth weight was approximately 3.63 kg (80%ile) with weight at 5 months being 5.028 kg (<1%ile) and length measuring 57 cm (<1%ile). The patient had a significant umbilical hernia, had not undergone NBS, and was unvaccinated. She had been breastfed since birth and had severe constipation, with bowel movements occurring every 2 to 3 weeks. Developmental milestones were notably delayed, with a weak cry, difficulty raising her head, infrequent smiling, and the patient not sitting or rolling over. No medications were being taken at the time of visit and the family had no history of thyroid disorders. The mother and father had a negative experience around the hospitalization of an elderly family member during the COVID-19 pandemic and were hesitant to seek medical care for their baby. During the otolaryngology clinic visit, examination was notable for expiratory stertor with an abnormal cry, adventitious breath sounds, bradycardia, muscular hypotonia, and general sleepiness. The abnormal cry and stertor was felt to be secondary to laryngeal myxedema, macroglossia, and general diffuse hypotonia. Social work was consulted for support pending admission to the hospital for an expedited workup to assess the differential diagnosis of FTT. The family left clinic against medical advice but returned later that day, and the patient was admitted to the hospital.

Diagnostic Assessment

Laboratory studies at the time of admission were notable for undetectable free thyroxine (T4) (<1.29 pmol/L [0.1 ng/dL]; normal range, 11.6-21.9 pmol/L [0.8-2.7 ng/dL]) and high TSH (563 mIU/L; normal range, 0.8-6.3 mIU/L) diagnostic for primary congenital hypothyroidism. The patient also presented with upper abdominal gaseous distention, severe constipation, poor developmental growth, and was observed to have poorly sustained suck and ineffective transfer of milk at the breast, emphasizing the need for nutritional support.

Treatment

Given severe hypothyroidism, with low free T4 and elevated TSH, tablets of levothyroxine were immediately prescribed, starting on 37.5 mcg (7.5 mcg/kg/day) once daily. We opted to start at a lower dosage than typically recommended guidelines, due to the chronicity and severity of the patient's CH, and possible risk of cardiac adverse events with overly rapid correction [10, 11]. However, physicians should be cognizant that data for cognitive outcomes in children with CH suggest that faster normalization of the free T4 leads to better long-term IQ outcomes [12]. This benefit is weighted against potential risks of rapid normalization of T4, including cardiovascular complications (low risk) and intracranial hypertension (felt to be especially low risk for this case given that the fontanelle in an infant should be open) [13]. Continued cardiopulmonary monitoring was implemented during initiation of levothyroxine and nutritional replacement due to the compounded risk of refeeding syndrome. In addition to 10 minutes of breastfeeding on each breast every 3 hours, the patient was also fed via NG tube at 3-hour intervals with 70 mL of breastmilk or infant formula (20 kcal/oz), with a goal weight gain of at least 25 to 35 g/day and planned weekly weight checks. A pediatric multivitamin drop was also prescribed. Levothyroxine dosage was titrated over time, as outlined in Table 1, eventually stabilizing at 37.5 mcg. NG feeding was discontinued 3 months after initiation, and the patient was enrolled in occupational therapy and physical therapy.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged 4 days after admission, and follow-up care was conducted with her primary care provider (PCP) and outpatient pediatric endocrinologist. The remainder of the California NBS was obtained, as were newborn well-child tests, such as hearing screening, prior to discharge from the initial admission. TSH and free T4 levels, alongside levothyroxine dosage adjustments, are documented in Table 1. Free T4 normalized to 15.48 pmol/L [1.2 ng/dL] 2 weeks after initiation of levothyroxine, and TSH fell into the normal range (4.47 mIU/L) after an additional 3 weeks. Four months after the initial examination, the patient's weight increased to 8.4 kg (49%ile), and her height measured 66.5 cm (3%ile). Reports from occupational therapy and physical therapy providers described substantial developmental progress. At her most recent outpatient endocrinology visit, TSH measured 0.33 mIU/L, slightly below the goal range of 0.5 to 2.0 mIU/L, while free T4 remained within the goal range at 27.348 pmol/L [2.12 ng/dL]. Despite borderline TSH levels, the patient was euthyroid, demonstrating substantial growth and development. Therefore, her levothyroxine dosage remained unchanged at 37.5 mcg, with continued monthly thyroid function tests and developmental monitoring by her PCP. It is noted that the normal range for thyroid function assays does not include young children or sufficient young children, and therefore lab results must be taken contextually with the clinical presentation. Subsequent relocation of the family necessitated a transition of care.

Discussion

We report a patient with previously undiagnosed and untreated severe CH, due to lack of NBS, presenting with weak cry, severe constipation, muscular hypotonia, developmental delay, and FTT. Initial labs demonstrated markedly low free T4 and high TSH, leading to diagnosis and prescribing of high-dose levothyroxine. The patient was initially started on 37.5 mcg/day by tablet to avoid rapid hormonal shifts and adverse effects. Dosage was increased after a week to 50 mcg/day, following recommended dosage for full-term infants for severe CH [1, 14]. Regular follow ups with endocrinology and the PCP were made.

The primary goal of patient treatment was to correct CH while also avoiding both undertreatment and overtreatment with monitoring of weight gain, possible cardiac instability, in addition to thyroid function tests. Undertreatment can lead to irreversible intellectual disability and impaired growth [15]. Conversely, excessive dosing may induce a hyperthyroid-like state, rare allergic reactions, and further cognitive or behavioral problems; general adverse drug reactions can lead to cardiovascular effects, including tachycardia, palpitations, and arrhythmia [14, 15]. The goal was to maintain T4 levels on the higher end of normal and TSH on the lower end of normal. The patient's T4 levels normalized within 2 weeks, and TSH in 5 weeks. Despite a borderline-low T4 at the last follow-up, clinically, the patient demonstrated no adverse effects, was euthyroid, and exhibited significant developmental progress.

This case also highlights the diagnostic value of constipation when it presents alongside other signs of CH, including fatigue, weak cry, and an umbilical hernia. Constipation affects an estimated 12% of CH patients, but it can be a misleading sign for gastrointestinal (GI) disease, such as in the case report by Tahan et al, in which a 1-year-old patient with CH was misdiagnosed with Hirschsprung disease [16].

Cases of untreated CH are extremely rare in the United States, with more than 98% of neonates undergoing NBS. However, a recent study also found a 16% prevalence for delayed CH diagnosis due to delayed TSH rise [17]. Additionally, in other countries without mandated NBS programs, the risk of untreated CH and its complications is significantly higher. A significant number of newborns globally—approximately 71%—do not receive NBS [18]. Our patient exhibited significant FTT and high risk for impaired growth and cognitive development. These findings underscore the importance of verifying NBS status for infants displaying classic signs of CH, especially those born outside of hospitals or in countries without mandated NBS, including immigrant children in the United States [19].

Finally, this case highlights the crucial role of social work in fostering trust, improving overall adherence, and understanding of treatment. Effective collaboration between physicians and social workers to build trust and mitigate families' medical hesitancy to ensure optimal patient outcomes is crucial.

Learning Points

Confirming or repeating NBS in infants who may not have undergone NBS due to birth outside of a hospital or residence in a country without mandated NBS is crucial for early diagnosis and treatment of CH to prevent adverse effects or complications.

Healthcare providers should have high clinical suspicion of CH when a patient presents with classic signs—severe constipation, hypotonia, weak cry, and umbilical hernia—especially if NBS was not performed. Even when NBS has been done, additional testing may be necessary when signs are present, as milder cases can be missed and TSH rise may be delayed.
