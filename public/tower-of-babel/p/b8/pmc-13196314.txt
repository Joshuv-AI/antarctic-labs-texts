Unique repair of a mycotic extent IV thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm using a visceral branching technique with previous axillobifemoral bypass minimizing ischemia time
Authors: Michael Youngjin Lee, Seyed Pairawan, Mouhammad Halabi, Alexander Shepard, Loay Kabbani, Andi Peshkepija
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case study demonstrates visceral branching approach for a mycotic type IV thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm in a 78-year-old man with previous axillobifemoral bypass. He underwent debridement, resection, and repair with a visceral branching technique using a modified rifampin-soaked bifurcated Dacron graft with perfusion catheters inserted into the visceral arteries. Contemporary repair involves aortic cross-clamping, producing significant hemodynamic effects. Visceral branching decreases ischemia time, and circumventing the hemodynamic effects of aortic clamping, particularly for those with limited physiological reserve. This case demonstrates a mycotic extent IV thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm modified graft repair without needing extracirculatory support owing to a previous extra-anatomical bypass.

Mycotic aortic aneurysms (MAAs) result from infections associated with or abutting the arterial wall, posing significant risk of life-threatening rupture. MAA are rare with incidence of 0.65 to 2.00% of all aortic aneurysms,1 but carry a high risk disproportionate to incidence.2 If untreated, MAAs progress to rupture, sepsis, or multiorgan failure.2 Antibiotics and medical management are insufficient—surgical therapy is required for survival.3

Current MAA repair standard of care is wide debridement, graft reconstruction, and coverage including omental flap. An alternative is aortic ligation with extra-anatomical bypass.4,5 MAA endovascular management with lifelong antibiotic therapy for bacteria with indolent virulence shows growing evidence of acceptable outcomes.6

Thoracoabdominal MAAs with visceral vessel involvement are particularly challenging—posing unique risks including mesenteric/hepatic ischemia, acute kidney injury, and rarely spinal cord ischemia.7

Herein we present a unique open repair for a mycotic, infrarenally thrombosed thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAAA) in the setting of previous axillobifemoral bypass using visceral branching.

Case report

The patient was a 78-year-old man with a past medical history of chronic lymphocytic leukemia in remission, peripheral arterial disease, coronary artery disease with coronary stent placement (ejection fraction of 68%), infrarenal abdominal aortic occlusion, and right axillary to bilateral profunda femoris bypass for chronic limb-threatening ischemia 2 years prior. He presented with abdominal pain and constipation for 4 days. Abdomen/pelvis computed tomography angiography demonstrated periaortic fat stranding and gas in occluded infrarenal aorta and surrounding sac, concerning for MAA with impending rupture (Figs 1, A, B). The patient was admitted to surgical intensive care unit (SICU) for anti-impulse control. Blood cultures drawn resulted with multidrug-resistant Bacteroides fragilis, and broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated. An echocardiogram showed preserved systolic function (ejection fraction of 69%). The patient underwent open aneurysm resection with visceral artery reconstruction using a Dacron graft. The patient was placed in a modified right lateral decubitus carefully, positioned to avoid compression of axillobifemoral ringed bypass graft with thoracoabdominal entry via a ninth-intercostal space incision. Diaphragm was partially divided circumferentially, and after medial visceral rotation in a retro-left renal plane, abdominal aorta was exposed. Celiac trunk, superior mesenteric artery (SMA), and left renal artery were individually dissected and controlled. Distal descending thoracic aorta was exposed by incising pleura. During exposure, a 20 × 10 mm bifurcated Dacron graft was modified, two 7-mm diameter grafts were anastomosed end-to-side to the medial side of the right and lateral side of the left 10-mm limbs approximately 1 cm distal to the bifurcation for visceral revascularization. Then it was soaked in a rifampin solution (60 mg/mL) for 30 minutes before implantation.

After heparinization, the distal thoracic aorta was side-clamped and opened longitudinally for end-to-side anastomosis with beveled end of modified Dacron graft (Fig 2). A TA-60 stapler (Medtronic) with 3.5-mm staples was fired distal to the graft.Fig 2Placement and proximal anastomosis of rifampin-soaked Dacron graft to the distal descending thoracic aorta with end-to-side configuration.

Placement and proximal anastomosis of rifampin-soaked Dacron graft to the distal descending thoracic aorta with end-to-side configuration.

Dacron limbs were tunneled in the retroperitoneum through the aortic hiatus. The aneurysm was opened, visceral vessels were individually cannulated with 9F Pruitt irrigation occlusion catheters (LeMaitre Vascular) and sequentially attached to graft limbs. The right 10-mm limb was sewn end-to-end to celiac, the medial 7-mm graft was sewn to left renal artery, the left 10-mm limb was sewn to the SMA, and the lateral 7-mm limb to the right renal artery. Grafts were positioned so their course was straight without significant redundancy or kinking. The celiac limb was approximately 15 cm in length, and the SMA and renal artery grafts were 20-cm.

Visceral vessel flow was restored after a maximum ischemic interval of 10 minutes, verified with Doppler auscultation (Fig 3). Radical excisional debridement of the aneurysm sac from the transected distal thoracic aorta down to the aortic bifurcation was performed with evacuation of purulent debris in the thrombosed iliac artery aneurysms. A concurrent splenectomy was performed for multiple splenic abscesses. No aortoenteric fistulas were detected. An omental flap with verified left omental artery perfusion was created for anastomotic coverage. Uninterrupted lower extremity flow was maintained throughout the procedure through axillobifemoral bypass.Fig 3Visceral and renal 9F Pruitt perfusion catheters attached to Dacron graft limbs.

The procedure lasted 12 hours with 10 L blood loss. He developed significant coagulopathy requiring significant blood products. Given the need for resuscitation, an Abthera wound vacuum (Solventum) was applied for temporary closure. Patient was transferred to the SICU.

The first postoperative night, patient developed temporary paraparesis, resolved via raising mean arterial pressure to 80 to 100 mm Hg and emergent spinal drain placement with cerebral spinal fluid drainage. The following day, patient returned to the operating room and the retroperitoneum was hemostatic. The previously created omental flap was tunneled in retrocolic fashion through the transverse colon mesentery to cover all prosthetic graft material and the stapled aortic stump (Fig 4). Large-caliber closed suction drains were placed around the bilateral iliac arteries, and the thoracoabdominal incision was closed. Bilateral pedal Doppler signals were present postoperatively.Fig 4Omental flap placement in retroperitoneum and through diaphragmatic aortic hiatus to cover the Dacron graft limbs and proximal anastomosis to the distal descending thoracic aorta.

The patient recovered in the SICU for 9 days. The spinal drain was removed on postoperative day 6 without paraparesis recurrence. Operative cultures matched B fragilis, and a 6-month metronidazole course followed by lifelong suppressive amoxicillin-clavulanate were prescribed. Patient received postsplenectomy vaccines before discharge to inpatient rehabilitation on postoperative day 18 with stable vital signs; he was afebrile and tolerating diet with regular bowel and renal function.

He presented to vascular clinic at 2, 9, and 19 months with no symptoms, normal renal function, and antibiotic compliance. Follow-up imaging demonstrated patent grafts without reinfection (Fig 5). Discussion

We present a unique mycotic extent IV TAAA repair. The presence of a patent, previous axillobifemoral bypass allowed visceral branching technique along with aortic ligation.

Antibiotic therapy and lifelong suppression is a MAA treatment mainstay.8,9 Purulence was present in aorta, iliac arteries, and spleen. Blood and operative cultures were positive for B fragilis, a colonic anaerobe and rarely reported cause of MAA, with most common pathogens being Salmonella or Staphylococcus species.3,6,9 He was treated with lifelong suppressive antibiotics.

The patient previously underwent extra-anatomical bypass to revascularize critical limb-threating ischemia owing to occluded infrarenal aorta as part of a type IV TAAA, resulting in his distal descending thoracic and proximal abdominal aorta as the reconstruction focus. Intraoperative perfusion of the visceral and renal arteries allowed for the radical debridement of infected tissues for adequate source control without significant ischemia.

Ballard et al10 described TAAA repair using visceral debranching with trifurcated prosthetic grafts for revascularization. Visceral/renal warm ischemia times ranged from 10 to 33 minutes with mortality rates (6.3%), transient renal failure (6.3%), and no mesenteric ischemia (0%). In our case, Pruitt catheters attached to graft limbs perfused the visceral vessels during debridement; vital organ warm ischemia time was minimized to 10 minutes, allowing a careful and meticulous debridement of aneurysm sac and retroperitoneum. After debridement, the visceral anastomoses were swiftly completed. The patient exhibited no acute creatinine elevation, minimal elevation of his liver function studies, and no bowel injury.

Despite best efforts to prevent spinal cord ischemia with preservation of segmental arteries, he required emergent spinal drain placement with resolved symptoms and no deficits at discharge. This complication may reflect perioperative hypoperfusion, challenging already tenuous spinal cord collateral flow.

Conclusions

We described mycotic extent IV TAAA reconstruction using visceral debranching technique to minimize vital organ ischemia in a patient with previous axillobifemoral bypass. Other treatments included antibiotics, debridement, and omental coverage. Our patient is 19 months from reconstruction and doing well with stable cross-axial imaging and no infection concerns.
