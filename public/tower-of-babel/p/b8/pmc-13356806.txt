Deep learning models for cell cycle phase prediction from single-cell RNA sequencing data
Authors: Halima Akhter, Debra Piktel, Laura F Gibson, Gangqing Hu, Donald A Adjeroh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of cell cycle phases is essential for mitigating confounding effects in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis and for understanding how certain diseases (e.g. cancer) develop and respond to treatment. We evaluated traditional machine learning (AdaBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM) and deep learning approaches (DNN3, DNN5, CNN, hybrid models, ensembles) for cell cycle phase prediction from scRNA-seq data. Models were trained using consensus phase labels derived from four established tools (CellCycleScore, ccAFv2, Revelio, and Tricycle) applied to single-cell gene expression datasets representing diverse biological contexts, including a human leukemia cell line (REH), peripheral blood mononuclear cells (10× PBMC), human pluripotent stem cells and their differentiated derivatives (GSE75748), 10 k embryonic mouse brain cells, and mouse hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (GSE81682). Performance was evaluated on three independent datasets: human embryonic stem cells (GSE64016; 247 cells), human osteosarcoma cells (GSE146773; 1152 cells), and mouse embryonic stem cells (E-MTAB-2805; 288 cells), all with experimentally verified labels. REH-trained models achieved strong cross-dataset performance, reaching 74.35% accuracy on GSE146773 (Top 3 decision fusion (DF), and score fusion (SF)) and 72.9% on GSE64016, while attaining 56.9% on the cross-species Buettner mESC benchmark. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis confirmed enrichment in cell-cycle-related gene sets for features. Notably, models trained on samples with lower recovery rates of cell cycle markers exhibited lower performance. These results highlight the strength of deep learning for robust, scalable cell cycle phase prediction from scRNA-seq data.

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) resolves transcriptomic heterogeneity at individual cell resolution, enabling a deeper understanding of cancer evolution, cell-to-cell interaction, and cellular response to external stimuli [1, 2]. Cells undergo a cycling process across distinctive phases: G1 (cell growth and maintenance), S (DNA synthesis and replication), G2 (further growth and preparation for mitosis), and M (mitosis and cytokinesis) (Supplementary Figure S1) [3]. Cells of the same type may exhibit distinctive expression patterns at different cell cycle stages, often dominated by cell cycle phase-specific gene expression. This within-group heterogeneity represents a confounding factor which, if not normalized, could mask biological signals directly relevant to the problem of interest [4].

Accurate cell cycle phase identification is essential for reliable scRNA-seq analysis, particularly in studies where cell cycle dysregulation is expected (e.g. cancer versus normal cells) [5]. Cell cycle information constrains mathematical models of gene expression and improves biological interpretation of single-cell data [6]. As reported by Barron and Li [4], regressing out cell cycle effects improved clustering accuracy and enabled biologically meaningful interpretations from scRNA-seq data. Modern applications include: (i) understanding transcriptional dynamics in development and disease, and (ii) identifying therapeutic targets in proliferation-driven diseases [7].

Several computational tools (Table 1) have been developed for cell cycle phase prediction from scRNA-seq data. These methods span four categories: marker-gene based methods (e.g. Seurat CellCycleScore [7], Revelio [8]) offer high interpretability and simplicity by utilizing predefined marker sets, yet they may struggle to capture subtle transcriptomic dynamics or assume marker consistency across non-canonical cell types. Projection and pseudotime approaches (e.g. Tricycle [5]) effectively model the continuous nature of the cell cycle along circular manifolds, providing fine-grained temporal resolution. Supervised machine learning methods (e.g. ccAFv2 [9]) utilize sophisticated classification architectures to achieve high accuracy on target platforms. Finally, unsupervised or autoencoder-based methods (e.g. Cyclum [10]) excel at identifying latent periodic patterns and novel co-expression modules without requiring prior biological labels. A detailed description of additional computational tools is provided in the Supplementary Materials, Section 2.0.

Despite significant progress, cell cycle phase prediction accuracy remains challenged when extended to different cell types, species, and scRNA-seq technologies, underscoring the need for robust alternatives.

We address these limitations through a systematic benchmarking framework integrating consensus training, comprehensive model evaluation, and validation with experimental data. Our consensus training strategy harmonizes predictions from four methodologically diverse tools (CellCycleScore [7], Tricycle [5], ccAFv2 [9], and Revelio [8]) to generate high-confidence labels without requiring experimental annotations. We evaluate traditional machine learning and deep learning models across multiple single cell gene expression data, generated by different sequencing technologies (10× Chromium, Fluidigm C1, Smart-Seq) and from different data modalities: e.g. total RNA from 10× scMultiome scRNA versus PolyA RNA from 10× scRNA-seq. Feature interpretation based on SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [16] identifies cell cycle pathway enrichment, establishing biological validity of learned representations. Furthermore, to assess the species generalizability of our models, we included cross-species validation using mouse and human single-cell expression data sets in training and testing models.

Materials and methods

Study design overview

We developed a systematic benchmarking framework consisting of: (i) consensus label generation from multiple prediction tools, (ii) model training and optimization across traditional ML and deep learning architectures, (iii) independent evaluation on experimentally annotated benchmarks, and (iv) biological validation using feature interpretation, with training and testing data sets spanning different scRNA-Seq platforms and covering human/mouse samples (Fig. 1). This design enables rigorous assessment of model generalizability across platforms and species while maintaining biological interpretability.

This pipeline ensures rigorous cross-platform and cross-species validation.

Cell lines and data generation

We generated scRNA-seq data for two human leukemia cell lines: REH (ATCC #CRL-8286) and SUP-B15 (ATCC #CRL-1929), with cells maintained as described previously in [18]. We prepared 10× scMultiome libraries for each cell line as previously [19] and sequenced them using Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The scMultiome assay captures both gene expression (scRNA-seq) and chromatin accessibility (scATAC-seq) from the same cells; however, only the RNA expression data was used in this study, with the ATAC-seq data being reserved for future projects.

These datasets (REH and SUP-B15) are publicly available under GEO accession [20] GSE293316.

Training dataset selection criteria

To investigate whether models trained on different biological contexts generalize to cell cycle prediction benchmarks, we trained models on six datasets with distinct cellular characteristics.

GSE293316 (REH/SUP-B15 human B-cell acute leukemia cell line, RNA expression data derived from 10× scMultiome)

Actively cycling leukemia cell lines profiled using droplet-based UMI capture chemistry. These datasets exhibit similar sparsity structure and capture characteristics and were included to evaluate model behavior under matched technical conditions as well as across closely related biological contexts.

GSE75748 (human embryonic stem cell–derived differentiation dataset, scRNA-seq)

This dataset showed the highest cell cycle gene detection rate among training datasets This dataset was profiled across undifferentiated H1 and H9 human ES cells, their derived progenitor cells (neuronal progenitor cells, definitive endoderm cells, endothelial cells, and trophoblast-like cells), and human foreskin fibroblast cells, totaling 1776 single cells [21].

10× Human PBMC (scRNA-seq)

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells representing heterogeneous immune populations, including T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, and monocytes. This dataset predominantly comprises quiescent immune cells and was included to evaluate model behavior in biologically heterogeneous, low-proliferation contexts [11].

10×10 k E18 mouse brain (pure scRNA-seq)

Embryonic cortical, hippocampal, and subventricular zone cells, primarily post-mitotic neurons with minimal cell cycle activity (15.2% canonical markers detected, mean expression = 0.17) and G1-dominated distribution [22].

Nestorowa [23] (mouse hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, Smart-seq2 scRNA-seq; GEO: GSE81682)

A single-cell transcriptomic dataset profiling mouse hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) populations using full-length Smart-seq2 chemistry. This dataset captures transcriptional heterogeneity across stem-to-progenitor transitions and was included to evaluate model performance in actively cycling hematopoietic populations under cross-species conditions.

Because PBMC and embryonic mouse brain datasets comprise heterogeneous cell populations that differ substantially from the primary training data, models trained on these datasets showed reduced generalization to FUCCI/FACS-labeled benchmarks. All four datasets were processed identically using consensus labeling approach and the same model architectures for model training described below. This experimental design enabled systematic evaluation of how training data—independent of sequencing platform and cell biology sources—impacts performance validation using FUCCI/FACS-labeled benchmarks.

Detailed cell cycle gene expression statistics and phase distribution comparisons across all datasets are provided in the Supplementary Materials, Section 3.5.

Benchmark datasets

We evaluated the models on three independent benchmarks with experimentally validated labels: GSE146773 (1152 human osteosarcoma U2OS cells with FUCCI-based labels, Smart-Seq platform) [13], GSE64016 (247 human embryonic stem H1 cells with FUCCI-based labels, Fluidigm C1 platform using SMARTer/SMART-Seq chemistry) [12], Buettner mESC (288 mouse embryonic stem cells with ground-truth G1/S/G2M labels from Hoechst 33342 staining and FACS sorting, scRNA-Seq data generated through the Fluidigm C1 protocol, available via Bioconductor version 3.21) [17]. For each training dataset, we used training-specific gene intersections with all benchmarks to avoid imputation artifacts while ensuring a fair evaluation (details in the Supplementary Materials, Section 3.4).

Standardized preprocessing pipeline

All datasets were processed using a unified pipeline to ensure comparability:

Normalization

Log-normalization with scaling factor (Seurat NormalizeData) [7]. Only the benchmark dataset GSE146773 was provided in log-normalized format; therefore, no additional log-normalization was applied.

Training-specific gene intersection strategy

Feature selection was performed prior to model training by defining a training-specific gene intersection across the selected training dataset and all benchmark datasets, thereby ensuring a shared gene feature space and avoiding imputation of missing genes during cross-dataset evaluation.

Scaling

RobustScaler [14] fit on training data and applied consistently to all benchmarks.

Quality control

Filtered cells based on genes count thresholds and mitochondrial content.

Tool selection for consensus

We selected four tools representing distinct methodological families to generate consensus labels:

Seurat CellCycleScore: Marker-gene based scoring [7]

Tricycle: Projection-based circular regression [5]

Revelio: Latent cycle inference using PCA and gene scoring [8]

ccAFv2: Supervised ML [9]

These tools provide methodological diversity (rule-based, ML-based, and latent-variable approaches). We evaluated additional methods (e.g. Cyclum [10]) during preliminary experiments, but they showed lower accuracy or unstable predictions on FUCCI benchmarks. Including such tools substantially reduced consensus agreement and training set size. Using the four methods provided optimal balance between diversity, reliability, and sufficient high-confidence cells for training.

Machine learning models

We implemented and compared multiple neural network architectures alongside traditional machine learning baselines [25]. Traditional machine learning models included Random Forest (ensemble of decision trees with bootstrap aggregation), AdaBoost (adaptive boosting with sequential weak learner training), and LightGBM (gradient boosting framework optimized for efficiency and accuracy). These methods establish performance baselines and enable comparison between classical statistical learning and deep learning approaches.

Our deep learning implementations encompass several architectural paradigms. The DNN3 architecture employs a three-layer dense network with 256, 128, and 64 neurons per layer, utilizing ReLU activation functions and dropout regularization (with dropout rate = 0.3) to prevent overfitting. Building upon this foundation, DNN5 extends the architecture to five layers and incorporates an attention mechanism that learns to weight features based on their relevance to cell cycle prediction, enabling the model to focus on the most informative gene expression patterns. The CNN architecture applies convolutional layers (32 and 64 filters) with max pooling operations to capture local patterns and co-expression relationships within the gene expression profiles. The Hybrid CNN-Dense model combines convolutional feature extraction pathways with dense integration layers, leveraging both local pattern detection and global feature integration. The Feature Embedding model first applies dimensionality reduction through a learned embedding layer before performing classification, creating a compressed representation that captures essential cell cycle-related variance.

To enhance predictive robustness and reduce model-specific biases, we developed three ensemble strategies. The Top-3 Decision Fusion approach implements majority voting across the three best-performing deep learning models, combining their discrete predictions. The Top-3 Score Fusion method averages probability scores from the three deep learning models before making final predictions, incorporating prediction confidence into the ensemble decision. The Embedding3TML strategy learns a combined representation from three traditional machine learning models (Random Forest, AdaBoost, LightGBM), creating a meta-model that integrates their complementary strengths. Full architecture diagrams, layer-by-layer specifications, and hyperparameter configurations are provided in the Supplementary Materials, Section 4 (Supplementary Figure S6).

Training and optimization

Models were trained using nested cross-validation with Bayesian hyperparameter optimization via Optuna [24]. Detailed training procedures, regularization strategies, and optimization algorithms are described in the Supplementary Materials, Section 3.3 (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5).

Performance metrics

Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics while considering robustness to class imbalance. Standard accuracy provides overall correct prediction rates but is less reliable with imbalanced classes. F1-score computes the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) offers a balanced metric that is effective even with unequal class sizes. Cohen’s Kappa measures agreement beyond chance levels. Balanced accuracy computes average recall across classes to mitigate impact of class imbalance. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve (AUC) quantifies overall discriminative ability. Given that our problem is a 3-class problem, for AUC calculation, we used a one versus rest approach. Evaluation encompasses cross-platform validation (10× Chromium to Fluidigm C1 and Smart-Seq) and cross-species validation (human to mouse).

Interpretability analysis

We used SHAP [16] to identify features contributing to model predictions. SHAP assigns importance values to each gene based on its contribution to the output, enabling biological validation of learned features. SHAP values were computed using fixed models trained on the consensus-labeled training data and applied to training datasets or independent benchmark datasets, without retraining. This analysis identifies genes that drive model predictions in unseen data. We compared SHAP-ranked genes from DNN3 with established marker genes (Seurat + Revelio combined set) and performed enrichment analysis [26] to test biological relevance. To evaluate the biological relevance of the genes driving model predictions, we compared SHAP-ranked genes from DNN3 with established cell-cycle marker genes (combined Seurat and Revelio sets) and subsequently performed functional enrichment analysis using Metascape [26]. Metascape is a web-based platform for gene annotation and pathway enrichment that integrates over 40 curated biological databases, including Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG, and Reactome, enabling systematic interpretation of the functional roles and pathways associated with SHAP-identified genes.

Results

Statistics on consensus-based training sets

The consensus labeling process harmonized heterogeneous phase vocabularies from four tools into a unified three-class label space (G1, S, G2M). Agreement distribution varied across training datasets, with the percentage of cells retaining ≥3/4 tool consensus ranging from 24% to 66% (REH: 56%, SUP-B15: 51%, PBMC: 24%, Mouse Brain: 47%, GSE81682: 66%, GSE75748: 60.5%). Cells with <3/4 agreement were excluded to maintain label quality. Cells with <3/4 agreement were excluded to maintain label quality. The final training set contained high-confidence cells (REH: 4219, SUP-B15: 3998, PBMC: 2431, Mouse Brain: 3050, GSE75748: 1076, GSE81682: 514), substantially larger than any single FUCCI-labeled dataset. Detailed consensus heatmaps showing over-representations are provided in the Supplementary Fig. S3, Section 3.2.

Validation of consensus-based labeling approach

Before relying on consensus labels from the four available tools, we needed to ensure that the consensus approach produces labels reflective of known cell cycle phases. To validate this, we applied the consensus labeling approach to three benchmark datasets with experimentally derived ground truth labels, then compared the consensus labels against the ground truth. Table 2 shows the performance of the consensus approach on these benchmarks. The consensus approach demonstrated strong concordance with experimental ground truth across all three benchmarks. For the human datasets (GSE146773 and GSE64016), consensus labels achieved 73%–75% accuracy with Cohen’s kappa of 0.59–0.62, indicating substantial agreement. For the mouse dataset (Buettner mESC), consensus achieved 52% accuracy with kappa of 0.27, reflecting lower tool coverage (only 33% of cells reached ≥3 tool agreement).

Impact of training data characteristics on model performance

We observed that model generalization to FUCCI/FACS-labeled benchmarks correlated with the biological characteristics of the training data, including proliferative state and population composition, rather than sequencing platform alone (Fig. 4). Neural network models trained on actively cycling, relatively homogeneous populations (REH leukemia) achieved the highest mean accuracy on independent FUCCI/FACS-labeled benchmark datasets, while those trained on more heterogeneous or predominantly quiescent [27] populations (PBMC) demonstrated the lowest accuracy (Supplementary Figure S4).

Benchmark evaluation (GSE146773, GSE64016, Buettner mESC)

We evaluated model performance on three independent benchmarks: two human datasets with FUCCI-based experimental annotations (GSE146773: 1152 osteosarcoma cells, Smart-Seq; GSE64016: 247 embryonic stem cells, Fluidigm C1) and one cross-species dataset with FACS-sorted ground truth (Buettner mESC: 288 cells). For cross-species validation, model input features were defined as the intersection of genes shared between the training dataset and the three benchmark datasets to ensure consistent input dimensionality across evaluations. Figure 4 illustrates cross-dataset performance trends using AUC, F1, Kappa, Balanced Accuracy, and MCC. Across all three benchmarks, deep learning and ensemble-based approaches consistently demonstrated stronger phase discrimination, reflected in higher MCC and Kappa values.

On GSE146773, ensemble and deep learning models demonstrated strong and balanced discrimination, with Top-3 ensembles achieving highest accuracy (74.3%) and stable MCC (62%), while Random Forest reached peak AUC (89.2%), indicating robust phase separation across classes. On GSE64016, performance variability increased due to smaller sample size (247 cells), yet Top-3 ensembles and FE model maintained strong accuracy (71%–73%) and competitive MCC (59%–61%), with Top-3 SF achieving highest AUC (90.2%), reflecting robust class-balanced prediction despite dataset constraints. In the cross-species Buettner mESC benchmark, models preserved moderate AUC (67%–76%) and consistent MCC (30%–36%), confirming that REH-trained architectures capture conserved cell-cycle transcriptional signals transferable across species. Corresponding results for identical model architectures trained on hPSCs (GSE75748), PBMC, GSE81682, and mouse brain datasets and evaluated on the same three benchmarks, are provided in Supplementary Sections 5.1–5.4 (Figs. S7–S10, S11–S16 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/).

Figure 5 presents overall classification accuracy across the same benchmarks. On GSE146773, Top-3 ensembles achieved highest accuracy (74.3%), closely followed by DNN5 (74.0%) and DNN3 (73.4%), substantially exceeding several existing tools. On GSE64016, Top-3 ensembles (72.5%–72.9%) and FE model (71.3%) performed competitively with existing methods, demonstrating robust generalization despite smaller sample size. For Buettner mESC, DNN3 achieved 56.9% cross-species accuracy, followed by CNN and Adaboost (56.3%) and FE model (54.9%), demonstrating stable transfer of learned proliferative patterns from human leukemia cells to mouse embryonic stem cells.

Comparison with existing tools

While some existing tools achieved strong performance on individual benchmarks (SC1 [15]: 77.3% on GSE64016; Tricycle: 57.3% on Buettner mESC), our trained models demonstrated distinct advantages: On GSE146773 (largest benchmark, 1152 cells), ensemble models achieved 74.3% accuracy, outperforming all existing tools (best: Revelio 68.3%). More importantly, our models showed superior multi-metric balance: high MCC (60%–62%) and stable precision-recall across all three phases (Fig. 4), whereas existing tools often showed phase-specific biases. Additionally, once trained, our models provide unified single-framework predictions without requiring multiple tool installations or consensus voting, improving practical applicability while maintaining competitive cross-species generalization (56.9% on mouse data).

Class-wise performance analysis

Phase-specific precision and recall analyses across the three benchmarks (Fig. 6) reveal distinct patterns. On GSE146773, ensemble models achieved the most balanced performance across all phases (Top-3 SF: G1 85.3%/68.5%, G2M 74.4%/67.7%, S-phase 67.3%/88.0%), with FE model and DNN5 showing similar stability. G1 precision remained consistently high (78%–85%) while S-phase recall exceeded 83% across top models, confirming effective phase discrimination. On the smaller GSE64016 benchmark, FE model maintained strongest S-phase recall (97.5%) with stable G2M performance (77.6%), while Top-3 ensembles achieved balanced predictions across phases (G2M recall 77.6%, S-phase recall 96.3%). DNN3 showed high S-phase recall (92.5%) but lower G1 recall (47.3%), reflecting sensitivity to dataset size. For cross-species Buettner mESC, overall performance declined as expected. Models maintained high G2M recall (82%–89%) but struggled with S-phase classification (recall 29%–42%), reflecting increased difficulty transferring S-phase transcriptional patterns across species. DNN3 achieved highest S-phase recall (36.5%) among deep learning models. Overall, G2M predictions remained most stable across benchmarks, while S-phase classification proved most sensitive to dataset shift.

Model interpretability and biological validation

For SHAP [16] analysis, we selected DNN5 (Enhanced Dense model) as the top-performing standalone deep learning model, serving as a core component of the best ensemble approaches (Figs. 4–6). SHAP analysis was performed using the REH training dataset as the background distribution and the human FUCCI-labeled benchmark dataset GSE146773 as the foreground (Supplementary Figure S17). The top 787 SHAP-ranked genes were selected to match the size of the combined Seurat and Revelio marker gene list (n = 787). Functional enrichment analysis of these 787 SHAP-derived genes is shown in Fig. 7(a). The most significantly enriched biological process was SCF-dependent proteasomal ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process, with a − log10(P) value approaching 70, indicating extremely strong statistical significance. Additional highly enriched terms included translation factors (−log10(P) ≈ 50), regulation of translational initiation (−log10(P) ≈ 40), Fanconi anemia pathway (−log10(P) ≈ 15), DNA repair (−log10(P) ≈ 10), and cell population proliferation (−log10(P) ≈ 9). These pathways represent core regulatory mechanisms underlying cell cycle progression, including cyclin turnover via SCF-mediated ubiquitination, translational control of phase-specific transcripts, and maintenance of replication fidelity during S phase. Direct comparison between SHAP-ranked genes and canonical Seurat–Revelio marker genes is shown in Fig. 7(b). Of the 787 SHAP-identified genes and 787 marker genes, only 26 genes overlapped, while 761 genes were unique to each set. Thus, only 3.3% (26/787) of SHAP-ranked genes coincide with established marker genes, indicating that the Enhanced Dense model does not merely rediscover predefined phase-specific transcripts but instead identifies a largely distinct predictive gene set. For reference, enrichment analysis of the Seurat–Revelio marker genes is shown in Fig. 7c, where classical cell cycle–associated processes dominate, including cell cycle, mitotic cell cycle, regulation of cell cycle process, M phase, and DNA replication. Although gene-level overlap between SHAP-derived genes and canonical markers is limited (Fig. 7b), both gene sets converge at the pathway level on fundamental proliferative and genomic stability mechanisms (Fig. 7a and c).

To further assess cross-species robustness, we performed the same SHAP-based enrichment analysis using the best-performing Nestorowa-trained model (DNN3). The top 787 SHAP-ranked genes—selected to match the size of the combined Seurat–Revelio marker set—were analyzed under two configurations: using the human FUCCI-labeled dataset GSE146773 as the foreground and, separately, the Buettner mouse embryonic stem cell dataset as the foreground. In both cases, Metascape enrichment analysis revealed significant overrepresentation of conserved cell cycle–related processes, including DNA metabolic and RNA metabolic pathways. These findings indicate that SHAP-identified features derived from the mouse-trained model capture conserved proliferative programs that generalize across species. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Section 6, Interpretability of Mouse-Trained Models (Supplementary Figure S19).

All models were trained on a high-performance computing cluster with SLURM job scheduling using NVIDIA A100 GPUs (up to 80GB VRAM) and AMD EPYC 7513 32-core processors. Detailed computational cost analysis is provided in Supplementary Table S3, Section 6.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study systematically evaluated machine learning and deep learning frameworks for cell cycle phase prediction from scRNA-seq data. Our consensus-labeling approach harmonized predictions from four methodologically diverse tools (CellCycleScore, Tricycle, ccAFv2, Revelio), enabling the generation of high-quality training datasets larger than existing FUCCI-labeled ones. We evaluated the existing tools and machine learning models across three independent experimentally validated datasets (GSE146773, GSE64016, Buettner mESC) and cross-species (human versus mouse) benchmarks.

Key findings include: (i) Consensus-based labeling (≥3 of 4 tools agreeing) provides reliable cell cycle phase assignments, achieving 73%–75% accuracy and substantial agreement (Cohen’s kappa coefficient, κ = 0.59–0.62) with experimental ground truth on human benchmark datasets, validating this approach for generating training labels where ground truth is unavailable.

(ii) Deep learning models outperformed traditional ML across all benchmarks; (iii) DNN5 showed overall good performance in cross-platform and cross-species generalization; (iv) ensemble strategies (Top-3 DF, Embedding3TML) provided stable predictions; (v) SHAP analysis revealed biologically relevant features enriched in cell cycle pathways; (vi) Datasets with <20% detectable canonical cell cycle markers consistently underperformed in model training and should be used cautiously, as they provide limited phase-informative signal.

Practical guidelines for selecting appropriate cell cycle phase prediction model

Our systematic comparison provides evidence-based guidelines for selecting cell cycle prediction methods. Deep learning models excel when training data contains actively cycling cells with robust cell cycle gene expression (>40% canonical markers detected). However, 10× Chromium platforms (SUP-B15, PBMC, 10 k mouse brain cells) inherently produce sparse matrices with G1 phase bias, requiring approaches to handle data imbalance problems, such as SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) to balance underrepresented S and G2M phases. Despite SMOTE correction, 10× Chromium-trained models achieved 65%–75% accuracy on human benchmarks, reflecting technical constraints of sparse data. Beyond class balancing, the specific strategy used to handle gene sparsity is a critical determinant of model success (Supplementary Figure S5). Our findings indicate that imputing missing values with zeros or mean estimates severely degrades model performance by introducing artificial patterns and reducing the strength of genuine biological signals. Consequently, we recommend utilizing a gene intersection approach that restricts the feature set to genes shared between a specific training dataset and all target benchmarks to preserve data integrity and ensure fair comparison. In contrast, GSE75748 (Smart-Seq-like chemistry) exhibited well-balanced phase distributions (33% per phase) and achieved 64%–71% accuracy without requiring SMOTE. Despite differing marker detection rates (REH: 44%, GSE75748: 70%), both achieved comparable accuracies (64%–75%), demonstrating that marker recovery percentage is not a limiting factor. Instead, proper class balancing—whether through SMOTE augmentation or well-balanced natural distributions—enables effective deep learning regardless of platform sparsity. Further, the biological coherence of the training dataset—specifically, the presence of a relatively homogeneous, actively cycling cell population with consistent phase-specific transcriptional programs—emerged as a key determinant of cross-dataset generalization performance. Models trained on the relatively homogeneous and proliferative REH leukemia cell line achieved 64%–75% accuracy on independent human benchmarks, whereas models trained on biologically heterogeneous and largely quiescent PBMC samples showed substantially reduced benchmark performance (32%–50%), despite class rebalancing. In contrast, PBMC (mixed B cells, T cells, monocytes) achieved only 32%–50% despite SMOTE, indicating that cellular heterogeneity dilutes coherent cell-cycle signatures. Summary recommendation: Model selection should consider the biological composition of both the training and target datasets. When the training dataset consists of a relatively homogeneous and actively cycling population with robust phase-specific transcriptional signal, deep learning models demonstrate strong cross-dataset generalization. In contrast, when the training data are biologically heterogeneous and dominated by quiescent or mixed cell populations, marker-based methods (e.g. Seurat, Revelio) may provide more stable and interpretable phase assignments. Thus, the effectiveness of a given approach depends primarily on the strength and coherence of proliferative signal in the training data relative to the intended application dataset.

Comparison with alternative strategies

Why not ensemble predictions directly?

We did ensemble existing tools through consensus labeling (majority voting requiring ≥3 of 4 tools to agree after remapping outputs to G1/S/G2M). However, this consensus ensemble served to generate training labels rather than final predictions. As shown in Table 2, consensus achieved 73%–75% accuracy on human benchmarks.

Consensus bias mitigation

Consensus labeling can inherit systematic biases from constituent tools. We mitigated this by: (i) combining four methodologically diverse approaches (marker-based, supervised ML, projection-based, latent inference), diluting any single method’s bias, (ii) retaining only cells with multi-tool agreement after label harmonization, reducing tool-specific noise, and (iii) evaluating on independent FUCCI/FACS-labeled datasets. Any systematic training bias would manifest as poor benchmark performance. Our models instead achieved accuracy comparable to or exceeding state-of-the-art tools (DNN5: 70%–74.3%), validating the consensus approach. Cross-species validation (human training → mouse evaluation) further demonstrates that learned features generalize beyond training distribution.

Why ensemble models and DNN5 generalize best

Our analysis reveals that Top-3 ensemble approaches (Decision Fusion and Score Fusion) achieved superior performance across human benchmarks, reaching 74.3% accuracy on GSE146773 and 72.5–72.9% on GSE64016. Among individual deep learning models, DNN5 demonstrated strongest performance on large-scale human data (73.95% on GSE146773), likely stemming from: Architecture capacity: Five hidden layers with attention mechanisms provide sufficient depth to capture complex transcriptional patterns while batch normalization and dropout (0.3) prevent overfitting. Ensemble advantage: Top-3 fusion methods leverage complementary strengths of multiple architectures (likely DNN3, DNN5, and CNN/FE models), achieving consensus predictions that reduce individual model errors. Notably, ensembles maintained strong performance even on smaller datasets (GSE64016, n = 247), contradicting assumptions that ensembles require large samples. Cross-species trade-off: While DNN5 excelled on human benchmarks, DNN3 showed superior cross-species transfer to mouse data (56.94% versus 53.82% for DNN5), suggesting that moderate architectural depth (3 versus 5 layers) better generalizes conserved cell cycle signals across species without overfitting to human-specific patterns. For interpretability analysis (SHAP), we selected DNN5 as the best-performing individual architecture, as ensemble models aggregate predictions from multiple models rather than learning features independently.

Biological insights from SHAP

SHAP analysis revealed genes enriched in cell proliferation and mitotic regulation pathways. Novel genes identified beyond canonical markers were significantly enriched in G2/M transition, DNA metabolic processes, and chromosome organization. This suggests deep learning models can uncover less-characterized cycle-related genes, potentially identifying new biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Cross-species SHAP comparison reveals conserved versus species-specific cell cycle-related features.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our approach simplifies cell cycle labeling to three broad phases (G1, S, G2M), potentially missing finer biological distinctions such as quiescence (G0) or sub-phases (early versus late G1). Second, while we now demonstrate cross-species generalization (human ↔ mouse), evaluation across additional species and tissue types would further validate robustness. Third, consensus labeling, although validated by testing model predictions against experimental benchmarks, inherits some systematic tendencies from constituent tools. Future work could explore: (i) multi-class prediction including G0 and sub-phases, (ii) integration of multi-omic data (ATAC-seq) for improved accuracy, and (iii) extension to organisms beyond human and mouse.

Conclusion

We developed and validated a systematic evaluation framework for cell cycle phase prediction from scRNA-seq data, demonstrating that deep learning models—particularly ensemble approaches—achieve robust performance across platforms and species. Our consensus training strategy, comprehensive evaluation across multiple experimentally validated benchmarks, and biological validation through interpretability analysis establish practical guidelines for model selection. These findings support the adoption of deep learning for cell cycle analysis in diverse biological contexts and provide a foundation for future multi-omic and cross-species studies.
