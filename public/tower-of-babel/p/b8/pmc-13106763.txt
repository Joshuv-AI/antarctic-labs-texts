A trimeric architecture reveals the glucitol PTS transporter as a distinct superfamily
Authors: Tingyue Deng, Xueli Liu, Jianwei Zeng, Xiaofei Ge, Jiawei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The bacterial phosphoenolpyruvate:sugar phosphotransferase system (PTS) catalyzes the transport and phosphorylation of carbohydrates. The glucitol (Gut) PTS transporter from Escherichia coli has often been discussed in relation to the Glucose–Fructose–Lactose (GFL) superfamily, although other work has suggested that it may instead form a separate PTS superfamily. This uncertainty is linked to its unusual genetic organization, in which the transmembrane IIC domain is divided into two polypeptides (IIC1/GutE and IIC2/GutA). Here, we present the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the complete Gut transporter, which resolves this discrepancy by revealing a homotrimeric architecture for its transmembrane domain—a fold unprecedented among sugar-transporting PTS permeases. This structural evidence supports the view that the Gut family represents a distinct PTS superfamily. Within the trimer, the protomers are captured in inward-facing and inward-occluded conformations, providing a structural basis for an alternating-access transport mechanism. Furthermore, the structure suggests a unique in-trans phosphotransfer pathway between the IIB and IIC domains of adjacent subunits and identifies the substrate-binding pocket at the GutA/GutE interface. Our work redefines the structural landscape of PTS transporters and provides a mechanistic framework for sugar transport by this unique trimeric porter.

Introduction

The bacterial phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP): sugar phosphotransferase system is a multifaceted pathway that mediates the uptake and concomitant phosphorylation of carbohydrates, while also serving as a central regulator of carbon and nitrogen metabolism1. Ubiquitous among eubacteria but absent in eukaryotes, the PTS comprises two general cytoplasmic components—enzyme I and HPr—that sequentially transfer a phosphoryl group from PEP to a variety of sugar-specific enzyme II complexes. These membrane-associated complexes typically consist of IIA, IIB, and IIC domains or subunits, which cooperate to phosphorylate the substrate as it is transported across the membrane2,3.

Based on phylogenetic analyses of their IIC transmembrane components, PTS transporters are classified into seven families (4.A.1–4.A.7)4–6. These are further grouped into three structurally defined superfamilies: the Glucose–Fructose–Lactose (GFL; 4.A.1–4.A.4), Ascorbate–Galactitol (AG; 4.A.5 and 4.A.7), and Mannose (Man; 4.A.6) superfamilies (https://www.tcdb.org)7. Members of the GFL8–13 and AG14–16 superfamilies are homodimers, with each protomer operating through an elevator-type alternating-access mechanism17. In contrast, the Man family forms a homotrimer18, but functions primarily as a receptor for phages19 and bacteriocins20–23, as well as a principal sugar transporter.

Historically, the glucitol (Gut; 4.A.4) family has usually been assigned to the GFL superfamily7. However, this classification is inconsistent with its unique genetic organization: its IIC transmembrane component is split into two separate polypeptides, IIC1 (GutE) and IIC2 (GutA), with the IIB domain fused to the N terminus of IIC124,25. This unusual architecture raises fundamental questions about the true quaternary structure of the Gut transporter and its mechanistic relationship to the canonical dimeric GFL fold.

Here, we report the cryo-electron microscopy structure of the E. coli glucitol transporter. The transmembrane region assembles as a homotrimer. This organization is clearly different from the dimeric transporters of the GFL and AG superfamilies, although trimeric architectures are also known within the PTS, such as in the Man family. The Gut transporter nevertheless adopts a distinct structural layout and functional arrangement, consistent with its classification as a separate PTS superfamily. This structure provides a basis for understanding both its architecture and mechanism.

Results

Architecture of a trimeric PTS transporter

The E. coli glucitol-specific PTS transporter comprises three subunits: GutE (a fusion of IIB and IIC1), GutA (IIC2), and GutB (IIA). For structural analysis, the full complex was overexpressed in E. coli, solubilized from membranes, and purified. Single-particle cryo-EM yielded a 3D reconstruction at 3.44 Å resolution (Supplementary Fig. 1, Table 1), enabling de novo model building of the transmembrane module (Supplementary Fig. 2). This module consists of the C-terminal IIC1 domain of GutE and the IIC2 domain of GutA. The most striking feature of the structure is its homotrimeric assembly (Fig. 1A, B), which contrasts sharply with the canonical homodimers observed in other PTS transporters of the GFL superfamily. The trimer adopts an asymmetric tripod-like architecture, deviating from perfect C3 symmetry, with a height of 5.6 nm. It tapers from a 10.0 nm-wide cytoplasmic base to an 8.4 nm-wide periplasmic apex. The three protomers exist in two distinct conformational states: two in an inward-occluded state (Protomers 1 and 2) and one in an inward-facing state (Protomer 3*). Each protomer comprises tightly interleaved transmembrane helices from GutE-IIC1 and GutA-IIC2. Trimer stability is maintained by two interlocking structural layers: a periplasmic collar formed by long tilted helices from GutA, and a cytoplasmic collar composed of long helices from GutE. Scaffold and transport domain organization

Each protomer is composed of GutE and GutA, each contributing three transmembrane helices (TMs), two hairpins (HPs), and an amphipathic helix (AH) (Fig. 2A). The two subunits exhibit a twofold pseudo-symmetry perpendicular to the membrane plane, but differ at the HPa–HPb connection: a short α-helix in GutE replaces a flexible loop in GutA. Based on this topology, each subunit can be divided into a scaffold domain (SD) and a transport domain (TD). TM1 constitutes the SD, while TM2, TM3, and the hairpins form the TD, with the AH linking the two (Fig. 2B). The subunits adopt opposite orientations across the membrane: GutE has its N terminus in the cytoplasm and C terminus in the periplasm, whereas GutA is inverted. Within the trimer, the GutE-SD layer lies approximately 1 nm below the GutA-SD layer (Fig. 2C).

The substrate-binding site is formed at the interface of GutE-TD and GutA-TD (Fig. 2D). TM2 and both HPs from each subunit converge to enclose the glucitol molecule (Fig. 2E). The structural separation between a rigid SD—responsible for trimerization, and a mobile TD—harboring the substrate, supports an elevator-type transport mechanism (Fig. 2F), wherein the TD undergoes rigid-body displacement relative to the SD to move the substrate across the membrane.

Conformational rearrangements confirmed an elevator-type transport mechanism

The structure captured two conformational states, inward-occluded and inward-facing (Fig. 3A). In Protomers 1 and 2, the binding site is occluded within the membrane; in Protomer 3*, it opens toward the cytoplasm. The internal structures of the scaffold and transport domains remain remarkably rigid during the conformational transition. This is evidenced by the low root-mean-square deviation (r.m.s.d.) values of 0.47 Å for the SDs (91 Cα atoms) and 0.36 Å for the TDs (222 Cα atoms) upon superposition of Protomers 1/2 and Protomer 3*. This structural rigidity strongly supports the model of the TD undergoing a rigid-body elevator-like movement relative to the stationary SD (Supplementary Video). Alignment of the two states based on the stable SDs reveals a rigid-body displacement of the TD by ~4.5 Å (Fig. 3B–C), moving the substrate-binding site from the membrane midplane toward the cytoplasmic side. Structural considerations suggest a potential in-trans phosphotransfer mechanism

The cytoplasmic IIB domain of GutE (residues 1–153) was modeled using AlphaFold226 and positioned relative to the experimentally determined transmembrane module (GutE-IIC1, residues 154–end). To explore possible spatial arrangements of the IIB domain, we aligned the α-helical segment observed in our cryo-EM structure of GutE with the corresponding helix in the AlphaFold-predicted full-length GutE model. In this configuration, the phosphorylatable Cys71 residue from the IIB domain of one protomer is positioned in proximity to the substrate-binding pocket of a neighboring protomer (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, access of Cys71 to the substrate-binding site within the same protomer appears geometrically less favorable in this particular arrangement.

This structural observation suggests the possibility of an in-trans phosphotransfer event, in which the phosphoryl group attached to Cys71 is transferred to the O6 atom of glucitol bound in an adjacent protomer (Fig. 4A, B). However, given the flexibility of the IIB–IIC1 linker, alternative configurations, including a more conventional in-cis phosphotransfer mechanism, cannot be excluded.

To assess the functional importance of Cys71, we generated a Cys71→Ser mutant and performed complementation assays in a ΔgutBEA strain grown with glucitol as the primary carbon source. The Cys71→Ser mutation resulted in a marked reduction in cell viability compared with the wild-type control (Fig. 4C), confirming Cys71 as a critical catalytic residue in GutE (Fig. 4D).

Discussion

Based on our structural data, we propose that the glucitol transporter operates through an elevator-like mechanism to translocate the substrate across the membrane (Fig. 5). In this model, the trimeric SDs remain stationary, while the TDs undergo rigid-body movements that alternately expose the substrate-binding site to either the periplasm or the cytoplasm. The transport cycle likely begins with the TD in an outward-facing conformation, permitting glucitol binding from the periplasm (Step 1). The TD then progresses through an inward-occluded intermediate (Step 2-3), before adopting an inward-facing state to release the substrate into the cytoplasm (Step 4-5). Meanwhile, the soluble IIA domain transfers a phosphoryl group from the PEP-dependent phosphorylation cascade to the IIB domain of GutE (Step 3). The IIB domain may subsequently donate the phosphoryl group to the glucitol molecule bound in the TD, potentially occurring between adjacent protomers in an in-trans manner (Step 4–5). Given the flexibility of the IIB–IIC1 linker, alternative configurations, including in-cis phosphotransfer within the same protomer, cannot be excluded. Our structure establishes the glucitol (Gut) transporter as the founding member of a distinct PTS superfamily, characterized by its unique homotrimeric architecture. This finding resolves the long-standing paradox arising from its atypical gene organization and provides a refined structural framework for understanding bacterial sugar uptake systems. The trimeric assembly, stabilized by interlocking SD collars, is fundamentally different from the dimeric architectures of the GFL and AG superfamilies.

The conformational heterogeneity observed within the trimer—capturing both inward-facing and inward-occluded states—provides direct structural support for an elevator-type mechanism. The large-scale rigid-body movement of the TD, enabled by flexible amphipathic linkers, mirrors motions seen in other elevator-type transporters17. However, a potential coupling between these conformational transitions and an inter-protomer phosphorylation event represents a previously unexplored possibility. Such an arrangement would imply that the trimer could function as an integrated unit, in which the conformational state of one protomer may influence the activity of its neighbors, potentially allowing allosteric coordination across the complex.

A potential in-trans phosphotransfer mechanism enabled by the trimeric architecture represents an intriguing possibility suggested by our structural model. By positioning the IIB domain of one protomer near the substrate-binding site of another, the transporter may allow cytoplasmic phosphorylation to be coordinated with transmembrane transport within a compact trimeric framework. Such an arrangement could help explain the evolutionary conservation of the trimeric state by creating transient catalytic interfaces at protomer junctions. The use of phosphorylation to facilitate TD resetting would directly link chemical energy input to completion of the transport cycle, although further experimental validation will be required.

While our structure provides a compelling framework for this mechanism, several questions remain open for future study. The precise sequence of events linking phosphorylation to domain resetting, and the potential cooperativity among protomers during the transport cycle, remain to be elucidated. Nevertheless, this structure fills a critical gap in our understanding of the PTS by suggesting a previously unrecognized mode by which transport and phosphorylation could be coupled through a cooperative trimeric assembly. It provides a new conceptual framework for understanding the evolution and functional diversification of bacterial carbohydrate uptake systems.

Methods

Plasmid construction, expression and purification

The Escherichia coli genes gutA (YP_026180.1), gutE (YP_026181.1), and gutB (NP_417184.1) were cloned into a single plasmid using the pQLink system, with a C-terminal 6×His tag appended to gutA. The plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) and cultured overnight at 37°C. Cells were inoculated into LB medium (1% v/v) and induced at OD₆₀₀ ≈ 1.0 by lowering the temperature to 18°C and adding IPTG. After 18 h of induction, cells were collected and resuspended in lysis buffer (pH 7.5 TRIS 25 mM, NaCl 150 mM, Glucitol 2 mM). Cell disruption was performed by high-pressure homogenization, followed by sequential centrifugation at 12,000 × g (10 min) and 41,000 × g (1 h) to isolate the membrane fraction. Membranes were solubilized in 2% DDM at 4°C for 2 h, and insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation at 41,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was loaded onto a Ni²⁺–NTA column, washed with buffer containing 25 mM imidazole and 0.02% DDM, and eluted with buffer containing 250 mM imidazole and 0.02% DDM. The eluate was concentrated to ~5 mg mL⁻¹ using a 30 kDa MWCO Amicon filter and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column equilibrated with lysis buffer containing 0.07% digitonin. Protein purity was verified by SDS–PAGE.

Functional complementation and growth curve assay

Escherichia coli C600 was used as the strain background for functional complementation assays. The gutB–gutE–gutA gene cluster was replaced with a kanamycin resistance cassette using the λ Red recombination system. The resulting knockout strain was transformed with either the empty pQlink vector, pQlink-gutB–gutE–gutA, or pQlink-gutB–gutE(C71S)–gutA. Single colonies were inoculated into LB medium and grown overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm to saturation. Cultures were diluted 1% (v/v) into fresh LB medium and induced with IPTG at a final concentration of 0.5 mM. After induction for 6 h at 37 °C, cultures were diluted 0.1% (v/v) into M9 minimal medium containing 0.5% (w/v) sorbitol as the primary carbon source. Because E. coli C600 is auxotrophic, the M9 medium was supplemented with L-leucine (0.1 mg mL⁻¹), L-threonine (0.1 mg mL⁻¹), and thiamine (vitamin B₁, 5 μg mL⁻¹). Cell growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) at the indicated time points. Each condition was performed with six independent biological replicates.

Statistics and reproducibility

Growth curve experiments were performed with four independently grown cultures per strain (n = 4) under identical experimental conditions. For each culture, optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) was measured at the indicated time points from the same culture over time. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (S.E.M) unless otherwise stated. No data points were excluded from the analyses.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

Protein at a concentration of 4 mg/mL were applied (4 μL) onto glow-discharged Cu 300 mesh grids (Quantifoil R1.2/1.3). Grids were glow-discharged for 30 s at medium intensity using a Harrick Plasma cleaner (PDC-32G-2) after a 2 min evacuation. Vitrification was carried out using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 8°C and 100% humidity. After a 45 s equilibration period, grids were blotted from both sides with a blot force of −1 for 3.5 s using filter paper, and then plunge-frozen in liquid ethane.

Cryo-EM data acquisition

Grid quality was check using a Talos Arctica transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 200 kV equipped with a K2 Summit camera (Gatan). High-resolution data were collected on a Titan Krios G4 electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 kV and equipped with a Falcon 4 direct electron detector. Micrographs were recorded at a nominal magnification of 130,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.96 Å/pixel. A total of 13,876 movie stacks were collected with an accumulated electron dose of ~48 e⁻/Å² per stack. Automated data collection was performed using EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Image processing and 3D reconstruction

For EM image processing, motion correction was executed using MotionCor2 (v1.4.7)27. CTF estimation was conducted using CryoSPARC28. Micrographs with a CTF fitting resolution worse than 6 Å were excluded.

All subsequent data processing steps were carried out in cryoSPARC. After particle picking and manual inspection, a total of 4,146,795 particles were extracted from the micrographs. Several rounds of 2D classification were performed, resulting in 1,092,329 high-quality particles that were used for ab initio model generation. A well-defined class was selected for a second round of ab initio reconstruction, yielding a homogeneous subset of 237,825 particles. These particles were re-extracted at full resolution (bin1, 320 pixels) and subjected to non-uniform refinement with C1 symmetry, resulting in a final map at 3.44 Å overall resolution.

Protein model building and structure refinement

Protein models were built de novo using EMBuilder29. Subsequent manual adjustments to the models were performed using UCSF Chimera30 or COOT31. The refinement of the protein structures was conducted using either PHENIX32 or Refmac533. Statistical details regarding the 3D reconstruction and model refinement processes are provided in Table 1. Visual representations of the structures were generated using PyMol34 or UCSF ChimeraX35.
