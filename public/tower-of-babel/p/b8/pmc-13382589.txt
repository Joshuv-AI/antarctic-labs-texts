Spatial trade-offs between environmental sustainability and crop production in China under SDG-aligned scenarios
Authors: Yetong Li, Shiliang Liu, Xutong Wu, Shuang Song, Yifei Zhao, Yuanze Li, Wanting Wang, Jingyang Fan, Ziang Tian, Jiayi Lin, Zongyi Jin, Gang Wu, Lam-Son Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryReconciling lower environmental impacts from agriculture with food security is a core national objective in China. We develop spatially explicit projections for nine major crops to 2030 and 2060 under Business-as-Usual (BAU), BAU with carbon-efficiency constraints (BAU-CE), and Sustainable Development Goal (SDG)-aligned pathway (SDG-60). Despite improved input-use efficiency, national output declines modestly by 2030 relative to 2020 (−11.4% BAU and −11.5% BAU-CE). By 2060, efficiencies approach ∼90%, with substantial reductions in aggregate inputs (fertilizer −30%, pesticides −50%, and irrigation water −34.4%). Under SDG-60, cropping greenhouse-gas emissions are ∼72% lower than under BAU-CE, while aggregate production across the nine crops declines by ∼50.5%. Spatial heterogeneity reveals trade-off zones where large mitigation gains and reduced chemical and water use coincide with output and income losses, particularly in rice-intensive provinces. Provinces with below-median labor productivity and income but above-median mitigation burdens are identified as structurally disadvantaged, highlighting where differentiated targets, incentives, and transition support are most needed to balance mitigation, food security, and rural livelihoods.

Introduction

Achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement requires a rapid global transition toward net-zero emissions, not only in the energy and industrial sectors but also in land-based systems such as agriculture.1,2,3,4,5 Agriculture and related activities contribute approximately one-quarter of the global greenhouse-gas (GHG) emissions, yet receive relatively less attention in global mitigation strategies.4,6 While input-intensive farming systems have historically driven yield growth, they have also generated substantial environmental costs, especially through the overuse of fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation water.4,5,7 As the climate crisis deepens, identifying pathways for agricultural sustainability without compromising food security becomes a global imperative.

As the world’s largest agricultural producer and consumer, China occupies a central position in global efforts to align food systems with climate goals.8 Over the past four decades, its domestic food security has largely been secured through production gains achieved by intensifying input use, particularly chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and groundwater irrigation. This strategy, while effective in boosting yields, has led to mounting ecological degradation, including soil nutrient imbalance, water pollution, and excessive carbon emissions from farmland.5,9 These environmental challenges jeopardize multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly those related to Zero Hunger (Sustainable Development Goal 2 [SDG 2]), Clean Water and Sanitation (SDG 6), Responsible Consumption and Production (SDG 12), and Climate Action (SDG 13).2,10,11,12 In response, China launched the National Sustainable Agricultural Development Plan (2015–2030), aiming to enhance resource use efficiency, reduce environmental externalities, and promote green development.13 While this marks an important policy step, agriculture still lacks explicit sectoral GHG targets compared with energy and transport, raising questions about its alignment with the national 2060 carbon neutrality goal.2,14,15

Despite remarkable yield gains since the Green Revolution, China’s agricultural productivity is now approaching biophysical and technological plateaus.16,17 The diminishing marginal returns from conventional input intensification are further exacerbated by demographic and structural constraints. Over 230 million smallholder farmers manage fragmented plots averaging just 0.52 ha, and the rural labor force is aging rapidly, with younger generations migrating to urban centers.18,19,20 These trends restrict both the adoption of modern technologies and the institutional capacity for large-scale agricultural transformation. At the same time, the pressure to feed a growing and increasingly affluent population continues to rise. Ensuring national food security while reducing emissions and improving rural livelihoods thus presents a 3-fold challenge that is particularly acute in systems dominated by smallholders.21,22 The complexity and diversity of China’s agricultural landscape make it a critical case study for understanding how sustainability transitions may unfold in developing-country contexts.

Against this backdrop, we examine the system-level implications of sustainability interventions. While smallholder dominance provides essential policy context, we do not model household behavior or farm-operation types. All quantitative results are produced at national and provincial scales using gridded projections. Bridging the agricultural productivity gap between China and developed economies often requires further intensification of inputs such as fertilizers, water, and land.10,12,23,24,25 However, such input-led strategies conflict with mitigation and environmental objectives, especially where agroecological capacity is already stressed. While household-level responses in China’s smallholder settings remain an important research frontier, they are beyond the scope of this study. Here, we assess the spatial consequences at national, provincial, and grid scales. Scenario-based assessments of agricultural sustainability have employed process-based crop models (e.g., the DSSAT and EPIC model families), economy-wide and sectoral models (e.g., computable general equilibrium and partial-equilibrium frameworks), and data-driven or machine learning (ML) approaches.26,27,28,29,30 These studies have advanced the understanding of how climate and policy shocks affect yields, land and water use, and emissions. However, four limitations remain common. First, many analyses report national totals or focus on single provinces or crops, offering limited spatial detail for policy design. Second, most studies examine yields/production or emissions in isolation; few present integrated projections of inputs (fertilizer, pesticides, and irrigation), emissions, production, and farm economics (costs and profits) within a unified framework. Third, spatial cross-validation and operational scenario parameter tables are not consistently reported, complicating reproducibility and comparison across scenarios. Fourth, while trade-offs are acknowledged in aggregate, the locations where mitigation efforts coincide with losses in output and farm income are rarely mapped in a way that supports region-specific targets and compensation.

Here, we provide a unified, spatially explicit projection system that links national, provincial, and grid scales for nine major crops to 2030 and 2060 under three scenarios (Business-as-Usual [BAU], BAU with carbon-efficiency constraints [BAU-CE], and SDG-60). Methodologically, we implement a side-by-side Random Forest/Extreme Gradient Boosting (RF/XGBoost) ensemble with spatial blocking to reduce cross-fold spatial autocorrelation leakage, and we report model agreement. Substantively, we jointly project production, GHG emissions, fertilizer and pesticide use, irrigation water demand, input costs, and net profits, thereby quantifying multi-objective trade-offs across SDG-aligned pathways. To inform policy design, we operationalize and map trade-off zones where mitigation and input reductions coincide with output and profit declines and identify structurally disadvantaged provinces with below-median agricultural labor productivity and income but above-median mitigation burdens. Together, these advances clarify where differentiated targets, incentives, and transition support are most needed to reconcile climate mitigation with food security and farmer livelihoods.

Results

Sustainability trade-offs between environmental protection and food security

Figure 1 summarizes the framework and national production totals. By 2030, projected improvements in input efficiency are substantial across all three scenarios. Fertilizer use efficiency is expected to increase by 14.4% on average, with total fertilizer input reduced by 7.5%. Pesticide efficiency improves by 10.8%, while total use declines by 12.5%; irrigation water efficiency rises by 6.2%, with total irrigation remaining roughly stable (Figure 1A). However, these efficiency gains are insufficient to maintain baseline crop output. Based on the equal-weighted ensemble mean of the RF and XGBoost models, national crop production in 2030 is projected to decline by 11.4% under the BAU scenario and by 11.5% under the BAU-CE scenario relative to 2020. Under the SDG-60 pathway, which imposes stricter sustainability constraints, production declines by 2.9% in 2030 (AVG in Figure 1B). The near equivalence of production outcomes under BAU and BAU-CE in 2030 reflects the fact that both scenarios inherit post-2015 input control policies and approach similar efficiency plateaus in the near term. As a result, the additional carbon-efficiency requirement embedded in BAU-CE is not yet binding by 2030. By contrast, the smaller production decline under SDG-60 at this horizon reflects that the most stringent constraints in this pathway are not fully binding in the near term under our operationalization (Figure 1B and Table 1). Divergence across scenarios becomes pronounced only over longer horizons, as cumulative GHG constraints tighten toward 2060. Results are consistent across the RF and XGBoost models, supporting the robustness of the ensemble-based estimates. By 2060, China’s agricultural input efficiencies are projected to approach levels observed in developed nations. Fertilizer, pesticide, and irrigation water efficiencies are expected to reach approximately 90%, enabled by advancements in precision agriculture, soil testing, and improved irrigation infrastructure. These efficiency gains are accompanied by substantial reductions in input use, with fertilizer consumption falling by 30%, pesticide use by 50%, and irrigation demand by 34.4% relative to 2020 baselines (Figure 1A). Yet the benefits of high input efficiency come at a steep cost to food production under stringent sustainability constraints. In the SDG-60 scenario, national crop output is projected to decline by 50.5% in 2060, compared to just 6.5% under BAU and 1.0% under BAU-CE. This steep reduction in output under the SDG-60 pathway is primarily driven by strict limitations on input intensification and higher emission reduction requirements. Agricultural GHG emissions decrease by 72.1% relative to the BAU-CE scenario, indicating substantial mitigation success, although this reduction is accompanied by increased risks to food self-sufficiency. Rather than inferring import shares, our results indicate heightened risks to domestic self-sufficiency under SDG-60 unless productivity-enhancing innovations materialize; a rigorous assessment of import needs requires explicit demand and trade modeling, which is beyond our scope.

Impacts of the crop production system based on structural pathways

To complement the ML projections, we use structural equation modeling (SEM) to characterize how input intensification, agronomic performance, and environmental and economic outcomes co-vary in historical data. SEM provides a pathway view of the system (associations, not causal effects), helping interpret where efficiency gains may or may not translate into production and profit, and thereby complementing the spatial patterns identified by the ML-based projections.

Crop production in China is shaped by intricate interactions among input intensities, agronomic outcomes, and broader socio-environmental consequences. To examine these relationships, we employed SEM on the statistical data of relevant indicators in 2000, 2010, and 2020 to evaluate both direct and indirect effects across the input-output system (Figure 2), focusing on the roles of pesticides, fertilizers, irrigation water, and production costs in determining crop performance and sustainability outcomes. The SEM indicates that fertilizer input is the single strongest correlate of production quantity (standardized coefficient = 1.057, p < 0.001). Pesticides (0.026), irrigation water (0.008), and production costs (0.053) also contribute positively, albeit with smaller magnitudes. By contrast, the link to yield is extremely limited; although production costs (0.023) are positively associated with yield, the explained variance is negligible (R2 = 0.003), consistent with limited per-unit efficiency gains despite higher total inputs.Figure 2SEM illustrating the relationships among sustainability dimensions in China’s crop production system∗∗Asterisks indicate statistical significance, with ∗p < 0.01. From an environmental perspective, GHG emissions (R2 = 0.580) rise with both fertilizers (0.153) and production quantity (0.048), with production costs contributing an additional indirect pathway. Yield shows a small negative association with emissions (−0.080), suggesting scope for efficiency-oriented mitigation. Overall, the pathways underscore that intensification, especially fertilizer-led intensification, coincides with higher emissions. The economic implications are mixed. Net profit (R2 = 0.860) correlates positively with production quantity (0.018) but negatively with production costs (−0.614), revealing a profitability trade-off in high-input systems. Emissions also show a positive association with profit (0.774), indicating that current revenue structures are tightly coupled with carbon-intensive practices. Land use, measured as harvested area, mediates several pathways by increasing fertilizer application and affecting cost structures, while its association with production quantity (−0.442) and with GHG is near zero and directionally uncertain (coefficient −0.011).

Taken together, this SEM analysis suggests that China’s current cropping system remains fertilizer dependent for production expansion, with embedded environmental and economic trade-offs. Shifting toward input efficiency and decoupling profitability from emissions is critical to aligning agricultural development with national sustainability and climate targets. Strategic investments in precision fertilization, cost-effective irrigation, and low-emission technologies are, therefore, essential.

Impacts of agricultural SDG pathways on crop production

Projected changes in crop production quantities reveal pronounced regional disparities across China’s agricultural landscape (Figures 3A–3R, S2, and S3). Spatially, significant yield losses are observed across more than half of China’s total agricultural areas by 2030 under all three scenarios (BAU, BAU-CE, and SDG-60), particularly in key production zones such as the Songliao Plain (maize, rice, and soybean), North China Plain (wheat, maize, and soybean), Middle-Lower Yangtze Plain (rice), South China (rice, maize, soybean, and rapeseed), Southwest China (maize, sugarcane, and cotton), and the Tianshan Mountains (maize, soybean, and cotton) (Figures 3A–3R, S2, and S3). These regions account for over 90% of the national staple crop production. Although the general patterns across scenarios are consistent, the SDG-60 scenario shows relatively higher losses than BAU-CE due to stricter GHG constraints. Among all crops, cotton experiences the steepest decline, exceeding 50%, while other crops generally show production decreases below 25% (Figures 3S–3X). By 2030, predicted production declines relative to 2020 for wheat, rice, and maize are 13.0% (−59.6 Mt), 10.6% (−48.5 Mt), and 0.8% (−0.39 Mt), respectively. Looking ahead to 2060, crop production exhibits a more heterogeneous pattern. Modest recovery is projected in regions such as the Songliao and North China Plains (wheat, rice), Middle-Lower Yangtze Plain (maize), and Southwest China (wheat). However, under the BAU-CE scenario, the three major food crops are still projected to decrease by 7.1% compared to 2020. Under SDG-60, total production drops a further 56.6% relative to BAU-CE, underscoring the trade-offs between climate mitigation and food supply. Rice production is particularly vulnerable under GHG emission constraints. Under our accounting framework, rice is associated with relatively higher emission intensity than several other major crops (Table 2), and its yields in primary growing regions such as the Middle-Lower Yangtze Plain and South China are projected to remain stagnant or decline under the BAU-CE scenario. In these regions, reduced agricultural inputs result in rice production losses exceeding 15%. In contrast, oilseed and sugar crops show relatively smaller declines and even increases under the BAU-CE scenario, with production gains over 20%. Cotton, however, consistently declines by over 50% across all future scenarios, signaling systemic vulnerability under sustainable development constraints.

Provincial disparities in achieving agricultural SDGs

Achieving China’s national targets for sustainable agricultural development necessitates coordinated efforts across all provinces. However, significant regional disparities exist in natural endowments, economic development levels, agricultural industrial structures, technological adoption, and rural labor availability. Figure 4 illustrates the provincial deviations in crop production capacity from national averages under three future scenarios. For food crops, major wheat- and maize-producing provinces, such as Henan, Hebei, Inner Mongolia, and Heilongjiang, exhibit production advantages, with both yield and total output surpassing national averages. In contrast, key rice-producing provinces lag behind, indicating weaker production performance relative to the national baseline. This is partly due to rice’s greater sensitivity to input factors like fertilizers, pesticides, and especially irrigation water. For instance, rice cultivation in Heilongjiang is significantly more reliant on irrigation than in southern provinces. Among the non-grain crops, groundnut, rapeseed, and sugar beet production areas demonstrate strong advantages, while soybean, sugarcane, and cotton production regions underperform. When comparing the growth rates of yield and output across scenarios, food-producing provinces perform more robustly under the BAU-CE scenario, often exceeding national benchmarks. If future GHG emissions follow Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) projections, food crop output in these key regions could rise by over 10%. However, outputs for non-grain crops remain more variable and uncertain. Under the SDG-60 scenario, projected crop yields in major production provinces may decline by nearly 50% by 2060, exceeding the FAO’s estimated 40% reduction in low-income countries.31

It is noteworthy that less agriculturally advantaged provinces such as Shanxi, Sichuan, Chongqing, Ningxia, Guizhou, and Yunnan display rapid growth across all scenarios. These regions face natural constraints such as karst desertification and arid-zone degradation, but exhibit strong potential through investment in modern agricultural infrastructure, including high-standard farmland construction. Over the past two decades, southeastern China has experienced significant losses of high-quality arable land, diminishing the rice production capacity of provinces such as Hunan, Jiangxi, and Fujian, all of which fall below the national averages in both yield and output.32 Similarly, northeastern provinces face emerging threats to productivity, as black soil degradation continues to erode the crop production capacity.

Implications for farm profits under agricultural SDGs

Increasing farmers’ incomes is a core objective of SDG2 and a crucial aspect of sustainable crop production systems. However, until now the spatial and temporal dynamics of crop production profitability under sustainable practices have not been fully assessed. Our analysis of production costs shows that inputs such as fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation constitute about 17.2% of the total production costs (Figure S4A). This indicates that implementing sustainable agricultural measures can substantially reduce input expenses. Indeed, our research projects that these measures will yield crop production cost savings of US$208.6 million by 2030 and US$946.4 million by 2060, respectively.

Despite these cost savings, aggregate farm profits are expected to decline. Nevertheless, rising global food prices and the adoption of sustainable agricultural policies mean that more regions are shifting to positive net profits, improving the overall agricultural income landscape (Figures S4B–S4H). In 2030, the southern part of the North China Plain and the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River Plain are projected to be the main regions experiencing profit growth. By 2060, this growth trend expands to encompass most of the North China Plain, the Songliao Plain, and southwestern China (Figures 5A–5F). At the crop level, profitability outcomes vary by scenario. In 2030, projected profit changes are similar across scenarios. With the exception of soybean and rapeseed, all other crops incur overall profit losses, with sugar crops experiencing the largest losses, exceeding 90%. By 2060, however, higher crop prices and lower production costs lead to a significant improvement in profitability under the BAU and BAU-CE scenarios. In these cases, all crops except sugar achieve higher returns than in 2020. Regional disparities in profit changes are evident at the provincial level. Provinces with the highest total profits in 2020 tend to experience the greatest profit losses over the next 40 years (Figures 5I and 5J). This pattern primarily reflects scale effects, as provinces with larger baseline production and profit volumes contribute more to aggregate national changes even under comparable relative adjustments. Fourteen such provinces collectively account for about 98.4% of the national profit loss by 2030 (averaged across three scenarios). These provinces include Xinjiang, Guangxi, Inner Mongolia, Anhui, Shandong, Jiangsu, Henan, Hubei, Guangdong, Hebei, Jilin, Yunnan, Liaoning, and Chongqing. By 2060, the contribution of these provinces to national profit change is projected to shift significantly. This shift indicates that profit impacts become more spatially diffuse over time, as longer-term adjustments and price effects redistribute gains and losses across regions. Averaged across the BAU and BAU-CE scenarios, their combined contribution is about −29.4% (indicating net gains for this group of provinces), whereas under the SDG-60 scenario, it reaches 110.2%. Notably, this group of provinces constituted 79.3% of total farming profits in 2020, so their declines (or gains) largely drive the nationwide trend. In particular, Xinjiang, Guangxi, Inner Mongolia, Anhui, and Guangdong consistently show especially large profit losses in all scenarios, contributing disproportionately to the national decline. In contrast, many provinces with smaller initial profits in 2020 are expected to see substantial rebounds, shifting to profit growth in the future. This pattern supports the earlier observation that, although total profits decline overall, a growing number of regions move into positive net profit territory. These findings suggest that provinces with high baseline profits may require more tailored implementation of agricultural SDG measures to avoid disproportionate profit losses and sustain long-term profitability.

Finally, we consider these profit trends in the context of farmers’ overall incomes. The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences projects that the average farmer’s disposable income will roughly double from its 2020 level (around US$2,600 per year) by 2030, and reach US$14,871 by 2050 (about 5.7 times the 2020 level).33,34 Currently, however, crop farming represents only one component of the rural household income, as wage income, household operations, transfers, and other non-farm sources also contribute substantially to the rural disposable income.35 Our forecasts indicate that under the agricultural SDG scenarios, the contribution of crop profits to these overall income gains will become even smaller. This trend could widen the wealth gap between those employed in food production and those in other sectors, especially in provinces where agriculture remains the primary industry. Ultimately, this gap has the potential to intensify the outflow of farmers to other sectors, resulting in a reduction of their investment in crop cultivation.

Discussion

Our findings highlight system-level trade-offs between environmental targets and production outcomes. In this study, trade-off zones are identified through cross-scenario comparisons among BAU, BAU-CE, and SDG-60 and refer to regions where increasingly stringent emission and input constraints deliver substantial mitigation benefits but are accompanied by declines in crop production or farm profits. Even with sizable improvements in resource-use efficiency, national production declines in 2030 (−11.4% BAU, −11.5% BAU-CE, and −2.9% SDG-60) and diverges strongly by 2060, with SDG-60 showing a large contraction (−50.5%), while BAU/BAU-CE changing modestly (−6.5%/−1.0%) (Figure 1B). These patterns are also reflected in economic outcomes. Although fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation constitute ∼17.2% of current costs and decline under the scenarios, aggregate profits still decline in many places, with gains concentrated in specific regions as prices and input savings rebalance (Figures 5 and S4). On the mitigation side, cropping GHG under SDG-60 falls by ∼72% relative to BAU-CE by 2060, underscoring that ambitious decarbonization can be achieved, although maintaining output would require productivity-enhancing innovations. Taken together, these patterns define the core mitigation-production trade-offs evaluated in this study and frame the spatial identification of trade-off zones discussed below. These trade-offs highlight the challenge of achieving high-ambition SDG targets in developing-country contexts such as China while maintaining food security and social equity.15,19

The SEM pathways (Figure 2) show that fertilizer intensity is the dominant correlate of output and that emissions co-move with inputs. These results indicate that efficiency gains alone cannot stabilize national production when growth in chemical and water inputs is bounded by efficiency and GHG targets. Output declines, while emissions fall, and the net profit effect depends on the relative magnitudes of output losses, input-cost savings, and price movements.

Spatially, our projections reveal pronounced heterogeneity. Trade-off zones are concentrated in key rice-producing regions, such as the Middle and Lower Yangtze River Basin and South China. In these areas, substantial mitigation and input reductions coincide with declines in crop output. This pattern reflects rice’s relatively higher emission intensity under our scenario-consistent accounting framework, as well as its strong sensitivity to irrigation and other input constraints.36 Cotton exhibits persistent declines across scenarios, indicating systemic vulnerability under sustainability constraints. By contrast, parts of the North China and Songliao Plains show partial recovery or resilience by 2060 for major grains, and several oil/sugar crops display smaller declines or gains under BAU-CE (Figures 3, 4, S2, and S3). These patterns support differentiated policy design. First, rice-focused mitigation should prioritize methane-targeted measures and water-saving agronomy where feasible. Second, technology and extension support should be strengthened in emerging synergy zones to maintain abatement without output losses. Third, early warning and support packages are needed for crops or regions with persistent negative outlooks, such as cotton.

Provincial contributions to profit losses are also highly uneven. A small set of provinces, including Xinjiang, Guangxi, Inner Mongolia, Anhui, and Guangdong, account for the majority of the national profit declines in 2030, although the balance shifts by 2060 and varies by scenario (Figures 5I and 5J). This concentration motivates compensation and transition instruments, including output-linked support or contract buy-downs in high-cost mitigation provinces, phased targets where near-term abatement is most expensive, and investment in yield-preserving abatement, such as precision fertilization and irrigation upgrades, where our maps indicate the largest trade-offs.37,38

Interpreting these patterns in China’s smallholder context is policy relevant, although we do not model households or farm types. Fragmented landholdings, with an average farm size of only about half a hectare, significantly limit the adoption of efficient mechanization, precision agriculture, and sustainable agronomic practices.5,30,39 Moreover, an aging rural workforce further constrains technological innovation and adaptive capacity, as older farmers typically demonstrate lower propensity to adopt new technologies due to risk aversion and limited access to training and extension services.40,41 These demographic and institutional factors may constrain the scope for productivity enhancement under stringent sustainability targets. We reference these factors as contextual mechanisms that may condition policy effectiveness in the identified trade-off zones, rather than as quantities estimated by our models. Future work linking our projections to microdata would allow direct tests of how farm structure mediates mitigation-production responses.

Despite these uncertainties, our results suggest that environmental sustainability and farmer incomes need not be in tension if policy is spatially differentiated and crop specific. For example, by targeting methane-intensive rice systems with tailored abatement, prioritizing investments in synergy zones, and compensating high-cost regions during transition, China can better reconcile climate mitigation with food security and rural livelihoods.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The projections are based on historical data from 2000, 2010, and 2020 and are designed to evaluate scenario-conditional spatial responses to specified input-efficiency and GHG constraints. Although spatial block cross-validation and agreement checks between RF and XGBoost were used to improve robustness, the analysis does not explicitly model endogenous market feedbacks, trade adjustments, dietary change, or farmer-level behavioral responses.

In addition, some scenario parameters are stylized representations of policy-aligned pathways, and provincial-average allocations may not fully capture within-province heterogeneity. Crop-specific emission intensities are treated as uniform across space and time. Therefore, mitigation burdens should be interpreted as relative, scenario-consistent comparisons. Future work incorporating higher-frequency data, micro-level responses, and region-specific policy experiments would further strengthen assessments of agricultural sustainability transitions.

Method details

Crop definitions

We analyzed nine major crops in mainland China: wheat, rice, maize, soybean, rapeseed, groundnut, sugar beet, sugarcane, and cotton. These crops jointly represent the majority of China’s primary crop production and harvested area.42 Other crops, including vegetables, fruits, flue-cured tobacco, medicinal herbs, and hemp, were not included because consistent crop-specific gridded input and economic data were not available. Gridded crop production, yield, and harvested-area data were obtained from the Spatial Production Allocation Model (SPAM) datasets at 5-arc-minute resolution. We used SPAM 2000 version 3.0.7, SPAM 2010 version 2.0, and SPAM 2020 version 1.0.0. All grid-level quantities were harmonized to the same spatial resolution and were aggregated to provincial and national scales by crop.43,44,45

Historical input and output data

We denote grid cells by i (i = 1, 2, …, 72,000) and crops by z. All grid-level quantities were aggregated to provincial and national scales by summing over i and z.

Fertilizer use

Fertilizer use in grid i (TFNi) was calculated as:TFNi=∑zHAi,z×FNi,zwhere HAi,zis harvested area (ha) and FNi,z is fertilizer intensity (kg ha-1). Because fine-scale fertilizer-intensity data are unavailable, FNi,z is proxied by provincial averages from Cost-benefit of Agricultural Products in China (CBAP)46,47,48 and assigned uniformly within provinces. Fertilizer use during the historical period consisted mainly of nitrogen, phosphate and potash fertilizers.

Pesticide use

Pesticide use in grid i (TPTi) was calculated as:TPTi=∑zHAi,z×PTi,z,PTi,z=PTCi,zpPwhere PTCi,z is pesticide cost per hectare (USD ha-1) from CBAP and pP is the pesticide unit price (USD kg-1) from the National Bureau of Statistics of China (NBS). This converts expenditure to physical use (kg ha-1).

Irrigation water use

Irrigation water use in grid i (TIWi) was calculated as:TIWi=∑zHAi,z×IWQi,zwhere IWQi,z is irrigation water use per hectare (m3 ha-1). We obtain provincial IWQ from the China Water Resources Bulletin49 and assign within provinces. We convert via IWQi,z = IWCi,z/pW, with IWC irrigation-water cost per ha and pW water price (USD m-3), and document pW was taken from the Cost-benefit of Agricultural Products in China.46,47,48

Production cost

Production cost in grid i (TPCi) was calculated as:TPCi=∑zHAi,z×PCi,zwhere PCi,z is non-land production cost per hectare (USD ha-1), from CBAP (2000–2022).

Land cost

Land cost in grid i (TLCi) was calculated as:TLCi=∑zHAi,z×LCi,zwhere LCi,z is land cost per hectare (USD ha-1), from CBAP/NBS (years as above).

Farmer incomes (farm net profit)

Farmer incomes at the grid level (TFIi) were calculated as:TFIi = ∑zHAi,z×NetProfiti,zProfiti,z=Revenuei,z−Costi,z=Prodi,z×Pz−(cF+cP+cW+cO+cL)i,zwhere Profiti,z is production (t), Pz is crop price (USD kg−1), and cF, cP, cW, cO, cLare per-ha costs (fertilizer, pesticide, irrigation water, other non-land, land) multiplied by HAi,z. To reduce short-run volatility we use three-year averages around 2000/2010/2020.

Cropping GHG emissions

GHG emissions in grid i (TGHGi) were calculated as follows.TGHGi=∑zHAi,z×GHGi,zwhere GHGi,z is the crop-specific GHG intensity (MgCO2eq ha−1), taken from OECD Agriculture statistics.50 These coefficients are national average values and are applied uniformly across grid cells for a given crop. They are treated as time invariant and are used to construct internally consistent scenario pathways, rather than to represent spatially explicit or process-based emission inventories.

Scenario design

To estimate changes in agricultural production resulting from China's transition from 2020 conditions toward achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), we constructed a set of hypothetical scenarios. Since the turn of the millennium, China’s agricultural production structure has remained dominated by smallholder farming and grain-oriented planting patterns.51,52 Thus, we developed three scenarios to evaluate how China’s crop production system may respond to sustainability-oriented input and greenhouse-gas constraints by 2030 and 2060.

The Business-as-Usual scenario (BAU) represents the continuation of existing agricultural input-control policies and gradual improvements in input-use efficiency. This scenario incorporates ongoing measures for fertilizer, pesticide, irrigation water, and production-input management.

The BAU with carbon-efficiency constraints scenario (BAU-CE) extends BAU by incorporating a greenhouse-gas emissions trajectory consistent with medium-term OECD-FAO agricultural outlook assumptions. It represents a policy-continuation pathway with gradual technological progress and productivity improvements, without accelerated mitigation measures beyond those implied by existing development pathways.

The SDG-aligned pathway (SDG-60) represents a more ambitious sustainability pathway aligned with the objective of achieving agricultural decarbonization by around 2060, consistent with China’s national carbon-neutrality timeline. This scenario imposes stronger greenhouse-gas and input-efficiency constraints than BAU and BAU-CE.

Across all scenarios, harvested area for each crop within each province was held constant relative to the baseline cropping structure. This assumption reflects the persistence of grain-oriented planting patterns and allows the analysis to isolate the effects of input-efficiency and greenhouse-gas constraints. We did not model land-use change driven by energy-crop expansion, endogenous crop switching, or market-driven planting adjustments. Agricultural and agri-related production technologies were treated as scenario-consistent but not endogenously simulated. External shocks to crop prices were excluded from the analysis.

Scenario parameters were compiled from national action plans, sectoral assessments, and international sustainability pathways cited in the manuscript. For 2030, fertilizer-use efficiency was assumed to increase by 14.4%, with total fertilizer use decreasing by 7.5%; pesticide-use efficiency was assumed to increase by 10.8%, with total pesticide use decreasing by 12.5%; and irrigation-water-use efficiency was assumed to increase by 6.2%, with total irrigation water use remaining approximately stable. For 2060, fertilizer, pesticide, and irrigation-water-use efficiencies were assumed to approach approximately 90%, with fertilizer use decreasing by 30%, pesticide use decreasing by 50%, and irrigation-water demand decreasing by 34.4% relative to the 2020 baseline. Detailed quantitative parameters are summarized in Tables 1 and S3. Greenhouse-gas trajectories under BAU-CE and SDG-60 were used to construct scenario-consistent mitigation pathways rather than spatially explicit process-based emission inventories.

Input-use assumptions

Fertilizer and pesticide assumptions were derived from China’s recent input-reduction action plans, high-standard farmland construction information, and longer-term sustainable agriculture assessments.14,53,54 For 2030, we assumed that fertilizer-use efficiency would increase by 14.4% and total fertilizer use would decline by 7.5% relative to 2020. Pesticide-use efficiency was assumed to increase by 10.8%, with total pesticide use declining by 12.5%. For the 2060 projection, we drew on reports from the School of Agriculture and Rural Development at Renmin University of China, the Sustainable Development Report 2024, and long-term reduction plans from Japan and the European Union.55,56,57,58 By then, China is expected to reach input efficiency levels comparable to those in developed countries, with fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation water utilization approaching 90%.55,59 While the European Union’s Farm to Fork Strategy initially proposed a 50% reduction in pesticide use and a 20% reduction in fertilizer use by 2030, the subsequent withdrawal of these targets highlights the difficulty of implementing stringent reduction measures.60 In contrast, Japan’s Carbon Neutral Green Growth Strategy 2050, which aims to reduce pesticide use by 50% and fertilizer use by 30%, presents a more feasible reference scenario.58 Given China’s national carbon-neutrality target for 2060, we adopted SDG-aligned targets of a 30% reduction in fertilizer use and a 50% reduction in pesticide use by that year.2

Irrigation-water assumptions were based on the National Agricultural Sustainable Development Plan, water-saving rates from high-standard farmland construction, and long-term water-efficiency projections. For 2030, irrigation-water-use efficiency was assumed to increase by 6.2%, while total irrigation water use remained approximately stable. For the 2060 outlook, we drew on irrigation-efficiency projections from previous studies and global estimates of blue water use in agriculture provided in the Sustainable Development Report 2024, particularly the section Transforming Food and Land Systems to Achieve the SDGs.56,59 Irrigation-water-use efficiency was assumed to approach approximately 90%, and total irrigation demand was assumed to decline by 34.4% relative to the 2020 baseline.

Emission assumptions

Cropping greenhouse-gas emission trajectories were specified separately for BAU-CE and SDG-60. For BAU-CE, emissions were estimated using the OECD-FAO agricultural outlook, representing a continuation of existing agricultural and environmental policy trajectories.50 For SDG-60, emissions followed a more ambitious SDG-aligned decarbonization pathway consistent with achieving agricultural net-zero emissions around 2060. Additional sources, including food-system decarbonization assessments, sustainable development reports, and greenhouse-gas emission coefficient studies for Chinese agricultural products, were used to cross-check the scenario trajectories.31,56,61,62 The relevant data are provided in Table 2. These trajectories were used to impose scenario-consistent greenhouse-gas constraints for projection and comparison, not to construct a full process-based emission inventory.

Machine-learning framework

We used machine-learning models to project grid-cell-level crop production under the three scenarios. Machine learning was selected because relationships between agricultural inputs, production outcomes, and environmental pressures are nonlinear and interdependent.63,64 The models used spatial heterogeneity across approximately 72,000 5′ grid cells from the 2000, 2010, and 2020 cross-sectional datasets, rather than fitting long temporal trends.

Random Forest (RF) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) models were trained separately for the nine crops. The target variable was grid-cell-level crop production. Predictor variables included fertilizer use, pesticide use, irrigation water use, production cost, land cost, harvested area, and simple time features. In alternative specifications, scenario-related greenhouse-gas indicators were also included as predictors.

Scenario parameters were not used during model training. Instead, models were first trained using historical data, after which scenario assumptions were imposed by adjusting the corresponding input variables for 2030 and 2060. The trained models were then used to generate scenario-conditional projections. Models were fitted at national and provincial levels, and final projections were obtained by averaging national- and provincial-level predictions to balance overall consistency and regional specificity.

Random forest model

The RF model was used to capture nonlinear relationships and interactions among agricultural input variables and crop production.65,66 RF models were trained using bootstrap samples and random feature subsets. We used 200 trees, a minimum leaf size of five, and two features per split.67 These conservative hyperparameter settings were selected to reduce overfitting while maintaining stable predictive performance. RF models were evaluated using the same spatial cross-validation design described below.

XGBoost model

The XGBoost model was used as a complementary tree-based algorithm with explicit regularization.68 XGBoost models were trained with L2 regularization, subsampling, column subsampling, and early stopping. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 20 rounds based on validation performance within the spatial cross-validation folds. XGBoost predictions were compared with RF predictions and combined with RF outputs using an equal-weighted ensemble mean unless otherwise stated.

Farm-profit estimation

We estimated how projected changes in crop production under reduced agricultural input conditions in 2030 and 2060 affected farm-income outcomes across regions. The estimation process involved two steps. First, we derived material and land costs for the base year 2020 using data from the Cost-Benefit of Agricultural Products in China. We then subtracted the projected reductions in fertilizer, pesticide, and irrigation-water expenditures to obtain adjusted base costs for 2030 and 2060. Second, we projected future total costs using linear extrapolation based on provincial GDP growth rates from 2021 to 2060. This approach accounts for regional income trends while integrating input-saving effects. Finally, farm profits Profit(s,t) for each province in 2030 and 2060 were calculated as the difference between projected crop revenue and the estimated total cost under each scenario.

Cost adjustment by component. For each (i, z), current 2020 costs were decomposed into fertilizer, pesticide, irrigation water, other non-land, and land cost components. Trade-off-zone definition

Trade-off zones were defined to identify locations where environmental gains coincide with adverse production or economic outcomes. A grid cell or province was classified as a trade-off zone if it simultaneously exhibited a reduction in cropping greenhouse-gas emissions relative to 2020 under a given scenario and a decline in crop production quantity and/or net farming profit relative to the 2020 baseline.

Structurally disadvantaged provinces were identified using national median thresholds. A province was classified as structurally disadvantaged if it had both below-median agricultural labor productivity and below-median per-capita farm income in 2020, while also facing an above-median mitigation burden under SDG-aligned scenarios. Mitigation burden was measured as the proportional reduction in cropping-related greenhouse-gas emissions. This definition identifies provinces with weaker economic foundations and relatively higher transition pressure.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Structural equation modeling

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to characterize historical associations among agricultural inputs, agronomic outcomes, greenhouse-gas emissions, and farm profits. The SEM was estimated using statistical data for 2000, 2010, and 2020, pooled across regions into a stacked cross-section.

The SEM was implemented in R version 4.3.2 using the lavaan, haven, Hmisc, and semPlot packages.40,69 We fitted a recursive path model with observed variables using robust maximum likelihood estimation. Coefficients were standardized to allow comparison across variables. Model fit was assessed using CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR, with the chi-square statistic reported for completeness.70 Statistical significance was evaluated using two-sided tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The SEM was used to characterize associations and pathway structure in historical data, rather than to infer causal effects or generate scenario projections.

Model evaluation

Machine-learning performance was evaluated using spatial block five-fold cross-validation. Grid cells were grouped into 1° spatial blocks, corresponding to 12 × 12 blocks of 5′ grid cells. Folds were constructed at the block level to reduce spatial autocorrelation leakage between training and validation data.

For machine-learning analyses, n represents grid-cell-by-crop observations from the 2000, 2010, and 2020 cross-sectional datasets. Model performance was assessed within each spatial validation fold. Agreement between RF and XGBoost predictions was evaluated at both grid-cell and provincial aggregation levels and used as a robustness check. Final scenario projections were reported as the equal-weighted ensemble mean of RF and XGBoost predictions unless otherwise stated.

No randomization or blinding was applied because the study used observational gridded and statistical datasets rather than experimental treatment groups. Inclusion criteria were defined by the availability of crop-specific gridded production, harvested-area, input, cost, and emission data for the nine target crops. Grid cells without relevant crop area or required input variables were not used for crop-specific model fitting.

Threshold definitions

Trade-off zones and structurally disadvantaged provinces were identified using explicit quantitative criteria. For trade-off zones, changes in greenhouse-gas emissions, crop production, and net farming profit were calculated relative to the 2020 baseline under each scenario. Regions meeting the joint condition of emission reduction and production and/or profit decline were classified as trade-off zones.

For structurally disadvantaged provinces, national median thresholds were calculated for agricultural labor productivity, per-capita farm income, and proportional mitigation burden. Provinces below the national median for both labor productivity and per-capita farm income, but above the national median for mitigation burden, were classified as structurally disadvantaged. These thresholds were used for classification and mapping rather than formal hypothesis testing.
