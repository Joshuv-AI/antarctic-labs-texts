In-situ recomposition of polyethyleneimine additive enables a multiprocess long-lifetime thermocell
Authors: Xinya Wu, Chunlin Pang, Qikai Li, Yu-Ting Huang, Sijia Wang, Chun Cheng, Wei Li, Chuan He, Qiyu Deng, Hengjia Zhu, Meng Ni, Yun Chi, Liqiu Wang, Shien-Ping Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIonic thermocells offer a compelling route for converting low-grade heat into electricity, yet their real-world deployment is hindered by performance decay under fluctuating conditions and limited synergistic pathways. Herein, we introduce polyethyleneimine as a self-limiting sacrificial additive in a K3Fe(CN)6/K4Fe(CN)6 thermocell. During initial operation, only a fraction of amine groups in polyethyleneimine engages in redox with Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64-, while the remaining amine groups mediate thermally induced adsorption-desorption, selective condensation, and homogeneous catalysis. These cascaded effects boost the thermopower from 1.4 mV K-1 to 7.76 mV K-1. Crucially, the self-limiting nature and temperature-dependent reactivity of polyethyleneimine-Fe(CN)63- reaction not only generates solvation-perturbing species but also ensures long-term functional stability (>1000 hours). A proof-of-concept panel delivers over 5 V and 7.5 mW under a 50 K temperature difference, demonstrating system scalability. This work highlights the potential of sacrificial additive engineering to enable durable and high-performance thermocells for sustainable heat-to-electricity conversion.Subject terms: Thermoelectric devices and materials, Electrochemistry, Devices for energy harvesting

Introduction

Ionic thermocells have attracted considerable attention as versatile platforms for harvesting low-grade heat, owing to their simple architecture and high thermopower (Si)1–3. Among various redox couples, the thermogalvanic ferri/ferrocyanide [Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64-] system remains the benchmark aqueous thermogalvanic system, with an intrinsic Si of ~1.4 mV K-1 4,5. Despite this advantage at modest temperature differences ( < 50 K) compared to conventional electronic thermoelectric systems, the practical output voltage and power density of Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- remain insufficient for most real-world applications6–8. In liquid cells with strong natural convection, thermodiffusion is largely suppressed, so the achievable thermopower is mainly constrained by the reaction entropy difference (Δs). Moreover, most previously reported strategies focus on either electrode engineering or bulk electrolyte modification, typically delivering only incremental and one-sided improvements9–12.

To overcome the voltage and power limitations inherent to Δs-only enhancement, recent work has sought to introduce controlled concentration gradients (ΔC) via selective binding or thermosensitive crystallization/precipitation of one redox state13–17. By coupling Δs and ΔC contributions, these approaches can significantly increase Si; for example, thermosensitive crystallization-dissolution cycles can boost performance by selectively precipitating one redox species at a single electrode. However, such systems usually rely on a fixed cold-over-hot orientation and a sufficiently low cold-side temperature to maintain crystallization, making them vulnerable to long-term instability under realistic fluctuating thermal conditions18,19. Additional additives can further enhance output, but the inherent orientation dependence remains and multi-additive formulations introduce complex, often uncontrolled side-interactions that compromise robustness and reproducibility20,21.

In this work, we employed polyethyleneimine (PEI) as a single, multifunctional sacrificial additive in a Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- electrolyte, achieving a thermopower up to five times larger than that of pristine Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- aqueous thermogalvanic cell, while simultaneously broadening the operational temperature window and preserving long-term performance. These substantial improvements are attributed to the interfacial behavior of PEI at the electrode-electrolyte interface, its multiple, selectively temperature-triggered interactions with Fe(CN)63- and Fe(CN)64-, and the beneficial roles of its dynamically evolving products. This study highlights that rational pairing of a benchmark redox couple with a carefully chosen multifunctional additive provides an effective route to simultaneously enhance thermopower, conversion efficiency and operational stability in ionic thermocells.

Results and Discussion

Asymmetric electrical double layers between hot and cold sides

As a weak alkaline electrolyte that dissociates in water into ionized polycations with neutral and protonated amine groups (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c), the thermal characteristics of PEI were initially examined using a two-electrode system (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Although neither the graphite electrodes nor PEI is redox-active under open-circuit conditions, a pronounced thermal voltage is generated in the PEI aqueous solution, and the thermopower increases monotonically with PEI concentration (Fig. 1a, left and Supplementary Fig. 1e). To exclude thermodiffusion, an isothermal three-electrode configuration was used to monitor the temperature dependence of the open-circuit potential (VOC) in a PEI-only solution (Supplementary Fig. 1f). The substantial VOC shift with temperature indicates temperature-dependent interfacial processes at the electrode surface. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were used to understand the behavior of PEI near the electrode surface. At 293.15 K, PEI molecules formed multi-layer adsorption structures near the electrode surface, whereas increasing the temperature to 343.15 K drives PEI away from the interface toward the bulk electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 1h, left). This is reflected by the cumulated density profiles, in which the main peak of PEI shifts from 10.85 to 25.61 Å, whereas abundant water molecules remain within 9.98 Å (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1h, right). Accordingly, PEI polycations adsorb comparably on both electrodes initially, and the elevated temperature at the hot electrode will cause partial desorption. Because PEI is ionized, this asymmetric coverage between the hot and cold electrodes leads to different electrical double-layer (EDL) structures and gives rise to a thermal potential difference (ΔV=VH−VC=φ0H−φ0C). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) further reveals that the EDL capacitance (Cdl) decreases with increasing temperature (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2), a trend typically attributed to an increased EDL thickness due to thermal expansion22, in good agreement with the MD results.

To confirm that this adsorption-desorption-driven thermopower is not unique to carbon electrodes, analogous measurements were carried out using inert platinum-coated titanium (Pt@Ti) electrodes. PEI solutions on Pt@Ti also produce a clear thermovoltage, but with a smaller thermopower than that on graphite (Supplementary Fig. 1g). This reduced response is attributed to stronger PEI binding on the metal surface, which suppresses temperature-induced desorption and thus weakens the interfacial charge asymmetry. MD simulations of PEI at the Pt/electrolyte interface show that the PEI density remains tightly confined near the Pt surface upon heating, in contrast to the more pronounced migration into the bulk on graphite (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). Contact-angle measurements further indicate better wetting of PEI solution on Pt@Ti than on graphite (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e), consistent with stronger PEI-metal and PEI-water interactions. Together, these observations support a common mechanism in which temperature-modulated PEI adsorption/desorption at the electrode/electrolyte interface governs the interfacial potential, while the magnitude of the thermopower is tuned by the strength of PEI-surface interactions.

Due to the simultaneous changes in charge and capacitance, the thermopower of PEI solutions is theoretically not linear with increasing temperature or concentration. Here, for simplicity in calculation, we followed the general calculation method used in ionic thermocells (Si=−ΔVΔT=−VH−VCTH−TC) to determine the thermopower of PEI by averaging the voltage change corresponding to the temperature change in the PEI aqueous solution. The thermopower of PEI solution increased from 0.24 to 4.86 mV K-1 as the mass concentrations of PEI varied from 25 mg mL-1 to 125 mg mL-1(Fig. 1a, left). For comparison, we also tested strong polyelectrolytes, non-ionic polymers and zwitterion species and found that the thermosensitive behavior observed in PEI is outstanding (Supplementary Table 1). From a thermodynamic perspective, we derived a relationship between thermopower and the entropy change associated with adsorption/desorption (Supplementary Text 1.1), showing that the potential bias can be positive or negative depending on the charge type of polyions and their entropy changes during the adsorption and desorption process. Unlike gel-based systems, establishing a significant EDL contrast between the hot and cold ends in liquid thermocells is generally challenging due to the strong convection and diffusion effects under a temperature gradient23. Here, the thermally induced adsorption-desorption of PEI provides an effective route in liquid systems to create asymmetric EDLs and generate substantial thermovoltage.

Selective condensation at cold side

The amine groups of PEI can work in concert to form complexes with other components through hydrogen bonds or non-covalent interactions, including electrostatic and Van der Waals forces24,25. By introducing and optimizing the amount of PEI in 0.4 M/0.4 M K3Fe(CN)6/K4Fe(CN)6 solution, a maximum thermopower of 7.76 mV K-1 can be obtained at PEI content of 75 mg mL-1, which is over five times the value of 1.4 mV K-1 of pristine redox electrolyte (Fig. 1a, right and Supplementary Fig. 4). We have named this optimized thermocell as PEI-mediated thermocell (PMTC). By comparing the superimposed thermopower of PEI and Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- (Fig. 1a, left) with that of PEI-Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- mixed solutions (Fig. 1a, right), it is obvious that there are other interactions occurring within the system.

According to Manning theory26,27, counterions containing more charges condense primarily on polyion chains. Here, Fe(CN)64- ions, with higher effective charge density, indicate a selective interplay between PEI and Fe(CN)64- (Fig. 1d). The central parameter of the theory is the charge-density parameter ξ,

where ∣z∣ is the absolute value of the charge of counterions, ε0 is the permittivity of free space, εr is the relative dielectric constant of the solvent, kB is the Boltzmann constant and b is the distance between two charged groups on the polyion.

A critical central parameter ξcritical=1∣z∣ is used to evaluate whether condensation occurs. When ξ>ξcritical, the counterions condense on the polyion surface until ξ=ξcritical, while no condensation takes place if ξ<ξcritical. As shown in Fig. 1d, ξcritical=13 for Fe(CN)63- and ξcritical=14 for Fe(CN)64-. If the cold-side charge-density parameter ξC<14, no counterion species condense and the PEI polycations are totally dissociated in the solution (Fig. 1d, left). When 14<ξC<13, only Fe(CN)64- condenses on the polycations; even if Fe(CN)63- transiently settles on the polycations, they will eventually be replaced by Fe(CN)64- at the equilibrium state until ξC=13 (Fig. 1d, middle). In terms of ξC>13, both Fe(CN)63- and Fe(CN)64- condense (Fig. 1d, right). By raising the temperature, the hot-side charge-density parameter ξH can be reduced to a reasonable value (ξH<14), thereby deactivating the counterion condensation (Fig. 1d, bottom).

In the PMTC, the ξ should be adjusted to fall within the range of 14<ξ<13, where a significant fraction of Fe(CN)64- condenses with PEI to form ion-polymer complexes at the cold side, while Fe(CN)63- remains predominantly in the dissolved state. Once the solubility of these complexes is exceeded, they precipitate as a solid PEI-Fe(CN)64- rich phase, thereby creating an additional concentration disparity within the redox couple and further enhancing the thermopower of PMTCs. This solid precipitated phase exhibits a melting transition at ~43 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5). Our findings demonstrate that the thermopower enhancement in PMTCs shows a trend from rising to falling as PEI concentration (CPEI) increases (Fig. 1a, right). This is because both Fe(CN)63- and Fe(CN)64- condense on polyions when PEI is in excess (ξ>13 when CPEI>100mg mL-1), eliminating the ΔC between the redox couple. As seen in Supplementary Fig. 6, when the amount of PEI was increased in 0.4 M K4Fe(CN)6 and 0.4 M K3Fe(CN)6 solutions, precipitation appeared preferentially in the K4Fe(CN)6 solution. When the PEI concentration continued to rise, precipitation was also observed in K3Fe(CN)6 solutions. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the precipitates of PEI-K3Fe(CN)6 and PEI-K4Fe(CN)6 revealed patterns distinct from those of K3Fe(CN)6 and K4Fe(CN)6·3H2O (Supplementary Fig. 7a), indicating that Fe(CN)63- and Fe(CN)64- undergo condensation on PEI and form new compounds. Here, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) confirmed that both precipitates, characterized by a C ≡ N stretching vibration at 2038 cm-1, primarily consist of Fe(CN)64- (Supplementary Fig. 7b). It is speculated that when PEI is in excess, Fe(CN)63- initially reacts with PEI, leading to the reduction of Fe(CN)63- to Fe(CN)64-, which subsequently precipitate. It is important to note that the interaction between PEI and Fe(CN)64- occurs not only through the precipitation resulting from the phase change but also involves the counterion binding in the liquid phase. The isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurement was used to measure the binding interaction between PEI and Fe(CN)64-, Fe(CN)63- ions in the liquid phase (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 2). The dissociation constant (KD) at 20 °C shows a threefold stronger binding affinity of PEI toward Fe(CN)64- compared to Fe(CN)63- (1.06 × 10-6 versus 3.42 × 10-6 M). This demonstrates that PEI would selectively regulate the solvation shells of Fe(CN)64-, leading to the formation of a complex in the electrolyte. (Supplementary Text 1.2 and 1.3).

In practical waste heat recovery scenarios where heat flow direction is variable, Manning condensation provides a complementary approach for the flexible adjustment of the activity of one redox state across two phases, beyond specific kosmotropic-chaotropic interaction28 or micellar effect29. As shown in Fig. 1f (left), the cold-over-hot configuration facilitates the crystallization-redissolution cycle through gravitational effects. However, if the thermopower enhancement depends entirely on concentration gradients established solely by crystallization and balanced by phase-change redissolution, switching to a hot-over-cold configuration would cause gravitational accumulation of precipitates on the bottom cold side (Fig. 1f, middle), disrupting the cyclic equilibrium and degrading thermopower. In our PMTC system, the PEI-induced Manning condensation not only generates solid-phase precipitates but also liquid-phase solvated complexes of PEI-Fe(CN)64-. When operated in the hot-over-cold orientation, the higher concentration of PEI-Fe(CN)64- at the bottom cold side continues to drive diffusive transport of these complexes towards the upper hot side, thereby maintaining a condensation-release cycle (Fig. 1f, right), and sustaining device performance. Consistent with this picture, PMTC exhibits lower variability across heat flow directions compared to other concentration-gradient-based thermocells13,30–33, with only a 14% decrease in output power and 16% decrease in thermovoltage in the hot-over-cold orientation relative to its maximum values in the cold-over-hot orientation (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 8).

Temperature-activated selective catalytic reaction at hot side

The amine groups of PEI can be oxidized under certain conditions. As shown in Fig. 2a, the PEI and K3Fe(CN)6 solutions undergo a thermally activated process; the color of the as-prepared K3Fe(CN)6-only solutions remained virtually the same after being kept in the dark, with or without heating for two hours. In contrast, the addition of PEI causes a minor color shift, while upon heating the PEI-K3Fe(CN)6 solution, a significant color change occurs from light yellow to dark amber, indicating a temperature-dependent interaction or reaction between the components. In-situ ultraviolet-visible light spectroscopy (UV-vis) was used with a lower ionic concentration solution to investigate the interplay between Fe(CN)63- and PEI (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 9). When Fe(CN)63- is mixed with PEI and heated, the absorbance of Fe(CN)63- at 423 nm decreases with rising temperature, while the absorbance of Fe(CN)64- at 219 nm increases. In the reverse cooling process, no obvious change in UV-vis absorbance is observed, indicating that the reverse process [Fe(CN)64-→Fe(CN)63-] is thermodynamically unfavorable. In-situ nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was used to monitor changes in electrolyte composition before and after heating. In the 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 2c), both PEI-Fe(CN)63- and PEI-Fe(CN)64- electrolytes exhibited broad overlapping resonances at δH 2.7 ~ 2.4 ppm, corresponding to PEI34. The PEI-Fe(CN)63- spectrum displayed a split peak at δH 2.55 ppm, with an overall downfield shift compared to PEI-Fe(CN)64- and PEI-only solutions, indicating that Fe(CN)63- alters the hydrogen environment on the PEI chain. After heating, both PEI-Fe(CN)63- and PEI-Fe(CN)64- electrolytes exhibited deshielding relative to the PEI-only solution (Fig. 2d), suggesting that Fe(CN)63- and Fe(CN)64-, as strong chaotropic agents, disrupt the hydrogen bonding network at elevated temperature. The resonance pattern of PEI-Fe(CN)64- remained consistent with pure PEI before and after heating, demonstrating that the interaction between PEI and Fe(CN)64- is primarily Coulombic. In contrast, the peak at δH 2.55 ppm in the initial PEI-Fe(CN)63- solution further splits into two peaks atδH 2.96 ppm and δH 2.95 ppm associated with hydrogens on carbons adjacent to secondary amines35. By analyzing regions indicative of PEI oxidative degradation (Supplementary Fig. 10), it is suggested that Fe(CN)63- selectively oxidizes PEI while being reduced to Fe(CN)64-, with elevated temperatures accelerating this process.

Electrochemical properties of Fe(CN)64- and PEI were evaluated in aqueous electrolytes using differential pulse voltammetry (DPV). At 20 °C, an evident oxidation peak (E1) at 0.638 V was observed in the Fe(CN)64--only solution, while the anodic stability potentials of PEI (E2) was around 0.716 V, suggesting that Fe(CN)64- would be oxidized preferentially (Fig. 2e). However, the PEI electrolyte exhibited a more cathodic E2 at 0.436 V compared to E1 at 0.526 V as the temperature increased to 70 °C. This indicates that the electrochemically oxidized species of Fe(CN)64- (i.e., Fe(CN)63-) would oxidize PEI at elevated temperatures. Consistently, a cathodic shift of E1 from 0.638 V to 0.592 V at 20 °C was observed in the mixed PEI-Fe(CN)64- solution (Fig. 2f), suggesting that the electron transfer step during oxidation is coupled to a chemical reaction involving PEI as a reactant. A depletion region between 0.69 V to 0.75 V after E1 indicates that the oxidations of Fe(CN)64- and PEI act as two independent electrochemical reactions at 20 °C. By contrast, at 70 °C, the current after E1 showed only a limited decrease, suggesting that Fe(CN)64- were replenished through the chemical reaction between Fe(CN)63- and PEI during anodic scanning (Fig. 2g). In our system, a thermal gradient generates thermovoltage via thermogalvanic effect, driving the reoxidation of Fe(CN)64- at the hot side and completing one cycle. This process can essentially be regarded as an electrocatalytic reaction at the solid-liquid interface, with Fe(CN)63- acting as the catalyst36,37.

Accordingly, the system exhibits clear temperature selectivity, transitioning from independent electrochemical reactions at lower temperatures to a catalytically dominant mechanism at elevated temperatures, as evidenced by both thermodynamic and kinetic analyses.

The aged PMTC electrolytes were analyzed to gain deeper insights into the PEI-evolving products after the selective catalytic reaction between PEI and Fe(CN)63-. After over 1000 hours of operation, a significant amount of precipitate was still observed in the aged PMTC. The precipitate was filtered and further characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 14). The peaks at 708.22 eV (Fe 2p) corresponding to Fe2+ and at 284.93 eV (C 1 s) and 397.71 eV (N 1 s) attributed to C ≡ N indicate the predominant presence of Fe(CN)64- ions39. Concurrently, signals corresponding to amine (399.36 eV, N 1 s) and imine (401.33 eV, N 1 s) groups were also identified40, aligning with earlier FTIR spectroscopic findings (Supplementary Fig. 7b). After removing the insoluble solids, the filtrate was characterized by ITC, NMR and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS). ITC experiments on the aged electrolyte revealed that the selectivity of PEI for Fe(CN)64- was preserved even after 1000 hours of operation (Supplementary Fig. 15), which means the aged electrolyte contains polycations or other species that can selectively regulate the solvation structures of redox ions. Upon inspecting the 1H and 13C{1H} NMR spectra, imine derivatives (δH 8.35 ppm and δC 171.1 ppm), alcohol species (δC 75.4 ppm) and chain-cleaved PEI fragments (δH 1.21 ppm and split resonances at δC 50 ~ 54 ppm) were identified (Supplementary Fig. 16), with these assignments are consistent with other reports34 and further corroborated by corresponding molecular fragments in the ESI-MS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 17). Besides, the peak at δC 165.3 ppm that identified free CN- was not observed in the aged electrolyte, indicating that there is no ligand exchange between the PEI and Fe(CN)63-/ Fe(CN)63- redox species, further demonstrated the system stability and safety.

The spectroscopic and electrochemical characterization collectively substantiate that the thermogalvanic redox couple is involved in a specific reaction pathway (Supplementary Text 2). As shown in Fig. 2j, Fe(CN)64- functions as a redox mediator that undergoes oxidation to Fe(CN)63- at the hot side [Eq. (3)], followed by a subsequent reaction with the PEI [Eq. (4)]. Following electron transfer to PEI, Fe(CN)63- is regenerated to its original Fe(CN)64- state, enabling its reuse in subsequent cycles. Here, the formation of amine radical cation observed in the electronic paramagnetic resonance (EPR, Supplementary Fig. 18) is the rate-determining process that may be influenced by reactant concentration and applied temperature42, which is consistent with our previous FOWA results. The generated amine radical cation is inherently unstable and undergoes subsequent deprotonation to yield an α-carbon radical, which can follow several pathways to yield more stable products. In the first pathway, oxidation generates iminium ions (path 1). The radical can also react reversibly with water to form C-O bond, with water acting as a leaving group, facilitating elimination to form iminium ions (path 2). In the alkaline PMTC electrolyte, the water adduct may deprotonate to yield an aminol alcohol (path 3), which can then eliminate a hydroxyl group to form an iminium ion (path 4). Alternatively, proton transfer (pathways 3 and 5) can convert the amine group into a better leaving group, promoting cleavage of the PEI backbone and generating small polyamines. Among these products, imines and their derivatives, such as iminium ions, are primary as they are more stable in PMTC. Both the primary products (iminium ions and protonated PEI) and secondary products (aminol alcohols and small polyamines) generated by this reaction would positively influence the long-term performance of the PMTC, as discussed later. Here, note that protonated amines are less prone to oxidation43, which will lead to a gradual diminish in the selective catalytic reaction but enhancement in the selective condensation in the PMTC over extended operation.

Cascade effects in PMTC

The large thermovoltage of PMTC arises from four coupled mechanisms: thermally induced adsorption-desorption of PEI on both electrodes, thermogalvanic effect of Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- redox couple, selective condensation of PEI-Fe(CN)64- at the cold side, and selective catalytic reaction at the hot side. Figure 3a–d illustrates these synergistic effects at the electrode-electrolyte interface and in the hot- and cold-side electrolytes. In Fig. 3a, PEI adsorption on the hot and cold electrodes becomes asymmetric under a temperature gradient. Initially, comparable amounts of PEI polycations absorb on both electrodes. When a temperature difference is applied, the elevated temperature at the hot side induces partial desorption of PEI polycations, whereas adsorption at the cold side remains essentially unchanged. This thermally driven adsorption-desorption process provides an entropy change Δs1, and generates a potential difference ΔV1 (Supplementary Text 1.1 and 3.1). In Fig. 3b, Δs2 associated with Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- redox couple give rise to the thermogalvanic contribution ΔV2 (Supplementary Text 1.2 and 3.2). Importantly, Δs2 stems not only from the intrinsic entropy change of the pristine redox couple, but also from alterations of the solvation shell induced by Manning condensation and by the presence of evolving reaction products. In Fig. 3c, PEI enables selective Manning condensation of Fe(CN)64- at the cold side. The more charged Fe(CN)64- preferentially condenses onto the polycationic PEI chains, and a single polyion chain can capture multiple counterions, thereby lowering their activity through electrostatic interactions. As the amount of PEI-Fe(CN)64- complexes increases, the condensed species precipitate. Under the cold-over-hot configuration, both dissolved complexes and precipitates settle from the cold side to the hot side, where they either decouple or redissolve. This condensation-release cycle establishes a ΔCR in Fe(CN)64- across the cell and produces an additional potential contribution ΔV3 (Supplementary Text 1.3 and 3.3). In Fig. 3d, free PEI reacts selectively with Fe(CN)63- at the hot side. Based on Le Châtelier’s Principle, this reaction accelerates the consumption of Fe(CN)63- and increases the local supply of Fe(CN)64-, thereby enhancing the entropy-driven thermoelectrochemical conversion of Fe(CN)64−→Fe(CN)63−+e− at the hot electrode. From a thermodynamic perspective, the Nernst equation indicates that even if only a fraction of Fe(CN)63- is converted by PEI, the concentration difference between the oxidized and reduced forms (ΔCO and ΔCR) is enlarged, which slightly raises the open circuit potential ΔV4 (Supplementary Text 1.4 and 3.4).

Overall, the optimized addition of PEI drives four cascaded and mutually reinforcing effects (ΔV1~ΔV4) that together determine the PMTC thermovoltage. Note that not all interactions in ionic thermoelectric systems are necessarily synergistic; however, in this work, these four mechanisms interact constructively, as summarized in Supplementary Text 3.5 and 4, and the estimated contributions of each component are shown in Supplementary Fig. 19. Moreover, each mechanism discussed above can, in principle, be isolated and exploited individually or in tailored combinations to design thermocells. Detailed design guidelines and rationales for additive selection within this multi-mechanism framework are provided in Supplementary Text 5.

Long-term operation stability

While the above cascaded mechanisms maximize the thermovoltage of the PMTC, practical deployment also requires robust performance under continuous thermal bias and realistic temperature fluctuations. Compositional changes during thermo-electrochemical reactions pose a challenge to achieving long-term stable operation in thermocells44. As shown in Fig. 4a, the PMTCs exhibit exceptional durability: under ΔT=30 °C (TC = 20 °C, TH = 50 °C), demonstrated by an extensive (>1000 h) continuous monitoring for >1000 h reveals that the thermovoltage remains above 140 mV, approximately four times that of the pristine Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- thermocell. The PMTC voltage exhibits moderate fluctuations during operation, which reflects its nature as an energy-generating system involving dynamic processes such as Manning condensation and evolving catalytic reactions. In practical applications, such energy-generating systems are typically paired with energy storage modules to deliver a smooth electrical output.

We further investigated the dynamic process governing the prolonged operation of PMTC. 1H NMR spectra reveal that neutral amine groups in the solution undergo progressive oxidation to imines and other small molecules, as evidenced by time-dependent enhancement of the resonances at δH 8.35 ppm and 1.21 (Fig. 4b). By contrast, the broad overlapping resonances at δH 2.7 ~ 2.4 ppm remain prominent after 1000 hours, confirming retention of the PEI backbone structure. To assess the redox state of Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- after long-term operation, Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- species in the aged electrolyte were precipitated as a Ni-based Prussian blue analog, and the isolated solid was analyzed by XPS (Supplementary Fig. 20a, b). Semi-quantitative fitting of the Fe 2p spectrum reveals coexisting Fe(III) and Fe(II) components with an Fe(III):Fe(II) ratio of ~1:6.7 (Supplementary Fig. 20c). On this basis, a simple material-balance analysis (Supplementary Text 6) shows that only ~17% of PEI amine sites are oxidized after 1000 hours, indicating that the Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- redox couple and the majority of PEI remain available to sustain the thermoelectric response.

During the initial phase, unreacted amine groups on the catalysis-derived polycations (polyiminium ions and protonated PEIs) experience relatively weak intramolecular electrostatic repulsion, which enables their adsorption onto the graphite electrode surface through Van der wall forces45, thereby sustaining ΔV1. As oxidation progresses, neutral polyamines are either protonated to form polyammonium ions or converted into polyiminium derivatives, both of which promote Manning condensation and amplify ΔV3. In addition, the PEI-derived products, such as smaller polyamines and amino alcohols, disrupt the hydrogen-bond-rich solvation structures of the redox ions via their polar functional groups. In particular, iminium ions exert dual effects: electrostatic interactions with the redox center and weakening of the hydrogen-bond network28. As shown in the Raman spectra (Fig. 4c), strengthening of the C ≡ N bond in the redox ions was observed, which can be attributed to noncovalent interactions with their hydration shells46,47; this “water-breaking” effect increases the reaction entropy change and contributes to the overall thermovoltage46,48–50.

Over extended operation, while catalytic thermovoltage contribution (ΔV4) diminishes owing to the reduced amount of neutral amine groups, three voltage components persist: (1) ΔV1 from residual PEI, (2) ΔV2 from thermogalvanic redox couple, (3) ΔV3 amplified by Manning condensation involving residual PEI and its oxidation products (Supplementary Fig. 21). Consequently, the thermovoltage of PMTC still remains above ~75% of its initial value even after 1000 hours, with PEI and its evolving products synergistically underpinning this stability. In addition, the persistent precipitated phase endows PMTC with a low thermal conductivity of ~0.25 W m-1 K-1 (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23), which provides an additional benefit for heat utilization32.

In real-world scenarios without active cooling, the temperature of the cold side often drifts above ambient due to mismatched heating and cooling (Supplementary Fig. 24). Such variations, either a rise in cold-side temperature or expansion of the high-temperature region, can disrupt the kinetic balance between the crystallization and dissolution, resulting in performance degradation. To evaluate the robustness of the PMTC under these conditions, we carried out uninterrupted measurements of PMTCs over 120 hours at ΔT=30 °C (TC = 20 °C, TH = 50 °C or TC = 40 °C, TH = 70 °C) and ΔT=50 °C (TC = 20 °C, TH = 70 °C). In all cases, PMTCs delivered stable and high output voltages of ~180 mV, ~120 mV and ~220 mV, respectively (Fig. 4d). These results highlight that introducing thermosensitive adsorption, condensation and catalytic reaction not only boost thermocell output but also improves adaptability to temperature fluctuations, which is especially beneficial in diverse practical scenarios for waste heat recovery.

To further improve the charge transfer at the electrolyte-electrode interface, we modify the electrode materials with platinum nanoparticles51, the optimized PMTC can achieve a current density of 24.17 A m-2 at ΔT=30 °C, with a power density of up to 1.81 W m-2 (Fig. 4e, pink line, and Supplementary Fig. 25a-e). Using commercially available carbon paper plated with platinum nanoparticles, the power density can be further boosted to 2.78 W m-2 with a current density of 26.76 A m-2 (Fig. 4e, blue line). We believe that PMTC performance could be further improved by integrating previously reported optimization strategies52 (Supplementary Figs. 25f, g and 26). Unsurprisingly, PMTC excels not only in Si but also in Carnot-relative efficiency (ηr) (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Text 7)9,13,14,17,31,33,48,50,53–62. Here, the high thermopower and ηr over 5% endow PMTCs with potential for commercial applications2,19. Although the peak ηr of the PMTC is not the absolute highest among reported liquid thermocells, it is distinguished by its long-term stability, wide operating temperature window, and adaptability to various operational orientations.

Proof-of-Concept Demonstration

The PMTC exhibits a wide operational window and considerable output performance, making it capable of large-area applications for low-grade heat recovery. Figure 5a shows a PMTC panel, which comprises 16 PMTCs connected in series. The PMTC panel can provide 3.15 V and 2.88 mW at ΔT=30 °C, 5.13 V and 7.5 mW at ΔT=50 °C (Fig. 5b, c), which satisfies the power demand of many electronics. To assess its practicality, the PMTC panel was used to power an electrochromic window at ΔT over 30 °C (Fig. 5d). During summer daytime, the temperature of windows can soar up to 68 °C63, providing an ideal heat source and a potential application scenario for PMTC as an off-grid power source. Besides, we demonstrated that the assembled PMTC panel could directly charge earbuds and a fitness tracker at ΔT=50 °C without using a voltage booster (Fig. 5e, f, Supplementary Movie 1). The scalability and flexibility of the PMTC are further demonstrated by a down-scaled 6 × 6 cm2 flexible panel with nine units (Fig. 5g–i and Supplementary Movie 2), highlighting its practical potential. As known, temperature differentials are ubiquitous in daily life, arising from operating machines in the ambient air, or the contrast between indoor and outdoor temperatures in seasonal extremes. Despite their prevalence, most scenarios offer only modest temperature gradients below 50 °C. Consequently, generating a useful voltage at the range of 3 V ~ 5 V requires either the integration of numerous unit cells or a voltage booster. Compared with other reported thermocells, our PMTC panel delivers high voltage output with just half or even fewer unit cells (Supplementary Fig. 27). This advancement not only reduces the cost but also decreases the size of the device, making it more practical and cost-effective (Supplementary text 8).

In summary, we present an effective approach to augmenting the thermopower of liquid thermocells by utilizing a multifunctional mediator. PEI simultaneously mediates the electrodes, electrolyte, and electrode-electrolyte interface. For both electrodes modified with PEI, thermal-induced adsorption and desorption under temperature difference lead to the generation of a voltage. At the cold side, selective Manning’s condensation between PEI and Fe(CN)64- establishes a concentration gradient, with both solid and liquid phases contributing to the adaptability of temperature variations and orientations. At the hot side, the selective catalytic reactions between PEI and Fe(CN)63- enhance performance, and the PEI-evolving products can also undergo the mentioned Manning’s condensation, thereby aiding in sustaining performance. These three mechanisms synergistically complement the thermogalvanic effect of the original Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- redox couple, resulting in an exceptional long-term operation for over 1000 h to meet the continuous power needs of various electronics. The optimized PMTC achieved a maximum power density of 2.78 W m-2 with a high thermopower of 7.76 mV K-1 and a relative Carnot efficiency of 6.62%, which guarantees scalable thermoelectric modules/panels with fewer unit requirements. Our study introduces a pathway to unleash the potential of liquid thermocells by leveraging the synergistic effects of mediators to regenerate electricity from untapped low-grade heat sources.

Methods

Materials

Potassium hexacyanoferrate(III) ( ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), potassium hexacyanoferrate(II), trihydrate ( ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), polyethylenimine (PEI, branched, Mw≈800, Macklin), chloroplatinic acid hydrate (99.95% trace metals basis, Aladdin), trimethylamine N-oxide (95%, Aladdin), polyethylene glycol (Mn 400, Aladdin), poly(diallyldimethylammonium chloride) solution (Mw <100,000, 35 wt.%, Macklin), guanidinium chloride ( ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), sodium borohydride (99.99% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich), polyvinylpyrrolidone (Average Mw=8000, Acros), nickel(II) sulfate hexahydrate ( ≥ 99.99% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich), nickel(II) chloride hexahydrate (99.9% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich), sodium sulfate ( ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), sodium acetate trihydrate ( ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich) were used without further purification. Graphite sheets and graphite felt were purchased from ZhongNuoTanSu, Ltd. (China). Deionized water (18.2 MΩ) was used throughout the experiments.

Fabrication of PMTC devices

Potassium hexacyanoferrate(III) (1.316 g), potassium hexacyanoferrate(II) trihydrate (1.69 g) and a certain amount of polyethylenimine corresponding to the desired mass concentrations were volumized to 10 mL in deionized water as electrolytes of PMTCs for all experiments. Unless otherwise stated, the concentration of K3Fe(CN)6/K4Fe(CN)6 redox couple was fixed at 0.4 M/0.4 M.

The single planar PMTC comprised of a core poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) frame, with an internal volume of 2 × 2 × 1.5 cm3 (chamfer radii of 5 mm), which was sealed by graphite sheets (thickness of 3 mm) and subsequently filled with the as-prepared electrolyte. The distance between the two electrodes was 1.5 cm, and the cross-sectional area of the cell was 3.785 cm2.

To fabricate the PMTC panel, the PMMA frame consisting of 16 individual cells was filled with the as-prepared electrolyte, which had been sealed using graphite felts and graphite sheets (with a thickness of 3 mm), as illustrated in Fig. 5a. Subsequently, the individual cells were connected in series by utilizing silver-clad copper wires. The distance between the hot and cold electrodes was maintained at 1.5 cm, and the cross-sectional area of each individual cell was 10.285 cm2 (7 × 1.5 cm2 with chamfer radii of 5 mm).

Fabrication of nano-platinum-decorated electrodes

A previously reported method was employed to modify graphite electrodes51. Initially, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was solubilized in deionized water under constant stirring. Subsequently, the chloroplatinic acid precursor was dissolved into this solution. After that, the reductant, sodium borohydride solution, was slowly added, leading to a rapid color change from brown to black, indicating the formation of PVP-capped platinum (Pt) nanoparticles. The reaction was accomplished within 30 minutes while maintained at room temperature. And then the decorated electrodes were fabricated using a dip coating process. To be specific, the graphite sheets or graphite felt were immersed into the PVP-Pt suspension for 10 min followed by rinses with deionized water and subsequent drying in open air. Finally, the coated electrodes were heated at 270 °C for 10 min in a furnace to obtain the desired end product.

Fabrication of nickel oxyhydroxide-decorated electrodes

Hierarchical graphite felt-NiOxHy electrodes were synthesized following a previously reported procedure64. To electroplate nickel oxyhydroxide, an electrolyte composed of 0.39 M nickel sulfate, 0.1 M sodium sulfate, and 0.13 M sodium acetate was prepared. An electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) was applied to a standard three-electrode system with the graphite felt as the working electrode, a platinum mesh as the counter electrode, and a saturated Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode. A three-step constant current density deposition was used, starting from a current density of 0.005 mA cm-2, then increasing to 0.05 mA cm-2 and continuingly increasing to 0.5 mA cm-2 for every 30 min at room temperature. The electroplated hierarchical electrodes were rinsed with deionized water several times and dried in open air.

Preparation of Ni-Prussian blue analog from aged electrolyte

A previously reported procedure was adapted to precipitate Fe(CN)63-/Fe(CN)64- species from the aged electrolyte using nickel salts65. First, 40 mL of aged electrolyte (containing both the liquid and any suspended precipitates) was collected from PMTC and adjusted to pH 7 by titration with 1 M HCl (aq) to suppress the formation of Ni(OH)2 in the subsequent precipitation step. In parallel, 50 mmol of NiCl2·6H2O was dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water. The two solutions were then simultaneously added dropwise into a beaker under magnetic stirring and N2 protection. The resulting precipitate was aged for 12 h at room temperature in the dark. The solid was subsequently collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly six times with deionized water, and finally dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 12 h.

Two-electrode thermo-electrochemical measurement and temperature control system

A potentiostat (Gamry reference 3000) and an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) were used to conduct thermo-electrochemical measurements of thermocells under temperature gradients.

For a single planar cell, a homemade temperature-controlling system consisted of thermocouples, thermoelectric (TE) modules and LabVIEW was used to control and record the upper and bottom temperature of the thermocell as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1d. Two Peltier plates were placed on the bottom and top of the cell to apply the temperature gradient. The real-time temperatures of Peltier plates were recorded by two thermocouples. Then the LabVIEW program based on the feedback signals regulated the output power of Peltier plates to establish stable temperature differences.

For PMTC panel, two water plates were placed on the bottom and top of the panel. During measurement, a heated circulator (PolyScience SD07H170-A12E) and a cooled circulator (CNSHP DC-1006) flow water into the water plates to apply a temperature difference. The real-time temperatures of each side of PMTC panel were recorded by thermocouples.

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed at a scan rate of 100 mV s-1 to obtain the voltage-current (V-I) curve. The instantaneous output power was calculated as P=V×I, and the areal power density was obtained by normalizing P to the electrode cross-sectional area.

Three-electrode isothermal thermo-electrochemical measurement

To evaluate the isothermal temperature coefficient of PEI electrolytes, a standard three-electrode system was applied for measurement. Two identical pieces of graphite paper with size of 1 cm2 were used as working and counter electrodes while Hg/HgO electrode was used as reference electrode. Firstlly, the prepared electrolyte was poured into a glass double-shell container sealed by a threaded polytetrafluoroethylene lid. Rubber rings were used to ensure air-tightness. A temperature-controlled circulated water bath (PolyScience SD07H170-A12E) was used to control the temperature of the electrochemical cell. And a thermocouple was immersed into solution to record the real-time temperature. Before heating, the electrochemical cell was maintained at room temperature for over 3 h until the adsorption process achieved equilibrium. An electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) was applied to collect the open-circuit potential data during measurement. All the electrolytes used 1 M KCl as the supporting electrolyte except for further notice.

To conduct foot-of-the-wave (FOWA) analysis, the differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) characterizations of K3Fe(CN)6/K4Fe(CN)6 electrolytes with or without PEI at different temperatures were carried out by the same experimental setup, except for electrolytes and electrodes. A graphite sheet with an area of 1 cm2 was used as working electrode, a 4 cm2 graphite as counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (3 M KCl) was employed. CVs were collected at a scan rate of 50 mV s-1. DPV measurements were performed with the following parameters: amplitude=50 mV, step height=4 mV, pulse width=0.05 s, sample width=0.0167 s and pulse period=0.5 s.

During all measurements, the reference was thermally isolated from the working electrode by a salt bridge/Luggin capillary and was additionally cooled by a forced air flow, so that it remained effectively at ambient temperature while the electrolyte in the main cell was heated. Under these conditions, no explicit temperature correction of the reference potential was necessary.

EIS measurement

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was performed using a two-electrode cell with the same configuration as PMTC. The measurements were conducted over a frequency range from 10 kHz to 0.1 Hz within an applied AC voltage of 5 mV amplitude at open circuit potential. The Nyquist plots of aqueous PEI electrolytes (without supporting salts) at varying concentrations and temperatures were analyzed using an equivalent circuit model, Ru (Cdl Rct), in the ZsimpWin software. In this model, Ru represents the uncompensated resistance, Rct denotes the charge-transfer resistance and Cdl corresponds to the double-layer capacitance, which was approximated by a constant phase element (CPE) in this study.

Thermal conductivity measurement

The thermal conductivity measurement of electrolytes was conducted in a steady-state method4,67. Supplementary Fig. 22a shows the device used in the measurement. The cell is a polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) holder with a central cavity of volume 2 × 2 × 1.5 cm3 (chamfer radii of 5 mm) with top and bottom walls as heat transfer walls with a thickness of 6 mm and a thermal conductivity of 0.15 W m −1 K −1. The remaining three side walls are 10 mm thick for thermal insulation from the environment. The open side is sealed with a 0.02 mm thick polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film and supplemented with polystyrene foam for thermal insulation during heating (Supplementary Fig. 22b). ITC measurement

Thermodynamics and kinetic parameters of such interactions were evaluated by ITC using Malvern MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Automated. A water-water calibration titration was conducted before use. 360 μM PEI solution and 40 μM solutions of K3Fe(CN)6 and K4Fe(CN)6 were prepared, respectively, where PEI acted as the ligand in the syringe and Fe(CN)64- and Fe(CN)63- were receptors in the cell. For the measurement of the aged PMTC, 192 μL supernatant extracted from the PMTC after 1000 h operation and then was diluted into 50 μL as ligand solution. All solutions were degassed before use. The ITC syringe was stirred at 800 rpm, and the cell was equilibrated at 20 °C before titrations. All titrations were carried out using the standard injection titration with a 300 s time interval. Each injection was 1.3 μL, except for the initial injection which was 0.4 μL. The titration of deionized water into K3Fe(CN)6 or K4Fe(CN)6 solutions and PEI or aged electrolyte solutions into deionized water were conducted to be control experiments.

Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy characterization

The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorbance measurements were performed using Lambda 365 UV/Vis Spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer) equipped with Lambda 365 Peltier Temp Ctrl Unit (PerkinElmer). All the electrolyte solutions were prepared with deionized water. The concentration of PEI in the mixed electrolytes was maintained at a fixed value of 0.0235 mg mL-1, while the concentrations of both K3Fe(CN)6 and K4Fe(CN)6 solutions were maintained at 0.125 mM L-1. In addition, pure 0.0235 mg mL-1 PEI, 0.125 mM L-1 K3Fe(CN)6 and 0.125 mM L-1 K4Fe(CN)6 solutions were characterized as controls.

X-ray diffraction and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy characterization

The PEI-induced precipitates in K3Fe(CN)6 solution or K4Fe(CN)6 solution were gently washed with deionized water and ethanol and separated by vacuum filtration. The resulted powder samples were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns recorded on an X-ray powder diffraction instrument (Rigaku Smart Lab SE) using Co Kα radiation and Fourier-transform infrared spectra (FTIR) collected on PerkinElmer FTIR Spectrometer (Spectrum Two) in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode. In addition, commercially available K3Fe(CN)6, K4Fe(CN)6·3H2O were characterized as controls.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy characterization

The crystals of a PMTC after 1000 hours of operation were separated and gently washed with deionized water. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source on a Escalab QXi instrument (Thermo Scientific). Commercially available K3Fe(CN)6 and K4Fe(CN)6·3H2O were characterized as controls.

Raman spectroscopy characterization

The Raman spectra were collected using WITec RAMAN alpha 300 R (Oxford Instruments). The aged electrolyte was directly extracted from a PMTC after 1000-hour operation, while the as-prepared 75 mg mL-1 PEI/0.4 M K3Fe(CN)6/0.4 M K4Fe(CN)6 and 0.4 M K3Fe(CN)6/ 0.4 M K4Fe(CN)6 aqueous solutions were characterized as controls.

Electrospray ionization-mass spectrometry characterization

High-resolution mass spectra were obtained by electrospray ionization on a quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer (QExactive, ThermoFisher Scientific). Briefly, the mobile phase consisted of water and the high-performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry was operated with electrospray ionization in positive polarity mode. The aged electrolyte was directly extracted from a PMTC after 1000 h operation, while the as-prepared 75 mg mL-1 PEI/0.4 M K3Fe(CN)6/0.4 M K4Fe(CN)6 aqueous solutions were characterized as controls.

Electron paramagnetic resonance characterization

Continuous-wave X-band electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker A300 spectrometer operating at a microwave frequency of 9.85 GHz. Samples were prepared by mixing 0.8 M K3Fe(CN)6 aqueous solution with 200 mg mL-1 PEI aqueous solution at a 1:1 volume ratio in the presence of N-tert-butyl-α-phenylnitrone (PBN) as a spin-trapping agent. After mixing for 10 min, the reaction mixture was transferred to a standard quartz EPR tube and measured at ambient temperature.

Contact angle measurement

Static contact angles were measured using a contact angle measuring system (DataPhysics, OCA 25). A droplet of approximately 10 μL was dispensed onto the sample surface using a 1 mL syringe (Hamilton) equipped with a dosing needle with an outer diameter of 0.23 mm.

Differential scanning calorimetry measurement

The DSC measurements were carried out on a TA Instruments DSC 250. Samples ( ~ 10 mg) were heated from 20 to 68 °C at a rate of 5 °C min-1 under N2 atmosphere. The DSC samples were crystals grown in the PMTC electrolyte: at room temperature, the 75 mg mL-1 PEI/0.4 M K3Fe(CN)6/0.4 M K4Fe(CN)6 solutions contain low-crystallinity precipitates, which gradually transform into large well-defined crystals upon aging for 7 days; these aged crystalline precipitates were collected and used for DSC analysis.

Nuclear magnetic resonance characterization

The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) characterizations were carried out on a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (Avance III HD 500 MHz, Bruker) with D2O as solvent to analyze the PEI electrolytes.

Scanning electron microscopy characterization

A Hitachi S-4300 field emission scanning electron microscope was used to acquire images of undecorated and decorated electrodes.

Theoretical calculations

MD simulations were employed to investigate the interactions between the composite materials at the atomic level. All simulations were performed using the Materials Studio software package. The composite structure was constructed using the Amorphous Cell module, which employs the Monte Carlo algorithm to generate a random morphology within a three-dimensional periodic cell. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) were applied in all three directions to model a bulk system. To ensure structural stability and prevent local energy accumulation, the composite structure underwent geometry optimization followed by dynamic simulation using the Forcite module. This allowed the system to relax and reach a stable equilibrium state. Subsequently, a 10,000 ps NVT (constant number of particles, volume, and temperature) simulation was conducted, with temperature control applied via the Nose-Hoover method (Q ratio=0.01). The COMPASSIII force field was employed to model both intra- and inter-molecular interactions. The charge distribution was assigned using the Forcefield method, while van der Waals interactions were computed with an atom-based summation method, and electrostatic interactions were handled using the Particle-Particle Particle-Mesh (PPPM) method. The time step for the simulation was set to 1 fs, with output frames generated every 5000 steps. For the graphite system, the dimensions of the simulation box were 54.120 × 54.120 × 86.800 Å3, with two identical graphite substrates h of 54.120 × 54.120 × 6.877 Å3. For the platinum system, the dimensions of the simulation box were 55.492 × 55.492 × 86.796 Å3, with two identical (111) platinum substrates of 55.492× 55.492 × 6.762 Å3. Both systems consisted of 6368 water molecules and 25 PEI molecules, with a mass ratio of PEI to water of approximately 7.5 wt%.
