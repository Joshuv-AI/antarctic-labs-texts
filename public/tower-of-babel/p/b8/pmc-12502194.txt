Effects of combined transcranial direct current stimulation and gait training in Parkinson’s disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Shutao Qiu, Yixin Wei, Chaoran Gao, Xiangxiang Zhang, Qiang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and gait training have been demonstrated as effective treatments for patients with PD. However, the effects of combined tDCS and gait training on PD remain unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of combined tDCS and gait training in PD patients.MethodsWe conducted literature searches in EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, the Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure databases, VIP, Wanfang, SinoMed, and the Chinese Medical Association Journal Database to identify the effects of combined transcranial direct current stimulation and gait training in PD patients.ResultsEight randomized controlled trials with a total of 183 PD patients were analyzed. Statistically significant improvements were observed only in double support time, both immediately after intervention (MD = 0.04, 95% CI, 0.02 to 0.06, I2 = 0%, P = 0.0003) and at follow-up (MD = 0.02, 95% CI, 0.00 to 0.03, I2 = 0%, P = 0.02). No significant differences were found in gait velocity, stride length, cadence, Time Up and Go test, Freezing of Gait Questionnaire, Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39, Berg Balance Scale, Six-Minute Walking Test, or Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III between the combined tDCS and gait training group and the gait training alone group (P > 0.05 for all outcomes). Heterogeneity across studies was low to moderate (I2 = 0–85%).ConclusionstDCS combined with gait training compared with gait training alone did not make a statistically significant improvement in efficacy in patients with PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02924-x.Keywords: Transcranial direct current stimulation, Gait training, Parkinson’s disease, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common degenerative disease of the central nervous system, which manifests as a consequence of dopamine loss in the substantia nigra [1–3], and is one of the fastest-growing chronic neurological diseases affecting more than 10 million people worldwide [4, 5]. It primarily impacts both the motor and non-motor functions of the nervous system, often manifesting as symptoms such as muscle rigidity, resting tremors, postural and gait disturbances, and bradykinesia [6, 7]. Over time, these symptoms significantly diminish the quality of life [8, 9].

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a noninvasive brain stimulation technique developed in recent years to regulate neuronal activity in the cerebral cortex [10, 11]. Previous studies have found that it can cause biphasic and polarity-dependent changes in the cerebral cortex, which can cause persistent cortical excitation or inhibition [12, 13], and has certain potential to alleviate motor and non-motor symptoms caused by PD [14–17].

The prevalence of freezing of gait (FOG) in PD can reach as high as 63%, particularly in the advanced stages of the condition [18]. Currently, there are no effective medications specifically for the treatment of FOG in PD. However, studies have shown that gait training interventions, such as treadmill training and visual cue-based gait training, can have a positive impact on alleviating FOG symptoms in these patients [19, 20].

Combining tDCS with gait training as a comprehensive treatment method aims to improve the gait, movement, and balance function of Parkinson’s patients by enhancing cortical plasticity and promoting neural recovery. However, although this program has shown positive effects in some clinical studies [21–23], its efficacy remains controversial due to differences in research design, sample size, intervention methods, and the effects of various outcome indicators are inconsistent.

Based on the above background, there has been no systematic review of the efficacy of tDCS combined with gait training on PD. Therefore, the aim of the systematic reviews and meta-analyses was to determine the scientific evidence for tDCS combined with gait training for the gait parameters, the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS-III), balance function, and other related outcomes in patients with PD.

Methods

This review was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024616673). The authors adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [24].

Literature search

To identify studies relevant to the aim of the meta-analysis, we searched EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, the Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure databases, VIP, Wanfang, SinoMed, and the Chinese Medical Association Journal Database utilizing Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords before November 30, 2024. The search was restricted to clinical research involving human subjects. We only included studies published as full-length articles in English or Chinese in peer-reviewed journals. In addition, we manually examined the references of relevant original and review articles for potentially pertinent studies. The detailed search terms are presented in Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the basis of their titles and abstracts in accordance with the following selection criteria: (1) compared the outcomes of combined tDCS and gait training with control therapy in patients with PD, (2) described the inclusion and exclusion criteria for participant selection, and (3) reported the detailed intervention regimen, frequency, and duration.

We excluded trials that (1) were observational or nonrandomized trials; (2) focused on participants with specific diseases other than PD; (3) were published as conference abstracts, unpublished data, reviews, or editorials; and (4) had not been published as full-text articles in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.

Data extraction and study quality evaluation

Two reviewers (SQ and YW) independently performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment. In cases of discrepancies, a final consensus was reached through discussion with a third reviewer (QG). Studies conducted by the same research group at the same hospital during the same period were identified. For studies with overlapping time frames and similar baseline characteristics, only the data covering the longest duration were included in the final quantitative synthesis, following the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook.

Data were extracted on study characteristics (e.g., author, year, country, and design), participant characteristics (e.g., number, age, duration, and disease severity), and adverse events. Outcome data related to motor function from baseline to post-intervention, including temporal and spatial gait parameters (velocity, stride length, cadence, and double support time), Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG-Q), Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39), Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), and UPDRS-III, were also extracted. A meta-analysis was performed for each outcome if at least two similar trials reported the same measure. Authors were contacted via email for additional data and clarifications as needed.

To evaluate the quality of the included studies, two reviewers independently assessed the methodological quality of the RCTs using the original Cochrane risk of bias (RoB) Tool. This tool covers the following domains: bias arising from randomization, bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect adherence), bias due to missing outcome data, bias in outcome measurement, bias in the selection of reported results, and the overall risk of bias. Each domain was rated as having a low risk of bias, some concerns, or a high risk of bias. Disagreements were discussed by the two reviewers and resolved by the third reviewer (QG).

Data synthesis

All analyses were conducted using Review Manager version 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK). Measures expressed as median and interquartile ranges were converted to mean and standard deviation using the method published by Wan et al. [25] A random-effects model was applied for the analysis. The mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated as the effect size for continuous outcomes. Outcome accuracy was assessed using 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The I2 statistic was used to assess heterogeneity between studies. I2values of less than 40%, 30–60%, 50–90%, and greater than 75% indicate low, moderate, substantial, and considerable heterogeneity, respectively [26]. When necessary, the means and standard deviations of pretreatment-to-posttreatment changes were estimated based on the reported pretreatment and posttreatment data [25, 27].

Publication bias

Funnel plots and Egger’s test were used to assess potential publication bias, conducted with Review Manager version 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and RStudio version 4.3.0 (RStudio, PBC). In the absence of publication bias, effect sizes are expected to be symmetrically distributed around the overall mean effect size, reflecting randomness due to sampling error. Egger’s test quantitatively evaluates the significance of any detected asymmetry, with non-significant asymmetry suggesting no publication bias. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05, as the statistical power of publication bias tests is generally low.

Quality of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system via the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool (version 3.6.1, McMaster University and Evidence Prime, 2022) [28]. Based on GRADE criteria, evidence quality was classified into four levels: very low, low, moderate, and high. The assessment accounted for several domains of study limitations, including risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias [29]. Furthermore, evidence quality could be upgraded if there was a large effect size, a dose–response relationship, or residual confounding that diminished the observed intervention effect.

The quality of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE system and was summarized in Table S2.

Results

Study inclusion

A flowchart describing the screening and selection process is presented in Fig. 1. We identified a total of 925 records using our initial search strategy, of which 562 remained after duplicates were removed and of which 506 were excluded on the basis of the criteria used for screening titles and abstracts. Finally, eight studies were included for the subsequent quantitative analyses [21–23, 30–34].

The sample size ranged from 16 to 34 people, with a total of 183 patients, all over 40 years of age. Risk of bias

The Cochrane ROB tool for RCTs was applied to appraise the ROB in the studies involved in the review (Fig. 2). Five studies did not mention allocation concealment (unclear risk of bias) [21–23, 31, 32]. Blinding was not mentioned in one study (unclear risk of bias) [22]. Four studies did not mention blinding of outcome assessment (unclear risk of bias) [22, 23, 31, 34]. None of the studies was clear about other biases (unclear risk of bias). No studies were identified or reported as having bias, as all studies utilized random allocation, and the outcome data were complete.

Temporal and spatial parameters of gait

The temporal and spatial gait outcomes analyzed included velocity, stride length, cadence, double support time, and their follow-up comparisons (Figs. 3 and 4). Six trials with a total of 161 participants assessing velocity were included in the meta-analysis [22, 23, 30, 31, 33, 34]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on velocity compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = − 0.00, 95% CI, − 0.06 to 0.05, I2 = 6%, P = 0.91, low-quality evidence). Similarly, three trials involving 68 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on velocity (MD = − 0.02, 95% CI − 0.11 to 0.07, I2 = 36%, P= 0.64, low-quality evidence) [23, 33, 34].

Six trials with a total of 161 participants assessing stride length were included in the meta-analysis [22, 23, 30, 31, 33, 34]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on stride length compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = 0.19, 95% CI, − 3.80 to 4.17, I2 = 0%, P = 0.93, very low-quality evidence). Similarly, three trials involving 68 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on stride length (MD = − 0.37, 95% CI, − 3.23 to 2.49, I2 = 0%, P= 0.80, low-quality evidence) [23, 33, 34].

Five trials with a total of 138 participants assessing cadence were included in the meta-analysis [22, 23, 30, 33, 34]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on cadence compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = 0.79, 95% CI − 3.05 to 4.63, I2 = 62%, P = 0.69, very low-quality evidence). Similarly, three trials involving 68 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on cadence (MD = 1.23, 95% CI, − 8.14 to 10.60, I2 = 85%, P= 0.80, low-quality evidence) [23, 33, 34].

Two trials with a total of 46 participants assessing double support time were included in the meta-analysis [23, 34]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had a negligible effect on double support time compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = 0.04, 95% CI, 0.02 to 0.06, I2 = 0%, P = 0.0003, high-quality evidence). Similarly, two trials involving 46 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had a negligible effect on double support time (MD = 0.02, 95% CI, 0.00 to 003, I2 = 0%, P= 0.02, moderate quality evidence) [23, 34].

Time up and go test

Five trials with a total of 112 participants assessing Time up and go test (TUG) were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 4) [21, 30, 32–34]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on TUG compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = − 0.26, 95% CI, − 0.60 to 0.08, I2 = 0%, P = 0.13, very low-quality evidence). Similarly, four trials involving 78 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on TUG (MD = − 0.18, 95% CI, − 1.30 to 0.93, I2 = 37%, P= 0.75, low-quality evidence) [21, 32–34].

Freezing of Gait Questionnaire

Four trials with a total of 93 participants assessing the freezing of gait questionnaire (FOG-Q) were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 4) [21, 23, 31, 32]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on FOG-Q compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = 0.30, 95% CI, − 2.26 to 2.85, I2 = 53%, P = 0.82, very low-quality evidence). Similarly, three trials involving 70 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on FOG-Q (MD = − 0.81, 95% CI, − 2.37 to 0.74, I2 = 0%, P= 0.31, low-quality evidence) [21, 23, 32].

The Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39

Three trials with a total of 73 participants assessing the “Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39” PDQ-39 were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 5) [21, 30, 33]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on the PDQ-39 compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = 3.07, 95% CI, − 0.63 to 6.77, I2 = 0%, P = 0.10, very low-quality evidence). Similarly, two trials involving 39 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on the PDQ-39 (MD = 1.63, 95% CI, − 2.49 to 5.75, I2 = 0%, P= 0.44, low-quality evidence) [21, 33].

Berg balance scale

Two trials with a total of 45 participants assessing the Berg balance scale (BBS) were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 5) [32, 33]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on BBS compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = − 0.38, 95% CI, − 4.19 to 3.43, I2 = 72%, P = 0.84, very low-quality evidence). Similarly, two trials involving 45 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on BBS (MD = − 0.08, 95% CI, − 3.17 to 3.01, I2 = 0%, P= 0.96, low-quality evidence) [32, 33].

Six-minute walking test

Two trials with a total of 40 participants assessing Six-minute walking test (6MWT) were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 5) [21, 32]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on 6MWT compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = 5.38, 95% CI, − 21.44 to 32.21, I2 = 26%, P = 0.69, low-quality evidence). Similarly, two trials involving 40 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on 6MWT (MD = 0.17, 95% CI, − 0.01 to 0.36, I2 = 0%, P= 0.07, “moderate” quality evidence) [21, 32].

Unified Parkinson’s disease rating scale part III

Four trials with a total of 92 participants assessing Unified Parkinson’s disease rating scale part III (UPDRS-III) were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 6) [21, 23, 32, 33]. The results indicated that the addition of tDCS had no or negligible effect on UPDRS-III compared to walking training alone after the intervention (MD = − 0.19, 95% CI, − 2.98 to 2.59, I2 = 0%, P = 0.89, low-quality evidence). Similarly, four trials involving 92 participants at follow-up suggested that the addition of tDCS also had no or negligible effect on UPDRS-III (MD = 3.76, 95% CI, − 7.04 to 14.55, I2 = 86%, P= 0.50, very low-quality evidence) [21, 23, 32, 33].

Safety/adverse events

There were no reports about safety issues during the intervention in all studies [21–23, 30–34].

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to examine the effects of tDCS combined with gait training in people with PD. Our meta-analyses of eight randomized trials demonstrated that tDCS combined with gait training provided no additional benefits over gait training alone.

Previous meta-analyses have shown that tDCS improves motor function in Parkinson’s disease patients [35]. Some researchers suggest that applying anodal tDCS before exercise training can activate cortical excitability, thereby enhancing the training effect [36]. This indicates that tDCS has a priming effect on exercise performance. The tDCS may produce both immediate and long-term therapeutic effects by modulating cortical excitability. There are two main mechanisms proposed to explain how tDCS improves Parkinson’s symptoms: First, anodal tDCS may induce dopamine release from the basal ganglia by activating glutamate cortical-striatal fibers [37–39]. Second, anodic stimulation may enhance compensatory inactive pallido-thalamo-cortical drives [40, 41]. These mechanisms provide a theoretical foundation for the therapeutic role of tDCS in Parkinson’s disease.

In addition to being used as a standalone treatment, tDCS can also be combined with other rehabilitation regimens to achieve better outcomes. Research has shown that tDCS, when used alongside other training programs, can promote motor learning and improve performance on motor tasks, with combined treatments often yielding superior results compared to single treatments [42–45]. However, recent high-quality randomized controlled trials investigating the combination of tDCS and gait training in PD patients have produced inconsistent results. Our meta-analysis also found no significant superiority of gait training combined with tDCS over gait training alone.

Notably, the study by Valentino et al. [46] found that while there was no significant improvement in UPDRS scores after five sessions of tDCS, a significant improvement was observed in the delayed effect assessment compared to the control group. Furthermore, studies have indicated that treadmill training has a more pronounced effect on gait improvement in advanced PD patients compared to those with moderate PD [20]. Therefore, in our study, a ceiling effect in gait improvement in patients with moderate PD may have masked the additive benefits of anodal tDCS stimulation.

Additionally, while some reviews note that studies using tDCS in PD patients generally show positive effects, the results remain inconsistent [47]. The efficacy of tDCS in PD may be closely linked to the role of the targeted brain region in performing specific tasks, which could explain the differences in efficacy [47]. Heterogeneity in patient characteristics may also contribute to the variability in outcomes across studies. Since PD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, it leads to significant cortical changes and reorganization [48]. Therefore, tDCS combined with gait training may be more effective in patients in the “ON” state but less so in those in the “OFF” state. Future research is needed to further explore the interaction between combination therapies and pharmacological treatments.

Currently, our understanding of the neurophysiological effects of tDCS in PD patients remains limited. Further studies are necessary to verify the additive effects of tDCS in gait training and to determine how specific pathophysiological characteristics and disease stages in PD patients influence their response to tDCS combined with gait training.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that tDCS combined with gait training did not demonstrate a significant advantage over gait training alone. In fact, the combined therapy was associated with a longer double support time both immediately after the intervention and at follow-up, suggesting that its effectiveness may be inferior to gait training alone. However, given the limited number of studies and the small sample sizes involved, larger and more robust randomized controlled trials are required in the future to further evaluate the efficacy of transcranial electrical stimulation combined with gait training in patients with PD.
