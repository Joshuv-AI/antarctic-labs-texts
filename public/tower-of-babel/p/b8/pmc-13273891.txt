A reproducible bibliometric-content analysis workflow for mapping research fields in computer science
Authors: Thanh-Cong Truong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Computer science literature indexed in Scopus and Web of Science has grown at double-digit annual rates, making comprehensive manual synthesis infeasible for individual researchers. Bibliometric workflows partially address this problem but rarely yield the interpretive depth needed to characterize a field’s accomplishments or gaps. This paper introduces a two-phase reproducible workflow integrating bibliometric science mapping (Phase 1) with structured thematic content analysis (Phase 2), implemented in R using the bibliometrix package. Phase 1 clusters publications by keyword co-occurrence; these clusters serve as the sampling frame for purposive selection of representative papers, which undergo deductive-inductive thematic coding in Phase 2. Thematic coding of this type typically requires dual-coder reliability checks; a test-retest procedure replaces that requirement, maintaining κ=0.82 without a second coder. Applied to 648 AI-FinTech publications (2017-2026), the workflow identifies four thematic clusters and achieves κ=0.82. Regulatory compliance gaps and AI-blockchain integration opportunities, invisible to bibliometric analysis alone, emerged only through thematic coding. A single researcher completes the process in approximately 22 active working hours without dedicated infrastructure.•Integrates bibliometric science mapping with structured thematic content analysis into a single reproducible R-based workflow applicable to any computer science sub-field.•Links Phase 1 cluster outputs to Phase 2 sampling via an explicit allocation formula, replacing ad hoc paper selection with a principled, data-driven decision rule.•Enables single-author reliability verification via a test-retest procedure (κ≥0.80), removing the dual-coder requirement as a practical barrier for PhD researchers.

Background

Computer science (CS) generates scientific literature at a rate that outpaces the field's capacity to synthesise it. Scopus indexed approximately 350,000 CS-classified documents in 2015; by 2025 that Fig. exceeded 730,000. Unlike medicine, where Cochrane reviews established systematic synthesis methods in the 1990s, CS has relied on informal narrative reviews that lack method transparency and resist replication. As CS sub-fields mature, researchers need procedures that determine not only what has been studied but what the accumulated findings mean for future work.

Two methodological traditions address this challenge from opposite ends. Bibliometric analysis applies quantitative methods to large bibliographic datasets; tools such as bibliometrix [1] and VOSviewer [2] automate keyword co-occurrence networks and productivity rankings across thousands of papers. Yet bibliometrics describes the surface structure of a field without addressing what those papers say. A cluster labelled “deep learning-fraud detection” reveals co-occurrence patterns but not which architectures researchers used, how well they performed, or where the gaps lie. Qualitative content analysis [3,4] addresses this by coding a sample of papers according to a predefined scheme, enabling structured extraction of methods, findings, and limitations. Its principal constraint is scalability: a single researcher can code fifty to one hundred papers in depth, but not two thousand.

Despite this complementarity, no standardised workflow combining both phases has been proposed for CS. Carrera-Rivera et al. [5] provide a step-by-step guide for systematic literature reviews in CS but omit bibliometric network analysis. Donthu et al. [6] describe bibliometric guidelines for business research but omit the qualitative interpretation phase. Zupic and Čater [7] survey bibliometric methods for management with the same omission. Atkinson [8] demonstrates an AI-assisted SLR pipeline that addresses automation but not interpretive synthesis. Researchers wishing to conduct a mixed-methods review of a CS sub-field must therefore assemble their own procedure from disparate sources. Published bibliometric reviews in CS rarely provide reusable code, keyword thesauri, or raw bibliographic exports, making replication difficult. This reproducibility deficit persists despite MethodsX-style publications addressing adjacent approaches [5,8].

The present study addresses these gaps by proposing and demonstrating a two-phase, reproducible workflow with four components. First, the workflow standardises the sequence from database export to thematic cluster identification using a transparent, code-first R pipeline. Second, it operationalises the link between Phase 1 cluster outputs and Phase 2 paper selection, so that sampling follows the empirical cluster structure rather than researcher discretion. Third, it provides explicit guidance for single-author reliability assessment via a test-retest procedure [9], the common case for PhD researchers and individual academics. Fourth, all components (keyword thesaurus, coding template) are packaged as open Supplementary Material to enable direct replication.

The demonstration domain is AI in FinTech (2017-2026), a corpus of 648 publications with clearly distinguishable thematic sub-areas, rapid recent growth, and genuine methodological heterogeneity. These properties make it representative of many active CS sub-fields.

Method details

Terminology

Throughout this paper, bibliometric analysis refers to the application of statistical methods to bibliographic metadata, following the typology of Donthu et al. [6], who distinguish performance analysis from science mapping. Content analysis refers to the systematic, rule-governed qualitative coding of full-text documents [3,4]. The term reproducible [10] is used in the computational sense: a workflow is reproducible if an independent researcher, given the same database search results and the supplied R script, obtains identical outputs.

Workflow overview

The workflow comprises two sequential phases and eight steps (Fig. 1). Phase 1 is quantitative; Steps 2-5 are fully executed in R from a single script, with Step 1 (search string formulation) requiring researcher judgment to define the domain scope; Phase 2 (Steps 6-8) is qualitative and researcher-executed, informed by the cluster structure Phase 1 produces. Step-by-step overview of the bibliometric-content analysis workflow.

Phase 1 begins with a structured database search (Step 1) using a PICOC-derived query string executed on Scopus and Web of Science. Step 2 merges, deduplicates, and keyword-normalises the raw exports in R. Steps 3 and 4 produce standard bibliometric indicators using the bibliometrix package [1]. Step 5 applies the thematic map procedure to partition keywords into clusters and position them on Callon’s strategic diagram [11], classifying sub-fields as motor themes, niche themes, emerging themes, or basic/transversal themes based on their centrality and density scores.

Phase 2 uses the cluster output from Step 5 as a sampling frame. Step 6 selects representative papers from each cluster via stratified purposive sampling, prioritising high normalised citation scores and papers published within the past years to ensure temporal coverage. These papers undergo thematic content analysis using a deductive-inductive coding scheme (Step 7). A test-retest procedure verifies coding reliability for single-author studies; κ≥0.80 is the threshold for proceeding to Step 8. Step 8 triangulates Phase 1 structural findings with Phase 2 thematic findings to produce an integrated synthesis.

This synthesis reflects a deliberate structural choice: both phases operate independently in execution but inform each other at the interpretation stage. Phase 1 outputs guide Phase 2 design (which clusters to sample, how many papers per cluster), while Phase 2 outputs validate and enrich Phase 1 labels (confirming that keyword cluster names reflect thematic coherence in the literature). This bidirectional relationship distinguishes the proposed workflow from both standalone bibliometric surveys, which lack interpretive depth, and standalone narrative reviews, which lack structural rigour.

Phase 1: Bibliometric analysis

Step 1: Data collection

Bibliographic data are retrieved from Scopus (Elsevier) and Web of Science (Clarivate). Scopus is preferred for computer science because it provides broader coverage of conference proceedings [5]; Web of Science is included to strengthen citation data accuracy. Search strings are constructed using the PICOC (population, intervention, comparison, outcomes and context) framework and executed via the advanced search interface of each database, targeting titles, abstracts, and author keywords (TITLE-ABS-KEY in Scopus; TS= in WoS). For the AI-FinTech demonstration example, the following string was applied:

TITLE-ABS-KEY(

("artificial intelligence" OR "machine learning" OR "deep learning")

AND ("fintech" OR "financial technology" OR "financial innovation")

)

AND DOCTYPE(ar OR cp)

Results are exported in BibTeX format (Scopus) and ISI plain-text format (Web of Science), saved as scopus.bib and ISI.txt respectively. Document types are restricted to journal articles and conference papers. Grey literature, editorials, and book chapters are excluded at this stage.

Step 2: Data preprocessing

Preprocessing uses the bibliometrix R package (v4.0.0)

The thesaurus distinguishes two mapping types: synonymic normalisation (e.g., AI = ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE) and taxonomic grouping (e.g., LSTM grouped under NEURAL NETWORK). Taxonomic decisions should be documented with rationale in the thesaurus file, as they constitute analytical choices rather than mechanical cleaning.

After preprocessing, records missing both author keywords (DE) and index keywords (ID) are flagged and, where possible, resolved manually before analysis proceeds.

Step 5: Cluster identification

Research clusters are identified using the Thematic Map procedure implemented in bibliometrix [14]. This method applies the Louvain community detection algorithm to the keyword co-occurrence network, partitioning keywords into clusters [15]. Each cluster is positioned on a strategic diagram defined by two axes: Centrality (degree of interaction with other themes) and Density (internal cohesion of the theme) [11]. This positioning classifies clusters into four quadrants: Motor Themes (high centrality, high density), Niche Themes (low centrality, high density), Basic/Transversal Themes (high centrality, low density), and Emerging/Declining Themes (low centrality, low density).

thematic_map <- thematicMap(M, field = "DE", n = 250, minfreq = 5, stemming = FALSE, size = 0.5, n.labels=3)

plot(thematic_map$map)

# Export cluster assignments for Phase 2

write.csv(thematic_map$words, "cluster_keyword_assignments.csv", row.names = FALSE)

Parameter guidance: The two parameters most sensitive to corpus size are n (maximum number of keywords in the network) and minfreq (minimum co-occurrence frequency). For corpora of 300-700 records, n = 250 and minfreq = 3-5 are recommended starting values. For corpora below 300 records, reduce minfreq to 2-3 to avoid over-sparsity; for corpora above 1,000 records, increase minfreq to 8-10 to prevent hairball networks. If the strategic diagram produces more than six clusters or clusters with fewer than three keywords, adjust minfreq upward by one step and re-run. The number of clusters is not a free parameter: it emerges from the data.

Phase 2 - Content analysis

Step 6: Selection of representative papers

Representative papers are selected from each cluster using a stratified purposive sampling strategy [16]. For each cluster, papers are ranked by normalised citation score (citations per year since publication), and the top-cited papers are selected first. Additional papers are selected to ensure that (a) different methodological approaches within the cluster are represented, and (b) recent publications (last three years of the study window) are included to capture emerging directions.

Allocation formula: The number of papers sampled from each cluster is determined by cluster size and thematic map quadrant position:(1)ncluster=max(5,round(Pcluster×T×Wquadrant))where Pcluster is the cluster’s proportion of total keywords in the network, T is the target total sample size (recommended: 40-60 papers for corpora of 300-700 records; 60-100 for corpora above 1,000), and Wquadrant is a quadrant weight (Motor Theme: 1.5; Basic/Transversal: 1.0; Niche Theme: 0.75; Emerging/Declining: 0.5).

The minimum of 5 papers per cluster ensures that even small or peripheral clusters contribute to the synthesis; there is no fixed maximum, as Motor Theme clusters in large corpora may justify 25-30 papers to adequately represent their internal diversity. The final sample is managed using Zotero for full-text retrieval and annotation. Supplementary Material S3 provides the coding scheme template, codebook, and a worked example comprising three coded papers per cluster (12 papers total) to illustrate how the coding dimensions operate in practice. The complete coded dataset for all 40 papers is available from the corresponding author on request.

Step 7: Thematic content analysis

Content analysis follows the qualitative content analysis procedure described by authors in [4], adapted for the bibliometric context, in two stages.Coding scheme development: An initial deductive coding frame is derived directly from the cluster labels and keyword profiles produced in Step 5. Inductive codes are added iteratively as full-text reading reveals themes not captured by keywords alone. The coding scheme records, for each paper: (i) research type (theoretical / empirical / applied); (ii) AI method(s) used; (iii) application domain; (iv) evaluation approach; and (v) reported limitations or open questions.Coding reliability: When two or more researchers are involved, inter-rater reliability should be assessed using Cohen’s Kappa , with a threshold of κ≥0.80 required before proceeding to synthesis; disagreements are resolved through structured discussion. When the study is conducted by a single author, a test-retest procedure is recommended as a minimum standard [9] : a random subset of at least 25-30% of the sample is re-coded independently after a minimum interval of two weeks (four to six weeks is preferable to reduce coding memory), and Kappa is computed between the two coding rounds. This procedure measures intra-rater consistency rather than inter-rater reliability and does not replicate the independence guarantee of dual-coding; it is offered as a practical floor for solo researchers, not as a methodological equivalent. Hence, researchers should use a second coder wherever feasible.

Step 8: Interpretation of research themes

In the final step, the results of Phase 1 (cluster structure, growth trends, citation patterns) and Phase 2 (thematic codes, methodological patterns, research gaps) are triangulated. This triangulation serves two purposes: first, it validates the bibliometric clusters by confirming that co-occurring keywords reflect coherent research themes; second, it adds an interpretive layer that explains why certain clusters are growing, what methodological choices characterise them, and where the gaps lie. The synthesised findings are reported as a structured narrative, organised by cluster, and accompanied by a research agenda table that maps identified gaps to recommended future directions.

Method validation

This section validates the workflow by applying it to the AI-FinTech domain (2017-2026, 648 publications). The purpose is not to report findings about AI-FinTech as a research domain, but to demonstrate what output each step produces, how that output should be read and interpreted, and how it feeds into the next step. The complete Phase 2 coding dataset (40 papers, all clusters) is reported in a companion study (in preparation); Supplementary Material S3 presents a 12-paper subset to illustrate the coding instrument, the reliability procedure, and the synthesis format.

How to read this output: Three diagnostics matter when reading this output. First, the Scopus-to-WoS ratio: in applied CS sub-fields, Scopus typically contributes more records. Second, deduplication loss: a rate above 30% may indicate that the search string is too broad or that the two databases have substantial overlap for the domain. Third, keyword vocabulary reduction: a ratio of 5:1 or greater indicates; that the raw keyword field contains substantial noise that, if uncorrected, would fragment the co-occurrence network in Steps 4-5.

In the AI-FinTech demonstration: The search string was executed on 10 March 2026. Scopus (TITLE-ABS-KEY) returned 618 records; Web of Science (TS=) returned 296 records (raw total: 914). Following DOI-based deduplication via mergeDbSources() and a manual quality screen, the final corpus was 648 unique records (365 Scopus, 283 WoS). Keyword normalisation via the domain thesaurus (Supplementary Material S2) reduced 1,827 raw keyword variants to approximately 320 normalised concepts: a 5.7:1 reduction ratio.

How to interpret this output: Four features of this chart warrant examination. First, the overall trajectory: is the field growing, stable, or contracting? The compound annual growth rate (CAGR) summarises trajectory as a single Fig., enabling comparison with adjacent sub-fields and alignment with funding cycle expectations.

In the AI-FinTech demonstration: Fig. 2 shows growth from 6 publications in 2018 to 214 in 2025, a CAGR of approximately 67%, consistent with a field in rapid-expansion phase. Three inflection points are visible: 2020-2021, when COVID-19-driven digitalisation accelerated adoption; 2023, when large language model integration entered FinTech applications; and 2024-2025, where the growth rate remained steep. Database balance is equally clear: Scopus-indexed records dominate in every year, confirming that conference proceedings constitute the primary dissemination channel. The 2026 bar is annotated as a partial year (January-March only).

Implication for Phase 2: A high CAGR with a late inflection point (2023-2024) indicates that a substantial proportion of recently published papers remain under-cited relative to their eventual impact. The Step 6 sampling procedure should explicitly include papers from the final two to three years of the window.

First, node count and minimum frequency threshold: minfreq values too high fragment the network; too low produce an uninterpretable hairball. Second, cluster count: the Louvain algorithm is resolution-sensitive; 3-6 clusters is interpretively meaningful for most CS sub-fields.

Steps 6-8 Output: Content analysis and triangulation

Step 6: Representative paper selection

Scope of the demonstration: The full Phase 2 analysis of the AI-FinTech corpus is reported in a companion study (in preparation). The steps below draw on a 12-paper subset provided in Supplementary Material S3, three papers per cluster, to demonstrate the coding instrument, the reliability calculation, and the synthesis format without duplicating the findings of that study.

Following the stratified purposive sampling protocol (Eq. 1), 40 papers were selected for content analysis: 16 from Cluster 1, 14 from Cluster 2, 5 from Cluster 3, and 5 from Cluster 4. Within Cluster 1, papers were distributed across three sub-domains: blockchain and DeFi applications (6 papers), AI-enabled banking and digital payments (5 papers), and FinTech adoption and regulatory innovation (5 papers). Within Cluster 2, papers were distributed across fraud detection and cybersecurity (7 papers) and credit risk and ML-based lending (7 papers). This sub-theme-aware allocation would not have been achievable from the keyword network alone: it required abstract reading of the top-cited papers to identify sub-theme boundaries within each Motor Theme cluster. In clusters where the top-cited papers do not reflect the cluster’s defining keywords (a situation common in Niche Theme clusters where high-citation general papers co-occur with specialist papers), selection prioritises papers whose author keywords match the cluster’s NLP/specialist profile over papers selected purely by citation rank.

Step 7: Coding and reliability assessment

A random subset of 12 papers (30% of the sample) was re-coded after a two-week interval. Agreement between coding rounds yielded κ=0.82, satisfying the κ≥0.80 threshold [17]. The two cases that came closest to the boundary were papers combining ML with blockchain (borderline Cluster 1/2 cases), resolved by referring to the paper’s stated primary contribution.

Limitations

Keyword dependency: The keyword co-occurrence network and thematic map depend on the quality and completeness of author keyword fields (DE) in the bibliographic records. Studies that lack author keywords, particularly older conference papers, are underrepresented in the network. The keyword normalisation thesaurus reduces spelling inconsistencies and abbreviation variation but does not eliminate them.

English-language bias: Scopus and Web of Science exports are predominantly English-language, creating geographic bias in both productivity counts and thematic coverage. Researchers wishing to include non-English literature must supplement the workflow with additional database exports (e.g., CNKI for Chinese-language publications).

Thesaurus-encoded domain framing: The keyword thesaurus encodes the author's domain conceptualisation. A researcher approaching the corpus from a Finance rather than CS background may construct the thesaurus differently, producing different clusters and a different Phase 2 sampling frame. Publishing the thesaurus as Supplementary Material S2 makes this design choice transparent and revisable, a property absent from most published bibliometric reviews.
