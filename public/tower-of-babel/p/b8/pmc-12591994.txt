Shared genes and pathways in dementia: Insights from genome‐wide association studies
Authors: Kyle J Loi, Noah Radtke, Chloe Kiriluk, Mohamed H Noureldein, Eva L Feldman, Kelly M Bakulski, Kevin S Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDementia, a collective term for neurodegenerative disorders marked by cognitive decline, affects millions worldwide, and is expected to rise significantly in prevalence. Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs), as highlighted in this review, have revolutionized our understanding of dementia by identifying contributing genetic loci and pathways, including for Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, frontotemporal lobar dementia, and Lewy body dementia. This review critically analyzes published findings to shed insight on shared genes and pathways, encompassing lipid metabolism, immune responses, lysosomal mechanisms, and ionic homeostasis, which underlie neurodegeneration in dementia. We discuss methodological advances, challenges posed by genetic heterogeneity and population diversity, and the utility of polygenic risk scores in disease prediction. Furthermore, the importance of integrating tissue‐specific expression data and non‐European ancestral study cohorts is emphasized. INTRODUCTION

Dementia is an umbrella term for neurologic conditions marked by abnormal and progressive loss of cognitive function. An estimated 57 million people were living with dementia in 2022, a number projected to rise to 152.8 million by 2050. 1 Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common dementia, characterized by the gradual loss of episodic memory and, eventually, core long‐term memory. 2 AD and related dementias (ADRD), such as vascular dementia (VaD), Lewy body dementia (LBD), and frontotemporal lobar dementia (FTLD), exhibit unique pathologies that highlight additional or distinct factors contributing to cognitive degeneration. 3 , 4 However, these dementia subtypes also share pathophysiologic pathways and overlapping clinical presentations, suggesting potential common or related underlying genetic cues that contribute to disease development. At this juncture, we now have the data and computational power to identify these connections across dementia subtypes.

Despite the multifactorial pathophysiology in dementia subtypes, the contribution of underlying genetic factors to disease is clear. Twin and family studies consistently show that AD is highly heritable, with genetic factors accounting for ≈ 60% to 80% of late‐onset AD risk. 5 , 6 , 7 FTLD also demonstrates substantial heritability with ≈ 40% of cases driven by autosomal dominant mutations in genes such as MAPT, GRN, and C9orf72. 8 , 9 , 10 In contrast, the heritability of LBD is less defined, with estimates ranging from 30% to 60%. 2 , 11 The genetic contribution to VaD is also ambiguous due to its frequent comorbidity with other cerebrovascular conditions like ischemic stroke. To date, the most robust estimate comes from the Mega Vascular Cognitive Impairment and Dementia Consortium, which reported a heritability of only 6.1%, underscoring the complex, multifactorial nature of VaD. 12

While extensive research into the genetics underlying dementia has been conducted for more than a century, the advent of next‐generation DNA sequencing techniques and computational methodologies has transformed how scientists study the disease and allowed the field to move beyond a focus on single heritable mutations in protein‐coding genes. 12 Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs), a relatively recent genomic analysis technique, uses genomic data to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to specific disease phenotypes. Since the development of GWASs, neuroscientists have uncovered > 80 significant genetic loci associated with AD and ADRD, 13 highlighting the multifactorial nature of dementia. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

This review highlights the discovery of shared and unique genetic loci across AD and ADRD, revealing key pathways in lipid metabolism, immune response, lysosomal mechanisms, and ionic homeostasis that contribute to neurodegeneration. The review also addresses methodological principles of GWASs, its power to uncover genetic risk factors, and the challenges posed by genetic heterogeneity, population diversity, and phenotype misclassification. Finally, it emphasizes future directions, such as managing study cohorts, integrating tissue‐specific expression data, and leveraging polygenic risk scores (PRSs) to refine diagnostics and therapeutic strategies for these polygenic diseases.

GWAS PRINCIPLES

GWASs hold significant potential by simultaneously analyzing millions of genetic variants from millions of genomes. GWASs also surpasses previous allele mutation discovery methods, such as candidate gene studies, by pinpointing specific SNPs across the entire genome in large populations. They excel at identifying common variants with small effect sizes, which older methods often miss due to limited scope or focus on strong‐effect mutations. By leveraging large‐scale population data and high‐throughput genotyping technologies, GWASs identify polymorphisms associated with complex phenotypes, revealing the underlying genetic architecture of common and rare diseases.

GWASs begin by precisely defining the phenotype of interest, whether binary (e.g., case–control) or quantitative, to ensure participants are accurately categorized (Figure 1A‐B). An appropriately powered cohort is then assembled, prioritizing large sample sizes and population homogeneity (e.g., European ancestry) to enhance statistical robustness. 18 Ancestral homogeneity reduces confounding factors that can arise when differences in allele frequency between populations are mistakenly associated with a trait due to shared ancestry rather than a true biological link. Consistency in phenotype description and genotype harmonization allow for meta‐analysis across cohorts to increase power. Large biobanks, such as UK Biobank, Million Veteran Program, and Biobank Japan provide extensive genomic data for such analyses. 18

Methodology of GWASs. GWASs are a powerful tool to identify underlying genetic contributors to disease. To begin, (A) a particular phenotype and patient cohort is defined, such as individuals with dementia. Recruitment must minimize bias and ensure clear clinical diagnosis. B, Biological samples, typically blood or saliva, are then collected from each participant to extract DNA for genotyping. C, DNA is analyzed using genotyping arrays or next generation sequencing platforms to identify genetic variants, primarily SNPs, across the genome. D, Data undergo rigorous quality control to filter out unreliable genotypes and samples. Population stratification is assessed via PCA, and outliers or low‐quality data are removed. E, Missing genetic variants are inferred using statistical imputation based on linkage disequilibrium with nearby genotyped SNPs. Reference panels like 1000 Genomes or Trans‐Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) are used to increase variant resolution. F, Statistical tests (e.g., logistic or linear regression) are applied to assess the association between each genetic variant and the dementia phenotype, accounting for covariates and population structure. G, Summary statistics from multiple studies or sub‐cohorts are combined in a meta‐analysis to increase statistical power and validate associations across diverse populations. H, Significant findings are validated in independent cohorts that are ancestrally matched and do not overlap with the discovery population, ensuring robustness and generalizability. I, Follow‐up analyses map associated variants to genes and pathways. Functional annotations, fine‐mapping, polygenic risk score construction, and integrative analyses (e.g., Mendelian randomization) are performed to interpret biological relevance. Genotyping is performed using next‐generation sequencing or high‐density SNP arrays, depending on the study's scope and variant frequency spectrum (Figure 1C). 19 Rigorous quality control protocols remove poorly genotyped samples and variants that do not meet thresholds for call rate, minor allele frequency, or Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, ensuring data integrity and absence of artifacts. 20 Missing genotype data are imputed using reference panels, such as the 1000 Genomes Project or Trans‐Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed), to improve resolution (Figure 1D‐E).

Association analyses leverage regression‐based models adjusted for confounding variables, with logistic models for binary traits and linear models for continuous traits (Figure 1F). To correct for population stratification, techniques like principal component analysis or linear mixed models are applied. Statistical significance is addressed by applying stringent thresholds, such as Bonferroni correction, to account for the large number of tests performed. 21

Post GWAS, odds ratios are then calculated to quantify the strength and direction of associations between genetic variants and traits. 22 Findings are validated by replication in independent cohorts and experimental studies to confirm robustness and uncover mechanistic contributions to the phenotype or disease (Figure 1H‐I). Finally, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis can be used to link SNPs to gene expression levels followed by identifying relevant biological pathways (Figure 1G). 18 This comprehensive approach ensures reliable insights into the genetic basis of complex traits and diseases, including dementia.

GWASs of AD

AD affects > 55 million people globally, with several known mutations shown to promote amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau pathologies (Figure 2). 23 Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) are the central histopathology of AD and are implicated in synaptic dysfunction and widespread neuronal loss (Figure 2). While these two pathologies are the hallmarks of AD, studies have identified a plethora of other processes contributing to disease pathophysiology, with many highlighted and even discovered using GWAS methods.

The first AD GWAS was conducted in 2007 by Grupe et al. and paved the way for larger and more comprehensive genome‐wide analyses. 24 This review highlights data from the seven largest and most recent GWASs conducted on AD (Table 1). 13 , 25 , 26 , 27 Of these, three stand out due to their identification of new genetic loci. Lambert et al. conducted a large‐scale GWAS meta‐analysis to elucidate AD genetic architecture. 28 Integrating data from 17 case–control cohorts and four population‐based studies encompassing 54,162 total individuals (17,008 AD cases, 37,154 controls), the study identified 11 novel susceptibility loci for AD risk, such as SLC24A4, FERMT2, and INPP5D. 28 Additionally, they confirmed previous GWAS‐identified genes, including apolipoprotein E (APOE), BIN1, 29 , 30 , 31 CLU, 29 , 30 CR1, 30 and MS4A6A. 32 , 33 In 2019, Kunkle et al. conducted another GWAS meta‐analysis involving 63,926 individuals (21,982 AD cases, 41,994 controls). 34 They discovered five novel AD risk loci: IQCK, ACE, ADAM10, ADAMTS1, and WDR81. Like Lambert et al., Kunkle et al. found APOE, BIN1, and CLU to be the three most significant genetic risk loci for AD.

Wightman et al. conducted the largest AD GWAS to date, leveraging a dataset of 1,126,563 individuals, including clinically diagnosed cases, proxy cases, and controls. 35 The study identified 42 loci associated with AD, of which 11 were novel, including TMEM106B, GRN, and HAVCR2. Additionally, APOE, BIN1, MS4A4A, TREM2, CR1, and PICALM were again identified as significant loci, supporting the analyses by Kunkle et al. and Lambert et al. 30 , 34 Study strengths included large sample sizes and validation of findings by comparison to eQTL data. A note should be made that this study, as well as others using cases from the UK Biobank, 25 , 26 , 27 included proxy cases that were not explicitly diagnosed with AD but had one or two parents with AD. Thus, while focused on the specific diagnosis of AD, the reliance on proxy cases in these studies introduced potential phenotypic noise, and the predominance of European ancestry limited generalizability to diverse populations.

These studies represent major milestones in AD genetics by identifying new therapeutic targets and reinforcing the polygenic nature of the disease. Importantly, most of these large studies relied on data sets supported and curated by the National Institute on Aging Genetics of Alzheimer's Disease Data Storage Site and the National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias. These discoveries underscore the significant impact of these organizations supported by the National Institute on Aging Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers and National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center.

GWASs of VaD

VaD is the second most common dementia, caused by several vascular factors related to cognitive impairment, including ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, hypoperfusion, microinfarcts, leukoaraiosis, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, microbleeds, and cerebral hemorrhages (Figure 2). 36 , 37 VaD cohorts often overlap significantly with stroke cohorts due to shared diagnostic criteria related to cerebrovascular damage, which presents challenges for genetic studies of VaD. 38 VaD patients also often have comorbid hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and atrial fibrillation, complicating modeling of GWASs. 37 , 39

Unlike AD, VaD GWASs are far less common, likely due to VaD heterogeneity, fewer available cohorts, and the historically larger research focus on AD. There have been three VaD GWASs conducted to date (Table 1), the first by Schrijvers et al. on a cohort of 5700 Rotterdam Study participants, 67 of which developed VaD (Table 1). 40 , 41 After replication, they identified one significant locus on chromosome 7q22, implicating PDE7A, a high‐affinity cAMP‐specific phosphodiesterase. However, the study faced notable limitations, including small sample size, as well as the “winner's curse,” in which effect sizes identified in the discovery phase tend to be overestimated, leading to larger effect estimates compared to those observed in the replication phase, diminishing robustness of results. 41

Kim et al. conducted a GWAS of ischemic stroke, a leading cause of VaD, using data from 284 Korean individuals (84 cases, 200 controls) and identified a significant association with variants near HDAC9, which is implicated in large artery atherosclerosis, a key ischemic stroke and VaD risk factor. 42 , 43 The relatively modest study size likely hindered the detection of rare or low effect size variants and was conducted in a Korean cohort, restricting generalizability to other ancestries. Despite these limitations, the study provided valuable insights into the shared genetic basis of ischemic stroke and vascular disease to cognitive impairment.

The Mega Vascular Cognitive Impairment and Dementia consortium conducted the largest GWAS for VaD to date. The study included 753,695 participants from 19 cohorts in the Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology project, of which 8702 individuals were diagnosed with VaD (Table 1). 17 While the analysis only identified the APOE locus as significant, it also revealed suggestive associations at previously known loci TRPC6 and TMEM106B, as well as novel loci including SPRY2, FOXA2, AJAP1, and PSMA3. 17 A strength of the study is its inclusion of a diverse cohort with African, Asian, European, and Hispanic ancestral backgrounds, which enhances generalizability of the findings. However, this diversity also introduces baseline variability that may reduce the statistical significance of ancestry‐specific risk factors. Nevertheless, the study provided novel insights into the genetic underpinnings of VaD, identifying variants at loci linked to angiogenesis and endothelial integrity, neuronal excitability, amyloid processing, and inflammatory microglial activation, which are also implicated in broader dementia subtypes. Given the high frequency and strong effect of the APOE locus, studying VaD in cohorts that exclude mutant APOE carriers could help identify significant, but rarer, genetic variants.

GWASs of LBD

LBD is defined by the accumulation of α‐synuclein into Lewy bodies within neurons, leading to cell death (Figure 2), and sits under an umbrella of other α‐synuclein–related diseases (synucleinopathies), such as Parkinson's disease (PD) and multiple system atrophy. LBD also encompasses PD patients who later develop dementia, also known as PD dementia. 44

Guerreiro et al. were the first to conduct a large‐scale GWAS of a cohort of 1216 LBD cases and 3791 controls, which identified five significant loci: APOE, BCL7C/STX1B, SNCA, GBA, and GABRB3 (Table 1). 45 Other previously known dementia‐related loci, including MAPT and BIN1, were found to be only nominally associated. A few years later, Guo et al. conducted a comprehensive multi‐trait association analysis that drew genetic similarities among LBD, AD, and PD. 46 Using GWAS summary statistics for LBD (2591 cases, 4027 controls), they identified 13 significant loci associated with LBD, including five previously reported loci (UBQLN4, BIN1, TMEM175, SNCA, APOC1) and eight novel loci (ELOVL7, HAVCR2, TREML2, FDFT1, CLU, FBXL19‐AS1, NCOR1, KANSL1; Table 1). This study highlights genes such as APOC1, SNCA, TMEM175, and MAPT, which point to mechanisms involving Aβ metabolism, α‐synuclein aggregation, and tau pathology.

The sample sizes for LBD studies remain relatively modest compared to AD and PD cohorts, which could limit the power to detect rare variants or smaller effect sizes. Nevertheless, Guerreiro et al. and Guo et al. provide notable insights into the shared and unique genetic architecture of LBD, offering valuable directions for further research. 45 , 46

GWASs of FTLD

FTLD is identified by selective frontal and temporal cortex atrophy, driven by pathological protein aggregation and disrupted neuronal homeostasis (Figure 2). It encompasses several clinical syndromes, including behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia, characterized by personality and social behavior changes, and primary progressive aphasia, marked by progressive impairments in speech and language. Due to similar pathologies, FTLD has recently expanded to include extrapyramidal motor disorders, such as progressive supranuclear palsy–Richardson syndrome, corticobasal syndrome, and pyramidal motor disorders, like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. 47

Van Deerlin et al. conducted the first FTLD GWAS in 2010, identifying only one significant locus in TMEM106B, which at the time was an uncharacterized transmembrane protein (Table 1). 48 In 2014, Ferrari et al. conducted a GWAS of 3526 cases and 9402 controls, finding three novel loci of interest: HLA, RAB38/CTSC, and BTNL2. 15 They also compared previously identified loci associated with other neurogenerative diseases, including C9ORF72, MAPT, APOE, and TMEM106B, to their FTLD cohort, finding modest associations (P values between 10−2 to 10−7) 15 . Notably, they excluded patients harboring GRN mutations, which is strongly associated with FTLD, to highlight additional risk genes. 15

Furthermore, a er et al., in collaboration with the International FTLD‐TDP Whole‐Genome Sequencing Consortium, conducted GWAS to identify genetic risk factors for FTLD with TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) pathology (FTLD‐TDP), the most common neuropathological subtype of FTLD (Table 1). 49 They found two previously discovered loci, UNC13A and HLA‐DQA2, along with one significant polymorphism in the DPP6 locus. Two years later, Reus et al. conducted another FTLD GWAS consisting of 354 cases and 4209 controls; however, they only identified C9ORF72 as a significant locus. 50

INTERSECTION OF GENES CONTRIBUTING TO AD AND ADRD

Although multiple GWASs have investigated various forms of dementia, a critical question remains: Do different subtypes of dementia share common pathological mechanisms, or does each subtype possess distinct pathophysiological pathways? Cross‐analyses of recent GWASs reveal a set of genes implicated in multiple dementia subtypes, highlighting shared mechanisms in neurodegeneration and dementia (Figure 3). However, further Mendelian randomization and mechanistic studies are warranted to confirm the effect of these genes. As our understanding of dementia evolves, disease classification is increasingly grounded in genetic defects and disease‐specific molecular pathways. Given that germline genetic variation occurs upstream of both pathology and clinical features, these findings provide valuable insights into the molecular convergence underlying AD and ADRD (Table 2).

Endolysosomal mechanisms

Disrupted cellular waste processing is implicated in many neurodegenerative diseases. TMEM106B, which encodes transmembrane protein 106B, was first identified by Van Deerlin et al. as synergistically interacting with GRN and C9ORF72 mutations to exacerbate FTLD, and has since been identified as an AD and VaD risk factor as well. 48 TMEM106B predominantly resides in lysosomal and endosomal membranes and aids in cellular trafficking (Figure 4). 51 Elevated or dysregulated TMEM106B expression exacerbates lysosomal dysfunction by impairing acidification, leading to enlarged lysosomes and interrupted protein degradation. 51 , 52 This lysosome enlargement may be due to increased anion trafficking into lysosomes or decreased ion release, which triggers a large aqueous influx into the lysosomal lumen to maintain osmotic homeostasis. Interestingly, two endolysosomal cation channels, TMEM175 (K+/H+) and TRPML1 (Ca2+/Na+), have been implicated in synucleinopathies. 16 , 53 , 54 Abnormal expression of TMEM106B may impact the function of these channels, leading to elevated intra‐endolysosomal cation concentrations.

Several GWASs identified TMEM175 as a significant risk locus for LBD and other synucleinopathies, like PD. 16 , 46 While previously identified as a K+ channel, recent studies have found that TMEM175 is permeable to both K+ and H+ (Figure 4). 55 The Gln65Pro TMEM175 polymorphism results in a gain‐of‐function mutation for the ion channel, enhancing neuronal autophagy and increasing resistance to stress. 56 In contrast, the loss‐of‐function Met393Thr polymorphism destabilizes lysosomal pH, disrupts the fusion of lysosomes with autophagosomes during autophagy, and impairs lysosomal membrane potential. 56 TMEM175's role in ion homeostasis also suggests that TMEM106B may similarly influence ionic balance, potentially contributing to lysosomal swelling.

GWASs also identified the BIN1 locus as highly statistically significant risk factor for AD and LBD. 13 , 16 , 25 , 34 , 46 The cellular mechanisms of BIN1, which encodes the bridging integrator 1 protein, are currently under investigation. Recent studies reveal that BIN1 isoform 1 induces neurodegeneration by disrupting neuronal endolysosomal trafficking, resulting in the accumulation of vesicles displaying both early and late endosomal markers (Figure 4). 57 Interestingly, loss of BIN1 in human‐induced neurons reduces early endosome size, which can be rescued by BIN1 isoform 1 expression. Additionally, BIN1 isoform 9 expression promotes tau protein release via extracellular vesicles in vitro and increases the percent area of hippocampal phosphorylated tau in vivo. 58 However, the BIN1 isoform 1 does not induce these same effects. This suggests that BIN1 expression is dynamic and acts in conjunction with other mechanisms to disrupt endolysosomal trafficking and promote tauopathies.

TMEM106B, TMEM175, and BIN1 underscore the central role of endolysosomal dysfunction in pathogenesis of dementia (Table 2). Mutations in these genes impair the clearance of misfolded proteins and damaged organelles, exacerbating cellular stress and neurotoxicity. 59 Synergistic interactions between these genetic factors may amplify disruptions in lysosomal homeostasis, leading to the accumulation of pathogenic protein aggregates and contributing to a shared mechanism of disease across various dementias.

Protein processing mechanisms

Disrupted protein degradation is frequently identified as an underlying pathological mechanism of multiple dementia types, and several genes responsible for protein metabolism have been implicated in ADRD GWASs. For example, the UBQLN gene family encodes ubiquilin, with three widely expressed members, UBQLN1, UBQLN2, and UBQLN4, identified as ADRD risk factors. 60 , 61 , 62 Ubiquilins are key players in protein quality control, facilitating substrate degradation through the ubiquitin–proteasome system, autophagy, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–associated protein degradation (Figure 4). 63 They also interact with chaperones to manage misfolded proteins, regulate DNA damage repair, and engage with RNA‐binding proteins and cytoskeletal elements to influence cell differentiation and development. 63 The association between UBQLN1 with AD was first reported in 2005 via a family‐based association analysis. 64 Although GWAS for FTLD and LBD did not establish a notable role for UBQLN1, they did identify UBQLN2 and UBQLN4 loci as significant risk factors (Table 2). 15 , 46 More than 20 mutations in UBQLN2 have been discovered that contribute to stress granule formation, TDP‐43 cytoplasmic inclusions, cellular clearance pathway dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and ubiquitin–protease system dysfunction. 65 Similarly, Guo et al. identified the UBQLN4 locus as a significant risk factor for LBD, although further studies in LBD cohorts have yet to confirmed this finding (Figure 4). 46 These findings suggest a common underlying mechanism involving impaired protein degradation in neurodegeneration.

Along a similar theme, Watts et al. were the first to identify VCP mutations as significant FTLD risk factors, 66 which was confirmed by a GWAS conducted by Ferrari et al. 15 VCP directs ubiquitinated molecules to the proteasome for metabolism, balances organelle repair and removal, and regulates granulomatosis (Figure 4). 67 Thus, VCP mutations are associated with decreased proteasome activity and accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins. 67 , 68 , 69

In parallel with impaired protein degradation, mutations that promote protein aggregation also contribute to dementia. Found in < 0.01% of the population, fully penetrant autosomal‐dominant mutations in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 cause early‐onset AD. 6 , 70 APP contributes to synaptic formation and neural plasticity, and there are currently 32 known APP mutations that trigger Aβ pathology in AD. 71 γ‐secretase cleaves APP within the plasma membrane, releasing two Aβ isoforms, Aβ40 and Aβ42, into the extracellular space (Figure 4). If it is not proteolytically degraded, Aβ42, which is largely insoluble, aggregates into Aβ plaques. Mutant APP or PSEN1/PSEN2 lead to misguided γ‐secretase cleavage, both favoring higher Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios, thus increasing Aβ aggregation, disrupting cellular homeostasis, inducing neuroinflammation, and contributing to synaptic dysfunction. 71 , 72 , 73 While not originally discovered by GWASs, GWASs have reinforced the importance of APP and PSEN1/PSEN2 in AD.

Microtubule‐associated tau, encoded by the MAPT gene, is an essential protein for proper cellular trafficking, and its aggregation is highly linked to AD pathology. 47 Loss of function as tau aggregates into NFTs within the axon and cell body reduces microtubule binding, disrupting microtubule dynamics and neurotransmitter trafficking and, thus, synaptic transmission in AD (Figure 4). 47 Like AD, FTLD also commonly presents with tau aggregates (FTLD‐tau). MAPT was the first gene identified in relation to FTLD, with three missense mutations (G272V, P301L, R406W) and three splice‐site mutations in the 5′ region of exon 10 that enhance tauopathy. 74 These aggregates promote further pathological inclusions, thereby contributing to the spread of neurodegeneration. FTLD cases also often present as FTLD‐TDP, characterized by TDP‐43 aggregates associated with loss‐of‐function in progranulin. 75 Progranulin, encoded by the GRN gene, is essential for lysosomal homeostasis, cellular trafficking, and regulation of neuroinflammation. 76 For example, progranulin facilitates the maturation of lysosomal cathepsin D, a protease critical for degradation of aggregation‐prone proteins like TDP‐43 (Figure 4). 77 Mutations in GRN impair this process, leading to TDP‐43 aggregation. Analyses demonstrate that MAPT and GRN loci are associated with neuronal loss in AD and FTLD. 13 , 14 , 78

Another major locus identified in GWAS studies of LBD is SNCA, which encodes α‐synuclein. α‐synuclein regulates neurotransmitter release by facilitating soluble NSF attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex assembly to promote vesicle docking during synaptic signal transmission. 79 Mutations in SNCA lead to post‐translational phosphorylation and nitration that drive α‐synuclein aggregation into oligomers and protofibrils, eventually forming Lewy bodies (Figure 4). 80 Although α‐synuclein aggregates play a central role in LBD pathology, SNCA mutant carriers predominantly exhibit motor diseases, such as PD, with relatively mild cognitive impairment. 44 This observation suggests that genes external to the SNCA locus or regulatory elements associated with SNCA expression may play a more direct role in the development of cognitive decline in LBD.

Mutations in C9ORF72 are the most common cause of familial FTLD and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. 81 , 82 Two GWASs identified the C9ORF72 locus as a significant FTLD risk factor (Table 1). 15 , 50 The C9ORF72 gene contains GGGGCC hexanucleotide repeat sequences between exons 1a and 1b. Variants in this reading frame result in hexanucleotide expansions to hundreds of repeats, disrupting RNA metabolism, synaptic trafficking, autophagy, and immune pathways (Figure 4). 82 , 83 RNA expansion transcripts can also undergo repeat‐associated non‐AUG translation, producing toxic dipeptide repeat polypeptides that aggregate within neurons. 84 A recent study conducted by Sahana et al. showed that these dipeptide repeats activate Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK) via the ER‐stress response protein, IRE1, which in turn promotes stress granule assembly through upregulation of G3BP1, further contributing to neurodegeneration. 85

Overall, the mounting evidence supporting impaired protein degradation and protein aggregation in dementia is clear. Aggregates of misfolded proteins, such as Aβ, tau, α‐synuclein, and C9ORF72 products, inhibit proper cell trafficking and signaling. To some degree, adaptive cellular mechanisms can help with misfolded protein clearance. However, if they persist, these processes are overwhelmed, leading to the accumulation of toxic aggregates, disrupted cellular homeostasis, and progressive neurodegeneration.

Lipid metabolism and transport mechanisms

Mutations to genes encoding apolipoproteins have been implicated in disrupted lipid transport, contributing to the pathology of AD and ADRD. APOE is a widely researched heritable gene across all dementias, and the APOE ε4 polymorphism is a significant risk factor for AD and ADRD. 12 , 34 , 46 It is estimated that ≈ 15% of the human population and 50% to 70% of the AD population carries at least one APOE ε4 allele. 70 , 86 Microglia and astrocytes engage in dynamic apoE‐mediated lipid exchange processes with neurons by synthesizing and packaging lipids into apoE‐coated lipoprotein particles that are secreted and taken up by neurons to support membrane and synapse formation (Figure 4). 87 Neurons then offload oxidized lipids onto apoE lipoproteins, which microglia and astrocytes detoxify. 87 The single amino acid mutation distinguishing APOE ε3 from APOE ε4 reduces the binding strength of apoE to lipids, thus stalling astrocyte/neuron lipid trafficking. 87 , 88 This allows oxidized lipoproteins and lipids to accumulate within neurons and aggregate with soluble tau protein. APOE ε4 is also less efficient than APOE ε3 at clearing Aβ from the extracellular matrix, further complicating AD pathology. 89 While direct mechanisms are still under investigation, apoE plays a clear role in cellular waste management, and when misfolded, allows for toxic aggregation.

APOC1 is adjacent to APOE on chromosome 19 and antagonizes apoE activity. 46 , 90 While largely investigated in relation to pancreatic and liver disease, the APOC1 locus has been identified as an AD and FTLD risk factor. 46 , 91 Though the mechanism remains largely unknown, APOC1 dysfunction is hypothesized to further complicate the already reduced ability of APOE ε4 to bind lipids, decreasing lipoprotein clearance within the central nervous system (Figure 4). 90 , 92 , 93

In 2009, Harold et al. and Lambert et al. were the first to implicate CLU (also known as APOJ) in AD. 29 , 30 CLU encodes the protein clusterin which, like APOE, is a highly expressed apolipoprotein involved in lipid trafficking between astrocytes and neurons (Figure 4). 94 CLU can protect against Aβ pathology by binding Aβ42 and preventing its self‐aggregation into insoluble neurotoxic fibrils and plaques. 95 , 96 However, CLU polymorphisms demonstrate similar pathology to APOE ε4, with disruptions in plasma membrane and synaptic health resulting in neuronal lipid accumulation. 94 , 97

Several GWASs also identified the ABCA7 locus as a risk factor for AD. 13 , 26 , 32 , 33 ABCA7 is primarily involved in trafficking lipids and other lipophilic molecules across microglial, astrocytic, and neuronal membranes (Figure 4). 98 Loss‐of‐function mutations in ABCA7 disrupt lipid transport between the ER and mitochondria, leading to imbalances in mitochondrial lipids which impairs mitochondrial dynamics, morphology, respiration, and adenosine triphosphate production. These disruptions also increase generation of reactive oxygen species and ultimately compromise neuronal function. 98 Furthermore, ABCA7 haplodeficiency impairs microglial clearance of Aβ by disrupting phagocytosis and lysosomal trafficking. 99

Another locus related to lipid metabolism identified by GWASs is GBA. Both Chia et al. and Guo et al. identified the GBA locus as a significant risk factor for LBD. 16 , 46 The GBA gene encodes glucocerebrosidase, a lysosomal enzyme critical for complex glycosphingolipid metabolism (Figure 4). GBA mutations promote α‐synuclein and glycosphingolipid aggregation, complicating synaptic communication. 100 GBA mutations are generally found in patients with high Lewy body pathology despite low levels of NFTs and Aβ plaques. 44 , 100

These insights from GWASs demonstrate that polymorphisms in genes involved in lipid transport and metabolism significantly impact neuronal health. Moreover, the presence of multiple polymorphisms likely exacerbates the effects of ADRD pathology. For instance, CLU mutations may aggravate the impact of APP mutations by increasing aggregation of Aβ42. Similarly, the reduced lipid‐binding efficiency of APOE ε4 might synergize with APOC1 or GBA mutations, further impairing lipid trafficking and lysosomal degradation.

Inflammatory response mechanisms

Inflammation is a central pathological mechanism in neurodegenerative diseases. Progranulin loss of function, described above in relation to protein aggregation, is also linked to microgliosis, astrogliosis, and neuroinflammation, with findings accentuated in aged mice. 76 , 101 Additionally, APOE and APOC1 mutations increase neuroinflammation due to immunosuppression of microglia and astrocytes. 102 In both mice and humans, the presence of APOE ε4 is associated with reduced APOC1 expression and diminished secretion of cytokines and chemokines from primary murine microglia and human macrophages, proteins that are essential for immune response regulation (Figure 4). 103

The TREM2 locus was associated with AD in two GWASs conducted in 2013 and has since been confirmed by several more. 14 , 26 , 28 , 34 , 35 , 45 , 104 TREM2 is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily and can bind to Aβ, lipopolysaccharide, and several apolipoproteins, such as apoE. 105 Under normal conditions, lipid binding to TREM2 through DAP10, a cell signal transducer, induces immune cell proliferation, phagocytosis, and chemokine/cytokine release (Figure 4). 106 However, TREM2 R47H knock‐in mice demonstrate decreased hippocampal microglial density and impaired anti‐inflammatory responses relative to wild‐type mice. 107 The TREML2 locus, which lies close to the TREM2 locus on chromosome 6 and was first identified by the International Genomics of Alzheimer's Project GWAS, 28 was also later found to be a risk factor for LBD (Table 2). 46 Unique from TREM2, TREML2 promotes the activation of neutrophils and macrophages during the immune response. 108

Hollingworth et al. were the first to identify the MS4A6A locus as an AD risk factor. 32 MS4A6A encodes a membrane spanning protein responsible for immune regulation and signaling. However, subsequent GWAS on AD and LBD demonstrated the MS4A6A locus may actually be protective for dementia. 26 , 46 In support of this, a recent preprint reported that MS4A6A–MS4A4A complexes impair TREM2 and microglial function, indicating that loss of function of MS4A6A may in fact improve neuroinflammatory responses (Figure 4). 109 The mechanisms by which MS4A6A contributes to neuroinflammation remain unknown.

Ionic homeostasis

Neurons rely on tightly regulated ion gradients, particularly of Ca2⁺, K⁺, and Na⁺, to facilitate synaptic signaling, energy metabolism, and waste clearance. 110 In AD, dysregulation of ionic homeostasis leads to synaptic dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, and exacerbated neuroinflammation.

In addition to its role in endolysosomal processes, BIN1 is a key player in oligodendrocyte and glutamatergic neuron Ca2⁺ homeostasis (Figure 4). Both heterozygous and homozygous BIN1 knockout in human‐induced pluripotent stem cell–derived neurons disrupts Ca2⁺ signaling and alters transcription of genes related to ion transport and synaptic function, paralleling changes observed in glutamatergic neurons from late‐stage AD brains. 111 Similar studies also demonstrate that knockout or decreased BIN1 expression reduces Ca2⁺ spiking and synaptic transmission. 112 , 113 , 114 These results suggest that disrupted neural circuit efficiency and transmission may have detrimental effects on neuronal health over time.

Several studies have also identified dementia‐related genes, such as VCP and PSEN2, that are associated with mitochondrial ion channels involved in Ca2⁺ homeostasis. PSEN2 knockout promotes mitochondrial Ca2⁺ uptake and ER Ca2⁺ leakage (Figure 4). 115 , 116 Additionally, PSEN2 knockout reduces cytoplasmic Ca2⁺ and inositol 1,4,5‐trisphosphate‐linked cell signaling, which is essential for autophagy. 117 VCP polymorphisms leave mitochondrial permeability transition pores vulnerable to high Ca2⁺ concentrations, which induce transition pore opening and thus mitochondrial depolarization and possible apoptosis. 118 However, the mechanism by which Ca2⁺ concentrations within the mitochondria remain elevated is still unclear.

TRPC6 encodes a non‐specific Ca2⁺ channel that was initially identified as a protective gene due to its ability to bind to APP and inhibit its cleavage by γ‐secretase, thus reducing Aβ plaque formation (Figure 4). 119 Its potential protective role in dementia was further supported when reduced TRPC6 mRNA levels were detected in patients with AD. 120 However, subsequent evidence suggests that TRPC6 expression may exacerbate cerebral ischemia and reperfusion injury. Two studies found that ​​TRPC3/6/7 knockout mice exhibit reduced brain injury and astrocyte apoptosis after ischemia/reperfusion injury, which was linked to decreased nuclear factor kappa beta phosphorylation, increased Akt phosphorylation, and expression of anti‐apoptotic proteins. 121 , 122 Investigating the differential expression ratios of TRPC isoforms could provide key insights into ADRD pathology.

These findings highlight the critical role of ionic dyshomeostasis in AD and ADRD, as disrupted regulation of Ca2+, K+, and Na+ contributes to synaptic dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, autophagic disruption, and neurodegeneration.

POLYGENIC RISK SCORES

The collected findings from GWASs can be applied to individuals through PRSs. Because multiple genetic mutations with small to moderate effect sizes can collectively promote dementia, it is imperative to assess risk based on the combination of all mutations an individual harbors, rather than focusing solely on single mutations. Unlike single‐gene models, which focus on rare, highly penetrant mutations (e.g., APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 in early‐onset AD), PRSs leverage the heterogeneous genetic nature of common, late‐onset dementias. 123 To do so, PRSs calculate a weighted score for each detected risk mutation, resulting in a combined score that predicts an individual's risk of developing dementia. This method has gained prominence in dementia research for its ability to capture the cumulative effects of genome‐wide risk loci identified by GWASs. By compiling this data into PRSs, researchers can systematically estimate a person's risk of developing a phenotype based on highly precise genetic markers. 124 Indeed, multiple studies have demonstrated that individuals with high PRSs for AD, particularly when combined with APOE ε4, have up to a 5‐fold higher risk of AD than those with lower scores. 125 , 126 , 127 As a quantitative metric, PRSs hold considerable promise for improving risk stratification in dementia. Broadly applicable PRSs could be used to predict overall dementia onset, while subtype‐specific PRSs may help distinguish between different forms of dementia based on their unique genetic architectures.

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

While GWASs have been instrumental in identifying genetic risk loci for AD and ADRD, their application faces several challenges. Polygenicity is a hallmark of AD and ADRD, in which numerous genetic variants collectively contribute to disease risk. 6 This makes it difficult to identify risk‐increasing variants and explain a substantial portion of heritability. Genetic heterogeneity across dementia subtypes further complicates the interpretation of GWAS findings, as overlapping risk loci may influence distinct pathways in different neurodegenerative disorders and classification of dementia subtypes is difficult in population‐based cohorts. 128 Even with fine mapping and cross‐validation, regions of high linkage disequilibrium make it difficult to establish the effect of a specific variant on dementia without additional Mendelian randomization analyses. Furthermore, GWAS results should always be interpreted in the biological context, and integrating multiple dementia phenotypes in joint analyses can help reveal shared genetic architecture from subtype‐specific presentations.

Population diversity challenges

Another key limitation of GWASs is the historic lack of diversity in GWAS cohorts, though recent efforts are improving representation. Many biobanks, such as the UK Biobank, often recruit participants with shared demographics, and these participants then tend to be healthier and wealthier than the general population. 129 Additionally, most studies have been disproportionately based on European genetic ancestry, which limits generalizability to people of distinct ancestries. Notably, of the 18 GWASs considered in this review, 17 were conducted on cohorts of largely European genetic ancestry. Additionally, limited resources in developing countries make it difficult to conduct GWASs in these settings. This demographic bias skews allele frequency distributions and undermines the equitable application of GWAS‐based PRSs, as transferability across genetic ancestries remains challenging. 130 , 131 Sherva et al. performed GWAS of dementia in a population of African ancestry, identifying four significant loci previously established in European and African cohorts, APOE, TREM2, CD2AP, and ABCA7, and identified two novel genes, ROBO1 and RP11‐340A13.2, not previously associated with European cohorts. 132 Although ancestral similarity reduces the variance among the sample population and allows for a stronger statistical significance in highly expressed polymorphisms, 133 GWAS conducted on diverse populations and meta‐analyses of these GWAS may reveal broader population‐specific polymorphisms contributing to AD and ADRD, thus providing a more comprehensive understanding of underlying disease mechanisms.

ADRD diagnosis, aging, environmental exposures

Difficulty diagnosing dementia subtypes remains a constraint hurdle in GWASs. Patients frequently present with comorbidities, including multiple forms of diagnosable AD and ADRD, different stages of AD and ADRD, or additional age‐related conditions, such as diabetes mellitus or cardiovascular disease. 134 These co‐occurring conditions could influence results from GWASs. GWASs also overlook social, behavioral, and environmental factors, such as diet, exercise, and exposure to harmful substances, which may contribute to AD and ADRD epigenetics. 135 , 136

Improving eQTL and risk loci identification: tissue‐specific and cell type–specific approach

Until recently, most eQTL studies have focused on risk loci in either brain or blood tissues. However, recent advances in single nuclei RNA sequencing enabled the discovery of cell‐specific eQTLs. In this review, we curated public eQTL databases for the GWAS hits to support and confirm reported findings about affected AD genes. Moreover, tissue‐specific eQTLs were discovered in blood and brain tissue of AD patients that weren't detected before in generic eQTL analysis. 137 In a cell type–specific context, Yang et al. investigated AD‐related PRSs restricted to genes expressed in astrocytes or microglia. 138 They found that the astrocyte‐based AD PRS was associated with diffuse and neuritic Aβ plaques, while the microglia‐based AD PRS was linked to neuritic plaques, microglial activation, tau pathology, and cognitive decline. In a separate neuroimaging cohort of cognitively unimpaired individuals (n = 2921), the astrocyte‐based AD PRS was again associated with Aβ, and the microglia‐based AD PRS correlated with both Aβ and tau accumulation. These findings highlight the importance of cell type–specific eQTL and PRS design in complementing genetic risk to distinct AD endophenotypes and GWAS data. 138 Bryois et al. confirm cell type–specific differential expression of AD risk genes; integration of eQTL with GWAS data revealed that most loci co‐localized a single risk gene to a specific brain cell type, particularly in microglia, providing evidence of cell type–specific genetic regulation linked to AD pathology. 139 Future bioinformatic analyses of AD and ADRD should prioritize integrating cell type–specific eQTL and PRS to enhance the resolution for computing genetic dementia risk. Expanding the use of single‐nuclei sequencing across diverse brain cell types and combining it with GWAS data will enable more precise mapping of genetic variants to specific gene expression, regulation, and their functional roles in a cell type–specific context. Future improvements could also benefit from extensive stratifications of study cohorts based on age, race, and AD stage to reduce cohort genetic variance. A more comprehensive database of eQTL results for both cell and tissue types across varying ethnic populations will help bridge the gap between genetic association, expression, and function.

CONCLUSION

This review highlights the substantial advances made through GWASs in understanding the genetic architecture of dementia subtypes, as well as uncovering shared AD and ADRD loci and pathways linked to lysosomal mechanisms, lipid metabolism, autophagy, neuroinflammation, and ionic homeostasis. Challenges such as genetic heterogeneity, population diversity, and comorbidities in cohorts complicate the interpretation and application of findings from GWASs. Importantly, GWASs have highlighted the diverse genetic underpinnings of AD and ADRD. As a result, therapeutics that target only single disease mechanisms are likely to be insufficient. Instead, a more holistic, genetics‐informed approach may offer a more comprehensive path toward effective treatment. Future research should prioritize integrating tissue‐specific eQTL data, expanding diverse cohort studies, and optimizing PRSs to refine diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
