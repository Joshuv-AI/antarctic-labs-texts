Estimated pulse wave velocity and risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity in middle-aged and older adults: a cohort study
Authors: Jin Zhang, Yufei Li, Jiayun Wu, Ziyi Sun, Wenxi Juan, Wenjie Liu, Mengwen Huang, Kuiwu Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) is a growing global public health challenge. Early identification of CMM is crucial for preventive management and reducing disease burden.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the association between estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV), a convenient measure derived from age and blood pressure, and risk of incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults.MethodsThis study used data from Health and Retirement Study (HRS). Cox proportional hazards regression models and restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were used to explore the association between ePWV and incident CMM.ResultsA total of 6551 participants were included in the analysis. The median age of the participants at baseline was 66 years [IQR: 58—73 years], and 2396 (36.57%) were men. Participants were stratified into three groups according to tertiles of ePWV, with median (IQR) values of 8.69 (8.01—9.19), 10.58 (10.11—11.08), and 12.82 (12.18—13.89) m/s, respectively. After multivariate adjustment, compared with the lowest tertile, the middle and highest tertiles were associated with a 80% (HR: 1.80, 95% CI: 1.38—2.34) and 89% (HR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.35—2.64) increased risk of CMM. The RCS regression model revealed a significant positive association between ePWV and the incidence of CMM (P = 0.0004). Subgroup analyses and competing risk analysis confirmed the robustness of the main results.ConclusionsA positive association was observed between ePWV and incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults. Background

Over the past two decades, the global prevalence of multimorbidity has witnessed a steady upward trend. Notably, multimorbidity affects 37.2% of the general adult population worldwide, while the burden is even heavier among the older adults [1]. Individuals with multimorbidity experience impaired physical function, diminished quality of life, a higher incidence of mental health disorders, more frequent complications, elevated disability rates, premature death, and increased overall mortality risk [2–5]. The profound, widespread effects of multimorbidity on population health and socioeconomic progress underscore an urgent imperative to advance targeted research, prevention, and control efforts. Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) is a major global public health challenge, associated with substantial individual and socioeconomic burdens and increased mortality [6, 7]. Aging populations face increased CMM risks due to progressive metabolic deterioration and accumulated risk factors [8, 9]. Given the global trend of population aging, effective identification and timely management of modifiable risk factors for CMM may help prevent or reduce the risk of CMM development.

Cardiometabolic disease (CMD) refers to a cluster of interrelated metabolic and cardiovascular conditions characterized by systemic glucose and lipid dysregulation. This underlying dysregulation drives key pathophysiological processes, including immune-inflammatory activation, heightened oxidative stress, and vascular endothelial dysfunction [10–12]. At the vascular level, these alterations manifest as endothelial impairment, phenotypic switching of smooth muscle cells, extracellular matrix remodeling, and calcification, culminating in arterial stiffness [13]. This indicator, which is associated with the early stages of vascular aging, has emerged as a valuable predictor of CMD risk [14, 15]. Early assessment of arterial stiffness is crucial, as this parameter often undergoes changes prior to the onset of clinical cardiovascular symptoms and the development of metabolic dysfunction [16, 17]. Although no specific pharmacological target exists, managing risk factors can improve arterial elasticity, making it potential treatment for cardiovascular and metabolic prevention in the aging population [18, 19].

Carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) is the gold standard for assessing arterial stiffness, but its clinical application remains limited due to requirements for specialized equipment, operator expertise, and time-consuming procedures [20, 21]. Comparatively, estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV) offers a simpler, more convenient, and cost-effective alternative, with studies demonstrating similar consistency with cfPWV measurements [22, 23]. These characteristics make ePWV particularly appropriate for large-scale preliminary screening in scarce medical resources.

Current evidence regarding the association between ePWV and incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults remains limited. Therefore, this study used longitudinal data from a nationwide cohort to explore the association between ePWV and incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults. In this study, CMM was defined as the coexistence of diabetes, heart disease, and stroke, consistent with prior epidemiological frameworks focusing on major clinical outcomes, while hypertension (HTN), dyslipidemia, and obesity were included as covariates [6, 8, 24, 25].

Methods

Data sources and study population

Participants were selected from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), which provides nationally representative data through its longitudinal survey of more than 37,000 individuals aged 50 and older across 23,000 households in the United States [26]. Employing a multi-stage area probability design, The HRS survey features geographic stratification, clustered sampling units, and targeted oversampling of specific populations [27]. Beginning in 1992, the research initially included 51–61 year olds (birth years 1931–1941) and their spouses, conducting biennial surveys covering income and wealth; health, cognition and use of healthcare services; work and retirement; and family connections [26]. The survey implemented biennial biomarker assessments starting in 2006 using an alternating cohort design, with one cohort providing samples in 2006, 2010, and 2014, and the other cohort in 2008, 2012, and 2016 [26, 28]. HRS received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan Health Sciences and the National Institute on Aging [28]. All participants provided informed consent. This study utilized individual-level data with biomarker measurements from wave 8 (2006) and wave 9 (2008) of the HRS as baseline data. Subsequent follow-up surveys were used to monitor incident CMM until the final follow-up survey, namely wave 15 (2020) of HRS. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) incomplete ePWV data at baseline; (2) presence of CMD or missing CMD information at baseline; (3) lack of CMM follow-up data; or (4) missing key variables, including history of HTN or use of antihypertensive medication at baseline.

Data assessment and definition

Calculation of ePWV

The ePWV was calculated as previously described according to the following formula [22, 23, 29, 30]: ePWV = 9.587 − 0.402 × age + 4.560 × 10−3 × age2 − 2.621 × 10−5 × age2 × MBP + 3.176 × 10−3 × age × MBP − 1.832 × 10−2 × MBP. Mean arterial pressure (MBP) was calculated from systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) as: MBP = DBP + 0.4 (SBP − DBP). In the HRS, blood pressure was assessed with an Omron HEM-780 Intellisense Automated blood pressure monitor, which was fitted with a ComFit cuff (Omron; Kyoto, Japan) [31]. During the measurement process, HRS interviewers initiated the device by pressing the start button, after which the cuff automatically inflated to the appropriate level and subsequently deflated, displaying SBP and DBP readings. The monitor was positioned out of view of participants, and interviewers were instructed not to provide feedback if participants inquired about readings while measurements were ongoing. Each recorded measurement was followed by an interval of approximately 60 s before the next was taken. A total of three measurements were obtained for each participant. Further specifics regarding HRS protocols for blood pressure measurement can be found elsewhere [31]. Since elevated ePWV has been shown to predict adverse outcomes [32], participants were stratified into three groups by ePWV tertiles.

Definition of CMM

This study outcome was incident CMM, defined as the presence of two or three comorbid CMDs (i.e., diabetes, heart disease, or stroke) [6, 8, 24, 25]. In line with prior research [24], diabetes was defined as self-reported physician diagnosis, current diabetes treatment, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL, or glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. Heart disease and stroke were ascertained based on the self-reported physician diagnosis [24, 25, 33]. Specifically, in HRS, participants were asked, “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” "Has a doctor ever told you that you had a stroke?” "Has a doctor ever told you that you have diabetes or high blood sugar?” The study endpoint occurred at either the first diagnosis of CMM or the censoring date, whichever event happened first.

Covariates

Covariates encompassed a range of variables, which can be broadly categorized into demographic factors such as age, sex, residence type, and educational level; behavioral and lifestyle factors including smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity; and clinical or biological markers, namely obesity defined by body mass index (BMI), use of antihypertensive medication, and HbA1c. Residence type was categorized as rural and urban. Educational level was categorized into three groups: middle school or below, high school or vocational school, and college or above. Smoking status was categorized into four levels: never smoker, light smoker (< 15 cigarettes/day), moderate smoker (15—25 cigarettes/day), and heavy smoker (> 25 cigarettes/day) [34]. Alcohol consumption was classified into three categories: never drinker, light to moderate drinker (≤ 2 drinks/day), and heavy drinker (> 2 drinks/day) [35]. Physical activity level was divided into three categories: vigorous (vigorous activity more than once a week), moderate (moderate activity more than once a week), and inactive (the rest) [36]. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Obesity was defined as a categorical variable according to the World Health Organization classification (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). HbA1c was measured as a dried blood spot, released into the NHANES equivalent assay scale [37]. Full methodological specifications concerning biological sample collection, storage, transportation, preservation, and laboratory analysis have been documented in prior publications [28].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentage) and compared using the chi-squared tests. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using t-test (two groups) or one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) (three or more groups). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (three or more groups). Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests were employed to visualize and compare CMM cumulative incidence across groups. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to investigate the association between ePWV and incident CMM. Based on the literature review, three models were estimated: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, residence type, educational level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and obesity; Model 3 adjusted for age, sex, residence type, educational level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, obesity, use of antihypertensive medication, and HbA1c. Since the proportional hazards assumption was violated for age and the ePWV tertile variable, the model was stratified by age. The age cutoff of 70 years was chosen based on biological aging characteristics, clinically meaningful thresholds from prior cardiovascular and metabolic epidemiology, and the substantially higher competing mortality risk in individuals aged 70 and older. Collinearity diagnostics indicated that variance inflation factors were below 5 for all variables, suggesting no severe multicollinearity. We also used restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models to explore the dose–response relationships between ePWV as a continuous variable and incident CMM. Models were fitted with 3 to 5 nodes, and the final number of knots was selected based on the smallest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Furthermore, to assess potential effect modification by age, we performed RCS analyses among participants aged < 70 years. Missing data were assumed to be missing at random and were imputed using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) package in R [38]. The imputation procedure employed a random forest algorithm, generating 5 imputed datasets. Each random forest model consisted of 100 decision trees, and the process was run for 10 iterations to ensure convergence. Subsequent analyses were conducted on each of the 5 imputed datasets independently, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules to calculate the combined (average) estimates across the imputed datasets. Additionally, we performed subgroup and interaction analyses to investigate the potential association between ePWV and incident CMM among different populations. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding participants with the use of antihypertensive medication. In addition, to account for the competing risk between incident CMM and all-cause mortality, the Fine-Gray model was fitted to evaluate the association between ePWV and incident CMM. To assess whether the association of ePWV with outcomes was independent of its mathematical determinants, we conducted an analysis using the residual component of ePWV obtained from a linear regression of ePWV on age and MBP. Moreover, we evaluated the predictive performance improvement of adding ePWV to the basic model using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Concordance Index (C-index), and net reclassification improvement (NRI). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (version 27.0), Stata (version 16.0), and R (version 4.2.0).

Association between ePWV and incident CMM

During a median follow-up of 13.50 years, 455 (6.95%) participants developed CMM. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that participants in Group 1 (T1) had significantly lower cumulative incidence rates of CMM compared to thosein Group 3 (T3) (log-rank P < 0.001) (Additional file 1: Fig.S1). Table 2 showed the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of ePWV tertiles with CMM risk, derived from models with different levels of multiple adjustments. With the T1 level of ePWV as the reference, its T2 and T3 levels increased CMM risk by 80% (HR: 1.80, 95% CI: 1.38—2.34) and 89% (HR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.35—2.64). Multivariable RCS models showed a significant positive association between ePWV and incident CMM (P = 0.0004) (Fig. 2A). In the subgroup analysis stratified by age, a linear association between ePWV and CMM was observed among participants aged < 70 years (P for nonlinearity = 0.254). In contrast, analysis of the overall cohort revealed a significant nonlinear relationship (P for nonlinearity = 0.0009), characterized by a decelerated increase risk at higher ePWV values beyond a certain threshold (Fig. 2A, B).

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Stratified analyses by sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and obesity showed that the association between ePWV and incident CMM remained consistent with the main results (Additional file 1: Table S2). In the sensitivity analysis restricted to participants not using antihypertensive medication, ePWV was analyzed as tertiles in Cox proportional hazards regression models and as a continuous variable in RCS analyses. Consistent with the primary analysis, these analyses revealed associations between higher ePWV and increased CMM risk. (Additional file 1: Fig.S2, Table S3). When accounting for competing risks from all-cause mortality, the results remained consistent with the primary analysis (Additional file 1: Table S4). In contrast to the primary findings for ePWV, analysis based on the residual component of ePWV revealed no significant association with the primary analysis (Additional file 1: Table S5). A RCS analysis further showed that the relationship between residual ePWV and CMM risk remained non-significant across its entire distribution, with wide CIs consistently crossing the null value (Additional file 1: Fig.S3). Based on covariates in Model 3 (including age, sex, residence type, educational level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, obesity, use of antihypertensive medication, and HbA1c), the basic model was constructed. Adding ePWV to this basic model improved the predictive ability (AIC 4371.54, C-index 0.733; AIC 4359.64, C-index 0.744; NRI: 8.09%).

Discussion

This study explored the association between ePWV and risk of incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults. Age-stratified analysis was conducted due to violation of the proportional hazards assumption in the Cox regression models and evidence of nonlinearity in the RCS analyses. The results revealed a positive association between ePWV and incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults. Given its simplicity and accessibility, this tool holds considerable potential for early screening of high-risk individuals in resource-scarce settings, which is critical for implementing timely CMM management strategies and optimizing patient prognosis.

As a key manifestation and primary measurement of arterial stiffness, PWV is associated with adverse clinical outcomes including cardiovascular events, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality [39–41]. Additionally, metabolic dysfunction is also associated with arterial stiffness [41, 42]. Arterial stiffness, measured by cfPWV, was prospectively assessed in Framingham Study participants over a median follow-up of 15 years revealed that each SD increase in cfPWV was associated with a significantly increased cardiovascular disease risk (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.06—1.36) and diabetes risk (HR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.11—1.58), indicating its independent predictive value beyond conventional risk factors [15]. More studies have employed ePWV as a simple and accessible non-invasive assessment method. Studies have shown that ePWV exhibits a significant positive correlation with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular event risk, establishing it as a reliable predictive and innovative indicator [43]. Furthermore, ePWV has also demonstrated predictive value for diabetes. Large-scale cohort studies have identified a risk threshold of 8.47 m/s and validated its predictive value independent of traditional factors in non-obese young adults [44, 45]. Collectively, these findings underscore the important supplementary value of ePWV in clinical risk stratification, highlight the potential utility of ePWV in identifying individuals at risk of CMM. However, current evidence on PWV is largely limited to its association with single-disease endpoints [44, 45], and therefore its prospective relationship with CMM, a distinct and multifaceted clinical outcome, remains underexplored. Our study showed that ePWV was similarly associated with the risk of CMM in middle-aged and older adults. This finding aligns with existing evidence on ePWV and single CMDs while importantly extending its predictive utility to the more complex outcome of multimorbidity. Furthermore, our analysis using RCS revealed a linear relationship between ePWV and CMM risk in participants aged < 70 years, where increases in ePWV likely reflect progressive arterial stiffness and corresponding elevations in clinical risk. In the overall cohort, the observed nonlinearity may be attributable to a ceiling effect among older adults aged ≥ 70 years. In this group, extremely high ePWV may signify advanced biological aging, with further increases offering limited prognostic value due to competing risks and cumulative comorbidity burden. These findings support the potential of ePWV as a quantifiable clinical indicator for vascular health and CMM risk assessment. Consequently, ePWV may serve as a practical biomarker for early identification of individuals at high risk for CMM, with particular relevance to the relatively younger individuals within the middle-aged and older adults, offering a potential tool for integrated risk management in clinical practice. Furthermore, findings from an individual participant data meta-analysis encompassing 17,635 subjects revealed that PWV was a more potent predictor of future atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events and mortality in middle-aged subjects compared to older populations [46]. To further clarify the independent role of ePWV in predicting incident CMM, residual ePWV analyses were conducted. Interestingly, while ePWV itself was positively associated with CMM risk, its residual component, representing the portion independent of age and blood pressure, showed no significant association. This suggests that the predictive value of ePWV is primarily driven by its integration of these two core determinants of vascular aging. Notably, the association between PWV and clinical outcomes is influenced by multiple risk factors, including lifestyle [47], disease status [48], and genetic predisposition [49]. In humans, established risk factors such as excessive food intake, altered lipid metabolism, smoking, and physical inactivity likely interact with age-related arterial changes, transforming aging arteries into a “fertile soil” that promotes the development and progression of arterial diseases [50]. While no statistically significant subgroup interaction was observed in our study, our findings indicated that the association between ePWV and CMM risk may be more pronounced among ever-smokers and individuals with heavy alcohol consumption.

Arterial stiffness reflects the material properties of the arterial wall, with functional consequences for pressure, blood flow, and arterial diameter change with each heartbeat. The pressure load of each heartbeat in large conduit arteries is borne mainly by the elastin and collagen components, with less contribution from smooth muscle in the muscular arteries [50]. By lowering blood pressure, all antihypertensive agents alleviate this mechanical stress on the stiff structural components of the arterial wall; this reduction in hemodynamic load, in turn, drives a passive decrease in PWV, thereby mitigating arterial stiffness [51]. This pharmacologically driven attenuation of arterial stiffness suggests that the predictive value of ePWV for CMM risk may be modified in individuals on chronic antihypertensive therapy. Impaired insulin sensitivity is a well-established driver of accelerated aging, and vascular aging, as quantified by ePWV, serves as a partial mediator of this association [52]. Our sensitivity analysis, which excluded participants receiving antihypertensive medications, further substantiated the robustness of this relationship. Higher ePWV tertiles remained significantly associated with elevated CMM risk even after comprehensive adjustment for confounders, including obesity and baseline HbA1c. These findings highlight the independent role of ePWV as a predictor of CMM risk, beyond traditional metabolic factors. Accordingly, the predictive value of ePWV for CMM appears to be contingent on the presence or history of HTN and concomitant metabolic dysregulation, a mechanism that is biologically plausible and clinically relevant. ePWV may therefore serve as an integrative marker reflecting combined vascular and metabolic dysfunction. In clinical practice, ePWV should therefore be interpreted alongside metabolic parameters and medication status.

The strengths of our study include its broad population combined with the long follow-up period. Regular measurement of ePWV enables early identification of individuals at high risk of CMM, facilitating timely interventions to slow disease progression and prevent adverse clinical outcomes. Although this indicator is not entirely accurate, ePWV remains valuable in areas with scarce medical resources. This is particularly evident in areas where systemic deficiencies in health literacy, healthcare accessibility, provider availability, and research infrastructure collectively hinder effective CMM prevention and management [53]. However, there are some limitations: 1) Consistent with prior public database studies, our definition of CMM was defined based on self-reported physician diagnoses of only diabetes, heart disease and stroke rather than broader cardiometabolic conditions such as dyslipidemia, obesity and hypertension, which may introduce misclassification bias and limit the generalizability of our findings to comprehensive cardiometabolic risk frameworks. 2) Notably, ePWV is not a direct hemodynamic measurement of arterial stiffness. While it offers practical advantages, its formula-based derivation may not fully capture the complex, non-linear biomechanics of the arterial tree or regional vascular differences assessed by the gold-standard cfPWV. This inherent limitation may compromise diagnostic accuracy, suggesting future studies should validate findings through device-measured PWV. Moreover, ePWV was assessed only at baseline, which may not fully capture longitudinal changes in vascular aging. This time-varying exposure misclassification could bias risk estimates. 3) As an observational study, it remains susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Future studies would benefit from inclusion of broader biological markers and detailed clinical measures. 4) As our cohort was restricted to middle-aged and older adults from HRS, the external validity of our findings may be limited. The applicability of these results to low- and middle-income countries requires further prospective validation despite the potential suitability of ePWV in resource-limited settings.

Conclusions

A positive association was observed between ePWV and incident CMM in middle-aged and older adults. With technological advances, more accurate assessment methods are expected. While precise measurements enable better predictions, this simple approach remains valuable in areas with scarce medical resources, where it could facilitate early intervention for CMM and potentially improve outcomes.
