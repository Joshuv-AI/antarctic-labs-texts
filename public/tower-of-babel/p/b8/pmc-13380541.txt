Effects of Gut Microbiome and Metabolic Pathways on Obesity: A Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization and Case-Control Study
Authors: Xiaoyu Fu, Yu Xia, Jing Han, Wei Wang, Boning Zhang, Heyuan Liu, Xue Yang, Shengnan Han, Chenggang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives: We employed Mendelian randomization (MR) and case-control studies to identify causal associations and validate biological relevance.Materials and Methods: Two-sample MR using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) summary statistics prioritized inverse variance-weighted analysis, supplemented by weighted median and MR-Egger regression. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out cross-validation, MR-PRESSO global test, and Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity/pleiotropy. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction identified robust associations. Clinical validation involved 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics in obese and case-control cohorts.Results: MR identified two microbiota (Streptococcus thermophilus, OR = 0. 98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99, P < 0.01; Lachnospiraceae bacterium 5_1_63FAA, OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, P < 0.001) and two pathways (4-aminobutanoate degradation V, OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.97, P < 0.001; Pyridoxal 5 phosphate biosynthesis I (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.98, P < 0.001) significantly associated with reduced obesity risk. Sensitivity analyses confirmed no heterogeneity/pleiotropy. Clinical data validated these findings: obese participants exhibited lower abundances of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lachnospiraceae bacterium 5_1_63FAA, reduced serum 4-aminobutanoate and pyridoxal 5-phosphate levels.Conclusion: Lachnospiraceae bacterium 5_1_63FAA and Streptococcus thermophilus, alongside 4-aminobutanoate degradation and pyridoxal 5-phosphate biosynthesis, represent protective factors against obesity. Targeted modulation of these targets may exert beneficial effects on the prevention and treatment of obesity.

1. Background

Obesity, characterized by excessive fat accumulation, is a chronic metabolic disorder and a major risk factor for leading global causes of mortality, including cardiovascular diseases, stroke, diabetes, and various cancers ( 1 ). It significantly reduces health outcomes and life expectancy, with over 43% of the global population classified as overweight and more than 1 billion individuals affected by obesity in 2022 ( 2 ). Obesity arises from complex interactions between genetic, dietary, lifestyle, and environmental factors, with energy metabolism imbalance serving as a critical underlying mechanism ( 3 ). Emerging evidence indicates that prebiotics or other interventions targeting gut microbiota dysbiosis may regulate metabolism and alleviate obesity ( 4 , 5 ); however, the causal links between specific microbiota and obesity remain unclear, which limits the development of precise interventional strategies. Recent advances in GWAS have provided robust datasets to address this challenge. MR, a method leveraging genetic variants strongly associated with exposure factors as instrumental variables (IVs), enables causal inference between exposures and outcomes while minimizing confounding biases ( 6 , 7 ). We integrated GWAS-derived single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to gut microbiota and metabolic pathways. During integration, we excluded confounding SNPs. Using these SNPs, we performed MR analyses to evaluate the causal roles of specific gut microbes and metabolic pathways in obesity. To validate these findings, we conducted a case-control study analyzing fecal and serum samples using 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics to characterize differential microbiota and metabolite profiles between obese and non-obese individuals.

2. Objectives

The primary objective of this study is to identify key microbial taxa and metabolic biomarkers driving obesity pathogenesis, thereby providing actionable targets for microbial and metabolite-based prevention and therapeutic strategies.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. MR Study

3.1.1. Gut Microbiota and BMI Data

The data used for MR analyses were derived from previously published GWAS datasets. Gut microbiota profiles were determined via shotgun metagenomic sequencing of fecal samples from 7,738 European-ancestry individuals in the Dutch Microbiome Project. Taxonomic classification included 207 features (5 phyla, 10 classes, 13 orders, 26 families, 48 genera, 105 species), with 205 microbiota-associated metabolic pathways. These exposure datasets were accessed from the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) ( 8 ). Body Mass Index (BMI) was selected as the outcome variable, with summary statistics obtained from the FinnGen database (r10.finngen.fi; n = 290,820 participants, 21,303,244 SNPs) ( 9 ). All GWAS data were publicly available, de-identified, and ethically approved by original study committees.

3.1.2. IVs Selection

MR employs genetic variants associated with exposure but independent of outcomes to estimate causal effects while minimizing confounding ( 8 , 10 ). IVs, Genetic variants that are strongly correlated with the exposure but genetically independent of the outcome, acting as proxies to mimic random assignment and estimate causal effects in Mendelian randomization.​ MR requires three assumptions: 1) IVs show strong correlation with exposure (genome-wide significance threshold); 2) IVs affect outcomes only through exposure (no direct effects); 3) IVs share no common causes with outcomes (no confounding) ( 6 , 11 ) (Supplementary Fig. S1). To satisfy the first assumption, we adjusted the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5×10-8) to a lenient cutoff (P < 1×10-5) due to limited SNPs meeting strict criteria ( 12 ), retaining variants associated with gut microbiota. Linkage disequilibrium (LD), the non-random association between alleles at distinct genetic loci that can confound MR analyses by inducing pleiotropy through correlated variants, was mitigated via clustering (R2 < 0.01, allele distance = 10,000 kb). This reduced spurious associations and selected independent instrumental variables (IVs) ( 13 ). BMI-associated SNPs and statistics were extracted from GWAS data. F-statistics > 10 excluded weak IVs ( 14 ), and non-palindromic SNPs were retained to align allele directions ( 15 ). For independence, PhenoScannerV2 (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) filtered SNPs linked to confounders (e.g., autoimmune diseases, hyperlipidemia) at P < 5×10-8 and R2 > 0.80 ( 16 ).

3.1.3. MR Analysis

We used inverse-variance weighted (IVW) as the primary analysis method, supplemented by MR-Egger regression and weighted median estimator (WME) ( 17 ). The IVW method is a weighted linear regression model assuming all genetic variants are valid IVs. It uses inverse-variance weighting (SE2) to fit outcomes and combines SNP-specific effect estimates via fixed-effects or random-effects models ( 18 ). MR-Egger regression adds an intercept term to detect horizontal pleiotropy ( 19 ), while the WME prioritizes robust causal estimates using the majority of genetic variation ( 20 ). Significant results (IVW P < 0.05) with WME support were considered statistically valid.

3.1.4. Sensitivity Analyses

We assessed heterogeneity using Cochran’s Q statistic (P < 0.05 indicated heterogeneity), selecting random-effects models for heterogeneous data and fixed-effects models otherwise ( 18 ). MR-Egger intercept tests and MR-PRESSO global tests evaluated horizontal pleiotropy and outliers. MR-Egger allowed pleiotropy under the InSIDE assumption (P < 0.05 indicated significance) ( 21 ), while MR-PRESSO offered higher precision for detecting pleiotropy/outliers ( 22 ). Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction was applied to adjust for multiple testing, controlling the FDR at Q < 0.05, leave-one-out analysis iteratively excluded individual SNPs to identify influential outliers.

3.2. Reverse MR

We performed reverse MR using IVW and WME-supported microbiota and metabolic pathways, treating BMI as the exposure and gut microbiota/metabolic pathways as outcomes to test for causal effects of BMI on these factors. The analytical workflow mirrored that of the forward MR analysis. We performed reverse MR analysis of the gut microbiota and metabolic pathways using both the IVW and WME methods. In this analysis, we considered BMI as the exposure factor and assessed its causal effects on the gut microbiota and metabolic pathways as outcomes. The steps involved in reverse MR are similar to those in forward MR.

3.3. Case-Control Study

3.3.1. Participant Recruitment

The case-control study was conducted from December 2023 to March 2024 in Fangshan District, Beijing. Participants included 27 individuals with obesity and 24 controls. Inclusion criteria: Age 18–65 years, BMI ≥ 18 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria: 1) Secondary obesity; 2) Comorbid major organ dysfunction (cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, renal); 3) Antibiotic/probiotic use within 1 month; 4) Severe mental illness or other critical organic diseases. Grouping criteria: 1) Obesity group: BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2; 2) Control group: 18 kg/m2 ≤ BMI ≤ 28 kg/m2. Demographic characteristics, clinical data (age, sex and smoking/drinking status), body composition, metabolic parameters, and blood/fecal samples were collected per standardized protocols.

3.3.2. Body Composition and Metabolic Parameter Measurements

Body composition parameters were measured using the X-CONTACT 350 analyzer (Beijing, China) based on bioelectrical impedance analysis. Metrics included body weight, body fat percentage (BFP), skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), visceral fat area (VFA), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and basal metabolic rate, with a reported accuracy of ≥ 97%. Fasting blood samples (8-hour fasting period) were analyzed for high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) using the Beckman Coulter AU680 automated biochemical analyzer (USA) via enzymatic assays.

3.3.3. Fecal Sample Processing, DNA Extraction, and 16S rRNA Sequencing

Fecal samples were collected using sterile fecal collection kits and stored at -80 °C. Total genomic DNA was extracted via the CTAB/SDS method followed by the Omega D4015 kit (Omega, Inc.). The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of 16S rRNA were amplified using primers 341F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’) with PCR. Amplicons were purified using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany), and libraries were constructed with the NEBNext® UltraTM II DNA Library Prep Kit. Libraries were quantified via Qubit and qPCR, and qualified libraries were sequenced on the Nova Seq 6000 platform.

3.3.4. Serum Sample Preparation and Metabolomics Analysis

Serum samples from 12 obese and 12 control participants were randomly selected for metabolomics analysis, the sample size per group was determined via a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1), assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s f2 = 0.15) for key metabolites (e.g., 4-aminobutanoate). With 80% power and α = 0.05, this design ensured sufficient participants to detect statistically significant differences, supporting robust metabolomic profiling. A 50 μL aliquot was mixed with 200 μL pre-chilled extraction solvent (acetonitrile: methanol: water = 2:2:1, containing 0.1% formic acid and isotope-labeled internal standards), vortexed for 30 s, ultrasonicated at 4 °C for 15 min, and incubated at −20 °C for 2 h to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation (14,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), 150 μL supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and analyzed via LC-MS. Quality control samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of supernatants. Chromatography: Separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) at 45 °C. Mobile phases: 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). Gradient elution: 0–1 min (95% B), 1–12 min (95→65% B), 12–12.1 min (65→40% B), 12.1–15 min (40% B) and 15.1–18 min (95% B) at 0.3 mL.min-1. Injection volume: 3 μl Mass spectrometry: Data were acquired using a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (HESI-II source) in positive/negative ion switching mode. Full-scan resolution: 120,000 (m.z-1 200), spray voltage ± 3.8 kV, sheath gas 45 Arb, auxiliary gas 15 Arb, capillary temperature 320 °C. Data-dependent MS/MS (Top10) used stepped collision energy (20/40/60 eV) at 15,000 resolution. Data acquisition was controlled by Xcalibur 4.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

3.3.5. Bioinformatics and Statistical Analysis

MR analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.0) using the Two Sample MR (v0.5.7) and Mendelian Randomization (v0.9.0) packages.16S rRNA sequencing data were processed via FLASH (v1.2.11) to generate raw tags, followed by quality control with fast QC to obtain clean tags. Chimeric sequences were removed using VSEARCH (v2.20.1) to yield effective tags. DADA2 in QIIME2 denoised effective tags to produce amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and feature tables, with taxonomic assignment performed by classify-sklearn using the SILVA (v138.1) database. Alpha diversity and Unifrac distances were calculated in QIIME2, and alpha-diversity visualizations (e.g., bar plots) and beta-diversity ordinations Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) were generated using ade4 and ggplot2 in R. Functional predictions for gut microbiota were inferred by aligning ASVs to the Greengenes database. Functional predictions were generated with PICRUSt2 (v2.5.0) by aligning amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) to the Greengenes 13_5 database. This tool predicts functional pathways (KEGG orthologs) from 16S rRNA data, allowing quantitative assessment of the gut microbiota’s metabolic potential. Metabolite annotations were conducted using HMDB, SMPDB, and LipidMaps databases, with differential metabolites identified as VIP > 1.0 (PLS-DA) and P < 0.05 (Student’s t-test). Pathway enrichment analysis utilized Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Statistical parameters (mean ± standard error) and Spearman’s correlation were computed in SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp.), while graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (v9.2.0). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

4. Results

4.1. MR Analysis

4.1.1. IVs Selection

For gut microbiota associated with BMI, SNPs were selected after removing LD (R2 < 0.01), retaining variants with F-statistics >10 to minimize weak IV bias, excluding palindromic SNPs, and filtering confounding factors (autoimmune diseases, hyperlipidemia, basal metabolic rate) via Phenoscanner. A total of 3,503 SNPs (p < 1 × 10-5) met independence assumptions, covering 196 microbiota features across six taxonomic ranks (phylum, class, order, family, genus, species) and 201 microbiota-related metabolic pathways.

4.1.2. Two-Sample MR Analysis

Two-sample MR (IVW) identified 3 microbiota taxa and 4 metabolic pathways associated with BMI (Fig. 1). Protective factors linked to reduced obesity risk included Lachnospiraceae bacterium 5_1_63FAA (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, P = 0.0005) and Streptococcus thermophilus (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99, P = 0.0099). The bacterial class Bacilli (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03, P = 0.0001) was a risk factor for higher obesity prevalence. Metabolic pathways associated with elevated obesity risk included Adenosylcobalamin salvage from cobinamide I (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04, P = 0.001). Protective pathways were Pyridoxal 5-phosphate biosynthesis I (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.98, P = 0.0005), Pyridoxal 5-phosphate biosynthesis and salvage (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.98, P = 0.0214), and 4-Aminobutanoate degradation V (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.97, P = 0.0001). These results were supported by the WME.

MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global tests showed no horizontal pleiotropy or outliers (P > 0.05 for both; Fig. 2). Cochran’s Q test (P = 0.12; Table 1) revealed no substantial heterogeneity, and the funnel plot (Fig. 3) demonstrated symmetrical distribution of effect sizes, indicating no evidence of small-study effects or publication bias. 4.1.3. Reverse MR Analysis

Reverse MR analysis of significant results from the forward MR analysis revealed no significant (P > 0.05) causal effects of BMI on differentially abundant microbiota or metabolites (Supplementary Table S1), indicating no evidence that BMI directly regulates these microbial and metabolic changes. This supports the unidirectional causal role of the identified gut microbiota in obesity protection, where microbial alterations precede and influence BMI, rather than being driven by it.

4.1.4. FDR Correction

FDR refers to the expected proportion of false positives among hypotheses rejected in multiple hypothesis testing. To reduce false-positive findings, FDR correction was applied to all significant results. Four features passed FDR correction: Lachnospiraceae bacterium_5_1_63FAA (P = 0.039), Streptococcus thermo-philus (P = 0.018), 4-Aminobutanoate degradation V (P = 0.007), and Pyridoxal 5-phosphate biosynthesis I (P = 0.039) (Table 1).

4.2. Case-Control Study

4.2.1. Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The obese group (n = 27) and control group (n = 24) showed no significant differences in sex ratio, age, height, smoking/drinking history, or basal metabolic rate (P > 0.05). Compared to controls, the obese group had significantly higher body weight, BMI (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 8.32–13.60), VFA (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 47.54–91.78), BFP (P = 0.001, 95% CI: 1.86–6.74), SMI (P = 0.01, 95% CI: 12.27–23.27), waistline (P = 0.025, 95% CI: 18.28–34.38), hip circumference (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 10.71–19.69), and WHR (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.07–0.16) (Supplementary Table S2).

4.2.2. Gut Microbiota Analysis

Species rarefaction curves (Supplementary Fig. S2A) confirmed adequate sampling depth and sequencing coverage. The Venn diagram (Supplementary Fig. S2B) showed fewer Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) in the obesity group compared to controls. Statistical comparisons revealed significantly lower Simpson index (P = 0.034, 95% CI: 0.01–0.06) and observed OTUs (P = 0.045, 95% CI: 1.93–159.50) in the obesity group (Supplementary Fig. S2C-S2E). Beta diversity analysis using Weighted Unifrac distances (Supplementary Fig. S2F) demonstrated significant compositional differences between groups.

To validate the results obtained from the MR analysis, we extracted the corresponding species abundance data from the 16S rRNA sequencing results of both groups and performed a T-test. The results demonstrated that the abundance of Streptococcus_thermophilus (P = 0.008, 95% CI: 0.0005–0.002) (Fig. 5A) and Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_5_1_63FAA (P = 0.0234, 95% CI: 0.0001–0.001) (Fig. 5B) was significantly lower in the obesity group compared with the control group. Pearson correlation analysis (Supplementary Table S3) revealed significant negative correlations between Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_5_1_63FAA and BMI (r = −0.37, P = 0.008, 95% CI: −0.59, 0.10), VFA(r = −0.34, P = 0.012, 95% CI: −0.57, −0.08), TC (r = −0.40, P = 0.001, 95% CI: −0.61, −0.15), and FBG (r = −0.42, P = 0.0005, 95% CI: −0.63, −0.17). Streptococcus thermophilus showed significant negative correlations with BMI (r = −0.32, P = 0.018, 95% CI: −0.55, 0.05), BFP (r = −0.28, P = 0.032, 95% CI: −0.52, −0.009), and TG (r = −0.30, P = 0.045, 95% CI: −0.53, −0.02), but a positive correlation with HDL (r = 0.32, P = 0.021, 95% CI: 0.05, 0.55) (Fig. 5C). T-tests on third-level functional categories (Fig. 5D) showed significantly reduced amino acid metabolism in the obesity group, including glycine/serine/threonine metabolism (P = 0.031, 95% CI: 0.00002–0.000325), taurine/hypotaurine metabolism (P = 0.018, 95% CI: 0.00002–0.0002), and selenocompound metabolism (P = 0.016, 95% CI: 0.00001–0.0001). Obesity also exhibited reduced cellular transport/catabolism (P = 0.018, 95% CI: 0.00001–0.0004) and adipocytokine signaling pathway activity (P = 0.047, 95% CI: 0.000002–0.0004) compared to controls.

4.2.3. Metabolic Pathways Validation

Metabolic pathways, acting as mediators of microbial-host interactions, exert decisive effects on host energy metabolism. Both anabolic and catabolic processes jointly influence body composition changes. Under positive or negative ion modes, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) scores demonstrated stable clustering of QC samples, distinguishing the Obesity group from the Control group (Fig. 6A). OPLS-DA scoring further separated the groups, revealing numerous distinct potential biomarkers. Using thresholds of P < 0.05 and VIP > 1, 93 differential metabolites were identified between groups, including 63 downregulated and 29 upregulated metabolites in the Obesity group (Fig. 6B). T-test revealed significantly lower GABA levels in the Obesity group compared to controls (Fig. 6C). Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) is one of the active forms of vitamin B6 ( 23 ). In the metabolomic analysis conducted in this study, two active forms of vitamin B6 were detected: PLP and pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP). T-test results indicated that the levels of both PLP and PMP were lower in the obese group compared with the control group, which is consistent with the MR analysis findings. Higher PLP levels were associated with lower BMI level. In this study, two active forms—pyridoxamine phosphate and pyridoxal phosphate—were detected as indirect biomarkers of pyridoxal 5’-phosphate levels. T-tests showed reduced pyridoxamine phosphate levels in the Obesity group (Fig. 6D-6E). Spearman correlation analysis revealed positive associations: Lachnospiraceae bacterium_5_1_63FAA correlated with pyridoxamine phosphate and pyridoxal phosphate, while Streptococcus thermophilus correlated with 4-aminobutanoate (Fig. 6F). KEGG enrichment analysis identified downregulated metabolic pathways in the Obesity group, including amino acid biosynthesis (valine, leucine, isoleucine) and disrupted 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism and propanoate metabolism (Fig. 6G-6H). These findings collectively highlight altered metabolic networks in obesity driven by microbiota-metabolite interactions.

5. Discussion

The objective of this study was to identify gut microbiota and targeted metabolites associated with obesity prevention through a combination of MR analysis and clinical case-control studies. To evaluate generalizability, we leveraged independent samples from European populations for both exposure (microbiota/metabolite measurements) and outcome (obesity status) data, and further externally validated MR findings using Asian independent populations. This multi-population strategy confirms our results have good universality and adaptability across diverse demographic groups. Previous research has suggested a potential link between obesity and gut microbiota. A clinical controlled trial demonstrated that short-term energy surplus alters the composition of gut microbiota ( 25 ). Supplementation with prebiotics or probiotics can modulate the gut microbial composition, regulate metabolic patterns, improve body composition in overweight and obese individuals ( 5 ), and ameliorate blood lipid, glucose, and insulin levels ( 6 ). Prior observational studies have linked gut microbiota to obesity, but causal relationships remain unclear—this gap is addressed in our study through MR analysis and clinical validation. By leveraging GWAS data and employing MR analysis, we identified two microbial taxa and two metabolic pathways strongly causally associated with changes in BMI. Our study provides a theoretical basis for accurate obesity intervention strategies, helps clinicians and obese patients to discover the potential risks of obesity in time, and provides intervention targets for obesity prevention and control, but further clinical verification is still needed.

S_Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_5_1_63FAA and Streptococcus thermophilus were identified as protective bacterial taxa against weight gain, while 4-aminobutanoate degradation and pyridoxal 5’-phosphate biosynthesis were found to be protective metabolic pathways. These results were further supported by the case-control study, providing additional evidence for the potential of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_5_1_63FAA as probiotics for ameliorating obesity, as well as the protective roles of 4-aminobutanoate and pyridoxal 5’-phosphate in weight regulation. Streptococcus thermophilus (Bacilli class) is a probiotic commonly used in dairy production, capable of surviving and exhibiting metabolic activity in the gastrointestinal tract while producing heterologous proteins ( 24 ). The strain MN-ZLW-002 activates macrophages to secrete IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α, and significantly inhibits 3T3-L1 preadipocyte differentiation and lipogenesis via phosphorylation of the P38/p44/42 MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway, with TNF-α playing a pivotal role in lipid metabolism regulation ( 25 ). Animal studies demonstrated that oral administration of this strain reduces high-fat diet -induced weight gain, lowers FBG, serum TC, and IL-1β/CCL3 levels in mice ( 26 ). Further revealed its potential to counteract High-Fat Diet-induced obesity and metabolic dysregulation by modulating bile acid metabolism ( 27 ).

Lachnospiraceae bacterium (Firmicutes, Lachnospiraceae) is abundantly present in the gut of healthy individuals. Increased abundance upregulates butyrate, pyruvate, and valeric acid levels ( 28 ).

Pyruvate, a key regulatory factor in glycolysis, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism ( 29 ), enhances fatty acid oxidation and energy expenditure to inhibit lipid accumulation while improving insulin sensitivity, thereby reducing lipid deposition—particularly in insulin-resistant obesity ( 29 ). Naringin supplementation significantly increases Lachnospiraceae bacterium 28_4 abundance, promoting adipose thermogenesis and browning to ameliorate obesity ( 30 ). Dietary butyrate selectively promotes its proliferation, enhancing metabolic health ( 31 ). Studies identified Lachnospiraceae bacterium 3_1_57FAA_CT1 as a protective biomarker against obesity-induced insulin resistance, correlating with butyrate production ( 32 ). Butyrate improves mitochondrial function, increases energy expenditure and fatty acid oxidation, reduces lipid accumulation and oxidative stress ( 33 , 34 ), and induces glucagon-like peptide-1secretion. Notably, Lachnospiraceae bacterium_5_1_63FAA may serve as a gastric cancer protective biomarker ( 35 ) highlighting its potential to modulate host metabolism via microbiota-metabolite interactions.

4-Aminobutanoate, also known as γ-aminobutanoate (GABA), is a key neurotransmitter that regulates sleep, mood, and metabolic homeostasis, Its supplementation can be achieved through direct dietary intakeor indirectly produced by probiotics such as Lactobacillus brevis and Lactobacillus plantarum ( 36 , 37 ), It is noteworthy that Streptococcus thermophilus also possesses the potential to produce GABA ( 38 ), aligning with our microbiota-metabolite correlation analysis. GABA degradation pathways significantly influence metabolism by enhancing insulin secretion ( 39 ), while dietary poly-γ-glutamic acid supplementation elevates serum GABA levels in obese rats, reducing BMI ( 40 ). The GABA metabolite succinic acid activates the TCA cycle to enhance glycolipid metabolism and suppress lipogenesis ( 41 ). Clinical trials demonstrate that GABA supplementation improves cardiovascular risk in dyslipidemia patients ( 42 ), and a GABA-rich rice bran diet modulates gut microbiota to increase short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), alleviating insulin resistance and promoting energy expenditure ( 43 ). Additionally, GABA enhances satiety via vagus nerve activation, regulating appetite ( 44 ). These findings suggest that GABA supplementation may lower cholesterol and triglycerides through glycolipid and amino acid pathways, offering a novel obesity intervention strategy. Pyridoxal 5-phosphate (PLP), the active form of vitamin B6, serves as a cofactor for over 140 enzymes (e.g., GABA transaminase), governing glucose, lipid, amino acid, and neurotransmitter metabolism ( 23 , 45 ). Synthesized via pyridoxal kinase (PDXK) and lipogenesis pathways, PLP levels in subcutaneous adipose tissue positively correlate with PDXK expression, which is enriched in adipocytes. PDXK may regulate adipocyte differentiation and lipid accumulation through folate-mediated one-carbon metabolism ( 46 ). However, PLP treatment promotes fatty acid β-oxidation and inhibits lipid uptake, improving energy metabolism ( 47 ). Cohort studies link PLP to reduced LDL and elevated HDL levels ( 48 ). Gut microbiota both consume and produce PLP, underscoring their complex interplay ( 49 ). PLP supplementation may represent a preventive strategy for metabolic disorders.

Functional predictions revealed that gut microbiota regulate host metabolism, with reduced amino acid metabolism (glycine, serine and threonine) in obese individuals, consistent with prior studies ( 50 ). This metabolic axis serves as a critical hub for molecular synthesis, transport, cellular repair, and antioxidant defense, enhancing SCFA and polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) metabolism to reduce obesity risk ( 51 ). Selenocompound metabolism increases intestinal butyrate and propionate production, which act as primary energy substrates for intestinal epithelial cells to alleviate oxidative stress, promote lipid metabolism, and maintain energy homeostasis ( 52 ). Selenium, as selenite, suppresses inflammatory cytokine release by upregulating antioxidant selenoproteins, thereby inhibiting adipocyte differentiation and lipid deposition ( 53 ).

Metabolic pathway enrichment identified amino acid metabolism (valine, leucine, isoleucine biosynthesis) as a major dysregulated pathway in obesity. These branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) regulate protein synthesis, fatty acid oxidation, and energy metabolism. Dietary BCAA supplementation en-hances fat oxidation, reduces cardiovascular risk, and improves ketogenesis, contribute to glucose homeostasis ( 54 - 56 ). Additionally, 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism (α-ketoglutarate, pyruvate, aceto-acetate) was significantly downregulated in obesity. Reduced 2-oxocarboxylic acid levels impair TCA cycle flux and energy production, as shown in vitamin D-deficient obese mice ( 57 ). Propanoate, a microbial fermentation product, inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis, reduces adiposity, and improves insulin sensitivity via SCFA-mediated mechanisms ( 58 , 59 ). These findings collectively demonstrate that microbiota-driven disruptions in amino acid and SCFA metabolism synergistically drive obesity pathogenesis.

Our study has several limitations. First, the MR analysis was based on GWAS data primarily from European populations, while the case-control study included Asian participants. Although no formal subgroup analysis was performed due to limited sample size, preliminary consistency of key findings (e.g., reduced abundance of S. thermophilus in obesity) across these populations supports initial generalizability. However, this does not negate potential population heterogeneity, and future cross-ethnic studies are warranted to confirm robustness. Second, the GWAS databases used in MR analysis covered only 207 gut microbiota taxa and 204 metabolites—far fewer than the expanding catalog of microbial species and metabolites enabled by advances in metagenomics and metabolomics. This discrepancy highlights potential inadequacies in microbial and metabolite coverage, which may limit the completeness of identified associations. Third, the identified microbiota–metabolite–obesity associations remain unvalidated through in vivo animal experiments. Functional validation via mechanistic studies (e.g., gnotobiotic mouse models) is critical to unravel the biological significance of these findings and bridge correlative observations to causal pathways. Future directions should primarily focus on: expanding microbial and metabolomic coverage via cutting-edge omics technologies, incorporating larger multi-ethnic cohorts to mitigate population heterogeneity, and validating findings through in vivo experiments to establish causality.

6. Conclusion

MR analysis to preliminarily investigate the strong causal associations between two microbial taxa and two metabolic pathways with obesity, specifically Streptococcus thermophilus, Lachnospiraceae bacterium 5_1_63FAA, 4-aminobutanoate degradation, and pyridoxal 5-phosphate biosynthesis. Their effects as protective factors for BMI were validated using clinical data. These findings suggest that targeted modulation of these gut microbiota and metabolites may contribute to the amelioration of obesity. Potential interrelationships may exist among these microbial species and metabolic pathways; however, the underlying crosstalk mechanisms among gut microbiota, metabolic pathways, and the host require further investigation.
