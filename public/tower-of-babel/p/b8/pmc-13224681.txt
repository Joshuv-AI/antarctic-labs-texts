Do maternal self-reported socio-demographic and green space characteristics influence neonatal birth weight? Evidence from Ghana
Authors: Sylvester Kumi Boakye, Leslie Danquah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal birth weight is a critical indicator of infant survival and long-term health, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where the burden of low birth weight remains high. While maternal socio-demographic factors are well-established determinants of birth weight, growing evidence suggests that environmental exposures such as access to and interaction with green spaces may also influence pregnancy outcomes through pathways related to stress reduction, physical activity, and improved environmental quality. However, empirical evidence on the role of maternal green space exposure in shaping neonatal birth weight is limited in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana. This study, therefore, aimed to examine the relationship between maternal self-reported socio-demographic and green space characteristics and neonatal birth weight in Sunyani, Ghana.MethodsThis study was cross-sectional and exploratory. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was administered to 383 mothers in a hospital-based setting in Sunyani, Ghana. Eleven (11) Socio-demographic and seven (7) green space characteristics were investigated. Chi-square, Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify relationships among factors.ResultsAmong the defined maternal age categories, mothers aged 25–29 years had significantly higher odds of having neonates with normal birth weight compared to those aged < 20 years (AOR = 12.18, 95% CI: 1.06–139.15). Mothers who often visited green spaces during pregnancy were two times more likely to have a normal birth weight compared to others who ‘rarely’ did (AOR = 2.73, 95%CI [1.14–6.48]). Mothers who indicated green spaces and natural vegetation were ‘somewhat visible’ were less likely to have neonates with normal birth weight (AOR = 0.11, 95%CI [0.01–0.95]) compared to those who were ‘highly visible’.ConclusionsOur study findings suggest that neonatal birth weight is influenced by both maternal socio-demographic and green space characteristics. Child health policy must not only look at maternal socio-demographic or clinical factors, but green space exposure during pregnancy as well. Further studies are recommended to explore relationships using objective measures of green space.Keywords: Green space, Neonatal birth weight, Exposure, Sunyani, Ghana

Background

The influence of environmental factors on maternal and child health outcomes has become a significant area of study in public health, particularly the role of green spaces in improving pregnancy outcomes such as birth weight. Green spaces are defined as areas of land covered by natural vegetation, such as parks, gardens, urban forests, and other types of greenery that serve as critical components of healthy urban environments [1]. These spaces provide opportunities for physical activity, social interaction, and stress relief, all of which are factors linked to improved health outcomes [2]. For expectant mothers, green spaces can offer substantial health benefits by reducing stress, improving air quality, and encouraging physical activity, which may positively influence birth outcomes, particularly birth weight [3, 4].

Birth weight is one of the most crucial indicators of neonatal health and is associated with both immediate and long-term health outcomes for the child [5]. Babies born with low birth weight (LBW), defined as weighing less than 2500 g, are at higher risk for neonatal mortality, developmental delays, and chronic health issues later in life, such as cardiovascular diseases and diabetes [6–8]. Globally, approximately 20 million infants are born annually with low birth weight, accounting for 15–20% of all live births, with the highest prevalence in low- and middle-income countries [5, 9]. This makes birth weight a key area of focus for improving maternal and child health outcomes, particularly in regions with limited healthcare resources and environmental challenges.

In addition to environmental exposures, maternal socio-demographic characteristics play a critical role in shaping pregnancy and birth outcomes, including birth weight. Factors such as maternal age, educational attainment, marital status, employment status, household income, parity, and access to antenatal care have been consistently associated with variations in neonatal birth weight across different populations. Both younger maternal age and advanced maternal age have been linked to increased risks of low birth weight due to biological, social, and obstetric factors [6, 10, 11]. Lower maternal education and socioeconomic status are often associated with inadequate nutrition, limited access to quality antenatal services, and increased psychosocial stress, all of which can adversely affect fetal growth [12, 13]. In low- and middle-income countries, including Ghana, socio-demographic inequalities remain a major contributor to disparities in maternal and neonatal health outcomes, making them an essential consideration in birth weight research [9, 14, 15].

In recent years, there has been increasing recognition of the potential relationship between green space exposure and birth outcomes, particularly in urbanized and rapidly developing regions, where socio-demographic characteristics often influence residential location and access to environmental resources [3, 10]. Green spaces are known to reduce stress levels in expectant mothers through their calming and restorative effects, which are critical during pregnancy when maternal stress can negatively impact fetal development [11, 12]. Chronic stress during pregnancy is associated with preterm birth, low birth weight, and other adverse birth outcomes, as elevated stress hormones like cortisol can impair placental function and fetal growth [13, 14]. In this context, green spaces act as a buffer against environmental stressors, promoting psychological and physiological well-being, which in turn can lead to healthier birth outcomes [3, 15]. Moreover, green spaces provide opportunities for physical activity, which is another critical factor in improving maternal health during pregnancy. Regular physical activity has been linked to better birth outcomes, including higher birth weights and reduced risks of gestational complications such as preeclampsia and gestational diabetes [16]. Pregnant women who engage in physical activities, such as walking in parks or natural settings, experience improved cardiovascular health, reduced stress levels, and better overall physical condition, all of which contribute to a healthier pregnancy and optimal fetal growth [17]. Green spaces, by offering safe, accessible environments for physical activity, play a crucial role in promoting maternal health during pregnancy, especially in urban areas where access to natural environments may be limited [1].

In addition to stress reduction and physical activity, the presence of green spaces in urban environments has been associated with improved air quality, which is a critical factor for maternal and fetal health. Air pollution, especially in rapidly urbanizing areas, has been linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as low birth weight, preterm birth, and increased infant mortality [18]. Trees and vegetation in green spaces help to mitigate air pollution by filtering harmful pollutants, improving the overall air quality in urban environments [19]. Expectant mothers who live in areas with better air quality are less likely to experience pregnancy complications related to respiratory issues or exposure to environmental toxins, which can have a direct impact on birth weight and other neonatal health outcomes [4]. Therefore, the availability of green spaces can be a critical environmental factor in reducing exposure to air pollution and improving pregnancy outcomes, particularly in densely populated urban areas.

While research in high-income countries has established a clear link between green space exposure and improved pregnancy outcomes, including higher birth weights [15, 19], this relationship is not yet fully understood in middle-income countries, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa. In these regions, rapid urbanization, environmental degradation, and socioeconomic inequalities present unique challenges to maternal and child health [20]. For example, Ghana, like many other middle-income countries, has experienced significant urban growth in recent decades, with the expansion of cities often coming at the expense of green spaces [21]. The reduction of green areas in urban centers, combined with rising air pollution levels and inadequate infrastructure, poses significant risks to maternal and child health in these environments [22].

The prevalence of low birth weight in Ghana is reported as 14.2% [21]. Socio-demographic disparities across regions and population groups have been identified as key drivers of this burden [22]. Several studies have investigated birth weight in Ghana and associated factors [23–25].

A study conducted in southern rural Ghana concluded that infant birth weight ≥ 2.5 kg was associated with socioeconomic status, gender of an infant, parity, occupation and maternal age [26]. In another study in the Upper East Region of Ghana, the sex of the neonate, maternal age and marital status were identified as determinants of childbirth weight [27]. Also, a study in Sunyani focused on maternal obstetric and socio-demographic determinants [28]. The study noted that significant predictors of childbirth weight were maternal educational level, residence, hemoglobin level, parity, number of anti-natal clinic (ANC) visits, and gestational age. Another Bono Region of Ghana study identified preterm delivery, maternal education, living alone during pregnancy, and not taking daily required iron supplementation as significant determinants of childbirth weight [29].

The research foci, in the Ghanaian context, have primarily been on socio-demographic determinants [26–28], spatial analysis of factors [25], fetal-maternal characteristics [24], maternal body mass index [30], dietary patterns [31], and antenatal care utilization [32]. However, there remains a paucity of literature examining maternal environmental factors, particularly green space exposure, in relation to birth weight. To the best of our knowledge, no study in Ghana has explored the relationship between maternal self-reported green space characteristics and neonatal birth weight. This study, therefore, seeks to fill this gap by jointly examining maternal socio-demographic characteristics and self-reported exposure to green spaces in relation to birth weight. The findings have the potential to inform public health interventions, urban planning policies, and maternal care programs aimed at improving birth outcomes through integrated social and environmental approaches in Ghana and other middle-income countries.2.1 Study setting.

This study was conducted at the Public Health Department, Clinic 5, of the Bono Regional Teaching Hospital. The hospital is located in the Sunyani Municipality of the Bono Region of Ghana [33]. Bono Regional Teaching Hospital is the main tertiary referral facility in the region and provides comprehensive maternal and neonatal services to residents of Sunyani and surrounding districts [34]. According to routine hospital delivery records, the facility recorded approximately 2,200-2,400 deliveries annually between 2022 and 2023. Of these deliveries, about 13–15% were classified as low birth weight (less than 2.5 kg), while the remaining 85–87% were normal birth weight deliveries (≥ 2.5 kg). These figures are consistent with national estimates of low-birth-weight prevalence in Ghana and highlight the continued public health relevance of birth weight within this setting [35]. The high volume of deliveries, coupled with the coexistence of both low and normal birth weight outcomes, makes the Bono Regional Teaching Hospital an appropriate and representative facility for examining factors associated with neonatal birth weight. Sunyani serves as the capital city of the Bono Region and is located approximately 480 km northwest of Accra, the capital of Ghana. The municipality has experienced rapid population growth, urban expansion, and infrastructural development in recent decades, factors that have implications for residential environments, access to green spaces, and maternal health outcomes [33].

Study design

We employed a facility-based cross-sectional design to investigate the relationship between neonatal birth weight and maternal self-reported socio-demographic and green space characteristics. The unit of analysis was neonates and their respective mothers. Mothers who had single births were eligible for the study. However, mothers with twin or multiple births were excluded from the study because multiple gestations are independently associated with lower birth weight due to shared intrauterine environments and higher risks of preterm delivery, which could confound the assessment of relationships between maternal socio-demographic characteristics, green space exposure, and neonatal birth weight. We applied a quantitative approach to explore the relationships between the variables of interest.

Sampling

For this study, we employed accidental sampling to enlist 383 mothers who attended the postnatal clinic. This sample size accounted for approximately 64% of the clinic’s average monthly attendees (approximately 600 mothers). This sampling technique aligns with previous maternal health studies that employed similar methodologies [36–41]. Each consenting mother participated in a single structured interview, conducted within the hospital setting. This approach enabled an exploration of the complex and potentially localized factors influencing neonatal birth weight [41].

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was determined using the single population proportion formula. An assumed prevalence of low birth weight of 14.2% in Ghana was used, based on findings from the Ghana Demographic and Health Survey. A 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error were applied. To account for potential non-response and incomplete data, a 10% contingency was added to the calculated sample size. Based on these assumptions, a minimum required sample size was estimated, and a total of 383 mothers were recruited for the study.

Questionnaire design

We used an interviewer-administered questionnaire to get information from mothers. The prior research by [42–48] served as the basis for the questionnaire design. Socio-demographic characteristics, neonatal birth weight statistics, and maternal green space features were its three sections.

Variables

We designated neonatal birth weight, measured in grams at delivery and recorded in the hospital birth records, as the dependent variable. Birth weight was obtained from routine delivery records and reflected the neonate’s weight measured immediately after birth. It was coded as ‘0 = Low’ and ‘1 = Normal’. A neonate was classified as having a normal birth weight if the recorded birth weight was 2,500 g or higher, and low birth weight if the recorded birth weight was less than 2,500 g, in accordance with World Health Organization criteria. In this study, green spaces were operationally defined as areas covered with vegetation, such as parks, gardens, forests, natural reserves, botanical gardens, and open spaces for recreational activities.

Seventeen independent variables were examined in this study, comprising eleven maternal socio-demographic characteristics and six self-reported green space characteristics. The socio-demographic variables included maternal age, household size, marital status, educational level, employment status, ethnicity, monthly household income, district of residence, sex of the neonate, age of the neonate, and parity. Maternal age was categorised into five groups consistent with Table 1 and coded as: 1 = < 20 years, 2 = 20–24 years, 3 = 25–29 years, 4 = 30–34 years, and 5 = ≥ 35 years. Household size was categorised and coded as 1 = ≤ 3 persons and 2 = ≥ 4 persons. Marital status was coded as 1 = married and 2 = not married. Educational level was categorised and coded as 1 = no formal education, 2 = primary education, 3 = secondary education, and 4 = tertiary education. Employment status was coded as 0 = unemployed and 1 = employed. Maternal ethnicity was categorised and coded as 1 = Akan, 2 = Ewe, 3 = Ga-Adangbe, 4 = Hausa, and 5 = Mole-Dagbani. Monthly household income was dichotomised and coded as 1 = ≤ GHC 1600 and 2 = > GHC 1600, while district of residence was coded as 1 = Municipal and 2 = West. Parity was recorded as a continuous variable.

Socio-demographic and green space characteristics

All maternal green space characteristics were self-reported and categorical. Frequency of visits to green spaces during pregnancy was coded as 1 = daily, 2 = weekly, 3 = monthly, 4 = rarely, and 5 = never. The perceived amount of greenery or vegetation in the neighbourhood was coded as 1 = abundant, 2 = moderate, 3 = scarce, and 4 = none. Visibility of green spaces or natural vegetation from the residence was coded as 1 = highly visible, 2 = somewhat visible, 3 = not very visible, and 4 = not visible at all. Frequency of time spent in areas with natural vegetation was coded as 1 = daily, 2 = several times a week, 3 = once a week, 4 = rarely, and 5 = never. Perceived quality of green spaces close to the home was coded as 1 = excellent, 2 = good, 3 = fair, and 4 = poor. Use of green spaces for leisure activities or physical exercise was coded as 0 = no and 1 = yes.

Data collection

Four nurses were recruited to assist with the data collection. They were trained in questionnaire administration and data entry. We deployed the questionnaire into KoboToolbox, a free web tool that facilitates data collection using Android mobile phones [49]. Thereafter, the questionnaire was pre-tested using 20 mothers who came to the clinic. Following that, corrections were made, and an updated questionnaire was deployed for data collection. Data collection took place from June to August 2024. Birth weight was a continuous variable and it was assessed directly from the child’s health records book provided by the mother. Entries into the platform were examined daily and any mistakes or omissions were looked into and fixed. When a respondent was unable to understand the English version of the questionnaire, it was translated into Twi, a regional dialect.

Data analysis

Stata 18 (College Station, Texas 77845 USA) was used for the data analysis. Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with normal birth weight. For the logistic regression analyses, five variables were dichotomised as follows: highest educational level completed (0 = no formal or primary education; 1 = secondary or tertiary education); household size (0 = ≤ 3 persons; 1 = ≥ 4 persons); frequency of green space visits during pregnancy (0 = rarely; 1 = often); perceived amount of greenery or vegetation in the neighbourhood (0 = scarce; 1 = moderate; 2 = abundant); and frequency of time spent in areas with natural vegetation (0 = rarely; 1 = often). Parity was included as a control variable in the regression models. Odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported as measures of association. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The University of Energy and Natural Resources Committee for Human Research and Ethics granted ethical permission for this study (CHRE/AP/288/024). The Bono Regional Teaching Hospital’s research directorate granted further approval. Before administering the questionnaire, each participant gave their informed consent. Participants were made aware that they could withdraw from the study at any time and that their answers would only be utilized for scholarly research. Additionally, the study did not include any personally identifiable information.

Methods

Results

Socio-demographic and green space characteristics of respondents

Table 1 presents the socio-demographic and green space characteristics of the respondents. More than two-thirds of the mothers were aged 30 years or older (73.5%), and 66.8% resided in the Sunyani Municipality during pregnancy. Over half of the respondents were married (55.2%), and the majority had completed secondary or tertiary education (84.8%). Most mothers were employed (84.3%), belonged to the Akan ethnic group (70.5%), and reported a monthly household income of GHC 1600 or less (75.2%).

Regarding neonatal characteristics, 62.3% of the neonates were aged less than 12 months, and 53.8% were female. The majority of neonates had normal birth weight (85.4%), while 14.6% (n = 56) were classified as having low birth weight. The mean birth weight was 2.8 kg (SD = 0.4 kg). When stratified by sex, the mean birth weight was 2.9 kg for male neonates and 2.7 kg for female neonates.

With respect to self-reported green space characteristics, 53.0% of mothers reported visiting green spaces weekly during pregnancy, while 19.4% reported visiting them rarely and 0.8% reported never visiting. More than half of respondents described the amount of greenery or vegetation in their neighbourhood as scarce (56.1%), while 34.7% reported moderate greenery. Additionally, 51.1% indicated that green spaces or natural vegetation were not very visible from their residences, and 42.3% reported that they rarely spent time in areas with natural vegetation. Only 5.5% rated the quality of green spaces close to their homes as excellent, whereas 56.8% reported that they did not utilise green spaces for leisure activities or physical exercise.

Green space characteristics of respondents

Concerning self-reported green space characteristics, more than half of mothers visited green spaces weekly during pregnancy (53.0%) and also described the amount of greenery in their neighborhoods as scarce (56.1%). Similarly, more than half of respondents indicated that green spaces and natural vegetation were not very visible from their residences (51.1%). More than a third rarely spent time in areas with natural vegetation (42.3%). Whereas few rated the quality of green spaces close to their home as excellent, more than half indicated they did not utilize green spaces for leisure activities or physical exercises (56.8%) (Table 2).

Socio-demographic characteristics and neonatal birth weight

Descriptively, a higher proportion of neonates with normal birth weight were born to mothers aged 30–34 years (38.4%), those with tertiary education (41.0%), mothers who were employed (72.1%), and those reporting a monthly household income of GHC 1600 or less (64.8%). In addition, neonates with normal birth weight were more frequently born to mothers of Akan ethnicity (60.8%), those residing in the Sunyani Municipality (56.4%), and those who were married (48.3%).

With respect to green space characteristics, among mothers whose neonates had normal birth weight, 30.0% described the amount of greenery or vegetation in their neighbourhood as moderate, while 43.6% reported that green spaces or natural vegetation were not very visible from their residence. Furthermore, 36.6% of these mothers reported rarely spending time in areas with natural vegetation, and 45.9% indicated that they utilised green spaces for leisure or physical activity. Most mothers of neonates with normal birth weight rated the quality of green spaces close to their homes as fair (41.8%) (Table 4).

After adjusting for potential confounders, the multivariate logistic regression model remained statistically significant (χ² = 24.53, p = 0.054) and explained 19.4% of the variance in neonatal birth weight (Nagelkerke R² = 0.194). Maternal age remained statistically significant, with mothers aged 25–29 years being approximately twelve times more likely to have neonates with normal birth weight compared to those aged less than 20 years (AOR = 12.18, 95% CI: 1.06–139.15).

With respect to green space characteristics, two variables were significantly associated with normal birth weight. Mothers who often visited green spaces during pregnancy were more likely to have neonates with normal birth weight compared to those who rarely visited green spaces (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.14–6.48). In contrast, mothers who reported that green spaces or natural vegetation were somewhat visible from their residence were less likely to have neonates with normal birth weight compared to those who reported high visibility (AOR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.01–0.95).

Other socio-demographic variables, including sex of the neonate, educational level, employment status, household income, household size, ethnicity, district of residence, and marital status, were not statistically significant in the adjusted model. Similarly, the amount of neighbourhood greenery, time spent in areas with natural vegetation, perceived quality of green spaces, and use of green spaces for leisure or physical activity were not significantly associated with neonatal birth weight.

Discussions

We explored maternal self-reported socio-demographic and green space characteristics and their relationship with neonatal birth weight. Approximately 15% of neonates in our study had low birth weight. This prevalence was lower than the 26% reported in Northern Ghana [30] but higher than the 12.9% reported in the Volta Region [50]. These regional disparities may reflect differences in maternal health interventions, socio-economic factors, and environmental conditions across Ghana. Maternal age emerged as a significant factor in the univariate analysis. Mothers aged 25–29 years were significantly more likely to have neonates with normal birth weight compared to younger mothers. This finding aligns with the results of Belay et al. (2022) in Ethiopia, who observed higher odds of normal birth weight among mothers aged 20–29 years [51]. Similarly, Mondal (2000) reported that mothers aged below 19 years had a higher likelihood of delivering low birth weight neonates in Nepal [52]. The physiological readiness and cumulative reproductive experience of mothers in their late twenties likely contribute to these outcomes. Conversely, a study by Atuahene et al. (2015) in Accra, found no significant association between maternal age and birth weight, suggesting that the impact of maternal age may vary depending on socio-economic and health system contexts [53]. Mothers who frequently visited green spaces during pregnancy were almost three times more likely to have neonates with normal birth weight. This finding is consistent with Nieuwenhuijsen et al. (2019), who demonstrated in a multicenter study across 16 European cities that exposure to green spaces positively influenced birth weight [54]. The stress-reducing benefits of green spaces, mediated by lowered cortisol levels [15], could explain these results. Similarly, a study by Torres Toda et al. 2020 highlighted the beneficial impact of visual access to green spaces on neonatal health, emphasizing the restorative potential of natural environments [55].

Our findings also revealed that the visibility of green spaces significantly affected birth outcomes. Mothers who reported green spaces as “somewhat visible” had lower odds of delivering neonates with normal birth weight compared to those who reported “highly visible” green spaces. This observation parallels the findings by [3], who underscored the importance of high-quality and accessible green spaces in fostering maternal health during pregnancy. Visibility of green spaces or natural vegetation may enhance psychological comfort, encourage physical activity, and indirectly improve maternal health by mitigating environmental stressors such as air pollution [4].

Interestingly, factors such as the perceived quality of green spaces and their use for leisure or exercise were not significant in our analysis. This divergence from other studies, such as [56], could stem from contextual differences in how urban green spaces are utilized or the socio-cultural preferences of the study population. Our study adds to the growing body of literature highlighting the interplay between environmental factors and maternal health. However, it is essential to acknowledge that our findings did not corroborate those of [57, 58] who identified other socio-demographic factors, such as maternal education and income, as significant predictors of birth weight. These discrepancies underscore the need for further research to unravel the nuanced relationships between green spaces, socio-demographics, and neonatal health outcomes.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study have important clinical, public health, and policy implications for maternal and neonatal health in urban Ghana and similar low- and middle-income settings. The observed association between maternal age and neonatal birth weight reinforces the need for age-sensitive antenatal care strategies, particularly for younger mothers who may be biologically and socio-economically more vulnerable. Antenatal care providers should prioritise targeted counselling, nutritional support, and closer monitoring for mothers in higher-risk age categories to mitigate adverse birth outcomes.

Importantly, the study highlights maternal green space exposure during pregnancy as a potentially modifiable environmental factor associated with neonatal birth weight. The finding that frequent visits to green spaces were associated with higher odds of normal birth weight suggests that environmental and lifestyle interventions could complement conventional clinical care. Maternal health services could integrate guidance on safe and accessible outdoor activities, such as walking in neighbourhood green spaces, into routine antenatal education. Such interventions may be particularly valuable in urban settings where psychosocial stress and environmental exposures are common.

Furthermore, the significant role of visibility of green spaces underscores the importance of environmental design in maternal health. Visual access to green spaces from residential environments may contribute to stress reduction and improved mental well-being during pregnancy, even in the absence of active use. This finding supports the integration of green infrastructure into urban planning and housing policies as part of broader maternal and child health strategies. Collaboration between health professionals, urban planners, and local governments is therefore essential to promote environments that support healthy pregnancies.

Our research has some policy and research implications. The findings from the current study suggest that socio-demographic factors may play a role in influencing neonatal birth weight. Similarly, environmental factors such as green space exposure may play a role in neonatal birth weight. Given that policies such as the Ghana National Newborn Health Strategy and Action Plan 2019–2023 [59], Child Health Standards and Strategy 2017–2025 [59], including the Ghana Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health and Nutrition Action Plan 2020–2025 [60] do not explicitly address environmental factors such as green spaces, there is a need for further research to investigate these relationships.

Strengths of the study

This study has several notable strengths that enhance its contribution to the literature. First, it is among the few studies in Ghana to empirically examine the relationship between maternal green space exposure and neonatal birth weight, addressing a critical evidence gap in sub-Saharan Africa. By integrating environmental variables with socio-demographic factors, the study provides a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of neonatal birth weight within an urban Ghanaian context.

Second, the use of hospital-recorded birth weight data strengthens the validity and reliability of the outcome measure, reducing the likelihood of reporting or recall bias. Birth weight was obtained from routine delivery records and classified according to World Health Organization criteria, ensuring consistency with international standards.

Third, the study employed a relatively large sample size and included multiple maternal and environmental variables, allowing for both univariate and multivariate analyses. The analytical approach enabled adjustment for potential confounders, thereby improving the robustness of the observed associations.

Finally, data collection was conducted using trained research assistants and a structured questionnaire, with pre-testing to ensure clarity and consistency. This methodological rigor enhances the internal validity of the study and supports the credibility of the findings.

Limitations of the study

This research has some limitations. First, the reliance on self-reported data for maternal exposure to green spaces introduces the potential for recall bias, as participants may not accurately remember or may overestimate their exposure to green environments. While self-reported measures are useful for capturing subjective perceptions, they may not always align with objective assessments of green space accessibility or usage. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish causality between green space exposure and birth outcomes. Although associations were identified, it is not possible to determine whether green space exposure directly causes improved birth weights or if other unmeasured factors, such as overall maternal health or socioeconomic status, may also play a role. Third, the study’s sampling method, an accidental sampling approach targeting mothers attending post-natal clinics, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study did not control for certain environmental or lifestyle factors, such as air pollution, noise levels, or dietary habits, which could also influence birth outcomes. While the focus on green space exposure is critical, the lack of control for these confounding variables may affect the robustness of the conclusions drawn. Therefore, further research in diverse settings is needed to validate the generalizability of the relationship between green space exposure and birth outcomes in different contexts. Regardless of these limitations, this study represents a first attempt at understanding maternal green space characteristics and their relationships with neonatal health. The findings from this study contribute to the growing literature on green space exposure and birth outcomes.

Conclusions

In this study, we found evidence suggesting that maternal self-reported socio-demographic and green space characteristics influence neonatal birth weight. The findings highlight the importance of maternal exposure to green spaces in relation to pregnancy outcomes, particularly birth weight. An association was observed between self-reported exposure to green spaces and normal birth weight, indicating that maternal interaction with natural environments may be linked to healthier pregnancies. Notably, maternal age, frequency of visits to green spaces during pregnancy, and the visibility of green spaces and natural vegetation from mothers’ residences were significantly associated with neonatal birth weight. Overall, the study demonstrates that environmental factors, especially green space exposure, alongside socio-demographic characteristics, play a role in shaping maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations are proposed. Maternal health programmes should consider incorporating guidance on safe and regular exposure to green spaces during pregnancy as part of antenatal care education. Promoting simple activities such as walking in accessible green spaces may support maternal well-being and improve birth outcomes.

Urban planning and public health stakeholders should prioritise the preservation and integration of green spaces within residential environments to enhance visibility and accessibility, particularly in rapidly urbanising settings. Improving green infrastructure may contribute to healthier living environments for pregnant women.

Future research should employ longitudinal study designs and objective measures of green space exposure to establish causal relationships and better understand the pathways linking environmental factors and neonatal birth outcomes. Expanding such studies to include rural and peri-urban settings would also improve generalisability.

Finally, further research is needed to explore the long-term health benefits of green space exposure for both mothers and children in low- and middle-income countries, focusing on identifying barriers to accessing green spaces and developing solutions tailored to the local context.
