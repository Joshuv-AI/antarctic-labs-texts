Patient journey and disparities in the diagnosis and treatment of patients with hidradenitis suppurativa
Authors: Raj Chovatiya, Julie Gayle, Robert Low, Tae Oh, Isabel Gomez, Ning Rosenthal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe compared the patient journey of adult and pediatric patients with hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) and suspected HS and of patients with HS stratified by race, ethnicity, and social determinants of health. Data were from the United States Premier Healthcare Database (including hospital discharge and billing data from inpatient and outpatient encounters) and its linked medical and pharmacy claims database. After diagnosis, adult patients (n = 3065) with HS initiated biologics more quickly and were less likely to have all-cause hospitalizations or all-cause emergency department visits than adult patients with suspected HS (n = 27,280). Adult patients with HS also reported more disease-related outpatient visits (mean ± SD: 3.7 ± 4.9 vs 2.9 ± 4.7) and lower overall costs ($22,128 ± $61,671 vs $36,359 ± $82,762) after diagnosis. Significant differences were found in the patient journey across ethnic groups (adult patients) and social vulnerability index categories (adult and pediatric patients). Differences in the patient journey were evident, particularly between adult patients with HS and adult patients with suspected HS. Although a confirmed diagnosis of HS is beneficial, disparities were prevalent. Increased awareness of these differences may minimize diagnostic delays, facilitate disease management, and reduce costs.Keywords: Healthcare resource utilization, Hidradenitis suppurativa, Patient journey, Social determinants of health

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition, characterized by painful, recurrent lesions and abscesses in the skin, which can substantially impact QoL. In the United States, the estimated prevalence of HS is 0.1%. Patients can experience prolonged delays in diagnosis, such as approximately ≤10 years from symptom onset, and are frequently misdiagnosed, even when they present with symptoms of HS (eg, abscess, cyst, boil, furuncle, and/or folliculitis). Major barriers to timely diagnosis include low disease awareness among both healthcare providers and patients, misdiagnoses (often with symptoms of HS), and disparities in access to appropriate care. Along with racial and ethnic disparities, social determinants of health (SDoH), including socioeconomic status (SES) and insurance payer type, can influence the diagnosis and treatment journey (Garg et al, 2023, 2020; Ingram, 2020; Jaleel et al, 2024; Kimball et al, 2024; Kokolakis et al, 2020; Nguyen et al, 2021; Patel et al, 2022; Polaskey and Chovatiya, 2024; Serrano et al, 2022; Snyder et al, 2023; Wang et al, 2022).

Delays in diagnosis are associated with greater disease severity and disease burden. High healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) and costs associated with the management of HS may additionally contribute to the substantial economic burden for both adult and pediatric patients. In turn, the debilitating symptoms of HS can limit a patient’s ability to work or engage in daily activities, thereby exacerbating this financial burden (Garg et al, 2023; Kimball et al, 2024; Kokolakis et al, 2020; Seivright et al, 2021; Snyder et al, 2023).

Ultimately, a better understanding of these factors in the diagnosis and treatment journey may improve patient care and decrease disease burden. To understand this real-world landscape of HS in the United States, we included patients who were formally diagnosed with HS and patients with suspected but undiagnosed HS, using data from the Premier Healthcare Database (PHD) and its linked medical and pharmacy claims database. The objective of the study was twofold: to compare the demographics, clinical characteristics, diagnosis, treatment journey, HCRU, and costs between adult and pediatric patients with HS and patients with suspected HS (ie, patients with symptoms of HS, including abscess, cyst, boil, furuncle, and/or folliculitis) and to characterize the diagnosis and treatment journey of adult and pediatric patients with HS across race, ethnicity, and SDoH (ie, social vulnerability index [SVI], used as a proxy of SES, and payer type).

Results

Comparisons between patients with HS and those with suspected HS

Overview

In total, 3411 patients with HS (adult patients: n = 3065; pediatric patients: n = 346) and 28,799 patients with suspected HS (adult patients: n = 27,280; pediatric patients: n = 1519) who met the selection criteria were identified in the PHD and its linked closed health insurance claims data (Figure 1). The PHD included hospital discharge and billing data on inpatient and outpatient encounters from >1300 hospitals across all 9 United States census divisions, whereas the linked claims database included medical and pharmacy claims from both acute and non-acute care settings. Study period consisted of the baseline period (24 months before the index visit [earliest visit with HS or HS symptom]), index visit, and follow-up period (12-month period after the index visit) (Figure 2).

Patient and hospital characteristics

The age (mean ± SD) of adult patients with HS was significantly lower than that of adult patients with suspected HS (38.1 ± 13.0 vs 48.7 ± 16.6 years); it did not differ between pediatric patients with HS and pediatric patients with suspected HS (both: 15.1 ± 1.6 years). In both the adult and pediatric cohorts, patients with HS were significantly more likely to be female (adult patients: 76.6 vs 51.3%; pediatric patients: 78.9 vs 59.3%) and Black (adult patients: 38.9 vs 13.9%; pediatric patients: 33.2 vs 16.5%) than patients with suspected HS. However, across the cohorts, most of the patients were White. The distributions of payer type and SVI categories were significantly different between HS and suspected HS patient cohorts among adult patients, but neither was significantly different among the pediatric patient cohorts. In addition, adult patients with HS were significantly less likely to be homeless (0.5 vs 1.8%) than adult patients with suspected HS, which was not true for the pediatric patient cohorts. For both the adult and pediatric cohorts, patients with HS were significantly more likely to be electively admitted to a hospital (adult patients: 40.8 vs 19.2%; pediatric patients: 48.3 vs 24.9%) (Table 1).

Diagnosis and treatment journey

Antibiotic usage did not significantly differ among the adult patient cohorts, yet a significantly higher percentage of pediatric patients with HS used antibiotics than pediatric patients with suspected HS (66.8 vs 53.1%). Although biologic usage was limited compared with antibiotic usage throughout the study period, a significantly higher percentage of patients with HS used biologics than patients with suspected HS in both the adult (12.8 vs 1.3%) and pediatric (9.0 vs 0.6%) patient populations (Figure 3).

Patients with HS and patients with suspected HS also had differences in the timing and type of treatment. Adult patients with HS had significantly less time from diagnosis to initiation of first biologic than adult patients with suspected HS (109.8 ± 103.4 vs 165.5 ± 110.5 days). Adult patients with HS had significantly more time on antibiotic treatment from diagnosis to first dermatology visit (33.4 ± 37.7 vs 19.3 ± 23.2 days) and from diagnosis to initiation of first biologic (44.2 ± 45.1 vs 23.1 ± 17.2 days) than adult patients with suspected HS. However, these findings were not significantly different among pediatric patients (Figure 4). Finally, adult patients with HS had significantly more surgical procedures for disease-related treatment before first dermatology visit than adult patients with suspected HS (3.0 ± 5.0 vs 1.5 ± 0.9 surgical procedures). This was not significantly different across the pediatric patient cohorts (Figure 5).

HCRU

During the baseline period, adult patients with HS had significantly more disease-related emergency department (ED) visits than adult patients with suspected HS (2.2 ± 2.1 vs 1.8 ± 1.6 visits). There was no significant difference across the 2 pediatric patient cohorts. For both the adult and pediatric patient cohorts, number of disease-related inpatient visits did not significantly differ. However, number of disease-related outpatient visits was significantly higher for patients with HS than for patients with suspected HS (adult patients: 4.3 ± 5.2 vs 2.7 ± 7.5 visits; pediatric patients: 3.8 ± 3.8 vs 2.0 ± 1.5 visits) (Figure 6).

During the follow-up period, number of disease-related ED visits and inpatient visits did not significantly differ for either the adult or pediatric patient cohorts. Number of disease-related outpatient visits remained significantly higher for adult patients with HS than for adult patients with suspected HS (mean: 3.7 ± 4.9 vs 2.9 ± 4.7 visits), yet this number did not remain significantly different between the pediatric patient cohorts (Figure 6). In both the adult and pediatric patient cohorts, a significantly lower proportion of patients with HS had 30-day risks (after the index visit) of all-cause hospitalization (adult patients: 0.8 vs 3.7%; pediatric patients: 0.3 vs 1.7%) and of all-cause ED (adult patients: 2.6 vs 11.7%; pediatric patients: 2.9 vs 18.4%) visits than patients with suspected HS (Figure 7).

Costs

During baseline, adult patients with HS had significantly lower overall costs ($23,946 ± $51,567 vs $39,442 ± $111,505) and disease-related costs ($5,968 ± $21,562 vs $8,061 ± $29,865) than adult patients with suspected HS. Overall and disease-related costs were, in contrast, not significantly different across the pediatric patient cohorts (Figure 8).

At index, disease-related costs were not significantly different across the adult and pediatric patient cohorts among patients with HS and patients with suspected HS (Figure 8).

During follow-up, adult patients with HS again had significantly lower overall costs than adult patients with suspected HS ($22,128 ± $61,671 vs $36,359 ± $82,762). However, disease-related costs were not significantly different across the adult patient cohorts. As during the baseline period, overall costs were not significantly different in the pediatric patient cohorts, but disease-related costs became significantly higher for pediatric patients with HS than for pediatric patients with suspected HS ($7,473 ± $41,809 vs $3,377 ± $11,580) (Figure 8).

Disparities in HS diagnosis and treatment journey

Adult patients

Disparities were observed when adult patients with HS (n = 3065) were stratified by race, ethnicity, and SDoH (payer type and SVI). Regarding race, ethnicity, payer type, and SVI, Black adult patients, Hispanic adult patients, adult patients with Medicare insurance, and adult patients with social vulnerability of 0.75 < SVI ≤ 1 had the longest times from diagnosis of HS to initiation of first biologic. These differences (means) from the overall population of adult patients with HS were +27.5, +53.4, +17.0, and +16.8 days, respectively. Other comparisons are presented in Figure 9.

Discussion

We found differences in the demographics, clinical characteristics, diagnosis, treatment journey, HCRU, and costs between adult and pediatric patients with HS and patients with suspected HS, which highlight the importance of early and appropriate diagnosis. Characteristics at the index visit, including that adult patients with HS were significantly younger, less likely to be homeless, more likely to have elective hospital visits, and had a lower baseline and index comorbidity burden than adult patients with suspected HS, may have helped contribute to a timely diagnosis of HS.

More adult patients with HS were treated with biologics, and after diagnosis, they more quickly initiated biologics and were treated with antibiotics for a longer period before first dermatology visit and before first biologic initiation, all in comparison with adult patients with suspected HS. These significant results evince that a diagnosis of HS may expedite treatment. Since HS is a progressive disease, a multimodal approach with biologics and antibiotics over extended periods, as observed in this study, may be important for holistic disease management. Adult patients with HS also had more surgical procedures for disease-related treatment before their first dermatologist visit, further supporting the comprehensive treatment approach needed for HS. Timely, multimodal treatment is thus prompted by a confirmed diagnosis of HS, and therefore, having a diagnosis of HS is beneficial for these patients. Adult patients with suspected HS who lacked a confirmed diagnosis of HS may have been receiving targeted treatment for individual symptoms of HS as opposed to comprehensive treatment for the disease itself (Ocker et al, 2022; Ring et al, 2022; Snyder et al, 2023).

During both baseline and follow-up, adult patients with HS had more disease-related outpatient visits than adult patients with suspected HS. Thirty-day risks of all-cause hospitalization and of all-cause ED visits after the index visit were also lower for adult patients with HS. Altogether, these significant results indicate that a diagnosis of HS may facilitate more routine follow-up and tailored disease management, which may in turn help decrease the risks of hospitalization and of ED visits. As mentioned earlier, the importance of a diagnosis is shown. Adult patients with HS also had lower disease-related costs during baseline, along with lower overall costs during both baseline and follow-up, thereby highlighting other benefits of a confirmed diagnosis for these patients. As with other studies, timely diagnosis of HS may lead to more prompt and focused disease management in lower-cost healthcare settings, which could potentially reduce disease progression and complications requiring inpatient care. This could ultimately help mitigate the high financial burden of HS (Edigin et al, 2022; Kirby et al, 2014; Kokolakis et al, 2020; Lee et al, 2017; Marvel et al, 2019; Snyder et al, 2023).

It is important to note that more differences in the patient journey were significant within the adult patient populations than within the pediatric patient populations and that not all of the findings indicated advantages for adult patients with HS over adult patients with suspected HS. For example, adult patients with HS had significantly more disease-related ED visits during baseline than adult patients with suspected HS, which was in line with previously reported high ED usage among patients with HS. Conversely, at the index visit, adult patients with HS were significantly less likely to be emergently admitted. Prior to diagnosis, patients may have received targeted treatment for acute flares instead of more comprehensive treatment for HS as a chronic disease. Likewise, although patients with HS received multimodal treatment, the use of biologics and surgical procedures for disease-related treatment could indicate that patients with a confirmed diagnosis of HS simply had different treatment needs that affected HCRU (Kirby et al, 2014; Ocker et al, 2022; Ring et al, 2022).

Although these results have shown benefits of a confirmed diagnosis of HS for these patient populations, differences in the diagnosis and treatment journey among patients with HS were observed across race, ethnicity, and SDoH, revealing disparities in access to timely and appropriate care. Significant differences were found for time from diagnosis of HS to initiation of first biologic across ethnic groups (adult patients with HS) and SVI categories (pediatric patients with HS), and for time on antibiotic treatment from diagnosis of HS to first dermatology visit across SVI categories (adult patients with HS). These findings agree with past research identifying racial and ethnic disparities in diagnosis and treatment. Prior studies have also reported an association between HS and low SES; patients with low SES may face difficulties in accessing care, and the negative impact of HS on a patient’s professional life may exacerbate these issues. Subanalyses by race, ethnicity, and SDoH could elucidate other correlations about diagnosis or treatment, and these should be investigated further. Altogether, these disparities should be addressed to ensure early diagnosis and equitable access to treatment across all patient populations (Deckers et al, 2016; Garg et al, 2020; Jaleel et al, 2024; Patel et al, 2022; Polaskey and Chovatiya, 2024; Serrano et al, 2022; Wertenteil et al, 2018).

The present study has potential limitations. Although folliculitis is a common misdiagnosis for HS and was characterized as suspected HS, it may have confounded the analysis of the patient cohorts with suspected HS. Folliculitis has been associated with comorbidities such as diabetes and liver disease, and adult patients with suspected HS had significantly higher prevalences of most comorbidities, including these 2 (except for non-chronic diabetes). These conditions, in turn, may increase susceptibility to recurrent skin infections, which may not indicate that these patients indeed had undiagnosed HS. Nonetheless, folliculitis as an HS-proxy broadened the definition of “suspected HS” so that more patients could be included, as aligned with previous research, and was balanced by other conditions categorized as proxies, such as abscesses and boils (Choudhury et al, 2018; Toschi et al, 2025; Wang et al, 2022; Winters and Mitchell, 2023). To ultimately improve on our study design, future studies could remove folliculitis from the definition to assess its confounding impact, including on the frequencies of all-cause hospitalizations and of all-cause ED visits, as these were higher among patients with suspected HS.

Furthermore, patients were only eligible for inclusion if they received care in a PHD hospital, which may not be entirely generalizable to the larger patient population. However, this study assessed inpatient and outpatient data; inpatient care is a critical component of management, for disease management in inpatient settings is more common for patients with HS than for patients with other dermatologic conditions. Moreover, the PHD has data for 1 in every 4 inpatient discharges in the United States. Another limitation is that patients from our study represented only a subset in the PHD. In addition, misclassification through Common Procedural Terminology and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnosis codes is possible; nonetheless, previous studies have used International Classification of Diseases diagnosis codes to classify diagnoses of HS. Disease severity, which may have provided further predictive context to diagnosis and treatment patterns, was not available in the PHD and was not assessed. This may have also confounded the results, as higher disease severity is associated with more intensive treatment. Given that other research has identified approximately ≤10-year delays in the diagnosis of HS, our study timeframe may not have been sufficient to assess the full patient journey. Despite this timeframe limit due to data availability, all patients with HS were diagnosed within about 2 years (range from HS symptom onset to diagnosis in adult patients: 1–730 days; pediatric patients: 1–634 days). Finally, significance was not measured for the analysis of differences by race, ethnicity, payer type, and SVI from the overall population of patients with HS (Garg et al, 2023, 2020, 2017, 2016; Kirby et al, 2014; Kokolakis et al, 2020; Marvel et al, 2019; Maskan Bermudez et al, 2025; Snyder et al, 2023; Wang et al, 2022).

This retrospective, observational, United States-based cohort study comprehensively showed that demographics, clinical characteristics, diagnosis, and treatment journey, along with HCRU and costs, vary between patients with HS and patients with suspected HS. Our findings evince that a formal diagnosis of HS may facilitate multimodal disease management, reduce complications of HS, and lower costs. Nevertheless, even with a confirmed diagnosis of HS, racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic disparities existed in the diagnosis and treatment journey, which should be addressed. Some strategies that healthcare providers may undertake are developing improved and standardized screening guidelines for HS and advocating for financial aid to help reduce the costs of treatments and transportation to appointments, along with transparent, inclusive policymaking and other public health measures that empower all patients (Marzouk et al, 2024; Polaskey and Chovatiya, 2024). Ultimately, understanding these differences may assist healthcare providers in supporting timely diagnosis and holistic treatment and in mitigating the multidimensional burden of disease management among patients with HS across demographic groups.

Materials and Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study used data from the PHD and its linked medical and pharmacy claims database. The PHD included hospital discharge and billing data from >1300 hospitals, >162 million unique inpatient encounters, and >1.3 billion outpatient encounters in 45 states and the District of Columbia. The linked medical and pharmacy claims database provided a comprehensive picture of the patient journey, as it included supplemental data for healthcare services occurring in non-acute care settings.

The study population included adult (aged ≥18 years) and pediatric (aged 12–17 years) patients who had, within a 12-month period between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2021, ≥1 inpatient or outpatient encounters at a PHD hospital with a principal or secondary discharge diagnosis of HS (patients with HS), or ≥2 inpatient or outpatient encounters at a PHD hospital with symptoms known to be common misdiagnoses for HS (ie, abscess, cyst, boil, furuncle, and/or folliculitis; patients with suspected HS). This aligned with a recent study that included patients diagnosed with an HS-proxy (folliculitis, cellulitis, abscess, carbuncle, or furuncle, which were all common HS misdiagnoses) (Wang et al, 2022). Diagnoses were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes. Index visit was defined as the first inpatient or outpatient visit between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2021 with a principal or secondary discharge diagnosis of HS (patients with HS), or of an HS symptom, but not HS (patients with suspected HS). Data were collected for a 24-month baseline period before the index visit and a 12-month follow-up period after the index visit. Patients with a diagnosis of HS during the baseline period without linked claims data or continuous data during the baseline, index, and follow-up periods were excluded. Study period included the baseline period, index visit, and follow-up period (Figure 1, Figure 2).

Study variables and outcomes

Patient demographics were assessed at index. Charlson Comorbidity Index, Elixhauser comorbidities, and Charlson comorbidities were assessed using primary or secondary International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnosis and procedure codes during the baseline period and index visit. Diagnosis and treatment variables examining the timing of events in the patient journey (ie, number of days between diagnosis of HS or suspected HS and initiation of first biologic) were assessed at baseline and follow-up. Time from diagnosis of HS or suspected HS to initiation of first biologic was calculated among patients with biologic usage. Biologics included adalimumab, anakinra, etanercept, guselkumab, infliximab, secukinumab, and ustekinumab. Disease-related treatment was defined as treatment for HS or suspected HS; surgical procedures, per the Common Procedural Terminology and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes in Table 5, were limited to deroofing, incision and drainage, excision, and surgery for HS (AAPC; ICD10Data.com). Among patients with HS, diagnosis and treatment variables were also stratified by race (self-reported at the index visit and categorized as White, Black, Asian, or other/unknown), ethnicity (self-reported at the index visit and categorized as Hispanic, non-Hispanic, or unknown), and SDoH (payer type and SVI) to evaluate differences in the patient journey. SVI, used as a proxy of SES, was calculated based on patient residence county on a 0–1 scale, with higher values indicating greater social vulnerability. The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention developed the SVI based on SES, household composition and disability, minority status and language, and housing type and transportation; we categorized 2018 SVI data from the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention as 0 ≤ SVI ≤ 0.25, 0.25 < SVI ≤ 0.5, 0.5 < SVI ≤ 0.75, and 0.75 < SVI ≤ 1 in our database (Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry, 2024; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were not stratified by sex (recorded at the index visit and categorized as male, female, or unknown), race, ethnicity, payer type, or SVI.

HCRU was evaluated in terms of the number of disease-related healthcare visits (categorized as ED, inpatient, or outpatient) and procedures during the baseline period (with an HS symptom as the principal or secondary diagnosis) and follow-up periods (with HS or an HS symptom as the principal or secondary diagnosis). Thirty-day risks of all-cause hospitalization and of all-cause ED visits were also calculated for all patients; these risks were defined as the number of patients who were hospitalized or had an ED visit within 30 days after index discharge. Overall costs (total costs of care for visits in the baseline and follow-up periods) and disease-related costs (costs for visits with an HS symptom as the principal or secondary diagnosis during baseline and costs for visits with HS or an HS symptom as the principal or secondary diagnosis during index and follow-up) for inpatient and outpatient visits were both assessed. All costs are reported in 2022 United States dollars.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed separately for adult and pediatric patients, and descriptive analyses were used to assess all variables. Descriptive tables were generated using R, version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Significance was defined as P < .05. Outcome variables were compared between patients with HS and patients with suspected HS using simple comparisons and standardized mean differences. As previously explained, outcome variables describing the patient diagnosis and treatment journey were also compared across patients with HS stratified by race, ethnicity, payer type, and SVI. Continuous variables were summarized with means and SDs and were compared between cohorts using 2-sample t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Missing values for continuous variables were excluded from statistical analyses. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages and were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Missing values for categorical variables were categorized as unknown.
