Climate model large ensembles as test beds for applied compound event research
Authors: Flavio Lehner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummarySome of the most impactful climate and weather events result from compounding drivers. To robustly assess the current and future risk from such compound events, a better understanding of the associated sources of uncertainty is needed. Internal variability confounds detection and attribution of human-induced climate change and imposes irreducible limits on the accuracy of climate projections. Response uncertainty can lead to divergent projections for many societally important quantities such as precipitation. Combined with unknown future greenhouse gas emissions, these uncertainties can result in a socio-economically paralyzing range of future storylines. Climate model large ensembles are uniquely positioned to assess these uncertainties and are rightfully gaining popularity in compound event research, but they need to be accompanied by rigorous model validation and robust observational constraints to reach their full potential in terms of usefulness for practitioners. This perspective discusses these opportunities and challenges at the example of water resources and provides an outlook on application-oriented compound event research with large ensembles.

Introduction

Recent years have seen a proliferation of research into climate and weather-related compound events, that is, extreme events with multivariate drivers that result in impacts that often exceed what might be expected from the summed impacts of individual drivers.1 While multivariate extreme event research is not new,2 there are a range of possible reasons for the current surge in this subfield. The increasing frequency of extreme events with substantial socio-economic impacts and the partial attribution of many of these events to climate change have likely focused the public eye and research on event-based climate science.3,4,5,6 Socio-economic risk management and infrastructure planning increasingly seeks to integrate climate model projections, but when doing so is confronted with large uncertainty in future projections of compound events.7 Greater availability of climate and exposure data,8 new statistical methods,9,10 and advances in computational tools11,12 harbor promise to improve our understanding of compound events and how they might change under warming.13

Sample size is a ubiquitous challenge for the analysis of all compound event types, be it pre-conditioned, multivariate, temporally and/or spatially compounding events.14 Depending on the dimensionality of the event under study, the sample size required to robustly assess climatological distributions—let alone changes therein—can be multiple times larger than for univariate events.13,15 It would take a prohibitive amount of time for the observational record to become long enough to enable robust empirical statistics, time that is typically not available under the pressure of adaptation planning for ongoing climate change.

Different statistical and physical modeling approaches are used to address the sampling issue (see Table 2 in Raymond et al. 5) and an increasing number of studies are combining the two approaches.6,16 On the physical modeling side, the availability of multiple climate model large ensembles has greatly increased the sample size and thus the number of plausible but so far unseen compound events,17,18 a characteristic of great value to various impact modeling communities that are already using large ensembles.19,20,21,22,23 Large ensembles have also strengthened our ability to separate different sources of projection uncertainty24 and serve as a testbed for the development of statistical methods to interpret the observational record25 or build cost-effective emulators of climate models.26

These growing analytical opportunities on large scale climate data are bumping up against the capacity of traditional approaches of adaptation studies, where a selection of climate models is downscaled and then fed to impact models. While valued for their more realistic representation of the fine-scale details of possible futures, these approaches risk undersampling several sources of projection uncertainty. Practitioners thus seek to combine information from climate model large ensembles and knowledge about where and when fine-scale information matters to best assess future risks for a specific system. However, a lack of guidance on how to vet and integrate large ensembles in applied science is a roadblock to broader adoption.

In this perspective, using an idealized case study from water resource management, we highlight Single Model Initial-condition Large Ensembles (SMILEs) as a practical tool and test bed for impact-oriented compound event research, discuss their strength and limitations, and propose new analytical strategies to deepen our understanding of compound events and their impact on human and natural systems. We expand on recent perspectives on the value of SMILEs13,27 by using a larger number of SMILEs, including from CMIP6, and illustrate more explicitly how SMILEs can be evaluated before deployment.

Case study: Water reservoir in a drought-prone region

Background

Recent years have seen a number of prominent hot-dry compound events with socio-economic and ecosystem impacts, such as the 2015–2017 Western Cape drought in South Africa,6,28 the 2018 and 2022 summer droughts in Europe,29,30 or multi-year droughts in the Southwestern US31,32,33,34 and the Horn of Africa.35 Hot-dry compound events are among the best studied due to their high-profile impacts, but perhaps also due to the wider availability of observational and model data for temperature and precipitation as compared to other climatic variables.

Here, we use the ongoing US Southwest drought as a hot-dry compound event case study to illustrate the value and challenges of using climate model large ensembles for applied problems. This drought is arguably a multi-year to decadal compound event, in which a sequence of low-precipitation years since the early 2000s combined with rapidly rising temperatures led to reduced surface water availability.31,32,36 This expresses itself perhaps most prominently in declining levels in the two largest U.S. reservoirs, Lake Mead and Lake Powell.37 At the end of the water year 2022 the combined storage of the two reservoirs has dropped to its lowest level since the reservoirs were built, containing less than one year’s worth of average annual flow from its main tributary, the Colorado River.38

The Upper Colorado River Basin is projected to continue to warm this century, according to 29 models and 4 emissions scenarios from the CMIP6 archive (Figures 1A–1C; Table S1), with emissions scenario differences being the dominant source of uncertainty after about 2060 (Figures 1D–1G)—assuming that the different scenarios are equally likely, something that is debated.39,40 In contrast, precipitation projections remain much more uncertain (Figures 1B–1E and 1H): while the multi-model mean points toward wetter conditions overall, model uncertainty is large and includes the possibility of precipitation declines. Internal variability is also large and will remain influential as an irreducible source of uncertainty throughout this century.27 Finally, the frequency of hot-dry compound events (fHD) is projected to increase as well, with the largest changes in regions that see precipitation declines (Figure 1C). Internal variability and model uncertainty dominate the overall uncertainty of both precipitation and fHD projections, leaving little relative importance to the choice of emissions scenario (Figures 1F–1I). Using SMILEs instead results in a similar picture, though internal variability becomes relatively more important in that case (Figure S1). This points to a well-documented issue with uncertainty partitioning at regional scales: estimating the forced response from a single simulation can alias in aspect of internal variability, especially for noisy variables such as precipitation, leading to an underestimation of the importance of internal variability.24 While this is not a large issue for the general projections as shown in Figure 1, it can be important for more nuanced questions, such as estimating non-monotonic forced changes43 or changes in internal variability itself,44 as explored in results.

Data and methods

Against the backdrop of this changing regional hydroclimate, we deploy a reservoir model that mimics some of the relevant processes and interactions of this multi-scale system and how it evolves under future climate trajectories. Note that the reservoir model here is highly simplified and primarily for illustration purposes. Such toy models are widely used in the literature, often described as “rainfall-runoff” or “bucket” models.45,46 Here, we start from the formulation used in Henley et al.47 but simplify it by only focusing on water year mean values (October–September).

The schematic in Figure 2A illustrates the processes included in the reservoir model. Figure 2B identifies the modulators, drivers, potential for preconditioning, hazards, and impacts associated with hot-dry compound events affecting reservoir storage, following the template of Bevacqua et al.21 Here, climate change is viewed to only influence precipitation and temperature, while in practice other drivers might be influenced as well. The reservoir model takes precipitation (P) and temperature (T) anomalies from either observations or climate model output, both of which are being spatially averaged over the Upper Basin of the Colorado River (approximately 37–42°N/112-106°W) for our case study. P primarily affects runoff (Q), which constitutes the sole inflow into the reservoir. The translation of P to Q is governed by runoff sensitivities that depend on P, T, and the preceding year’s Q through a multivariate regression model trained on naturalized observed Q,41,48 specifically ΔQ/ΔP = 2.6%/% and ΔQ/ΔT = −9.1%/°C. Note that a range of sensitivity estimates exist in the literature and could be substituted here.49 Demand (D) is dictated by smoothed historical consumption data (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation50), but it is modulated slightly on an annual basis by P and T, representing higher demand primarily from irrigation when precipitation is low (dry years) or evaporative loss is high (warm years) or both. Baseline D is kept constant from 2020 onwards. Water can leave the reservoir through evaporation (E) or release (R). E here varies only with T, using an empirical formulation for evaporation from a water surface51 with climatological values for net radiation estimated from ERA5 reanalysis data. Release R depends on demand D and the available storage S in the reservoir. If S > D, then R = D, otherwise R = S. In other words, the reservoir does not have any management rules that would prevent it from running dry if D > S, a key difference to perhaps any real-world reservoir management situation. Observations of T and P to drive the reservoir model are taken from PRISM.52 We also use output from 14 SMILEs with 20 members each (Table 1). The ideal ensemble size always depends on the research question.44 Here, 20 members is chosen as it offers a good compromise between the potential error from undersampling and the number of SMILEs available for estimating regional changes in T and P and their variability (Figure S2): allowing smaller ensemble sizes only marginally increases the number of SMILEs available, while strongly increasing the potential error; using larger ensemble sizes quickly reduces the number of SMILEs available with only small reductions in potential error. Thus, SMILEs entail a few tradeoffs compared to the standard CMIP6 output (as used in Figure 1): SMILEs sample fewer models and scenarios than CMIP6 but due to the larger sample size per model enable a more robust separation of forced response and internal variability and a more comprehensive sampling of internal variability. The SMILEs used here encompass both CMIP5 and CMIP6 models and a variety of emissions scenarios, though only one scenario per model is used. The scenarios were chosen to cover the spread of the full CMIP6 archive (Figure S3). The SMILEs also span a wide range of climate sensitivity values (Table 1). All SMILEs were regridded to 2.5 ° × 2.5 ° spatial resolution (bilinear for T, conservative for P). Individual P time series from SMILEs are bias-corrected to have the same mean and variability as observations over the 1950–2023 period by subtracting the mean difference to observations and scaling the time series by the ratio of interannual variances. All data are normally distributed (according to an Anderson-Darling test at 95% confidence), allowing for this simple bias correction. More sophisticated bias correction methods are likely needed in practice, for example to also account for biases in correlation structure or trends, as will be discussed in climate model validation. All time series start in 1950, which is the common start year among climate model simulations used; in reality, Glen Canyon Dam (Lake Powell) was not yet built at that time, but the fraction of the total combined capacity of Lake Mead and Powell was about 35%, which is what we use as initial value for S.

Climate model validation

Validation of climate model representation of compound events is important to establish credibility in the projected changes. Disagreement between observations and models can arise for several reasons: (i) true model bias, (ii) internal variability, (iii) measurement uncertainty, or (iv) forcing uncertainty. Separation of (i) and (ii) can be challenging due to the limited observational sample size. Thanks to SMILEs we know the forced response and variability magnitude of a given model. However, in the absence of making assumptions about the forced response in observations, we do not know whether the observations sit at the edge or the center of their own hypothetical distribution. Thus, a model distribution that is centered on the observed value and one that barely overlaps with it are, without more information, indistinguishable in terms of their performance. Measurement and forcing uncertainty (iii-iv) are not addressed here but can be tackled with a range of independent observational datasets or processing methods (not always available; e.g., Newman et al.68) and different forcing estimates applied to the same climate model (very rarely available; e.g., Holland et al.69).

Given the sample size challenge of compound events, one can use SMILEs to conduct a series of tests that provide a probabilistic assessment of a model’s realism.6 Here, for the period of 1950–2023, we test (1) the distribution of T and P anomalies, (2) the interannual correlation between T and P, and (3) the distribution of trends. Specifically, an SMILE is considered “good” if (1) 95% or more of its ensemble members have an anomaly distribution not significantly different from observations (using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test at 95% confidence), (2) the observed correlation falls inside the 95% range of simulated correlations across ensemble members, or (3) at least one ensemble member is inside the 83.4% confidence interval of the observed trend.

The tests are illustrated in Figures 3A–3E and summarized in Figure 3F. Only one of the 14 models performs well in all five tests. For example, many models have too wide of a precipitation distribution (Figure 3B). Most strikingly, for this region of the world, the models tend to overestimate the warming trend (Figure 3D) and underestimate the correlation between T and P (Figure 3C). These results are relevant because they suggest that models will underestimate the risk of hot-dry compound events due to their correlation structure but overestimate it due to potentially excessive warming. Figure 3C also shows how the T/P correlation is projected to change over time, calculated as a 74-year running correlation. Regardless of their initial bias, models agree that the T/P relationship will strengthen in the future, consistent with other studies projecting an intensifying land-atmosphere coupling in this region.70 Finally, with the rise of the CMIP6-related “hot model” framing,71 there is an acute need to verify whether that framing actually applies to a given regional case study and variable; one cannot blindly cull models based on that global temperature focused perspective. To that end, Figures 3D and 3E show the relationship between historical and future changes in T and P. There is a weak relationship between historical and future warming, which would support a constraint on the strongest warming rates (Figure 3D). Fundamentally, these tests make clear that SMILEs cannot be deployed in quantitative impact studies without bias-correction motivated by case-specific research questions. However, they allow for a more robust performance assessment72,73 and reduce the risk of mischaracterizing model performance as compared to assessments based on a single simulation per model. This is particularly important for hard-to-sample metrics such as trends and correlations, biases in which can persist through conventional downscaling and bias-correction methods. These tests also suggest that whether a model is “fit for purpose” depends on the purpose and that a broad set of performance metrics does not guarantee a meaningful or easy-to-interpret constraint on future projections.74

Results

The reservoir model qualitatively captures historical combined Lake Mead and Lake Powell reservoir level fluctuations when fed with observed P and T, with maxima in the 1980s and around 2000 and a rapid decline thereafter (Figure 2C). Despite aforementioned climate model biases, the SMILE-based trajectories encompass the observations and the majority of them also result in storage maxima between 1980 and 2000 and declines thereafter (Figure 2C). This arises primarily from the dominant role of historical prescribed D, though external forcing of both T and P42 might lead to some synchronization of observed and historical model-simulated trajectories. Future trajectories, however, depend strongly on the particular climate model, consistent with the expectations from Figure 1.

Importantly, this reservoir model illustrates several pathways how hot-dry events can compound to affect reservoir levels (Figure 2B), thus making a hot-dry year on average more impactful than the sum of individual hot and dry years (Figure 2D). It also incorporates temporal compounding through the fact that the reservoir can store multiple years’ worth of inflow, which is designed to create resilience to successive drought years, but also represents vulnerability to more extensive periods of drought—both scenarios that have occurred in the last 40+ years in the Southwestern U.S. Additionally, the model could account for spatial compounding by making D dependent on T and P from an adjacent region that would draw water from the reservoir for irrigation (e.g., like U.S. states in the Lower Colorado River Basin that depend on water from the upstream reservoirs), though this is not explored here.

The 280 trajectories from SMILEs (14 models x 20 members) are then used to map out a risk surface of reservoir storage falling below 20% in T and P space (Figure 4), providing a scenario- and climate sensitivity-agnostic perspective, similar to the unifying metric of warming levels that can be used when trying to circumvent discussion of scenario and response uncertainty.75 Rather than creating such a risk surface by synthetically sampling all possible T and P combinations,47 the surfaces are calculated empirically and thus preserve and illustrate the range of discrete outcomes from the combination of model uncertainty and internal variability that one is confronted with when using a collection of climate models. Reservoir storage is largely stable during 1950–1999, with little risk of falling below 20%, while by 2050–2099 it encompasses a much larger area of the T and P space and ranges from <10% to >90% risk (Figure 4A). Further, thanks to their sample size, SMILEs allow to more robustly decompose the projected storage changes into effects from individual characteristics of the climate change signal. Removing the forced response from P isolates the influence from warming alone, which drives robust storage declines (Figure 4B). In turn, isolating changes in P by detrending T shows that changes in P alone lead to a broader range of storage levels than historically seen, though the risk for the reservoir to run dry remain small due to the overall tendency of models for increasing P (Figure 4C). Finally, by detrending both T and P, the influence from forced changes in variability itself can be isolated (Figure 4D). Increased P variability leads to more volatile reservoir levels, including outcomes below the historical range (Figure 4D). While mean changes in P are highly uncertain in this region, changes in variability are actually consistent and robust.11,78,79,80 This increased variability by itself suggests an increased risk of low storage levels and could, in the absence of consistent projections of mean changes in P, provide the impetus for adjusting current reservoir management strategies.

Discussion and conclusions

Climate model large ensembles are a useful test bed for impact studies, in particular ones that require large samples of spatio-temporally consistent realizations as encountered in compound event research. Here, we apply SMILEs to obtain a first order understanding of the Southwestern U.S. reservoir storage risks in the face of internally generated and externally forced changes in temperature and precipitation—as well as their interaction. We find that changes in both mean climate and its variability widen the envelope of possible reservoir levels compared to the historical range, however, there is a clear tendency for increased shortage risk. Much of this increased risk is driven by the ubiquitous warming, as precipitation and thus also hot-dry compound events are not strongly dependent on the emissions scenario but instead are subject to large model response uncertainty. These results are consistent with many existing studies (Lukas and Payton,37 and references therein) but here emerge from a larger ensemble of SMILEs. Nonetheless, it still only represents an “ensemble of opportunity”.81 Further work will need to probe the sensitivity of these results to additional parameters, such as the runoff sensitivity values used, the future water demand scenario assumed, seasonal aspects of the climate change signal, and possibly model weighting based on performance and independence.

SMILEs enable a robust understanding of the sources of projection uncertainty at global to regional scales and can thus inform scientific and technical decisions further down the modeling chain. Due to computational limitations, traditionally only a select number of climate models can be downscaled and used in impact studies. Computationally efficient climate model emulators10 or boosted climate model ensembles82,83 might be able to fill this gap. Meanwhile, some model selection schemes are vulnerable to internal variability and would benefit from method testing within an SMILE. There is a long-standing issue in the downscaling community that models are selected for their performance in metrics that have little predictive value of future model behavior.74 For example, whether a model is on average too cold or warm today might not have any relationship with how much it warms in the future. Instead, a model that warms too much over the historical period is likely to also warm more in the future, providing an opportunities for observational constraints.84 Quantifying and understanding such biases in sensitivity rather than mean state is objectively more challenging and requires process-oriented diagnostics, but SMILEs at least allow to approach this issue probabilistically,6,85 as illustrated here.

SMILEs’ biases and their coarse spatial resolution often prevent them from direct deployment in high resolution impact-oriented analyses. For example, reliable Colorado River streamflow projections might require resolving orography86 and seasonal shifts in snowpack-runoff dynamics,87,88 aspects only crudely represented in current SMILEs. Also, there are myriads of more complex impact models requiring multiple input variables, an issue tackled by the ISIMIP cross-sectoral climate impact assessments protocol.89 Nevertheless, the range of SMILEs-based projections as presented here is similar to outcomes from more comprehensive modeling exercises,37 suggesting that broad trends and uncertainty ranges are driven by climate projections, even as details will depend on additional fine scale modeling. Thus, to avoid overconfident projections, SMILEs should be considered alongside higher-resolution and more storyline-oriented analyses to provide the context of how a fuller depiction of internal variability and uncertainties at larger scales might affect a study’s outcome. At the same time, this dominance of projection uncertainty by climate parameters motivates further work on observational constraints.40

In nonlinear systems there is often unrecognized potential for greatly increased risk from even just briefly stepping outside the (potentially undersampled) historical climate variability,19,76 again something that SMILEs can help explore. Several impact-oriented research communities, including water resource management, are embracing the approach of robust decision-making in the face of deep climate uncertainty, combining SMILEs with impact models and facilitating the necessary collaboration between the climate modeling community and practitioners.7,77,90,91 Such integration across disciplines is necessary to provide actionable climate information in other socio-economic sectors.
