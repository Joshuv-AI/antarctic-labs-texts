A methodology to evaluate the effectiveness of interprofessional education strategies based on the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework among vocational healthcare students in China
Authors: Liping Kong, Shaoman Li, Zhanfeng Li, Xun Mi, Jing Li, Ningxin Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Interprofessional education (IPE) is rapidly becoming a core element of health professions preparation programs worldwide, but the effectiveness of different IPE strategies and their impacts in different regions and populations remain unclear, especially in the vocational education setting. This article describes the overall research design including the development, testing and preliminary evaluation of the IPE education interventions for Chinese vocational healthcare students, following the procedure outlined in the UK Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for developing and implementing complex interventions in healthcare. The objective is to develop and adapt role-based IPE strategies and evaluate their feasibility and effectiveness on interprofessional competencies of vocational healthcare students in China, with nursing students being our focus.•This study adopts a mixed-methods approach to develop two IPE strategies related to role interaction (role-taking and role-playing) in comparison to one conventional IPE strategy (pure group discussion) and investigate the different effectiveness in vocational healthcare students’ attitudes to IPE, perception of professional roles, and interprofessional collaborative competencies by three valid scales.•The study is divided into four stages: development, feasibility testing, evaluation and final experimental verification.•This study helps provide scientific and appropriate IPE strategies for vocational healthcare educators, so as to improve the interprofessional learning and collaborative ability of vocational healthcare students.

Background

Interprofessional education (IPE) has been defined by the Center for the Advancement of Interprofessional Education (CAIPE) as “occasions when members or students of two or more professions learn with, from and about each other to improve collaboration and the quality of care and service” [2]. Research from the Institute of Medical Research (IOM, 2001) shows that patients are more likely to receive safe and high-quality services when health professionals understand each other's roles, communicate and collaborate effectively (IOM, 2001). Vocational healthcare students are still the main resource of clinical work in China, and they will face complex clinical cooperation after graduation. As the closest people to patients, nurses are often the first to perceive and reflect effective information from patients, which requires their ability to play a positive role in teamwork. Currently, IPE strategies have quickly become the focus of attention in many universities around the world and receiving interprofessional education before graduation helps students improve their ability to communicate and cooperate in teams [3].

More and more evidence suggests that participating in IPE can also help students improve their cognition, confidence, self-efficacy, professional skills, and teamwork [4]. A recent review relating to the impact of IPE on behavioral change has indicated that IPE has a positive impact in different professions of healthcare [5], and IPE can improve interprofessional collaborative knowledge, skills, and behavior based on objective measurements [6].

In terms of the context within which IPE teaching is implemented and delivered, a comprehensive review [7] reported that key factors related to the success of IPE teaching were professional participation, high-quality facilitation, facilitator preparation and organizational context and support. A recent review which mapped the different types of IPE teaching and learning found that simulation-based education and problem-based learning were the most prevalent approaches. However, the authors called for further research into the effectiveness of the different teaching approaches in the development of IPE competencies and longer-term outcomes such as patient impact [8,9]. Several reviews have focused on the impact of one particular approach such as simulation [10] or rural clinical placements [11]. Whilst it is critical to ascertain whether IPE is effective, it is also important to know which aspects of IPE programs are key to ensuring successful outcomes. This can be challenging, however, because IPE programs frequently incorporate a combination of approaches [8]. Given the heterogeneity and complexity of IPE interventions, researchers have been unable to disentangle the differential impact of various IPE educational approaches on different populations [[5], [6], [12], [13]]. This hinders the development and delivery of successful interprofessional interventions. To date, there have been few attempts to establish the comparative impact of specific teaching strategies (e.g., simulation versus clinically based IPE) on a range of short- and longer-term professional outcomes. There have also been very limited direct comparisons of the effectiveness of different types of IPE strategies.

Vocational education is an important pathway of national education in China, which refers to post-secondary education that provides vocational and technical training, preparing students for specific professions or industries [14]. The aim is to develop learners with appropriate theoretical knowledge, practical skills and good professional ethics for social production, service and management [15]. According to the latest data from the Ministry of Education (MOE), China has already developed the world's largest vocational education system, with 11,295 institutions and over 26 million vocational students [16]. Students are trained in different pathways to obtain diverse diplomas for healthcare practice in China. Compared with undergraduates, vocational students are still the main human resource for primary healthcare workers, especially nurses in China. However, students are likely to face complex collaborative clinical situations after graduation, and they may lack the ability to work effectively as a team due to the significant gap in IPE in Chinese vocational education [17]. IPE is also not currently a part of the primary curriculum for vocational health education due to the lack of trained teachers and relevant resources. Therefore, vocational healthcare students must receive IPE and improve their team collaboration abilities before graduation.

This study aims to develop and adapt role-interaction-related IPE teaching materials for application to vocational healthcare students in China and to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effect on interprofessional competencies of Chinese vocational health professional students.

Method details

Study methodology

As per the MRC complex interventions framework guidelines, a process evaluation using a mixture of qualitative and quantitative methods was conducted to evaluate the acceptability and influence of the IPE intervention by exploring students’, teachers’ and stakeholders’ perceptions [[18], [19], [20]]. The quantitative data collection aims at evaluating students’ attitudes toward IPE, perception of professional roles and interprofessional collaborative competency, while qualitative data could help to understand students’ subjective perspectives and experiences in IPE learning. They complement each other to build an overall evaluation of the IPE intervention.

According to the MRC framework, there are four phases of study design [19,21]. As it is a new attempt to implement interprofessional education strategies for vocational health students in China, this research conducts the two phases of development and feasibility to test the facilitation and barriers of the teaching process, as outlined in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Study design using the MRC framework.Fig. 1

Development phase

Among the four stages of the MRC framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions, the development phase is considered to be the most critical and should put more emphasis on, as it determines the success of the entire evaluation and implementation process [19]. During the development phase, the researchers should design a practical and reliable research protocol, considering every aspect like funding and human resources, to make the final full-scale trial more meaningful and not too difficult to conduct. A systematic approach was used in the development of this interprofessional education intervention to enhance attitudes toward IPE, perception of roles and interprofessional competencies in Chinese vocational health students.

The objectives of the development phase were to (1) Explore theories and strategies of role-playing and role-taking on enhancing healthcare professional students’ interprofessional knowledge, attitudes and behaviors. (2) Describe and formulate the mechanisms through which role interventions enhance interprofessional knowledge, attitudes, perceptions and competencies in vocational healthcare professional students. (3) Identify IPE outcome measures that are likely to be influenced by role-playing and role-taking educational interventions.

To meet the above specific objectives, three studies were conducted below, which are summarized in Fig. 3. (1) Theoretical exploration of the mechanisms through which role interventions may impact interprofessional education outcomes. (2) Comprehensive Literature Review: a) investigate the evidence for the effectiveness of role-based interventions on interprofessional competencies among healthcare professionals, and b) identify the essential components of successful interprofessional education interventions to inform implementation of the role interventions. (3) Modelling and revision of the role-based interventions and case-based group learning without role interaction strategy according to the feedback from an Expert Group Meeting consisting of senior educators from Nanjing Vocational Health College.Fig. 3The interaction between the staged studies of the development phase informing the modelling and design of the role-playing & role-taking IPE interventions.Fig. 3

Based on previous studies, our team designed three IPE strategies that meet the characteristics of vocational healthcare students. However, more professional support and expert advice are needed to design more practical teaching intervention processes. Therefore, an Expert Advisory Group (EAG) [22] was recruited for ongoing guidance of the instructional program. The group consisted of a researcher, six facilitators, three professors from different professions, an IPE expert, an education administrator and six students from different professions. During the development phase, which lasted six months, three EAG workshops were held, at each of which all members assumed equal decision-maker roles and contributed their strengths and expertise. The IPE strategies were initially developed by the research team after discussion with the relevant departments of the school. All relevant contents and details in the curriculum, such as learning objectives, case design, teaching methods, evaluation and other arrangements, were discussed through the EAG and checked by experienced professors of each profession. These consultations would facilitate the development of a detailed teaching plan to guide the implementation of interventions. After each workshop, the knowledge and ideas generated were compiled into a manuscript and subsequently presented at the next workshop, allowing the brainstorming design to be further critically discussed and refined. At the final workshop of the joint design and construction work, EAG carried out final revision and refinement of the overall scheme in preparation for subsequent practical feasibility testing. Feasibility testing phase

Based on the above development stage, a brief model intervention prototype and its main components were well-designed, including interprofessional teaching strategies and outcome measures. Then, the feasibility and acceptability of the prototype were tested through an exploratory randomized controlled trial and focus-group interviews of student participants and facilitators. The primary purpose of the feasibility testing and focus group was to collect participants' subjective views on the intervention prototype and components, as well as to understand their experiences in the process. The participants' ability to understand and complete these outcome measures was also assessed in the feasibility study. These findings were then used to refine the intervention prototype and modify the implementation strategy to assess the feasibility and effectiveness of the improved intervention for the next phase of the exploratory randomized controlled trial (RCT).

The main objectives of the feasibility study were to: (1) Pilot test the effectiveness of IPE role-taking and role-playing in an exploratory pilot randomized controlled trial. (2) Test the process of the main study, including recruitment, randomization, intervention, and follow-up assessments to evaluate whether these components are functioning smoothly. (3) Provide preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of IPE role-playing and role-taking interventions on knowledge/skills, attitudes, perceptions, and competencies among vocational healthcare students. The feasibility study was a three-arm randomized controlled trial, each arm of which used a different teaching strategy to deliver interprofessional learning to healthcare students: (1) Group A (positive education: role-taking): Interprofessional case-based group learning with role-taking other participants’ professional roles. (2) Group B (positive education: role-playing): Interprofessional case-based group learning and role-playing of participants’ own roles. (3) Group C (passive education without role interaction): Interprofessional case-based group learning without role-playing and role-taking.

Participants

The participants are vocational healthcare students from six different professions (nursing, midwifery, medical imaging, medical equipment, rehabilitation and elderly care) at a vocational health college in Nanjing, China. In addition, each participant can be awarded a mandatory elective score from attending the IPE programme over a school year.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All participants should meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) being enrolled in a health professions programme of study at Nanjing Vocational Health College (Nanjing Health School) in their third year; (2) consent to participate in the IPE programme; (3) have not been diagnosed with any psychological conditions. Exclusion criteria: Students were excluded if they have (1) previously participated in any interprofessional learning programmes or activities; (2) incomplete responses to questionnaires; (3) absence at any of the training sessions; (4) withdrawal from the continuation of the study.

Sample Size

As the proposed study was not a definitive trial, there was no formal basis for a power calculation. The study examined preliminary evidence that estimated effect sizes and informed sample size calculations for the future definitive trial. Based on published guidance [23] and advice from the faculty statistician, a sample size of 12 per group is recommended as being appropriate for a pilot study. As a result, 90 health professional students, including 15 from each profession, who fulfilled the study inclusion criteria, were recruited for the pilot study. They were allocated randomly to three intervention arms, with 30 students in each arm. Each arm was further divided into five small mixed-professions teams with six individuals separately from the six professions: one nursing student, one midwifery student, one medical imaging student, one medical equipment student, one rehabilitation student, and one student from elderly care.

Randomization and blinding

Stratified randomization was used to randomize students in equal numbers in each arm of the trial. The randomization was conducted using the statistical software SPSS After being provided by the registrar's office at the study site with the number of students enrolled on the training programme, the researcher created a list of unique identifiers for the students. The randomization process relies on a random number generator function to generate a random sequence of numbers that would link each unique student-identifying code to either Group A, Group B, and Group C. To avoid researcher bias, this involved the statistician in the research team independently inputting the list of unique identifying codes for all the recruited students of the training programme. Next, the randomization function of the software randomly allocated the students to either Group A, Group B, or Group C. Concealment of allocation was achieved through the standard approach of using opaque sealed envelopes. Every participant was given a candidate number when they signed up, and the statistician in the school created the randomization sequence and prepared the envelopes. Then, a research assistant who was unaware of the groups would give the envelopes to the students. Due to the nature of the course and the design of the study, facilitator blindness may not be possible, but participants were unaware of which group was precisely the intervention, comparison, or control one. Outcome assessment retained blinding since the use of questionnaire scales was inherently a blind process.

Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS): RIPLS is a 19-item instrument using a 5-point Likert rating scale (1=strongly disagree to 5=strongly agree) to examine health professionals’ readiness and attitudes toward Interprofessional learning (IPL) [24]. On the scale, items 1–9 were labelled “teamwork and collaboration,” items 10–16 were labelled “professional identity,” and items 17–19 were labelled “roles and responsibilities.” In the original English version, the RIPLS was found to be valid and reliable. This scale has excellent reliability with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.90 [24]. Subsequent studies using healthcare professions have also found the RIPLS to demonstrate acceptable levels of validity and reliability. There is a Chinese version of RIPLS developed by a research team from Tianjin Medical University in China, and its content validity index is excellent at 0.91 [25]. Additionally, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient was reported to be 0.92 [26]. Although we have the Chinese version of RIPLS, we still choose to use the English version as the evaluation tool for students because they can understand the English content of the scale and using the original version can help minimize research bias. In addition, the other two questionnaires used in the research do not have Chinese editions. Therefore, the English versions of all three instruments were used for consistency. Interdisciplinary Education Perception Scale (IEPS): IEPS is an 18-item instrument with four subscales which explore the participants’ cooperation with each other and their understanding of roles as well as values of other professions. Interprofessional Education Collaborative (IPEC) Competency Self-Assessment: This version of the IPEC Competency Self-Assessment Tool is a 42-item self-assessment questionnaire with items based on the competencies defined by the Interprofessional Education Collaborative expert panel. It is designed for health professional students to rate their ability level on each competency using a 5-point Likert scale [28]. The IPEC scale contains four components, ‘Teams and teamwork’ domain, ‘Values and ethics’ domain, ‘Roles/Responsibilities' domain and ‘Interprofessional communication’ domain. In total, the components accounted for 79% of the variance in the response data. Data management

Data collection

During the development phase, all workshop processes were recorded and verbatim transcribed and photographs of the on-site workshop were taken with the informed consent of the participants. Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis [1]. Topics were identified from the data using induction without attempting to fit them into pre-existing coding frameworks, but all textual content was based on EAG members’ understanding and suggestions of the draft plan. As the study focused on designing and reviewing the construction of interventions, descriptive analyses of the data were performed to generate broad themes related to the content, overall design, feasibility, effectiveness, and ways to enhance participation in interventions.

During the feasibility evaluation and final verification phase, each participant was required to complete demographic questionnaires about gender, age, and profession; At the same time, students' general attitude towards IPE, cognition of other professional roles and communication and collaboration abilities were assessed using identified tools (Time 0). Immediately after all interventions, three valid questionnaires were administered to each participant to collect data on attitudes, role perception, and other relevant abilities (Time 1). Then, after three months, the same scales were distributed to all participants in the study for follow-up surveys (Time 2).

Data processing and storage

Participants were told how their data would be used in the Participant Information Sheet and Consent Form. They also needed to tick the box to agree that their information was being processed for research purposes. Information on all participants was anonymous, except for the principal investigator, supervisors, and consulting statistician. This would be ensured by using a unique identification number in place of any other identifying information and therefore cannot be identified as personal data. In addition, any stored data is kept on password-protected computer systems and/or encrypted USB and stored in a locked filing cabinet. Data is stored in electronic files such as Excel spreadsheets for quantitative data, Word files for qualitative data, mp3s, for interviews and focus group records, and PDFs for all files that need to be manually scanned before shredding. Only the main investigator has access to personal information relevant to the study. Statisticians and other members of the research team only have access to the data after it has been anonymized. All data will be processed no later than 4 years after study completion.

Statistical analysis

This study used a repeated measures design to evaluate and compare the effects of different IPE interventions, immediately after intervention and at three months follow-up, and to compare data before and after intervention and between groups. The data was encoded and entered into SPSS 22.0.

Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were used to describe categorical and quantitative variables, such as students’ demographic and personal profile data. Quantitative data from the scales used in the feasibility study involving participants’ attitudes toward IPE, perception of different roles, and interprofessional communication and collaboration abilities were expressed as mean (standard deviation). The study employed the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine whether variations existed in scales (RIPLS, IEPS, and IPEC) among three groups with different types of interventions at different time points. To further assess if there is a significant difference between the mean values of two distinct groups, the parametric test, the independent sample t-test was chosen to compare mean values of the quantitative variables among different categorical variables (type of IPE strategy and timepoint of intervention). The paired samples t-test was used to compare the differences between the quantitative results variables from pre- and post-study within one group. The statistical significance was determined through a p-value of <0.05 and 95% confidence intervals for estimation accuracy.

Evaluation phase

The new framework defines evaluation as going beyond questioning whether an intervention is effective in achieving its expected results, including determining its other impacts, how it theoretically works, considering how it interacts with the context of implementing it, how it contributes to system changes, and evidence that can be used to support the real world [21].

The objectives of the evaluation are to (1) Assess the detailed effectiveness of the role-based interprofessional interventions. (2) Decide primary and secondary outcomes. (3) Consider key elements and understand the change process related to contextual factors. (4) Assess cost-effectiveness of interventions in the real world.

Implementation phase

The objectives of the implementation are to (1) Fully verify the effectiveness of IPE in a prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial. (2) Provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of IPE strategies related to role-playing and role-taking interventions on knowledge, skills, attitudes, perceptions, and abilities of vocational health students and promote them in a wider region.

Method validation

Due to the study complexity of interprofessional education and changes in attitude/perception, pure quantitative or qualitative methods would not be able to completely achieve the objectives in this study, therefore, a mixed method research design was adopted in this trial [30]. In this way, the quantitative and qualitative data could complement each other and explore the deep complex mechanisms behind the changes in students’ interprofessional attitudes and behaviors.

A feasibility/piloting study often plays an important role as the basis for the development of RCT [31,32]. When testing the potential effectiveness of complex interventions in evidence-based studies, RCT is generally regarded as the gold standard for clinical trials [33]. Investing much money and time in RCTs is often a requirement in doing complex interventions, but sometimes it may not necessarily bring the desired results. The MRC guidance for developing and evaluating complex interventions emphasizes the importance of using feasibility/pilot studies before definitive RCTs [19,21]. Based on these suggestions, this study carried out a feasibility/pilot study in the form of an exploratory RCT to test the intervention process designed in the development phase, providing indicators of response and dropout rate, observing participants’ performance in the procedure and learning about their feedback on interprofessional learning. The findings could be used to guide the recruitment process and further refine the intervention prototype of the final RCT in the future. The pilot study also tested the proposed randomization process and clarified the suitability of current resources, such as study survey scales, information sheets, consent forms, interventions and their comparative conditions, for understanding whether participants have any difficulties with them.

There were three arms designed in the exploratory RCT, one intervention group named Group A using the role-taking teaching strategy, one comparison group named Group B with the role-playing strategy, and one control group named Group C using case-based group discussion without role interactions. In the meanwhile, the feasibility trial utilized three relevant valid tools to measure the outcomes of each group at three time points: immediately before (T0) and after the intervention (T1), then three months later (T2), for pre-post and between-group comparisons.

Given that a lack of awareness of other professional roles has been identified as a significant barrier to IPE [34], it is not surprising that simulated interventions based on role-play were particularly successful at changing students’ awareness of other professions. Indeed, interventions which provide the chance to practically apply this knowledge as part of a role-taking are likely to consolidate this learning [35]. However, whilst simulated role-play has significant potential in facilitating a better understanding of different professional roles, the process of developing simulations is time-consuming, and it is still necessary to provide well-trained facilitators to deliver a successful program which achieves better learning objectives.

At the beginning of the IPE strategy design, there was a heated debate in our team about the professions we would like to include, and the final consensus was that compared to other professions, we preferred to see positive outcomes for nursing students, so we chose several professions that are closely related to clinical nursing as a starting point for our exploration. This may not be fair to the other students, but throughout the study, we have been paying attention to students' perceptions and mental states to ensure this strategy design is reasonable.

Limitations

This study also has some limitations. First, due to the impact of the intervention method, double-blind between facilitators and students could not be achieved, which may lead to bias in the results. In addition, due to the complexity of the research methodology, the final evaluation relied on questionnaire results, which led to the final analysis results not fully reflecting the true state of participants, although we tried to use different statistical analysis methods. It is worth noting that the specific composition, mechanism and application of IPE is still a very complex issue, the results of a single-centre study may be limited by population, region, and culture.
