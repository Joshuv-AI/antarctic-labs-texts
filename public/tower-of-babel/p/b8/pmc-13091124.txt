Biomechanical Evaluation of Powered Acetabular Extraction Tool Reduces Force, Time, and Surgeon Burden Compared to Manual Tools in a Simulated Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty Model
Authors: Dimitri J Mabarak, Shujaa T Khan, Khaled A Elmenawi, Matthew J Hadad, Chao Zhang, Viktor E Krebs, Michael R Bloomfield, Nicolas S Piuzzi, Matthew E Deren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRemoving acetabular implants is time- and labor-intensive, prompting the development of powered and manual extraction systems. Comparative data on force requirements, ergonomics, and operative time remain limited. We compared a powered vs a manual system in novice and expert surgeons using an in vitro model.MethodsComposite foam blocks implanted with a 54-mm hemispherical acetabular shell emulated a well-fixed cementless construct and were mounted on a force sensor. Three board-certified, fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons and 3 residents each performed 3 trials with a powered extractor and a manual extractor. Participants wore triaxial accelerometers on the back and both hands and an in-line force sensor. Outcomes were peak acceleration (proxy for vibration), peak applied force, and time to extraction.ResultsRelative to the powered device, the manual device was associated with higher back acceleration (estimate 0.55; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.49-0.61; P < .001), greater applied force (5.75; 95% CI: 4.04-7.47; P < .001), and longer time to component removal (61.31 s; 95% CI: 48.04-74.58; P < .001). Surgeon experience did not affect force (estimate −1.03; P = .696) or time (estimate −1.03; P = .696).ConclusionsThe powered acetabular extraction tool reduced vibration exposure, applied force, and extraction time compared with a manual device across both novice and expert operators. In a field with substantial work-related musculoskeletal injury and growing emphasis on surgeon well-being, powered extraction may improve efficiency while helping preserve surgeon health and career longevity. These data provide objective support for selecting powered systems when available and justify further study in cadaveric and clinical settings to confirm external validity.Keywords: Arthroplasty, Revision, Acetabular, Extraction, Ergonomics

Introduction

As the prevalence of primary total hip arthroplasty increases, so too has the prevalence of revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) [1]. rTHA presents unique challenges, particularly in cases requiring the removal of well-fixed acetabular components [2]. The removal of acetabular implants requires significant surgeon time and effort, which has led to the development of specialized extraction systems, including both powered and manual devices [3]. The goal of these devices is to extract the acetabular cup in a safe, efficient manner while minimizing bone loss. Due to the increasing demand for quick and safe methods of cup removal, there has been ongoing development of technology to meet this need.

The conventional manual acetabular extraction tool is equipped with a curved blade that is designed to break the cup-bone interface and rotate around the cup for removal. This is completed in a staged manner, where a short blade is first utilized to create a path for the longer blade, which reaches the apex of the cup. The radius of curvature of the long blade is to match that of the cup to minimize the quantity of bone loss. The surgeon then manually rotates the long blade about the cup, breaking the cup-bone interface. This technique is similar among manual extraction devices from several different manufacturers [4,5]. While this manual technique has been utilized for many years and has proven successful, it has also been described as labor-intensive and time-consuming compared to newer, powered models [3].

Powered acetabular extraction devices were developed for the purpose of the safe removal of acetabular cups with more efficiency and less surgeon effort than manual models. These devices are generally composed of a hemicircular saw blade that is placed onto the outer face of the cup and a ball which fits into the center of the cup to centralize and stabilize the saw. With the use of a trigger, the saw rotates at high speed as it is pushed around the cup in a manner that closely matches the radius of curvature of the cup. This results in a cup with a thin sheet of bone surrounding it. This technique has been shown in prior in vitro studies to not only be faster than manual methods but also to place less force onto the anterior column, posterior column, and quadrilateral surface [6]. The Stryker EZout (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) is one such powered system that uses a semicircular oscillating blade to dissect the bone-implant interface [7], while the Kincise system from Johnson & Johnson has recently introduced an acetabular osteotome to aid in revision cup removal (Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) [8].

While studies have measured the force placed onto the pelvis, there are scarce data on the amount of force and vibration acetabular extraction puts onto the surgeon. While both primary and revision hip arthroplasty surgeries are known to be physically demanding, the scale of how demanding they can be is only recently being quantified [9]. In a study by Alqahtani et al., back pain was rated as the most frequent work-related complaint by adult reconstruction surgeons, followed by lateral epicondylitis, shoulder tendonitis, and lumbar disc herniations [10]. In their study, 27% of adult reconstruction surgeons reported taking time off work because of an injury sustained at work [10]. As the demand for primary total hip arthroplasty and surgeon caseloads of rTHA increase, there is an urgency to improve the ergonomics of how these procedures are performed.

Though acetabular extraction technology impacts a crucial step in rTHA, there are limited comparative data on the forces each of these devices require in the hands of surgeons or on the amount of operative time taken for removal. The purpose of this study is to compare the ergonomic measurements such as force and vibration each of these systems demand in both novice and expert surgeons utilizing in vitro models. The results of this study may then inform surgeons of how these devices affect their own bodies as well as the surgical outcome of their patients.

Material and methods

A total of 72 composite foam blocks with a foam density of 30 pounds per cubic foot and with an implanted 54-mm hemispherical acetabular shell were used to emulate a well-fixed cementless construct. The acetabular cups were press-fitted using foam adhesives. The foam block was mounted on fixed platform that contained a force sensor, and video was utilized to capture the beginning and end of the removal process to verify the time taken for each trial.

Three board-certified, fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons and 3 orthopaedic residents dawned force- and acceleration-measuring sensors and performed 3 trials each using both a powered extraction device (EZX, Brasseler, Savannah, GA) [11] and manual extractor (Cup-X, Innomed, Savannah, GA) [12] on a composite foam block. Acceleration (back, left hand, right hand), acting as a proxy for vibrations felt, applied force, and time to extraction were measured using sensors and video verification. Acceleration was utilized as a proxy for vibration, defined here as the mechanical jarring and impulsive forces transmitted to the surgeon’s body during the extraction process. This term encompasses both the high-frequency oscillations of the powered tool and the lower-frequency, high-amplitude accelerations inherent to manual tool use.

A linear mixed-effects mode was fitted for each outcome (back, left, and right acceleration, as well as force and time) to assess the effects of device (old vs new) and experience (novice vs expert), with a random intercept for surgeon to account for repeated measures. Fixed-effect coefficients are reported with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values. Also included are the variance components, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and both marginal and conditional R2.

Results

Use of the manual extraction device was associated with significantly greater ergonomic burden compared to the powered device across several domains:

Back acceleration (vibration)

The manual device resulted in significantly higher back acceleration, with an estimated increase of 0.55 units (95% CI: 0.49-0.61; P < .001), regardless of surgeon experience (Table 1) (Fig. 1).

Applied force

Surgeons using the manual device applied significantly more force compared to the powered device (Estimate: 5.75; 95% CI: 4.04-7.47; P < .001) (Table 1).

Time to component removal

The manual device also took significantly longer to remove the cup (estimate: 61.31 s longer; 95% CI: 48.04-74.58; P < .001) (Table 1) (Fig. 2).

Time utilized by each participant using each device.

Left and right hand acceleration

There were no statistically significant differences in left-hand (estimate: −0.46; 95% CI: −1.05 to 0.12; P = .118) or right-hand acceleration (estimate: −0.13; 95% CI: −0.89 to 0.63; P = .727) between the 2 devices (Table 1).

Effect of surgeon experience

No significant differences were found between novice and expert users for any metric (eg, force estimate: −1.03; P = .696; time estimate: 1.25; P = .885), suggesting device performance was consistent regardless of experience level (Table 1).

Model performance and variance

Substantial between-surgeon variability was observed, particularly in right-hand acceleration (ICC = 0.74) and back acceleration (ICC = 0.54).

The models demonstrated strong fit, with marginal R2 of 0.67 and conditional R2 of 0.85 for back acceleration, indicating that both device type and surgeon-level factors contributed meaningfully to observed outcomes.

Discussion

The removal of a well-fixed acetabular cup remains a demanding task that often requires significant surgeon effort and time to be completed safely and efficiently [13]. This study quantified this effort by comparing the amount of acceleration felt by the surgeon (as a proxy for vibration), force required by the surgeon, and time taken for removal. The manual device was found to have significantly higher back vibration and applied force when compared to the powered device. The time taken for cup removal was also significantly increased by a factor of 5 with the manual device. These differences were comparable between the novice and expert users. This is the first study which has quantified the differences in ergonomic measurements felt by surgeons performing acetabular extraction with both powered and manual devices.

These results support the ergonomic superiority of the powered acetabular extraction device in the removal of well-fixed implants. As these differences are appreciated in both novices and experts, surgeons of different skill levels may benefit regardless of where they are in the learning curve for these tools. While this study did not directly measure bone loss, previous studies have found that there is either less bone loss with powered device, or equivocal bone loss between powered and manual devices [6]. Each trial with the powered device during the present study resulted in an extracted cup with a thin and fine layer of foam representing bone. The study by Chung et al. described similar findings, and while the manual device in their study did often result in similar post-explant bone defects, the bone loss was much less consistent and there was an occasional large fragment of bone removed with the cup [6].

Time is also a significant factor with regard to arthroplasty surgery. A longer operative time is not only associated with higher blood loss and greater anesthesia risk but is also associated with higher infection risk [14,15]. The manual device in this study took 5 times as long to remove the cup as compared to the powered device. This is more forgiving as compared to the study by Chung et al. who described the powered device as taking on average of 36 seconds compared to 10 minutes for their manual device [6]. These findings underscore a major ergonomic advantage of the powered device: it reduces musculoskeletal strain on the surgeon. As back pain and overuse injuries are among the most frequently reported occupational hazards for adult reconstruction surgeons, technologies that mitigate these physical demands are critical. The decreased time and effort afforded by the powered device may not only reduce intraoperative complications associated with prolonged operative time but also protect surgeon health and longevity, particularly for high-volume arthroplasty surgeons [16].

This study does include some limitations. The composite foam block was utilized in this study, which has limitations as to how it may emulate a pelvis with a well-fixed acetabular cup. While a true well-fixed cup relies on porous bony ingrowth as a means for biological fixation, the model in the study relies on an adhesive. Inevitably, this could lead to variance in the functionality of each given device. However, these models do have a good track record in comparing surgical techniques in vitro, and the results of this study are comparable to prior completed publications [3,6]. Another limitation of this study is that all models included a well-fixed cup, which may limit the generalizability of these data. For example, it may not be clear whether one device is superior to the others in cups with mild loosening from osteolysis or osteomyelitis. All cups were also implanted without screws, which is another common consideration for revision arthroplasty. However, each of these devices would require the prior removal of the poly insert and screws before their use, so this would be unlikely to greatly affect the results. Another limitation is that bone loss was not measured in this present study. Utilizing an adhesive to emulate bony ingrowth limits the ability to measure bone loss, and thus a different model may be helpful in future studies to better measure this variable.

Conclusions

A powered acetabular extraction tool reduced back vibrations, applied force, and time required for acetabular cup removal compared to a manual device. These benefits were consistent across both novice and expert surgeons. Given the physical demands of revision arthroplasty, powered extraction systems may improve surgical ergonomics and efficiency without compromising usability for less experienced surgeons.
