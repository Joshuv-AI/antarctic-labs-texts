Mind the gap! Typing Escherichia coli O157:H7 in the pre- and post-genomic revolution eras
Authors: Ching-Ying J. Poh, David R. Greig, Marie Anne Chattaway, Derren Ready, Claire Jenkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC) serotype O157:H7 emerged in the 1980s as a cause of foodborne outbreaks of infectious intestinal disease. Over the last decade, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has replaced phage typing (PT) as the typing method of choice to detect outbreaks and monitor trends of STEC O157:H7. The aim of this study was to correlate historical PT data with WGS outputs and to describe the continuous trends in STEC O157:H7 notifications in England over a 40-year period from 1985 to 2024.At the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA), both PT and WGS typing schemes were in use for a 6-year period from 2016 to 2021, providing an opportunity to compare the two typing datasets. We focused on the three major phage types, PT2, PT21/28 and PT8, as the numbers of other phage types were comparatively low and did not make a notable impact on the trends. Lineage and sub-lineage typing data from 2016 to 2024 were superimposed on PT data from 1989 to 2021.Between 2016 and 2021, 3,229 isolates of STEC O157:H7 referred to UKHSA for typing were linked to PT, Shiga toxin (stx) subtype and WGS data. For PT21/28 stx2, there was a good correlation between sub-lineage Ic (n=495/499, 99.2%). PT8 stx2 isolates were associated with sub-lineage IIb (n=215/262, 82.1%), and PT8 stx1+2 isolates aligned with sub-lineage IIc (n=851/857, 99.3%). Notifications of PT2 stx2 were low, and the correlation with lineage I/II during the overlapping time frame was less robust (n=48/85, 56.5%), although analysis of historical data indicated that the association was stronger when PT2 notifications were at their peak in the 1980s and 1990s.We extrapolated the sub-lineage data for the four major STEC O157:H7 PT and stx profiles in the UK, to bridge the gap across the pre- and post-genomic revolution eras and to facilitate future trend analyses based on sub-lineage alone. We created a window to the past to review the WGS data in the context of the historical PT data. This enabled us to link common exposures, learn from previous outbreak investigations and predict, investigate and prepare for emerging threats to public health.

Impact Statement

To maintain backwards compatibility with historical typing data linked to STEC O157:H7 following the implementation of whole-genome sequencing (WGS), we reviewed a large dataset comprising phage typing (PT), Shiga toxin gene (stx) typing and WGS data to determine whether PT and stx profile could be used as a proxy marker of WGS-derived lineage and sub-lineage. We showed that PT2 stx2, PT21/28 stx2, PT8 stx2 and PT8 stx1+stx2, the four dominant types of STEC O157:H7 in the UK, correlated with lineage I/II, and sub-lineages Ic, IIb and IIc, respectively. This enabled us to monitor and describe the continuous trends in STEC O157:H7 notifications in England over a 40-year period from 1985 to 2024.

Since the 1980s, the emergence of dominant sub-lineages was most likely driven by the acquisition of bacteriophage-encoded stx2a, known to be associated with more severe clinical outcomes, and more efficient transmission of STEC O157:H7 within the animal reservoir. We showed that the overall decline in STEC O157:H7 infections over the last decade was driven by the decrease in cases infected with the highly pathogenic sub-lineage Ic (historically PT21/28). We demonstrated that the recent uptick in notifications of STEC O157:H7 since the COVID-19 pandemic is due to the increased detection of sub-lineage IIc (historically PT8 stx1+stx2), associated with both travellers’ diarrhoea and outbreaks caused by contaminated fresh produce, and the increased detection of the ovine sub-lineage IIb (historically PT8 stx2).

Linking PT to sub-lineages enables us to monitor and determine the drivers of the overarching trends over a 40-year time frame spanning the pre- and post-genomic revolution era. Using these data, we can predict, investigate and prepare for emerging threats to public health, establish sub-lineage specific animal reservoirs and transmission routes and learn from previous outbreak investigations.

Introduction

The Gastrointestinal Bacteria Reference Unit (GBRU) is a national specialist reference laboratory within the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA), with a remit to provide microbiological typing data for a range of gastrointestinal pathogens in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. These data are used to detect and investigate foodborne outbreaks and to monitor trends in gastrointestinal infections, including Salmonella and Shigella species and Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC). Historically, typing schemes for these pathogens were based on a combination of phage typing (PT) and serotyping [15]. Molecular typing techniques, such as pulsed field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) and multi-locus variable number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA), were carried out on a small proportion of isolates and were used to confirm microbiological relatedness between isolates from cases where an epidemiological link had already been established [69].

STEC emerged in the 1980s as a cause of outbreaks of the systemic, life-threatening condition haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) characterised by renal failure and neurological and cardiovascular sequelae, as well as severe gastrointestinal symptoms, including bloody diarrhoea, abdominal pain and vomiting [10]. STEC are zoonotic and can be transmitted to humans via direct contact with animals or their environment, or by consumption of contaminated food or water. In the UK, the animal reservoir is ruminants, mainly cattle and sheep. The early outbreaks in the 1980s were caused by STEC serotype O157:H7, and the surveillance system was focused on this serotype [1113]. As the number of notifications of STEC O157:H7 increased, PT and Shiga toxin gene (stx) typing were implemented in 1989 to provide a more discriminatory level of typing [1415]. This approach was rapid and cost-effective, as well as informative, and was used at GBRU for over 30 years to detect outbreaks and monitor trends [16].

In 2015, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was implemented as the first-line typing method at GBRU [5]. The WGS data can be mined for information about virulence factors (including stx typing), antimicrobial resistance genes and the genetic relatedness of strains [5,1719]. WGS is a powerful tool for investigating outbreaks of STEC O157:H7, offering higher resolution and more accurate strain typing than PT and fragmented-based molecular methods, such as PFGE or MLVA [9]. Analysis of WGS data provides a more discriminatory approach to outbreak detection and case ascertainment, informs outbreak investigations, facilitates public health interventions and elucidates a better understanding of the evolution and pathogenicity of STEC strains.

At UKHSA, although the intention was always to withdraw PT, it was maintained until 2022 because it was rapid and cost-effective, and health protection practitioners were familiar with the nomenclature. The overlap in PT and WGS technologies also provided an opportunity to bridge the gap of transitioning from the pre- to post-genomic revolution eras. There are three main lineages of STEC O157:H7 (I, II and I/II) and six sub-lineages, Ia, Ib, Ic, IIa, IIb and IIc. The primary aim of this study was to assess the evidence base for aligning PT and stx typing with WGS outputs, specifically STEC O157:H7 lineages and sub-lineages, and, secondly, to describe the trends in STEC O157:H7 notifications in England over a 40-year period from 1985 to 2024.

Methods

Bacterial strains

Strains of STEC O157:H7 described in this study were isolated from faecal specimens following culture on cefixime-tellurite sorbitol MacConkey agar, from hospital and community cases of gastrointestinal disease submitted to local hospital laboratories in England. Isolates of STEC O157:H7 were submitted to the GBRU at UKHSA for serotyping, stx typing and PT (traditional methods) from 1985 to 2021, and for WGS from 2016 to 2024.

Traditional methods: serotyping, Shiga toxin profiling and PT

Serotyping is a process that types E. coli based on their surface antigens, specifically lipopolysaccharide O (somatic) and flagella H antigens. Antisera to the O and H antigens were raised in rabbits and added to cultures of E. coli to determine whether or not agglutination reactions occur [2].

Over the decades, the detection of stx1 or stx2 was initially performed using DNA hybridization probes, followed by a block-based PCR, which was replaced in 2012 with real-time PCR targeting stx1 or stx2 [2023].

Phage stocks and control strains for the phage-typing scheme developed in Canada were kindly supplied by H. Lior, Ottawa, Canada [2425]. Cultures for phage-typing were grown in nutrient broth for 18 h at 37 °C, and 0.1 ml was inoculated into 3 ml of nutrient broth and grown for 1–2 h at 37 °C with aeration. Plates were inoculated by flooding with 2 ml of the fresh culture, and, when dry, 0.01 ml samples of phage at routine test dilution were applied. Plates were read after overnight incubation at 37 °C [14].

Whole-genome sequencing

At GBRU, the cultures were inoculated into nutrient broth and propagated overnight at 37 °C. Genomic DNA from isolates of STEC O157:H7 was extracted on the QiaSymphony system (Qiagen). The sequence library was prepared using the Nextera XT kit and sequenced on the HiSeq 2500 and NextSeq 1000 platforms (Illumina). Trimmomatic (v0.36) [26] was used to trim sequence adapters. Reads that fell below a PHRED score of 30 or if the read length was less than 50 bp, the read and its pair were discarded.

High-quality reads were aligned to the reference STEC O157:H7 strain, Sakai (GenBank accession BA000007), using BWA MEM (v0.7.2) [27]. The sequence alignment map outputs from BWA were sorted and indexed to produce a binary alignment map using Samtools (v1.1) [28]. Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v2.6.5) was then used to call variants from each sample’s alignment, which were further filtered to extract only SNP positions of high quality [mapping quality >30, depth >10 and variant ratio >0.9] [29]. Any variants called at positions that were within the known prophages in Sakai were masked from further analyses. The remaining variants were imported into SnapperDB v0.2.8 [30]. Hierarchical single-linkage clustering was performed on the pairwise SNP difference between all isolates at descending distance thresholds (Δ250, Δ100, Δ50, Δ25, Δ10, Δ5 and Δ0) [30]. The result of the clustering is an SNP profile, or SNP address. Lineage and sub-lineage assignments were performed based on discriminatory SNPs, extracted directly from SnapperDB v0.2.8, that define the population structure, as described previously [530].

Serotypes were derived from the genome data using GeneFinder (https://github.com/ukhsa-collaboration/gene_finder), based on the SerotypeFinder database [31], and the best match to each of the O and H determinants was reported, as described previously [2]. Sequence type assignment was performed using the Metric Oriented Sequence Typer (https://github.com/phe-bioinformatics/MOST). Shiga toxin (stx) subtyping was performed as previously described [17].

The sequences for all isolates in this study were uploaded to the National Center for Biotechnology Information, Sequence Read Archive BioProject PRJNA248042.

Results

Description of the dataset

This study describes a total of 25,831 isolates of STEC O157:H7 received by GBRU over a 40-year period from 1985 to 2024, summarized in Table 1. Prior to 1989, there were 155 isolates from sporadic cases in the GBRU archive that were serotyped at the time of receipt, and retrospectively stx typed and phage typed in 1989 [1416]. In 2014, 29 out of 155 (18.7%) were retrospectively sequenced [5] (Table 1). There were 20,407 isolates of STEC O157:H7 that were referred to GBRU from local and regional hospital microbiology laboratories from 1989 to 2015. All isolates were serotyped, stx typed and phage typed at the time of receipt [16], and in 2014, 686 out of 20,407 (3.4%) of isolates were randomly selected for sequencing as part of a pilot study prior to the implementation of genome sequencing the following year [832] (Table 1). From 2016 to 2021, there were 3,242 isolates of STEC O157:H7 referred to GBRU. All isolates were phage typed and genome sequenced on receipt (Tables 1 and S1). In 2022, PT was withdrawn, and the isolates from 2022 to 2024 (n=2,027) were linked to genome sequencing data only (Tables 1 and S1).

PT data, 1989–2021

Prior to 1989, the most common phage types were PT2 (31.6%), PT1 (24.5%) and PT49 (14.2%) [14]. The PT data trends from 1989 to 2012 were previously reviewed by Adams et al. [16] and are briefly summarized here for completeness. From 1989, the proportions of PTs changed annually, and by 1996, PT2 (37%), PT8 (12.9%) and a new PT, designated PT21/28 (13.9%), were the most common PTs, and the proportion of PT1 (4.2%) and PT49 (6.4%) declined (Fig. 1). Over the next 15 years (1997–2012), the proportion of isolations of PT2 declined steadily, and PT1 and PT49 were rarely observed (Fig. 1). Concurrently, notifications of PT21/28 rapidly increased, accounting for 44.1% cases by 2005, and remained the most frequently detected PT until 2010 (Fig. 1). STEC O157:H7 PT8 notifications had been increasing steadily since 1995 and by 2011, and PT8 was the most common type (Fig. 1) [33].

Proportions of the most common phage types (PTs) and total counts of STEC O157 identified in England, 1989–2021.

Despite the introduction of WGS for all isolates of STEC O157:H7 referred to GBRU in 2016, PT was performed until 2021. The proportion of isolates belonging to PT21/28 continued to decline from a peak of 44.1% in 2005 to 14.6% in 2021. STEC O157:H7 PT8 remained the most frequently detected PT overall, fluctuating between 25.9% in 2016 and 41.6% in 2021. Other PTs were detected, including PT1, PT4, PT14, PT32 and PT49; however, the proportions were low (less than 10%) (Fig. 1) [34]. PT8 was the most frequently detected PT, followed by PT21/28, while PT2 was rarely observed (Fig. 1).

Comparison of PT and sub-lineage, 2016–2021

Between 2016 and 2021, 3,229 out of 3,242 (99.6%) isolates of STEC O157:H7 had both PT and WGS results available, and the association of PT, stx subtype and sub-lineage was compared for PT2, PT8 and PT21/28 (n=1699) (Table 2). We focused on the three main phage types, PT2, PT21/28 and PT8, as the numbers of other phage types were low and did not have a notable impact on the trends (Fig. 1). Lineage and sub-lineage typing data from 2016 to 2024 were superimposed on PT data from 1989 to 2021 (Figs 2 and S1–S3).

Between 2016 and 2021, 507 isolates were typed as PT21/28 (stx2 n=499, 98.4%; stx1+stx2 n=8, 1.6%). For PT21/28, there was good correlation between PT, stx subtype and sub-lineage assignment, with almost all isolates of PT21/28 belonging to sub-lineage Ic (n=495/499, 99.2%) (Table 2 and Fig. S1).

Between 2016 and 2021, 1,133 isolates were identified as PT8, of which 857 (75.6%) isolates had stx1+stx2, 262 (23.1%) had stx2 only, and 14 (1.2%) had stx1. PT8 stx2 isolates were primarily associated with sub-lineage IIb (n=215/262, 82.1%), followed by sub-lineage IIa (n=39/262, 14.9%) (Table 2 and Fig. S2). PT8 stx1+stx2 isolates exhibited good correlation with sub-lineage IIc (n=851/857, 99.3%) (Table 2 and Fig. S2).

Between 2016 and 2021, notifications of STEC O157 PT2 were relatively low, averaging 14 per year. Of the 86 isolates identified as PT2, 85 had stx2 (98.8%), and 1 had stx1+stx2. Although most isolates of PT2 stx2 fell within lineage I/II (n=48/85, 56.5%), the correlation between PT and sub-lineage was less consistent, with 38 isolates falling into sub-lineages IIb (n=14/85, 16.5%), Ia or IIa (n=9, 10.6%) (Table 2 and Fig. S3).

Sub-lineage typing data, 2016–2024

Superimposing the sub-lineage data onto the four major phage types/stx profiles, PT2 stx2, PT21/28 stx2, PT8 stx2 and PT8 stx1/stx2, illustrated the correlation with lineage I/II and sub-lineages Ic, IIb and IIc, respectively, thus enabling us to continuously monitor the trend analyses moving forward, based on sub-lineage alone (Fig. 2). By extrapolating the data, we were able to show that the proportion of isolates belonging to sub-lineage I/II has continued at base-line levels, while the proportions of the declining sub-lineage Ic, despite stabilizing from 2019 to 2023, decreased again in 2024 (Fig. 2). A surge of notifications of the emerging sub-lineage IIb in 2022 was caused by a large outbreak traced back to contaminated salad leaves [35]. After the outbreak, the proportions of isolates belonging to sub-lineage IIb fell, although they continued to be higher than sub-lineage Ic. With the exception of 2022, sub-lineage IIc has been the most commonly detected sub-lineage since 2013, and in 2024, over 40% of isolates of STEC O157:H7 referred to GBRU belonged to this sub-lineage (Fig. 2).

Discussion

To maintain backward compatibility with historical typing data following the implementation of WGS, we reviewed a large dataset comprising PT, stx typing and WGS data to determine whether PT and stx profile could be used as a proxy marker of sub-lineage. In the pre-WGS era, PT2, PT21/28 and PT8 were the dominant types of STEC O157:H7 [516]. In this study, using data collated during 2016–2021, we demonstrated a good correlation between PT and stx type, and lineage and sub-lineage, specifically PT2/stx2 and lineage I/II, PT21/28 stx2 and sub-lineage Ic, PT8 stx2 and sub-lineage IIb and PT8/stx1+stx2 and sub-lineage IIc. Linking the three most common phage types to sub-lineages enables us to record trends in notifications of STEC O157:H7 in England over a 40-year time frame spanning the pre- and post-genomic revolution eras.

Monitoring the underlying STEC O157:H7 PT data, and, more recently, sub-lineage data, enables us to better understand the drivers of the overarching trends. From 2011 to 2021, despite the widespread implementation of PCR for the detection of STEC in England, which is a more sensitive method of detection, notifications of STEC O157:H7 have declined. The decline in STEC O157:H7 infections appears to mirror the decrease in cases infected with sub-lineage Ic (historically PT21/28) [36]. Cases infected with PT21/28 more frequently develop HUS [3738], and the decline in sub-lineage Ic may be driving an overall decrease in the number of individuals infected with STEC O157:H7 presenting to primary healthcare [39]. That said, STEC O157:H7 belonging to sub-lineages IIb and IIc, although less likely to cause HUS [37], often cause severe gastrointestinal symptoms, and reports of bloody diarrhoea and hospitalization among patients infected with STEC O157:H7 have remained stable [3639].

The trend data capture a sharp decline in notifications from 2020 to 2021, which has been previously linked to the restrictions implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic, in line with other gastrointestinal pathogens [4041]. Furthermore, similarly to other gastrointestinal pathogens, the notifications of STEC O157:H7 rapidly increased in 2022, most notably for sub-lineages IIb and IIc. Our analysis showed that the dramatic uptick in cases post COVID in 2022 was driven by the large outbreak of STEC O157 sub-lineage IIb, caused by consumption of contaminated salad leaves [35]. In 2024, increasing trends in notifications of sub-lineages IIb and IIc contributed to the overall numbers of STEC O157:H7 being higher than they have been since 2018.

Over the decades, the spikes in the STEC O157:H7 trend data are often driven by outbreaks. A summary of outbreaks of STEC O157:H7 that occurred in England before 2013 was documented by Adams et al. [16]. Since 2013, we investigated outbreaks of lineage I/II in 2013 and 2017, linked to spinach leaves and undercooked beef burgers, respectively [4244]. Outbreaks of STEC O157 sub-lineage IIc caused by handling raw vegetables (leeks and potatoes) occurred in 2011 and contaminated cucumber in 2020 (an ingredient of a fast-food meal) [4546]. The peak of sub-lineage IIc in 2014 was associated with multiple small clusters, epidemiologically linked to contaminated salad leaves (UKHSA in-house data). The emerging sub-lineage IIb has been associated with two outbreaks caused by the consumption of contaminated salad leaves in 2015 and 2022 [3547], and an outbreak linked to exposure during a mud obstacle event, to an environment contaminated with ovine faeces in 2018 [48]. Foodborne outbreaks in England since 2012 caused by sub-lineage Ic have been linked to contaminated unpasteurized milk, meat products and raw pet food [34,4953].

The big picture view of STEC O157:H7 in England illustrates the rise and fall of five different sub-lineages starting with the emergence of sub-lineage Ia and lineage I/II in the 1980s, followed by dominance of sub-lineage Ic from 1995 to 2010, and, more recently, the emergence of sub-lineages IIc and IIb [1416,36, 54, 55]. In the post-genomic revolution era, we captured the emergence of sub-lineage IIb and were able to describe the genome-level evolutionary events. We showed that strains of STEC O157:H7 sub-lineage IIb harbouring stx2c had been circulating in the animal reservoir, most likely specifically the UK sheep population, since the 1990s. We concluded that the emergence of sub-lineage IIb was linked to the acquisition of bacteriophage-encoded stx2a, known to be associated with more severe clinical outcomes, and may increase the efficiency of STEC O157:H7 transmission between animals and restricts [54, 56]. We speculated that the emergence of I/II in the 1980s and Ic in the 1990s was also driven by the acquisition of stx2a by strains of E. coli O157 (with either no stx or with stx2c) already endemic in the UK cattle and sheep populations [3257]. Historically, the stx2a toxin subtype has been rarely detected in sub-lineage IIc [58]. It has been suggested that certain clades of sub-lineage have become established in the UK ruminant population relatively recently, introduced by returning travellers and/or imported food or animals [58]. STEC O157:H7 sub-lineage IIc remains a travel-associated lineage [1936] and has been associated with outbreaks linked to contaminated fresh produce, from both imported and domestically cultivated sources [4546 58 59].

The reasons for the decline of lineage I/II and sub-lineages Ia and Ic over the last 40 years are unclear. Despite the high numbers recorded in the 1990s, notifications of cases infected with lineage I/II have been at base-line levels for the last 10 years. Historically, the decline of lineage I/II has been linked to the culling of cattle during the 2001 foot and mouth epidemic, distorting the ecology of STEC O157:H7 in cattle and providing an opportunity for strains belonging to sub-lineage Ic to occupy the vacant niche [16]. Although the foot and mouth epidemic may have been a contributing factor, our data indicate that the rise of sub-lineage Ic was already in train prior to 2001 and that this emerging sub-lineage was better equipped to out-compete the older lineages. For example, there is evidence to suggest that PT21/28 is a super shedder, thus facilitating transmission between herds of cattle, and from cattle to humans via the environment, and meat and dairy products [5660 61].

Strains belonging to sub-lineage IIc have caused a number of outbreaks in recent years associated with contaminated produce, although the fitness advantage held by this type is less clear given its stx2a-negative status [58]. Preliminary in-house data suggested that sub-lineage IIc isolates were more metabolically active than those belonging to sub-lineage Ic. It has been speculated that, once colonization of the cattle gut has been established, IIc isolates are better able to survive in the environment and contaminate food cultivated in the field, although this remains unproven [62; UKHSA in-house data].

Following the implementation of WGS in 2016, biochemistry and serotyping of STEC at GBRU were withdrawn, although PT of STEC O157:H7 was initially maintained to ease the transition from a phenotypic to genotypic approach, as service users were familiar with the scheme. The reagents and consumables required for PT are inexpensive; however, the staff costs were high as preparation, and maintaining the typing phages was labour-intensive and required a certain skill set and level of expertise, and our intention was always to ultimately withdraw the scheme. Following the transition period, we now have over 9 years of sub-lineage data to facilitate monitoring of recent trends. By overlapping the two typing schemes, we have created a window to the past that enables us to review the WGS data in the context of the historical PT data, establish common exposures, learn from previous outbreak investigations and predict, investigate and prepare for emerging threats to public health.
