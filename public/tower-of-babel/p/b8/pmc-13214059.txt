Clinical and imaging study of unilateral biportal endoscopy in lumbar revision surgery
Authors: Xiule Fang, Chunliang Guo, Tao Ding, Zhiyun Feng, Dongyu Jiang, Qidong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the surgical technique and evaluate the preliminary clinical outcomes of Unilateral Biportal Endoscopy (UBE) for lumbar revision surgery.MethodsThis retrospective case series included 11 patients who underwent lumbar revision surgery using the UBE technique between 2023 and 2024. Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores for leg pain and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores were recorded preoperatively and postoperatively to assess clinical improvement. Perioperative parameters, including operative time, blood loss, and complications were documented. Clinical outcomes were categorized using the MacNab criteria at the 6-month follow-up. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR).Results All surgical procedures were completed successfully without intraoperative neural injury. The median operative time was 96.0 min (IQR: 79.0–111.5), and the median intraoperative blood loss was 49.0 ml (IQR: 40.0–56.5). No postoperative complications, such as infection or dural tears, were observed. At the 6-month follow-up, the median leg pain VAS score decreased substantially from 7.0 (IQR: 6.0–8.5) preoperatively to 2.0 (IQR: 1.0–2.0). Similarly, the median ODI score improved from 66.0 (IQR: 58.0–74.0) to 30.0 (IQR: 27.0–33.0). According to the MacNab criteria, 90.9% of patients achieved excellent or good outcomes.ConclusionPreliminary findings suggest that the UBE technique is a feasible and safe minimally invasive option for lumbar revision surgery. The procedure achieved satisfactory early clinical outcomes with manageable perioperative trauma. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to validate these initial observations.Keywords: Unilateral biportal endoscopy, Lumbar revision surgery, Minimally invasive spine surgery

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar diseases, including lumbar disc herniation, lumbar spinal stenosis, and lumbar spondylolisthesis, are common conditions encountered in spinal surgery [1]. The associated symptoms, such as low back pain, radiating leg pain, and intermittent claudication, significantly impair patients’ quality of life. For patients who are unresponsive to conservative management or experience progressive symptom exacerbation, surgical intervention is crucial for relieving nerve compression and restoring spinal stability [2]. Current standard clinical approaches include traditional open or minimally invasive procedures, such as discectomy, spinal canal decompression, and vertebral fusion with internal fixation [3]. However, a subset of patients may experience postoperative symptom recurrence, necessitating revision surgery to address persistent pathology and reconstruct spinal function [4].

With the accelerating global aging population [5] and the concomitant rise in the incidence of degenerative lumbar diseases, the annual volume of lumbar surgeries has shown a steady upward trend. This has directly driven a growing clinical demand for lumbar revision surgery. Furthermore, as the interval from the primary surgery lengthens and the proportion of complex cases increases [6], lumbar revision surgery has emerged as a pressing clinical challenge in spinal surgery.

Compared to primary surgery, revision procedures present significantly greater technical challenges. Scar tissue proliferation disrupts anatomical landmarks, thereby heightening the risk of injury to nerve roots and the dura mater. Additionally, bone loss and structural alteration of the vertebrae resulting from the primary surgery further complicate the decompression procedure. Moreover, patients requiring revision surgery are predominantly middle-aged or elderly and often present with comorbidities such as cardiopulmonary, hepatic, or renal insufficiency. Their reduced tolerance to surgical trauma poses substantial challenges for surgical planning and execution [7].

The evolution of medical technology towards minimally invasive, visualized, and intelligent solutions represents the future trajectory of the field. In recent years, with continuous advancements in minimally invasive spinal techniques, Unilateral Biportal Endoscopy (UBE) has been increasingly applied in the treatment of degenerative lumbar diseases. By establishing two independent portals—one for endoscopic observation and the other for instrument manipulation—this technique provides a clearer surgical field of view, free from interference by instrument movements [8]. It enables effective neural decompression and spinal canal enlargement under direct visualization while preserving spinal stability to the greatest extent possible, offering advantages such as minimal trauma, reduced blood loss, and rapid recovery [9].

As UBE technology has matured in recent years, its application has expanded beyond simple discectomy or laminectomy. While current research demonstrates the advantages of UBE in complex spinal surgeries, reports regarding its efficacy, safety, and feasibility specifically in first-time lumbar revision surgery remain scarce. This retrospective study analyzes data from lumbar revision surgeries performed using the UBE technique in our department between 2023 and 2024, aiming to evaluate the radiographic parameters and clinical outcomes of UBE in lumbar revision procedures.

Materials and methods

General data

This retrospective analysis enrolled 11 patients treated with UBE for lumbar revision surgery at the Orthopaedic Department of Wuxi People’s Hospital between June 2023 and June 2024, based on exclusion and inclusion criteria. This study adheres to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As a retrospective study (not a clinical trial), approval from the institutional ethics review board was waived in accordance with local regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, covering publication of treatment details and case specifics, including the use of clinical records and radiographic images.

Inclusion criteria: 1 History of prior open or minimally invasive lumbar surgery, with symptom relief lasting over 6 months, followed by recurrent radiating leg pain that was unresponsive to conservative treatment for 3 months; 2 Confirmation via CT or MRI that the responsible segment causing current radiating leg pain was identical to the previous surgery site; 3 Patients undergoing lumbar revision surgery via UBE.

Exclusion criteria: 1 Presence of significant lumbar instability or spondylolisthesis; 2 Severe scoliosis or kyphotic deformity; 3 Specific pathologies such as lumbar tumors or infections; 4 Follow-up duration of less than 6 months or loss to follow-up.

Given the scar tissue proliferation at the original surgical site, even the traditional UBE approach posed challenges in distinguishing microscopic anatomical structures, increasing surgical difficulty and the risk of intraoperative nerve root injury. Based on preoperative CT three-dimensional reconstruction, a trans-facet-joint surgical approach was planned to bypass the original surgical area and scar tissue. (Fig. 1) Following satisfactory anesthesia, the surgical segment was confirmed under fluoroscopic guidance. Two incisions were made along the L5-S1 left facet joint: one 1.5 cm superior to the midline and one 1.5 cm inferior. The cephalic incision served as the endoscopic channel, while the caudal incision functioned as the working channel. Blunt dissectors were used to retract the paraspinal muscle groups, and radiofrequency electrodes were employed to remove soft tissue from the bony surfaces. Using a burr, bone chisel, and bone forceps, the medial margin of the left L5-S1 facet joint was partially resected. Severe adhesions between the ligamentum flavum, scar tissue, and the nerve root were observed in the primary surgical area, making differentiation difficult. A hook knife and nerve root dissector were carefully employed to release the nerve root, removing as much adhered ligamentum flavum and scar tissue as possible. (Fig. 2) Neural decompression was performed until the medial wall of the pedicle was clearly visualized, ensuring satisfactory nerve root mobility and spontaneous dural pulsation, and a drain was placed. The operation lasted 60 min. Postoperatively, the patient experienced complete resolution of leg pain. Discharged on the second postoperative day, the patient remained symptom-free and able to perform normal daily activities at 6 months postoperatively.

Radiographic imaging revealed collapse of the L5-S1 intervertebral space, the vacuum phenomenon, and stenosis of the left intervertebral foramen. Consequently, decompression of the exiting L5 nerve root was performed using the UBE technique via a transforaminal approach (Fig. 5). Under anteroposterior fluoroscopic guidance, the target site was localized 1 cm lateral to the lateral margin of the pedicle. On the lateral view, centered on the tip of the S1 superior articular process, skin incisions were made 1 cm cranially and 1 cm caudally. Lateral foraminal exposure using UBE constitutes a specialized approach. After initial debridement of the soft tissues, a Kirschner wire was placed into the pars interarticularis under fluoroscopic guidance to obtain a preliminary confirmation of the osseous anatomy. The soft tissues were further cleared with a radiofrequency probe to expose the tip of the S1 superior articular process. The tip of the S1 superior articular process was then thinned intra‑articularly with a burr and resected. After identifying the accessory process cranially, the inferior margin of the transverse process was partially burred medially to expose the lateral wall of the pedicle. Partial bone removal was performed along the lateral margin of the L5 pars interarticularis with a burr to expose the medial border of the pedicle. Between the neural elements and the medial border of the pedicle, the soft‑tissue sleeve formed by the ligamentum flavum and the intertransverse ligament was dissected in a lateral and caudal direction. After releasing the dorsal compression of the nerve root, the root was retracted cranially, when necessary, to expose ventral compressive pathology, such as osteophytes or herniated disc material, which was then burred down or excised. (Fig. 6). After irrigation was discontinued, thorough hemostasis was achieved. A drainage tube was placed, the arthroscope and instruments were withdrawn, and the incisions were sutured to complete the procedure.

Assessment criteria

Preoperative and postoperative Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores were recorded to assess leg pain, and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores were obtained to evaluate leg pain and its impact on activities of daily living. Additionally, perioperative parameters—including operative time, intraoperative blood loss, drainage volumes on postoperative days 1 and 2, total length of hospital stay, and postoperative length of hospital stay—were documented. Clinical efficacy was evaluated using the MacNab criteria at the 6-month follow-up.

Data analysis

Data were managed and analyzed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Due to the small sample size (n = 11) and the descriptive nature of this case series, inferential statistical hypothesis testing (e.g., P-values) was not performed to avoid misleading conclusions associated with limited statistical power. Instead, the analysis focused on the feasibility of the surgical technique and the magnitude of clinical improvement. Continuous variables (e.g., operative time, blood loss, VAS and ODI scores) are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR, 25th–75th percentiles) to describe the central tendency and dispersion of the data. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

Results

This retrospective analysis enrolled 11 patients who underwent lumbar revision surgery using the UBE technique at the Department of Orthopaedics, Wuxi People’s Hospital, between June 2023 and June 2024, based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. The median age was 53.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 47.5–64.0), and the median body mass index (BMI) was 25.4 kg/m² (IQR: 24.4–26.2). All patients successfully completed the surgery. The median operative time was 96.0 min (IQR: 79.0–111.5), and the median intraoperative blood loss was 49.0 mL (IQR: 40.0–56.5). The median postoperative drainage volume was 44.0 mL (IQR: 34.0–54.0) on day 1 and 16.0 mL (IQR: 11.0–22.0) on day 2. Discussion

Degenerative lumbar spine disease is a common and frequently occurring condition among middle-aged and elderly individuals [10]. With the accelerating pace of population ageing, its incidence continues to rise, imposing a significant burden on patients, families, and society [11]. The pathogenesis of this condition is complex, involving multiple factors such as biomechanical imbalance, ageing, genetics, obesity, and metabolic syndrome. Early manifestations typically include mechanical low back pain aggravated by activity and relieved by rest. As degeneration progresses, disc protrusion, facet joint hyperplasia, and ligamentum flavum hypertrophy may cause narrowing of the neural foramen or central spinal canal, leading to unilateral or bilateral sciatica and intermittent claudication. Severe cases may present with foot drop, saddle anesthesia, and urinary or bowel dysfunction [1].

For patients without significant neurological impairment, standard conservative treatment is the preferred approach. Combining non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs with physical modalities (traction, electrotherapy, ultrasound) and core muscle rehabilitation exercises can reduce nerve root inflammation, alleviate pain, and restore spinal biomechanical balance [12]. Surgical intervention is indicated if pain persists following at least 3 months of conservative management or if significant symptoms of nerve root compression develop. Surgical options include lumbar fusion, isolated laminectomy, or discectomy [13].

However, surgery does not represent a permanent solution [14]. On the one hand, ongoing degenerative progression—including recurrent protrusion of residual disc material, facet joint hyperplasia, thickening of the ligamentum flavum, or persistent segmental instability—may lead to recurrent nerve compression within months to years post-operatively. This can manifest as progressively worsening radicular pain or symptoms of spinal canal stenosis [15]. Furthermore, new compressive factors may arise from epidural fibrous scarring, intervertebral space collapse, and degenerative changes in adjacent vertebrae. When systematic conservative treatment fails to alleviate residual or worsening symptoms, and imaging confirms that the responsible lesion correlates with clinical presentation, revision surgery becomes the sole effective approach to halt neurological deterioration, restore stability, and relieve pain.

Initial surgery is frequently accompanied by abnormal scar tissue proliferation and extensive intra-spinal adhesions, thereby increasing the risk and complexity of subsequent procedures. Within three months post-posterior decompression, a dense fibrous lamina 2–5 mm thick forms dorsally to the dura mater. This exhibits a markedly elevated collagen I/III ratio and forms extensive adhesions with the dura, nerve roots, and adjacent vessels, obliterating the normal ‘ligamentum flavum-dura-lamina vertebralis’ anatomical space [16]. Revision surgery necessitates additional resection of bony structures to breach the scar barrier, leading to prolonged operative time, increased blood loss, and heightened risk of iatrogenic instability [17].

In recent years, the UBE technique has matured significantly. Its indications have expanded from early applications in simple discectomy and laminar decompression to encompass complex degenerative conditions, spinal instability, and revision cases. Indeed, UBE technology is rapidly evolving into a comprehensive minimally invasive solution for spinal surgery. In this retrospective cohort study, we explored the application of the UBE technique for revision surgery following primary lumbar procedures, enrolling a total of 11 patients, all patients successfully completed the surgery. The median operative time was 96.0 min (IQR: 79.0–111.5), and the median intraoperative blood loss was 49.0 mL (IQR: 40.0–56.5). The median postoperative drainage volume was 44.0 mL (IQR: 34.0–54.0) on day 1 and 16.0 mL (IQR: 11.0–22.0) on day 2. The median postoperative hospital stay was 6.0 days (IQR: 4.5–7.0), and the median total hospital stay was 7.0 days (IQR: 6.0–8.5). No serious complications, such as mortality or neurological injury, occurred. Assessments using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) demonstrated improvements in pain and functional disability compared to preoperative levels, preliminarily indicating the positive short-term efficacy of the UBE technique in lumbar revision surgery.

In revision surgery, the core challenge lies in safely managing scar tissue while avoiding nerve injury. Compared to single-channel spinal endoscopy, UBE technology separates the observation channel from the working channel, providing a broader field of view and more flexible working angles. Scar tissue exhibits abundant blood supply yet lacks elasticity, rendering it highly susceptible to dura mater laceration upon traction. Kim et al. [18]noted that employing ‘bone-centred manipulation’ and the ‘pull-and-cut technique’ during UBE revision surgery enables safe and effective separation of scar tissue, significantly reducing the risk of dural tear. This technical approach enables surgeons to utilize independent instrument channels for precise manipulation under clear endoscopic vision, facilitating accurate soft tissue management.

Furthermore, for challenging cases following PLIF (Posterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion) where decompression is required without removing internal fixation, Jiang et al. [19] utilized the flexible maneuverability of the UBE to achieve thorough neural decompression by bypassing the screw-rod system while preserving the original internal fixation implants. This approach not only avoids prolonged fusion and additional trauma but also offers a novel surgical strategy for patients where traditional endoscopic procedures are difficult to perform due to obstruction by internal fixation.

UBE technology, with its unique advantages in scar management and preservation of internal fixation, holds promise as a significant therapeutic approach for lumbar revision surgery. It not only addresses recurrent disc herniation and adjacent segment disease-induced neural compression via a minimally invasive approach but also maximizes spinal stability through specific techniques such as contralateral decompression. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small (n = 11), which limits the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Due to this limitation, we refrained from performing inferential statistical testing to avoid overstating the efficacy of the procedure, presenting the results instead as a descriptive technical report. Second, the retrospective design is inherently prone to selection and information bias. Third, the length of hospital stay varied across patients, raising the possibility of a Hawthorne effect, whereby patients with longer hospitalizations might receive more attention and thus report greater subjective improvement. However, we observed that, patients with longer hospital stays did not demonstrate superior clinical improvements compared to those with shorter stays, suggesting that the varied discharge times did not systematically bias the patient-reported outcomes. Furthermore, this study is limited by its retrospective nature, single-center design, and small sample size. Moreover, the follow-up duration was relatively short, and long-term efficacy requires further verification. Consequently, the efficacy and safety of the UBE technique in lumbar revision surgery remain to be confirmed by prospective controlled studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods.
