Associations between anxiety, somatic pain and sleep disturbances in major depressive disorder and chronic pain: A cross‐sectional study with gender‐specific insights
Authors: Cuizhen Zhu, Jian Cheng, He Chen, Junwei Yan, Chao Li, Xiaoming Kong, Jiayi Hu, Jianliang Gao, Loufeng Zhang, Peijun Ju, Qingrong Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) and chronic pain (CP) demonstrate marked sex disparities, with higher prevalence and severity in females. However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.AimsWe aimed to examine the intricate interplay among anxiety, physical pain and sleep disruptions in inpatients with depression and chronic pain, specifically addressing potential gender differences.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study enrolled 122 participants and split them into three groups: the comorbidity group (inpatients with MDD and CP, n = 37), the MDD group (inpatients with MDD without CP, n = 37) and the control group (healthy controls, n = 48). Sex‐stratified piecewise regression tested pain/latent variable threshold effects in depression‐comorbidity subgroups. Generalised additive models (GAMs) with tensor‐product smoothing and S‐splines were used to model associations between a standardised somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity, capturing nonlinear patterns and subgroup‐specific interactions.ResultsThe comorbidity group exhibited greater impairments in pain intensity, anxiety and depressive symptoms, and quality of life compared to other groups. Piecewise regression analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity, with female inpatients in the comorbidity group experiencing higher pain intensity and anxiety severity compared to those in the MDD group. The GAM results showed a peak in pain intensity at a moderate–low threshold in the MDD and comorbidity groups, suggesting an optimal clinical breakpoint (8 points on the somatic anxiety subscore of Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale‐14) for targeted interventions in females.ConclusionsOur findings offer novel insights into the sex‐specific dynamics linking anxiety, somatic pain and sleep disturbances in MDD‐CP comorbidity, providing an empirical foundation for personalised therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Individuals struggling with major depressive disorder (MDD) and concomitant chronic pain (CP) exemplify the intricate relationship between mental and physical health. 1 , 2 , 3 MDD currently affects approximately 3.8% of the global population—over 300 million people—with epidemiological studies demonstrating a persistent annual increase in prevalence. The World Health Organization (WHO) projects that MDD will become the leading contributor to the global disease burden by 2030. 4 MDD and CP exhibit a clinically significant bidirectional association, with comorbidity rates of 30%–60% in pain populations. Defined by persistent symptoms lasting more than 3 months, CP is a pervasive global health challenge, affecting over 1.5 billion individuals and imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens through direct healthcare costs and indirect productivity losses. 5 Notably, empirical studies reveal that this population's distress extends beyond CP and existential despair. These conditions frequently coexist, creating a complex interplay of enduring physical discomfort, depressed mood and clinically significant anxiety accompanied by sleep disturbances. 6 , 7 , 8 In this category, anxiety disorders are often observed both as distinct illnesses and as overlapping symptoms of MDD. 9 Among the most prevalent sleep disturbances are insomnia and other sleep disorders, which exacerbate mood dysregulation and pain perception, leading to a vicious cycle of suffering. 10 Therefore, to fully understand and address the needs of this population, it is critical to consider the combined effects of anxiety and sleep disturbances within the framework of comorbid CP and MDD.

Recent research studies have shifted from examining depression, CP, anxiety and sleep difficulties in isolation to investigating their intricate interconnections. Studies consistently demonstrate that individuals with comorbid MDD and CP exhibit a significantly higher likelihood of developing concurrent anxiety and sleep disturbances than those with either diagnosis alone. 11 Epidemiological research studies further suggest a synergistic effect: when these disorders coexist, their severity tends to amplify, and symptom overlap becomes pronounced. 12 Individuals with CP and MDD often have higher scores on anxiety measures, reflecting generalised anxiety and pain‐specific concerns, and resulting functional limitations. 13 Moreover, this comorbid population exhibits a markedly higher prevalence of sleep disturbances, particularly insomnia. Such disturbed sleep patterns, characterised by difficulty initiating sleep, frequent nocturnal awakenings, and early‐morning arousal, frequently lead to chronic fatigue, heightened irritability and diminished cognitive performance. 14 , 15

Despite these developments, many questions remain. Previous epidemiological studies demonstrate significant sex‐specific differences in the prevalences of anxiety, CP and MDD. 16 , 17 Studies show that the incidence of CP and MDD in females is almost twice that in males, and these differences persist across all age groups, particularly for pain and depressive symptoms, which are more frequent or severe in women. 17 , 18 The higher prevalence of MDD and CP in females may stem from sex‐specific triggers. Preclinical and clinical studies indicate that the mechanisms underlying MDD and CP differ between sexes: depressed females tend to experience more prolonged or recurrent episodes, with earlier onset and lower overall quality of life. 19 Additionally, depressed females often exhibit greater weight gain, higher levels of anxiety and more pronounced physical symptoms compared to their male counterparts. In contrast, depressed males are more likely to exhibit externalising disorders, such as substance use, impulse control issues and antisocial personality disorders. 20 Despite these observed differences, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Given the divergent symptom profiles, tailored therapeutic approaches may be necessary; however, sex‐specific treatments for MDD and CP have yet to be developed. 21 , 22

A comparative analysis of sex differences could enhance our understanding of individual susceptibility to MDD and CP, thereby improving treatment and prevention of pain across all populations. 23 , 24 However, existing research studies often overlook the influence of social, environmental and cultural factors on the development and persistence of these conditions. Additionally, a significant gap remains in translating research findings into clinical practices, hindering the implementation of evidence‐based interventions. Therefore, understanding how individual differences—such as gender differences, personality, coping strategies, and life events—affect symptom severity and progression is crucial for developing targeted treatments.

To further investigate gender differences in individuals with comorbid MDD and CP, we conducted a three‐group cross‐sectional study involving healthy controls, inpatients with MDD, and inpatients with comorbid MDD and CP. Symptom assessments were obtained using both validated self‐report instruments and comprehensive psychiatric evaluations. Standardised associations between the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity were examined using generalised additive models (GAMs) with tensor‐product smoothing and S‐spline functions. This analytic approach allowed us to characterise nonlinear interaction patterns and to explore the interrelationships among depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms and insomnia, thereby providing insight into the potential mechanisms linking these symptom domains. Furthermore, we examined the intricate relationship among anxiety, physical pain and sleep disruptions in inpatients with comorbid MDD and CP, specifically addressing potential gender differences. These findings may offer promising avenues for improving the overall quality of life in this patient population.

METHODS

Participants

Anhui Mental Health Centre (AMHC) conducted this cross‐sectional study from January 2019 to December 2024. The medical ethics committee of AMHC authorised this study; every participant gave written consent to participate in this study in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. Following the study protocol, two qualified professional psychiatrists used the Chinese version of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (C‐MINI) 5.0.0 for evaluation. Initially, 212 participants were screened: 66 healthy people were screened from the hospital's physical examination centre, and 146 depressive inpatients were screened from AMHC. Among inpatients with MDD, 15 did not complete the evaluation, 15 refused to provide written informed consent, 24 did not meet the study's requirements, and 18 withdrew their consent. Among the healthy controls, 3 did not complete the evaluation, 11 did not provide written informed consent, 2 did not meet the study's requirements, and 2 withdrew their consent (figure 1). Ultimately, 122 participants were enrolled and split into three groups: a control group (healthy controls, n = 48), a comorbidity group (inpatients with MDD and CP, n = 37) and an MDD group (inpatients with MDD without CP, n = 37). The trial clinical registration number is chiCTR2000029917.

The following were the inclusion criteria for inpatients of the comorbidity group: (1) meeting the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) criteria for MDD and the International Association for the Study of Pain criteria for CP (defined as persistent or recurrent unpleasant sensory experiences lasting over 3 months); (2) aged between 18 and 65 years; (3) having persistent pain not attributable to major somatic illness, inflammatory disease or traumatic physical injury and (4) having a Pain Intensity Numerical Rating Scale (PI‐NRS) score ≥ 3. The following were the inclusion criteria for the MDD group: (1) aged between 18 and 65 years; (2) PI‐NRS < 3; (3) meeting the DSM‐5 criteria for MDD, as confirmed by two independent senior psychiatrists. Healthy individuals were screened from the AMHC physical examination centre, with no history of MDD, CP or other condition meeting the exclusion criteria. The following were the exclusion criteria for every participant: (1) severe somatic or craniocerebral trauma; (2) severe neurological, inflammatory or tumour‐related diseases; (3) substance abuse or other mental disorders; (4) serious physical illness; (5) pregnancy or lactation and (6) modified electroconvulsive therapy administered within 3 months prior to enrolment.

Assessment instruments

Chinese version of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview 5.0.0

The C‐MINI 5.0.0 is the gold standard for diagnosing depressive disorders in China (inter‐rater reliabilities are outstanding, with κ scores over 0.94), demonstrating good psychometric qualities. 25

Hamilton Depression Rating Scale‐24

The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale‐24 (HAMD‐24) stands out as the leading tool for assessing depression globally, renowned for its exceptional specificity in gauging the severity of depressive symptoms. 26 HAMD‐24 (Cronbach's α = 0.91) demonstrated good‐to‐excellent reliability in the current cohort.

Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale‐14

The Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale‐14 (HAMA‐14) is one of the earliest validated instruments for assessing anxiety severity, and it remains a clinical standard. 27 HAMA‐14 (Cronbach's α = 0.88) demonstrated good to excellent reliability in the current cohort. A higher score on the HAMA‐14 indicates more severe symptoms of anxiety.

Pain Intensity Numerical Rating Scale

The PI‐NRS is a self‐report questionnaire designed to gauge the pain intensity with strong construct validity and consistency. Due to its simplicity and user‐friendliness, it has become a cornerstone in assessing clinical CP conditions. Typically measured on an 11‐point scale, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the utmost agony), it provides a standardised means of quantifying pain levels. 28

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) comprises 19 self‐rated questions and assesses a wide range of sleep quality factors. It is divided into seven component scores, each weighted equally on a scale of 0–3. The seven component scores are then summed to produce a PSQI total score, which ranges from 0 to 21. 29 PSQI (α = 0.83) demonstrated good reliability in the current cohort; higher scores indicate worse sleep quality.

The 36‐Item Short Form Health Survey

The 36‐Item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐36) is a widely validated generic quality‐of‐life questionnaire with eight domains (physical functioning, role‐physical, bodily pain, global health, vitality, social functioning, role‐emotional, and mental health). Each score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating better self‐perceived quality of life. 30 In this study, the scale's Cronbach's alpha is 0.89.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted utilising the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26.0; IBM Corporation) and Python 3.9.13. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with statistical significance set as α < 0.05. Data visualisation and model construction were implemented in Python. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated with Levene's test. Differences between the two groups were analysed using a two‐tailed unpaired t‐test. Differences among multiple groups were analysed using one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between severe degrees of depression, pain, anxiety and quality of sleep and life. A polynomial regression approach was used to explore potential nonlinear relationships between specific variables and pain intensity. A piecewise regression analysis stratified by sex was conducted on the somatic anxiety subscore, with median sleep disturbances as the breakpoint. The standardised somatic anxiety subscore was analysed using a generalised additive model (GAM) incorporating tensor‐product smoothing functions. p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographic, depressive properties and pain intensity of the control, MDD and comorbidity groups

The final analysis included 122 participants divided into three groups: 48 healthy controls (20 females, 28 males), 37 MDD inpatients without CP (21 females, 16 males) and 37 MDD inpatients with CP (25 females, 12 males). The three groups did not differ significantly in terms of age, body mass index (BMI), years of education, household income or marital status (p > 0.05). The clinical properties of depression and anxiety were comprehensively assessed by psychiatrist‐evaluated HAMD‐24 and HAMA‐14 scales. Scores from these two scales in the comorbidity group were significantly higher than those in the MDD group (t = 2.188, p = 0.004; t = 2.508, p = 0.012). Pain intensity was also found to be more severe in the comorbidity group than in the MDD group (t = 6.152, p < 0.001). The findings of sleep symptoms revealed variations in the sleep quality (F = 44.921, p < 0.001), sleep onset time (F = 16.902, p < 0.001), sleep duration (F = 8.835, p < 0.001), sleep efficiency (F = 11.188, p < 0.001), sleep disturbance (F = 32.483, p < 0.001), medication (F = 41.280, p < 0.001), and daytime dysfunction (F = 37.347, p < 0.001). The assessment results for the eight dimensions of general quality of life varied across the three groups: physical functioning (F = 17.227, p < 0.001), physical role functioning (F = 93.265, p < 0.001), bodily pain (F = 51.011, p < 0.001), general health (F = 74.418, p < 0.001), vitality (F = 131.701, p < 0.001), social functioning (F = 75.732, p < 0.001), emotional role functioning (F = 105.996, p < 0.001), mental health (F = 123.810, p < 0.001), health change (F = 13.033, p < 0.001), physical health summary (F = 105.457, p < 0.001) and mental health summary (F = 179.505, p < 0.001). Correlations between HAMD‐24, HAMA‐14, pain intensity, sleep quality and quality of life

After comparing the differences in variables (clinical symptoms, sleep status, pain intensity and general quality of life) between MDD inpatients and comorbidity inpatients, we further analysed their interrelationships. In the MDD group, we found significant correlations between sleep and anxiety/pain parameters (figure 2B). Specifically, sleep quality and duration were positively correlated with psychic anxiety (r = 0.508 and 0.500, respectively, both p = 0.001); sleep duration was also associated with HAMA‐14 total scores (r = 0.505, p = 0.001); sleep efficiency demonstrated positive correlations with both the somatic anxiety subscore (r = 0.594, p < 0.001) and HAMA‐14 total scores (r = 0.648, p < 0.001); sleep disturbances were significantly linked to higher pain intensity (r = 0.550, p < 0.001), psychic anxiety (r = 0.518, p = 0.001) and HAMA‐14 total scores (r = 0.501, p = 0.001). Physical health revealed inverse relationships: physical functioning was negatively correlated with HAMD‐24 total scores (r = −0.520, p < 0.001), psychic anxiety (r = −0.587, p < 0.001), the somatic anxiety subscore (r = −0.749, p < 0.001), and HAMA‐14 total scores (r = −0.701, p < 0.001); physical health summary was negatively associated with the somatic anxiety subscore (r = −0.596, p < 0.001) and HAMA‐14 total scores (r = −0.530, p < 0.001).

In the comorbidity inpatients, significant associations were observed between sleep parameters and quality of life/clinical measures (figure 2C). Sleep duration was negatively correlated with emotional role functioning (r = −0.388, p = 0.017), whereas sleep efficiency was positively associated with health change (r = 0.461, p = 0.004) and the somatic anxiety subscore (r = 0.394, p = 0.010). Notably, sleep disturbances exhibited multiple negative correlations, including with physical functioning (r = −0.369, p = 0.020), bodily pain (r = −0.467, p = 0.003), general health (r = −0.530, p < 0.001), social functioning (r = −0.392, p = 0.010), physical health summary (r = −0.576, p < 0.001) and SF‐36 total scores (r = −0.497, p = 0.001), while showing a positive association with pain intensity (r = 0.332, p = 0.040). Daytime dysfunction was negatively linked to physical functioning (r = −0.408, p = 0.010), vitality (r = −0.460, p = 0.004), physical health summary (r = −0.381, p = 0.010), mental health summary (r = −0.392, p = 0.010) and SF‐36 total scores (r = −0.454, p = 0.004). Bodily pain was negatively correlated with the somatic anxiety subscore (r = −0.342, p = 0.010).

Multiple linear regression results by sex in the MDD and comorbidity groups

Furthermore, we used separate multiple regression models for females and males in both the MDD and comorbidity groups to examine how symptom severity, quality of life and sleep status affect pain intensity across sexes. The dependent variable was pain intensity, and the independent variables were bodily pain, sleep disturbances, PSQI total scores, physical functioning, general health, social functioning, physical health summary, SF‐36 total scores, the somatic anxiety subscore and HAMA‐14 total scores. Multiple linear regression analyses revealed that, in female inpatients with MDD, sleep disturbances (β = 1.644, t = 2.651, p = 0.020), PSQI total scores (β = −0.240, t = −2.360, p = 0.030), the somatic anxiety subscore (β = −0.692, t = −2.621, p = 0.020) and HAMA‐14 total scores (β = 0.388, t = 2.262, p = 0.040) significantly impacted pain intensity after adjusting for other independent factors. These results showed that greater pain intensity was correlated with higher levels of anxiety and more severe sleep disturbances. However, neither sleep status, quality of life nor symptom severity showed significant associations with pain intensity in male inpatients with MDD or in the comorbid group across both sexes. Nonlinear somatic anxiety subscore‐pain link in female comorbidity inpatients

The preceding results indicated that pain intensity in females in the comorbidity group did not show significant linear correlations with (1) sleep disturbances, (2) PSQI total scores, (3) the somatic anxiety subscore or (4) HAMA‐14 total scores. These findings suggested either no significant association or a nonlinear relationship between these symptoms and pain intensity in females in the comorbidity group. To investigate potential nonlinear relationships, we conducted polynomial regression analysis using the female inpatients in the comorbidity group (labelled as fCOM) as a new dummy variable. This approach aims to explore the potential nonlinear relationships between specific variables (bodily pain, sleep disturbance, PSQI total scores, physical functioning, general health, social functioning, physical health summary, SF‐36 total scores, the somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14 and HAMA‐14 total scores) and pain intensity. The analysis revealed a significant interaction effect between the somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14 and fCOM (somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14 × fCOM) on pain intensity (β = 0.799, p = 0.040). This finding indicated that the relationship between the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity was significantly different in females in the MDD and comorbidity groups compared to other groups. Specifically, pain intensity in this subgroup showed stronger covariation with somatic anxiety subscore levels, suggesting a unique psychosomatic interaction pattern.

We further examined potential threshold effects of pain‐related variables in female inpatients using piecewise regression, with median sleep disturbance as the breakpoint. This analysis revealed a significant interaction effect between the somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14 and fCOM (somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14 × fCOM) on pain intensity (β = 0.820, p = 0.030), indicating that the impact of the somatic anxiety subscore on pain intensity was significantly stronger in females in the comorbidity group than in the MDD group. However, the observed group difference varied depending on whether sleep disturbance levels were above or below the median, indicating an inconsistent pattern of results. Dynamic somatic anxiety subscore‐pain interaction in female inpatients within the MDD and comorbidity groups

To investigate the complex associations and interaction effects between the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity in females in the MDD and comorbidity groups, we constructed an interaction model using a generalised additive model (GAM) with a tensor‐product smoothing function to analyse the relationship between the standardised somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14 and pain intensity. This method was combined with smoothing splines (S‐splines) to flexibly capture nonlinear relationships, enabling a nuanced exploration of how the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity interact within each subgroup.

For the somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14, the GAM effect curve in female MDD inpatients displayed a peak predicted pain intensity of approximately 3.8, occurring when the HAMA‐14 raw somatic anxiety subscore reached 8 points, followed by a subsequent decline (figure 5A). In contrast, the comorbidity group demonstrated a peak predicted pain intensity of approximately 6.8 (higher than that in the MDD group), corresponding to a HAMA‐14 raw somatic anxiety subscore of 8 and decreasing more gradually compared to the MDD group (figure 5B). Furthermore, regardless of whether they were in the comorbidity group or the depression group, GAM interaction analysis also revealed a significant nonlinear relationship between somatic anxiety scores and predicted pain intensity in both the female inpatients in the MDD group (fMDD) and fCOM groups. Specifically, an inverted U‐shaped trend was observed: pain intensity increased sharply with somatic anxiety until reaching a threshold (inflection point = 8.0), beyond which it began to decline. Notably, the fCOM group exhibited a markedly higher overall pain burden than the fMDD group, with peak predicted pain levels nearly doubling those of the fMDD group (approximately 6.8 vs. 3.8). This suggests that inpatients with fCOM experience significantly greater pain severity at equivalent levels of somatic anxiety and indicates that anxiety‐related hyperalgesia operates differently depending on diagnostic subtype (figure 5C).

DISCUSSION

Main findings

MDD comorbid with pain shows higher prevalence and severity in females, whereas current prevalence estimates primarily come from representative general population studies. Thus, a comparison of MDD with pain among clinical inpatients across sexes will improve our understanding of individual differences. Our research study aimed to characterise the sex‐specific heterogeneity in MDD‐CP comorbidity, providing deeper insights into its potential mechanisms. Our results demonstrated that, in comparison to the control and MDD groups, the comorbidity group had worse quality of life and sleep, as well as more severe depression and anxiety symptoms, and pain intensity. Additionally, the piecewise regression analysis results indicated that the relationship between the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity was nonlinear in the MDD and comorbidity groups, and that the somatic anxiety subscore has a stronger effect on pain intensity in female inpatients in the comorbidity group than in the MDD group. Furthermore, the GAM results showed that at severe somatic anxiety subscore levels, female inpatients in the comorbidity group reported higher pain levels, whereas the timing of the somatic anxiety subscore associated with the intervention was the same across groups.

Numerous studies show that inpatients with comorbid MDD and CP experience poor clinical outcomes. 31 This comorbidity is one of the most common manifestations of mental illness and is strongly associated with severe disability, impaired health‐related quality of life and increased mortality. 14 , 32 , 33 Similarly, the results of this study showed that inpatients in the comorbidity group exhibited significantly more severe symptoms of depression and anxiety, higher perception of pain intensity, more severe sleep disturbances and more impaired quality of life than inpatients in the MDD group. These results further demonstrate that pain represents not merely a nociceptive experience but rather a complex network including depression, anxiety and insomnia, all of which profoundly impair inpatients' quality of life. 34 , 35 , 36 Notably, our results revealed a linear correlation between anxiety levels/sleep disorders and CP in female inpatients in the MDD group, whereas females in the comorbidity group exhibited a nonlinear relationship between anxiety levels and pain. Anxiety level, sleep quality, and severity of depressive symptoms were not associated with CP in male inpatients in either the MDD group or the comorbidity group. These results suggested that the sex differences in pain perception should be considered when developing interventional treatments for this patient population, as this may enhance treatment precision and efficacy.

Epidemiological studies reveal a striking 2–10 times higher prevalence of depressive and anxiety disorders in CP populations compared to the general population. 37 Furthermore, systematic review and meta‐analysis showed that women, younger people and people with nociplastic pain were most likely to have depression and anxiety, suggesting potential biological and psychosocial mediators in this population. 38 A growing body of research suggests that sensory and affective pain manifestations may involve distinct neuroadaptive mechanisms. 39 Neuroimaging studies demonstrate reduced anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) volume in inpatients with MDD comorbid with CP. Our findings further revealed significant sex differences in pain perception, with female inpatients exhibiting heightened pain sensitivity compared to males. In female populations, the somatic anxiety subscore and pain‐related distress demonstrate bidirectional amplification, whereby nociceptive signals exacerbate psychological hypervigilance while heightened anxiety states potentiate neural sensitisation, creating a self‐perpetuating pathophysiological cycle. 22 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 These findings differ from those of earlier research. For example, Robinson et al. report no gender differences in psychological aspects, nor in self‐reported or induced low back pain; however, in their study, both self‐reported and induced low back pain were substantially more strongly associated with pain‐related anxiety in men than in women. 44

This persistent gap in understanding sex‐specific pain processing mechanisms substantially hinders the development of sex‐tailored therapeutic approaches for inpatients experiencing depression with CP comorbidity. 45 Subsequent, polynomial and piecewise regression analyses further indicated a significant nonlinear interaction between the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity in female inpatients in the comorbidity group. At high levels of the somatic anxiety subscore, pain intensity in the comorbidity group may not decrease as much as in the MDD group and may even begin to increase. Additionally, the effect of the somatic anxiety subscore on pain intensity appears to be stronger in the comorbidity group compared to the MDD group, which is consistent with previous studies. 32 The observed disparity remains consistent regardless of whether sleep disturbance levels are above or below the median. Given the established sex differences in mood disorders and CP manifestations, we recommend tailored interventions to optimise clinical outcomes. Our findings demonstrated that clinical interventions were most effective when the raw somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14 reached 8. One plausible explanation for the turning point observed in the fMDD and fCOM group is that the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis desensitisation brought on by persistent anxiety is a tenable explanation for the fMDD breakpoint. Early to moderate worry increases catecholamines and cortisol, which in turn increase nociception through central sensitisation. In line with stress‐induced interoceptive dampening, prolonged severe anxiety reduces cortisol responsiveness and pain perception by blunting HPA function and causing hypocortisolism. This evidence underscored the critical need for (1) systematic research on sex‐specific patterns of CP comorbid with depression/anxiety, (2) standardised clinical screening protocols and (3) equitable access to specialised care across demographic groups.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size may limit the statistical power to detect differences among the three groups. Our preliminary results require further validation through hypothesis‐driven studies with larger cohorts to confirm the sensitive and specific moderating effects of disease characteristics on different stages of MDD comorbid with CP; specifically, subgroup analyses were underpowered in males, limiting generalisability. Future studies should recruit balanced sex ratios. Second, as a cross‐sectional study, it relied solely on subjective sleep symptom assessments through scales and did not incorporate objective measures such as polysomnography or evaluate the efficacy of sleep‐promoting medications in inpatients with insomnia. Future research studies should integrate objective sleep monitoring and medication analysis for more comprehensive insights. Third, although existing evidence highlights strong associations among mood, CP and oestrogen levels in female inpatients, this study did not account for endocrine hormonal influences. Subsequent investigations should address this gap to clarify potential biological mechanisms. Despite these limitations, this study offers deeper insights into the comorbidity of MDD and CP. Furthermore, the observed correlations among depressive/anxiety symptoms, CP, insomnia and quality of life suggested that these factors could serve as valuable screening indicators during initial clinical assessments to identify MDD comorbid with CP. These findings may contribute to optimising therapeutic strategies for inpatients with MDD and CP, ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes.

Implications

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that inpatients with MDD comorbid with CP exhibited significantly more severe depressive symptoms, insomnia, anxiety and quality of life impairments compared to those with MDD alone. A nonlinear relationship between the somatic anxiety subscore and pain intensity was identified in the comorbidity group, with female inpatients reporting higher pain intensity and anxiety severity than those in the MDD group. Notably, the optimal clinical intervention point for female inpatients in both groups was identified at 8.0 points in the raw somatic anxiety subscore of HAMA‐14.
