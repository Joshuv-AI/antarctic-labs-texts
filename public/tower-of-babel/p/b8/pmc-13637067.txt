Hydroxamic Acids as HDAC Inhibitor Drug Leads for Malaria
Authors: Wisam A. Dawood, Jacinta R. Macdonald, Christian Anzenhofer, Tina S. Skinner‐Adams, Robert C. Reid, Thomas Kurz, David P. Fairlie, Katherine T. Andrews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMalaria is a global health threat, with an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 malaria‐associated deaths reported in 2024. Most mortality is due to infection by Plasmodium falciparum parasites, with the highest burden occurring in Sub‐Saharan Africa. Malaria eradication is a global priority; however, progress has been hampered by the lack of broadly applicable and highly effective vaccines and by parasite drug resistance. To address drug resistance, the discovery of drugs with novel modes of action is crucial. Histone/lysine deacetylases (HDACs) are enzymes that control the posttranslational modification of lysine acetylation on histone and non‐histone proteins. Due to their essential roles in regulating key processes in Plasmodium parasites, including transcriptional and developmental control, HDACs are prospective drug targets for malaria. Here, the potential of HDAC inhibitors as antimalarial drug leads is reviewed, including an assessment of antiplasmodial structure‐activity relationships for more than 750 hydroxamic acids with published in vitro activity against P. falciparum. These comparative data provide unprecedented insights into potency, selectivity and physicochemical properties that can help to guide investigations of this chemotype as drug leads for malaria and other parasitic diseases.

Malaria Overview

Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites transmitted to people through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. With an estimated 282 million cases of malaria and 610,000 malaria‐associated deaths in 2024, malaria remains a major cause of global morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. While the majority of cases and deaths occur in sub‐Saharan Africa due to infection with P. falciparum parasites, malaria also has significant health and economic impacts in South‐East Asia, the Americas, the Western Pacific and Eastern Mediterranean regions, largely due to P. vivax infections [1]. Children under the age of five years, pregnant women, immunocompromised individuals, and travelers to endemic areas are at the highest risk of developing severe malaria [3, 4, 5]. Clinical signs of malaria occur during the asexual intraerythrocytic stage of the parasite's development and can range from mild symptoms such as fever, chills and headache, to more severe symptoms including reduced consciousness, severe anemia, respiratory distress, cerebral complications and coma which can result in death [6]. While mortality has decreased since 2000, from 861,000 deaths to 610,000 deaths in 2024, malaria case incidence has plateaued and is beginning to rise, with 282 million cases in 2024 rising 3% from 2023 [1]. If this trend continues, global malaria eradication targets are unlikely to be met [1, 7]. Further, changing temperatures and rainfall patterns are impacting geographical ranges for Anopheles mosquitoes, increasing the risk of future infections [1, 8].

Plasmodium parasites undergo sexual reproduction in female Anopheles mosquitoes as the definitive host, and asexual reproduction in humans or other mammals as the intermediate host, depending on the infecting species [9]. During feeding by an infected mosquito, sporozoites can be inoculated into the blood stream where they migrate to the liver and can invade hepatocytes. Within the exoerythrocytic lifecycle stage, hepatic schizonts containing thousands of merozoites are formed [10]. Following hepatic schizont rupture, merozoites are released into the blood stream where they can invade erythrocytes and begin the asexual intraerythrocytic lifecycle stage, which is responsible for the clinical manifestations of malaria [9, 11]. Within erythrocytes, parasites progress through ring, trophozoite, and schizont developmental forms, with each schizont containing approximately ~8‐32 daughter merozoites [12]. Following schizont rupture, merozoites are released and can invade erythrocytes beginning a new asexual intraerythrocytic cycle, the duration of which varies between Plasmodium species (e.g., ~44–48 h for P. falciparum) [13]. During the asexual intraerythrocytic lifecycle, a subset of parasites may differentiate into male and female gametocytes, which can be taken up by female Anopheles mosquitoes during a blood meal. Within the mosquito, fertilization and sporogony occur, completing the transmission cycle [14]. This complex, multi‐stage lifecycle is tightly regulated at molecular and epigenetic levels, enabling the parasite to adapt to distinct environments and contributing to drug resistance [15, 16, 17], while also providing multiple points to target vaccines and drugs.

There are currently two malaria vaccines approved for use within the African region, both of which target sporozoites during the pre‐erythrocytic stage of the Plasmodium lifecycle. However, these vaccines are only recommended for children, do not give complete protection (~30% for RTS,S/AS01 [18, 19] and ~75% for R21/Matrix‐ M [20]), and their long term efficacy is not yet known [21]. Added to this, P. falciparum drug resistance is compromising the efficacy of most available drugs, including the gold standard artemisinin combination therapies (ACTs) that are recommended for treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria. Resistance to ACTs has now been reported in both South‐East Asia [22, 23, 24] and Sub‐Saharan Africa [15, 16, 17]. While the novel imidazolopiperazine ganaplacid, in combination with lumefantrine, has recently shown promise in clinical trials against drug resistant parasites [25, 26], this would be the first truly novel drug introduced for malaria treatment in decades [27]. New tools are therefore urgently needed within the malaria eradication arsenal including additional vaccines and highly effective new drugs that target Plasmodium parasites with different mechanisms from current options to overcome parasite drug resistance [28]. Here, histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors are reviewed as a potential new drug class for malaria. Furthermore, an antiplasmodial structure‐activity relationship analysis of > 750 hydroxamic acids is presented to encourage and guide the development of this compound class as new drug leads for malaria.

Plasmodium HDACs

Lysine/histone acetyltransferases (KATs/HATs) and lysine/histone deacetylases (KDACs/HDACs) are epigenetic regulatory proteins and pivotal regulators of gene expression in malaria parasites, controlling essential processes such as lifecycle progression, host‐parasite interactions and enabling parasite adaptation to the host environment [29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. In eukaryotes, these enzymes control the reversible modification of lysine acetylation on the N‐terminal tail of histones and of lysine residues on other proteins [34]. This results in alterations to chromatin structure and gene expression, leading to the regulation of a diverse range of cellular processes [35, 36, 37, 38]. Dysregulation of normal HDAC function can therefore have a significant impact on physiological pathways and is associated with cancers [39], immunological disorders [40], inflammatory diseases [41] and neurodegenerative diseases [42]. In Plasmodium, the lysine acetylation state of histones, together with other posttranslational modifications (e.g., methylation [43]), has been shown to be essential for transcriptional control and gene expression [44, 45]. Interfering with normal histone acetylation using HDAC inhibitors or genetic knockdown [31, 46, 47] leads to changes in gene expression that influences parasite lifecycle progression and survival.

There are 18 human HDACs, grouped into four classes [48, 49]. Class I HDACs, which include HDAC1‐3 and HDAC8, require zinc as a co‐factor for activity [48]. Class II HDACs also require zinc as a co‐factor [34, 50] and comprise class IIa (HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC7, HDAC9) and class IIb (HDAC6, HDAC10) members. Class III HDACs comprise seven members, termed silent information regulator 2 (Sir2) proteins (or sirtuins; SIRT1‐7) [50, 51]. These HDACs require nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD + ) rather than zinc as a cofactor for activity and share low homology to class I and II human HDACs [50, 52]. Human HDAC class IV contains only one member, HDAC11, which is zinc‐dependent and has low homology to class I and II human HDACs [48]. In contrast, P. falciparum has five annotated HDACs; three homologs of human class I/II HDACs and two Sir2 proteins (Table 1; Supporting Information: Table S1). In addition, P. falciparum has two putative HDACs which include a pseudogene (gene identification number PFE0328W; Table 1) and an unclassified chromo‐domain‐helicase‐DNA binding protein 1 homolog (PfCHD1; Table 1; Supporting Information: Table S1) [59].

The NAD + ‐dependent PfSir2A and PfSir2B proteins (Table 1; Supporting Information: Table S1) play a role in P. falciparum gene regulation, including directly regulating the promoters of the var multi‐copy gene family (~60 copies per parasite genome) that encode P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) variant antigens [57, 60, 61]. These proteins contribute to antigenic variation, immune evasion and the cytoadhesion of infected erythrocytes to host endothelium and are important virulence factors [31, 62]. However, regulation of var genes does not account for the full range of PfSir2 substrates and interacting partners (e.g., histone and non‐histone targets, as well as associations with chromatin remodellers [57, 60]), stage‐specific functions beyond the asexual blood stage (e.g., potential roles in gametocyte development and transmission stages [61]), the effects of parasite metabolic state (e.g., NAD + ‐dependent regulation and stress‐induced changes such as heat shock [61]), or a direct mechanistic link to monoallelic var expression [57, 60]. Heat shock has been shown to alter sirtuin expression and var gene transcription [61], and P. falciparum heat shock protein 90 (PfHsp90) plays a role in PfSir2A regulation where disruption can lead to de‐repression of var genes, highlighting a potential mechanistic interaction [63]. Despite the important roles these proteins play in virulence, PfSir2A and PfSir2B are not considered essential for asexual parasite survival [53, 56, 57, 61, 64], and are therefore not being pursued as classical drug targets for malaria. The putative class II HDAC pseudogene (PFE0328W) and unclassified chromo‐domain‐helicase‐DNA binding protein (PfCHD1 [59]; Table 1) are both dispensable in asexual stage parasites [54] and are not further discussed here. In contrast, the essentiality of PfHDAC1‐3, together with their amino acid homology differences to human HDACs (Table 1; Supporting Information: Table S1), has led to interest in these enzymes as potential druggable targets for malaria [55, 65, 66, 67, 68] and is the subject of this perspective review. Data in Table 1 shows sequence similarity of PfHDACs to human HDACs generated using both full length proteins and predicted deacetylase domains [58]. The putative deacetylase domain of PfHDAC1 is annotated as a single continuous region, whereas PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 show discontinuous domain annotation based on sequence analysis [69]. Structural predictions indicate that these segments may form a single continuous catalytic domain, with intervening disordered regions (Figure 1).

PfHDAC1

PfHDAC1 is predicted to be essential for asexual intraerythrocytic P. falciparum growth [53, 54] (Table 1). During this developmental stage, PfHDAC1 is predominantly localized within the parasite nucleus [72, 73] and is expressed more abundantly in trophozoites and schizonts than ring stage parasites [30, 73]. PfHDAC1 is also expressed in sexual stage gametocytes [72, 74], more abundantly in male gametocytes [75]. Pull‐down experiments with anti‐PfHDAC1 antibody have shown that this protein co‐precipitates with heat shock proteins (PfHSP70‐1, PfHSP90, PfHSP110 and PfHSP60) indicating that chaperone complexes may be associated with the function of this HDAC in Plasmodium [76], as reported for HDACs from humans and other eukaryotes [38, 43]. Within asexual stage P. falciparum, PfHDAC1 has dynamic occupancy over a broad range of developmentally important genes and plays a role in parasite proliferation and host cell invasion [73]. The genomic occupancy of PfHDAC1 has been shown to change in response to stress (treatment with dihydroartemisinin (DHA) the active metabolite of artemisinin), and overexpression of PfHDAC1 has been linked to P. falciparum DHA resistance in vitro [73]. Conversely, DHA has been shown to interrupt important PfHDAC1 functions, including deacetylase activity and posttranslational phosphorylation [73]. Artemisinin‐resistance has also been linked to changes in transcription timing, with PfHDAC1 being consistently downregulated, allowing parasites to shift their developmental cycle [77]. However, altered PfHDAC1 expression or genome occupancy may reflect compensatory stress responses rather than a direct causal role, and as artemisinin resistance is multifactorial involving K13 mutations and metabolic adaptations [73, 78], PfHDAC1 effects may be secondary [73, 77]. Further studies are needed to establish whether targeting PfHDAC1 can help overcome drug resistance or enhance efficacy of existing antimalarial treatments [73, 77].

Compared to human HDACs, full length PfHDAC1 (~51 kDa; Table 1) has highest amino acid identity (57.1%) to human HDAC2, increasing to 65.9% identity when comparing only the deacetylase domain (Table 1; Supporting Information: Table S1). The crystal structure of PfHDAC1 remains unsolved. However, in silico homology models based on the crystal structures of human HDAC2 [71, 79], human HDAC8 [80, 81, 82] and HDAC homolog HDLP from the hyperthermophilic bacterium Aquifex aeolicus [83], predict an arginase fold, made up of eight β‐sheet strands that are surrounded by 14 alpha helical sections. The loop regions (L), of which L1‐L7 are involved in forming the enzyme binding site (L1‐L2: upper region of the binding site, L3‐L7: lower region of the binding site comprising catalytic machinery), are predicted to connect these secondary structures [81]. PfHDAC1 primarily varies from human HDAC1‐2 at the binding site entry, possessing an additional two residues (Figure 1A; Ala95 and Thr96). This may change the orientation of the Asp97 residue which is conserved in class I and II human HDACs and could be important for HDAC inhibitor binding for PfHDAC1 [70, 71, 84, 85] and potentially be exploited to develop Plasmodium‐selective inhibitors.

To further examine the structural differences between PfHDAC1 and human HDACs, in this study we generated additional models using AlphaFold3 [86] (Figure 1; Supporting Methods). This artificial intelligence (AI) based platform predicted PfHDAC1 (Figure 1A) to have the same structural features described as in published literature, showing the conserved catalytic site residues that coordinate the zinc ion (Asp174, His176 and Asp262) that are commonly observed in class I human HDACs [80, 83, 87, 88], as well as the extended loop incorporating two additional residues (Figure 1A; Ala95 and Thr96 [70]). Within the model, PfHDAC1 has conserved aromatic amino acid residues, such as two histidine residues (His138 and His139), which are predicted to play a role in zinc coordination [89] and two phenylalanine residues (Phe148 and Phe203) which have been shown to play a role in inhibitor binding by formation of π‐π stacking in both class I and II human HDACs [90, 91, 92, 93]. PfHDAC1 also contains a tyrosine (Tyr301 [70, 71]) that is conserved within class I, IIb and IV (but not class IIa) human HDACs [34, 89] and a proline (Pro25 [70, 71]) within its binding pocket (Figure 1A). The presence of this Tyr in PfHDAC1‐3 may be important; this residue is critical in class I human HDACs for deacetylase activity and missing from class IIa human HDACs with studies showing that the deacetylase activity of human HDAC4 (class IIa) was improved 1,000‐fold following a histidine to tyrosine mutation [34, 87].

PfHDAC2

PfHDAC2/PfHDA1 (Table 1) has been shown to be expressed in asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasites, gametocytes and sporozoites [30, 94, 95, 96]. While piggyBac transposon mutagenesis studies indicate that this protein is essential in asexual stage parasites [54], relatively little is known about PfHDAC2. Transcription of the PfHDAC2 gene has been shown to be upregulated just prior to merozoite egress from asexual schizonts [97] and downregulated in parasites harboring drug‐resistant variants of the P. falciparum chloroquine resistance transporter (pfcrt) [98]. Full‐length PfHDAC2 has only 15.7%–19.8% amino acid identity to class I, II and IV human HDACs (Supporting Information: Table S1), with highest homology to HDAC6 (Table 1). The deacetylase domain of PfHDAC2 shares 20.2%‐21.0% amino acid identify to class I human HDACs and 23.6%‐27.6% amino acid identity to class II human HDACs with the highest homology to human HDAC9 (Supporting Information: Table S1). The protein has an ankyrin repeat‐containing domain (CATH Superfamily 1.25.40.20 [99]), a motif involved in protein‐protein interactions [100], and is predicted to have five transmembrane domains (Supporting Information: Figure S1) [59], however, the topology of PfHDAC2 could also be globular (Supporting Information: Figure S2).

There are no crystal structures of PfHDAC2 (~269 kDa; Table 1) and due to its low homology with HDACs from other organisms, in silico homology models are of poor quality and often disordered [84]. However, the catalytic domain of PfHDAC2 can be modeled using structure prediction approaches such as AlphaFold3 [86] (Figure 1B; Supporting Methods). The predicted catalytic domain of PfHDAC2 (amino acids 1559‐1896; Figure 1B, UniProt ID: A0A144A4T0 [69]) shows conserved zinc binding amino acid residues (Figure 1B, Asp1737, His1739 and Asp1847) and two conserved histidine residues (His1684 and His1685 [34, 89]) when compared to PfHDAC1 (Figure 1A). PfHDAC2 also shares a conserved phenylalanine residue with PfHDAC1 (Phe1708 and Phe148, respectively) in the same predicted position on the model (Figure 1). However, the PfHDAC1 Phe203 residue is substituted for a tyrosine (Tyr1792) in PfHDAC2, in the same predicted position in the respective models (Figure 1). Furthermore, within the binding pocket, the model predicts that PfHDAC2 shares a conserved tyrosine residue with PfHDAC1 (Tyr1892 (Figure 1B) and Tyr301 (Figure 1A), respectively); this residue is found in class I and IIb human HDACs and is required for catalytic deacetylase activity. In contrast, class IIa HDACs have a histidine in this position [87, 101]. The conserved substitution of a tyrosine residue in the C‐terminal catalytic site of class I/IIb human HDACs has been shown to play a role in deacetylase activity [87, 101, 102, 103, 104] with a study showing that mutating His976 in human HDAC4 to a tyrosine increases deacetylase activity by > 1,000‐fold compared to the wildtype (which was essentially inactive) [87]. While this tyrosine is present in PfHDAC1‐3, the functional consequences of mutating this amino acid have not yet been explored in Plasmodium (Figure 1).

PfHDAC3

PfHDAC3/PfHDA2 (Table 1) has been shown to be expressed in P. falciparum exoerythrocytic liver‐stage, asexual intraerythrocytic and gametocyte stage parasites [30, 31, 94]. In asexual stage P. falciparum, PfHDAC3 has been localised near the nuclear periphery and is essential for gametocytogenesis [31]. PfHDAC3 can regulate the expression of var genes, that encode PfEMP1 variant antigens, by controlling P. falciparum heterochromatin protein 1 (PfHP1) [31]. PfHP1 is an epigenetic reader that recognizes methylated histone residues and that is responsible for creating transcriptionally inactive heterochromatin [64, 105].

Full‐length PfHDAC3 (~282 kDa) has 16.2%‐20.9% amino acid identity to human HDACs, with highest homology to human HDAC3 (Supporting Information: Table S1; Table 1). The deacetylase domain of PfHDAC3 shares 21.6%‐24.0% amino acid identify to class I human HDACs and 29.9%‐34.8% amino acid identify to class II human HDACs, with the highest homology to human HDAC6 (Supporting Information: Table S1; Table 1). Sequence analyses of PfHDAC3 predicts a inositol phosphate kinase (IPK) domain (Pfam: PF03770 [69], CATH Superfamily 3.30.470.160 [99]) which has important physiological roles in other eukaryotes such as regulation of cellular signaling, energy metabolism, and phosphate homeostasis [106, 107]. HDAC proteins containing an IPK motif have also been shown to be present in other protozoa such as Tetrahymena thermophila (Thd2 [108]) and Toxoplasma gondii (HDAC1 [109]). There are no crystal structures of PfHDAC3 and due to low amino acid sequence identity with HDACs from humans (Supporting Information: Table S1) and other organisms, homology models of the catalytic domain are of poor quality [84]. In comparison to PfHDAC1 and PfHDAC2, the predicted catalytic domain of PfHDAC3 [86] appears to comprise three discontinuous amino acid regions (residues 528‐627, 898‐1036 and 1164‐1287) contributing to a single binding domain (Figure 1C, Q8IJW3 [69]). The sequences between these regions are predicted to be highly disordered and therefore in silico predictions (Alphafold3 [86]) are of low confidence. PfHDAC3 also has zinc binding amino acid residues (Figure 1C, Asp1008, His1010 and Asp1227) that are conserved with PfHDAC1 (Figure 1A) and PfHDAC2 (Figure 1B) as well as two conserved histidine residues (His970 and His1971 [34, 89]). Like PfHDAC1 and PfHDAC2, PfHDAC3 has a conserved phenylalanine residue (Phe980). However, like PfHDAC2, PfHDAC3 has a tyrosine residue Tyr1168; Figure 1C) instead of a second phenylalanine seen for PfHDAC1 (Phe203; Figure 1A). As discussed above, PfHDAC1‐3 share a conserved tyrosine residue (Tyr1271) which has been shown to play an important role in deacetylase activity in eukaryotes (Figure 1C [87, 101]), however the function of this amino acid in Plasmodium has not been investigated. PfHDAC3 possesses a valine residue (Val529) within the binding pocket in the place of proline found in PfHDAC1 (Pro25) and PfHDAC2 (Pro1280) in the corresponding position (Figure 1). This valine residue could alter flexibility or pocket geometry in PfHDAC3 and could be explored in the development of isoform‐selective PfHDAC inhibitors.

HDAC Inhibitor Discovery for Malaria

The typical structure of HDAC inhibitors comprises a zinc‐binding group (ZBG) that binds in the active site of the enzyme and coordinates the catalytically essential Zn ion, a linker spanning the HDAC active site tunnel, and a cap group that protrudes from the entrance to the active site [110, 111, 112] (Figure 2). HDACs are validated therapeutic targets for some cancers and other diseases, with some HDAC inhibitors currently approved for clinical use by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), such as three compounds with hydroxamic acid ZBG's (vorinostat, belinostat and panobinostat; Figure 2) and the cyclic peptide prodrug romidepsin [116, 117]. Valproic acid, a simple carboxylic acid‐based HDAC inhibitor that has been approved for epilepsy [118] and evaluated as a potential treatment for breast cancer [119] and glioblastoma, has also demonstrated weak HDAC inhibitor activity [120]. The hydroxamic acid ZBG HDAC inhibitor givinostat (Figure 2) has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy [121]. In addition, tucidinostat, an ortho‐aminoanilide‐based (benzamide) HDAC inhibitor, is approved for use in China as a treatment for pancreatic cancer [122, 123]. While no HDAC inhibitors have yet been approved for use against human parasitic diseases, the essential roles of PfHDAC1‐3 in Plasmodium and their low homology to human HDACs, in‐particular PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 (Table 1), makes HDAC inhibitors of interest for antimalarial drug discovery.

More than 750 HDAC inhibitors have been evaluated for antiplasmodial activity (Supporting Information and references therein) since 1996, when the antiplasmodial activity was reported for the cyclic tetrapeptide apicidin. Apicidin is a broad‐spectrum inhibitor of all zinc‐containing human HDACs and was found to inhibit growth of the asexual intraerythrocytic stage of P. falciparum (PfIC50 ~0.2 µM in vitro), but with low selectivity for the parasite over human cells (SI < 1 [124]). Since then, chemotypes investigated include hydroxamic acid ZBG derivatives (e.g., vorinostat [125]; Figure 2), ortho‐aminoanilides (e.g., entinostat [82]), cyclic depsipeptides (e.g., the disulfide prodrug romidepsin [65]), and carboxylic acids with broad disease spectrum (e.g., valproic acid [125]). While early studies focused on inhibiting survival of asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasites, which are responsible for the clinical symptoms of malaria, more recently HDAC inhibitors have also been investigated against other developmental stages. Targeting exoerythrocytic liver‐stage parasites [126, 127] is of interest for the development of prophylactic drugs, whereas targeting sexual‐stage gametocytes [46, 74, 114, 127, 128, 129] is as a strategy to prevent transmission via the mosquito vector. Some HDAC inhibitors have shown dual‐stage or triple‐stage activity against Plasmodium parasites (e.g., [46, 127, 130, 131]) (Supporting Information: Figure S3). In addition to targeting P. falciparum, a limited number of HDAC inhibitors have been investigated against other human‐infecting Plasmodium species, which would be an advantage for new malaria drugs [132, 133, 134, 135]. HDAC inhibitors have demonstrated activity against P. vivax and P. knowlesi (e.g., vorinostat with PfIC50 301 nM [136], PvIC50 170 nM [136] and PkIC50 370 nM [71]; panobinostat with PkIC50 9 nM [71]; belinostat with PkIC50 370 nM [71]; and romidepsin with PkIC50 210 nM [71]). P. vivax, the predominant Plasmodium species in the Americas, Eastern Mediterranean regions and South‐East Asia [1], causes recurrent infections due to dormant liver‐stage parasites (hypnozoites) reactivating and returning to the bloodstream after a primary infection [137, 138, 139, 140]. Zoonotic P. knowlesi, which normally infects macaque monkeys, can cause severe symptoms in humans [141].

The FDA approved anti‐cancer drugs vorinostat (Figure 2), panobinostat (Figure 2) and romidepsin have not been pursued for malaria [71, 113] due to poor activity in in vivo murine malaria models (discussed below) which is likely due to a combination of potency and pharmacokinetic factors (e.g., short in vivo half‐lives; Supporting Information: Table S2). Instead, studies have focused on investigating inhibitors of human HDACs with efficacy tested in cancer or other disease models. Analogs were synthesized based on antiplasmodial (IC50) structure‐activity relationships for compounds, as well as selectivity for parasites versus human cells (SI) [46, 65, 68, 82, 113, 125, 126, 142, 143, 144]. This was necessary, rather than based on structure‐activity relationships (SAR) for PfHDAC1‐3 proteins, because pure recombinant PfHDACs are not available for enzyme assays or crystal structures. As discussed in Section 2, the lack of reliable homology models for PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 has further challenged in silico SAR‐driven approaches to drug design for PfHDACs. To address this, conditional knockdown mutants of PfHDAC1 and PfHDAC2/PfHDA1 have been developed [46] showing that reduced PfHDAC1, but not PfHDAC2/PfHDA1, expression heightened sensitivity to the HDAC inhibitor JX21108 indicating a preference towards PfHDAC1 for this compound [46]. JX21108 (Pf3D7 IC50 3.6 nM, SI 1,069) is a derivative of quisinostat (PfIC50 5 nM, SI 8) [46], a compound that has > 230‐700‐fold selectivity towards human HDAC1 and HDAC2 proteins [145], which have highest sequence identity to PfHDAC1 (Supporting Information: Table S1).

Hydroxamic Acid Inhibitors of P. falciparum

Of the different HDAC inhibitor classes, compounds with hydroxamic acid have been most extensively studied for antiplasmodial activity (Supporting Information: Table S3). To better understand the antiplasmodial SARs of this chemotype, we analysed 792 compounds and compared antiplasmodial potency (PfIC50), parasite versus human cell activity or selectivity (SI), predicted drug‐like parameters, and effects of compound linker/connecting unit and capping group moieties. The HDAC inhibitor dataset was assembled by updating our previously published compilation [146] with compounds from the literature and online databases such as SciFinder [147], PubChem [148] and ChEMBL [149]. Predicted drug‐like parameters were generated using the KNIME analytics platform Version 6.7 [150] using Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) strings. The complete list of compounds, structures and references is shown in Supporting Information: Table S4. It is important to note that the data presented and investigated in this study was derived from whole‐cell phenotypic assays against P. falciparum. While there is indirect evidence of PfHDAC inhibition for some compounds in the assessed data set (as discussed in Section 4.1), there is no direct evidence that these compounds target a PfHDAC due to the lack of recombinant PfHDACs. Compounds with a primary hydroxamic acid may be associated with HDAC inhibition in mammalian cells due to this moiety binding in the HDAC ZBG and based on enzyme inhibition data [110, 111, 112]. However, off target effects are also possible [151, 152, 153].

Antiplasmodial Activity and Selectivity Profiles of Hydroxamic Acid Inhibitors

The 792 hydroxamic acids showed diverse inhibitory properties against P. falciparum asexual stage parasites in vitro (PfIC50 0.23 nM to > 446 mM; Supporting Information: Figure S4). According to target candidate profile (TCP) criteria defined by the Medicines for Malaria Venture, a global malaria drug discovery and development public private partnership [154], 277 compounds have PfIC50 value's within the range for “validated hits” (PfIC50 > 0.1‐1.0 µM), 272 compounds have PfIC50 value's within the range for “early leads” (PfIC50 > 0.01‐0.1 µM), and 95 compounds have PfIC50 value's within the range for “late‐leads” (PfIC50 ≤ 0.01 µM) (Supporting Information: Figure S4). Of the 586 compounds with selectivity data available (Supporting Information: Table S4), 253 compounds met the TCP selectivity criteria for “early‐leads” (SI > 100), and 32 meet the criteria as “late‐leads” (SI > 1,000; Supporting Information: Figure S5). Only ten HDAC inhibitors (Figure 3, compounds 5–14) meet “late‐lead” criteria for both antiplasmodial potency and selectivity (i.e., PfIC50 < 0.01 µM; SI > 1,000). Of the ten HDAC inhibitors meeting “late‐lead” criteria for antiplasmodial potency and selectivity, five compounds (5–9; Figure 3 and Figure 4) are analogs of quisinostat (Pf3D7 IC50 5.2 nM, SI 8; Figure 3, red arrow). These compounds have modified linker groups, where the pyrimidinyl 4‐aminomethyl piperidine moiety of quisinostat is replaced with either a 2,6‐diazaspiro[3.4]octane (5 and 6 [157]), 2,7‐diazaspiro[4.4]nonane (7 and 8 [46]) or 2,6‐diazaspiro[3.3]heptane (9 [158]) linker. Compared to quisinostat, compounds 5–9 have ~140‐940‐fold higher selectivity for P. falciparum versus human HepG2 cells (SI 1,148–7,491) yet retain their in vitro antiplasmodial potency (Pf3D7 IC50 1.8‐8.7 nM) [46, 157, 158]. Compounds 10 and 11 (Figure 3 and Figure 4) are both alkyl‐hydroxamic acid‐based inhibitors possessing 4‐phenylthiazole‐derived cap group with PfIC50 0.6‐0.9 nM and SI 1,080–3,556 [156]. Compounds 12 (PfIC50 4.0 nM, SI > 1,000 [159]), 13 (PfIC50 8.8 nM, SI 1,483 [160]) and 14 (PfIC50 8.0 nM, SI 1,041 [130]) are peptoids with a phenylene linker (Figure 3 and Figure 4), which in human cells is known to confer selectivity for the class IIb HDAC6 versus other human HDACs [161, 162]. Interestingly, the entrance region of the active site in all three PfHDACs has a tyrosine residue (PfHDAC1, Tyr301; PfHDAC2, Tyr1892; PfHDAC3, Tyr1271; Figure 1) that is a structural feature shared with class I and IIb human HDACs [87]. This has led to interest in the investigation of selective inhibitors of human HDAC6 for use against Plasmodium parasites [159, 162, 163, 164, 165, 166].

As PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 recombinant proteins are not available, and PfHDAC1 is of low purity [167], enzymatic data on the isoform selectivity of antiplasmodial hydroxamic acids is unavailable. However, the ability of compounds to alter the acetylation of histones in lysates prepared from P. falciparum parasites, as detected using Western blot or ELISA (e.g., [70, 113, 126, 168]), has been used as a marker of HDAC inhibition. Of the ten HDAC inhibitors meeting late‐lead criteria for antiplasmodial potency and selectivity (Figure 3), seven have been examined using Western blot. Compounds 7 and 9 caused hyperacetylation of P. falciparum histone H3 [46, 158], 10 hyperacetylated H3 and H4 [156], and peptoid‐based compounds 12 and 13 hyperacetylated H4 [159, 160] but peptoid 14 did not [130]. The difference seen for 12 and 13 versus 14 could be due to differences in compound permeability, specificity for another protein substrate, or parasite developmental stage, however this was not tested [130, 160]. While previous work from our group has shown that the acetylation state of an ~80 kDa non‐histone P. falciparum protein can be altered by HDAC inhibitors (e.g., vorinostat, panobinostat, belinostat and romidepsin [113]), none of the ten HDAC inhibitors meeting late‐lead criteria for antiplasmodial potency and selectivity criteria have been evaluated for non‐histone protein acetylation changes. Likewise, we recently developed a solvent protein stability Western blot method [169], demonstrating stabilization of PfHDAC1 by vorinostat and other HDAC inhibitors, however compounds 5–14 have not been assessed using this approach.

Predicted Physicochemical Properties of Hydroxamate Compound Set

Predicting “druggability” of compounds by calculating physicochemical properties can help guide design of new drug leads. To predict orally deliverable compounds, Lipinski's Rule of Five (Ro5) [170, 171] is often applied and includes the following parameters: molecular weight (MW) ≤ 500 g/mol; calculated n‐octanol‐water partition coefficient (clogP) ≤ 5 [171]; hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) ≤ 10 and; hydrogen bond donors (HBD) ≤ 5 [171]. The cLogP often relates to membrane permeability, absorption, solubility and stability. The numbers of HBA and HBD highlight components that can form hydrogen bonds with a target protein to influence binding affinity and selectivity [171, 172], but most importantly they influence free energy of solvation/desolvation in water which is crucial in dictating membrane penetration and cell uptake. The physicochemical properties of compounds within the entire dataset have a median MW of 390.1 g/mol, HBA 5‐6, HBD 3 and cLogP 2.69 (Figure 5, Supporting Information: Table S4). In comparison, compounds meeting late‐lead profiles (PfIC50 and SI) have observed ranges where the median falls between these values (MW 362–473 g/mol, HBA 4‐8, HBD 2–4, cLogP 1.2–4.0) (Figure 4). While these align with Lipinski's Ro5, these ranges are too broad to guide chemical optimization. The limited in vivo data (for very few compounds) currently precludes clear correlation between physicochemical properties and therapeutic outcomes. As discussed in Section 4.4, future in vivo studies are essential to establish the SAR trends required for translational impact.

Of 792 hydroxamic acids examined in this study, ~89% met all Ro5 criteria (Figure 5; Supporting Information: Table S4), including ~99% of the 93 compounds with PfIC50 < 0.01 µM. Most compounds (96%) had cLogP ≤ 5 (Supporting Information: Table S4). A set of 16 cyclic nonpeptides [173] had the highest molecular weights examined (MW 861–1,064) and exceeded both HBA and HBD criteria (Figure 5, dashed boxes). However, it should be noted that most cyclic compounds are administrated parenterally as drugs [174, 175], and parenteral routes require a different physicochemical profile from oral drugs. For example, the antifungal medication caspofungin (MW 1,093 g/mol) has an oral bioavailability of < 0.2% but a 92% tissue distribution within 36–48 h following intravenous infusion in rabbits [176]. Given that long‐acting injectables are now being considered for malaria seasonal chemoprevention [177], HDAC inhibitors with suitable potency, high selectivity for parasite versus human cells and physicochemical properties that fall outside Lipinski's Rule of Five for oral candidates may be of interest for further development.

Antiplasmodial SAR Survey of Hydroxamic Acids

Generally, all HDAC inhibitors can be deconstructed into three separate parts: ZBG, linker and cap, as introduced in Figure 2. Most HDAC inhibitors that have been investigated for growth inhibition against asexual stage P. falciparum are hydroxamic acids, so compounds with other zinc binding groups are not the subject of this review. What follows is a discussion, although not exhaustive, of the antiplasmodial SAR of compounds divided into four classes based on structural similarities within the linker component. These include (a) linear alkyl chains (vorinostat analogs) (b) 1,4‐phenylene linked hydroxamic acids (AR‐42 and nexturastat A analogs) (c) 2‐(piperidin‐1‐yl)pyrimidine linkers (quisinostat analogs) and (d) cinnamoylhydroxamates (panobinostat derivatives).

Alkyl Linkers

The most common linker observed within 792 hydroxamic acids was a linear alkyl chain (~33%; Supporting Information: Table S4). Most compounds within this subset contain either an amide (15) or alkoxyamide (20) group (61 and 89 compounds, respectively) that links the hydroxamate to the cap (Supporting Information: Table S4). Amide‐containing HDAC inhibitors contain modified capping groups similar to vorinostat (1) such as the phenylthiazolyl cap group compounds 10 (PfIC50 0.59 nM, SI 1,080) and 11 (PfIC50 0.9 nM, SI 3,556) (Figure 4), as well as their potent but less selective analog 16 (PfIC50 0.8 nM, SI 675; Figure 6A) [156]. Compound 10 and analog 11 are among the ten hydroxamic acids that met the late‐lead criteria for antiplasmodial potency and selectivity (PfIC50 < 0.01 µM; SI > 1,000; Figure 3). However, compound 10 has been reported twice (also as YC‐II‐88: PfIC50 0.23 nM, SI 17 [155]), with a > 60‐fold difference in SI values between these studies despite only ~2‐fold change in PfIC50 [155, 156]. This might be due to the different cell lines used (RAW macrophages [156] vs. HeLa cell lines [155]) having different HDAC expression or inhibitor sensitivities [182, 183, 184, 185]. Potent analogs of vorinostat include 17 (PfIC50 80 nM, SI 51; Figure 6A), a quinoline‐substituted HDAC inhibitor identified using a quantitative structure‐activity relationship (QSAR) virtual screening approach [146]. Strategies fusing the cap group of vorinostat to procainamide, a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor [186] and class IA antiarrhythmic agent [187], have also been explored, such as compounds 18 (PfIC50 41 nM, SI 205) and 19 (PfIC50 52 nM, SI 442) [164]. These derivatives exhibited a ~2‐3‐fold lower PfIC50 and ~3‐10‐fold higher selectivity compared to vorinostat (PfIC50 120 nM, SI 46) (Figure 6A).

HDAC inhibitors with an alkyl chain and alkoxyamide connecting unit (20) have also been reported as antiplasmodial agents (Figure 6B). These compounds most commonly contain monocyclic or bicyclic cap groups [74, 127, 178, 179] (Supporting Information: Table S4) derived from 21 (LMK‐235, PfIC50 100 nM, SI 13) (Figure 6A) investigated as an anticancer agent [127]. Optimization of the substitution pattern of the cap group has led to increased antiplasmodial potency and selectivity. For example, substitution of a benzothiophene in compound 22 (PfIC50 70 nM, SI 25 [178]) to an indole in 23 (PfIC50 70 nM, SI 207 [179]) resulted in an ~3‐16‐fold higher SI with a retained PfIC50 (Figure 6B). Using a phenylthiazole‐derived cap group has led to the even more potent and selective compound 24 (PfIC50 20 nM, SI 257) [169]. Other derivatives with alkoxyurea‐based connecting units were less potent (e.g., 25, PfIC50 160 nM, SI 31 [180]) (Figure 6B). Various connecting units (e.g., triazole) within linear alkyl chains of 5‐9 methylene groups have been explored [181]. Compounds 26‐28 with five or six methylenes exhibited the highest potency (PfIC50 85‐321 nM, SI 18‐45), whereas extension to seven methylenes led to significantly reduced activity (PfIC50 2,133 nM, SI 8) regardless of modifications to the cap group [181] (Figure 6C).

Phenylene Linkers

Compounds with a phenyl or phenylene linker comprise ~29% of the hydroxamic acids examined in this study (PfIC50 range 0.001 µM ‐ 77.7 µM, SI range of 0.3‐7,353; Supporting Information: Table S4). Most of these compounds were split between two subsets, those that contain a phenyl (29a) or benzyl (29b) linker (Figure 7). The phenyl‐containing subset consisted primarily of analogs of 30 (AR‐42: PfIC50 22 nM, SI 35 [70]) and 1,3‐diphenylurea‐based compounds [165] (Supporting Information: Table S4). The two most potent compounds from this subset were the cyclopentane‐substituted analogs 31 (PfIC50 5 nM, SI 100 [70]) and 32 (PfIC50 7 nM, SI 76 [70]), which had > 3‐4‐fold increased potency compared to 30 (Figure 7A). The compound within this set with the highest SI was 33 (PfIC50 260 nM, SI > 769 [165]) which is > 21‐fold higher than for 30, however this compound was ~11‐fold less active against P. falciparum (Figure 7A).

The benzyl subset (29b; Figure 7) contained derivatives with a similar scaffold to human HDAC6‐selective inhibitors nexturastat A (34: PfIC50 230 nM, SI 93 [165]) and tubastatin A (37: PfIC50 150 nM, SI 16 [168]) (Supporting Information: Table S4). A large portion of these were peptoid‐based HDAC inhibitors that have complex cap groups [130, 159, 160, 188, 189]. The five most potent compounds from this subset included the peptoid‐based compounds 12 (PfIC50 4.0 nM, SI > 1,000 [159]), 13 (PfIC50 8.8 nM, SI 1,483 [160]) and 14 (PfIC50 8 nM, SI 1,041 [130]) (Figure 4), as well as the less selective derivative with an isopropylbenzamide cap, 35 (PfIC50 5.2 nM, SI 889 [160]) and N,N‐dimethylamino substituted derivative 36 (PfIC50 9 nM, SI 654 [159]) (Figure 7B). Derivatives of 37 (tubastatin A: PfIC50 150 nM, SI 16; Figure 7B) that contain a tricyclic cap group have also been investigated, including those with modified thiaheterocyclic cap groups, These compounds had increased selectivity for the parasite versus human cells; e.g., ~62‐fold higher SI and similar PfIC50 for the bromo‐substituted compound 38 (PfIC50 110 nM, SI 991 [163]) and ~42‐fold higher SI, but ~2‐fold reduced antiplasmodial potency, for the fluoro‐substituted compound 39 (PfIC50 400 nM, SI > 673 [163]) (Figure 7B).

2,5‐Disubstituted Pyrimidine Linker

Compounds with 2,5‐disubstituted pyrimidine linker (40), such as derivatives of quisinostat (41: PfIC50 5.2 nM, SI 8 [46]), made up ~28% of the hydroxamic acids examined (PfIC50 range 1.3 nM ‐ 325 nM, SI range 1–7491; Supporting Information: Table S4). Within this set, almost a third (~28%) of compounds have PfIC50 < 10 nM and more than half (~53%) have SI > 100 (Supporting Information: Table S4). As discussed above (Figure 4), compounds 5 (PfIC50 1.8 nM, SI 5,136) and 6 (PfIC50 2.7 nM, SI 7,491) are some of the most potent and selective hydroxamic acid‐type HDAC inhibitors with antiplasmodial activity identified to date, demonstrating potential for further development for malaria [157]. While compound 42 (PfIC50 1.6 nM, SI 176) was the most potent analog, this compound exhibited a > 29‐42‐fold lower SI for human cells over Plasmodium (Figure 8). Similarly, compound 43 (PfIC50 1.3 nM, SI 810; Figure 8) is a more potent but less selective analog of compounds 7 and 8 (PfIC50 3.6‐8.7 nM, SI 1,069–1,148) (Figure 4). Compounds 44 (PfIC50 3.2 nM, SI 266 [47]) and 45 (PfIC50 3.9 nM, SI 152 [158]) (Figure 8) are spirocyclic compounds more potent but less selective than compound 9 (PfIC50 8.6 nM, SI 1,175 [158]), one of the ten compounds to achieve “late‐lead” criteria (Figure 4). The last set of compounds have a cis‐1,4‐cyclohexanediamine group [190], with compound 46 (PfIC50 11.5 nM, SI 1,113 [190]) being the most potent (Figure 8). Modifications to the cap group have also been investigated for quisinostat‐derivatives such as compound 47 (PfIC50 23 nM, SI 2,174 [191]) with a > 271‐fold increase in selectivity for Plasmodium parasites versus human cells (Figure 8).

Cinnamoyl Linker

Compounds with a cinnamoyl hydroxamic acid (~5% of dataset), exemplified by belinostat (2: PfIC50 60 nM, SI 40) and panobinostat (3: PfIC50 10 nM, SI 7) [113], have a broad antiplasmodial activity range (PfIC50 0.0026 µM ‐ 17.76 µM; Supporting Information: Table S4). More potent and selective analogs of panobinostat (e.g., 48: PfIC50 40 nM, SI 2 [114, 192]; and 49 PfIC50 6 nM, SI 23 [146]) were reported but fail Medicines for Malaria Venture P. falciparum versus human cell selectivity criteria [154] for early‐leads (SI > 100; Figure 9). An exception is a hybrid with dihydroartemisinin 50 (PfIC50 2.6 nM, SI 750 [193]; Figure 9) and its analogs (Supporting Information: Table S4), but they did not have greater antiplasmodial potency compared to dihydroartemisinin alone [193]. Future studies could investigate other such hybrid compounds for enhanced potency and selectivity [193, 194].

In vivo Efficacy of HDAC Inhibitors Against Plasmodium Parasites

To date, ~20 HDAC inhibitors have been investigated for in vivo efficacy against Plasmodium parasites in mouse infection models and one (10; WR301801/YC‐II‐88) in a non‐human primate model (Supporting Information: Table S2). Seven HDAC inhibitors were able to clear parasites in Plasmodium infected mice, including YC‐II‐88 (10; 50 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection in a prophylactic model [155], however this compound did not cure via oral administration [156]. Compound 30 (AR‐42, 50 mg/kg) and its analogs 31 and 32 (JT83 and JT21b; 25 mg/kg) cleared parasites in Plasmodium infected mice in an oral, treatment model [70] (Supporting Information: Table S2). While pharmacokinetic data for analogs 31 (JT83) and 32 (JT21b) is not available, following a 50 mg/kg oral dose, 30 has maximum serum concentration (Cmax) of 14.7 µM and terminal half‐life (t1/2) of 11.1 h, with a calculated area under the curve (AUC) 29.9 μM × h corresponding to an oral bioavailability (F%) of 27% [195]. This is the highest duration of exposure and oral bioavailability of the HDAC inhibitors investigated for oral efficacy in mouse malaria models and may explain the efficacy of 30 (AR‐42) and its analogs. Cures were also observed in mice treated via intraperitoneal injection with four derivatives of quisinostat containing different linker moieties, but not quisinostat itself (Supporting Information: Table S2 [46, 47, 157, 158]). Oral vorinostat [71], panobinostat [71], pracinostat [126] and apicidin (cyclic peptide [124]) all delayed the onset of parasitemia but did not cure animals of infection at the doses investigated (Supporting Information: Table S2). It is likely that the pharmacokinetic profile of these compounds (e.g., t1/2 0.7–2.9 h, Cmax 0.3–11.0 µM, AUC 0.36–12.1 μM × h; Supporting Information: Table S2) provides insufficient duration of exposure relative to the potency of these compounds to control infection. While HDAC inhibitors possessing an ortho‐aminoanilide‐based ZBG have longer half‐lives in humans, such as entinostat (t1/2 33–150 h [196, 197]), compared to first‐generation HDAC inhibitors such as vorinostat (t1/2 1–2 h [198]), ortho‐aminoanilide‐based ZBG have poor in vitro antiplasmodial activity (e.g., entinostat PfIC50 8.3 µM, SI > 2 [168]). An additional challenge when testing in vivo efficacy is that the pharmacokinetic profile of some HDAC inhibitors can differ in humans compared to mice. For example, oral panobinostat has a 6–10‐fold greater half‐life in humans (t1/2 16–30 h) versus mice (t1/2 2.9 h) and a five‐fold greater oral bioavailability (F%) [71, 199].

Perspectives and Conclusions

The investigation of HDACs as prospective drug targets for malaria is supported by the importance of these proteins in Plasmodium biology [31, 33, 59, 73], with PfHDAC1‐3 being essential for parasite survival (Table 1) [31, 44, 54] However, their low homology with human HDACs, in particular PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 [84] (Table 1; Supporting Information: Table S1), and the unavailability of these pure proteins for enzymology or structure determination by crystallography, has made it difficult to predict which PfHDACs are most druggable and which inhibitors of human HDACs may be effective inhibitors of different PfHDACs. These limitations have hampered medicinal chemistry optimization of compounds with increased potency and selectivity for Plasmodium versus human cells. While recombinant PfHDAC1 is available, it has low purity (60%, Abcam, UK) which compromises its use [167]. Recombinant PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 are not available. High quality in silico homology models of PfHDAC1 are available [70, 71, 79, 81, 82] (Section 2.1) and have the potential to be used in computer‐aided drug design of PfHDAC1‐specfic inhibitors. However, it is important to note that successful use of homology modeling approaches usually requires higher resolution accuracy [200]. In addition (Sections 2.2 and 2.3), the low homology of PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 to known HDAC structures means that predictions are compromised by disorder and low structural confidence [84].

Despite these challenges, some inhibitors of human HDACs have been found with antiplasmodial activity against P. falciparum, inhibiting growth of asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasites that are responsible for the clinical symptoms of malaria. Most of these compounds are hydroxamic acids, some of which meet Medicines for Malaria Venture criteria as “late‐leads” with respect to high antiplasmodial potency in vitro (PfIC50 < 0.01 µM) and high selectivity in vitro for antiproliferative activity against parasites over human cells (SI > 1,000 [154]) (Figure 3; Supporting Information: Figure S3). It is also promising that some inhibitors have demonstrated multi‐stage activity against both asexual stage parasites and exoerythrocytic stages and/or gametocytes (Supporting Information: Figure S4 and Figure S5) [46, 114, 127, 130, 157, 169, 188, 201], a desirable feature for new antimalarial drug leads [202, 203]. Compounds that target exoerythrocytic liver‐stage parasites are of interest for malaria prevention, while targeting gametocytes can block parasite transmission [204, 205, 206, 207]. However, several challenges hamper progression of HDAC inhibitors as drug candidates for malaria.

As an alternative strategy, we reported the use of first and second‐generation quantitative structure‐activity relationship (QSAR) models, developed using the antiplasmodial activity of > 400 hydroxamic acids, as an approach to predicting compounds with antiplasmodial activity [146, 208]. As with all in silico models, predictive power is limited by training data and new experimental data is needed to improve outcomes [209]. This is particularly true for the development of models that incorporate compounds that are not hydroxamic acids (e.g., ortho‐aminoanilide‐based [114, 128, 210] and ethylhydrazide‐based HDAC inhibitors [211]). These models also do not provide PfHDAC isoform‐specific or off‐target information as they are trained on whole cell activity data [146, 208]. Advances in machine‐learning and AI technologies such as AlphaFold3 [86] may provide structural insights into PfHDACs (Figure 1) and could potentially be combined with QSAR models for virtual screening [212, 213, 214, 215]. Another challenge for progressing towards drug leads for malaria is obtaining suitable pharmacokinetic profiles for orally delivered HDAC inhibitors [65, 66, 216, 217]. Hydroxamic acid‐based HDAC inhibitors often suffer from sub‐optimal pharmacokinetics due to rapid glucuronidation, short plasma half‐lives, and high clearance, which limits potency in vivo despite good in vitro potency [218, 219, 220]. To date very few studies have reported efficacy for HDAC inhibitors in mouse models of malaria infection (Supporting Information: Table S2), with only AR‐42 (30) and two analogs (31 and 32) reported to cure infected mice following oral administration [70]. Alternative ZBG's could also be investigated to identify selective HDAC inhibitors to PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3, a strategy previously explored to identify isoform‐selective HDAC inhibitors in human HDACs (e.g., selective class IIa TFMO‐based [104] and selective HDAC6 DFMO‐based [221] HDAC inhibitors). Non‐hydroxamates such as ortho‐aminoanilides, alkylhidrazides, TFMO and DFMO‐based ZBG's have shown to have higher bioavailability [219].

With respect to SAR analyses, some potential limitations must be acknowledged. Differences in genetic background and drug resistance of P. falciparum or mammalian cell lines could impact SAR analyses. While the potential impact of these differences may be limited in the P. falciparum data set which were primarily derived from 3D7 or its parental line NF54 (Supporting Information: Table S4; ~87% of the Pf data) [222], mammalian cell data were derived from 14 different lines. Mammalian cell activity data were also often limited due to low inhibitory activities which resulted in ~26% of compounds having undefined IC50 values. The potential impact of the assessed mammalian cell line on SI data was demonstrated by studies which reported activities against multiple cell lines (e.g., HepG2 and HEK293T, 154 compounds [46, 47, 157, 158, 190]; NFF and HepG2, three compounds [159]; reference compound vorinostat [113, 159, 223]), where an ~1–4‐fold difference in sensitivity was seen. Ideally, future studies should investigate compounds against multiple P. falciparum or mammalian cells lines (or use a consistent cell line) to account for potential differences. Another consideration that may impact activity results is the assay methodology and duration used in the different studies. The most frequently used methods for determination of PfIC50 values were [3H]‐hypoxanthine incorporation (~43% of compounds) and the SYBR Green I (~35% of compounds) labeling (Supporting Information: Table S3). The former quantitates nucleic acid replication [224], while the latter measures total parasite DNA as a proxy for growth [225]. Likewise, while most of the P. falciparum activity data was derived from assays performed for 72 h (63%), some of the data were derived from 48 h, 96 h or assays with an undefined length (~17%, ~4% and ~16% of PfIC50 data, respectively). Such assay differences may lead to IC50 variations, and while this has not been systematically investigated, only modest differences in PfIC50 was observed for the control hydroxamate vorinostat against Pf3D7 across three different detection methods with an assay duration of 72 h (e.g., [3H]‐hypoxanthine IC50 0.12 µM [113], PfHRP2 ELISA IC50 0.24 µM [191] and SYBR Green I IC50 0.10 µM [223]) or against Pf3D7 in 48 h, 72 h or 96 h assays (IC50 0.25 µM [71]), 0.12 µM [113] and 0.17 µM [164], respectively). This potential limitation also applies to mammalian cell data, which are more complicated within the data set with nine different assays used (e.g., CellTiter‑Glo, > 31% of compounds) and with assay durations of 24 h, 48 h and 72 h (~3, ~15, ~71% of compounds, respectively) (Supporting Information: Table S3).

This review of hydroxamic acid activity against P. falciparum may also inform research on other apicomplexan species where epigenetic inhibitors are of interest for drug discovery. For example, T. gondii shows similar sensitivity to vorinostat (IC50 0.25 µM) [226] as P. falciparum (IC50 0.12 µM; Figure 2 [113], and to four other hydroxamic acid‐based compounds (TgIC50 0.005‐0.075 µM) [226]; PfIC50 0.004–0.244 µM [210]) (Supporting Information: Table S4). As such, the ~368 compounds with an antiplasmodial activity of ≤ 0.1 µM (Figure 3), could provide a useful drug discovery starting point for T. gondii or other apicomplexan species.

In summary, while substantial progress has been achieved in discovering and developing HDAC inhibitors as novel antimalarial drug leads, including testing > 750 hydroxamic acids for in vitro activity against P. falciparum, to date only ten compounds with “late‐lead” in vitro target candidate profile activity and selectivity (PfIC50 < 0.01 µM; SI > 1,000; Figure 3) have been identified. To enable rational design of new potent and selective inhibitors of PfHDAC, including some with PfHDAC isoform selectivity, high quality recombinant PfHDAC proteins are needed along with their high‐resolution crystal structures. Critically, more in vivo data together with improvements in the pharmacokinetic profiles of HDAC inhibitors targeted to malaria are also needed to meet the target product profiles (TPPs) for malaria drugs [154, 207]. Also based on the economic and geographical challenges in treating people infected by such parasites, inexpensive oral candidates are preferred, ideally as a single dose used as part of a drug combination that could achieve a > 12 log10 unit reduction in asexual blood‐stage parasite load in > 95% patients [207].
