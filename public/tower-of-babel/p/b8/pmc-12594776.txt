Effects of acute exercise on inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers in women: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Catherine Duggan, Jean de Dieu Tapsoba, Jude Warner, Atreya Dash, Ching-Yun Wang, Anne McTiernan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanisms linking exercise to reduced breast cancer risk are poorly understood. 103 healthy women, 18–75, were randomized to 45-minutes of acute exercise at 60% VO2max (N = 54, intervention), or 45-minutes of rest (N = 49, control). Blood samples were collected at baseline, 45- and 105-minutes, analytes measured by immunoassay, and mean changes from baseline to 45- and 105-minutes modelled using generalized estimating equations, adjusted for confounders. Mean percent changes levels (Δ%) increased in exercisers vs. controls for monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1 Δ45 min + 17.1% (P < 0.0001), Δ105 min + 12.0% (P = 0.005); interleukin(IL)-6, Δ45 min + 103.5%, Δ105 min + 92.3%; and at 45-minutes for glucose Δ45 min + 8.8%; insulin Δ45 min + 82.4% (all P < 0.0001). Differences between arms for vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) Δ45 min + 9.8%; c-reactive protein Δ45 min-6.6%; irisin Δ45 min-5.1%; or plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI)-1, Δ45 min-3.6% were non-statistically significant, with similar results at 105-minutes. Results suggest that acute exercise has specific effects on circulating metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers, implicated in breast cancer etiology, which differ from weight-loss-induced changes. Trial registration: Clincialtrials.gov identifier NCT03779867.Subject terms: Predictive markers, Cancer epidemiology, Biomarkers, Risk factors

Introduction

Physical activity decreases risk for breast cancer development and reduces risk of all-cause and breast-cancer specific mortality rates1–5, with a recent meta-analysis of 28 cohort studies reporting a 13% lower risk of breast cancer (odds ratio = 0.87, 95% CI 0.84–0.90), associated with regular exercise6. However, underlying mechanisms linking increased physical activity and reduced breast cancer risk, including in overweight women, remain poorly understood.

Overweight and obesity are associated with both systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, and with increased risk for chronic disease, including postmenopausal breast cancer, and breast cancer mortality at any age5,7,8. Levels of circulating inflammatory biomarkers are often increased in obesity and in persons with low levels of regular physical activity, including c-reactive protein (CRP)9–12, angiogenic factors13, and insulin resistance7,9,14.

While metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers are neither validated clinical surrogates for breast cancer outcomes, nor used in clinical risk prediction or diagnosis —although interleukin (IL)-6, for example, is a prognostic marker in breast cancer—15 evidence has accumulated implicating them in key biological processes involved in either the development or progression of breast cancer. Hyperinsulinemia and elevated glucose promote mitogenic signaling in breast tissue through the insulin/insulin-like growth factor axis16, and higher circulating levels of glucose and insulin are associated with increased risk of breast cancer in women17,18. IL-6, CRP, and monocyte chemoattractant (MCP)-1 are associated with a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory mileu19, and increased circulating levels of CRP and IL-6 levels are associated with increased risk of developing and progression of breast cancer11. IL-6 activates the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway, and promotes breast tumor initiation and progression, survival, invasion, metastasis, and chemo-resistance20–22. MCP-1 expression is regulated through STAT322, and is involved in tumor cell migration, promoting breast cancer invasion and metastasis23. Both vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI)-1 regulate angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling in breast tissue24: PAI-1 is a serine protease inhibitor that promotes angiogenesis, and is a prognostic marker for poor outcome in a number of cancers including breast25,26, and VEGF is a key regulator of angiogenesis, and promotes the growth, survival, migration and invasion of cancer cells27. Irisin is an exercise-induced myokine that has tumor suppressive effects on the number, migration, and viability of malignant epithelial breast cancer cells through induction of cell apoptosis and suppression of NF-kB activity28,29, and has shown anti-tumor effects in preclinical models; its expression in breast cancer tissue is associated with obesity30. Finally, in epidemiologic models, increased levels of these biomarkers are associated with increased risk of breast cancer, and worse prognosis7,9–12,16,31. Thus, these biomarkers serve as indicators of systemic physiological responses that may link physical activity to cancer-related biological pathways.

Previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrated significant chronic exercise effects on inflammation-related biomarkers, angiogenesis markers, and sex hormones. However, many of these effects were mediated through reductions in weight and fat loss, where statistically significant reductions in circulating biomarkers were observed in participants randomized to dietary weight-loss interventions with or without exercise, rather than to exercise-only interventions, compared to controls32–38.

Most RCTs examining the impact of exercise on tumorigenic and pro-inflammatory biomarkers have focused on chronic exercise, spanning durations of 3–12 months, with biological sampling typically occurring 24 h post-exercise39,40. There is increasing evidence suggesting that acute bouts of exercise confer beneficial health effects that last several hours, a phenomenon known as the “last bout” effect41–43. Although the precise mechanisms are not fully understood, exercise-induced myokine secretion is believed to negatively influence cancer cell growth44. However, the differential mechanisms contributing to individual adaptations to acute versus chronic exercise remain largely unexplored. Additionally, studies investigating acute exercise effects have predominantly targeted cardiovascular disease, with most participants being men, and with relatively small sample sizes.

This study investigates the effects of a 45-minute acute exercise session in women, randomized to either a 45-minute exercise session at 60% of VO2max (intervention arm) or a 45-minute seated resting period (control arm) on circulating levels of glucose, insulin, CRP, IL-6, MCP-1, VEGF, PAI-1 and irisin, and on calculated Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) scores, with blood samples collected immediately pre- and post-intervention/control period, and at 1-hour post intervention/control.

Results

Participants

A total of 741 women completed the eligibility screening questionnaire (Fig. 1), 338 (45.6%) were eligible for Clinic Visit 1, N = 146 were screened in clinic and signed informed consent. Of these N = 137 (93.8%) were eligible for randomization. Twenty-three participants subsequently dropped out or were lost to follow-up. One hundred and fourteen women (80.6%) were randomized (N = 59 intervention arm; N = 55, control arm). Eleven participants dropped out post-randomization, N = 103 (90.3%) participants completed the intervention (N = 54) and resting control (N = 49), after which recruitment ceased. Ninety-seven (94.2%) participants had blood samples drawn at all three timepoints. No clinically significant adverse events were reported.

Discussion

We report higher increases in levels of circulating IL-6, MCP-1, glucose, insulin, and HOMA-IR scores, immediately post-exercise, among women randomized to a 45-minute acute bout of exercise vs. resting control, which met our pre-specified p-value for statistical significance. Differences at the statistically significant level persisted at 105 min for MCP-1 and IL-6 only. While CRP levels were higher at 45 min among controls vs. exercisers, and VEGF levels increased by Δ45 min + 9.8% in exercisers compared to controls, these changes were not statistically significant. There were no statistically significant differences between groups at either 45- or 105 min post baseline, for irisin or PAI-1. However, the high inter-batch CV for the irisin assay suggests that these results be interpreted with caution. No effect modification was observed for any of these biomarkers when comparing biomarker changes from baseline to 45- and 105-minutes in controls vs. exercisers, stratified by BMI, menopausal status, baseline fitness, or step count.

We report a peak in glucose and insulin among participants randomized to the exercise intervention at 45 min, likely due to the fasting status of our participants, unlike post-prandial exercise, when insulin levels decrease45. Two meta-analyses demonstrated significant heterogeneity in the effects of aerobic exercise on insulin and glucose levels in RCTs comparing fasting vs. non-fasting states. Postprandial aerobic exercise significantly decreased insulin concentrations, whereas fasted aerobic exercise non-significantly increased insulin compared to resting controls46,47. Skeletal muscle accounts for ∼80% of whole-body insulin-stimulated glucose disposal48. During physical activity, in individuals without diabetes, a catecholamine response—norepinephrine and epinephrine —inhibits insulin release, and triggers hepatic glucose production from stored glycogen to maintain blood glucose levels and fuel muscle contraction in tandem with adipose tissue lipolysis, resulting in glucose and fatty acid supply for oxidation in active skeletal muscle during exercise49. As exercise intensity or time increases, catecholamine-induced glucose release from the liver from stored glycogen may temporarily outpace muscle uptake, triggering a short-term insulin response49. The increase in insulin concentrations corrects glucose levels and restores muscle glycogen at the cessation of exercise50–52. Repeated bouts of exercise, whose effects may last up to 72 h, appear to have an additive effect on improvements in insulin sensitivity43,53, seen in the reductions in insulin and glucose in previous studies in response to year-long chronic exercise interventions with daily amounts of exercise similar to the ACE trial’s one-day exercise bout54.

Skeletal muscle communicates with key metabolic organs, influencing energy metabolism through the secretion of endocrine factors55. Exercise-induced skeletal muscle damage triggers an inflammatory response that plays a crucial role in subsequent muscle regeneration. In contrast, obesity-related chronic inflammation—mediated in part through macrophage invasion triggering an upregulation of inflammation-related signaling —induces insulin resistance, angiogenesis, and changes in the microenvironment, all contributing to a pro-tumorigenic environment. In this study, CRP levels were reduced, but this was not statistically significant, like the results from our previous year-long moderate-to-vigorous exercise interventions. In our previous studies, the majority of CRP reductions were mediated through fat loss36,37. In a meta-analysis of 38 RCTs examining the role of chronic exercise training in healthy individuals compared to control groups, exercise greater than 12 weeks, multicomponent exercise, or at a frequency of three or more times a week, demonstrated a larger reduction in CRP levels. Of interest, exercise intensity was reported as a potential source of heterogeneity, and the reduction in CRP level induced by long-term exercise training was weakened by increasing exercise intensity56. Notably, CRP changes are reported to peak 24–48 h post-exercise57, which may explain why we found no exercise-induced CRP decrease.

IL-6 increased immediately after exercise compared to controls and was maintained at 105 min post-baseline, in contrast to no change in chronic exercise interventions with or without weight loss in our previous RCTs36,37. IL-6 levels are associated with exercise duration, training intensity, and the amount of muscle mass mobilized by exercise, and have been shown to increase by 2.0–40.0-fold immediately after interval running and interval cycling, depending on the length and intensity of exercise. Similar to our findings, an uncontrolled study comparing the effects of four 10-minute acute exercise bouts reported a significant increase of IL-6 levels immediately post-exercise, maintained at 240 min post-exercise, with no changes in CRP post-exercise58,59. Finally, a randomized trial in 32 breast cancer survivors demonstrated an increase in IL-6 levels immediately after and sustained at 30 min post exercise in participants randomized to either resistance training or high-intensity interval training60.

An increase in IL-6 levels in response to exercise seems counterintuitive given its increase in overweight and obesity, and associations with pro-tumorigenic environments. However, it appears that the biological effects of IL-6 are context dependent61,62, with an immediate IL-6 release from contracting myocytes during exercise. IL-6 coordinates short-term energy allocation via IL-6 signaling62, and triggers the release of free fatty acids from lipolysis, glycogenolysis in the liver that promotes the release of glucose, and induces peripheral glucose metabolism via activation of AMPK63,64. IL-6 binds to the IL-6 receptor and induces glucose uptake into skeletal muscle, independently of insulin action65. Other exercise-induced anti-tumorigenic effects of IL-6 include release of IL-10 and IL-1RA from macrophages, the inhibition of TNFα release from monocytes, a reduction of macrophage infiltration in adipose tissue, and polarization of macrophages towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype61. Unlike adipose tissue, skeletal muscle can produce IL-6 during contraction in the absence of elevated measures of inflammation, linking IL-6 to metabolism rather than inflammation, and via JAK-STAT, may elicit longer-term anti-inflammatory responses in non-muscle cells20,66.

The IL-6-induced inflammatory response triggers expression of the chemokine monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, which is a critical mediator of macrophage migration and myoblast proliferation during muscle regeneration and tissue-repair67,68, mediated via IL-6/STAT3 signaling69. Of interest, the macrophage M1/M2 activation profile in skeletal muscle injury changes in response to the tissue microenvironment, with an initial pro-inflammatory phenotype, switching to an anti-inflammatory phenotype at a later stage in the repair process. In a study of 14 participants who underwent two rounds of repeated lengthening contractions, MCP-1 levels increased, and correlated statistically significantly with increased macrophage and CD8 + T-cell infiltration in muscle70.

MCP-1 also plays a role in cancer progression by enhancing tumor growth and invasiveness, recruiting monocytes, and triggering macrophage polarization into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. This, in turn, enhances angiogenesis, metastasis, and the creation of immunosuppressive T-cells71. MCP-1 also activates signaling pathways, including NF-kB, ERK/STAT2, and P13k/Akt72, and plays a role in breast cancer progression, with increased MCP-1 associated with poor prognosis73. In obesity, MCP-1 expression triggers insulin signaling in fat cells74, and increases expression of IL-6 and TNF-α, and continues to respond to insulin in insulin-resistant states72,74. In a mouse model of genetically induced insulin resistance combined with diet-induced obesity, insulin resistance in adipocytes increased MCP-1 expression thereby recruiting monocytes and activating proinflammatory macrophages75.

In our study, MCP-1 levels were statistically significantly greater at exercisers vs. controls at both timepoints. However, in exercisers, MCP-1 levels peaked at 45 min, but returned to baseline levels by 105 min, suggesting that in healthy, non-insulin resistant individuals, MCP-1 expression is limited to the duration of exercise in response to acute bouts of moderate activity. Studies examining the effect of acute bouts of exercise tend to be small and in men, with few studies examining longer-term post-exercise expression of MCP-1. A within-study comparison of nine men who underwent three 1-hour acute exercise bouts —high, medium, and low-intensity— on separate occasions, reported that MCP-1 increased significantly (P < 0.05) after all three exercise bouts, immediately after and 1 h post exercise, compared to baseline76. In an RCT of 22 healthy sedentary men randomized to 6 sessions over 2 weeks of moderate intensity at 60% VO2peak training (MIT) or sprint intensity training (SIT), MIT, but not SIT, increased MCP-1 concentrations77, suggesting that exercise intensity impacts MCP-1 expression. A single bout of high-intensity training (HIT) at 100% of their VO2max increased circulating MCP-1 concentrations in eight men, compared to rest59. Finally, thirty-seven inactive obese adults were randomized to 10 sessions of HIT vs. moderate-intensity in the absence of weight/fat loss, showing no change in MCP-1 levels, which may suggest that chronic expression of MCP-1 by adipocytes masks exercise-induced changes78.

We found no association between exercise and irisin levels between arms. Irisin is a hormone released from muscle and adipose tissue in response to exercise. It induces browning of white fat, thus increasing energy expenditure and improving glucose homeostasis78. It has also been shown to have direct tumor suppression effects on the number, migration, and viability of malignant epithelial breast cancer cells through induction of cell apoptosis and suppression of NF-kB activity28,79. Several small studies have studied the effects of a single bout of exercise on circulating irisin levels; one recent study of high-intensity interval exercise training in women at higher risk for breast cancer found no effect on irisin levels80; another showed an increase after a single bout of exercise in eleven obese women81. However, it should be noted that the inter-batch coefficient of variation (CV) for the irisin assay was 33.47%, limiting confidence in observed results.

Our previous studies on the effects of chronic exercise— a yearlong moderate-vigorous exercise program, 5 times per week in postmenopausal women— on insulin and glucose54,82, IL-637,83, CRP37,84, PAI-I and VEGF32,33 show different patterns compared to findings from the current study. CRP was reduced in exercisers compared to controls in all three studies, regardless of intervention intensity. In contrast, IL-6, insulin, and glucose were statistically significantly increased after an acute exercise bout, compared to reductions seen in the intervention arms in the NEW and PATH studies. VEGF was also increased in ACE and reduced in NEW and PATH, but these effects were not statistically significant.

We previously demonstrated that weight loss rather than exercise appeared to account for the majority of the reductions in both VEGF and PAI-1 —both identified as drivers of tumor angiogenesis— with increasing weight loss associated with statistically significant linear decreases in all analytes32,33. In the current study, an acute bout of exercise did not induce large changes in either analyte compared to controls. However, we did observe a non-statistically significant increase in VEGF in exercisers at 45 min (+3.2%), and a subsequent drop to −13.0% at 105 min compared to baseline. During exercise, the uptake of oxygen and nutrients needs to increase dramatically, supported by increased capillary formation in active muscle fibers85–87, and a study of acute exercise in adults demonstrated an increase in VEGF mRNA in muscle tissue88. Of interest, increased skeletal muscle capillarization after aerobic exercise improves insulin sensitivity89.

Strengths of this study are the focus on acute exercise and metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers that have been implicated in breast tumor development and progression, whereas most trials on exercise and cancer have focused on chronic effects. The intervention was a moderate-intensity continuous acute exercise; most studies examining acute exercise effects on glycemic control and other biomarkers focus on high-intensity interval training with a heart rate max of ~90%, which is unlikely to be replicated by most non-athletes. Our follow-up to 105 min post-baseline is uncommon. In addition, we examined effect modification and found no differences in exercise effects within categories of BMI, menopausal status, median baseline step count, or levels of fitness.

There were several limitations. Most participants were non-Latina White, and 87% had at least a college degree, limiting generalizability. However, the trial included approximately equal numbers of pre- and post-menopausal, with a range of baseline fitness levels. The comparison of exercise effects on some analytes did not meet our pre-specified criteria for statistical significance, and future studies should be designed with sufficient power to accommodate analytes with large standard deviations. However, our power calculations showed that we had good power to detect differences in analytes (see Supplementary Table 6). Estimation of the follicular phase using self-reported menstrual diaries is error-prone, although we asked participants to collect data for three consecutive months to improve accuracy90. Finally, we used the Astrand-Ryhming Cycle Ergometer test (ARCAT) to predict VO2max. While validation studies reported that ARCAT can accurately estimate VO2max in healthy populations of both males and females, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.9433 comparable to that of the observed VO2max91,92, it can still introduce error in exercise intensity prediction. Future studies may benefit from using ventilatory thresholds or direct VO₂max measurements to improve precision in exercise intensity prescription.

In conclusion, a 45-minute acute bout of moderate-intensity exercise significantly increased circulating glucose and insulin levels compared to controls immediately post-exercise. CRP levels were lower in exercisers at 45 min, but not at 105 min. MCP-1 levels increased in exercisers, as did IL-6; the latter is in response to exercise-induced damage and plays a two-fold role in energy signaling and in post-exercise muscle repair. MCP-1 also plays a role in tissue repair and has likely different roles in adipose tissue, especially in individuals with obesity. Unlike effects seen with exercise- or diet-induced weight loss32,33, levels of VEGF and PAI-1 were little affected by acute exercise. Further studies are needed to disentangle the different mechanistic effects of acute and chronic exercise, which will aid in developing prescriptions for daily exercise goals for cancer prevention.

Methods

The Acute Exercise in Women study (ACE; Clincialtrials.gov identifier NCT03779867, Fig. 1), conducted from 2019–2022, was a 2-arm randomized controlled trial to compare the effect of a 45-minute bout of acute exercise at 60% VO2max (intervention), compared to 45 min of rest, seated (control), on circulating biomarkers associated with increased breast cancer risk. Since the completion of this study in women without breast cancer, a study extension was approved to extend the population to include breast cancer survivors; these data from breast cancer survivors are not included.

Ethics approval

All study procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was reviewed and approved by the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center (FHCC) Institutional Review Board (FWA00001920) in Seattle, WA. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Setting and recruitment

Women resident in the greater Seattle area were recruited using flyers, advertisements on FHCC social media, and clinical trial registries. Participants received $25 after completion of sthe tudy data collection. The recruitment goal was N = 100 participants. Our sample size was determined by statistical precision. As shown in Table 3, we identified six primary endpoints. Our aim was to ensure that the minimum detectable absolute difference between exercisers and controls would be approximately 20% or less of the mean value for these endpoints. Based on power calculations, we selected a total sample size of 100 participants. This sample provides sufficient power to detect a 20% or smaller difference in at least two of the six primary endpoints.

Eligibility criteria

Individuals assigned female at birth, aged 18–75, able to perform all study requirements, consuming on average less than 2 alcoholic drinks/day over the past year assessed by self-report on the screening questionnaire, nonsmokers, with fasting fingerstick glucose levels <126 mg/dL93.

Exclusions included contraindications to exercise or to exercise testing, history of cancer or diabetes, and use of anti-hypertensive or glucose-lowering medications. Additional exclusion criteria for premenopausal women included: currently pregnant, breast-feeding, or using hormonal contraceptives, Depo-Provera, or hormone-containing IUD. Premenopausal women were also required to currently have regular menstrual cycles to allow scheduling of the intervention/control during their follicular phase. Premenopausal women were asked to fill out a menstrual cycle diary for 3 consecutive months to facilitate the calculation of the follicular phase. Additional exclusion criteria for postmenopausal women included current use of menopausal hormone therapy or other sex hormone therapy.

Physical assessment, VO2max estimation: Clinic Visit (CV) 1

Interested individuals completed a telephone or online screening questionnaire to assess eligibility. Screening-eligible women were invited to an in-person screening visit, where they signed informed consent, answered questions on their medical and health history, underwent a brief physical exam, and had fasting glucose measured. Medication and supplement intake were recorded. Pre-menopausal women underwent a pregnancy test.

Study staff measured height and weight and calculated body mass index (BMI, kg/m2). Participants completed a baseline questionnaire on exercise habits, based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)94.

Submaximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) estimation

If eligible after screening assessment, participants underwent VO2submax (ml/kg/min) testing at the same clinic visit. VO2submax was estimated using the Astrand-Ryhming cycle ergometer test (ARCET) VO2 submaximal testing protocol95,96, administered by an ASCM-certified exercise physiologist. During this test, participants were asked to pedal on a cycle ergometer (Monark 915E Exercise Cycle, Vansbro, Sweden). Baseline fitness levels were calculated for each participant, and categorized as fair/poor/very poor vs. good/excellent/superior97.

Between clinic visits 1 and 2, participants were asked to wear an actigraph movement monitor for 7 days. Actigraph data were downloaded using ActiLife 6 software (ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL). Step counts and minutes per day of Moderate and Vigorous Physical Activity (MVPA) were averaged over the 7 days.

Randomization

Participants were randomized via a statistician-designed program to intervention or control. The block size was four, with participants blocked on age group (</>50 years) and BMI (</> 25 kg/m2). The program was run by study staff after participants completed CV1 and were eligible for the study; study staff informed clinic staff who administered the protocol. Investigators and statisticians were blinded to randomization status.

Intervention/Control and biospecimen collection: Clinic Visit 2

Participants were scheduled for the second clinic visit and fasted for 12 h before a morning clinic visit. They remained fasting throughout the exercise testing, intervention period, and sample collections. Pre-menopausal women were scheduled for their second visit during the follicular phase (days 5–7) of their menstrual cycle, estimated using data from their menstrual cycle data collected over the preceding three months.

Participants randomized to the exercise intervention cycled for 45 min on a cycle ergometer. Exercise testing (see above) was used to develop the participant-specific exercise bout intensity corresponding to 60% VO2max. Participants had a 5-minute aerobic warm-up, a 35-minute cycle ergometer exercise bout, followed by a 5-minute aerobic cool-down (total 45 min). Participants randomized to the control arm rested in a quiet room, seated, for 45 min. After this, both groups of participants were asked to rest, seated, for a further 60 min (105 min post-baseline).

Biospecimen collection

30 mLs of fasting blood were obtained via venipuncture at baseline, immediately post-exercise/resting at 45 min, and at 105-minutes post-baseline. Samples were processed as they were collected, aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Assays were performed in serum samples, except for PAI-1, for which plasma was used.

Assays

All assays were performed at the Department of Laboratory Medicine, Boston Children’s Hospital. Insulin was quantified by a 48-hour, PEG-accelerated, double-antibody radioimmunoassay. Glucose was quantified using a Clinical Chemistry Autoanalyzer by measuring the combined catalytic activities of hexokinase and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase. HOMA-IR was calculated as (insulin (μIU/L) × glucose (mmol/dL))/22.5. CRP was measured using a high-sensitivity assay (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics GmbH, Marburg, Germany); IL-6, MCP-1, Irisin, VEGF, and PAI-1 were measured using Quantikine immunoassays (R&D Systems, MN). Duplicate pooled blood samples were included for quality assurance (QA) purposes. All samples from each participant were included in the same batch, and participants’ sample sets were randomly placed across batches. Laboratory staff were blinded to the study arm. The inter-batch coefficients of variation (CV) were as follows: Glucose, 0.75%; Insulin, 0.93%; IL-6, 4.24%; CRP, 0.54%; MCP-1, 1.75%; VEGF, 9.34%; Irisin, 33.47%; PAI-1, 5.7%.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive data are presented as geometric means (95% confidence intervals (CI)). Analyte data were log-transformed to account for non-normal distributions, using the Shapiro-Wilk Test of Normality. Intervention effects on biomarkers were examined in participants who gave blood samples at all three timepoints (N = 97; 95.1%). Primary outcomes were mean changes in analytes from baseline to 45- and 105-minutes, stratified by arm, obtained using the generalized estimating equation model (GEE) with identity link function for the comparison of the difference in change in the biomarker from baseline to 45- and baseline to 105-minutes between exercisers vs. controls. Intervention effects are presented as both absolute and relative percent change (δ%) compared to baseline. GEE models were adjusted by menopausal status (pre/post), BMI (</>25 kg/m2), VO2max (ml/kg/min), and 7-day average ActiGraph step count/day.

In preplanned secondary analyses, we examined changes in analytes for intervention compared to controls, between categories of participant characteristics: (1) BMI</> 25 kg/m2; (2) pre- or post-menopausal status; (3) average daily step count (median split) </>7294 steps/day; and (4) baseline fitness level (fair/poor/very poor vs. good/excellent/superior). We corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni corrections, for 9 tests, with statistical significance set at 0.05/9 = 0.006.

Calculating percent change in biomarkers between arms

We calculated the absolute difference between arms by subtracting the percent changes (δ%) in biomarkers from baseline to follow-up in controls, from percent changes in the exercise arm at 45 min [Δ45 min%] and 105 min [Δ105 min%].

All statistical tests were two-sided. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 8.2, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Note that due to space constraints and readability issues, we provide percent change in analytes in the Results section. Absolute values are shown in Table 2.
