ATR and TopBP1 oppose to control dormant origin activity and global replication dynamics, providing a first defense against replication stress
Authors: Stéphane Koundrioukoff, Nathan Alary, Su-Jung Kim, Thibault Collin, Antoine Toffano, Rodrigo Melendez-Garcia, Xia Wu, Yaqun Liu, Stefano Gnan, Sami El-Hilali, Olivier Brison, Filippo Rosselli, Chun-Long Chen, Michelle Debatisse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReplication stress and resulting genome instability, a major driver of cancer progression, stem from perturbations of replication fork progression. The first defense against this stress is activation of “dormant” replication origins, which supports replication completion. To determine whether ATR, in itself, contributes to this compensation process, we submitted human cells to a range of low stresses sufficient to activate ATR, not CHK1. Using molecular combing, we developed a dose-response assay that quantifies compensation efficiencies, enabling accurate comparison of cells with different genotypes. Combined with Repli-seq and OK-seq, this assay revealed that ATR activation is key to compensation triggering. We next asked how TopBP1, the main ATR activator, impacts compensation. In stark contradiction to what would be expected from its checkpoint function, we found that TopBP1 represses compensation and acts downstream of ATR. Instead, the function of TopBP1 in replisome assembly, which remains unclear in mammalian cells where the protein is not essential, well-accounts for our results positing that TopBP1 locks dormant origins at the pre-initiation stage, an intermediate in the assembly process, and that ATR activation allows assembly to resume. TopBP1 engagement in the pre-initiation complex would thus serve as a switch linking replisome assembly to the stress response.

Introduction

Duplication of large metazoan genomes relies on the firing of thousands of replication origins that must be tightly coordinated to avoid under- or over-replication. Assembly of functional replisomes is a step-wise process, which provides multiple opportunities for regulation. First, ORC directs the assembly of two MCM hexamers in head-to-head orientation, constituting the pre-replication complex (pre-RC) [1, 2]. This process is well conserved across eukaryotes although the specification of ORC-binding sites is not [2]. The second step consists in recruiting Treslin, MTBP, TopBP1, CDC45, the GINSs, and DONSON in metazoans, to form the pre-initiation complex (pre-IC) [1, 3, 4]. In addition to the S phase cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), this step requires the CDC7–DBF4 kinase (DDK), whose activity promotes recruitment of the Treslin–MTBP complex and TopBP1 onto the pre-RC. DDK then contributes to several key processes during S and G2 phases [5], and during mitosis [6]. The third step is the eviction of Treslin–MTBP and TopBP1, which permits emergence of the functional replicative helicase i.e. CDC45/MCMs/GINs (CMG complex) associated with DNA polymerase ε (CMGE complex) [7]. Finally, a fourth round of protein recruitment, including polymerases α and Δ, AND1 and MCM10 [3, 8], completes replisome assembly. Replication initiation then occurs according to a cell-type-specific timing program regulated at the level of large domains [9] by a complex combination of cis-regulatory sequences [10], trans-acting factors [11], 3D genome architecture, and chromatin organization [12–14].

In addition to its role in pre-IC assembly, TopBP1 has long been identified as a key activator of ATR, the apical kinase of the DNA replication checkpoint (DRC). ATR is recruited on RPA-coated ssDNA thanks to ATRIP, the mandatory ATR cofactor [15]. Execution of TopBP1 activator function relies on the RAD17–RFC complex that recognizes RPA-coated ssDNA at free 5′-ended ssDNA–dsDNA junctions, a feature found at slowed replication forks. Then, RAD17–RFC loads the RAD9–RAD1–HUS1 clamp complex, allowing TopBP1 to be recruited in a correct position to interact with, and to activate, ATR [16]. An alternative ATR activator, ETAA1, has more recently been identified in mammalian cells [17]. It directly binds to RPA-coated ssDNA and interacts with ATR, independently of TopBP1. Therefore, the two ATR activators strongly differ in their recruitment mode, which may adapt checkpoint activation to the nature of the replication stress [15].

One long-described response to replication stress is the occurrence of extra-initiation events resulting from the firing of so-called dormant origins [18, 19]. Although this compensation process is of prime importance, it remains unclear whether it requires an activation of the DRC [20]. To date, two main hypotheses, not mutually exclusive, are under consideration. A first one proposes that dormant origins are ready to fire but inactivated by forks coming from earlier/stronger neighboring origins. In this model the rate of fork progression i.e. the level of stress, passively determines the window of time during which a dormant origin has the opportunity to fire. A second one proposes that dormant origins are blocked at an intermediate step of the replisome assembly. In this case, the DRC would be an obvious candidate to actively restart the process. A major argument against this second hypothesis is that, from yeast to mammalian cells, activation of the ATR-CHK1 pathway represses replication of domains that have not yet started to replicate, which leaves unexplained how the checkpoint could simultaneously activate dormant origins locally and repress initiation globally [21, 22], a situation commonly referred to as “the local versus global paradox.”

A possible explanation for this paradox comes from two previous reports. One distinguished two steps in DRC activation [23]. First, cells treated with low concentrations of aphidicolin, an inhibitor of replicative DNA polymerases, display gradual chromatin recruitment of ATR and key proteins required for its activation, notably RAD17, RAD9, and TopBP1. However, accumulation of phosphorylated histone H2AX on S139 (γ-H2AX), p53 on S15, or CHK1 on S317 and S345 cannot be detected. In contrast, RAD17 phosphorylation on S645, a specific ATR target [24], increases markedly. Therefore, low stress conditions that do not induce DNA damages are sufficient to activate ATR, but at levels that remain below the threshold required to activate CHK1. This checkpoint status will hereafter be referred to as “basal ATR activation.” Higher levels of stress trigger CHK1 activation, which induces a wide range of downstream events, a situation referred to as “full checkpoint activation.” The second study [25] revealed that ATR constantly monitors the level of RPA at the forks. Under stringent stress, accumulation of RPA promotes ATR crowding, which triggers signal amplification and full checkpoint activation. Together, these findings suggest that ATR, on its own, induces local events while CHK1 elicits a large spectrum of additional responses at the whole-cell level.

In contrast to the contribution of TopBP1 to ATR activation, the role of TopBP1 in replisome assembly remains elusive in mammalian cells. Indeed, it is generally accepted that TopBP1 is essential to pre-IC assembly in these cells, like in yeast models [26] and Xenopus egg extracts [7]. However, several reports show that mammalian cells depleted of TopBP1 continue cycling although progressively accumulating DNA damages, which commits them to senescence or apoptosis depending on the cell model [27–30], a phenotype reminiscent of a checkpoint defect rather than a failure of replisome assembly. In support to this assertion, mouse embryo deficient in ATR or TopBP1 both die at pre-implantation stage E8.5 [28, 31]. In addition, a recent study identified the Treslin–MTBP complex as a key factor limiting the number of origins that can be simultaneously activated within a given time window in HCT116 cells. TopBP1 was found much more abundant than Treslin–MTBP [32], showing that it is not limiting for pre-IC assembly. Consistently, previous results have shown that, in contrast to TopBP1 deficient mice, TopBP1WT/Δ mice are normally viable [28]. These observations therefore raise questions about the role of transient loading of TopBP1 onto the pre-IC.

Here, focusing on cells undergoing basal ATR activation, we asked how a gradient of low replication stresses impacts the replication dynamics. Using molecular DNA combing, we found that a linear relationship links the density of initiation events to fork speed within replicating domains. The rate at which the distance between initiation events decreases as a function of fork slowing i.e. the slope of the line, allows precise quantification of the ability of cells to activate dormant origins in response to stress. The slope of the line therefore provided us with a tool, which had been lacking until now, for accurate comparison of the compensation efficiencies in various mutant contexts. We found that ATR deficiency causes a drastic flattening of the line compared to the control, while CHK1 deficiency does not. Therefore, basal checkpoint activation is necessary and sufficient to trigger compensation. Surprisingly, a very steep slope was seen in TopBP1-depleted cells, which indicates that the protein represses compensation, a role at odds with its ATR-activating function. We thus inferred that TopBP1 regulates compensation primarily via its action at the pre-IC level. Supporting this hypothesis, we showed that DDK, which enhances TopBP1 loading/stabilization on the pre-ICs [5], represses compensation like TopBP1 does. This dose-response approach thus yielded consistent results supporting previous works showing that, when linked to pre-IC, TopBP1 prevents CMGE emergence [3, 7]. Furthermore, our results strongly suggest that ATR and DDK antagonize to control TopBP1 eviction. We also generated data using OK-seq [33] and Repli-seq [34], which together support the key role of basal ATR activity in activating dormant origin. In contrast, not only constitutive origins fire in ATR-deficient cells, but their activity increases with the level of stress. Taken together, our results therefore (i) demonstrate, unlike common view, that basal ATR activity does not repress but actually promotes dormant origin firing, (ii) solve the “local versus global” paradox, (iii) pave the way for thorough understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing compensation, (iv) confirm that TopBP1 is neither essential nor limiting for replisome assembly in mammalian cells, (v) show that TopBP1 represses dormant origin firing in the absence of stress while constitutive origins escape this regulation, confirming that constitutive and dormant origins belong to different origin classes, (vi) show that basal level of ATR activation does not impact the relative timing of replication domains, but contributes to regulate the pace of their activation, and (vii) provide a new and simple procedure derived from Repli-seq, enabling mapping of early constitutive origins.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection procedures

JEFF cells (EBV-immortalized human lymphocytes) were grown in RPMI 1640 + GlutaMAX-I medium (61870-010, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and HCT116 and HCT116 TopBP1 Dg cells in McCoy’s 5A Medium (26600-080, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Both media were supplemented with 10% foetal calf serum (CVFSVF00-0T, Eurobio) and 100 μg/ml of penicillin and streptomycin (151140-122, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Incubation was at 37°C in humid 5% CO2 atmosphere. For transfections, 2 × 106 lymphoblasts were suspended in 100 μl of Nucleofector C solution (VCA-1004, Lonza Cologne AG) with 0.6 μM RNAi and transfected with the Z-001 program according to manufacturer’s instructions. Mixtures of RNAi directed against ATR (HSS100876, HSS100877, HSS100878, Thermo Fisher Scientific), CHK1 (HSS101854, HSS101855, HSS101856, Thermo Fisher Scientific), TopBP1 (HSS117145, HSS117146, HSS117147, Thermo Fisher Scientific), or ETAA1 (HSS122911, HSS122912, HSS122913, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for transfection. The AllStars Negative Control siRNA (1027281, QIAGEN) was used for siCtrl.

Inhibitors: source and treatment conditions

Treatments were performed with Aphidicolin (A0781, Sigma–Aldrich), VE-822 (2452, Axon Medchem), CHIR-124 (Axon Medchem, Axon 1636), 3-indol Acetic Acid (IAA, I3750, Sigma–Aldrich), PHA-767491 (S2742, Selleckchem), and hydroxyurea (H8627, Sigma–Aldrich). Aphidicolin treatments were for 4 h (including labeling periods when relevant). In the case of treatments combining aphidicolin and another drug, the cells were preincubated with the other drug before being submitted to 4 h of dual treatment. Unless specifically mentioned, preincubation times were: for VE-822, 16 h in lymphocytes and 4 h in HCT116 TopBP1 Dg cells, for CHIR-124, 30 min and for PHA-767491, 5 min in lymphoblasts, and for IAA, 4 h in HCT116 TopBP1 Dg cells.

Flow-cytometry and FACS sorting

Flow-cytometry: Cells were grown with BrdU 50 µM (5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine, B5002, MERCK) for 30 min to label newly synthesized DNA. 1.106 cells were incubated 20 min at 37°C in 100 µl solution BD cytofix/cytoperm (BD Pharmingen™ BrdU Flow Kit, 552598, BD Bioscience), washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and re-suspended in 700 µl of staining buffer (PBS with 3% SVF). After incubation with an anti-BrdU antibody coupled to APC (Allophycocyanine), DNA was labeled with 5 µg/ml propidium iodide (IP4864-10ML, Sigma–Aldrich). DNA content was measured on Accuri Flow Cytometer C6 plus (BD Bioscience).

FACS sorting for Repli-seq analyses: BrdU labeling during 1 h for cells not treated with Aph and 3 h for cells treated with Aph to reach sufficient amounts of labeled DNA. After fixation with 70% ethanol and incubation over-night with 15 μg/ml Hoechst 33342 (H3570, Thermo Fisher Scientific), cells were re-suspended in 1× PBS and sorted in six fractions at a time by flow cytometry based on their DNA content using a BD Biosciences INFLUX cell sorter.

Molecular DNA combing

Molecular DNA combing was performed as previously described [35]. In brief, newly synthesized DNA was labeled by two successive pulses (30 min each) with IdU 20 μM (5-iodo-2′-deoxyuridine, 57830, MERCK) and CldU 100 μM (5-chloro-2′-deoxyuridine, C6891, MERCK). Cells were embedded in agarose block and DNA was prepared and combed on silanized coverslips. IdU and CldU detection, and DNA counterstaining were performed as previously described [35].

Protein extractions and western blotting

Total protein extracts were prepared with 2.106 cells re-suspended in 50 µl of loading buffer (B7709S, Biolabs) containing 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 10 mM DTT and 10 mM MgCl2, then incubated with 50 U of benzonase (D0017, Millipore) for 30 min at room to digest DNA. Chromatin fractionation was performed as described [36]. Proteins were analyzed by electrophoresis in NuPAGE™ 4%–12% Bis–tris Gel (NP0323, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or NuPAGE™ 3%–8% Tris–Acetate Gel (EA03755, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the running buffer MOPS SDS (NP0001, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Tris–Acetate SDS (LA0041, Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. Proteins were transferred with an Iblot machine (kit IB301001, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using program 3. Saturation was performed in PBS with 0.05% Tween 20 and 5% BSA. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies then secondary antibodies coupled to horseradish peroxidase overnight at 4°C and 1 h at room temperature, respectively. Membranes were then washed three times for 10 min in PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20. The chemiluminescence signals were revealed using the WesternBright ECL kit (K-12045, Advansta) and acquired with the Amersham Imager 680 (GE HealthCare). Signals on digital images were quantified with the Image Gauge software (Fujifilm).

Statistical analysis of stressline parallelism

To assess whether the stressline slopes differed between conditions and groups, we performed a series of linear regression analyses examining the relationship between median inter-origin distance (IOD, kb) and median of fork speed (kb/min) according to treatment. The homogeneity of regression slopes was tested with the R function analysis of variance (ANOVA) (v.4.2.1). For each comparison, we fitted two nested linear models: (i) a common-slope model, which assumed a uniform relationship between the median IOD and the median fork speed across compared groups, resulting in a single shared slope, and (ii) a variable-slope model, which included an interaction term between treatment and IOD, allowing different conditions to have distinct slopes. We conducted an ANOVA to compare these models and evaluate whether including the interaction term significantly improved the model’s fit. The ANOVA framework uses an F-test to gauge the statistical significance of slope differences by comparing the variance explained by the regression model to the residual variance. The null hypothesis (H0) states that the interaction term is equal to zero, implying that the slopes are parallel between conditions. The alternative hypothesis (H1) posits that the slopes for the two compared conditions differ. Significant P-values (P < .05) provide strong evidence against H0 in favor of slope differences. Conversely, large P-values (P ≥ .05) indicate insufficient evidence to reject H0, therefore, the data not providing strong support for a difference in slopes. This approach was applied to pairwise comparisons of stressline slopes between conditions (Table 1). It was also used to categorize groups of treatment conditions with no statistically significant differences in their stressline slopes (Fig. 3D). The median IOD and fork speed of those conditions were combined, and the grouped conditions were treated as single entities, allowing subsequent inter-group stressline slope comparisons.

Repli-seq data processing

The Repli-Seq data were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq2 (v2.15.0, v2.18.12). Read quality was assessed using FastQC [37] and Illumina adapters were removed with Cutadapt [38] (v1.3–v1.9), retaining reads with a minimal length of ten nucleotides. Filtered reads were aligned on the human reference genome (hg19) using bwa-mem2 [39] (v2.2.1). The polymerase-chain reaction duplicates were removed with Picard (MarkDuplicates, v3.3; http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Only properly paired-end reads with a minimum mapping quality score (MAPQ) of 6 were retained for downstream analysis. Read density was then computed in nonoverlapping 10 kb windows for each sample and normalized to the sample with the lowest number of reads. We defined as “consensus” sample the average read density of the 6 ATRAP-seq (three replicates at 300 nM aphidicolin and three at 600 nM aphidicolin; all replicates cotreated with ATRi) processed as above. For visualization, counts were computed in 1 kb nonoverlapping windows, smoothed with a centered 50 kb rolling mean (1 kb step), and the total read count was normalized to 15 million. Read filtering and density computation were performed using the R package bamsignals (v1.34, https://bioconductor.org/packages/bamsignals). Read normalization and conversion to BED and BigWig was done using custom scripts.

ATRi + Aph peaks calling

Bursts of newly replicated DNA have been mapped genome-wide on 10 kb nonoverlapping, normalized G1/S1 Repli-seq profiles using an in-house peak caller described below in detail. For each chromosome, the read-density profile was convolved with the first derivative of a Gaussian kernel (σ = 3 bins = 30 kb) to detect local maxima and with the second derivative of the same Gaussian (kernel extent ± 9 bins = ±90 kb) to define inflection points. Peaks were called when a local maximum lay between two adjacent inflection points of opposite sign and were retained only if a local maximum overlapped the candidate region. Peaks identified on the “consensus” sample were called consensus peaks. Early consensus peaks with a mean replication timing value (S50) lesser than 0.4, selected using our previous study of nontreated cell [35], were defined as ATRi + Aph peaks. S50 is defined as the time point during S phase at which a given genomic sequence has been replicated in 50% of the cell population, expressed on a normalized scale ranging from 0 (earliest replication) to 1 (latest replication). Convolution and peak calling were performed using custom R scripts.

Over-activation of early constitutive origins

To define the early constitutive origins with increased G1/S1 Repli-seq signal in ATR-depleted cells treated with Aph, genome-wide differential enrichment between ATRi + Aph (n = 6) and nontreated control (NT, n = 4) samples was performed on raw read counts in 10-kb nonoverlapping windows using DESeq2 [38] (v1.36; Wald test, fitType = “local”). Raw count matrices were supplied to DESeq2, which performs internal size-factor normalization using the median-of-ratios method to estimate per-sample size factors. Regions were considered significantly enriched in ATRAP-seq samples when the Wald-test P-value was <.05 and log2-fold change >0.45. Over-activated early constitutive origins were defined as ATRi + Aph peaks overlapping with windows significantly significantly enriched in ATRAP-seq samples compared to NT. Similar differential analysis have been performed using different control samples i.e. 4 APH versus 6 ATRi + Aph and 3 ATRi versus 6 ATRi + Aph (Supplementary Fig. S3D). DESeq2 calls and downstream overlap analyses were performed in R using custom scripts.

OK-seq: EdU labelling and data processing

Lymphoblasts at 0.7–0.8 × 106 cells ml−1 were pulse-labeled with 20 µM 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) for 2 min. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (2.5 mM) was then added and the flasks were rapidly chilled in ice-cold water. Cells were harvested and stored at −80°C until further processing. OK-seq libraries were generated as previously described [33]. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500/550 system using paired-end reads (75 cycles). Raw sequencing data were pre-processed into genome-aligned files with standard tools and default parameters: FastQC [37] (v0.11.5) for quality control of sequencing reads, Cutadapt [38] (v1.15) for adapter and low-quality read trimming, alignment to hg19 with BWA [40] (v0.6.2-r126), duplicate marking/removal with Picard (v2.22.5, http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Samtools [41] was thereafter used to merge two technical replicates to increase sequencing depth, sorting, and indexing the aligned files. Data were analyzed with OKseqHMM with default parameters to compute the replication fork directionality (RFD) and determine the locations of genome-wide initiation zones (IZs) as previously described [37].

Results

Development of a tool for precise measurement of the compensation efficiencies

To explore the relationship between the rate of fork progression and dormant origin activation, the landmark of compensation efficiency, we used molecular DNA combing (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A). This technique makes it possible to measure fork speeds and the distances separating adjacent initiation events, commonly referred to as IODs, on individual DNA molecules. DNA counterstaining allowed us to ensure that series of signals do lie on the same DNA molecule and to focus on molecules longer than 0.5 Mb, thereby avoiding bias toward measurement of slow-moving forks and short IODs [43]. It should be noticed that this technique remains the only one giving access, in routine, to molecules over 0.5 Mb in length.

We first analyzed human EBV-immortalized lymphocytes (lymphoblasts) challenged with 30–300 nM of aphidicolin (Aph), a range of concentrations that induces RAD17 phosphorylation on S645 but no, or weak, increase in CHK1 phosphorylation on S317 and S345 [23], a status called below basal ATR activation. Unless specifically mentioned, this range of concentrations was used here in all experiments involving lymphoblasts. We found that even the lowest levels of stress impact the IODs and that a linear relationship connects the median of IODs to that of fork speeds (Fig. 1B and C), which agrees with results previously obtained upon treatment of these cells with low doses of hydroxyurea (HU) [44]. As the linear relationship is independent of the mechanism responsible for fork slowing, we named this line “stressline.”

To extend these observations, we sought to study epithelial cells. We chose the HCT116 line and HCT116TopBP1Dg, a clone derived by Bass and Cortez [45] in which both TopBP1 alleles encode degron-tagged TopBP1 protein. Since HCT116 and HCT116TopBP1Dg cells were found to be much more sensitive to Aph than lymphoblasts, we treated them with concentrations ranging from 7 to 70 nM (used below in all experiments involving these cells). Indeed, in this range of concentrations, Aph does not induce CHK1 phosphorylation on S345 (Supplementary Fig. S1B) and alters fork progression to levels that permit accurate measurement of the length of IdU and CldU tracks with the same pulse-labeling time than that used for lymphoblasts, securing comparisons. Of note, representing IODs as a function of fork speeds rather than of Aph concentrations permits direct comparison of cells with different Aph sensitivity. We found again that a linear correlation links the medians of IODs to the medians of fork speeds (Fig. 1C), which generalizes this property.

The stressline, which depicts how the IODs evolve according to fork speed variations, constitutes a classic dose-response assay. Therefore, the slope of the line provides a precise measure of the compensation efficiency. Here, the slopes obtained with lymphoblasts, HCT116 and HCT116TopBP1Dg cells, seem to be very similar (Fig. 1C), an observation confirmed by ANOVA test (used below for all slope comparisons, see the “Materials and methods” section) (Table 1). The fact that the three stresslines are parallel rather than superimposed results from differences in the coordinates of the points corresponding to cells not treated with Aph (no-Aph points), which are most likely due to uncontrolled differences in their respective genetic background. Noticeably, since the no-Aph points align with those obtained after treating the cells with Aph, they are integral points of the stresslines but do not provide information on the compensation efficiencies in themselves (Fig. 1C).

ATR depletion severely reduces the compensation efficiency

We then took advantage of the stressline tool to examine the impact of ATR depletion on the compensation efficiency in lymphoblasts (Supplementary Fig. S1C). We conducted a preliminary experiment to determine how Aph treatments affect S phase fitness in cells transfected with siCtrl (control oligonucleotides with no known target) or siATR (Fig. S1D). In nontransfected cells and cells transfected with siCtrl, flow-cytometry analyses showed that Aph triggers modest accumulation of cells in very early S phase. In addition, a decrease in BrdU signal intensity per cell is visible throughout the S phase, indicating a general attenuation in the rate of DNA synthesis according to Aph concentration. In cells transfected with siATR, we observed a slight decrease in BrdU incorporation in the absence of Aph that, compared to siCtrl, does not further influence the response to Aph, securing interpretation of subsequent results.

Molecular combing analyses showed that the linear relationship connecting IODs to fork speeds persists whether the cells were transfected with siCtrl or siATR. Visual comparison of the two stresslines showed marked flattening of that obtained with ATR-depleted cells (Fig. 1D). ANOVA test analyses first showed that, compared to nontransfected cells, transfection with siCtrl does not significantly impact the stressline slope (Table 1), we thus combined the two data sets to create a lymphoblast reference line (Ref-L; slope 48) that we used in all comparisons involving lymphoblasts. Then, comparison of the stressline of ATR-depleted cells with Ref-L confirmed that the slope is significantly attenuated in the absence of ATR (Table 1). We conclude that ATR depletion strongly reduces the ability of these cells to activate dormant origins in response to fork slowing.

ATR inhibition reduces the compensation efficiency in lymphoblasts and epithelial cells

To determine whether ATR activity is involved in this compensation regulation and whether this phenomenon is specific to lymphoblasts, we treated lymphoblasts and HCT116TopBP1Dg cells with the ATR inhibitor VE-822 (ATRi). Preliminary experiments showed that after 4 h of treatment, the shortest duration compatible with the usual time of treatment with Aph, 100 nM of ATRi efficiently suppress RAD17 S645 phosphorylation in both cell types (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1E and F). This concentration was therefore used below. Next, flow cytometry analysis of lymphoblasts showed that ATRi, associated or not with Aph, impacts cell cycle distribution and BrdU incorporation in the same proportions as ATR depletion (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Consistent results were obtained with HCT116TopBP1Dg cells (Supplementary Fig. S1G). Molecular combing then showed that the linear relationship connecting IODs to fork speeds persists upon ATRi treatment, in both cell types (Fig. 1F and G). In lymphoblasts, the stressline slope was not statistically different from that obtained in ATR depleted cells (Table 1), demonstrating that our approach is robust and that dormant origin firing relies on ATR activity. In HCT116TopBP1Dg, the stressline slope of ATRi-treated cells was even more attenuated than in lymphoblasts (Fig. 1G and Table 1). Together, results show in lymphoblasts and epithelial cells that ATR activity is essential for efficient compensation.

CHK1 depletion or inhibition does not impact the compensation efficiency

As previously mentioned, CHK1 phosphorylation on S345 was undetectable by western-blot in lymphoblasts [23] and HCT116TopBP1Dg cells in the stress conditions we used (Supplementary Fig S1.B), suggesting that basal ATR activity is sufficient to trigger compensation. To consolidate this assertion, we analyzed lymphoblasts in which CHK1 was depleted or inhibited. Of note, we have previously studied the consequences of CHK1 depletion/inactivation in the same cells, in the absence of exogenous stress (no-Aph point) [44]. We have found, contrasting with ATR deficiency, that CHK1 deficiency triggers nuclease activation, resulting in DNA damages. These damages activate the ATM pathway, which perturbs the functioning of the ribonucleotide reductase and consequently limits DNA precursor availability. As a result, replication forks slow down and IODs shorten, regardless of the ATR status [44].

Here, we depleted lymphoblasts of CHK1 (Supplementary Fig. S1C) or treated them for four hours with 100 nM of CHIR-124 (CHK1i), a concentration that inhibits CHK1 without inducing more γ-H2AX accumulation and S15 phosphorylation of TP3 (p53) than CHK1 depletion (Supplementary Fig. S1H). Flow cytometry analyses showed that, alone or in combination with Aph, CHK1 depletion affects the distribution of the cells in the cycle and BrdU incorporation in proportions rather similar to those observed upon ATR depletion. CHK1i disturbs the cell distribution more than CHK1 depletion, notably cells accumulate in the first half of the S phase (Supplementary Fig. S1D). We then used molecular combing to compare the coordinates of the no-Aph point of cells in which CHK1 has been depleted or inactivated to those of proficient cells. Reproducing previous results [44], we found that these coordinates differ between deficient and proficient cells (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Table S1).

We then determined how CHK1 depletion/inhibition impacts compensation. We found that the linear relationship connecting the IODs to fork speeds persists whether the cells were depleted of CHK1 or treated with CHK1i. Analysis of the stressline slopes showed that they do not significantly differ from each other, again confirming that the assay is robust, nor do they differ significantly from Ref-L (Fig. 1H and Table 1), which agrees with results of previous analyses suggesting that compensation still functions in CHK1-deficient human HeLa and MRC5 cells, and chicken DT40 cells [46], as well as in vitro in the Xenopus egg extract model [47]. The same conclusion was also reached using microscope analysis of immunofluorescence-labeled replication foci in U2OS cells [48]. This consistency between results obtained in different experimental models with different methodologies, including our newly developed quantitative approach, definitely shows that CHK1 is not involved in compensation regulation. Taken together, results demonstrate that, with regard to the DRC pathway, basal ATR activation is necessary and sufficient to commit dormant origins to firing. In addition, the fact that the no-Aph point in CHK1 deficient cells is determined by a series of events ATM- not ATR-dependent [44], exemplifies a rational for why this point does not, on its own, predict the compensation efficiency.

ETAA1 depletion does not impact the compensation efficiency

The role of basal ATR activation in compensation being well established, we asked which of its two best-known activators i.e. ETAA1 and TopBP1 [17], contribute to dormant origin activation. We first focused on ETAA1 and determined how its depletion (Supplementary Fig. S2A, left panels) impacts lymphoblast distribution in the cell cycle. Flow-cytometry analyses (Supplementary Fig. S2A, middle panels) showed that ETAA1 depletion, alone or in combination with Aph, affects the distribution of the cells in the cycle and BrdU incorporation in the same proportion as ATR or CHK1 depletion. Molecular combing then showed that the linear relationship between IODs and fork speeds persists and that the stressline slope does not significantly differ from that of Ref-L (Supplementary Fig. S2A, right panel and Table 1). ETAA1 depletion thus does not impact compensation.

TopBP1 is neither essential nor limiting for pre-IC assembly

We then sought to determine whether TopBP1 is involved in compensation regulation and, if so, how. Since TopBP1 might impair cell survival, we performed preliminary experiments to determine whether cell homeostasis is affected upon transfection of lymphoblasts with siTopBP1 (Fig. 2A) or treatment of HCT116TopBP1Dg cells with 500 µM of IAA to induce TopBP1 degradation (Fig. 2B). We found that both cell types are still capable of performing at least two generations post-depletion with no (lymphoblasts) or modest (HCT116TopBP1Dg) doubling-time lengthening (Fig. 2A and B), which agrees with previous reports showing that TopBP1 depletion does not severely impair cell growth, at least at short term, in somatic mammalian cells [27–30].

In order to study more precisely the impact of TopBP1 depletion on cell cycle progression, we performed a time-course analysis of lymphoblasts post pulse-labeling (Fig. 2C, upper panel). We found that the timing of entry into S phase was similar and that the rate of replication progression was only slightly delayed, regardless of whether cells had been deprived of TopBP1 since the previous cycle (Fig. 2C, lower panels, dark-blue points). Therefore our results, combined with those previously published [27–30], confirm and generalize the conclusion that TopBP1 is not essential for replisome assembly in mammalian cells.

In addition, a recent study showed that the Treslin–MTBP complex, not TopBP1, limits the number of origins that can be simultaneously activated in HCT116 cells [32]. To extend this conclusion to lymphoblasts, we determined the amounts of PCNA and CDC45 bound to the chromatin. The amount of PCNA reflects the number of ongoing forks, therefore, the total number of initiation events occurring in exponentially growing cells. The amount of CDC45, in addition, reflects the number of pre-IC. We found that these amounts remain comparable whether or not the cells were depleted of TopBP1 and/or treated with Aph (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig 2B). Of note, a direct comparison of the results obtained under these conditions is possible because flow-cytometry analysis of lymphoblasts showed that TopBP1 depletion modestly impacts BrdU incorporation as ATR, CHK1, or ETAA1 depletion do in these cells, and that in combination with Aph the decrease in incorporation remains similar to that of Aph alone, at least in the experimental condition we used (Supplementary Fig. S2C). We therefore concluded that TopBP1 depletion limits neither the assembly nor the functioning of replisomes in lymphoblasts. In contrast, HCT116TopBP1Dg treated with IAA alone showed a decrease in BrdU incorporation, which potentiates the decrease resulting from treatment with Aph alone (Supplementary Fig. S2D).

We then asked how TopBP1 depletion impacts basal ATR activation in cells treated as described in Fig. 2A and B (upper panels). We found that RAD17 phosphorylation is abolished in HCT116TopBP1Dg cells while only attenuated in lymphoblasts (Fig. 2E and F left panels, and Supplementary Fig. S2E and F). TopBP1 depletion thus affects all the phenotypes analyzed above, more stringently in HCT116TopBP1Dg cells than in lymphoblasts, which may be explained by differences in the level of depletion reached using siRNA or degron, or in the genetic background of the cells, or both.

TopBP1 depletion over-activates compensation and suppresses ATR requirement

Molecular combing analyses showed that the linear relationship connecting the IODs to fork speeds persists in TopBP1 depleted cells. Compared to corresponding controls, we found that the stressline slope is considerably steeper in both cell lines, meaning that many more dormant origins are activated in the absence of TopBP1 than in its presence for equivalent levels of fork slowing (Fig. 2E and F, right panels and Table 1). Therefore, these results again confirm that the pool of pre-ICs is not limiting under the conditions we used, and identify TopBP1 as a repressor of dormant origin firing, the opposite of what would be expected from depleting an ATR activator. In addition, the fact that TopBP1 depletion over-activates compensation while suppressing RAD17 phosphorylation in IAA-treated HCT116TopBP1Dg cells (Fig. 2F) strongly suggests that TopBP1 acts downstream of ATR in the sequence of events controlling dormant origin activation.

To confirm this latter conclusion and extend it to lymphoblasts, in which RAD17 phosphorylation is not completely suppressed upon TopBP1 depletion (Fig. 2E, left panel), we codepleted these cells of ATR and TopBP1. Preliminary flow cytometry analyses (Supplementary Fig. S2G) showed that dual depletion affects cell progression in the cycle as does single depletion of ATR or TopBP1, regardless of Aph treatment. Next, molecular DNA combing showed that the linear relationship linking IODs to fork speeds persists and that the stressline slope of codepleted cells does not differ significantly from that of cells depleted of TopBP1 alone (Fig. 2E, right panel and Table 1). Therefore, in both cell lines, ATR acts upstream of TopBP1 in the pathway regulating compensation, the opposite of its positioning in the DRC. Collectively, our results allow us to conclude that TopBP1 action in the replisome assembly process is the actual driver of compensation regulation. We propose that basal DRC activation allows TopBP1 to be released from dormant pre-ICs, thereby committing them to firing.

DDK represses compensation

Our model predicts that inhibiting DDK, whose activity promotes the loading then stabilizes the binding of TopBP1 to the pre-ICs [5], will over-activate compensation similarly to TopBP1 depletion. Previous work has shown that among a series of inhibitors tested, PHA-767491 most efficiently inhibits those DDK functions [49]. We thus used this drug (referred to as DDKi) in ensuing experiments, at a concentration that strongly reduces DDK-specific MCM2 phosphorylation on S40 and S53 while preserving CDK-specific S27 phosphorylation (Fig. 3A, left panels).

Flow-cytometry analysis of cell cycle progression confirmed previous works [49, 50] showing that so-treated cells fail to enter S phase (Fig. 3A, right panels). Time-course experiments showed that cells that have already entered S phase progress through the rest of the cycle, albeit slowly (Supplementary Fig. S3A). To avoid perturbing the cell cycle too severely, we thus treated them as depicted in Fig. 3B (upper panel). Under such short treatment (≈ 4 h total), DDKi impacted cell cycle distribution and BrdU incorporation to the same extent as ATR inhibition, regardless of cotreatment with Aph (Supplementary Fig. S3B, left panels). In addition, DDK has been repeatedly involved in replication stress signaling and checkpoint activation [51, 52] but low levels of stress were not checked. We found here that, with or without DDKi, neither CHK1 S345 nor CHK2 T68 are phosphorylated in cells challenged in the stress conditions we used (Supplementary Fig. S3C), and that Aph-induced RAD17 phosphorylation was only slightly reduced, if at all (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S3C). These results therefore indicate that DDK is weakly, if at all, involved in the control of DRC activation in the experimental conditions we use.

Molecular combing again showed that the linear relationship connecting the IODs to fork speeds persists and, strongly supporting our model, that the stressline slopes of cells treated with DDKi or depleted of TopBP1 do not significantly differ (Fig. 3C and Table 1), which classifies DDK as an inhibitory protein in terms of compensation (Fig. 3D). We also studied cells challenged with both DDKi + ATRi and treated as above (Fig. 3C). Results of flow-cytometry analysis of so-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. S3B, right panels) remained roughly similar to those obtained with DDKi or ATRi alone. The stressline slope resulting from dual inhibition (36) is intermediate between those of Ref-L (48) and ATRi-treated cells (26) (Fig. 3C and Table 1). Therefore, DDK deficiency attenuates the requirement of ATR for compensation but, in contrast to TopBP1 deficiency, does not fully suppress it. Taken together, results strongly suggest that ATR and DDK antagonize in the control of dormancy, at least in part independently of each other.

At this point, statistical analysis (Methods) identifies three groups of proteins: activating (ATR), noninvolved (CHK1, ETAA1), and repressing (TopBP1, DDK), (Fig. 3D). These results do not agree with the conclusions of previous works that analyzed cell deficient in ATR or CHK1 [53, 54], TopBP1 [54], DDK [55] and SIRT1 [56, 57]. These inconsistencies stem from the fact that only the coordinates of points corresponding to cells not subjected to exogenous stress (no-Aph points here) were used while we show that these coordinates, on their own, do not predict the compensation efficiencies, as illustrated in Supplementary Table S1.

Repli-seq identifies discrete peaks in cells treated with ATRi + Aph

So far, we have investigated the impact of fork slowing on the density of active origins throughout replication domains, but whether, and if so how, the replication timing of these domains is affected upon stress has not been checked. We thus used the Repli-seq technique [34] (Fig. 4A) to compare the timing profiles of lymphoblasts untreated or treated with ATRi, Aph, or ATRi + Aph. By visual inspection, the profiles in untreated and ATRi-treated cells remained unchanged (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4A and B). The profiles were also essentially unchanged in Aph-treated cells (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4A and B). Strikingly, the profile of cells cotreated with ATRi + Aph displayed discrete peaks localized in early replicating domains (Fig 4B, G1/S1). Then, the profiles remained similar in all conditions at each step of the cycle. We checked the robustness of peak formation by performing independent biological experiments, focusing on cells sorted in G1/S1 following treatment with ATRi + Aph 300 or 600 nM (Fig. 4C). After normalization (the “Materials and methods” section), data obtained from the initial experiment and these replicates gave highly reproducible profiles (Fig 4C and Supplementary Fig. S3C). We identified 5403 consensus peaks (peaks detected on the mean profile of all ATRi + Aph samples), called ATRi + Aph peaks, across regions replicating with S50 timing values <0.4 on a scale of 0 (earliest) to 1 (latest), the S50 being the moment in S phase when a sequence has been replicated in 50% of the cells (the ““Materials and methods” section). Using the 4923 one-to-one overlapping peaks shared between cells treated with 300 and 600 nM aphidicolin, we found that peak widths were significantly broader at 300 nM than at 600 nM (p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹6, one-sided paired Welch’s t-test; Fig. 4D), which is consistent with the level of fork slowing [23].

The appearance of peaks can be explained by the combination of slow fork progression and selective reduction in the number of functional origins, suggesting the existence of two distinct origin classes. The first would correspond to origins whose firing relies on ATR activation i.e. dormant ones, and the second to efficient early origins that fire independently of ATR, most likely constitutive ones.

ATRi + Aph peaks co-map with early constitutive OK-seq initiation zones

To confirm this latter assumption, we used the OK-seq technique [33] to map constitutive IZs i.e. genome regions where initiation events take place recurrently [58], in WT lymphoblasts used here in the absence any treatment.

We identified 5657 zones across in early replicating genome, referred hereafter as constitutive OK-IZs, (S50 < 0.4; Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S4D). Remarkably, while stringent thresholds of significance were chosen for each technique (the “Materials and methods” section), we found that 80.5% of constitutive OK-IZs co-map with an ATRi + Aph peak and, conversely, that 72.4% of ATRi + Aph peaks co-map with an early constitutive OK-IZ (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S4D). Therefore, early origins functioning in ATRi + Aph do are constitutive ones, supporting the hypothesis that dormant and constitutive origins distribute in different classes [19, 57, 59]. In addition, our results provide a very simple method allowing mapping of early constitutive IZs.

ATRi + Aph treatment stimulates the efficiency of early constitutive IZs

Over 80% of early ATRi + Aph peaks (4394/5403) displayed a number of reads significantly higher than (P-value <.05, log2(FC) > 0.45, Wald test) those observed at the corresponding positions in untreated cells (Figs 4C and 5A). Those peaks were far less enriched in cells grown in Aph, and not at all in ATRi, showing that enrichment essentially relies on the combination of fork slowing and ATR deficiency (Fig. 5A). The mean OK-seq profiles showed that enriched peaks co-map with strong constitutive OK-IZs, while nonenriched peaks co-map with weaker IZs embedded in regions displaying complex profile (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, IZs associated with enriched peaks fire earlier (mean S50 values <0.20) than those associated with nonenriched peaks (mean S50 ≈ 0.26) (Fig. 5C). Since even strong origins do not fire in every cell at every cycle [60], their overactivation increases the density of initiation events, providing support to the replication rate.

Genome-wide density of active origins is ATR- and fork speed-independent

Molecular combing showed that, whether or not coupled with Aph treatment, ATR depletion does not impact the overall fork density (the number of forks per Mb of DNA, see the “Materials and methods” section), which reflects the overall density of active origins (Fig. 5D). Confirming this observation, western blot analyses showed that the amounts of chromatin-bound pol δ and PCNA remain constant in cells treated with ATRi alone and ATRi + Aph as described in Fig. 1G (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. S5A and B). Of note, the combination of Aph treatment with ATR deficiency does not substantially affect the distribution of the cells in the cycle in the experimental conditions we used (Supplementary Fig. S1D), enabling direct comparison of the results. Therefore, unlike the density of initiation events within individual domains, measured by the IODs, the density of those events across the whole genome, measured by the overall fork density, is independent of fork speed and of the ATR status. These observations are consistent with recent work showing that the Treslin–MTBP complex is limiting and thus determine the number of origins that can be simultaneously activated [32]. Therefore, our latter results can be explained by the combination of an increase in constitutive origin activity and a modulation of the pace of timing domain activation (Supplementary Fig. S5C).

Discussion

Although replication stress is recognized as a major feature of cancer cells and a key driver of their genomic instability [61], the processes modulating the replication initiation program in response to stress had remained obscure [62, 63]. Thanks to molecular combing, currently the only available technique permitting analysis of individual molecules longer than 500 kb in routine, we have developed a dose- response approach relying on direct observation of initiation events along replication domains, whose density accurately measures the ability of cells to recruit dormant origins according to the level of stress. Using low-stress conditions to avoid the many consequences of CHK1 activation on cell cycle progression and replication fitness [64], we found that firing of dormant origins relies on basal ATR activation. Therefore, at higher levels of stress, full checkpoint activation triggers both firing of dormant origins across domains engaged in the replication process, via ATR on its own, and the long-described repression of replication of later domains, via CHK1 activity [21], which solves the “local versus global” paradox. Our results thus offer a comprehensive view of the regulation of replication origin usage under step-wise DRC activation.

Together, the dose-response assay and Repli-seq provide a series of converging results supporting the conclusion that the pre-IC is the actual ATR-dependent step involved in the control of dormancy. This conclusion does not call into question previous observations showing that partial depletion of MCM or ORC subunits attenuates compensation efficiency and alters genome stability [63]. Indeed, it is expected that reducing the number of pre-RCs passively limits the density of pre-ICs, hence that of potential compensation origins. In addition, our conclusion is in line with reports showing that the balance between the complex RIF1-PP1 (phosphatase 1) and DDK, which controls pre-RC to pre-IC switching, is ATR-independent [49], and that RIF1 depletion accelerates early embryonic cell cycles in the Xenopus model, a feature accounted for by compression of the pace of replication domain activation, not by IOD shortening in active clusters [65]. In a more molecular perspective, we propose that TopBP1 blocks the switch from CMG to CMGE, an hypothesis supported by a recent structural study concluding that the TopBP1–GINS interaction is sterically incompatible with the binding of DNA polymerase epsilon to GINS [7]. This latter finding and our results are also consistent with single-molecule imaging experiments in Xenopus oocyte egg extract, showing that TopBP1 is always released from the initiation sites before DNA synthesis begins [66]. Then, commitment to firing of dormant origins would depend on the balance between ATR activity required for TopBP1 eviction and DDK activity required for TopBP1 loading/retention on the pre-IC. The molecular mechanism responsible for releasing TopBP1 is currently unknown. Notably, it remains to be determined whether ATR acts merely as an intermediate in the stress signaling pathway, or whether it directly promotes unlocking via physical interaction with CMG-bound TopBP1, or both.

Regardless of the answer to this question, we found that, unlike dormant origins, early constitutive ones do not need basal ATR activation for firing. Indeed, we observed that Repli-seq profiles obtained under combination of ATR inhibition and fork slowing display discreate peaks, specifically in very early replicating domains. Using OK-seq, we then mapped IZs in unstressed lymphoblasts. Comparison of the two datasets shows a remarkable overlap between early IZs and Repli-seq peaks. Therefore, the peaks correspond to newly synthesized DNA originating from very early constitutive origins, which raises the question of how those origins escape TopBP1 repression. Two hypotheses, not mutually exclusive, may account for this feature. First, constitutive and dormant origins may actually belong to distinct origin classes that respond to different types of regulation. This hypothesis has long been suggested by the different positioning of the two types of origins in the nucleus [59], and more recently by the observation that different epigenetic modifications mark constitutive and dormant origins [67]. Secondly, it is well established that DDK activity is low in G1 phase, starts to increase around G1/S transition, and culminates in mid S- and G2-phases [5]. DDK may therefore be severely limiting in very early S phase, so that the balance between this kinase and ATR would resemble that of cells cotreated with ATRi and DDKi, in which pre-IC unlocking appears to be largely independent of ATR activity.

We also observed that the efficiency of immediate ,early constitutive origins increases in ATRi-treated lymphoblasts according to the level of exogenous stress, which provides partial compensation backup. On the other hand, we found that the overall density of initiation events is independent of TopBP1, ATR and fork speed in the low-stress conditions we used. The two sets of observations can be reconciled if we consider that limiting factors determine the total number of origins that are allowed to fire within a given time window, as has been demonstrated in various organisms. For example, a set of factors including CDC45, Sld3/Treslin and Sld7/MTBP are synthesized in amounts well below those required to assemble all pre-ICs in budding yeast. Consistently, it was established that the time at which Sld3 and Sld7 associate with the origins during the S phase dictates the order of their firing [68, 69]. As mentioned above, a recent work shows that the Treslin–MTBP complex determines the overall number of initiation events and establishes the replication timing program in human HCT116 cells [32]. These findings together support a model whereby 3-D genome organization and/or epigenome features at the origins determine their ability to capture limiting factors. Firing of the most successful ones would then allow these factors to be recycled and replication to progress sequentially [11]. Adapted to the present study, this model implies that ATR and limiting factors jointly govern the distribution of initiation events, not their global number i.e. that compensation, evidenced by the density of initiation events along domains undergoing replication, passively modulates the time-course activation of later domains.

Our observations strongly contradict the repeatedly proposed hypothesis that ATR activity represses origin firing [70]. Indeed, current model posits that ATR depletion/inactivation de-represses origin firing, which results in overconsumption of DNA precursors, thus in fork slowing and IODs shortening [70]. Contradicting this sequence of events, we have previously reported that exogenous supply of DNA precursors fails to restore fork speed in ATR-deficient lymphoblasts, while they do in CHK1-deficient cells [44]. In addition, our finding that the overall number of initiation events occurring in a given time window is independent of ATR now rules out the idea that ATR represses origin activation. With regard to dormant origins, previous models proposed to explain their regulation are based on results obtained with ATR-deficient cells studied either in the absence of exogenous stress [70], condition that does not predict compensation efficiencies as demonstrated here, or upon cell treatment with a single high dose of drug, condition that does not provide accurate quantification of compensation efficiencies and in which CHK1 activation may interfere with the effects of ATR deficiency itself. The same experimental flaw compromised analyses of the role of CHK1, TopBP1, and SIRT1 in dormant origin regulation, significantly skewing interpretations. For example, it has been proposed that ATR is a repressor and TopBP1 an activator of dormant origin activation, the opposite of what we find here [53, 54, 70]. Of note, the idea that binding of TopBP1 to the pre-IC triggers origin firing not only contradicts our results but also those of previous reports strongly suggesting that TopBP1 is not required for replisome assembly in mammalian cells [27–30], and that TopBP1 is always released from initiation sites before the onset of DNA synthesis [66].

In agreement with these latter reports [27–30, 66] we show that lymphoblasts and HCT116TopBP1Dg cells depleted of TopBP1 are able to perform several cell cycles in the absence, or at least severely reduced amounts of the protein. In addition, in both cell types, TopBP1 depletion strongly increases the density of initiation events within replicating domains and does not impact their number across the whole genome. These observations strikingly differ from the marked attenuation of the compensatory efficiency that occurs upon partial depletion of MCMs or ORC subunits [63]. This comparison substantiates the notion that TopBP1 is neither essential nor limiting for replisome assembly, a conclusion that raises questions about its function in this assembly process. Our finding that TopBP1 plays a key role in compensation resolves this issue which, in turn, strengthen our model. Interestingly, recent works have shown in mammalian cells that loading/unloading of the Treslin–MTBP complex onto the pre-ICs contributes to establish a regulatory loop that couples completion of replication to G2 phase onset [71]. Therefore, the three proteins whose removal from the pre-ICs precedes completion of replisome assembly play major and complementary roles in coordinating S phase progression with global cell cycle fitness.
