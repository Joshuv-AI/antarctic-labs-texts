Visualization of OPN‐Targeted Self‐Assembled Micelles Encapsulated With an SIRT6 Activator and Doped Indocyanine Green for Attenuating Vascular Aging
Authors: Yan Fang, Wenyue Li, Cheng Qin, Mengqi Xu, Naiyuan Sun, Tingting Lu, Zhiyi Fang, Yan Ma, Linghuan Wang, Sulei Li, Xiaojing Wan, Hui Li, Renzheng Chen, Xiaoying Shen, Lei Tian, Jing Liu, Yi Hou, Feng Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesSirtuin 6 (SIRT6) is a longevity‐associated gene with recognized therapeutic potential for vascular aging through the regulation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) senescence. This study aims to develop a visualized nanodelivery system for a SIRT6 activator to attenuate vascular aging in a mouse model.MethodsThe expression of the vascular aging marker, osteopontin (OPN), was detected by Western blot and histological staining in the aortas of naturally aging C57BL/6 mice. The SIRT6 activator MDL800 and the near‐infrared (NIR) agent indocyanine green (ICG) were encapsulated by poly(caprolactone)–poly(ethylene glycol) (PCL‐PEG), which was modified with OPN‐targeting peptides, to construct OPN‐targeting micelles loaded with MDL800 and ICG (OPN‐MI). Particle size and zeta potential were assessed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). The biocompatibility and in vitro NIR signals were evaluated in senescent VSMCs. NIR fluorescence imaging was then performed to visualize the temporal variation and biodistribution of OPN‐MI micelles in aging vessels. After 8 weeks of intravenous injection into naturally aging mice, bioinformatics analysis, pulse wave velocity (PWV) measurement, pathological staining, and molecular biological experiments were conducted to explore the anti‐aging mechanism of OPN‐MI micelles.ResultsThe expression of OPN in the aortas of naturally aging mice was significantly higher than that in control mice (p < 0.001), confirming OPN as a target for vascular aging. The OPN‐MI micelles exhibited a particle size of 107.008 ± 9.572 nm and a zeta potential of −7.293 ± 2.291 mV. These micelles demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, and their strength of NIR fluorescence signals increased proportionally with the concentration of OPN‐MI micelles. In vivo and ex vivo NIR fluorescence imaging revealed that OPN‐MI micelles accumulated significantly in the arterial walls of aged mice compared to MI micelles (p < 0.001). Bioinformatics analysis and validation showed that MDL800 reduced MMP9 expression by activating SIRT6 to deacetylate H3K9ac and H3K56ac. After 8 weeks of injection, the PWV of abdominal aortas in the OPN‐MI micelles group decreased (p = 0.031), the number of elastin breaks in the OPN‐MI micelles group was reduced (p = 0.016), and the intimal‐medial thickness in the OPN‐MI micelles group was alleviated (p = 0.048) compared to the aged group. Mechanistically, OPN‐MI micelles reduced MMP9 expression (p = 0.001) through SIRT6 deacetylation.ConclusionsThe visualized OPN‐targeted micelles encapsulating MDL800 and ICG effectively attenuate vascular aging in a naturally aging mouse model. This delivery system holds promise as a therapeutic strategy for delaying vascular aging in the future.Keywords: indocyanine green, micelles, osteopontin, sirtuin 6, vascular aging

Introduction

The dysfunction of the anatomical morphology and contractile function of blood vessels promotes the progression of age‐related vascular diseases, including large artery stiffness, hypertension, atherosclerosis, vascular calcification, and heart failure. These diseases involve a variety of pathophysiological processes and mechanisms. Vascular aging is one of the key pathological factors contributing to vascular dysfunction, characterized by the deposition of extracellular matrix, increased intima‐media thickness (IMT), fragmented elastic fibers, and accelerated pulse wave velocity (PWV) [1, 2]. Senescence of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) has been implicated in many of these age‐related changes [3]. Numerous studies have focused on alleviating vascular aging through interventions targeting cellular senescence, such as the selective clearance of senescent cells using senolytic agents [4]. However, the significant side effects associated with these senolytics limit their applicability and suitability for clinical use [5]. Therefore, identifying new ideal therapeutic targets and strategies is imperative for the effective intervention of vascular aging.

In recent years, epigenetic modifications have emerged as a crucial hallmark of cellular senescence and have demonstrated significant potential in the treatment of vascular aging [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Sirtuin 6 (SIRT6), an enzyme involved in epigenetic modifications, has been identified as a key contributor to species longevity, offering new targets for antiaging interventions [12]. Previous studies have confirmed that SIRT6 exerts its antiaging effects through the deacetylation of histone 3 at the promoter region of target genes, thereby regulating DNA double‐strand breaks, telomere maintenance, inflammation, and metabolism [12, 13, 14, 15]. Moreover, SIRT6 has shown promising therapeutic potential in VSMCs, inhibiting atherosclerosis, reducing vascular calcification, and protecting against thoracic aortic aneurysms. Therefore, the development of effective and precise SIRT6 interventions represents a critical step toward clinical translation. Given the broad roles of SIRT6 deacetylation, there is considerable interest in discovering activators of this enzyme [16]. MDL800, identified as a selective activator of SIRT6, enhances the catalytic efficiency of SIRT6 deacetylation [17]. Structural and mutational data have confirmed that MDL800 binds directly to the surface allosteric site of SIRT6, increasing the binding affinities of acetylated substrates and cofactors and exerting its deacetylation effects in various cell types. However, the development of small molecule drugs faces challenges such as insufficient vascular specificity and a short half‐life, necessitating multiple doses. Thus, it is imperative to optimize the delivery strategy for MDL800 to achieve vascular specificity and long‐lasting effects in vivo.

Engineered nanomaterials hold significant promise for enhancing treatment efficacy and precision. Nanoparticles can improve the stability and solubility of encapsulated cargos, facilitate transport across biological membranes, prolong circulation times to enhance safety and efficacy, and specifically target molecular markers to overcome the limitations of conventional free small molecule drug delivery [18, 19]. Polymeric nanoparticles are at the forefront of many drug delivery platforms for therapeutic compounds. Among these, polymeric micelles are most frequently used to protect and solubilize small hydrophobic drugs in their core through self‐assembly in an aqueous environment [20]. Thus, encapsulating MDL800 in polymeric micelles allows for easy in vivo delivery with long‐term circulation and specific targeting to vascular aging via modification with aging VSMCs markers.

Recent single‐cell RNA sequencing of multiple arteries has revealed that VSMCs undergo a phenotypic transition from a contractile to a synthetic, apoptotic, and senescent state during vascular aging [21]. Synthetic SMCs with high expression of osteopontin (OPN) significantly increase during aging, and OPN has been identified as a protein marker and potential regulator of vascular aging. Previous studies have confirmed that coupling OPN‐targeting peptides (OPNTP) to nanoparticles enhances identification and binding properties for OPN molecules on the membrane surface [22, 23]. To achieve satisfactory fluorescence imaging for tracking drug distribution, a fluorescence dye with a relatively longer circulation time and good biocompatibility is essential. Among imaging dyes, indocyanine green (ICG) is the only near‐infrared (NIR) spectrum agent approved for clinical use by the Food and Drug Administration [24]. To address the intrinsic drawbacks of ICG, such as nonspecific binding of plasma proteins, self‐aggregation, and a short half‐life in liquid solutions, we employed a micelle encapsulation strategy.

In this article, with the aim of targeting SIRT6 activation in aging VSMCs and enabling near‐infrared (NIR) imaging, we designed and synthesized an OPNTP‐modified micelle to encapsulate MDL800 and ICG. As illustrated in the Graphical Abstract, PCL‐PEG‐OPNTP was synthesized via a Michael addition reaction between OPNTP and polycaprolactone‐polyethylene glycol‐maleimide (PCL‐PEG‐mal). Subsequently, the SIRT6 allosteric activator MDL800 and the NIR agent ICG were encapsulated within the PCL‐PEG‐OPNTP micelles using a self‐assembly method. The characteristics of the PCL‐PEG‐OPNTP@MDL800@ICG (OPN‐MI) micelles were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and zeta potential measurements. The NIR optical imaging system was employed to monitor the temporal variation and biodistribution of OPN‐MI micelles in aging vessels. Additionally, PWV, bioinformatics analysis, and molecular biological experiments were conducted to elucidate the antiaging mechanisms of OPN‐MI micelles. This study aims to develop a visualized nanodelivery system of MDL800 and ICG to attenuate vascular aging in a mouse model, offering a promising strategy for achieving therapeutic efficacy and precision in future clinical applications.

Materials and Methods

ICG, MDL800, and angiotensin II were bought from MedChemexpress Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (New Jersey, United States). OPN targeting peptide (OPNTP) was purchased from Qiangyao Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), trypsin, and antibiotics were all bought from Gibco (Gaithersburg, MD, United States). Cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8) was obtained from Dongren Chemical Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Mouse aortic vascular smooth muscle cells were kindly provided by the Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). OPN antibody, α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) antibody, p16 antibody, p21 antibody, SIRT6 antibody, H3K9ac antibody, histone H3 antibody, β‐actin antibody, and GAPDH antibody were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology Co. Ltd. (Massachusetts, United States). H3K56ac antibody was purchased from Invitrogen Co. Ltd. (California, United States). MMP9 antibody was purchased from Abcam Co. Ltd. (Cambridge, United Kingdom). Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining kit, elastin van Gieson (EVG) staining kit, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining kit were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). The siRNA was obtained from Shanghai Genechem Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine RNAiMAX was bought from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co. Ltd. (Massachusetts, United States). All other reagents were of analytical grade unless otherwise noted.

Mice and Natural Aging Models

Male C57B/L6 mice (6 weeks) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Before experimentation, all mice were acclimatized for 1 week. C57B/L6 mice were maintained until 18 months of age to develop the natural aged mice model. Eight‐week‐old C57B/L6 mice were used as controls. All mice experiments were approved by the laboratory animal welfare and ethics committee of PLA General Hospital.

Pulse Wave Velocity Evaluation

The pulse wave velocity (PWV) was measured to monitor the stiffness of aging arteries. The abdominal aorta PWV was measured in mice using a small animal ultrasound imaging system (Vevo F2 LT; Fujifilm VisualSonics, Canada). Before ultrasonography, the hair on the abdomen of the mice was removed. Mice were anesthetized with a mixture of 1.5% isoflurane and air, and placed on a heating pad in the supine position to maintain a body temperature of 37°C. The mice ultrasound transducer was placed over the abdominal aorta to visualize the vessel's long axis. The imaging system software was used to measure and record PWV.

Synthesis of PCL‐PEG‐OPNTP

Attachment of OPN targeting peptide (OPNTP) to PCL‐PEG was based on a reported method in the literature via the Michael addition reaction between OPNTP and PCL‐PEG‐Mal. Generally, PCL10K‐PEG5K‐mal 100 mg was dissolved in 3 mL DMF, peptides (1.1 eq.) and triethylamine (3.0 eq.) were added to dissolve completely, and the reaction was carried out for 12 h at room temperature. The reaction solution was transferred to a dialysis bag (molecular weight interception 10,000 Da) and dialyzed in pure water for 24 h. The dialysis solution was collected and freeze‐dried to obtain the product.

Synthesis of PCL‐PEG‐OPNTP@MDL800@ICG

PCL‐PEG‐Mal was a long‐chain polymer. PEG (MW: 5000 Da) served as a hydrophilic unit to form the shell while PCL (MW: 10,000 Da) was a hydrophobic unit to form the hydrophobic cavity with ICG and MDL800 in the core. PCL‐PEG‐OPNTP@MDL800@ICG (OPN‐MI) was synthesized through the self‐assembly method. In brief, PCL‐PEG‐OPNTP, ICG, and MDL‐800 were precisely weighed and dissolved in 1 mL of a chloroform/methanol mixture (1:1), and then, each component was mixed and stirred evenly. Under the condition of ice bath ultrasound, the mixed solution was slowly added to 8 mL deionized water, and the mixture was stirred until the oil and water mixture formed a uniform emulsion under the ultrasound. Then the emulsion was transferred to a beaker, and the chloroform and methanol were removed by stirring at 37°C until the solution was transparent. Large particles were removed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and dispersed with water. According to the same scheme, nontargeted PCL‐PEG@MDL800@ICG (MI) was prepared by PCL‐PEG, ICG, and MDL‐800.

Characteristics of OPN‐IM and IM

The morphology of OPN‐IM and IM was illustrated by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) (JEM‐2010F; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The mean hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of OPN‐NP and NP were measured by Zetasizer Nano‐ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, United Kingdom) at 25°C. Each sample was repeated three times for each measurement, and the average value was taken.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading capacity (LD) in both formulations were measured by ultraviolet–visible spectrometer (UV‐3100PC; Mapada Instruments, Shanghai, China). In Vitro Stability Evaluation

The absorption emission spectra were used to assess the stability of OPN‐IM and IM in the liquid solution. Store the prepared samples in the dark at 4°C for 5 days. At the scheduled time points (0, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h), a UV–vis spectrometer was used to examine the absorption spectra.

Cell Lines and Induced Senescence

Mouse aortic vascular smooth muscle cells (MOVAS) were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin and streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The MOVAS senescence model was induced by angiotensin II treatment for 48 h and verified by β‐galactosidase staining.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity and Cellular Uptake

Cell Counting Kit (CCK‐8) assay was used for the cell viability assessment. Generally, MOVAS in the logarithmic phase were seeded into 96‐well plates. After attachment, new media containing OPN‐IM and IM at different concentrations (0, 10−5, 10−4, 10−3, 10−2, 10−1) were added into the wells to replace the before media, respectively. Cells were incubated with nanoparticles for 24 h, and CCK‐8 was used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of nanoparticles by the absorbance value at λ = 450 nm.

The cellular uptake was observed by a laser confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8 STED, Germany). Briefly, MOVAS were seeded in a Falcon 8‐well culture slide (354118; Corning) and treated with angiotensin II for 48 h to induce cell senescence. Then new media containing OPN‐IM and IM were added into the wells to replace the before media, respectively (an equivalent ICG concentration: 1 mg/mL). After being incubated for 12 h, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with DAPI. Finally, the laser confocal fluorescence microscope was used for observing the cellular uptake of the nanoparticles. The parameters were set at λex 405 nm for the nuclei and λex 633 nm for ICG. Image J was used for further quantitative analysis.

In Vivo NIR Fluorescence Imaging and Distribution

When the mice were 18 months of age, they were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 6), followed by injection with OPN‐IM, IM, or PBS via tail vein (an equivalent ICG dose: 1 mg/kg), respectively. In addition, another group of young mice (8 weeks) injected with OPN‐NP was used as the control group. Mice were anesthetized with a mixture of 1.5% isoflurane and air. At selected time points of 0, 10, 30, 60, and 240 min postinjection, the NIR fluorescent signals were acquired with VISQUE InVivo Smart‐LF in vivo small animal live imaging system (Vieworks, South Korea) with λex = 730 nm and λem = 830 nm. After imaging, the aorta and main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) of mice were isolated to visualize the fluorescence signals under the same conditions as described above. The imaging system software was used to quantify the fluorescent intensity at selected ROIs.

Histological Analyses

After the mice were sacrificed, gross arteries from the carotid artery to the furcation of the common iliac artery and main organs were isolated. The main organs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for HE staining to assess organ toxicity. Carotid artery sections were fixed for HE staining and EVG staining, while abdominal aorta sections were used for immunofluorescence (IF). After paraffin embedding, the sections with a thickness of 5 μm were collected consecutively from the proximal to distal aorta.

Photography was carried out using a digital pathology slice scanner (NanoZoomer‐SQ, Hamamatsu, Japan). HE staining was performed to evaluate IMT, and EVG staining was performed to evaluate the grade of elastin degradation in the carotid artery. The IMT and the grade of elastin degradation were quantified by a researcher who was blinded to the group assignment. Briefly, IMT was determined by the mean thickness of the outline from the lumen to the lateral aspect of the media using ImageJ software. The elastic fiber quantification is a score for the entire section and the elastic fiber degradation grades. IF staining was performed by the method below. The slides were deparaffinized, blocked with goat serum, and incubated with diluted OPN (88742; CST) and α‐SMA (19245; CST) primary antibodies, fluorescence‐conjugated secondary antibodies, and DAPI in the dark, respectively. The images were captured by a laser confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8 STED, Germany) using the same settings and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Western Blot

Thoracic aorta with adventitia removed was frozen in liquid nitrogen, and protein was extracted with lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor. The homogenates were centrifuged at 4°C to obtain the supernatants and perform protein quantification. The proteins were then separated using SDS‐PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with standard blocking solution and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After being incubated with an HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody, the membranes were exposed to the ECL substrate to obtain protein bands. The primary antibodies used were OPN (88742; CST), SIRT6 (12486; CST), MMP9 (ab283575; Abcam), GAPDH (2118; CST), p21 (37543; CST), p16 (29271; CST), H3K9ac (9649; CST), H3K56ac (MA5‐32138; Invitrogen), and β‐actin (4970; CST). The Western blot bands were quantified using ImageJ software, and the relative expression of target protein was calculated relative to the β‐actin or GAPDH.

GEO Bioinformatics Analysis

GSE246209 data was loaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Genes with Fold Change > 2.0 and adjusted p‐value < 0.05 were designated as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and analyzed using the DESeq2 R package (1.10.1). Visualization of DEGs was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 to generate volcano plots. Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analyses and visualization were conducted using the online site (https://cn.string‐db.org/), and the DEGs related to Aging signaling pathway were presented by heatmap using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) networks of the DEGs related to Aging signaling pathway were obtained from the string website (https://cn.string‐db.org/).

Transfection and Angiotensin II Treatment

Sirt6‐specific siRNA or scramble siRNA plasmid (Genechem, China) was transiently transfected into MOVAS using lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778150; Thermo). After transfection overnight, angiotensin II (1 μM) was added and maintained for 48 h to induce cell senescence. Finally, the cells were harvested for quantitative real‐time PCR and Western blot (Sirt6 and Mmp9).

Relative mRNA Quantification

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. After RNA purification, first‐strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using a reverse transcription system. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on an BD machine with cDNA as a template. Primers were designed to span exon boundaries to avoid genomic DNA amplification, and the primer sequences are provided in Appendix S1. Target mRNA expression was calculated relative to β‐actin. The delta–delta Ct formula was used to calculate gene expression.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Assay

Passages 3–8 of cells at 50%–70% confluence were used for chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP). Cells were incubated in medium containing 1 μM angiotensin II and treated with or without MDL800. After washing with PBS, 1% formaldehyde was used for cross‐linking. The reaction was quenched with glycine, and the cell pieces were cleaved by cell lysis buffer. Nucleus/chromatin preparation buffers I and II were used to isolate the nuclei. The nuclear pellet was washed, resuspended, and sonicated to generate chromatin fragments with an average size of 200–500 bp. Chromatin lysates were cleared by centrifugation and subjected to immunoprecipitation with ChIP‐grade antibodies against H3K9ac (9649, CST), H3K56ac (MA5‐32138; Invitrogen), and normal rabbit IgG (2729; Cell Signaling Technology). The DNA‐protein complex was precipitated with protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen) and reverse crosslinked using Proteinase K and RNase A. The DNA was purified and analyzed using qRT‐PCR. Specific primers were designed to amplify the Mmp9 promoter. The primer sequences used are provided in Appendix S1.

In Vivo Anti‐aging Effect

Mice were maintained until 18 months of age to establish the natural aged mice model. Eighteen‐month‐old mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 6 each) and injected weekly with OPN‐IM, IM, and PBS, respectively. Eight‐week‐old C57B/L6 mice were used as controls. After injection for 8 weeks, in vivo antiaging effects were evaluated by abdominal aorta PWV. After the mice were sacrificed, the carotid arteries were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for HE staining and EVG staining. Mice aortas with adventitia removed were frozen in liquid nitrogen, and the protein was extracted for Western Blot.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by SPSS 25.0 software. Quantitative data were demonstrated as mean ± SD, and the t‐test was applied for comparisons between two groups. The differences were considered significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Upregulated OPN in Aging VSMCs

C57BL/6 mice aged 18 months were used to establish a natural aging mouse model, while 8‐week‐old C57BL/6 mice served as controls. To evaluate the functional changes in aging arteries, pulse wave velocity (PWV) of the abdominal aorta was measured. As shown in Figure 1A,B, the aortic PWV in naturally aged mice was significantly higher than that in control mice (p = 0.002), indicating significant arterial stiffness and loss of elasticity in aged mice.

Upregulated OPN in aging VSMCs. (A, B) Pulse wave velocity and quantitative analysis to evaluate the stiffness of aged arteries. (C–E) HE and EVG staining and quantitative analysis to evaluate the intima‐media thickness and elastin changes of aged arteries. (F–I) P16 and p21 expression and quantitative analysis to evaluate the senescence of aged arteries, and OPN expression was increased. (J, K) Immunofluorescence co‐staining of OPN and SMA, indicating increased OPN mainly in VSMCs, and quantitative analysis. (n = 6; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Histological changes in aging arteries were assessed using HE and EVG staining, as depicted in Figure 1C–E. Quantitative analysis of IMT revealed that the arteries of naturally aged mice were significantly thicker than those of control mice (p < 0.001). Additionally, the number of elastin breaks in the arteries of naturally aged mice was higher than that in control mice (p < 0.001).

To characterize vascular aging at the molecular level, the expression of senescence markers (p16 and p21) in thoracic aortas was detected by Western blot. As shown in Figure 1F–H, the expression of p16 and p21 was significantly increased in naturally aged mice compared to control mice. Concurrently, the expression of osteopontin (OPN) was enhanced in naturally aged mice compared to control mice (Figure 1I), suggesting that OPN can serve as a marker for targeting vascular aging. Fluorescence co‐staining of OPN and smooth muscle actin (SMA) confirmed that the upregulation of OPN was primarily localized in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), further validating OPN as a marker for targeting VSMCs in vascular aging.

Characterization of OPN‐IM and IM

After encapsulating the drug and dye, the micelles maintained their uniform size and spherical morphology, as observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 2A). Specifically, the particle sizes of OPN‐MI and MI micelles were measured to be 107.008 ± 9.572 nm and 95.473 ± 28.309 nm, respectively (Figure 2B,C). The zeta potentials of OPN‐MI and MI micelles were −7.293 ± 2.291 mV and −4.667 ± 1.319 mV, respectively. These measurements indicate that the OPN‐MI micelles have a slightly larger size and more negative charge compared to MI micelles, likely due to the presence of the OPN‐targeting peptide.

Characterization, in vitro cytotoxicity, cellular uptake, and fluorescence of micelles. (A–C) Transmission electron microscopy images, particle sizes, and zeta potential of OPN‐MI and MI. (D, E) Characteristic absorption peaks and emission peaks of OPN‐MI and free ICG. (F, G) MDL800 encapsulation efficiency, loading capacity, and release of OPN‐MI and MI. (H‐J) Cytotoxicity and cellular uptake of OPN‐MI and MI in vitro. (K, L) Fluorescence intensity increased with the increase in the concentration of OPN‐MI and MI. (n = 3; **p < 0.01).

The characteristic absorption and emission spectra of free ICG were observed at 780 and 842 nm, respectively (Figure 2D,E). OPN‐MI micelles exhibited similar absorption and emission peaks at these wavelengths, confirming the successful encapsulation of ICG within the micelles.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) of MDL800 in the micelles were determined using an ultraviolet–visible spectrometer. The EE of MDL800 was 82.798% ± 2.788% in OPN‐MI micelles and 84.605% ± 2.902% in MI micelles, with corresponding LE values of 4.604% ± 0.461% and 4.706% ± 0.202%, respectively (Figure 2F). These results demonstrate ideal encapsulation efficiency for hydrophobic drugs due to the hydrophobic nature of the PCL segment in the micelles.

The drug release profile is a critical characteristic influencing the in vivo performance of micelles. As shown in Figure 2G, the in vitro release of MDL800 from the micelles exhibited a typical biphasic pattern. An initial burst release of MDL800 was observed, with 32.733% ± 1.191% released from OPN‐MI micelles and 30.367% ± 1.327% released from MI micelles within the first day. This was followed by a sustained release phase, with more than 60% of the drug released after 120 h. This biphasic release pattern is advantageous for maintaining therapeutic drug levels over an extended period, thereby enhancing the efficacy of the drug delivery system.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity, Cellular Uptake, and Fluorescence Intensity

The in vitro cytotoxicity of the micelles is presented in Figure 2H. CCK‐8 assay results indicated that there was no significant difference in cell viability between cells incubated with micelles at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μg/mL. This finding demonstrates the excellent biocompatibility of the micelles, suggesting that they are nontoxic to cells within the tested concentration range.

To evaluate the targeting ability of the OPN‐MI micelles, senescent MOVAS cells induced by ANG II were used to assess cellular uptake efficiency. As shown in Figure 2I,J, red fluorescence was significantly more pronounced in cells incubated with OPN‐MI for 12 h compared to those incubated with MI micelles. Quantitative analysis revealed that the internalization of OPN‐MI micelles in senescent MOVAS cells was nearly 3‐fold greater than that of MI micelles (p = 0.007). These results confirm that OPN‐MI micelles can effectively promote drug accumulation in senescent MOVAS cells, which is crucial for developing targeted intervention strategies for in vivo applications.

Before proceeding to in vivo fluorescence imaging, the relationship between micelle concentration and fluorescence intensity was examined in vitro. As depicted in Figure 2K,L, the fluorescence intensity increased significantly with increasing micelle concentration. The results showed a strong correlation between fluorescence intensity and micelle concentration, indicating that the micelles can be effectively used for fluorescence imaging to monitor drug distribution in vivo.

In Vivo NIR Fluorescence Imaging and Distribution

When aged mice reached 18 months old, OPN‐MI micelles, MI micelles, and PBS were injected via the tail vein. As a control, 8‐week‐old mice were also injected with OPN‐MI micelles. At 10 min postinjection, the fluorescent signal intensity peaked and then gradually decreased, as shown in Figure 3A,B. In aged mice injected with PBS, no fluorescent signal was detected, while aged mice injected with OPN‐MI or MI micelles exhibited obvious fluorescent signals, confirming the encapsulation of ICG within the micelles. Notably, the fluorescence was more pronounced in aged mice injected with OPN‐MI compared to those injected with MI, as confirmed by quantitative analysis of fluorescent signals (p = 0.017) at 10 min postinjection. These results suggest that OPN‐MI micelles can enhance drug accumulation in aging vessels due to the targeting ability of OPN peptides. Consistent with the enhanced OPN expression in naturally aged mice compared to control mice (Figure 1), the fluorescent signal intensity in aged mice injected with OPN‐MI was higher than that in control mice injected with OPN‐MI (p = 0.024).

After imaging, the aorta and major organs of the mice were isolated to visualize the fluorescent signals. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging results (Figure 3C) showed a marked signal in the aorta of aged mice injected with OPN‐MI compared to aged mice injected with MI and control mice injected with OPN‐MI, let alone aged mice injected with PBS. To determine the distribution of micelles in various organs, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were isolated and imaged for fluorescence signals. As shown in Figure 3D,E, significant fluorescent signals were observed primarily in the liver and kidney, indicating that these organs are the major metabolic sites. Some fluorescent signal was also detected in the lungs. Following in vivo NIR fluorescence imaging, organ toxicity was assessed by H&E staining (Appendix S1).

Finally, immunofluorescence staining was used to observe the accumulation of micelles in the vascular wall. As depicted in Figure 3F,G, the fluorescent signal in the aorta of aged mice injected with OPN‐MI was significantly higher than that in aged mice injected with MI (p = 0.006) or control mice injected with OPN‐MI (p = 0.007). These results pathologically confirmed that the OPN‐MI micelles delivery strategy achieved vascular aging specificity in vivo.

Bioinformatics Analysis to Screen SIRT6 Intervention Target and Validation

GSE246209 is an open dataset from RNA‐Seq analysis focusing on the effects of SIRT6 knockdown in vascular smooth muscle cells. To identify potential SIRT6 intervention targets in vascular aging, we utilized the GSE246209 dataset for bioinformatics analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with a fold change > 2.0 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05. These DEGs were visualized using volcano plots, as depicted in Figure 4A.

To verify whether Mmp9 is regulated by SIRT6, we transiently transfected Sirt6‐specific siRNA or scramble siRNA plasmids into senescent MOVAS cells. As shown in Figure 4E–G, SIRT6 expression was significantly reduced by Sirt6‐specific siRNA compared to the control group (p = 0.004). Correspondingly, the upregulated expression of MMP9 protein (p = 0.024) and mRNA (p = 0.001) was confirmed, as depicted in Figure 4H. These findings highlight Mmp9 as a critical downstream target of SIRT6 in vascular aging, providing a molecular basis for potential therapeutic interventions.

MDL800 Regulates MMP9 by Activating SIRT6 Deacetylation

MDL800 is a selective activator of SIRT6 that enhances the catalytic efficiency of SIRT6 deacetylation by directly binding to the surface allosteric site of SIRT6. To investigate whether Mmp9 is regulated by MDL800, we introduced MDL800 into ANG II–induced senescent MOVAS cells. As depicted in Figure 5A–C, the expression of Mmp9 mRNA (p = 0.009) and MMP9 protein (p = 0.011) was significantly reduced following MDL800 treatment. Previous research has indicated that MDL800 primarily deacetylates acetylated H3K9 (H3K9ac) and acetylated H3K56 (H3K56ac) by activating SIRT6 in various cell types [17]. Consistent with these findings, our results shown in Figure 5D–G reveal that H3K9ac (p = 0.012) and H3K56ac (p = 0.007) levels were significantly decreased after MDL800 intervention, while SIRT6 protein expression remained relatively stable (p = 0.817). To further elucidate whether histone deacetylation occurs near the Mmp9 promoter sequence, we conducted ChIP assays to detect the binding of H3K9ac and H3K56ac to the Mmp9 promoter. As illustrated in Figure 5H–J, the binding of H3K9ac (p = 0.014) and H3K56ac (p = 0.010) to the Mmp9 promoter was significantly diminished after MDL800 treatment. These results indicate that MDL800 reduces MMP9 expression by activating SIRT6 to deacetylate H3K9ac and H3K56ac.

In Vivo Antiaging Effect and Mechanism

To evaluate the in vivo antiaging effects of micelles, aged mice were administered OPN‐MI micelles, MI micelles, or PBS for 8 weeks, with 8‐week‐old mice serving as controls. The functional changes indicative of antiaging effects were assessed using abdominal aorta PWV. As shown in Figure 6A,D, PWV was significantly elevated in aged mice compared to control mice (p = 0.002). This increase was partially mitigated by OPN‐MI micelle intervention (p = 0.031), whereas MI micelle intervention had no significant effect (p = 0.260).

Morphological changes associated with antiaging effects were evaluated through histological staining of carotid arteries using HE and EVG staining. As depicted in Figure 6B,C,E,F, aged mice exhibited a significantly higher number of elastin breaks (p < 0.001) and increased IMT (p < 0.001) compared to control mice. Following OPN‐MI micelle intervention, there was a partial improvement in both the number of elastin breaks (p = 0.016) and IMT (p = 0.048). In contrast, MI micelle intervention did not significantly reduce the number of elastin breaks (p = 0.450) or IMT (p = 0.817) in aged mice.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that OPN‐MI micelle intervention effectively mitigated age‐related changes in arterial function and morphology, whereas MI micelle intervention was ineffective, highlighting the significance of targeted therapeutic approaches. Mechanistically, as shown in Figure 6G–K, MDL800‐loaded OPN‐MI micelles, modified with vascular‐targeting OPN peptides, significantly reduced MMP9 expression (p = 0.001) by activating SIRT6 to deacetylate H3K9ac (p = 0.013) and H3K56ac (p = 0.002) in aged mice. This mechanism underscores the therapeutic potential of OPN‐MI micelles in alleviating vascular aging through targeted delivery of MDL800.

Discussion

Identifying molecular switches that can delay the onset of aging‐related changes has long been considered a major challenge in the field of aging interventions. Gerotherapeutics that directly target one or several hallmarks of aging to delay, prevent, alleviate, or treat age‐associated dysfunction and diseases are urgently needed for future clinical trials [25]. In this study, we designed and synthesized micelles modified with OPN‐targeting peptides to encapsulate MDL800 and ICG (OPN‐MI). We observed the temporal variation and biodistribution of OPN‐MI in aging vessels using an optical imaging system. Furthermore, bioinformatics analysis combined with arterial functional analysis (PWV), morphological analysis (HE and EVG staining), and molecular biological experiments demonstrated that OPN‐MI reduces MMP9 expression by activating SIRT6, thereby delaying age‐associated vascular dysfunction. This study presents a visualized nanodelivery system for MDL800 to attenuate vascular aging, offering a promising strategy to address the major challenges in aging interventions.

The spontaneous organization of dissimilar polymer chains into a highly controlled suspension of particles is known as self‐assembly [18]. Thanks to advancements in diverse polymer architectures and functional chemistry, the self‐assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers, such as PCL‐PEG in this study, has become a common method for creating polymeric nanoparticles. Moreover, polymeric nanoparticles can cross biological barriers to precisely target specific sites, encapsulate a wide range of therapeutic agents, and efficiently release these agents [26, 27]. In this study, PCL‐PEG‐Mal is an amphiphilic polymer where PEG acts as the hydrophilic unit forming the shell, while PCL serves as the hydrophobic unit creating the core that houses ICG and MDL800. The OPN‐MI micelles show great potential as drug delivery vehicles due to their ability to protect and solubilize hydrophobic drugs and respond to vascular aging. Therefore, MDL800 and ICG can be efficiently and accurately encapsulated in micelles via the self‐assembly method. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of MDL800 in PCL‐PEG self‐assembly micelles exceeded 80%, and the absorbance and emission peaks of the micelles were close to those of free ICG, ensuring the feasibility of in vivo NIR imaging and SIRT6 activation via PCL‐PEG self‐assembly micelles. ICG, which can be visualized in the NIR light spectrum (700–900 nm), has been used in preclinical and clinical trials due to its safety and NIR imaging properties [28, 29]. Recently, ICG NIR fluorescence imaging was employed to assess bowel perfusion intraoperatively in a multicenter, randomized, controlled, phase 3 trial [29]. ICG encapsulated in micelles serves as a powerful fluorescence imaging tool with good biocompatibility.

The hallmarks of aging are widely recognized and include genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, impaired macroautophagy, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation, and dysbiosis [30]. VSMCs senescence is characterized by DNA damage and telomere attrition, epigenetic modifications, defects in protein processing, reduced nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, and decreased stem cell availability [3]. SIRT6, an enzyme involved in epigenetic modifications, exerts an antiaging effect by deacetylating histone 3 at the promoter region of target genes, making it a promising target for antiaging interventions [12, 13, 14, 15]. MDL‐800 is a well‐characterized allosteric activator of SIRT6, which regulates the deacetylation activity of SIRT6 to reduce levels of H3K9ac and H3K56ac, thereby influencing various physiological and pathological processes [17, 31]. The dosing schedule should ideally align with the natural history of the disease being treated. For chronic conditions such as vascular aging, continuous dosing is often more effective in maintaining disease control. As detailed in our revised manuscript, naturally aging mice were treated with OPN‐MI, MI, and PBS for 8 weeks, respectively. This dosing schedule is considered appropriate for interventions targeting vascular aging. The long‐term safety of drug administration is indeed critical. After detailed calculations, the concentration of MDL800 loaded in the micelles is significantly lower than the toxicity threshold. Additionally, indocyanine green (ICG) is the only near‐infrared (NIR) spectrum agent approved for clinical use by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which ensures the long‐term safety of drug administration. The conventional concentration and volume of micelles injected into mice are 1 mg/mL and 100 μL per mouse, respectively. Upon calculation, the concentration of MDL800 loaded in the micelles is significantly lower than the effective concentration required for therapeutic efficacy. This finding further confirms that the OPN targeting ability can enhance the local enrichment of MDL800‐loaded micelles, thereby reducing the circulating concentration of MDL800. This targeted delivery approach not only improves therapeutic outcomes but also minimizes potential side effects by reducing systemic exposure to the drug. Due to the ability of OPN‐MI micelles to target senescent VSMCs and slow release MDL800, we observed marked improvements in functional, morphological, and molecular markers of vascular aging following intervention with OPN‐MI micelles in aged mice.

Matrix metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP9) plays a pivotal role in the vascular stiffness process. MMP9 is an enzyme that degrades extracellular matrix (ECM) components, including collagen and elastin, which are crucial for maintaining the elasticity and integrity of blood vessels. Under normal physiological conditions, the synthesis and degradation of ECM are in a dynamic balance. However, in pathological vascular aging states, the expression and activity of MMP9 are often upregulated. The excessive degradation of elastin fibers by MMP9 disrupts the elastic structure of the vascular wall. Elastin is responsible for the recoil of blood vessels during diastole, and its degradation leads to reduced arterial elasticity. At the same time, the degradation of collagen by MMP9 also affects the mechanical properties of the vascular wall. An imbalance in collagen metabolism can cause abnormal thickening and hardening of the vascular wall, further contributing to vascular stiffness.

MDL800 has been shown to have a significant impact on attenuating vascular stiffness, and this effect is closely related to its regulation of MMP9. We confirmed that MDL800 inhibits the transcription of the MMP9 gene by activating SIRT6 to deacetylate H3K9ac and H3K56ac, as demonstrated by bioinformatics analysis and experiments. This regulatory mechanism helps restore the balance of ECM components, thereby alleviating vascular stiffness and improving vascular function.

This study has several limitations. First, our research has primarily focused on in vivo and in vitro experiments in mice. Further studies are necessary to determine whether OPN‐MI micelles exhibit consistent targeting of vascular aging in humans. Second, in addition to acetylation modifications, senescent VSMCs undergo various other changes during the aging process. Investigating these diverse alterations may uncover new intervention strategies. Lastly, the uptake of micelles by senescent cells likely involves complex transformations at the organelle level. These processes need to be elucidated through advancements in experimental instrumentation and updates in the design concepts of drug delivery systems.

Conclusion

We have developed a visualized nanodelivery system incorporating MDL800 and ICG to attenuate vascular aging in a mouse model. This system holds promise for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and precision in future clinical applications. The micelles, loaded with MDL800 and ICG and modified with OPN‐targeting peptides, effectively reduce MMP9 expression. This anti‐aging effect is achieved through the activation of SIRT6, which deacetylates H3K9ac and H3K56ac, thereby alleviating vascular aging.
