Sleep disturbance in patients undergoing shoulder surgery: a current concepts review
Authors: Kyle N Kunze, Krish S Sardesai, Kristen Barton, Jason E Jesse, Brian E Caplan, Samuel P Alfonsi, Daniel Shinn, Brian J Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep is a critical and multifaceted contributor toward patient health. Its relevance toward peri- and post-operative health following shoulder surgery is underappreciated. The purpose of this review is to consolidate existing literature pertinent to sleep disturbance in shoulder surgery patients to guide clinical care and identify future directions of study.MethodsA narrative review of the English-language literature was performed. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched from January 2000 through September 2025 using combinations of the terms “sleep,” “sleep disturbance,” “insomnia,” “Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index,” “rotator cuff,” “shoulder pain,” “glenohumeral osteoarthritis,” “shoulder arthroplasty,” and “shoulder surgery.” Clinical studies, systematic reviews, and mechanistic investigations relevant to sleep in the shoulder-surgery population were included; non-English articles, case reports, and studies not reporting a shoulder-specific outcome were excluded. No quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis was performed.ResultsAcross multiple shoulder pathologies, sleep disturbance affects up to 80-90% of patients pre-operatively, which may persist post-operatively. Nocturnal pain appears to be a primary driver of sleep disturbance. The female sex, comorbid depression and anxiety, elevated body mass index, and persistent post-operative narcotic use are associated with disruptions in patients' sleep. Interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha may be associated with increased pain and inflammation. Following surgery, the use of sling immobilization, use of bedding that is personalized to patients' bodies and/or utilizes memory foam, and sleeping in a semi-reclined position may alleviate pain and disturbance. Non-pharmacologic interventions like cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness are also associated with improved outcomes.ConclusionSleep disturbance is a common and multifaceted pathology affecting shoulder surgery patients before and after surgery. Clinicians ought to be aware of the modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors, which span from age and body mass index to comorbid pathology and medication use.Keywords: Sleep disturbance, Shoulder surgery, Rotator cuff tear, Glenohumeral osteoarthritis, Adhesive capsulitis, Post-operative recovery, Pain, Actigraphy

Sleep is a universal biological need, specifically regulating pain perception and cognitive–emotional processing, domains relevant to surgical recovery. In large population studies, abnormal sleep durations are linked to higher infection risk, underscoring the central role of sleep in immune competence.12,67 Previous literature has reported that poor post-operative sleep is associated with markedly higher rates of wound complications in surgical patients.14 Beyond wound healing, both experimental and clinical studies demonstrate that poor sleep quality enhances pain perception and diminishes normal pain-inhibition pathways, while interventions that improve sleep consistently reduce psychological distress and enhance cognitive–emotional function.22,28,49 These factors are especially relevant in surgery, where successful recovery depends on the capacity to heal tissues, modulate pain, and maintain resilience throughout rehabilitation.47

Recognition of the importance of sleep in patients undergoing surgery has gained increased attention within perioperative medicine, where sleep assessments have been performed through adjuncts such as actigraphy (a wearable wrist-worn accelerometer-based device that objectively estimates sleep and wake patterns from movement), bispectral index monitoring, and validated patient-reported scales. Indeed, epidemiologic studies applying these tools have demonstrated that poor sleep quality is associated with impaired recovery and higher complication rates.43,47,74 Importantly, the relevance of sleep to surgical recovery has also been tested directly in other disciplines: in total knee arthroplasty patients, perioperative digital cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has been shown to improve sleep quality and enhance recovery scores compared with standard of care.34 Yet, this recognition has not been extended uniformly, and sleep remains underappreciated in areas such as shoulder surgery. This knowledge gap emphasizes the importance of examining sleep in the unique context of patients who have undergone shoulder surgery, where recent studies have shown that sleep disturbance is both prevalent and uniquely burdensome due to anatomic and post-operative challenges.3,41

Indeed, sleep disturbance is especially prevalent among patients undergoing shoulder surgery. In a recent multicenter cohort of 973 patients with rotator cuff tears (RCTs) undergoing arthroscopic repair, 88.4% reported poor sleep pre-operatively, with 59% describing nightly disturbance, and although sleep improved after surgery, 22% of patients continued to report poor sleep at 12 months.21 A systematic review of 15 studies including 1,818 patients confirmed this finding, reporting a pre-operative prevalence of poor sleep ranging between 76 and 100% and post-operative prevalence of 19–62%, with every study documenting improvement over time.52 Several factors may contribute to the high prevalence of poor sleep quality in patients with shoulder pathologies, including physiologic cytokine-mediated mechanisms of pain specific to shoulder pathology and leading to sleep disturbance and alterations in sleep posture due to nocturnal pain avoidance and sling immobilization in the post-operative period.15,21 The literature in this area remains fragmented; most available studies rely on small cohorts and heterogeneous patient-reported measures such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Short Form Health Survey, or American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) Standardized Shoulder Assessment Form, limiting comparability across studies.52

The current review clarifies the role of sleep in patients with shoulder pathologies undergoing surgical intervention by synthesizing the available clinical evidence and highlighting areas of unmet need. By consolidating fragmented evidence surrounding sleep and shoulder surgery, the overall purpose of this review is to raise awareness of this overlooked clinical issue and provide surgeons with practical insights to better recognize, monitor, and address sleep disruption in their patients.

Methods

This current concepts review was conducted as a narrative review of the published literature on sleep and shoulder surgery. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched from January 2000 through September 2025. Search terms combined concepts related to sleep (“sleep,” “sleep disturbance,” “insomnia,” “sleep quality,” “Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index,” “actigraphy”) with concepts related to shoulder pathology and surgery (“rotator cuff,” “shoulder pain,” “glenohumeral osteoarthritis,” “shoulder arthroplasty,” “rotator cuff repair,” “shoulder surgery”). Reference lists of included articles and relevant prior reviews were hand-searched to identify additional sources. Clinical studies (observational and interventional), systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and mechanistic or biomarker investigations published in English and directly relevant to sleep in the shoulder-surgery population were considered for inclusion. Non-English articles, conference abstracts without full-text availability, case reports, and studies that did not report a shoulder-specific clinical outcome were excluded. Given the heterogeneity of study designs, outcome measures, and sleep-assessment modalities across the included literature, no formal quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis was performed; findings are presented thematically.

Epidemiology and prevalence of sleep disturbance

Sleep disturbance in patients with rotator cuff tears

In the pre-operative period prior to arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR), Kunze et al41 reported the prevalence of sleep disturbance to be as high as 89%, with 59% of patients reporting sleep disturbance on a nightly basis.21 A recent study by Feltri et al suggests nocturnal pain appears to be the primary driver of sleep disturbance.21 Though a statistically significant decrease, the rates of pain remained high at 12 months post-operatively at 37.9%, which are associated with poor sleep quality.21 Indeed, although ARCR reduced the prevalence of sleep disturbance to 37.2% at 6 months and 22.0% at 12 months post-operatively, respectively, the proportions of patients still experience poor sleep quality is clinically relevant. Several modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors have been identified as drivers of sleep disturbance in patients with rotator cuff tears. Furthermore, Feltri et al21 found that the prevalence of persistent sleep disturbance at 12 months post-operatively was higher in female patients than male counterparts (27.0% vs. 19.0%). Beyond sex, comorbid anxiety and depression appear to be meaningful demographic factors that contribute to poor sleep quality in this population. Dolan et al15 reported that age may play a role in sleep disturbance after ARCR, reporting older patients (age ≥65 years) are more likely to report resolution of sleep disturbances compared to their younger counterparts. Persistent post-operative sleep disturbance and prolonged narcotic use have emerged as important drivers of long-term sleep quality in this population. Additional evidence from Austin et al3 reinforces the high burden of pre-operative sleep disturbance in this population, finding that 89% of patients with full-thickness RCTs reported poor sleep quality based on PSQI scores, with 38% continuing to experience disturbed sleep at 6 months post-operatively despite improvements in pain and function. Similarly, Horneff et al29 reported that 81% of patients experienced pre-operative sleep disturbance, and nearly 41% continued to exhibit poor sleep quality at 2 years post-operatively, with persistent narcotic use identified as a strong predictor of poor long-term sleep outcomes.

Sleep disturbance in patients with adhesive capsulitis

Mulligan et al62 concluded that sleep disturbance may be worse in adhesive capsulitis (AC), as compared to other pathologies like RCTs and subacromial impingement syndrome. Indeed, 86% of patients with AC met the criteria for poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥5), with significantly worse PSQI scores compared to rotator cuff tears and subacromial impingement syndrome patients. When stratified by PSQI domains, AC patients continued to exhibit significantly worse scores for sleep quality, sleep duration, and habitual sleep efficiency compared to other shoulder pathologies.62 Compared to healthy controls, AC patients exhibit significantly higher PSQI and visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores.9,76 Furthermore, AC patients exhibit worse anxiety and quality of life (QoL) compared to controls, displayed through significant differences in Beck Anxiety Inventory and World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale short form subscores for physical, psychological, and environmental QoL.76 Unlike that of RCTs, there is a paucity of longitudinal data in exclusively AC patient cohorts over multiple time points with or without intervention to demonstrate how patterns in sleep quality change. AC patients exhibit significant sleep disturbance, which may be associated with comorbid anxiety and worse QoL.

Sleep disturbance in patients with glenohumeral joint osteoarthritis

The pre-operative prevalence of sleep disturbance in patients with glenohumeral joint osteoarthritis (GHOA) has been reported to be 91%.61 In a cohort of patients with GHOA scheduled for anatomic or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA), Weinberg et al80 reported a significant improvement in PSQI at 6 weeks after TSA (ΔPSQI = −2.4), with further but nonsignificant improvement by 3 months post-operatively. In the same study, 41% of patients reported normal PSQI scores at 6 weeks post-operatively, improving to 73.9% of patients at 1 year post-operatively.80 To contextualize the clinical relevance of reported changes in sleep and shoulder-specific outcomes, the Minimal Clinically Important Difference for the PSQI has been estimated at approximately 4.4 points in musculoskeletal populations at 6-month follow-up,50 while reported Minimal Clinically Important Difference values for the ASES score in rotator cuff and shoulder arthroplasty cohorts generally fall in the range of 13.6–16.1 points.36 These thresholds should be kept in mind when interpreting the magnitude of perioperative improvements.

Improvements in Sleep Satisfaction Tool (SST) sleep scores conferred a greater ability to sleep comfortably, while improvements in ASES sleep scores specifically indicated an improved ability to sleep on the affected/diseased side.79 However, sleep disturbance persisted in a select patient cohort, as 13.2% and 14.8% of patients reported being unable to sleep comfortably (SST sleep) after anatomic and reverse TSA, respectively, at 12 months post-operatively. Furthermore, 34.2% and 37.1% of patients reported being unable to sleep on the affected side (ASES sleep) after anatomic and reverse TSA, respectively, at the same time point.

An investigation by Morris et al61 revealed higher pre-operative sleep disturbance was associated with significantly worse pain and functional limitations as measured through the Constant pain, Constant activity, and Western Ontario Osteoarthritis Shoulder indices. A total of 80% of this patient cohort reported no sleep disturbance at a mean 3.5-year follow-up after anatomic TSA, with patients reporting no sleep disturbance exhibiting significant improvements in the investigated outcome measures.61 Together, this literature suggests that sleep disturbance affects the vast majority of patients with shoulder pathologies including, RCTs, AC, and GHOA. Although there are reported improvements in sleep quality after surgical intervention, a clinically relevant proportion of patients continue to experience poor sleep quality post-operatively, which may negatively influence patient experience and recovery. Mechanisms of sleep disturbance in shoulder surgery patients

The mechanisms and pathophysiologic changes underlying sleep disturbance in patients with shoulder pathology are multifactorial. Indeed, etiologies of sleep disturbance include those related to pain physiology and an inflammatory state, central sleep architecture changes, psychosocial and neurologic abnormalities, and mechanical contributors such as sleep positioning and extremity immobilization. Pain physiology and inflammation

A heightened state of both local and systemic inflammation in combination with pain related to mechanical damage of anatomic structures around the shoulder are major drivers of pain and sleep disturbance.71 In fact, pain is the most common reported driver of sleep disturbance given that it can increase awakening frequency, cause discomfort, and induce anxiety.66 Likewise, this inflammatory milieu is present in the postsurgical state, where soft tissue damage due to surgical intervention causes an increase in local inflammatory cytokines. Few studies have investigated cytokine changes at the biomolecular level in this patient population.

With respect to sleep disturbance, a nocturnal surge in inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) has also been reported. Cho et al10 collected peripheral blood samples from 63 participants, in which 21 had a rotator cuff tear and sleep disturbance based on the PSQI, 21 had a rotator cuff tear and normal sleep, and 21 represented a control group. The authors reported that serum concentrations of TNF-α were significantly greater in the group with sleep disturbance compared with the control and non-sleep disturbance groups. Furthermore, a prior review of various biomarkers present in rotator cuff tendinopathy or tears suggests that samples of subacromial bursa, supraspinatus tendon, glenohumeral joint fluid, and synovium frequently possess high levels of IL-1β, IL-1ra, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and TNF-α.71 Furthermore, the authors suggest that IL-1β is positively correlated with pain in rotator cuff tendinopathy, while VEGF and TNF-α are associated with shoulder pain pre-operatively and TNF-α specifically with sleep disturbance.71

In a previous study by Terabayashi et al,75 blood flow and hemodynamics in 47 patients with RCTs were compared to healthy controls with pulse Doppler ultrasonography. Asano et al2 reported that the peak systolic velocity in the anterior humeral circumflex artery was positively correlated with the messenger RNA expression of IL-1β, IL-8, and MMP-3 genes.

Mechanistically, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β may modulate sleep-wake regulation through hypothalamic and brainstem signaling, promoting fragmented sleep, reduced slow-wave sleep (SWS), and nocturnal arousal, while VEGF- and MMP-mediated neovascularization and synovitis within the shoulder may amplify peripheral nociceptive input at night and contribute to awakening.10,31,63,86 Importantly, most of these biomarker data are drawn from cross-sectional or case-control studies, and a causal relationship between specific cytokines and sleep disturbance in patients with shoulder pathology has not been established.

Pain physiology is also closely related to inflammation and injury, with emerging evidence suggesting that central sensitization may be an important contributor to shoulder pain. Central sensitization is defined as nervous system hyperexcitability and pain signal amplification, which leads to hyperalgesia and allodynia.33 Iio et al30 performed a cross-sectional study of 167 patients (131 with rotator cuff tear but no GHOA, 23 with GHOA and rotator cuff tears, and 13 with GHOA but no rotator cuff tear) revealing that patients with GHOA had approximately 3 times the rate (27.8% vs. 8.4%) of central sensitization than patients with rotator cuff tears alone and that independent predictors of this included female sex, severe pain scores, and prolonged pain duration. Several other studies have corroborated the emerging association between central sensitization and shoulder pain.6,13,70 Together, these studies confirm that central sensitization is present among various subsets of patients with shoulder pathology and may be an important contributor to pain.

Sleep architecture changes

Commonly used anesthetic agents during shoulder surgery, such as sevoflurane and propofol, induce alterations to the circadian rhythm and sleep cycles intraoperatively, which may have implications post-operatively in that patients will need time to restore these changes.54 Anesthesia deactivates the thalamus, leading to cortical inhibition and inhibited rapid eye movement (REM) sleep.78 Likewise, there is a decreased slow-wave activity rebound following sleep deprivation, suggesting that anesthesia induces brain function similar to non-REM sleep.37 Given these deleterious effects of sleep architecture, regional anesthesia with peripheral nerve blocks and light sedation may be preferable to general anesthesia when feasible.

The use of opioid medications has been associated with disrupting circadian rhythm and sleep architectures by reducing the overall quality and length of REM and SWS cycles.16 This disruption has been posited to occur through signaling at the kappa opioid receptor in the anterior cingulate cortex, a brain region associated with emotions.46 This effect may be enhanced in the patient with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), where a component of respiratory depression is involved.66 In large-scale studies, mild to severe OSA has been associated with poorer global cognition indices, which is linked to metrics involved in sleep architecture including sleep maintenance efficiency and wakes after sleep onset.64 Furthermore, both intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation, which are hallmarks of OSA, enhance pain and potentiate opioid analgesic effects.42 Considering the aforementioned relationship between systemic inflammation and sleep disruption, major inflammatory pathways are also upregulated in the setting of OSA independent of injury state and therefore may also contribute to sleep disruption.42

It is worth noting that the effect of opioids on sleep is not uniform. Acutely, opioids may shorten sleep latency by virtue of their sedative properties and can subjectively be perceived by patients as improving sleep20; however, the net effect on sleep architecture and sleep quality is consistently detrimental, with higher arousal frequency, reduced sleep efficiency, and suppression of both REM and SWS.7,20 This distinction is clinically important because perceived short-term benefit may drive continued post-operative opioid use despite worsening objective sleep quality, reinforcing the rationale for opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia in this population.

Psychosocial contributors

The presence of psychologic disorders such as anxiety, depression, and pain catastrophizing has been posited to influence sleep quality. Longo et al53 studied 47 patients undergoing ARCR, revealing that pre-operative anxiety was significantly correlated with both poor sleep quality and pain; further, anxious patients had significantly worse sleep quality and Short Form Health Survey Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores. This relationship was also observed for patients with depression.53

Toprak and Erden76 reported that patients with AC demonstrated significantly higher VAS pain and Beck Anxiety Inventory scores compared with controls. These relationships have been corroborated among numerous other studies.8,56,85

Sleeping position

In a study by Werner et al,81 the authors evaluated a cohort of 20 healthy volunteers, revealing subacromial pressure was lower in the supine position compared with decubitus or prone positions. These findings should be interpreted with caution given that they were derived from asymptomatic healthy volunteers rather than patients with rotator cuff pathology or those recovering from shoulder surgery, which may limit their direct applicability to the perioperative population. Jacobson et al32 examined whether an experimental bedding design with firmness predetermined based on a patient's height and weight was associated with changes in shoulder and back pain among 22 subjects with documented sleep disturbance, back pain, and shoulder pain. The authors reported that the bedding system significantly reduced shoulder pain by 61% compared with personal bedding used by the subjections.

Sling immobilization

Sling immobilization after shoulder surgery is often recommended, with constructs ranging from simple shoulder slings to adjustable abduction orthoses. Galetti et al24 performed an epidemiological study where 300 patients revealed sling immobilization was a major contributor to difficulty with sleep. Gao et al25 performed a systematic review of 4 randomized trials comparing the use of a simple sling and abduction bracer after ARCR involving a total of 224 patients. The authors found no significant differences in pain at 1, 3, or 12 weeks between cohorts. However, a recent randomized trial by Li et al45 demonstrated that sleep quality and pain were significantly improved after ARCR in patients using a simple sling compared with those using an abduction brace, suggesting a potential association between sling use, pain, and sleep quality. Strategies for management of sleep disturbance in shoulder surgery patients

Patients can consider modifying their sleeping position (ie, semi-reclined position, sleeping on the uninjured side, sling, using pillows and positional supports), investing in wearable technology and objective monitoring, and utilizing both pharmacological (ie, melatonin, gabapentinoids, and zolpidem) and non-pharmacological therapies (cognitive behavioral therapy, music therapy, mindfulness, progressive muscle relaxation, blue light–blocking interventions, and sleep hygiene education) (Table III).

Positional strategies and recovery aids

Sleeping in a semi-reclined position may help to reduce stress on the shoulder, limit unnecessary movement, and minimize pain. This position can be achieved using a chair recliner or using positional supports in bed. Furthermore, using a post-operative sling and/or abduction pillow limits movement to the shoulder, which can improve pain management.

In a randomized control trial by Li et al,45 the authors found that the PSQI at 6 weeks postsurgery was higher for the brace group than the sling group, indicating a statistically significant advantage for the sling group. The authors concluded that the sling resulted in better sleep quality, less anxiety, and higher satisfaction compared with an abduction brace in the first 6 weeks postsurgery. However, functional outcomes and repair integrity were similar at one year post-operatively between groups.45

Wearable technology and objective monitoring

In healthy individuals, wearable technology can improve sleep quality and accurately measure sleep and cardiorespiratory variables.5 Such devices allow for monitoring components of sleep (pattern of activity, heart rate and its variability, sleep/wake estimation, skin conductance, temperature, etc.) that may offer insight into potential targets for improving post-operative fatigue, pain, and overall recovery after shoulder surgery.23,84 Furthermore, actigraphy, which is a non-invasive method used to objectively measure sleep patterns and rest-activity cycles, may show clinical utility after shoulder surgery as well.57,60 However, it should be emphasized that evidence supporting consumer wearables and actigraphy specifically in the shoulder-surgery population remains limited, with most supporting data extrapolated from other surgical or general populations; validation studies in patients undergoing rotator cuff repair and shoulder arthroplasty are needed before these technologies can be routinely recommended for clinical sleep monitoring in this setting.

Pharmacologic interventions

Melatonin

The immunomodulatory properties of melatonin are well known, as the medication acts on the immune system by regulating cytokine production of immunocompetent cells.18 Previous literature has shown that melatonin reduces adhesion molecules and pro-inflammatory cytokines and modifies serum inflammatory parameters.48 Perez et al68 have previously reported that melatonin use after ARCR led to improved sleep quality (PSQI) in the early post-operative period as well as improved functional outcomes (ASES and Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation [SANE] scores) and decreased narcotic use in the later post-operative period.

Gabapentinoids

Several studies involving orthopedic surgery, including shoulder surgery, have demonstrated benefits of pre-operative gabapentinoids in terms of pain control and opioid consumption in the immediate 24-hour post-operative period (ie, gabapentin and pregabalin).65 Also, administering gabapentin perioperatively and divided over a minimum duration of 4 days post-operatively may significantly reduce post-operative pain intensity and opioid consumption in rotator cuff repair surgery patients.19 However, gabapentinoids should be used with caution, as a recent study demonstrated that exposure to gabapentin during the perioperative period after TSA is independently associated with a slight increased risk of post-operative pulmonary complications.82

Zolpidem

Zolpidem is a short-acting nonbenzodiazepine hypnotic drug that enhances the activity of the neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid and is a well-documented sleep aid that has been shown to decrease opioid consumption, reduce pain, and increase QoL when used for a short period of time following orthopedic surgery.69 A previous study by Cho et al11 demonstrated that zolpidem for analgesia after ARCR provided a significant reduction in the need for rescue analgesic, without increasing adverse effects. While zolpidem is safe for short-term use in the immediate postsurgery period, long-term use is associated with the potential for developing tolerance and increases the risk of dependence.17

Multimodal pain management

Multimodal analgesia in shoulder surgery is commonly used for pain control and to reduce opioid consumption in the early post-operative period. Overall, patients undergoing TSA have decreased post-operative pain, improved pain control, decreased opioid consumption, and shorter hospital duration when given a multimodal analgesia regimen, with no increase in short-term complications or unplanned patient readmissions.35,58,65

Non-pharmacologic therapies

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia

CBT-I is a short, structured, and evidence-based treatment that focuses on changing thoughts and behaviors that contribute to insomnia. CBT-I has been shown to increase the quality of sleep and sleep time, decrease sleep onset latency, and decrease pain after surgery.68

Music therapy, mindfulness, and progressive muscle relaxation

Music-based intervention has been shown to reduce post-operative opioid requirements, reduce anxiety, reduce depression, and reduce pain catastrophizing after surgery.27 In a recent review, Kootstra et al40 concluded that mindfulness appeared to be positively associated with less pain, increased mood, and better function. Lastly, progressive muscle relaxation is a relaxation technique targeting the symptom of tension associated with anxiety and has shown to improving relaxation states at both the psychological and physiological level.77

Blue light–blocking interventions

Blue light therapy interventions have been shown to have a positive effect on improving subjective sleep quality, reducing the number of nocturnal awakenings and the duration of nocturnal awakenings, and improve daytime function and therefore may be considered after shoulder surgery.44,72,73

Sleep hygiene education

Poorer sleep hygiene is associated with both increased pain interference and poorer sleep quality among patients about to undergo orthopedic surgery on their upper extremity.26 Therefore, it is imperative to assess and educate patients on proper sleep hygiene prior to surgery to help foster high-quality restorative sleep that promotes post-operative recovery.26

Future directions

Addressing residual post-operative sleep disturbance

Despite meaningful improvements in pain, function, and sleep after rotator cuff repair and shoulder arthroplasty, a clinically important minority of patients continue to report sleep disturbance at intermediate- and long-term follow-up, with reported residual PSQI-defined disturbance ranging from approximately 14% to 41% depending on procedure, time point, and cohort.4,29 This persistent burden represents an important unmet need in the sleep-and-shoulder-surgery literature and warrants focused future investigation. Priority research directions include (1) prospective identification of patient-level predictors of persistent post-operative sleep disturbance (including pre-operative PSQI severity, psychosocial factors, opioid use, and comorbid sleep-disordered breathing); (2) evaluation of targeted interventions for this subgroup, such as CBT-I, structured opioid-sparing multimodal analgesic pathways, and referral to sleep medicine where indicated; (3) development of shoulder-specific sleep outcome instruments that more precisely capture positional and pain-related nocturnal symptoms beyond what the PSQI captures; and (4) incorporation of longitudinal, objective sleep monitoring (eg, validated actigraphy or consumer wearables with established reliability) into clinical-trial endpoints so that the durability of perioperative sleep improvement and the trajectory of residual disturbance can be characterized with greater rigor.

Multimodal perioperative sleep pathways

Enhanced recovery after surgery pathways have demonstrated benefits in other surgical populations, but few, if any, incorporate structured approaches to sleep optimization.38 A practical first step could involve pre-operative screening using validated tools such as the PSQI or actigraphy to identify high-risk patients.

Artificial intelligence and predictive modeling

There exists opportunity for artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning to aid in early detection and prediction of sleep disturbances in post-operative populations. However, there exists a paucity of literature examining the application of AI toward enhanced post-operative care or sleep medicine, necessitating further studies validating AI and machine learning models for sleep tracking and post-operative care optimization.1,39

Prospective trials needed

Despite growing awareness of post-operative sleep disturbance, few high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or longitudinal studies have evaluated interventions specifically in shoulder surgery populations.51,55,59,68 Future research should include RCTs comparing multimodal sleep optimization strategies, such as CBT-I, pharmacologic interventions, positional therapies, and wearable feedback systems. Outcomes should extend beyond sleep quality alone to include comparisons with pain trajectories, functional recovery scores (e.g., ASES, Constant Murley score), opioid consumption, and return-to-activity timelines. Additionally, longer follow-up intervals are needed to determine whether improved sleep translates into sustained gains in pain control, physical function, and QoL after shoulder surgery.

Equity and accessibility

Many sleep optimization strategies, including CBT-I, wearable devices, and sleep diagnostics, are not equally available to all patients due to cost, geographic barriers, or limited insurance coverage.83 Telemedicine platforms may provide scalable solutions, particularly for rural or underserved populations. Moreover, future studies should deliberately include diverse patient populations to broaden applicability.

Conclusion

Impaired sleep quality is highly prevalent among patients with shoulder pathologies presenting for surgical intervention. Many patients experience improvements in sleep quality post-operatively, though for some, residual sleep disturbance persists in the postsurgical period. More evidence is needed to formulate patient-specific strategies using these modalities to improve sleep quality, while future interventions should include integrated assessments of sleep disturbance and personalized monitoring.
