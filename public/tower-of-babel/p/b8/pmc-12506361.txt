Time series and factors associated with prematurity in live births in curitiba, brazil: time series analysis between the years 2000 and 2021
Authors: Marcelle Fernandes Da Silva Frigotto, Isabela Cristina Santos Freire de Paula, Isabelle Cristina Daniel, Arianny de Macedo Brondani, Angela Leite Mendes, Márcia Helena Baldani, Deborah Ribeiro Carvalho, Juliana Schaia Rocha Orsi, Renata Iani Werneck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrematurity stands as the primary cause of mortality among children under the age of five globally, defined as childbirth occurring before the completion of the 37th week of pregnancy. The study aims to examine the historical prevalence and temporal trend of prematurity in Curitiba (PR) from 2000 to 2021, considering factors associated with the mother, child, and pregnancy.MethodsThis quantitative, descriptive, and analytical study employs a time series approach, utilizing data from the “COOSMIC - Curitiba Maternal and Child Health Cohort” study conducted by the Pontifical Catholic University of Paraná in collaboration with the Curitiba Municipal Health Department. Data sourced from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC) focuses on variables including baby’s sex, mother’s age, schooling, and marital status, gestational age at delivery, single or multiple pregnancies, and prenatal care. In the first stage, prematurity serves as the dependent variable, while characteristics associated with the mother, child, and pregnancy are treated as independent variables over time/year using the Prais-Winsten regression model. In the second stage, the Poisson Regression model examines the association between Social Determinants of Health (SDH) and preterm births.ResultsThe analysis reveals an increasing trend in the prevalence of preterm births, with an Annual Significant Rate (ASR) of 2.4% (95%CI = 1.8; 3.0). Rising trends are observed across all stratifications examined, except for women lacking prenatal care visits.ConclusionDespite the initial low historical prevalence, an upward trend in prematurity is observed. Factors such as maternal age, schooling, marital status, gestational history, and prenatal care are identified as associated factors, highlighting the necessity for targeted interventions.Keywords: Time series studies, Premature birth, Prevalence, Social determinants of health

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines preterm birth as any birth that occurs before 37 completed weeks of gestation or less than 259 days from the first day of a woman’s last menstrual period (LMP) [1, 2]. This calculation is based on the reported interval between the first day of the mother’s last menstrual period and the date of birth. The lower the gestational age is, the greater the rates of morbidity, mortality, disability, and the need for intensive neonatal care associated with birth [2–4].

Multiple factors are associated with the occurrence of premature birth, with emphasis on clinical and socioeconomic conditions affecting both mothers and children [1, 2, 4]. Approximately two-thirds of single premature births are spontaneous, often without a known cause, while roughly one-third result from medical intervention (i.e., medical indication) to protect the health of the mother or baby [4]. It is estimated that more than one in ten babies are born prematurely each year, with approximately one million of these children dying due to complications arising from premature birth, making it the most frequent cause of infant mortality worldwide [2].

The global incidence of premature birth ranges from 3.8 to 17.5% of live births, with lower rates in high-income countries than in low- and middle-income countries. Brazil is among the ten countries with the highest recorded numbers of early births [5]. From 2012 to 2019, the proportion of premature births in Brazil, though still high, showed a decreasing trend, varying from 10.87 to 9.95%. However, Brazil continues to have a high proportion of premature births compared to European countries (8.7%), despite this downward trend. In Brazil, 60% of neonatal deaths occur among premature babies with a gestational age of 32 weeks or less [6]. Addressing premature birth is crucial for reducing neonatal and infant mortality and morbidity, particularly in poor and resource-limited settings [7]. Also, general evidence links premature birth to increased adult mortality and morbidity, as well as long-term neurological disabilities, with increased risks of various comorbidities [4, 8–10]. Preventing deaths and complications resulting from premature birth begins with ensuring a healthy pregnancy. High-quality care before and during pregnancy will ensure that women have a positive pregnancy experience [3].

Due to its importance, accurate epidemiological information on birth rates has become essential for monitoring and developing public policies tailored to specific population profiles. To this end, in the early 1990 s, the Department of Information and IT of the Unified Health System (DATASUS) developed the Live Birth Information System (SINASC). This nationwide system, managed by the Municipal and State Health Departments, is fed by information from Live Birth Certificate (LBD). The objective of the SINASC is to gather epidemiological data on reported births, support interventions related to women’s and children’s health, assist in care actions for pregnant women and newborns, and monitor the evolution of historical records. This works as a first steps to allows the identification of intervention priorities and contributes to the effective improvement of health systems across the national territory and at all levels of the health system [6, 7, 11, 12].

Of the 531,604 live births in the city of Curitiba between 2000 and 2021, 41,294 (7.8%) were born prematurely. While Curitiba displayed a percentage similar to that of European countries (8.7%) during the period studied and, in 2021, according to the Brazilian National Health Agency (ANS), exhibited a percentage of premature births well below the national figure (10.2% in Curitiba compared to 12.4% in Brazil), the city has demonstrated increasing prematurity rates over these 22 years, aligning with the global trend [5, 13, 14].

This study aimed to estimate and analyze the historical prevalence and temporal trend of prematurity in Curitiba, PR, from 2000 to 2021, considering factors associated with the mother, child, and pregnancy.

Methods

This is a quantitative, descriptive, and analytical research study, represented by a time series analysis using data from the study “COOSMIC – Maternal and Child Health Cohort of Curitiba,”. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of Pontifical Catholic University of Paraná (PUC-PR) (CAAE: 90052118.9.0000.0020) in partnership with the Municipal Health Department of Curitiba (CAAE: 90052118.9.3001.0101); project title: “COOSMIC – Maternal and Child Health Cohort of Curitiba,”. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the same committee, given the noninterventional design of the study and the fact that the data were collected from clinical records and without contact with the participants and the procedures performed in this study were part of routine care. All research procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional committee on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 revised in 2013.

The study utilized valid records from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC) in the city of Curitiba, Brazil, covering the period from 2000 to 2021. All births that occurred at a gestational age of less than 37 weeks were considered premature for the analysis of the longitudinal component of the time series.

The methodology applied was divided into two stages, the first stage was based on the following assumptions: characterizing the sample by calculating the prevalence of the variables through absolute (n) and relative (%) frequency distribution measures; calculating total prematurity rates, prematurity rates related to associated factors, year by year; and calculating annual growth rates (AGR) for total prematurity and prematurity related to associated factors. At this stage, prematurity and the characteristics associated with the mother, child, and pregnancy (such as the sex of the baby, maternal age and education level, marital status, number of living children, number of fetuses, and number of prenatal consultations) were considered dependent variables, while time (year corresponding to the records of the collected information) was considered the independent variable. The 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated for all estimates.

At this step, the statistical analysis model based on Prais-Winsten generalized linear regression was used because it is suitable for correcting the parameter estimates of a regression model in the presence of autocorrelation between successive observations in a time series. For each model, the indicator of interest was taken as the dependent variable, and time was taken as the independent variable. The Durbin–Watson hypothesis test was employed to assess the presence of autocorrelation in the errors of adjacent observations. Once autocorrelation was confirmed, the Prais-Winsten regression model was applied, following the steps described by Antunes and Cardoso in 2015 [15]. To facilitate this, the values for the dependent variables were logarithmically transformed, allowing the chosen statistical method to be applied effectively [15].

By analyzing the annual rate of change (AVR) or annual growth rate (AGR), the trend can be categorized as increasing (when the rate is positive), decreasing (when it is negative), or stationary (when there is no significant difference between its value and zero). The AVR or AGR indicates the percentage increase or decrease per year in the prematurity rate. For the tests applied, a 95% confidence interval (CI), a p value < 0.05, and a significance level of 5% were considered.

In the second stage, Poisson regression with crude and adjusted robust variance adjustment was employed to examine the relationships between associated factors and the prevalence of prematurity. This analysis also calculated the prevalence ratios (PRs) for the internal subdivisions of the sample, considering each associated factor in relation to the total sample. Demographic variables (baby’s sex, maternal age and education, marital status, and number of living children) and pregnancy-related variables (number of fetuses and number of prenatal consultations) were considered potential factors associated with prematurity. The backward hierarchical variable entry method was utilized to retain only variables with p < 0.20 in the model. Two hierarchical levels were considered: the first comprising demographic variables and the second comprising pregnancy variables. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for two-tailed analyses.

All analyses were conducted using STATA MP 14.1 software.

Discussion

This study revealed an increasing trend in the prevalence of prematurity between 2000 and 2021, with an annual rate of change of 2.4%, consistent with global statistics [1]. Additionally, the biological and social determinants of health examined remained associated with prematurity in both the crude and adjusted analyses.

General evidence links premature birth to increased mortality and morbidity. Although most individuals born prematurely survive into adulthood without significant comorbidities and report a high level of function and quality of life, a notable minority face an elevated risk of chronic diseases and premature mortality in early adulthood [9, 16].

All the graphs exhibit a notable surge in registrations in 2012. This phenomenon can be attributed to DATASUS implementing a new form in 2011 aimed at enhancing the information in SINASC. The year 2012 represented the transition phase between the two forms, during which both the old and new forms were filled out. In 2013, the number of registrations was normalized.

This study revealed a strong association between male sex and premature birth. Research conducted by the University of Toronto suggested that fetal genotype might influence placental enzyme activities and fetal pituitary adrenal function, thereby affecting the mechanisms that lead to the initiation of preterm labor. This could explain the higher prevalence of preterm births in male fetuses, reaffirming the findings of this study (51.7% of premature births in male fetuses) [17].

Primiparity was associated with a higher percentage of prematurity (52.7%). Although the biological mechanism responsible for this influence is not well defined, studies have shown a significant association between these factors. First children and those whose mothers had a previous premature child had a greater chance of prematurity. On the other hand, contemporary gestational characteristics, such as advanced or early pregnancy and a high proportion of unnecessary cesarean sections, have contributed to the frequent occurrence of prematurity in primiparous pregnancies, as well as previous prenatal results [18, 19].

Prematurity occurs much more frequently when more than one fetus is gestated simultaneously, primarily due to maternal physiological and anatomical issues related to fetal growth [20]. In 2021, according to this study, Curitiba exhibited a prevalence of prematurity of 63.7% in twin pregnancies compared to 8.7% in singleton pregnancies, confirming a twofold greater prevalence of prematurity in multiple pregnancies. These findings align with national and international studies on the impact of this variable on pregnancy outcomes [21, 22].

Prenatal care is widely regarded as one of the primary indicators for assessing quality in primary care, and its importance in influencing pregnancy outcomes is undeniable [23, 24]. In this study’s city, Curitiba, prenatal and postpartum care are offered by the Unified Health System (SUS) to pregnant women and babies, conducted by multidisciplinary health teams, including continued care actions for the pregnant woman/baby binomial [25]. These health support actions are displayed on the attention network called “Rede Mãe Curitibana Vale a Vida” and integrate the reception, attachment, and attendance of pregnant women for assistance.

One of the key criteria for evaluating prenatal care quality is the number of appointments (medical and/or nursing) scheduled during the pregnancy period. The Brazilian Ministry of Health (MoH) recommends a minimum of six prenatal consultations, ideally starting by the 12th week of pregnancy, with regular and continuous monitoring at predefined intervals (monthly until the 28th week, fortnightly from the 28th to the 36th week, and weekly thereafter) [24]. In Brazil, the “gold standard” for prenatal care typically comprises a total of 11 to 12 consultations, with this number varying based on the presence of risk factors or complications during pregnancy. The literature presents various standards regarding the number of visits to characterize the sample. The findings of this study revealed a linear trend in the associations between the number of prenatal consultations and prematurity (p < 0.01), with the number of consultations demonstrating an inverse relationship with the prevalence of prematurity.

There was a notable percentage variation in the prematurity rate over the study period (from 8.5 to 20.8%) among pregnant women who had 4 to 6 consultations. This group also exhibited a higher annual growth rate (4.2%). These figures suggest two potential factors to be considered: delayed initiation of prenatal care and/or challenges accessing health services [26]. In addition to the quantity of consultations, the impact of the quality of prenatal care on pregnancy outcomes should be emphasized [27].

The distinction between spontaneous and iatrogenic prematurity is relevant in studies focused on medical interventions. According to data from the survey “Born in Brazil: national survey on labor and birth” (largest population survey of pregnant women carried out in the country) released in 2016, the Brazilian prematurity rate is almost twice as high as that observed in many European countries, at 74% of these late premature babies (34 to 36 gestational weeks). In addition, this survey reveals that surgical delivery was performed in 52% of births, whereas the WHO recommendation is only 15% [18]. The variable indicating whether a cesarean section occurred before the onset of labor was only incorporated into the platform analyzed in this study in 2010, which made impossible the variable to be included in the analysis. Future research in Brazil must include this variable in the investigation.

The higher adjusted prevalence ratio found in women over the age of 35 can be justified by several factors. Older pregnant women may have a greater frequency of chronic medical conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, which are known risk factors for preterm birth. Additionally, these patients may be more prone to conditions such as preeclampsia and premature rupture of membranes, as well as a higher frequency of operative cesarean deliveries, all of which can increase the risk of preterm birth [28, 29]. Another important consideration is the increase in the number of assisted reproduction procedures in Brazil, which in turn increases the likelihood of multiple pregnancies, a known risk factor for preterm birth. Furthermore, older pregnant women, especially those who are nulliparous, and their obstetricians may have a lower risk perception threshold, leading to iatrogenic interventions that can further contribute to the risk of preterm birth [23].

In contrast, the study revealed that in Curitiba, the highest annual growth rate of preterm births occurred in pregnant women up to 19 years old, as well as in women with “up to 3 years of education” (4.3%) and “4 to 7 years” (3.6%) (Table 2). Education level and the occurrence of teenage pregnancy are considered significant social determinants of health due to their potential to influence preterm birth rates and outcomes [28]. Adverse social conditions, including limited educational opportunities, can impact the health of pregnant women and contribute to poor pregnancy outcomes [30]. A systematic literature review conducted in Brazil based on articles published between 2000 and 2011 concluded that teenage pregnancy is the leading cause of prematurity, followed by smoking and hypertension [31].

International studies have also established a correlation between prematurity and teenage pregnancy. In England, a study conducted between 2004 and 2006 revealed that pregnant teenage women face a greater risk of premature birth than adult women, and this risk increases further in the event of a second pregnancy [27]. In the United States, nearly three-quarters of teenage births are unintended, and according to research, unintended pregnancies are linked to an increase in premature births. Teenaged pregnant women are at a 17% greater risk of preterm birth, and they are also more likely to have a short interval between pregnancies [28]. The findings of the Birth in Brazil survey (2012) corroborate the results of previous studies indicating that a greater percentage of preterm births are associated with markers of social vulnerability, such as teenage pregnancy, low levels of education, unplanned and unwanted pregnancies, inadequate prenatal care, and delayed initiation of prenatal care [18, 19].

Although there is no specific research on the subject, maternal marital status has been shown to be associated with preterm birth, consistent with a similar association found in a study conducted in Pelotas (RS-BR) and published in 2010 [32]. While small, there was a notable difference in the prevalence of preterm births among pregnant women with a steady partner compared to mothers who reported not having a partner during pregnancy. Pregnancy itself, along with its circumstances and progression, can impose significant physical and psychological stress on women. The presence of a partner during this period can serve as an additional stress factor, particularly considering the potential presence of relationship violence [33–36].

The significance of the costs associated with premature infants for the health system deserves emphasis. According to a study conducted by the Paulista Center for Health Economics at the Federal University of São Paulo (UNIFESP) between 2009 and 2011 in a public hospital in the city of São Paulo (SP), the average cost of caring for a premature newborn was estimated at US$2,328.00. This is notably higher than the previously underestimated figure of US$979.00 calculated by the Ministry of Health in 2006 [37]. These findings underscore the importance of researching the subject and the necessity of developing and monitoring public policies related to this issue.

Although SINASC records have shown qualitative and quantitative improvement, the use of a secondary database presents limitations for this study. These limitations include concerns regarding the reliability of recording by healthcare professionals and the reliance on pregnant women’s memory to accurately report the date of their last menstrual period (LMP), which is used to estimate gestational age (GA). This reliance on LMP could lead to inaccurate estimations of late premature births, resulting in unreliable statistics. Additionally, the database used does not permit direct evaluation of the current effectiveness of the premises outlined by the municipal program for monitoring pregnant women and infants, which includes monitoring pregnant women affiliated with SUS health facilities. Therefore, we suggest conducting a new investigation using data exclusively from this public healthcare system to address these limitations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while the prevalence rate of prematurity may seem relatively low in terms of percentages (7.8%), it demonstrates an annual upward trend (ASR = 2.4%). Additionally, by examining the official records of live births in Curitiba over a span of 22 years, the study established a direct association between the listed factors and the occurrence of premature births. These factors include age, level of education, maternal marital status, number of previous pregnancies, number of fetuses born simultaneously, and quality of prenatal care.
