Prevalence, risk factors, and metabolic implications of alcohol use disorders among male workers in Hebei Province, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zixuan Zhou, Lan Wang, Wenting Lu, Ling Sun, Fengya Zhen, Shuo Wang, Ran Wang, Mei Song, Yuanyuan Gao, Xueyi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol is a psychoactive substance that can lead to alcohol use disorders (AUDs), which encompass a spectrum of conditions including alcohol dependence and abuse, causing significant mental and physical health issues. Excessive drinking is linked to various diseases, such as liver damage, metabolic disorders, and mental health conditions, with notable gender differences in drinking patterns, particularly among workers. This study investigates the prevalence, risk factors, and metabolic implications associated with AUDs among male workers in China.MethodUtilizing the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV-TR Disorders (SCID), we diagnosed alcohol dependence and abuse in a sample of 665 male workers, categorizing them into a non-problem drinking group (n = 339) and a problem drinking group (n = 326). We compared depressive and anxiety symptoms, as well as sleep quality between the two groups. Additionally, we assessed metabolic parameters including liver and kidney function, blood lipid profiles, blood pressure, and heart rate.ResultOur findings indicate that the problem drinking group exhibited significantly higher rates of alcohol consumption, including years of drinking, frequency, and quantity of alcohol intake, as well as a higher smoking prevalence (P < 0.05). Notably, sleep disturbances were more prevalent in the problem drinking group compared to the non-problem drinking group (P < 0.05). Metabolic assessments revealed that levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), uric acid, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein, fasting blood glucose, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and body mass index (BMI) were significantly elevated in the problem drinking group (P < 0.05).ConclusionMale workers with alcohol use disorders exhibit a significantly worse metabolic profile, placing them at increased risk for alcohol-related liver disease, cardiovascular events like hypertension and arrhythmias, and metabolic syndrome. Our findings also suggest that smoking history is a critical correlate of problem drinking. These results underscore the need for targeted screening and intervention programs in occupational settings.Keywords: Alcohol use disorders, Sleep disturbance, Smoking, Metabolic syndrome

Introduction

Alcohol is a widely consumed psychoactive substance. Excessive and prolonged drinking can lead to psychological and physical dependence, culminating in alcohol use disorders (AUDs). Alcohol use disorders include alcohol dependence and alcohol abuse, which are serious mental health conditions that seriously affect human health [1]. The global burden of disease attributable to alcohol consumption is substantial, contributing to millions of deaths and a significant loss of disability-adjusted life-years annually [2]. Alcohol use disorder has become a public health problem seriously affecting physical and mental health. It can co-occur with a variety of physical diseases (alcoholic liver disease, cardiovascular events, metabolic diseases) and mental health disorders (anxiety, depression), causing a serious burden to individuals and society [3, 4].

Similarly, excessive drinking can also lead to diseases related to metabolic dysregulation. For example, patients with alcoholic liver disease usually show alcoholic fatty liver in the initial stage, and may develop alcoholic hepatitis and cirrhosis in the later stage [5]. In cases of severe alcoholism, extensive hepatocyte necrosis may be caused, leading to liver failure. In addition, long-term high dose drinking will also affect glucose metabolism and lipid metabolism. Ethanol produces acetaldehyde in the liver through the catalysis of dehydrogenase, and produces reactive oxygen species, which leads to mitochondrial dysfunction of liver cells and damage of fatty acid oxidation, and further affects glucose metabolism [6, 7]. Therefore, it is necessary to thoroughly investigate the patterns of alcohol use in high-risk populations and carry out early intervention for individuals with AUDs.

Gender difference in drinking is a common social behavior, with studies consistently showing that males in China have a significantly higher prevalence of alcohol consumption and related problems than females [8, 9]. Male workers, in particular, may face unique occupational stressors and social environments that elevate their risk for hazardous drinking. However, there is a paucity of research that specifically investigates the prevalence of clinically diagnosed AUDs and their comprehensive metabolic and sleep-related consequences in this demographic. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence of AUDs among male workers in Hebei, China, identify the associated sociodemographic and behavioral risk factors, and comprehensively evaluate the impact of problem drinking on metabolic profiles, sleep quality, and depressive and anxiety symptoms.

Method

Subjects

A cross-sectional study was conducted to investigate the drinking status of male workers in Hebei Province, China, from January to December 2016. A multi-stage convenience sampling method was employed. First, several large-scale industrial enterprises across different cities in Hebei Province were contacted and agreed to participate. Second, male workers from these enterprises were invited to a health screening where the study was introduced. Those who expressed interest and met the eligibility criteria were enrolled after providing written informed consent. A total of 1058 cases were initially screened. Exclusion criteria were: (1) individuals who consumed alcohol less than once a year (non-drinkers); (2) female workers, as the study focused on males; (3) individuals with a history of severe mental illness (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder) that could confound the diagnosis of AUDs; (4) current or past diagnosis of other substance use disorders (excluding nicotine); (5) inability to provide informed consent or complete the questionnaires due to cognitive impairment or other reasons. After applying these criteria, 665 eligible participants were included in the final analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University. Written informed consent of all subjects was obtained in this investigation. The study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (No. ChiCTR-ICC-15007244, Date of Registration: 2015/10/20).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the formula for estimating a population proportion: n = Z² * P * (1-P) / d², where Z is the statistic corresponding to the level of confidence, P is the expected prevalence, and d is the precision or margin of error. Based on previous literature suggesting a prevalence of alcohol-related problems in Chinese males of approximately 15%, we set the expected prevalence (P) at 0.15. For a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a desired precision (d) of 4% (0.04), the required sample size was calculated to be 307. To account for potential incomplete data and to increase statistical power for subgroup analyses, we aimed to recruit a larger sample. Our final sample of 665 participants was therefore considered sufficient for the study’s objectives.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes of the study were the prevalence of alcohol use disorders (AUDs), defined as alcohol abuse or dependence according to DSM-IV-TR criteria, and the identification of associated risk factors through multivariate analysis. Secondary outcomes included the comparison of metabolic parameters (liver and kidney function, lipid profiles, glucose, blood pressure), sleep quality, as well as depressive and anxiety symptoms between the problem drinking group and the non-problem drinking group.

Diagnosis of alcohol use disorders

Subjects were diagnosed with an alcohol use disorder in the context of mental disorders using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th Edition, Text Revision (DSM-IV-TR) Axis I Disorders, Clinical Interview (Patient Version) (SCID-I/P). The assessment was carried out by 9 psychiatrists at an intermediate level or above, who had received professional training in SCID from Beijing Huilongguan Hospital. The training of each evaluator was tested for consistency, Kappa = 0.87.

Diagnostic criteria for alcohol abuse

(1) Symptom Criterion: Refers to a type of use in which repeated and excessive use of alcohol causes harm to physical or/and mental health and can lead to a variety of adverse social consequences, such as inability to perform important work or school work, inability to perform family functions, drunk driving or driving, crime, or marital crisis, etc. (2) According to the SCID, the diagnosis of alcohol abuse (lifelong) was made if one or more of the above-mentioned consequences occurred repeatedly over a 12-month period. (3) A diagnosis of alcohol dependence was excluded.

Diagnostic criteria for alcohol dependence

A maladaptive pattern of alcohol use, leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, as manifested by three (or more) of the following, occurring at any time in the same 12-month period: (1) Strong desire or impulse to drink; (2) Difficulties in controlling the beginning and end of drinking and the amount of drinking; (3) Physiological withdrawal syndrome occurs when alcohol is stopped or reduced; (4) Tolerance, as defined by a need for markedly increased amounts of alcohol to achieve intoxication or desired effect, or a markedly diminished effect with continued use of the same amount of alcohol; (5) As a result of drinking, they gradually lose other pleasures or interests, and spend more time on alcohol acquisition, drinking or recovery; (6) Compulsive drinking, regardless of the obvious harmful consequences, such as liver damage, depression or related cognitive impairment caused by drinking.

A total sample size of 665 subjects was collected in this study. SCID was used to diagnose alcohol dependence and alcohol abuse, and the subjects were divided into non-problem drinking group (n = 339, drinking more than once in the past one year, and not meeting criteria for alcohol abuse or alcohol dependence) and the problem drinking group (n = 326, meeting criteria for alcohol abuse or alcohol dependence).

General demographic characteristics

The self-made questionnaire survey included gender, age, education level, marital status, place of residence, and smoking history(smoking: smoking ≥ 6 months or accumulated smoking ≥ 100 cigarettes in one’s lifetime, and still smoking within the past 1 month; quit smoking: had smoked previously but had not smoked in the past month; no smoking: had never smoked or smoked less than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime).

Alcohol use detection

The self-report method was used to investigate the alcohol use in the past one year, including drinking frequency, drinking volume, drinking time (year), and drinking type. The alcohol consumption is calculated by the equation: pure alcohol (g) = alcohol volume (ml) * alcohol concentration (%) * 0.8 (g/ml).

Behavioral and lifestyle assessments

Smoking history was assessed via a self-report questionnaire (smoking: smoking ≥ 6 months or accumulated smoking ≥ 100 cigarettes in one’s lifetime, and still smoking within the past 1 month; quit smoking: had smoked previously but had not smoked in the past month; no smoking: had never smoked or smoked less than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime). Physical activity was assessed using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), which evaluates activity levels over the last 7 days. Total physical activity was calculated in metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes per week.

Assessment of depression and anxiety

The Center for Epidemiologic Study Depression Scale (CESD) is used to assess depressive symptoms, which consists of 20 questions. Subjects are asked about their actual situation or feelings in the past 1 week. Each question provides four choices: none or almost none (< 1 day), rarely (1–2 days), often (3–4 days), and almost always (5–7 days). Questions 4, 8, 12 and 16 are scored in reverse. The four choices are scored as 3, 2, 1 and 0 respectively. The other questions are scored as 0, 1, 2 and 3 respectively. The higher the score, the more depressed it is. The demarcation is divided into 16 points.

Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) is used to assess anxiety, with 20 questions in total, asking subjects about their actual situation or feelings in the past week. Each question offers four choices: no or little time, a little time, a fair amount of time, most or all of the time. Questions 5, 9, 13 and 19 are scored in reverse, while other questions are scored in forward. The higher the score, the more anxiety. The demarcation is divided into 50 points.

Assessment of sleep quality

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to evaluate the Sleep Quality of the subjects in the last 1 month. It can be divided into 7 factors, which are sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep disorders, hypnotic drugs and daytime function. Each factor score is calculated by 0–3 points, and the total score is the sum of all factors. The total score ranges from 0 to 21 points, and the higher the score is, the worse the sleep quality is. Demarcation is divided into 7 points, with a score > 7 indicating poor sleep quality. The use of hypnotic medication was also recorded as a component of this scale.

Metabolic and physical assessment

Physical activity was assessed using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), which evaluates activity levels over the last 7 days. Total physical activity was calculated in metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes per week. Following an overnight fast of at least 8 h, venous blood samples were collected. Laboratory tests included liver function (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], glutamyl transpeptidase [GGT]), kidney function (urea nitrogen, creatinine), uric acid, lipid profile (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], high-density lipoprotein [HDL], low-density lipoprotein [LDL]), and fasting blood glucose (FBG). Blood pressure and heart rate were measured in a seated position after 5 min of rest. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared.

Statistical analysis

All the data collected in this study were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed measurement data were expressed as median (interquartil range), and the comparisons were examined by Student-t test and Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric distribution), respectively. The categorical data were expressed as n(%), and the differences between the two groups were examined by chi-square analysis or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors for problem drinking. The dependent variable was the binary classification of ‘problem drinking group’. Independent variables entered into the model were those found to be significant (P < 0.05) in the univariate analyses, including age, smoking history, physical activity, drinking frequency, alcohol consumption, type of alcohol, GGT, and diastolic blood pressure. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), now integrates alcohol abuse and alcohol dependence into a single spectrum disorder known as Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) [10]. Although our diagnoses were made using the DSM-IV-TR criteria, our ‘problem drinking group,’ which combines participants with either alcohol abuse or dependence, aligns conceptually with the modern definition of AUD. This approach allows our findings to be interpreted in the context of current diagnostic frameworks. Our study confirms that AUD represents a significant public health challenge, characterized by a loss of control over alcohol consumption despite awareness of its detrimental effects on physical and mental health [11, 12]. Long-term heavy drinking has serious adverse effects on both physical and mental health.

Alcohol-related liver disease is the most common complication of chronic heavy drinking. The pathogenesis includes the following aspects: (1) Toxicity of ethanol and its metabolites: ethanol generates acetaldehyde after biotransformation in the liver, while the production of reactive oxygen species increases, affecting cell respiration and causing hepatocyte necrosis or apoptosis. In addition, ethanol can regulate transcription factors related to fat metabolism, promote fat synthesis and inhibit fat decomposition, leading to steatosis [13, 14]. (2) Chronic alcoholism can induce low-level enterogenous endotoxemia, disrupting the gut barrier integrity and increasing the permeability of the intestine. This allows gram-negative bacterial lipopolysaccharide to enter the liver through the portal vein, activating an inflammatory cascade and exacerbating alcoholic liver damage [15, 16]. (3) Nutrition deficiency: long-term excessive intake of ethanol is often accompanied by multiple micronutrient and vitamin deficiencies, which reduces the content of S-adenosylmethionine in the liver and affects the anabolism of the liver [17].

Glutamyltranspeptidase (GGT) is a sensitive biomarker of alcohol consumption and early liver injury. AST is abundant in many different types of tissues except liver, such as skeletal muscle, heart and smooth muscle, while ALT (alanine aminotransferase) is more concentrated in the liver. Therefore, serum ALT level is considered to be a more specific marker of liver injury than AST [18]. An elevated ALT can indicate acute hepatocellular injury. Consistent with the reported results, we found that the GGT and ALT of subjects in the problem drinking group was significantly higher than that in the non-problem drinking group, which indicated that people with alcohol use disorders were at risk of liver injury.

Chronic heavy alcohol consumption has profound effects on the cardiovascular system [19]. Heart rate is regulated by the autonomic nervous system, and alcohol can disrupt this balance, typically increasing sympathetic activity [20, 21]. The study found that alcohol consumption regulates heart rate in a dose-dependent manner. Occasional low-dose alcohol consumption has no negative impact on HR. Binge drinking may have a negative impact on heart rate [22]. Heavy drinking, even in young people, has a serious impact on heart rate [23, 24]. This suggests that heavy drinking can have a deleterious cardiovascular impact regardless of age [25]. In this study, we found that the heart rate of problem drinkers was significantly higher than that of non-problem drinkers, consistent with increased sympathetic tone. This finding highlights a potential mechanism for the increased risk of arrhythmias seen in individuals with AUDs.

The relationship between alcohol and blood pressure is complex. While some studies suggest small amounts of alcohol may not adversely affect blood pressure, heavy alcohol use is a well-established and modifiable cause of hypertension [26]. High blood pressure rises after excessive alcohol consumption, and studies have found that heavy alcohol use, or excessive drinking, is one of the most common causes of resistant hypertension [27]. The effects of ethanol on the human cardiovascular system are divided into short-term effects and cumulative effects. Acutely, heavy alcohol consumption, ethanol has a direct dilatory effect on the peripheral blood vessels, and at the same time causes the excitation of sympathetic nerves, and the physiological effects are manifested as a decrease in cardiac stroke volume, an increase in heart rate, and a transient drop in blood pressure. Long term heavy drinking, ethanol will interfere with the fat metabolism process, resulting in the rise of cholesterol and triglycerides in the blood, and thus hyperlipidemia. The long-term effect is that alcohol causes the lipids in the blood to deposit on the blood vessel wall, causing the narrowing and hardening of the vascular lumen, reducing the elasticity of the blood vessels, and thus increasing the blood pressure [28, 29].

Long term drinking can also affect glucose synthesis and catabolism after liver injury. And long-term high-dose alcohol intake will reduce the action of insulin on the surface of hepatocytes, inhibit IRS-1 phosphorylation, interfere with insulin signal transduction, reduce insulin sensitivity, and increase insulin resistance, and thus increase the risk of diabetes [30, 31]. In this study, the diastolic blood pressure, uric acid, fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglyceride and BMI of the problem drinking group increased to varying degrees compared with that of the non-problem drinking group, suggesting that problem drinking may increase the levels of FSG, blood pressure and triglyceride, thus leading to an increased risk of metabolic syndrome.

Contrary to much of the existing literature, our study did not find a statistically significant difference in the rates of clinically relevant depressive or anxiety symptoms between the problem and non-problem drinking groups. This finding warrants careful interpretation. It is possible that the screening tools used, CES-D and SAS, while effective for detecting symptoms in general populations, may lack sensitivity to capture the full spectrum of clinical disorders in this specific working population, as these instruments have not been adequately validated for occupational cohorts and may overlook AUD-specific manifestations [32]. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the study captures a single time point [33], and the temporal relationship between AUD and these symptoms—which is known to be complex and often bidirectional—cannot be established, thereby precluding causal inference [34, 35]. It is also plausible that in this cohort of actively employed males, the physical and metabolic consequences of problem drinking manifest more overtly or earlier than significant, self-reported psychological distress [36, 37], or that cultural factors and social stigma (e.g., masculine identity norms discouraging emotional disclosure) may suppress the reporting of psychological symptoms [38]. Future longitudinal studies using structured diagnostic interviews (e.g., CIDI, MINI) for psychiatric disorders would be essential to fully elucidate this dynamic relationship and address methodological limitations of symptom scales [39].

Alcohol significantly disrupts sleep architecture. While it may initially act as a sedative, helping to reduce sleep latency, its metabolic byproducts later in the night lead to fragmented sleep, reduced REM sleep, and overall poor sleep quality [40]. Alcohol relaxes muscles in the upper airway, which can exacerbate sleep-disordered breathing and lead to increased nocturnal arousals. This disruption of normal sleep cycles can result in daytime sleepiness and impaired cognitive function [41, 42]. In this study, it was also found that the proportion of sleep disorders in the problem drinking group was significantly higher than that in the non-problem drinking group, suggesting that problem drinking increased the risk of sleep disorders.

An interesting result of the study was the complex relationship between smoking status and problem drinking. Our logistic regression showed that, compared to non-smokers, current smokers had a higher risk, but those who had quit smoking had an even higher risk of problem drinking. This may suggest that some individuals use alcohol as a coping mechanism to manage nicotine withdrawal symptoms like anxiety and craving when they attempt to quit smoking [43]. Nicotine and alcohol have synergistic effects on the brain’s reward pathways, which can reinforce the use of both substances [44]. In addition, studies have found that alcohol increases the stimulant effect of nicotine, which promotes smoking behavior [45]. Therefore, it is suggested that clinicians should be aware of this potential for increased alcohol consumption during smoking cessation attempts and provide integrated support for both dependencies.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing causality; for instance, we cannot determine if sleep disturbances lead to drinking or vice versa. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the direction of these relationships. Second, the reliance on self-report for alcohol consumption and other behaviors is subject to recall and social desirability bias, which may have led to an underestimation of actual consumption levels. Third, we conducted multiple statistical comparisons, which could inflate the probability of a Type I error. Although many of our findings were highly significant (P < 0.001), future studies should consider applying corrections for multiple testing. Fourth, our sample was drawn from workers in Hebei province using a convenience sampling method, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other regions of China or different occupational groups. Finally, we did not assess other potential confounders such as diet, occupational stress levels, or detailed family psychiatric history, which could influence both drinking behavior and metabolic outcomes.

Conclusion

In this sample of male workers, problem drinking was associated with a significantly higher risk of adverse metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes, including elevated liver enzymes, dyslipidemia, higher blood pressure, and increased BMI. It was also strongly linked to poor sleep quality and a history of smoking. Glutamyl transpeptidase and diastolic blood pressure emerged as potential predictors of high-risk alcohol use. These findings highlight the importance of implementing targeted screening, prevention, and integrated treatment programs within occupational health settings to address the multifaceted health consequences of alcohol use disorders in this vulnerable population.
