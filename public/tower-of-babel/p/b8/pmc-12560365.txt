Association between SHR and mortality in critically ill patients with CVD: a retrospective analysis and machine learning approach
Authors: Wanlu Zhou, Junxi Liao, Ting Wu, Jingjia Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), a measure of glucose metabolism, has emerged as a novel indicator of illness severity in critically ill patients. This study aims to evaluate the association between SHR and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD).MethodsClinical data of 1,913 critically ill patients with CVD were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. The primary outcomes were 360-day, 28-day, and 7-day mortality. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression and Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to assess the relationship between SHR and mortality risk in critically ill patients with CVD. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted to estimate survival rates across SHR quartiles. Additionally, five predictive models were developed using machine learning (ML) algorithms, and the predictive value of SHR was assessed using the SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) algorithm.ResultsRCS regression analyses demonstrated a positive linear association between SHR and mortality risk, indicating that higher SHR were linked to an increased risk of adverse outcomes. Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox regression further confirmed that a higher SHR was significantly associated with an elevated risk of mortality in patients with CVD compared to a lower SHR. Predictive machine learning (ML) models were constructed. EXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm demonstrated the best performance, with SHR playing a critical role in prediction as identified by SHAP analysis.ConclusionsSHR is significantly correlated with mortality in critically ill patients with CVD. Based on ML-based predictive models and ROC curves, SHR appears to be a promising indicator in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01946-8.Keywords: Stress hyperglycemia ratio, Cardiovascular disease, Mortality risk, Machine learning algorithm

Introduction

CVD is the leading global cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide [1] and plays a critical role in the study of Health disparities. In 2018, CVD accounted for approximately one-third of the 2.8 million deaths reported in the United States. Among these, ischemic heart disease (IHD) accounted for 42% of all cardiovascular-related deaths, while stroke contributed 17% [2]. Despite significant advances in CVD prognosis—driven by the widespread adoption of interventional procedures and improvements in medical technology—the incidence of CVD remains alarmingly high, and its mortality continues to pose a major public health concern. Consequently, the early identification of CVD patients at high risk is essential for timely intervention and vigilant care.

Stress-induced hyperglycemia (SIH) is an adaptive physiological response characterized by a significant increase in blood glucose levels during periods of physiological or pathological stress, which helps to provide the body with energy to cope with these stressors [3]. Stress-induced insulin resistance [4] and elevated levels of circulating glucose-elevating hormones (such as catecholamine, steroid, and glucagon) [5] are frequently observed in critically ill patients. In patients with chronic illnesses or those exposed to prolonged stress, a hyperglycemic state may be linked to unfavorable clinical outcomes [3]. For example, hyperglycemia-induced hyperinflammation and oxidative stress are considered as key contributors of the development of atherosclerosis and plaque instability, leading to plaque rupture [6] and an elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular events [7]. Additionally, hyperglycemia also alters molecular, metabolic, electrical, and mechanical cardiac function, potentially leading to heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction [8]. Recent research has also identified a significant association between SIH and in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in patients with acute coronary syndromes (ACS) [9].

Blood glucose and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) are widely used markers to assess glycemic status. However, neither of these markers adequately captures SIH. SHR is a well-known indicator of SIH and is regarded as a marker of illness severity in critically ill patients [10]. Recent research indicated a significant U-shaped correlation between SHR and mortality in patients with acute decompensated HF and diabetes mellitus, with both elevated and reduced SHR predicting a poor long-term prognosis [11]. Additionally, Yan et al. [3] suggested that the SHR was a potential predictor of poor prognosis in patients with sepsis and was significantly associated with an increased risk of adverse events [3]. In a prospective, multicenter China Acute Myocardial Infarction (CAMI) registry, 5,308 patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) were included, including 2,081 with diabetes and 3,227 without diabetes. The results of the CAMI registry showed that the SHR was significantly associated with in-hospital mortality in AMI patients, independent of glucose metabolism [12]. Unlike conventional markers, SHR provides a more accurate assessment of blood glucose levels under stress, offering valuable insights into the patient’s glycemic response during critical illness.

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, ML methods, known as deep analysis “vehicle”, have led to the development of multiple predictive tools for clinical applications [13]. These methods have gained increasing attention and recognition from clinicians, demonstrating superior performance compared to traditional analytical approaches [14]. For example, Fan et al. [15] developed a prognostic model in critically ill patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (AKI), using multiple ML algorithms, while Huang et al. [16] built prediction models for outcomes for patients with cholelithiasis-induced acute cholangitis based on ML methods. In this study, five ML models were constructed and validated to predict the risk of mortality in critically ill patients with CVD, identify the model with the best predictive performance, and determine the most important feature variable.

The prognostic assessment of the SHR among patients with severe CVD remains inadequately explored in the existing literature. In this study, the prognostic value of SHR was evaluated in critically ill patients with CVD from the MIMIC database.

Methods

Data source, study population, and data extraction

Data for this study were acquired from the MIMIC-IV database, which is a large publicly accessible dataset containing 73,181 intensive care unit (ICU) stays from 2008 to 2019. The database is operated by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, Massachusetts, USA). This study was in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived as anonymous data were analyzed. Access to the database was granted after completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program, with certification obtained by one author, Wanlu Zhou (certification number: 56052208), who was responsible for data extraction. Our permission was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Massachusetts Institute of Technology (Cambridge, MA, USA).

Structured query language (SQL) was utilized to extract patient records based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients diagnosed with CVD, including IHD and/or stroke, were enrolled in this study according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-first ICU admission; (2) retention time in ICU ≤ 24 h; (3) age < 18 years; (4) lack of HbA1c and blood glucose on the first day. Finally, a total of 1,913 patients were included in this study and divided into four groups based on the quartiles of SHR. The patient selection process was illustrated in Fig. 1.

Patient selection process

Demographic data, including sex, age, and ethnicity, were collected. Data, including weight, vital signs, and oxford acute severity of illness score (OASIS), were extracted within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Complications for each patient, identified based on ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, included hypertension, myocardial infarction (MI), congestive heart failure (CHF), atrial fibrillation (AF), dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), respiratory failure (RF), severe liver disease, AKI, peripheral vascular disease (PVD), and sepsis. Maximum values of laboratory indicators were collected within the first 24 h of admission to the ICU. There is currently no consensus on the definition of SHR globally. In this study, SHR was calculated using the first recorded HbA1c and blood glucose levels within 24 h of admission to the ICU. SHR was defined as the following formula: SHR = (blood glucose [mg/dl])/(28.7 × HbA1c [%] − 46.7)] [11].

Outcome

The primary outcomes of this study included 360-day, 28-day, and 7-day mortality, while the secondary outcome was the ICU mortality. Follow-up began on the date of ICU admission.

Statistical analysis

All patients were equally divided into four groups. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and Student's t-test were used to compare these data. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were made using Fisher’s exact test or Chi-squared test. Variables with more than 20% missing data were excluded from the analysis. For variables with less than 20% missing data, multiple imputation was performed using RStudio. Outliers were addressed using winsorization, with the 1% and 99% as cutoff points. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) to ensure the independence of variables in the analysis. A VIF < 5 indicates that multicollinearity may not substantially bias the estimates [17]. The relationship between SHR levels and the risk of adverse outcomes was elucidated through Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and RCS regression analyses. To assess the association between SHR and the risk of death at different time points, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox regression analysis, with the lowest SHR interval serving as the reference group. Model I was unadjusted; model II was adjusted for gender and age; and model III was further adjusted for gender, age, weight, HR, hemoglobin, white blood cell (WBC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, anion gap, CHF, hypertension, AF, sepsis, AKI, RF, vasopressin, OASIS. Additionally, odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI were calculated using logistic regression to analyze the association between SHR and the risk of ICU mortality. To evaluate the predictive validity of SHR and other baseline risk factors, we assessed the incremental value of adding SHR to the baseline risk factors for predicting all-cause outcomes using ROC curves. Subgroup analysis was conducted to explore the association between continuous SHR and all-cause mortality across different subgroups.

Lasso regression

Due to the imbalance in the proportions of non-survivors and survivors, a random oversampling balancing technique was applied to the training dataset to achieve balance before model construction. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression is one of the most important methods for feature selection. 22 characteristics with p-value less than 0.05 were included in the Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression model for further screening, including gender, age, races, weight, heart rate, hemoglobin, BUN, WBC, ALT, Scr, anion gap, potassium, SHR, CHF, hypertension, AF, sepsis, severe liver disease, AKI, respiratory failure, vasopressin, OASIS. Supplementary Fig. 1 displayed two dashed lines representing lambda.min and lambda.1se. Lambda.min corresponded to the value of λ at which the cross-validation error was minimized, while lambda.1se was determined as the value of λ where the cross-validation error remained within one standard error (SE) of the minimum. In this study, five-fold cross-validation was employed to identify the optimal penalty term (λ), and lambda.1se was selected as the optimal lambda for Lasso-based feature selection. A regression coefficient equal to zero indicated that the variable had little effect on the outcome, while a non-zero coefficient indicated that the characteristic variable was selected.

Establishment and validation of the prediction models

The selected feature variables were incorporated into the ML algorithms. The dataset was divided into training and test set in the ratio of 8:2, with the training set used for model development and the test set for internal evaluation and validation. Five different ML algorithms were employed to build the models, including random forest (RF), logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), XGBoost, and feedforward neural network (FFNN). Hyperparameter tuning was performed during the model-building process. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to evaluate the model’s performance. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,913 critically ill patients with CVD were enrolled from the MIMIC-IV cohort (Table 1). The 360-day mortality and ICU mortality were 23.73% and 7.16%, respectively. The average age of the patients was 69.66 ± 14.61 years old, with 848 female patients (44.33%) and 1,065 male patients (55.67%). The mean values of SHR for all the enrolled patients were 1.09 ± 0.35. Patients were divided into four groups based on SHR level in the MIMIC-IV cohort (Q1 [n = 478], Q2 [n = 478], Q3 [n = 477], Q4 [n = 480]). Compared with survivors at the 360-day follow-up, non-survivors were more likely to have hypertension, CHF, AF, respiratory failure, severe liver disease, AKI, sepsis, as well as higher OASIS scores. Additionally, non-survivors exhibited elevated levels of WBC, ALT, ALP, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Scr), blood glucose, sodium, potassium, anion gap, and SHR value, along with a lower weight (all P < 0.05). Features selected in models

To further assess the significance of SHR, the LASSO method was employed to screen the significant variables. Variables were initially selected based on a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Non-zero characteristic variables were included in the Lasso regression analysis. The final variables selected for 360-day mortality via Lasso regression encompassed OASIS, age, respiratory failure, BUN, sepsis, potassium, SHR, Heart rate and Hemoglobin. Similarly, Lasso regression was also used to screen the characteristic variables for 28-day and 7-day mortality (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Machine learning-based predictive modeling predicting the mortality risks

Five ML models were developed to predict mortality risk, including the RF, LR, SVM, XGBoost, and FFNN. Model performance was primarily evaluated using ROC curves (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among these, the XGBoost algorithm demonstrated superior performance, with significantly higher AUC values compared to the other ML models (360-day: AUC, 0.847[95% CI 0.796–0.898]; 28-day: AUC, 0.900[95% CI 0.877–0.922]; 7-day: AUC, 0.967[95% CI 0.954–0.980). Supplementary Table 2 described the performance comparison of these predictive ML model. Consequently, based on its robust performance, the XGBoost algorithm was chosen as the optimal model for predicting CVD mortality risk in this study.

Interpretability analysis

The feature importance ranking in the XGBoost model was illustrated in Fig. 4 using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values. SHAP values were employed to evaluate the impact of each feature on 360-day, 28-day, and 7-day mortality predictions in the XGBoost model. Each dot on the plot represents a patient’s SHAP value for a given feature. Red indicated higher feature values, while blue indicated lower feature values. Dots are stacked vertically to show density. The further the point was from the baseline SHAP value of zero, the greater the effect on the model output. The SHAP average values were also used to assess the contribution of features to the model, SHR as one of the most important features in the order of importance (Supplementary Fig. 3). The probability of mortality in critically ill patients with CVD increases with the SHAP values of the features. These findings underscored the critical role of SHR in predicting mortality risk.

ROC curve analysis of SHR

The ROC curve illustrating the predictive capacity of SHR to predict mortality in patients with CVD is shown in Fig. 5. Additionally, an analysis was carried out to assess whether SHR could further enhance the predictive ability of the basic model, which included gender, age, weight, Heart rate, Hemoglobin, WBC, ALT, Scr, anion gap, CHF, hypertension, AF, sepsis, AKI, respiratory failure, vasopressin, OASIS. Subgroup analysis

To explore potential clinical heterogeneity, we conducted interaction and stratified analyses (Supplementary Fig. 4). We assessed the relationships between SHR and 360-day, 28-day and 7-day mortality in different subgroups of patients stratified by AKI, dyslipidemia, and vasopressin. No significant interaction effects were found between SHR and the 360-day, 28-day, and 7-day mortality (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

CVD is a critical condition characterized by high mortality rates. Early identification and management of risk factors are essential for improving patient outcomes. Despite advancements in clinical treatment, no universally accepted prognostic indicators currently exist. Clinical laboratory tests and iconography are frequently used to evaluate a patient’s condition. However, the prognostic value of these tests in CVD patients remains underexplored. Therefore, identifying a simpler and more effective prognostic marker is of significant importance to enhance clinical care and optimize patient management.

To date, this study is the first to demonstrate a significant association between elevated SHR levels and mortality at various time points in critically ill CVD patients. In the XGBoost model, SHAP analysis further highlighted SHR as a key contributor that is strongly associated with poor prognosis. Furthermore, SHR significantly improved the predictive accuracy of the baseline risk model. Notably, these findings remained consistent across various subgroups, including patients with AKI, dyslipidemia, and those receiving vasopressin. These results provide robust evidence that SHR is a reliable predictor of all-cause mortality in critically ill CVD patients, underscoring its value as a prognostic biomarker.

Stress-induced hyperglycemia (SIH) represents an acute elevation in blood glucose triggered by stress or critical illness and may serve as an indicator of disease severity and prognosis. Previous studies have commonly used admission blood glucose (ABG) as a parameter to assess SIH [18]. However, ABG levels are affected not only by acute stress but also by chronic glycemic status, limiting the ability to assess acute hyperglycemic changes [19]. Therefore, it is not rigorous to explore the relationship between blood glucose alone and the prognosis of the diseases. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), which reflects average blood glucose levels over the past 2–3 months, provides a more comprehensive view of glycemic status. Combining HbA1c with blood glucose to calculate SHR may offer a more rational approach for assessing the relationship between SIH and disease outcomes. Numerous studies have demonstrated that SHR is a superior biomarker of critical illness compared to ABG or HbA1c alone [20, 21]. In addition, SHR is calculated solely from ABG and HbA1c levels. This inherent biological characteristic—independent of racial or geographic factors—may confer cross-population applicability. Current evidence demonstrates favorable predictive value of SHR across Asian [22–25], African [26], and European populations [27], though optimal cutoff values may exhibit population-specific variations. Although a standardized and universally accepted definition of SHR is currently lacking, this study defined SHR based on ABG and HbA1c levels, consistent with its focus on critical care. Additionally, in this study, factors that could influence HbA1c measurements, such as hemoglobin levels, were adjusted to enhance the validity and accuracy of the findings.

During acute stroke and MI, the stress response stimulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, leading to an excessive release of epinephrine, norepinephrine, cortisol, and glucagon, thereby contributing to elevated blood glucose levels [28–30]. Numerous clinical studies have further confirmed that SIH is common among patients with CAD [25] and stroke [30, 31]. Previous research has established an association between disturbances in glucose homeostasis and the MI outcomes, including death, no-reflow phenomenon, and myocardial salvage following successful revascularization, as well as the development of new-onset AF or AKI [32]. SIH could contribute to atherosclerotic plaque instability, rupture and exacerbation of myocardial ischemia, which are associated with a heightened risk of adverse cardiovascular events [33]. One potential mechanism through which SIH increases mortality involves its role in promoting inflammation. Under normal conditions, the body maintains a balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses to restore homeostasis. However, in some patients, this balance is disrupted by an excessive pro-inflammatory response, resulting in systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), organ dysfunction, and, ultimately, and death [34]. Inflammation has been consistently linked to poor outcomes in critically ill patients [35, 36]. Inflammation is also a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of CVD (CVD) and is associated with poor clinical outcomes. Atherosclerosis, the primary risk factor for CVD [37], is characterized by immune cells such as T-cell, macrophages, and mast cells. These cells release inflammatory cytokines, free radicals, and vasoactive molecules, which destabilize plaques, induce thrombosis, ultimately leading to ACS [38]. Therefore, inflammation plays a pivotal role throughout the progression of atherosclerotic lesions, from the initial lesion formation to the eventual development of thrombotic complications [39]. Furthermore, many studies have demonstrated that the immune system and the inflammatory response play a key role in stroke [40]. After ischemic stroke, the integrity of the blood-brain barrier is disrupted, allowing peripheral immune cells to migrate into the brain. A study using confocal microscopy in animal models of ischemic stroke found that neutrophils begin to accumulate in the leptomeninges at 6 h post-stroke, in the cortical basement membrane at 15 h, and in the brain parenchyma at 24 h [41]. SIH has been shown to exacerbate in vitro and in vivo infections by increasing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [42]. Hyperglycemia stimulates the release of proinflammatory factors, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in vitro [43], thereby accelerating atherosclerosis progression via inflammatory mechanisms [44]. Biomarkers such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and neutrophil-to-platelet ratio (NPR) are recognized indicators of systemic inflammation and are linked to poor clinical outcomes in various CVD [45]. A recent study found that elevated SHR levels were associated with higher neutrophil counts and increased NLR levels in patients with large-artery atherosclerotic ischemic stroke [40]. In these patients, SIH may contribute to the progression of atherosclerosis by activating peripheral blood lymphocytes and neutrophils, thereby disrupting the blood-brain barrier [40]. Furthermore SIH induces an inflammatory infiltration of vascular endothelial cells, and disrupts the blood-brain barrier, increasing the risk of cerebral oedema and haemorrhage [46]. Furthermore, pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), play a significant role in the pathogenesis of SIH, and lead to a vicious cycle [42]. For example, hyperglycemia is exacerbated by the inflammatory response in AMI patients [47]. This vicious cycle is thought to contribute to poorer outcomes in critically ill patients, including increased all-cause mortality [48].

Additionally, oxidative stress is another major factor contributing to increased mortality in CVD patients. SIH is often accompanied by oxidative stress, which leads to the production of lactic acid, exacerbates endothelial dysfunction, promotes thrombosis, and aggravates ischemia-reperfusion injury, all of which worsen myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction [31, 49, 50]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia increases the risk of microvascular dysfunction, no-reflow, and death through oxidative stress in patients with ACS and in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention [32]. This may be one of the reasons why high SHR levels increases the incidence of adverse events.

However, there were some limitations in our study. Firstly, given the retrospective and observational nature of this study, our findings represent associations and should not be interpreted as establishing causality. It limits our ability to establish a definitive causal relationship between SHR and adverse outcomes in patients with severe CVD. While causal inference is scientifically paramount and typically requires randomization, such experimental designs may not be feasible in critical care settings [51]. Furthermore, although we implemented multivariable adjustments and subgroup analyses to address numerous outcome-related factors with unclear causal pathways, potential confounders were not eliminated and may still influence clinical outcomes. Secondly, due to limitations of the MIMIC database, we were unable to confirm whether all glucose and HbA1c results were obtained from fasting population. Additionally, cardiac ultrasound, NT-proBNP, troponin, and more cardiac enzyme results were also not included in the study due to significant missing data. Finally, this study had a modest sample size, and was conducted at a single center. The patient population at this institution consists predominantly of critically ill cases, with a racial composition (primarily Caucasian) that differs significantly from other global regions. And the MIMIC-IV database’s patient cohort (comprising mainly ICU cases) may not be representative of other clinical settings. To enhance the robustness of our conclusions, future multicenter studies across diverse healthcare settings are warranted to validate the generalizability of these findings to broader populations, thereby mitigating the inherent limitations of single-center research. Future studies should address the above limitations as much as possible to provide strong evidence to support our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, SHR is a significant predictor of all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with CVD, primarily associated with the 360-day, 28-day, 7-day, and ICU mortality. A higher SHR is strongly associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. Overall, our findings enhance a better understanding of SHR’s role in CVD prognosis and provide a foundation for developing more effective prevention and treatment strategies for this patient population.
