Hyperuricaemia-Induced Hypertension in Rats by Regulating the RhoA/ROCK Pathway
Authors: Yan Zhang, Zhongchao Wang, Yinli Xu, Nan Wang, Zihua Xu, Tianshu Ren, Qingchun Zhao, Weihong Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHyperuricaemia (HUA) is associated with hypertension, but the mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated whether the RhoA/ROCK pathway mediates HUA-induced vascular remodelling and hypertension.MethodsThirty male Sprague-Dawley rats were divided into control, HUA, and fasudil (ROCK inhibitor) groups. Serum uric acid, creatinine, and blood pressure were measured biweekly. Histopathology, qPCR, and Western blotting assessed thoracic aortic changes and RhoA/ROCK pathway activation. Rat vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) were treated with uric acid, with or without RhoA/ROCK inhibitors, to evaluate nitric oxide (NO) levels and protein expression.ResultsHUA significantly increased systolic blood pressure, aortic media thickness, smooth muscle cell proliferation, and expression of RhoA, ROCK 1/2, and MLC1, with elevated phosphorylation of MLC and MYPT-1. Fasudil reduced blood pressure and ameliorated vascular remodelling but did not lower uric acid levels. In VSMCs, HUA decreased NO content, enhanced RhoA membrane translocation, and increased ROCK 2 expression, effects reversed by RhoA or ROCK inhibitors.ConclusionHUA promotes hypertension by activating the RhoA/ROCK pathway, leading to vascular remodelling and dysfunction. Inhibition of this pathway attenuates HUA-induced hypertension, highlighting the mechanistic relevance of the RhoA/ROCK pathway.

Introduction

Hypertension is the most significant factor affecting the global disease burden. Its complications include stroke, coronary heart disease, heart failure, kidney disease, other disabilities, and high mortality [1]. There is a strong causal relationship between blood pressure, risk of cardiovascular disease, and death [2, 3]. Increasing evidence indicates that hyperuricaemia (HUA) is an independent risk factor for hypertension and is closely related to its occurrence, development, and prognosis. Studies have shown that approximately 25% of patients with hypertension also have HUA, whereas the prevalence of hypertension in patients with HUA is approximately 30% [4]. One study found that increased uric acid levels in childhood are associated with increased blood pressure in adulthood, suggesting that uric acid plays an important role in the development of hypertension [5].

Recently, the RhoA/ROCK signalling pathway has been found to play an important role in the occurrence of cardiovascular diseases and is closely related to a variety of cardiovascular diseases, such as chronic heart failure, hypertension, and atherosclerosis [6]. RhoA controls cell contraction by regulating ROCK activity. However, excessive RhoA activation can also cause pathological changes in the cardiovascular system, particularly in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), resulting in strong fibrogenic effects [7]. Excessive activation of RhoA-dependent signalling pathways may lead to abnormal vasoconstriction and participate in pathological remodelling of the cardiovascular system by regulating cell growth and migration [8]. ROCK, a serine/threonine kinase, is a downstream effector molecule of RhoA and is involved in regulating the adhesion, migration, proliferation, and differentiation of smooth muscle cells together with the Rho protein. ROCK consists of two different isomers, ROCK 1 and ROCK 2. ROCK 1 is widely distributed, while ROCK 2 is mainly distributed in cardiovascular tissues [6]. The Rho kinase is activated by RhoA-GTP to phosphorylate its substrates. Rho kinase substrates include myosin light chain (MLC), myosin phosphatase target subunit (MYPT-1), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), and so on [9]. The RhoA/ROCK pathway also plays an important role in VSMC differentiation, migration, and proliferation, specifically by regulating the expression of the VSMC actin cytoskeleton [10] and participating in the regulation of the VSMC proliferative response [11].

Although the association between HUA and hypertension is well recognized, the precise molecular mechanisms linking elevated uric acid to vascular dysfunction remain incompletely understood. The RhoA/ROCK signalling pathway is a key regulator of vascular tone and remodelling, and its overactivation is an established contributor to hypertension. However, whether and how HUA activates this specific pathway to drive hypertension has not been directly investigated. Therefore, this study aimed to test the novel hypothesis that HUA promotes hypertension primarily through activation of the RhoA/ROCK pathway, leading to vascular smooth muscle dysfunction and remodelling. To this end, we established a rat model of HUA and combined in vivo physiological assessments with in vitro cellular experiments to systematically examine the role of the RhoA/ROCK pathway in HUA-induced hypertension.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials

Experimental Animals and Groups

This study used 30 SPF male Sprague-Dawley rats (Hua Fukang, Beijing, licence number: SCXK [Beijing] 2019-0008), aged 6 weeks and weighing 190–200 g. The rats were randomly divided into three groups: negative control (NC) group, HUA group, and fasudil (FS) group, with 10 rats in each group. The sample size for animal experiments (n = 10 per group) was determined based on previous studies employing similar HUA rat models [12]. Rats in the HUA and FS groups were fed 20% yeast paste mixed with rat feed (1:4) and intraperitoneally injected with 200 mg·kg-1·d-1 potassium oxazine in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose to induce HUA [13]. From the 5th week, rats in the FS group received FS (10 mg·kg-1·d-1) daily via intraperitoneal injection for 2 weeks. The NC group received 0.9% sodium chloride. Body weights were measured weekly, and dosages adjusted accordingly. Rats had free access to food throughout the 6-week study.

Experimental Cells

Rat thoracic aorta smooth muscle cell line VSMC (ChuanQiu Biotechnology Co. Ltd, Shanghai) was cultured in 10% FCS+Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) in an incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 saturated humidity. When the cells adhered to the wall and grew to 70–80% confluence, the original culture medium was discarded, and serum-free DMEM was used to continue the culture for 24 h to synchronize the VSMCs. The cells were then divided into the control group and groups treated with serum-free DMEM containing uric acid (40 mg/L), uric acid (40 mg/L) with rhosin (3 μm) pretreatment for 1 h, uric acid (40 mg/L) with Y27632 (3 μm) pretreatment for 1 h, and only U46619 (thromboxane A2 analogue; 10 μm) for 3 h. This concentration of uric acid (40 mg/L) was chosen based on prior in vitro studies [14, 15] using similar supraphysiological levels (around 25–100 mg/L) to induce relevant pathological responses in vascular cells, such as proliferation, oxidative stress, and dysfunction associated with HUA.

Experimental Reagents and Equipment

Rhosin (MCE, China), U46619 (Cayman, USA), Y27632 (MCE), FS (Tianjin Hongri, China), yeast paste (Beijing Auboxing, China), potassium oxazine (MCE), uric acid (McLean), p-MLC antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), MLC 1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology), MYPT-1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology), p-MYPTThr853 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology), RhoA antibody (Abcam, UK), ROCK 1 antibody (Abcam), ROCK 2 antibody (Abcam), β-actin antibody (Proteintech), rat and rabbit antibody (ABclonal Technology), a serum creatinine detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Company, China), a serum uric acid (SUA) detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Company, China), and a nitric oxide (NO) detection kit (Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., China) were obtained from the respective manufacturers. A Nova6000 high-throughput sequencer (Illumina, USA), Qubit4.0 instrument (Thermo, USA), gel imaging system (Tianneng, China), a fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) instrument (Bio-Rad, USA), and automatic chemiluminescent imager (ImageQuant LAS 4000, Bio-Rad, USA) were also used in this study.

Experimental Methods

Collection and Detection of Rat Blood Samples

At weeks 0, 2, 4, and 6, blood samples from rat inner canthus veins were collected. The supernatant was used to detect SUA and creatinine levels using a serum creatinine detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Company) and a SUA detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These detection methods are based on well-established enzymatic colorimetric principles. Specifically, creatinine amidohydrolase catalyses the hydrolysis of creatinine, and the resultant products lead to a colour change measurable at 546 nm. Similarly, uricase catalyses the oxidation of uric acid to produce hydrogen peroxide, which is detected through a colorimetric reaction.

Blood Pressure Detection in Rats

Blood pressure and serum biochemical indices were monitored every 2 weeks. Prior to the first measurement, all rats underwent a 3-day acclimatization period involving daily handling and mock tail-cuff placement to minimize stress-induced variability. To measure blood pressure [16], the front part of the rat was netted and placed in a black insulated tube with the temperature of the tube set to 39°C, and the rat’s tail placed outside was wrapped with the non-invasive Softron sphygmomanometer (BP-2010 series). Once the rat calmed down, the blood pressure was measured, and each measurement was repeated three times. Measurements were consistently performed by the same trained operator at the same time of day to further reduce procedural variability.

Thoracic Aorta Vascular Collection in Rats

The rats were anaesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 0.5% isopentobarbital sodium (10 mg/kg). The thoracic aorta was exposed, and the surrounding tissue was stripped of fat and muscle tissue in a precooled phosphate buffer saline solution. A part of the thoracic aorta was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated stepwise with alcohol, and embedded in paraffin for pathological examination. The other part was quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored in refrigerator at −80°C for Western blotting, mRNA transcriptome sequencing technology (mRNA-seq), and real-time qPCR analysis.

Haematoxylin and Eosin Staining

After 2–3 days of tissue fixation, gradient alcohol and environmental dewaxing solutions were used for dehydration, transparency, and waxing. The procedure was as follows: paraffin sections were dewaxed in water, followed by haematoxylin and eosin staining, dehydration sealing, microscopic examination, image collection, and analysis [16].

Bioinformatics Analysis of Target Genes

To investigate transcriptional changes, thoracic aortic tissues from 5 randomly selected rats per group (NC and HUA) were used for RNA extraction and subsequent transcriptome sequencing. Sequencing was performed using the Illumina Nova6000 platform, with an average sequencing depth of approximately 30 million reads per sample. Image data obtained from high-throughput sequencing were converted into sequence data using CASAVA base recognition. For each sample, STAR software was used to conduct sequence comparisons between the pretreated sequences and reference genome sequences. DESeq2 software was used to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among different groups according to experimental design. Genes were considered differentially expressed if | log2FC | ≥1 and adjusted p value (Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate [FDR]) < 0.05. The significantly DEGs were classified according to their positions on related protein-coding genes, and target genes of DEGs were then predicted for Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. Functional annotation and classification of disease types in the DisGeNET disease database were performed for these genes.

Real-Time Quantitative Fluorescence Detection

Part of the thoracic aorta was carefully dissected and cut into small pieces in RNA fixation solution (TRIzol). The tissue pieces were then homogenized, and total RNA was extracted using a commercially available RNA extraction kit (TRIzol reagent) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of RNA were assessed by measuring absorbance at 260 nm using a spectrophotometer [17]. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene to normalize the expression of target genes. The mRNA expression levels of ROCK 1 and MLC1 were quantified using fluorescence qPCR. The primers used for amplification were designed as follows: forward, 3′-ACC​AAT​CCC​ACA​AAT​GCA​GAA​GT-5′, and reverse, 5′-CAC​GCA​GAC​CCT​CAA​CGA​AAT-3′. The ROCK 1 primer sequences were as follows: forward, 3′-GGA​AAC​GCT​CCG​AGA​CAC​TG-5′, and reverse, 5′-CTG​TTC​TCA​CTG​GGA​TTT​GCT​G-3′. qPCR was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol, using SYBR Green Master Mix or a similar reagent for amplification. The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. A melting curve analysis was performed to ensure the specificity of the amplification. The mRNA expression levels of ROCK 1 and MLC1 were normalized to GAPDH, and the results were analysed using the ΔΔCT method to calculate relative expression levels. For tissue validation, three biological replicates were used for each group in the qPCR assay.

Detection of NO

The intracellular NO content in cultured rat VSMCs was determined using the Griess reagent method. It should be noted that endothelial cells are the primary source of vascular NO in intact vessels; therefore, measurements in VSMCs may not fully reflect overall vascular NO bioavailability. Cells were lysed using the IP lysis solution, and Griess reagents I and II were added to each well containing the sample and standard reagent at room temperature. The absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer at 540 nm.

Western Blotting Analysis of Protein Expression

Proteins were extracted from thoracic aorta tissue or VSMCs, and then electrophoresed, transferred to a membrane, blocked, and incubated overnight with the primary antibodies (RhoA, 1:2,000; ROCK 1, 1:1,000; ROCK 2, 1:1,000; MLC1, 1:1,000; p-MLC, 1:1,000; MYPT-1, 1:1,000; p-MYPTThr853, 1:1,000; β-actin, 1:5,000) in a 4°C shaker. After washing the film three times, the respective secondary antibodies were added (mouse antibody, 1:10,000; rabbit antibody, 1:12,000), and the membranes were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes with TBST, an automatic chemiluminescent imager was used to capture images. ImageJ was used to analyse the grey values [18]. For tissue samples, n = 3 biological replicates were analysed; for VSMC experiments, n = 5 independent experiments were performed.

Statistical Analysis

The experimental data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 5 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Experimental results are shown as the mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons among the three groups at a single time point (e.g., protein expression, aortic morphology), one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used. For longitudinal data with repeated measurements (e.g., blood pressure, body weight), two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was applied with “group” and “time” as the two factors, and their interaction was also assessed, followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For the exploratory correlation analysis between SUA and blood pressure, the average value at each time point of each group was regarded as an analysis unit. Pearson correlation was used to assess the linear association, and Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate the monotonic relationship between SUA and systolic blood pressure or diastolic blood pressure. All tests were two-tailed. For multiple comparisons from high-throughput data, the FDR was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 or adjusted p (FDR) < 0.05 for RNA-seq data.

However, FS administration significantly lowered the systolic blood pressure in the FS group compared to the HUA group (p < 0.05). There was no significant difference in diastolic blood pressure among the three groups (p > 0.05, Fig. 1 and Table 4). Effects of HUA on the Morphology of the Thoracic Aorta

Haematoxylin and eosin staining and morphological analysis showed that the mean thickness of the thoracic aortic media was 72.7 ± 8.57 μm in the NC group and was significantly thickened to 96.41 ± 11.55 μm in the HUA group, with significant proliferation and disorder of smooth muscle cells (p < 0.05). Compared with the HUA group, vascular smooth muscle hyperplasia was significantly ameliorated in the FS group 72.95 ± 11.06 μm (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between the FS and NC groups (p > 0.05, Fig. 2 and Table 6).

Bioinformatics Analysis of DEGs between the NC and HUA Groups

There were 422 DEGs between the NC and HUA groups, 265 of which were upregulated and 157 downregulated; these DEGs and their p values were used to construct a volcano plot (Fig. 3a). Bidirectional hierarchical clustering was conducted on the DEGs, and heat maps showed that the differential genes were correctly classified. The NC and HUA groups could be distinguished based on their different gene expression profiles (Fig. 3b). Among these, genes related to vasoconstriction, such as ROCK 1 and MLC1, were upregulated.

DEGs were defined as |log2FC| ≥1 with FDR <0.05.

Compared to the NC group, the enrichment terms of the GO biological process analysis of rats in the HUA group focused on the development and morphological changes in striated muscle and myocardial tissue. Cell component analysis revealed that genes related to sarcomeres, myofibrils, contractile fibres, and myosin were significantly enriched. Molecular function analysis mainly focused on actin binding, cytoskeletal protein binding, and ATPase regulation (Fig. 3c). KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs showed that they mainly included the vascular smooth muscle contraction, cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)-PKG, cAMP pathway, adipokine signalling pathway, and insulin secretion pathway (Fig. 3d). According to the disease-type classification of DEGs between NC and HUA groups, the DEGs were associated with the occurrence of cardiovascular diseases, such as hypertension, heart failure, and myocardial ischaemia which accounted for a large proportion, suggesting that they are closely related to HUA (Fig. 3e).

Previous mRNA-seq and qPCR results suggested that the gene and mRNA expression levels of ROCK 1 and MLC1 in the thoracic aorta of the HUA group were significantly higher than those in the NC group. Therefore, Western blotting was performed on proteins involved in related pathways. The protein levels of RhoA, ROCK 1, and MLC1 in the thoracic aorta of rats in the HUA group were significantly higher than those in the NC group. There was no difference in RhoA expression, whereas ROCK 1 and MLC1 protein expression was significantly lower in the FS group than in the HUA group (p < 0.05, Fig. 5). MLC phosphorylation was higher in the HUA group than in the NC group. FS reduced the HUA-induced phosphorylation of MLC (p < 0.05). These results suggest that HUA can activate the RhoA/ROCK pathway and increase MLC phosphorylation, and that this effect can be inhibited by FS.

Discussion

HUA is a common metabolic disease observed clinically and is closely associated with a high-purine diet, hyperglycaemia, hyperlipidaemia, and obesity. Basal blood uric acid level is the strongest independent predictor of the incidence of essential hypertension [19], and the risk of hypertension increases 1.2 times with every 10 mg/L increase in uric acid [20]. Uric acid is a weak organic acid with low solubility in water. In primates such as humans and apes, uric acid cannot be catabolized. Although the uric acid levels of mammals with functional uricases are usually in the range of 10–20 mg/L, those of humans and great apes are 3–10 times higher [10]. In rats, normal uric acid levels range from 8 to 15 mg/L. In our study, we used 40 mg/L uric acid concentration, which is 2–3 times higher than that in normal rats, to mimic a pathological level relevant to human HUA. However, it should be noted that this elevated concentration may induce cellular responses that differ from those in chronic, mild HUA.

Through the bioinformatics analysis of DEGs between NC and HUA groups, we found that several genes related to vascular smooth muscle proliferation, contraction, and fibrosis were significantly altered in the HUA group. The upregulation of ROCK 1 and MLC1 reflects an increase in vascular smooth muscle contraction, a change that underlies vascular remodelling in HUA. The expression changes of these genes provide key insights into how HUA alters vascular function at the molecular level. These findings help explain how vascular remodelling leads to hypertension and offer potential molecular targets for future interventions.

Our study found that the upregulation of RhoA and ROCK 1 was closely linked to MLC phosphorylation, suggesting that the RhoA/ROCK pathway promotes hypertension by regulating vascular smooth muscle contraction. Specifically, the upregulation of ROCK 1 in HUA promotes smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, worsening vascular constriction and elevating blood pressure. Thus, our data suggest that the RhoA/ROCK pathway may play a key role in HUA-induced hypertension by potentially interacting with proteins like MLC and contributing to vascular remodelling. Further studies are needed to establish direct causality in vivo. Additionally, HUA was associated with increased body weight in rats. While the precise mechanism underlying this observation was not investigated here (e.g., kidney weight, fat mass, or other metabolic parameters were not measured), it underscores the systemic metabolic alterations in this model. The primary focus and robust finding of this study remain the activation of the RhoA/ROCK pathway and its contribution to vascular remodelling and hypertension.

FS, a moderately effective isoquinoline ROCK inhibitor, is used clinically against subarachnoid haemorrhages and has been found to have a good therapeutic effect in a variety of cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, coronary vasospasm, and atherosclerosis [21]. In patients with hypertension, the administration of FS can enhance the reactivity of forearm vessels to vasodilators [22]. Rhosin is a small-molecule inhibitor of the RhoA subfamily of Rho GTPases that is currently under investigation. In cells, rhosin blocks RhoA activation and RhoA-mediated MLC phosphorylation without affecting endogenous Cdc42 or Rac1 activity, or signal transduction events [23, 24]. Y-27632 is a medium-strength ROCK inhibitor that can exert smooth muscle relaxation and antihypertensive effects through the non-specific inhibition of ROCK 1 and ROCK 2. In hypertensive models, Y-27632 inhibited smooth muscle contraction, thereby reducing blood pressure [25, 26].

In this study, we found that FS significantly decreased systolic blood pressure in rats, but there was no significant change in diastolic blood pressure with HUA. This can be explained by the different mechanisms regulating systolic and diastolic pressure. Systolic blood pressure is influenced by cardiac function and peripheral vascular resistance, while diastolic pressure reflects vascular compliance, which is related to blood vessel elasticity. HUA increases peripheral vascular resistance through vascular remodelling and smooth muscle hyperplasia, elevating systolic blood pressure. FS reduces vascular smooth muscle contraction by inhibiting the RhoA/ROCK pathway, improving peripheral resistance, and lowering systolic pressure. However, diastolic pressure is more affected by vascular compliance, and while FS improved vascular contraction, its impact on compliance was limited. This explains why systolic pressure decreased but diastolic pressure remained unchanged [27]. Although FS effectively alleviates hypertension, it does not significantly affect blood uric acid levels. FS works by targeting the RhoA/ROCK pathway in vascular smooth muscle but does not directly influence uric acid metabolism. Therefore, FS’s primary effect is to reduce hypertension induced by HUA, rather than lowering uric acid levels, which explains its ineffectiveness in controlling uric acid in this study [28].

In this study, HUA significantly increased the expression of proteins involved in the RhoA-Rho kinase pathway. Inhibiting the RhoA or ROCK pathway alleviated the effects of HUA, suggesting that HUA-induced hypertension is closely related to the activation of the RhoA-ROCK pathway. Previous studies have shown that the RhoA/ROCK signalling pathway is involved in the occurrence and development of hypertension, with overactivation of RhoA and ROCK detectable in the vascular endothelium of various hypertensive rat models [21]. Additionally, the active form of RhoA was significantly upregulated in HEK cells treated with uric acid crystals [22]. RhoA is recruited from the cytoplasm to the membrane, where it is phosphorylated to activate downstream signals. Blocking the ROCK pathway can reduce the initial vasoconstriction effects caused by mechanical, humoral, and neural stimuli [23, 24].

Uric acid contributes to the reduction of NO bioavailability through various mechanisms, including blocking the uptake of l-arginine, stimulating the degradation of l-arginine, and clearing NO through oxidants produced by uric acid or by uric acid itself [25, 26]. Furthermore, activation of the RhoA/ROCK signalling pathway can inhibit eNOS protein expression, resulting in reduced NO synthesis, vascular diastolic dysfunction, and vascular constriction [29, 30]. Downregulated eNOS expression also impairs the endothelial cells’ response to NO [31, 32]. Similar results were observed in this study. HUA significantly reduced the intracellular NO content in VSMCs, and NO levels were improved after RhoA/ROCK inhibitor intervention, suggesting that inhibition of the RhoA/ROCK pathway may help preserve vascular contractile function under conditions of HUA in our experimental model. However, it is important to note that our NO measurements were performed in cultured VSMCs, whereas endothelial cells are the primary source of NO in intact vessels. Therefore, these in vitro findings may not fully represent overall vascular NO bioavailability in vivo. Based on previous studies, activation of the RhoA/ROCK pathway has been reported to suppress eNOS activity and reduce NO bioavailability, which may subsequently decrease cGMP signalling and further enhance RhoA activation [29, 33, 34]. In the present study, we observed reduced NO levels and increased RhoA/ROCK activation under hyperuricaemic conditions. Although eNOS expression/phosphorylation and cGMP levels were not directly measured, our findings are consistent with the possibility that the NO-cGMP-RhoA axis may contribute to the observed vascular dysfunction.

Based on the above findings, we propose a mechanistic model in which HUA promotes activation of the RhoA/ROCK pathway, leading to enhanced vascular contractility and elevated systolic blood pressure, potentially involving impaired NO signalling (Fig. 8). There are several limitations of this study that need to be addressed. Firstly, the high yeast diet and potassium oxoprophosphate-induced HUA model, in addition to increasing uric acid levels, may cause non-targeted metabolic or renal effects. Potassium oxoprophosphate and dietary intervention may affect blood pressure through mechanisms independent of uric acid, which may interfere with the interpretation of the observed hemodynamic changes. Secondly, since rodents have functional uricase, the results of mechanism studies should be used with caution when directly applied to human pathophysiology lacking uricase. Thirdly, this study mainly relied on pharmacological inhibitors; future studies using genetic methods such as gene knockdown/knockout will provide more direct causal evidence for the role of the RhoA/ROCK pathway. It is worth noting that although we observed a decrease in NO levels, we did not directly detect the expression or phosphorylation status of eNOS, nor quantify cGMP levels. Therefore, the proposed NO-cGMP-RhoA feedback loop’s role remains inferential and requires further experimental verification. Fourthly, this study did not measure kidney weight, fat mass, or other metabolic parameters, making it impossible to rule out the influence of other factors on vascular or renal remodelling. The direct impact of the RhoA/ROCK pathway on body weight still requires further physiological and metabolic assessment for more comprehensive research. Fifthly, the uric acid concentration used in the in vitro experiments of this study was often used in previous mechanism studies to induce clear cellular effects, but it is still a supraphysiological state compared to the typical levels of chronic HUA. This may trigger non-specific cellular stress, thereby limiting the direct translation of these in vitro findings to more gradual and physiological processes in vivo. Finally, this experiment was conducted only in male rats and did not explore potential gender-specific differences. Future research addressing these limitations will deepen our understanding of the association between HUA and hypertension.

HUA induces hypertension in rats via RhoA/ROCK pathway.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that experimental HUA is associated with an increase in systolic blood pressure in vascular tissues and the activation of the RhoA/ROCK signalling pathway. The pharmacological inhibition of ROCK alleviated vascular remodelling and hypertension, but did not affect SUA levels, highlighting the mechanistic relevance of this pathway. These findings reveal a new mechanism link between HUA and blood pressure regulation. Although clinical translation requires further validation in human studies, the RhoA/ROCK axis is a promising target for future research on the cardiovascular risks associated with high uric acid.
