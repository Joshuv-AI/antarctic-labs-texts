Qualitative research to understand the patient experience with advanced and/or metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Sally Lanar, Asma Kefsi, Maria Armaou, Fallon Jones-Lemmons, Marie de La Cruz, Kelly Lipman, Benoit Arnould, Cécile Gousset
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe present study aimed to explore the patient experience in advanced and/or metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).Materials & methodsA multi-phase research design was followed that included a targeted literature review (TLR), interviews with clinicians, and interviews with patients. Patient interviews were analyzed using an iterative, thematic analysis approach. An assessment of the conceptual saturation of all spontaneously patient-reported signs and symptoms was conducted.ResultsTen patient-centric qualitative studies were identified in the TLR. Five United States (US) clinicians and 15 US patients participated in semi-structured interviews, conducted via video conferencing, lasting approximately 60 minutes each. The five most commonly reported symptoms were fatigue, weight loss, weakness, reduction in appetite, and sleep disturbance. Furthermore, HCC impacted all aspects of patients’ lives, including physical functioning, social and family functioning, emotional functioning, physical activities, and activities of daily living. The concepts were mostly similar across the different data sources. A patient-centric conceptual model was developed based on the TLR and patient interviews.ConclusionThe study provides an in-depth description of signs/symptoms and impacts in advanced and/or metastatic HCC that can support the identification of suitable patient-reported outcome measures. Separate investigation is needed to distinguish between disease impacts and treatment impacts.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, patient experience, advanced/metastatic cancer, qualitative research, patient-reported outcomes, impact assessment, conceptual model

Introduction

Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer diagnosis and the third leading cause of death worldwide [1]. Patients with HCC have a high mortality rate with a relative survival rate of 31.0% [2]. Men are two to three times more likely to develop HCC than women. The highest incidence rates are among patients greater than 70 years of age [1,3,4]. Risk factors for HCC can vary by geographic region and include chronic viral hepatitis (hepatitis B virus [HBV] infection, hepatitis C virus [HCV] infection), alcohol-related liver disease, environmental exposures, particularly aflatoxin-contaminated foods, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [5].

According to the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification, HCC is comprised of four diagnostic stages (A-D). Staging takes into consideration the size, location, and number of tumors [6]. HCC is typically asymptomatic at early stages of the disease. As a result, the majority of patients (80%) are often diagnosed at later stages [7,8]. Patients with advanced and/or metastatic disease often present with more severe signs and symptoms, including abdominal pain, fatigue, weakness, jaundice, anorexia, weight loss, vomiting, dyspnea, and ascites. These symptoms impact patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and functional status.

HCC patients with advanced and/or metastatic disease face poor prognosis and have limited treatment options. It is, therefore, vital to be able to understand different facets of patient needs throughout the patient journey. The fact that HCC is diagnosed in late stages and typically in conjunction with chronic liver diseases reduces the possibility of curative strategies; as such, typical treatments aim to halt or decelerate progression. Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, which has been a common treatment for advanced/and or metastatic HCC, has usually been ineffective, with limited clinical benefits and significant toxic side effects on several organ systems [9–11]. With new systemic therapies, the role of chemotherapy in advanced and/or metastatic HCC is uncertain. Systemic therapies have significantly increased in the last decade. Sorafenib, a systemic therapy introduced in 2007, was the first tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) and the only effective systemic treatment for more than 10 years. Other TKIs such as lenvatinib, regorafenib, ramucirumab, and cabozantinib were approved starting in 2017 as second-line therapies [9]. In addition to TKIs, immunotherapies are gaining traction in treating advanced and/or metastatic HCC. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) accelerated the approval of nivolumab as a second-line monotherapy for advanced HCC following sorafenib failure in 2017. In 2018, Pembrolizumab (Keytruda) received accelerated approval by the FDA as a second-line treatment for HCC in patients who have previously been treated with sorafenib. In 2020, the combination of two immunotherapies (atezolizumab and bevacizumab) was recommended for first-line treatment of unresectable HCC [12]. Since then, different combinations of regimens have been investigated and approved. In particular, in 2017 Nivolumab was granted accelerated approval as a second-line monotherapy for advanced HCC following sorafenib failure Tremelimumab in combination with Durvalumab was approved for first-line treatment of unresectable HCC in 2022. Finally, the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab was initially approved as a second-line treatment for patients with HCC who progressed on or were intolerant to sorafenib in 2020; however, in 2025 it was approved as first-line treatment for unresectable or metastatic HCC, based on positive results from the Phase III CheckMate‑9DW trial [13–16].

Ongoing clinical trials exploring combination therapies demonstrate the shift toward multidrug-regimen HCC treatments, indicating the complexity of advanced and/or metastatic HCC and the need for diverse therapeutic approaches [5,17].

Overall, treatments have been found to enhance immune responses and provide increased clinical benefits; however, these combination therapies differ in terms of efficacy, toxicity, and adverse events. For example, the administration of sorafenib is associated with extended survival rates but it is essential to develop treatment plans that can offer effective management of adverse effects [18]. Common adverse events of sorafenib can include diarrhea, fatigue, anorexia, hypertension, and multiple dermatological toxicities, i.e., hand-foot-skin reactions [19]. Regrettably, the prevalence of adverse events can often result in patients frequently deviating from the prescribed pharmaceutical regimen, with many ultimately discontinuing the medicine entirely [19]. Sorafenib-related side effects have often been described as being a surrogate biomarker of treatment efficacy and different immune-related adverse effects have also demonstrated varying prognostic value [20,21]. For an effective management of treatment side effects, it is important to undertake individualized treatment strategies, characterized by flexible and tailored management of systemic treatment (e.g., prompt management of AEs, easy access to unscheduled visits, and consultations to detect AEs) [18].

Given these different treatment options, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) provide important information about the impact of treatment from the patients’ perspective. PROMs are an increasingly important endpoint in cancer clinical trials and are frequently used to examine patient perceptions of the treatment’s impact on patients’ quality of life [22]. A recent scoping review published in 2022 found that the existing evidence supports the use of PROMs for the assessment of patient experience in HCC but highlights the lack of their systematic evaluation [23]. Developing an in-depth understanding of patient experiences in advanced and/or metastatic HCC allows clinicians to select the most appropriate PROMs for the effective evaluation of disease impacts, treatment effects and burden both during clinical trials and in their clinical practice [24]. Moreover, qualitative exploration of patient experience matches the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)’s Patient-Focused Drug Development (PFDD) guidance series (2024) that recommends incorporating the patient voice in drug development by using methods such as qualitative research and fit-for-purpose COAs in oncology trials [25]. Qualitative data can contribute towards addressing expectations set forward by the FDA’s PFDD guidance series as well as allow other regulatory stakeholders to contextualize the patient experience, payers to reach value-based decisions, and prescribers to make decisions in a clinical context.

There have been only a small number of qualitative studies examining patients’ experience with HCC from the patient perspective that include patients in the advanced stages of HCC or patients with HCC who are near the end of their life [26–31]; there are a few other qualitative studies that did not make a distinction between the different stages of HCC progression [32–35]. Conclusions based on a sample of patients across the HCC spectrum may be limited in their usefulness for clinicians as patient experiences and treatment suitability across different disease stages can differ. For this reason, the aim of our study was to explore the patient experience specifically in advanced and/or metastatic HCC. The study’s research objectives were two-fold: (1) to describe the important concepts, including self-reported signs, symptoms, and perceived impacts on health-related quality of life (HRQOL) relevant to advanced and/or metastatic HCC, from the patients’ perspective and (2) to develop a patient-centric conceptual model of advanced and/or metastatic HCC.

Methods

This was a multi-phase non-interventional study that included a targeted literature review (TLR), interviews with clinicians, and interviews with patients. A conceptual model was developed based on the results of the TLR and the results of patient interviews, which had elicited qualitative data on patient-perceived HCC signs and symptoms, and their impacts on patients’ HRQOL. No specific HRQOL instrument was used as part of this study.

Targeted literature review

A TLR of qualitative research studies was performed to identify relevant concepts (symptoms and impacts) that described patients’ experience of advanced and/or metastatic HCC, using Medline and PsycINFO databases.

The search criteria of the TLR included disease terms (e.g., carcinoma) and qualitative study terms (e.g., qualitative research, concept elicitation, etc.). The searches were restricted to English language publications and articles published between 1 January 2001, and 29 December 2021. As preliminary search for advanced and/or metastatic HCC yielded only a few results the search strategy was broadened to include all stages of HCC. Based on the data extracted from the TLR, a draft conceptual model was constructed that represented HCC signs and symptoms and the impact of HCC signs and symptoms on HRQOL.

The results of the TLR also informed the development of the semi-structured clinician and patient interview guides, including topics for discussion, their structure, and probes about the patient experience. The TLR focused on qualitative research that identified relevant themes and concepts that described patients’ experience of HCC. These themes were then used to structure the topics of discussion in the clinician and patient interviews: diagnosis process, initial symptoms, disease progression, signs and symptoms, disease burden and impacts, disease support and coping (patient interviews only), treatment experience and expectations, and impacts of treatment. While clinicians and patients would first answer open-ended questions, the concepts identified in the TLR were then used to ask specific questions or probes to ensure that all important and relevant topics were discussed. For example, if patients did not discuss specific signs, symptoms, or impacts identified in the TLR, the interviewer would then specifically ask about these concepts to determine if they were relevant to the patient (e.g., “Have you ever experienced dizziness?” or “How has HCC has affected you emotionally?”).

Clinician interviews

Interviews with clinicians were performed in order to gain additional insights into relevant themes and concepts (signs, symptoms, impacts, treatment outcomes) initially identified in the TLR. The clinician interviews were considered as an informal research study, meant to provide supplemental data that were used to inform the conceptual model as well as the development of the patient interview guide, by ensuring that all relevant topics and concepts were covered. Because the clinician interviews were considered as an informal research study, no formal sample size calculation was performed and saturation was not assessed.

Five interviews were conducted with clinicians based in the United States (US) who had experience treating patients diagnosed with advanced and/or metastatic HCC. All clinicians had been practicing for at least 5 years and treated ≥50 patients with HCC per year. The semi-structured interview guide for the clinician interviews was developed based on the study objectives and was informed by the TLR results.

A market research recruitment agency first identified eligible US-based clinicians through its networks and invited them to participate in an interview with the ICON study team to share their insights about advanced and/or metastatic HCC patients. Clinicians were from different medical centers across the US. The ICON study team set up Microsoft Teams meetings and shared the meeting links with the recruiter who then forwarded them to each of the clinicians.

Clinicians provided their verbal consent to participate in the interviews and to be audio-recorded. Insights that emerged from the clinician interviews informed the development of the patient interview guide. A central Independent Review Board (IRB) in the US, Salus IRB, on 30 August 2025, provided a retrospective exception confirming that ethics approval was not required for the clinician interviews.

The interviews were conducted by an experienced qualitative researcher, who was a member of the ICON study team. The interviews took place between 20 July 2022, and 23 August 2022, and had an average length of 60 minutes. All interviews were audio-recoded, transcribed verbatim, anonymized, and analyzed using Excel.

Patient interviews

Qualitative concept elicitation (CE) interviews were conducted with adults diagnosed with advanced and/or metastatic HCC residing in the US. Interviews were comprised of open-ended questions that aimed to explore the patient disease and treatment experience, with a particular focus on capturing signs, symptoms, and HRQOL impacts of relevance and importance to patients. The data generated from these interviews were used to revise and finalize the draft conceptual model of patients’ experiences of advanced and/or metastatic HCC developed based on the TLR results.

Ethics committee approval

Prior to patient recruitment and interviews, ethical approval was obtained from a central Independent Review Board (IRB) in the US, Salus IRB, on 15 March 2023. Patients provided their written informed consent. The IRB reference for this study is 0085-0712/A-2023.04.14.

Patient interview study sample

Patients (aged ≥18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of advanced and/or metastatic HCC were recruited in the US with the assistance of a patient recruitment agency that used IRB-approved recruitment material. Given the qualitative nature of the patient interviews, there was no formal sample size calculation. Qualitative studies typically achieve saturation at 9–17 interviews and for this study, a target sample size of 15 patients was set in order to reach data saturation [36].

All patients were screened for eligibility using a study-specific screening questionnaire and were required to further confirm their eligibility through the submission of a confirmation of diagnosis. The confirmation of diagnosis was confirmed by the provision of a physician-completed confirmation of diagnosis form, or other appropriate form of confirmation, such as a copy of medical records, healthcare portal screenshots, or a letter from a physician prior to being fully enrolled in the study.

The recruitment agency collected patients’ confirmation of diagnosis and their informed consent forms.

The confirmation of diagnosis included the following: (1) date of the initial diagnosis with HCC, (2) date of diagnosis with advanced and/or metastatic HCC, (3) whether the patient had brain or leptomeningeal metastases, (4) whether the patient had a second malignancy either progressing or requiring active treatment within the last year, and (5) the patient’s current prescribed treatment regimen for advanced and/or metastatic HCC.

Any patients with known active brain metastases or leptomeningeal metastases or known second malignancies either progressing or requiring active treatment within the last year were excluded. Information about the stage of patients’ HCC, its etiology, or patients’ prior cancer treatments was not captured. No additional details about previous clinical tests were collected, such as BCLC or Child Pugh class.

Recruitment efforts were directed toward having a racially and ethnically diverse sample reflective of the demographics of the advanced and/or metastatic HCC patient population in the US. Patients were remunerated for providing the confirmation of diagnosis and for participating in the interview.

Data collection procedures

The recruitment agency was contracted by study team to complete patient identification and screening. The recruitment agency used IRB-approved recruitment messages to advertise the study through their patient database, email blasts, social media, and patient associations.

The recruitment agency collected the screening data, confirmation of diagnosis (COD) form, and informed consent form materials. All data was password-protected and shared securely with the study team. The study team reviewed the materials for acceptability.

Once deemed eligible for enrollment, patients were contacted by the recruitment agency to schedule interviews according to patients’ and interviewers’ availability. Upon scheduling the interview, the study team sent a Microsoft Teams link to the recruitment agency, who then forwarded the link to the participant.

Interviews were conducted from 21 June 2023, to 24 July 2023, by an experienced qualitative interviewer, a member of the study team, trained in the study background and materials. All patient personal data used to organize the interviews was password-protected and shared securely via the online file-sharing platform BOX®. Interviews took place via a videoconference platform and lasted approximately 60 minutes. Interviews were audio-recorded with patients’ permission. Verbatim transcription of the audio files was performed by a transcription company, transcripts were verified for accuracy by the interviewer, and transcripts were fully anonymized prior to analysis.

Data analysis

All interviews were analyzed using MAXQDA v2022 qualitative software [37]. Analyses were completed by experienced qualitative researchers. Data were analyzed using an iterative, thematic analysis approach compatible with FDA’s guidance capturing patient voice as an integral part of the drug development process [25,38].

Following the development of a qualitative analysis plan, a coding framework based on the interview guide was developed in MAXQDA to organize and structure the analysis. The lead analyst trained all the analysts and reviewed their first transcripts analyzed to confirm that they were following the same approach. The lead analyst merged all the analyzed transcripts and performed a quality check. The lead analyst also cleaned the codebook, merging codes or creating new codes as needed.

Conceptual saturation

An assessment of conceptual saturation, defined as “the point when no new relevant or important information emerges and collecting additional data will not add to the understanding of how patients perceive the concept of interest” was conducted at the total sample level [39]. Saturation was assessed using a codebook approach and saturation tables [40] and focused on all spontaneously elicited concepts related to signs and symptoms of advanced and/or metastatic HCC.

Results

Targeted literature review

Following an initial search, 172 references were identified for possible inclusion. After review, 10 references (nine full text articles and one dissertation paper) were selected which covered the disease experience of HCC from patients’, the caregivers’, and/or clinicians’ viewpoint [27,28,31–35,41–43] The full screening process is detailed in Figure 1 below.

PRISMA Diagram of TLR.

Seven articles covered the disease experience from the patient perspective, two from the caregiver perspective, and one from a health care provider (HCP) perspective. Four studies specifically examined advanced or terminal HCC. The studies were published between 2007 and 2021 and included patient samples from the US (n = 6), Taiwan (n = 2), Italy (n = 1), and China (n = 1). Eight studies used a semi-structured interview guide, one study used focus groups, and one study used a structured questionnaire which included some open-ended questions that patients could answer with free-text replies.

Concepts describing symptoms and impacts of advanced and/or metastatic HCC and its treatment were organized into a draft conceptual model that represented: (a) advanced and/or metastatic HCC signs and symptoms and (b) the impact of advanced and/or metastatic HCC signs and symptoms on HRQOL.

The 10 selected qualitative studies demonstrated that HCC is a multifaceted burden on the lives of patients diagnosed. Areas affected by HCC and its treatment include physical, psychological, family and social functioning, work, school, finances, and activities of daily living (ADL). The main symptoms include reduction in appetite/feeling full, weight loss, fatigue/lack of energy, loss of strength, diarrhea, nausea/queasiness, abdominal pain, difficulty concentrating, dizziness/vertigo, dry mouth, shortness of breath, bloating, vomiting, and physical changes to appearance.

Clinician interviews

Of the five clinicians, one specialized in medical oncology, one specialized in hematology, one specialized in internal medicine and specifically in gastroenterology, and two specialized in onco-hematology. Across clinicians, the median of practicing years’ was 25 (minimum: 12, maximum: 30). The median number of advanced and/or metastatic HCC patients per month was 10 (minimum: 7, maximum: 30) and 60 per year (minimum: 25, maximum: 100).

Clinicians said that common signs and symptoms presented by patients were cachexia (weakness), fatigue, pain/abdominal pain, reduction in appetite/malnutrition, nausea, encephalopathy, cirrhosis, ascites/abdominal distention, and gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding/varices/“throwing up blood.” According to clinicians, patients with early stage, locally advanced HCC and patients with advanced and/or metastatic HCC typically had comparable symptoms. However, as the illness progressed from early to advanced stage, symptoms usually worsened in severity. Common metastasis locations included the lymph nodes, the lungs, bones, and the peritoneum.

In terms of impacts, clinicians reported that advanced and/or metastatic HCC impacted all areas of patients’ lives, which resulted in patients not being able to do ADLs and not being able to function without assistance. These impacts included: inability to care for oneself, inability to work, inability to take part in social functions and personal relationships, loss of mobility, and weight loss. Clinicians said that advanced and/or metastatic HCC patients experienced depression and helplessness due to their treatment only providing short-term improvements in their HRQOL. Results from the clinician interviews informed the development of the patient interview guide.

Patient interviews

Sample characteristics

A total of 15 patients with advanced and/or metastatic HCC were interviewed. According to the confirmation of diagnosis form completed by physicians, 13 out of 15 patients were initially diagnosed with advanced and/or metastatic (de novo metastatic) HCC and two out of 15 patients were diagnosed with advanced and/or metastatic HCC at a later date. As far as patients’ HCC treatment is concerned, 9 were on Bevacizumab, 7 were on Atezolizumab, 2 were on Sorafenib at the time the confirmation of diagnosis form was completed (patients could be on multiple treatments at once). Six patients were not on any treatment at this time.

In our sample, eight patients were female and seven were retired. Additionally, 14/15 patients were married and had private health insurance. The sample was racially and ethnically diverse. The average age at the time of the interview was 60.1 years old and the average age at diagnosis was 59.4 years old.

Symptoms

Patients reported 33 symptoms across several body systems. Overall, the five most common symptoms, reported by 10 patients or more, were fatigue, weight loss, weakness, reduction in appetite, and sleep disturbance.

The most common spontaneously reported symptoms were fatigue, weakness, nausea/queasiness, reduction in appetite, stomach/abdominal pain, and pain/ache or swelling in different parts of the body.

If patients did not spontaneously report a symptom during an interview, they were explicitly asked (i.e., probed) about whether they experienced it. The most commonly reported probed symptoms included weight loss, difficulty concentrating, dry mouth and sleep disturbance.

Key gastrointestinal symptoms

Twelve patients reported having weight loss, of which seven reported it when probed about it and five reported it spontaneously. Patient PT-005 stated, “I lost I would say 40 pounds over […] the time from diagnosis to now.”

Ten patients reported having a reduction in appetite, of which seven patients spontaneously reported it and three reported it when probed. When describing his reduction in appetite, patient PT-003 stated that he “kept having trouble eating” and considered it to be “weird because I’m one of those big guys who likes to eat, so that—that was a little bit of a red flag.” Another patient, PT-007 said that her reduction in appetite resembled boredom with eating and food, noting that “I feel like I’m eating and I’m eating to pass the time, like I’m bored. When I sit down with some food it’s an instantaneous bored-ness that comes over me because I don’t want it.”

In terms of management strategies, two patients used food-related strategies (consuming calorie-rich drinks and eating specific foods) and two patients reported taking medication. Reduction in appetite impacted one patient to a point that they were drinking liquids more than eating solid foods and another patient had to force themselves to eat. Patient PT-013 commented, “I’m not wanting to go out,” with regards to the social impact of having a reduced appetite.

Eight patients reported having nausea/queasiness; all eight patients reported it spontaneously. PT-014 described nausea in the following way: “Discomfort in eating, but not necessarily vomiting.” Another patient PT-013 described nausea as being akin to seasickness explaining that “I couldn’t hold down a 7-UP [a soda drink] and things just made me extremely sick, [it was] like motion sickness. I had [it] real bad and it’s like you got on a boat and you immediately tasted the vomit in your mouth […] I couldn’t tolerate my own stomach.” To manage nausea, patients tried drinking soft drinks, taking medication, lying down, eating slowly, not eating at all even when hungry, and drinking herbal tea. The most common impact of nausea/queasiness was not eating and drinking altogether, followed by avoiding particular foods. Patient PT-003 for example said: “I want a certain thing, but I know I can’t go eat it because it’s not worth the risk [of nausea].”

Seven patients reported having dry mouth, of which five reported it when probed and two reported it spontaneously. PT-002, when describing having dry mouth, said he was not in pain due to it even if he experienced it “about every day.” PT-001 similarly described dry mouth as not an especially bothersome symptom: he had “some dryness here and there in the mouth […] then [he] would drink water and it would be fine.” Management strategies patients used focused on sipping water to avoid their mouth feeling dry.

Six patients reported having bloating, of which four mentioned it when probed and two reported it spontaneously. Patient PT-003 for example said: “I would get very uncomfortable and bloated just after a few bites of certain things […]. Especially if it’s […] kind of heavy or maybe an acidic sauce.” To manage bloating symptoms, patients avoided certain foods and took stomach medications, including medications received during urgent care visits due to the severity of their bloating.

Five patients reported dehydration, of which three mentioned when it probed and two spontaneously. One patient attributed dehydration to her management strategy of drinking less fluids to control nausea. Dehydration was often noticed by patients experiencing dry mouth or dry skin.

Five patients reported having gas/flatulence, of which four mentioned it when probed and one spontaneously. Patient PT-007 explained that his gas/flatulence worsened when he ate: “I don’t eat that much when I do try to eat […] I do have gas.” Patient PT-003 stated that he experienced gas/flatulence periodically; after changing his diet to eat lighter, he noticed a decrease in his gas/flatulence.

Five patients reported having vomiting; all five patients mentioned it spontaneously. Patient PT-012 sought medical attention for an initial diagnosis of HCC after enduring vomiting for an estimated duration of two weeks; she would later go on to liken her vomiting to “puking” during an episode of food poisoning. One patient said that the amount of vomiting she experienced made her feel dizzy and another that his vomiting worsened after eating because he had “trouble keeping [his] food down.”

Five patients reported having abdominal distention or swelling, of which four patients mentioned when probed and one reported it spontaneously. Patient PT-009 reported having abdominal pain along with bloating that was sensitive to the touch. Though patient PT-008 experienced abdominal distention or swelling she noted that she was not “gassy.”

Key symptoms related to pain/swelling

Eight patients reported stomach pain/abdominal pain, of which six patients reported it spontaneously and two patients were probed about it. Patients provided various descriptions of this symptom. One patient PT-001 described stomach pain/abdominal pain as “like a cramp” and patient PT-012 said that it was “sometimes, just not bloating pain, it’s not gas, it’s just like…it feels like a pressure pain.”

A number of patients discussed their symptom management strategies for stomach pain/abdominal pain. Five patients took medications, such as pain medications and gastrointestinal medications, one patient waited for the pain to abet on its own, one rested/laid down, and one used a heating pad. Last, two patients said food made their pain worse with one referring to overeating and the other to how she would feel generally after eating.

Seven patients reported headaches/migraines of which four patients reported the symptom spontaneously and three patients when probed about it. Patient PT-012 described her headaches in this way: “It’s more—sort of a pressure headache that just makes my head feel like it’s being crunched.” Three patients used medications/supplements as a management strategy for their headaches. One patient reported on the impact of headaches/migraines on his physical functioning, explaining that at his worst, he was bedbound.

Seven patients reported dry skin, of which five patients were probed about it and two patients reported it spontaneously. Patient PT-007 specified that this dry skin was also red and flaked off when he rubbed or scratched it, which had happened prior to his diagnosis. Patients linked dry skin to dehydration and conversely staying hydrated to having less dry skin.

Five patients reported jaundice in their skin, eyes or nails. All patients reported it spontaneously. PT-001 described jaundice as: “The whites of my eyes […] had like a yellow tint on it […] that had never been there before” and PT-007 as “I started having a yellow haze, a yellow gloss in my eyes.” One patient reported the social impact of others noticing jaundice in his eyes.

Fatigue and weakness

Fourteen patients reported feeling fatigue of which 13 reported it spontaneously and one when probed about it. Patients provided various descriptions of this symptom. Patient PT-003 described feeling fatigue as: “Very random. I could go […] a couple of weeks without having it be that bad and then all of a sudden […] I just can have a bad day, or I could go part of the day or part of a day not feeling too bad, it just hits me.” (PT-003). Another patient PT-007 described their persistent exhaustion explaining that: “After sleeping, […] and waking up, [I] still felt very, very drained.” Four patients said they made lifestyle changes to cope with fatigue that included sleeping more, taking naps during the day, making adjustments to cope better, drinking caffeinated beverages, and avoiding over-exertion. Three patients discussed the impacts from fatigue which included social impacts (not wanting to go out), irritability, and changes in physical functioning (needing a place to rest, not taking long walks alone, and being too tired to eat and cook meals).

Ten patients reported feeling weak, of which nine reported it spontaneously and one when probed about it. Patients provided various descriptions of this symptom. Patient PT-002 described feeling weak as: “It made me not want to do anything that […] I enjoyed, like […] playing with the grandkids, playing with the dog […] or even go to the mailbox often.”

One patient PT-010 associated feeling weak with not being able to eat enough, explaining that: “Because when you can’t eat enough you get real weak in general just from that.”

Key cognitive symptoms

Six patients reported that they had difficulty concentrating; they all reported it when they were probed about it. PT-007 said about her trouble concentrating that “It’s hard for me to read. I’m an avid reader or I was [an avid reader in the past]. I don’t read as much now […] concentrating while I’m reading and understanding. I’ve noticed I would have to re-read.”

One patient said that she had difficulty concentrating because she was fearful and sad about her illness. One patient reported that fatigue and pain, and specifically body aches, made the symptom worse.

Key symptoms related to sleep

Ten patients talked about sleep disturbance, five patients mentioned difficulty to stay asleep/having interrupted sleep, three patients reported inconsistent sleep (without specifying the specific sleep issue), and one patient each oversleeping, insomnia, and trouble falling asleep. Of the five patients who reported having difficulty staying asleep or experiencing interrupted sleep, three patients reported it spontaneously and two patients report it when they were probed about it. Patient PT-007 described interrupted sleep as: “It’s interrupted at night […] either from me having to go use the bathroom or if I change positions, I wake up and find out that I’m in pain or just don’t feel well.” Another patient PT-011 referred to experiencing infrequent sleep explaining that: “My sleep is infrequent. I wake up […] during the night, so […] it’s a big problem.”

Patients said that muscle cramps/stiffness, headaches, and racing thoughts worsened their sleep. One patient said that she had learned meditation breathing techniques and that she took cannabidiol (CBD) capsules to improve her sleep. One patient said that an impact of having difficulty to sleep was that he was “not his normal self” (PT-011) during the rest of the day.

Conceptual saturation

Saturation was assessed for all spontaneously elicited symptoms and signs of advanced and/or metastatic HCC. Saturation was reached at interview 13 out of 15. At this point, all spontaneously reported signs/symptoms that added to the understanding of the patient experience had been reported by patients.

Experience of symptom changes and perceived side effects while on treatment

Patients were asked about changes in symptoms they experienced while being on treatment for advanced and/or metastatic HCC. The results showed perceived changes in HCC-related symptoms as well as perceived side effects associated with current or past treatment. Most patients (n = 13) perceived several symptoms getting worse as result of the side effects of the treatment. For example, some patients (n = 6) experienced fatigue prior to starting treatment and considered that their fatigue worsened due to treatment side effects. Most patients (n = 13) also reported experiencing at least one physiological symptom that they did not associate with advanced and/or metastatic HCC. For example, three patients did not identify their diarrhea as an HCC-related symptom but rather as a side effect of their treatment.

Impacts on health-related quality of life

Patients were asked about the impacts of advanced and/or metastatic HCC on their HRQOL. Patients reported 39 impacts. A complete list of all impacts is provided as an appendix (Supplemental Material: Appendix B) Impacts that five or more patients reported are detailed below.

Twelve patients said they had difficulties taking care of themselves daily. Of these 12, 10 patients explained that their close family were their caregivers for a few days after they had underwent chemotherapy sessions. For example, patient PT-004 said: “[My] husband had to step in and so did the kids because I’d just buzz them even to go to the restroom, like I’d fall down […] I’d hold on tight to just the rails and I had to put the rails in the bathroom and get in the shower and I have […] a big bench in the shower […] tiled in.”

Twelve patients reported finding physical activities difficult to perform and 10 patients said they had difficulties exercising or performing strenuous activities, like going to the gym, doing gardening/yardwork, and swimming: “Before the diagnosis I was pretty active. I would go on a lot of walks. I’d go walking a lot […] I would do some gardening. I really can’t do that anymore.” (PT-001)

All patients (n = 15) referred to the impacts of advanced and/or metastatic HCC on their social life. Overall, 11 patients reported that they experienced changes in particular activities and social groups such as no longer being able to go out to restaurants and other social events: “There’s three of us and we went to the concerts, we went to the casinos […] we would go out to when it was lunch on the weekends or dinner during the week every now and then and have such a great time and I haven’t been able to do that.” (PT-007)

Eight patients explained that they socialized more often at home than outside. Seven patients said that they avoided or engaged less in socializing because they did not have enough energy. For example, PT-013 stated that: “I used to do all kinds of stuff that required energy outside the house. I’m not leaving the house. I don’t want to leave the house.”

Several patients talked about the impacts on their relationships with their family. Seven patients said that their family was caring and supportive toward them. That said, patients also explained that their family could be sad/depressed or worried/upset. As PT-014 said: “They’re pretty depressed about it. They see me completely devolve into a different person, so it’s terrible for them, too.”

All patients (n = 15) talked about emotional impacts from advanced and/or metastatic HCC. Seven patients said that they felt anxious, scared or uncertain in relation to the cancer’s progression and/or their family’s future. For example, PT-006 stated: “I’m worried about my husband, and I’m worried about my kids, and I’m worried about how are they going to be? Are they going to be able to manage this? Are they going to be able to manage anything?” Six patients reported that the diagnosis of advanced and/or metastatic HCC was a life-changing event for them.

Nine patients reported that advanced and/or metastatic HCC did not impact their professional lives; however, most of these nine patients (n = 7) had retired or stopped working before their diagnosis. Three patients reported that advanced and/or metastatic HCC did impact their professional live though; these patients either retired early due or had to go on sick leave. Nine patients reported that they did not experience financial impacts due to their advanced and/or metastatic HCC; five patients explained that this was because they had sufficient insurance coverage. Four patients said they were financially impacted by their cancer, however.

Discussion

This multi-phase study explored patient-reported disease-relevant symptoms and impacts in advanced and/or metastatic HCC and informed a patient-centered conceptual model of advanced and/or metastatic HCC.

Overall, there was a significant convergence between the results from the TLR and the patient interviews. The four symptoms reported by 10 or more patients (sleep disturbance, weight loss, reduction in appetite, fatigue, and weakness) align with previous qualitative research studies that have found these symptoms to be the most prevalent symptoms among HCC patients, including patients in the advanced stage of HCC [27,44,45]. Furthermore, our research confirmed HRQOL impacts that have been identified in previous qualitative studies such as impacts on social life, psychological/emotional functioning, and functional limitations [27,44,45]. For example, the emotions of anxiety, fear, and depressive symptoms (e.g., sadness, feelings of hopelessness) that interviewed patients frequently reported are a common part of the patient experience, as both our TLR results and the results of another review published in 2022, after our TLR was conducted, attest [45].

Although in general the results from the TLR and the patient interviews overlapped, there were a few differences between the concepts covered by the TLR and those that emerged in the patient interviews. Stigma, feelings of inferiority/shame, and spending more time in bed were concepts identified in the TLR [28,34,46] that were not reported in the patient interviews.

The underreporting of patient symptoms by clinicians is a common and well-documented phenomenon that has led to the integration of PROMs as endpoints into clinical trials to capture the patient voice. That said, patients who complete these PROMs are not always able to distinguish what is a symptom of their disease from what is a side effect of their treatment. In our study, several patients reported symptoms that they described as side effects of their treatment and not symptoms of their advanced and/or metastatic HCC. These side effects were either a worsening of preexisting symptoms (e.g., fatigue that pre-dated treatment which patients believed worsened due to their treatment while they were receiving it) or an appearance of new symptoms (e.g., diarrhea which, although it could be a symptom of advanced and/or metastatic HCC, the patient believed was a side effect of treatment). Examining patients’ perceptions of treatment side-effects can allow the development of more nuanced patient experience frameworks as the majority of HRQOL models focus more on patients’ symptoms experience and less on other factors that may impact their treatment journey (e.g., interventions’ outcomes, sociocultural characteristics) [47,48]. Such insights can also benefit the development of personalized treatment plans considering the prognostic value of adverse effects and their association with lower levels of pharmaceutical regimen adherence [18–20].

Patient self-reported attributions of causality, though, need to be analyzed with caution. Healthcare providers who use PROMs, as well as clinical trials that include them in their endpoints, need to carefully consider both the disease and treatment effects when evaluating how patients complete a PROM. Clear discussion with patients about potential treatment side effects and on-going monitoring of their condition can help patients to accurately attribute symptoms to their correct source. Future studies focused specifically on causality of symptoms would be needed to explore the exact nature of the causal link(s) between advanced and/or metastatic HCC, its treatment, and its symptoms.

Strengths and limitations

This study had three main strengths. First, it was comprised of stages that built upon each other. The TLR results informed the development of the clinician interview guide, and the clinician interview results informed the development of the patient interview guide. Such an approach allowed the study team to identify gaps that could be explored in the next phase of the study. As compared to a systematic literature review, a TLR does not require the inclusion of a separate quality assessment of the included studies; and compared to a scoping review, a TLR is characterized by a clear narrow scope aimed to inform decision-making rather than map evidence across a broad research area [49]. According to best practices, a TLR is conducted to identify what is important to patients regarding their experiences with their condition, prior to qualitative research in patients [25,39,50,51]. In combination with qualitative research, TLRs can offer in-depth understanding of patient experience, which can subsequently inform the development of new PROMs [39,52].

Second, the interviewed patients were racially and ethnically diverse and represented the demographic composition of the advanced and/or metastatic HCC patient population in the US. Third, although all the patient interviews were conducted in the US, the concepts that arose from the patient interviews largely overlapped with those from targeted literature review, which included manuscripts about patients from other countries. As such, the model may be considered applicable to the experience of patients in other countries.

There are also limitations to this study that need to be acknowledged. The first limitation is related to the number of qualitative studies on the advanced and/or metastatic HCC patient experience available in the literature. Due to the very small number of qualitative studies focused on this stage of HCC, the TLR search strategy had to expanded to include qualitative studies covering all stages of HCC. Therefore, the TLR results do not distinguish between different stages of HCC. Second, no further details were collected about the clinical background of interviewed patients, such as specifics about their diagnosis and their past treatments. As such, the complete disease and treatment journey of individual patients in our study is not available.

Conclusion

This study is the first qualitative study focused specifically on the patient experience in advanced and/or metastatic HCC. It incorporates information from several data sources. Results showed that disease-relevant symptoms and impacts were similar across different data sources (available literature, clinician interviews, and patient interviews). The five most common symptoms reported by patients were fatigue, weight loss, weakness, reduction in appetite, and sleep disturbance. Impacts patients reported extended to all aspects of patients’ lives, including physical functioning, social and family functioning, emotional functioning, physical activities, and activities of daily living. Advanced and/or metastatic HCC symptoms may resemble treatment side effects, which may make it difficult establish the cause of patient experience. As a result, clinicians and patients need to work together to identify causality of symptoms and appropriate relief measures.

Qualitative research such as this study identifies patient-centric symptoms and impacts in advanced and/or metastatic HCC that are key to selecting fit-for-purpose PROMs and developing COA endpoint strategies. These measures can then be used in either a clinical trial context or in the clinical setting.
