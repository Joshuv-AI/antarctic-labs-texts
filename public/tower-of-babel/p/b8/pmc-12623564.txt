Impact of Brief Mindfulness Intervention on Emotions, Sleep and Quality of Life in Patients with Newly Diagnosed Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: A Prospective Study
Authors: Xin Huang, Guanghao Hou, Hui Li, Yuchen Zhu, Tielun Yin, Na Zhang, Hong Liu, Yanjing Yang, Linna Bai, Wenxia Ban, Jiaying Zhang, Ruixue Zhang, Dongsheng Fan, Shan Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a devastating progressive neurodegenerative disorder causing significant suffering and mental health problems for patients. Many patients with ALS have a severe psychological reaction to the serious diagnosis when they are first diagnosed. This study aims to investigate the impact of a brief mindfulness intervention on emotions, sleep, and quality of life (QOL) in patients with ALS who have recently been diagnosed.MethodsThis prospective intervention study enrolled patients who had been newly diagnosed with ALS. Participants received cognitive training and audio-led mindfulness exercises involving breathing awareness and body scan sessions for 10 days. The effectiveness of the intervention was evaluated by several scales.ResultsNinety-one patients (aged 54.15 [10.10] years, 52 male/39 female [57.10%/42.90%]) were finally enrolled in the analysis. The Chinese Version Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS) (t = 2.05, P = 0.04) and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) (Z = −2.03, P = 0.04) scores significantly decreased, and the 40-item Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Assessment Questionnaire (ALSAQ40) significantly increased (Z = −2.93, P < 0.01) following the brief mindfulness intervention. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) (Z = −2.97, P < 0.01) and the 17-Item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-17) (Z = −3.43, P < 0.01) were significantly reduced in patients with ALS diagnosed with depression. QOL was significantly improved in other patients with ALS (Z = −2.51, P = 0.01).ConclusionOur study indicated that a brief mindfulness intervention could be a potential way to reduce depression and improve daytime sleepiness, while also improving the QOL for newly diagnosed patients with ALS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-025-00816-9.Keywords: Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Mindfulness, Emotions, Quality of life, Sleep

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? •Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a highly disabling and fatal disease with no effective cure to date.•Many patients experience psychological distress and feelings of despair upon being newly diagnosed with ALS, and mindfulness-based interventions have been shown to improve quality of life (QOL) and alleviate symptoms of anxiety and depression.•We designed a brief mindfulness program lasting 10 days to help these patients get through the desperate period. What was learned from the study? •This study demonstrated that a brief mindfulness intervention might help mitigate emotional issues, alleviate daytime sleepiness, and improve QOL for patients newly diagnosed with ALS.•Brief mindfulness interventions are likely to be promising as a component of the integrated treatment regimen for newly diagnosed ALS.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a devastating progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the loss of both upper and lower motor neurons [1]. The disease’s rapid progression results in severe disability, but there is no effective cure to date [2]. These factors contribute to profound suffering and significant mental health problems among patients with ALS [3]. Evidence has shown that the prevalence of neuropsychiatric disorders in patients with ALS is significantly higher than that in the general population [4, 5]. Among patients with ALS, 25–41% experience neuropsychiatric disorders, including anxiety, depression, and stress-related conditions [4, 6, 7].

The presence of neuropsychiatric disorders markedly degrades the quality of life (QOL) for patients with ALS [3, 6]. Considering the incurable nature of ALS, improving QOL—encompassing physical, psychological, and social dimensions—is of paramount importance [8, 9]. Recently, several randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that psychological interventions can effectively improve and sustain QOL in individuals with motor neuron disease [10], including ALS [11]. Mindfulness plays a significant role in ALS, being associated not only with longer survival but also with improved QOL and reduced levels of anxiety and depression [12]. Emerging evidence suggests that several mindfulness-based interventions have been specifically developed for individuals with ALS, and these interventions can effectively alleviate depression, anxiety, and mood-related symptoms and enhance QOL [11, 13–21]. In the context of ALS, studies have shown that patients who underwent an 8-week meditation program reported reduced levels of anxiety and depression, alongside improvements in QOL [22, 23]. However, long-term programs are usually associated with participant attrition. Brief mindfulness programs have been developed and also verified to be effective in reducing anxiety and improving positive affect and self-care [24–29].

In clinical practice, it has been observed that many patients with ALS have a severe psychological reaction toward the serious diagnosis when they are first diagnosed [30]. Receiving a serious diagnosis of ALS is a psychologically overwhelming experience, leading to insomnia and a variety of emotions like depression, anxiety, fear, and stress. To help them get through, we designed a brief mindfulness intervention program specifically for individuals who have recently been diagnosed with ALS. This study aims to investigate the impact of a brief mindfulness intervention on emotions, sleep, and QOL in patients with ALS who have recently been diagnosed.

Methods

Participants

This prospective intervention study enrolled patients who had newly been diagnosed with ALS at Peking University Third Hospital (PUTH) in Beijing, China. This study was conducted between February 2023 and July 2023.

The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) diagnosed with clinically definite ALS, clinically probable ALS, clinically probable laboratory-supported ALS, or clinically possible ALS according to the revised El Escorial criteria, independently by at least two experts; (2) age between 30 and 70 years; and (3) informed consent provided by the patient or legal representative.

The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) the presence of comorbid neurological disorders, including but not limited to stroke, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and frontotemporal dementia; (2) a history of head trauma; (3) inability to complete mindfulness intervention due to physical or cognitive limitations; (4) concurrent participation in another clinical intervention study; (5) illiteracy.

Mindfulness Procedures

All mindfulness interventions in this study were administered by a licensed professional psychiatrist who holds a nationally approved psychology counselor certificate. All participants were hospitalized at Peking University Third Hospital Airport Yard for the duration of the whole program.

Trained professionals provided patients with psychoeducation to understand the concepts, principles, and procedures of the mindfulness intervention. Eligible participants then officially attended a group mindfulness program lasting 10 days [27, 29].

Participants initially received daily cognitive training. Individuals were encouraged to adopt an attitude of acceptance toward life and illness. They were encouraged to observe and accept bodily sensations without judgment, including pain and weakness. Similarly, they were prompted to acknowledge and accept inner thoughts and emerging emotions like depression, despair, hope, and earnestness. The training tried to help individuals become aware of the remaining functional capacities and to shift patients’ focus from limitations imposed by their disease (e.g., “what I can’t do because of ALS”) to a more constructive awareness of their current experiences (e.g., “how I feel”, “what I can do now,” and “what the family and I want to do in the future”). By fostering this mindset, the intervention sought to help participants recognize and accept negative emotions in a healthy and adaptive manner [31, 32].

Subsequently, participants engaged in a structured 10-day regimen of audio-guided mindfulness practice. The brief outline of the program structure is shown in the Supplementary Materials. For participants who were already taking psychotropic drugs, we ensured that their psychiatric medication regimens remained unchanged throughout the program.

Clinical Information Collection

The demographic and clinical information was collected, including age, sex, education level, diagnostic delay, age of onset, site of onset, and diagnostic level. The stage of ALS was assessed using the King’s College staging system (KCSS). The severity of ALS was assessed by the Revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R). The cognitive function of ALS was assessed using the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS). These evaluation tools are classical and time-tested methods used for assessing ALS. The degree of upper motor neuron (UMN) impairment was assessed using the Penn Upper Motor Neuron Score (PUMNS) [33].

The effectiveness of the intervention was evaluated in three areas—emotions, sleep, and QOL—by several psychological scales that have been validated for the Chinese population and are commonly used in clinical practice. Emotions were evaluated by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [34, 35], the 17-Item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-17), the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), and the Chinese Version Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS). Sleep was evaluated by the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). QOL was evaluated by the 40-item Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Assessment Questionnaire (ALSAQ40). The scales were assessed before and after the mindfulness intervention.

Statistical Analysis

According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria [36], patients were divided into two groups for analysis: patients with depression and patients without depression. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (proportions) and were compared using the χ2 test. Continuous variables were presented as mean values (standard deviation [SD]) for variables following normal distribution and median values [interquartile ranges (IQRs)] for other variables. Differences between continuous variables with normal distribution were compared by the Student t-test and the paired-samples t-test. Differences between other continuous variables were compared by the Mann–Whitney U test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The Bonferroni correction was used to adjust p-values for multiple comparisons. The multivariate analysis of categorical variables was performed using logistic regression analysis. We used SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analysis. All statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethical Approval

The Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital approved the conduct of this study. The approval number of the study in PUTH was M2022511. Written informed consent was obtained from all sub jects or their representatives before enrollment. The study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments.

Results

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 149 patients with newly diagnosed ALS were screened to meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria from February 2023 to July 2023. Among them, 26 patients refused to engage in the program. Ultimately, 123 patients were enrolled in the program. A total of 32 patients withdrew from the program midway. A total of 91 patients (aged 54.15 [10.10] years, 52 male/39 female [57.10%/42.90%]) were finally enrolled in the analysis. Among the 91 patients with ALS, 24 patients were diagnosed with depression according to the DSM-5 criteria, and 67 patients were not. Further analysis was conducted to evaluate the specific effects of mindfulness on patients with ALS with depression. Patients were categorized into those with and without depression based on DSM-5 criteria. There were no significant differences between the two groups in age (t = −1.03, P = 0.31), sex (χ2 = 1.70, P = 0.19), diagnosis level (χ2 = 1.68, P = 0.64), KCSS (χ2 = 1.27, P = 0.74), onset site(χ2 = 1.51, P = 0.68), age of onset (t = 0.99, P = 0.33), diagnostic delay (Z = −0.89, P = 0.37) and ALSFRS-R scores (Z = −1.52, P = 0.13). Compared to patients with ALS without depression, patients with ALS with depression had lower education levels (Z = −2.02, P = 0.04) and lower ECAS scores (Z = −2.05, P = 0.04) (Table 1). For patients with depression, significant reductions in both PHQ-9 (Z = −2.97, P < 0.01) and HAMD-17 (Z = −3.43, P < 0.01) scores were observed following the mindfulness intervention, even after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (P < 0.02) (Table 3). For patients without depression, improvement was noted in ALSAQ40 (Z = −2.51, P = 0.01) (Table 4), and the significance remained after Bonferroni correction (P < 0.02). Then, we directly compared the magnitude of change before and after the brief mindfulness intervention between patients with ALS with and without depression. We found that the reductions in PHQ-9 (Z = −4.17, P < 0.01), HAMD-17 (Z = −4.54, P < 0.01), and HAMA (Z = −2.48, P = 0.01) scores were significantly greater in patients with depression than in those without depression (Table 5).

To identify factors associated with better response to mindfulness, patients with ALS were divided into two groups according to the median or mean threshold of factors that showed significant changes before and after mindfulness. These factors included CPSS, ALSAQ40, and ESS. Clinical characteristics including age, sex, education level, diagnosis level, KCSS, onset site, age of onset, diagnostic delay, UMND scores, ALSFRS-R scores, and ECAS scores were compared between the groups (Supplementary Table 1–3). No factors were significantly associated with CPSS or ESS decline after the brief mindfulness intervention. Time of diagnostic delay was found to be associated with ALSAQ40 improvement (Z = −2.36, P = 0.02). Patients with a shorter diagnostic delay showed greater improvement in QOL after the brief mindfulness intervention (Supplementary Table 2).

There were 32 patients withdrawn from the study midway. 81.25% of these patients withdrew in three days. We compared the baseline characteristics of patients who completed the program and those who decided to withdraw (Supplementary Table 4). There was no difference in age (t = 1.34, P = 0.19), sex (χ2 = 2.16, P = 0.14), educational level (Z = −0.28, P = 0.78), KCSS (χ2 = 5.40, P = 0.15), bulbar involvement (χ2 = 0.01, P = 0.92), diagnostic delay (Z = −0.43, P = 0.67), ALSFRS-R (Z = −1.86, P = 0.06), PHQ-9 (Z = −1.71, P = 0.09), HAMD-17 (Z = −0.87, P = 0.38), HAMA (Z = −0.77, P = 0.44), CPSS (t = −0.69, P = 0.50), ALSAQ40 (Z = −1.82, P = 0.07), or ESS (Z = −0.07, P = 0.95) scores between the two groups. Compared to patients who completed the program, patients who withdrew had lower ECAS scores (Z = −3.02, P < 0.01) and higher PSQI scores (Z = −3.98, P < 0.01). The diagnostic level was also different between the two groups(χ2 = 8.78, P = 0.03).

The relationship between withdrawal and associated factors was analyzed using logistic regression (Supplementary Table 5). The multivariable analysis revealed that patients who withdrew midway had significantly lower ECAS scores (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–0.99, P < 0.01) and higher PSQI scores (OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.08–1.40, P < 0.01). They may have different diagnostic levels(OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.03–3.40, P = 0.04).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that a brief mindfulness intervention might help mitigate emotional issues, alleviate daytime sleepiness, and improve QOL for patients newly diagnosed with ALS. Notably, depression severity, as measured by PHQ-9 and HAMD-17, was significantly decreased after the mindfulness intervention in patients with ALS with depression. Additionally, other patients with ALS exhibited notable improvements in QOL following the intervention. Improvements in QOL were associated with the duration of diagnostic delay.

ALS is widely recognized as a fatal disease, with most patients experiencing a life expectancy of 2 to 5 years from symptom onset [37]. Currently, no curative treatment is available. Unfortunately, many patients with ALS experience disease onsets in middle age. A 10-year cohort study showed that the mean age at the onset in Chinese patients with sporadic ALS was 49.8 years [38]. These people are often integral members of their families and societies. The diagnosis of ALS is frequently accompanied by overwhelming pain, stress, and psychological morbidity, particularly for patients who have recently been diagnosed. Roos et al. reported that patients with ALS had a highly increased risk of depression within the first year after diagnosis [30]. Therefore, providing effective support during this challenging period is crucial.

Extensive evidence has demonstrated that mindfulness interventions can be powerful tools to reduce stress and burnout, improve the acceptance and coping abilities of patients with ALS, and lead to a more serene emotional state [13–15, 24]. A small-scale study indicated reductions in anxiety and improvements in relaxation following a standard 8-week meditation training [23]. Pagnini et al. reported that ALS-specific meditation training could improve the QOL and reduce anxiety and depression levels in 4 months [39]. It is generally safe for most patients and has very few side effects. However, the long-duration training program faces a challenge due to a high attrition rate. Brief mindfulness interventions are increasingly popular means to reduce negative affect and stress [24–27, 29]. However, the effectiveness of brief mindfulness still requires further investigation. Therefore, we specifically selected newly diagnosed patients who were experiencing a severe psychological reaction toward the serious diagnosis in our study. We assumed that the brief mindfulness intervention could help them accept the disease and get through the overwhelming period as quickly as possible.

In our study, we designed an ALS-specific mindfulness intervention program over 10 days for patients who had been recently diagnosed. Our findings indicated that this brief mindfulness intervention improved the QOL of patients with ALS and reduced their depression levels. Our results also suggested that the mindfulness intervention had varying effects on participants with and without depression. For participants with depression, the depression levels significantly decreased. We also found that patients with shorter diagnostic delays exhibited greater improvements in QOL. These patients are likely to progress more rapidly and thus may be more helpless and hopeless. We posit that the mindfulness intervention may help them rebuild hope for life and improve QOL.

Thirty-two individuals dropped out midway. Individuals with poorer cognitive function and lower sleep quality were more likely to drop out. Since these participants were potentially more responsive to the mindfulness intervention, their dropout may have led to an underestimation of the intervention’s effects. This limitation should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. We assume that a lack of knowledge and confidence in non-pharmaceutical therapy may be the reason. Individuals with better cognitive function were probably more likely to comprehend the concepts of mindfulness, thereby facilitating better engagement in the mindfulness program. Furthermore, the diagnostic levels of patients may differ between the two groups. The specific associations and mechanisms require further studies to clarify. To minimize drop-out in future studies, patients with cognitive impairment and those with limited understanding should be given more one-on-one time, allowing the research staff to provide detailed and explicit explanations and to establish a friendly and trusting relationship with them.

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, this study is a single-arm, non-randomized controlled trial. The study's design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions from the observed associations. Secondly, the dropout of 32 participants may have introduced bias and affected the overall findings of the study. Thirdly, this study was limited to a single-center setting and included only hospitalized patients between 30 and 70 years old, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader ALS populations, particularly young patients or outpatient settings. Moreover, only immediate post-intervention psychological scores were obtained, and long-term outcomes were not assessed in this study. Future studies are required to verify our findings.

Conclusions

Our study indicated that a brief mindfulness intervention could be a potential way to reduce negative emotions like depression and daytime sleepiness, while also improving the QOL for newly diagnosed patients with ALS. Brief mindfulness interventions are likely to be promising as a component of the integrated treatment regimen for newly diagnosed ALS.
