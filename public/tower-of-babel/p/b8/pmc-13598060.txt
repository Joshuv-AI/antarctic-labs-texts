Transcriptomic landscape of peripheral T cells following CAR-T cell therapy in diffuse large B cell lymphoma
Authors: Ismael de la Iglesia-San Sebastián, Sara Fernández de Córdoba-Oñate, Mariana Bastos-Oreiro, Raquel Fernández-González, Miguel López-Esteban, Rebeca Bailén, Ignacio Gómez-Centurión, Dolores Fuentes, Javier Anguita, Mi Kwon, Ramón García-Sanz, Ismael Buño, Carolina Martínez-Laperche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCAR-T cell therapy has improved outcomes in relapsed/refractory large B cell lymphoma (LBCL), yet responses remain variable; baseline endogenous T cell state and post-infusion CAR-T differentiation may contribute to this heterogeneity, but the underlying transcriptional programs are incompletely defined.MethodsWe performed RNA-sequencing of CD3⁺ T cells from 26 LBCL patients at two time points—pre-apheresis (PA; n = 23) and day 14 post-infusion (D14; n = 9)—and applied differential expression, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and gene regulatory network inference.Results/InterpretationIn PA T cells, we identified 320 differentially expressed genes enriched for immune-related programs (TCR signaling, T cell differentiation, IL-2–related signaling, TCR regulation of apoptosis and misregulation of cancer) and signatures consistent with immune dysfunction. Unsupervised clustering defined two LBCL T cell expression profiles, which were strongly associated with early disease progression (91% vs 16%; P < 0.001), supporting a hyperactivated yet progressively dysfunctional T cell state that may compromise CAR-T fitness and persistence. At the pathway level, GSEA in LBCL PA T cells showed enrichment of PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MYC target programs together with apoptosis-related signatures.ConclusionsTranscriptomic heterogeneity in endogenous T cells is strongly linked to early post-CAR-T progression and may inform strategies to optimize CAR-T persistence and efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04444-6.Keywords: CAR-T cells, RNA-Seq, Relapse, Lymphoma, Cell therapy

Introduction

Large B cell lymphoma (LBCL) is an aggressive and genetically heterogeneous subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma that leads to impaired lymphoid differentiation and uncontrolled proliferation [1]. While first-line therapies such as R-CHOP or R-CHOP like regimens induce long-term remission in a significant proportion of patients, near 20% of them experience early relapse or refractory disease, for whom prognosis remains poor [2, 3]. In this context, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy, particularly anti-CD19 CAR-T products, has emerged as a transformative treatment option, offering high rates of durable responses in relapsed or refractory cases [4].

Despite this improvement, clinical responses remain variable and poorly predicted [5, 6]. Evidence suggests that both the baseline cellular composition of patient-derived T cells [7–9] and the differentiation programs [10] adopted by CAR-T cells after infusion may influence therapeutic efficacy [11]. In this context, different co-stimulatory domains engage different signaling programs in persistence, T cell subset distribution, exhaustion phenotypes, and metabolism [12, 13].

Moreover, prior chemotherapy is known to influence changes in T cell fates [14, 15]. Specifically, chemotherapy can shift T cells toward an effector memory compartment and promote a dysfunctional, exhausted phenotype [14, 15]. Overall, chemotherapy alters both the composition and function of the T cell compartment, with effects that can persist long after chemotherapy clearance [14], and may impair CAR-T cell efficacy by reducing CAR-T cell cytotoxicity [15]. Thus, in some patients allogenic CAR-T cells generated from healthy-donor T cells could overcome the chemotherapy effects.

On the other hand, CAR-T cell functionality diverges from that of conventional T cells. These differences likely reflect propound transcriptional, metabolic, and downstream phenotypic changes induced in vitro activation [16, 17]. Accordingly, comparative studies between CAR-T cells and conventional T lymphocytes have provided key biological insights, increasingly highlighting the contribution of the endogenous immune system, particularly the T cell compartment. T cells rely on endogenous TCR-mediated activation and co-stimulatory signals to execute their adaptive immune responses [18]. In contrast, CAR-T cells integrate synthetic receptor signaling with non-physiological co-stimulation [19], which leads to enhanced effector function but also to increased exhaustion and susceptibility to apoptosis [20]. These differences might be manifested in distinct transcriptional profiles and pathway enrichment in T cells and CAR-T cells, governed by the modulation of transcription factors (TF) that polarize T cell fate [11, 16, 21].

Here, we perform a comparative transcriptomic analysis of pre-manufacturing T cells and post-infusion CAR-T cells from LBCL patients. First, we aim to characterize disease-associated immune dysfunction in T cells from patients with LBCL and explore its potential implications for CAR-T cell performance. Second, we seek to describe the molecular programs that shape CAR-T cell identity and function by integrating differential gene expression analysis, pathway enrichment, and regulatory network inference.

Material and methods

Patients

We collected 26 consecutive patients diagnosed with LBCL who received anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy at our center as third-line or later treatment between 2019 and 2021 with a median follow-up of 176 days (range: 28–454), for whom biological material was available for analysis. Of these, 11 patients received Axicabtagene Ciloleucel (Axi-cel, Yescarta®) and 15 received Tisagenlecleucel (Tisa-cel, Kymriah®). Clinical and demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1, Supplementary Table 1. Among the cohort, 12 patients experienced early progression (< 3 months) following CAR-T cell infusion (median and range of duration of response: 76 days [28–119]), while 14 patients maintained a durable response at the time of study enrollment (median and range of duration of response: 366 days [231–454]). Clinical responses were assessed through Lugano criteria [22]. Six samples from individuals with non-hematologic conditions were used as healthy donors, referred as controls.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital General Universitario Gregorio Marañón (HGUGM), and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample processing and RNA-sequencing

Peripheral blood (PB) samples were collected from patients at two time points: before apheresis (pre-apheresis, PA) and on day + 14 after CAR-T cell infusion (D14). Among the 26 patients, 17 had only PA samples, 6 had samples collected at both time points, and 3 had only post-CAR-T samples. In total, 23 PA samples and 9 post-CAR-T samples were analyzed. PB mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. CD3 + T cells were isolated at the PA timepoint using anti-CD3 magnetic antibodies, while CAR-T cells were enriched at D14 using FITC-conjugated CD19 detection reagent, followed by anti-FITC magnetic separation on an autoMACS Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec). An initial validation was performed using 5 samples, showing a purity of more than 90% for both CD3 and CAR-T. All isolated cells were stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

RNA was extracted using the PicoPure™ RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA concentration and integrity were measured with the Invitrogen™ Qubit™ 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA libraries were prepared using the SMARTer® Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 (Takara Bio) with 100 ng of RNA per sample. Fragmentation time was set to 30 s to account for RNA quality variability. Library concentration and quality were assessed using the Qubit™ 4 Fluorometer and LabChip® GXII Touch™ system (PerkinElmer), respectively. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a paired-end 100-bp (PE100) configuration, targeting 20–25 million reads per sample.

Transcriptomic analysis

Raw sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.0). Adapter trimming and low-quality base filtering (Phred score < 20) were performed using Trim Galore! (v0.6.10). Reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38, Gencode annotation) using STAR aligner (v2.7.11a), and gene-level quantification was performed with featureCounts (v2.0.6). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (v1.44.0) in R (v4.4.0). A P-value threshold of < 0.001 was considered significant. For further analysis, if ribosomal genes were overrepresented, they were excluded from downstream analyses. For the study of the variance of each differentially expressed gene intragroup and intergroup, a coefficient variant (CV) was assessed as follows: standard deviation(x)/mean(x), being x each differentially expressed gene. Then, the median of CV per gene was calculated, and a Wilcoxon test was performed to study the differences between groups.

Tertiary enrichment analysis was performed using the Enrichr package (v3.2) in R. To confirm T cell lineage, the Human Gene Atlas and Jensen TISSUES databases were queried. Functional enrichment was performed using BioPlanet, KEGG, and Reactome for pathway analysis, and Gene Ontology (GO) terms (Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component) for broader biological insights. Enriched terms were considered significant when adjusted P-value < 0.1 and selected based on combined score (P-value and odds ratio).

GSEA was performed using the GSEA software (v4.3.3). Hallmark and GO gene sets were tested. Enrichment was considered significant if false discovery rate (FDR) q-value < 0.25 and a normalized enrichment score (NES) >|1.4|.

Gene regulatory network construction

To identify potential master regulators in differentially expressed genes, we constructed gene regulatory networks (GRN). A reference network was compiled from TRRUST and RegNetwork databases. Only interactions involving expressed genes (≥ 10 raw counts) were retained. Nodes without any connections were excluded.

Transcription factors (TFs with the highest network connectivity among DEGs were selected as candidate regulators (or hubs). These TF-centered networks were visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape, and colored based on expression fold change. Enrichment of TF subnetworks was assessed to determine their potential biological functions.

Results

Transcriptomic analysis of T cells: LBCL patients vs. controls

A total of 23 PA PB T cell samples from LBCL patients were analyzed and compared to 6 PB T cell samples from controls. In this analysis, 320 genes were differentially expressed between both groups (Table S2). After removing noncoding and unannotated transcripts a total 123 genes remained, of them 95 protein-coding genes were upregulated while 28 were downregulated.

As expected, tertiary enrichment analysis confirmed that the differentially expressed gene set was enriched in T cell-related protein-coding genes, according to the Human Gene Atlas. Pathway enrichment analysis using BioPlanet, KEGG, GO and Reactome databases identified significant overrepresentation of T cell-associated signaling and regulatory pathways. Integrative analysis confirmed that genes involved in processes related to T cell activation, signaling, and differentiation, which are important for T cell function and homeostasis, are highly enriched in PA PB T cell samples.

A heatmap (Fig. 2) summarizing the expression of key genes involved in these enriched pathways revealed marked inter-patient heterogeneity among LBCL samples, whereas controls showed a more homogeneous expression pattern. The median CV per gene was 1.04 in LBCL samples vs 0.49 in controls (P < 0.001). Regarding this dispersion, we identified two distinct groups. Group 0 (n = 11; s1–s9, s12, s17) showed higher expression of genes within enriched pathways, whereas Group 1 (n = 12; s10, s11, s13–s16, s18-s23) displayed lower expression. Notably, early progression occurred almost exclusively in group 0 (91%), while no patients in group 1 experienced early progression (P < 0.0016). The only patient in Group 1 who did not relapse was sample s9, corresponding to a case of PMBCL. Demographic and clinical variables are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Group 0 also showed significantly higher PA LDH, pre-infusion CRP and ferritin levels than Group 1 (all P < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed for other variables, including pre-CAR-T disease status, bulky disease, or number of prior lines of therapy.

To further investigate the biological processes underlying these transcriptomic differences, we performed GSEA to identify pathways differentially regulated in PB T cells from LBCL patients compared with controls (Fig. 3A–C). Three gene sets met the predefined GSEA significance criterion (FDR q < 0.25) and showed enrichment toward the LBCL end of the ranked gene list, as indicated by the accumulation of hits in the LBCL-associated region and the negative normalized enrichment scores: PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling (NES = − 1.46; FDR q = 0.238), MYC target genes (NES = − 1.46; FDR q = 0.212), and caspase-mediated apoptosis (NES = − 1.50; FDR q = 0.217).

Gene regulatory network of T cells from LBCL vs. controls.

Given the extensive transcriptomic changes observed, a gene regulatory network (GRN) analysis was performed to identify potential master regulators. TF are critical regulators of T cell fate, function and differentiation, so further GRN analysis may explain this T cell dysregulation. Four key TFs (Fig. 4) were identified as central hubs within the network: FOXO1, ETS1, GATA3 (all upregulated), and TCF3 (downregulated).

Subnetwork enrichment of Fig. 4 revealed associations with pathways such as transcriptional misregulation in cancer (FOXO1, TCF3, TRAF1, CDKN1B, MAF), Th1/Th2 differentiation (GATA3, RUNX3, CD3E, MAF) and regulation of metabolic process (FOXO1, TCF3, MAF, LPIN2, CDKN1B, CCND3, ETS1, RUNX2, GATA3, CD3E) (Table S4). These pathways support that uncoordinated transcriptional control and metabolic reprogramming may influence T cell differentiation and fate determination.

Transcriptomic analysis of CAR-T cells vs T cells from LBCL patients and controls

A total of 9 post-infusion (D14) CAR-T cell samples vs 22 of PA PB T cell samples were analyzed (s23 was considered an outlier, excluding it from this analysis), including 5 paired samples (s10, s13, s15, s16, s17).

In this analysis, 247 genes were differentially expressed between both groups (Table S5). After removing noncoding and unannotated transcripts a total 205 genes were protein coding, of them 151 were downregulated and 54 upregulated.

Initial enrichment analysis revealed pathways related to cytokine signaling as Interleukin-7 (IL-7) signaling pathway, Interleukin-2 (IL-2) signaling pathway and interferon gamma signaling, TCR-regulated apoptosis and T cell differentiation (Fig. 5, Table S7). Dynamics of most representative genes of enriched pathways between PA and D14 in paired samples are depicted in Figure S1. The median CV per gene is 0.76 in LBCL samples vs 0.53 in CAR-T cells (P < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we performed bulk RNA-seq profiling of autologous T cells collected after chemotherapy and prior to CAR-T manufacturing, as well as CAR-T cells post-infusion in D14, to uncover transcriptional programs associated with disease-related dysfunction. Because autologous CAR-T therapies depend on the functional integrity of patient-derived T cells, understanding the molecular alterations induced by prior treatments and the subsequent reprogramming that occurs during CAR-T cell manufacturing is crucial.

Our objective was to identify the signaling pathways most disrupted in autologous T cells before CAR-T engineering and, in parallel, to characterize the pathways engaged after ex vivo expansion and reinfusion. Although, the relatively small number of cases, particularly in the post-infusion CAR-T cell group, limits the statistical power and generalizability, these data provide insight into how autologous T cell fitness and manufacturing processes may contribute to shaping the immune landscape after CAR-T therapy in vivo.

In an initial analysis, we compared the transcriptomic profiles of T cells from LBCL patients with those from healthy controls and identified 123 differentially expressed after excluding non-annotated and noncoding genes. Enrichment analyses highlighted immune-related pathways, including TCR signaling, cytotoxic T cell programs, interferon-mediated signaling, cell–cell adhesion pathways, IL-2/IL-7 signaling, and TCR-associated apoptotic programs. Within the LBCL cohort, transcriptomic stratification identified two groups (Group 0 and Group 1; Fig. 2), with Group 0 showing higher expression of genes related to these pathways and a markedly higher rate of CAR-T therapy failure. Nevertheless, this association should be interpreted cautiously, as potential confounding factors—including LDH, ferritin, and CRP levels, all of which were associated with the clustering and are themselves linked to inflammatory status and tumor burden [23, 24]—may have shaped the observed transcriptional profiles. Accordingly, the identified signature may reflect the patients’ underlying clinical state rather than intrinsic CAR-T cell fitness.

In group 0, increased expression of core TCR signaling genes (CD3E, ZAP-70, FYN) was observed, consistent with a heightened activation-related transcriptional profile. Concurrent enrichment of cytotoxic programs, PD-1–associated signaling, and prostaglandin receptor genes (PTGER2, PTGER4) aligns with transcriptional states previously described in dysfunctional or chronically stimulated T cells [25–28]. Additionally, enrichment of IFNγ-related signatures and genes involved in cell–cell adhesion indicates broader remodeling of immune regulatory pathways, including upregulation of exhaustion-associated ligands such as PD-L1 [29, 30] and potential alterations in immune synapse formation and tumor–immune interactions processes [31, 32]. Both mechanisms have been linked to metabolic reprogramming and cancer progression.

The contribution of IL-2 signaling to these phenotypes is likely context-dependent. While IL-2 is essential for T cell expansion, sustained or excessive signaling can bias cells toward terminal effector differentiation, which is generally less persistent and less conducive to long-term tumor control than memory-like subsets [33, 34]. Consistent with this, enhanced IL-2-related programs have been associated with increased expression of exhaustion markers and apoptosis-related pathways in relapsed settings [35, 36]. Altogether, these observations suggest that the transcriptional group 0 profile may reflect a hyperactivated yet progressively dysfunctional T cell state, potentially compromising CAR-T cell efficiency, this contributing to a high post-CAR-T progression [37, 38].

At the cohort level, GSEA highlighted enrichment of PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MYC target, and apoptosis-related pathways in LBCL-associated T cell profiles, consistent with broad alterations in activation and metabolic signaling. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger, independent cohorts.

To further characterize transcriptional differences between peripheral T cells from LBCL patients and healthy controls, we performed a GRN analysis to identify transcription factors potentially contributing to the observed divergence. This analysis highlighted increased activity of FOXO1, ETS1, and GATA3, alongside reduced TCF3 activity, in T cells from LBCL patients. These transcription factors have been implicated in T cell differentiation, immune regulation, and metabolic control across diverse immunological contexts [11, 39–44]. While the present comparison does not allow attribution of these differences to specific mechanisms, the identified regulatory network underscores broad remodeling of T cell transcriptional programs in the setting of LBCL and its associated clinical context.

We next examined CAR-T cells collected at D14 after infusion. Relative to PA T cells from LBCL patients, CAR-T D14 cells displayed 205 differentially expressed genes after excluding non-annotated and noncoding genes. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed reduced expression of genes related to IL-2 and IL-7 signaling, together with downregulation of TCR-driven apoptotic programs. As described above, PA T cells from Group 0 showed higher overall expression levels of these pathways than both CAR-T D14 cells and control T cells. These findings suggest that the manufacturing and reinfusion process is accompanied by transcriptomic reprogramming, even additional factors could also contribute to these differences, including lymphodepletion chemotherapy and antigen-driven expansion. The reduction of specific gene expression programs after ex vivo expansion may reflect the acquisition of a distinct cellular state rather than an intrinsically favorable or unfavorable one. However, given the limited number of CAR-T D14 samples (n = 9), including only a single case from Group 0, subgroup analyses were underpowered and were therefore not pursued.

Interestingly, GSEA identified Hedgehog signaling as enriched in CAR-T cells at day 14 after infusion. While Hedgehog pathway components have been implicated in T cell activation and immune synapse dynamics in other experimental settings [45, 46], the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and do not establish a functional role for Hedgehog signaling in CAR-T effector activity.

GRN analysis to identify TF that may be potentially associated with CAR-T transcriptional programs. Observing upregulation of TF such as MAF, STAT5B, NFE2L2 (NRF2), and HMGA1. STAT5B, along with MAF, are critical TFs activated downstream of the IL-2 signaling, but also a key regulator of memory fate and survival [47–49]. NRF2—linked to IL-2 secretion and glucose metabolism—was downregulated, potentially indicating decreased IL-2 production and signaling [50].

In future studies, by increasing the cohort size and performing single-cell analyses, we aim to correlate the identified transcriptomic signatures with clinical outcomes, including treatment response and the development of toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome or neurotoxicity. This integrative approach may facilitate the discovery of predictive biomarkers and support the development of therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, we defined the transcriptomic landscape of peripheral T cells before CAR-T manufacturing and CAR-T-enriched cells after infusion in patients with LBCL. Pre-apheresis T cells showed extensive transcriptional alterations and marked inter-patient heterogeneity, with an activation-associated state associated with early disease progression after CAR-T therapy.

In contrast, CAR-T-enriched cells displayed a distinct transcriptional landscape consistent with extensive reprogramming associated with CAR-T manufacturing and in vivo expansion. These findings highlight the diversity of T cell transcriptional states in the context of CAR-T therapy and support further studies integrating transcriptomic and functional analyses.
