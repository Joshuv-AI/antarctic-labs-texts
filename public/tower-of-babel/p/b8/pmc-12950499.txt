Rasmussen Aneurysm in Pulmonary Tuberculosis—A Malaysian Case Study With Review of Global Literature
Authors: Sing Yee Ng, Kai Sin Leong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) remains a major global health challenge, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries where it contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality. Hemoptysis is a common complication of PTB and may result from a variety of mechanisms, including vascular involvement. Rasmussen aneurysm, a pseudoaneurysm of the pulmonary artery adjacent to a tuberculous cavity, represents a rare but potentially fatal cause of hemoptysis. We report a Malaysian patient with smear‐positive PTB complicated by Rasmussen aneurysm, successfully managed with selective pulmonary artery embolization. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and advanced imaging with CT pulmonary angiography to establish diagnosis and guide therapy. In addition, we performed a comprehensive review of published reports and studies on Rasmussen aneurysm to emphasise the diagnostic challenges, summarise management strategies, and highlight why this entity remains underdiagnosed, particularly in resource‐limited settings.Keywords: haemoptysis, pseudoaneurysm, pulmonary tuberculosis, Rasmussen aneurysm

Introduction

Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) remains a significant global public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), it was the 10th leading cause of death worldwide in 2021, ranking even higher in many low‐ and middle‐income countries [1]. In 2023, WHO estimated a global incidence of 134 cases per 100,000 population, with a corresponding mortality rate of 15.5 per 100,000 [2]. Data from the Ministry of Health (MOH) Malaysia has reported a national incidence of 80.23 per 100,000 population and a mortality rate of 7.86 per 100,000 in 2023, highlighting an ongoing impact of PTB towards the national public health system [3].

Hemoptysis is a well‐recognised manifestation of PTB. Its frequency varies with disease status, occurring in approximately 16.8% of new PTB cases and rising to 26.9% in patients with recurrent disease [4]. The underlying mechanisms are diverse, ranging from parenchymal destruction (e.g., tuberculomas or post‐tuberculous cavities colonised by aspergilloma), to airway involvement (e.g., bronchiectasis or endobronchial lesions), and vascular complications (e.g., pulmonary or bronchial arteritis, thrombosis, and formation of Rasmussen aneurysms) [5, 6].

Rasmussen aneurysm is a rare but potentially fatal pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm that develops adjacent to or within a tuberculous cavity [7]. In this report, we present one of the first peer‐reviewed Malaysian cases treated with pulmonary artery embolization. We focus our discussion on improving recognition, diagnostic imaging, and treatment intervention, as well as exploring why Rasmussen aneurysm remains underdiagnosed through a global literature review.

Case Report

A 56‐year‐old man presented with a 10‐month history of chronic cough accompanied by intermittent hemoptysis, occurring one to two times per month since June 2024. The most recent episode had occurred 2 weeks before admission. The hemoptysis was described as fresh blood mixed with sputum, resolving spontaneously without medical intervention. He also reported a weight loss of 10 kg over the same period, which he attributed to intentional intermittent fasting. He denied fever, night sweats, dyspnea, or known contact with patients diagnosed with PTB.

His medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia and ischemic heart disease. Coronary angiography performed in 2012 and again in August 2024 demonstrated two‐vessel disease. The patient had refused coronary artery bypass grafting and was maintained on lifelong single antiplatelet therapy and standard heart failure medications.

On admission in April 2025, he reported a persistent productive cough but no active hemoptysis. He was hemodynamically stable with a blood pressure of 118/60 mmHg, heart rate of 88 beats per minute, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Laboratory investigations showed a haemoglobin level of 12 g/dL, platelet count of 488 × 10^9/L, and normal coagulation profile (PT/APTT, INR 1.0). Chest radiography demonstrated diffuse nodular opacities, suggestive of PTB. Sputum analysis revealed a positive acid‐fast bacilli (AFB) smear, and subsequently positive sputum culture of Mycobacterium tuberculosis further confirmed the diagnosis of PTB, for which standard first‐line anti‐tuberculosis therapy was initiated.

On the following day, he developed a significant episode of hemoptysis, described as a large volume of fresh blood, though the exact amount was not quantified. Despite this, he remained hemodynamically stable. Supportive management included supplemental oxygen via nasal prongs, intravenous tranexamic acid (1 g), and temporary discontinuation of antiplatelet therapy in view of the risk of life‐threatening bleeding. Haemoglobin levels remained stable.

Urgent CT pulmonary angiography revealed a cavitary lesion in the superior segment of the left lower lobe measuring 3.9 × 3.6 × 3.3 cm, with an intralesional aneurysmal dilatation arising from the subsegmental branch of the left descending pulmonary artery, consistent with Rasmussen aneurysm (measuring 0.6 × 1.2 × 1.1 cm) (Figure 1). The patient subsequently underwent selective pulmonary artery embolization with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles, with no immediate post‐procedural complications. He remained hemodynamically stable throughout admission, maintained adequate oxygenation on low‐flow oxygen therapy, and did not require blood transfusion. He was later discharged in good condition and following the subsequent follow up reviews in the chest clinic, he reported significant improvement in his chronic cough and no recurrence of hemoptysis.

Discussion

Rasmussen Aneurysm, first described by and named after Fritz Valdemar Rasmussen in 1868, forms as chronic inflammation progressively replaces the vessel's adventitia and media with granulation tissue and fibrin, leading to thinning of the arterial wall and pseudoaneurysm formation of the pulmonary artery, which can rupture and cause massive hemoptysis [7]. Although exceedingly uncommon, Rasmussen aneurysm remains a clinically significant vascular complication of PTB.

In preparing this report, we conducted a focused literature review using PubMed, Google Scholar and ScienceDirect to identify publications on Rasmussen aneurysm, pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm and tuberculosis‐related hemoptysis. Articles were included if they reported radiologically confirmed Rasmussen aneurysm and provided information on clinical features, imaging findings or management, while aneurysms unrelated to tuberculosis or cases without adequate diagnostic confirmation were excluded. However most available data were individual case reports or small series, the findings were synthesised narratively. Consistent themes across the literature included the strong association with cavitary PTB, the diagnostic role of CT pulmonary angiography and the favourable outcomes of endovascular embolization. These patterns have led to our discussion of diagnostic challenges and the persistent under‐recognition of this complication in resource‐limited settings.

A large radiological study analysing 21,532 patients with confirmed PTB detected Rasmussen aneurysms in only 0.25%, based on multidetector CT angiography (MDCTA) [8]. Complementing these findings, one of the earliest autopsy reviews by Auerbach et al. in 1939 found pulmonary artery aneurysms in 4% of 1114 autopsied patients with chronic cavitary tuberculosis [9]. Taken together, these data underscore how rare Rasmussen aneurysm is—even in populations with high rates of cavitary TB.

The development of Rasmussen Aneurysm is strongly associated with cavitary pulmonary tuberculosis, where chronic granulomatous inflammation and enzymatic destruction progressively weaken the arterial wall. Almost all published cases describe aneurysms located adjacent to tuberculous cavities [7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. This consistent relationship underscores the importance of careful surveillance in patients with cavitary pulmonary TB who present with hemoptysis.

Currently, MDCTA is regarded as the most reliable diagnostic tool, offering rapid and detailed visualisation of the aneurysm and its vascular connections [8, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Unlike standard angiography, MDCTA can distinguish pulmonary arterial bleeding from bronchial artery sources, while simultaneously guiding interventional planning [16]. However, in some resources limited area, although it's not the best imaging modality, Contrast‐Enhanced CT (CECT) has been used to diagnosed in some of the reviewed cases [10, 13].

Management is largely dictated by clinical stability, aneurysm size, and local expertise. In hemodynamically unstable patients or those with large aneurysms, minimally invasive endovascular embolization, employing materials like coils, glue, or microparticles, is generally considered the first‐ line therapeutic option due to its efficacy and safety profile. A recent series reported complete haemostasis and absence of recurrence in multiple patients undergoing embolization for massive hemoptysis [7, 12, 14, 15, 16, 20, 21].

For patients who are stable or in settings where interventional radiology is unavailable, conservative approaches with anti‐tuberculous medication and antifibrinolytics (e.g., tranexamic acid) have been described [10, 12, 13]. While certain reports document favourable results, particularly in resource‐limited regions and patients with financial restrain, this strategy is generally reserved for small aneurysms (< 6 cm in diameter) or clinically stable individuals [22]. Surgical resection (lobectomy or pneumonectomy) is rarely performed in modern practice but remains an option when embolization is unsuccessful or inaccessible. Outcomes tend to be less favourable due to the emergent presentation and high perioperative risk [19]. One published case involved a giant Rasmussen aneurysm complicated by severe hemoptysis during angiographic procedure. Despite undergoing urgent right pneumonectomy, the patient died 3 days later as a result of postoperative pulmonary edema [23].

In addition to endovascular embolization with coils or glue and the less commonly performed surgical resection, a South African case series also reported the use of Amplatzer vascular plugs as an alternative treatment option. In this cohort of 25 patients with Rasmussen aneurysm, through transcatheter occlusion of the feeding pulmonary artery, 18 patients (72%) remained asymptomatic within 48 h, although six later experienced recurrent hemoptysis within 1 year, indicating good early control but variable long‐term durability [24].

Failure to recognise Rasmussen Aneurysm early carries a significant risk of mortality. Fatal outcomes have been reported in settings with delayed imaging and without access to embolization, such as a Japanese case where a patient treated conservatively suffered recurrent and ultimately fatal hemoptysis [25]. These experiences highlight the critical need for timely CTA imaging and urgent referral for interventional management in unstable cases.

Despite the high prevalence of tuberculosis in Southeast Asia, published reports of Rasmussen Aneurysm remains limited. This likely reflects under‐diagnosis rather than true rarity, attributable to restricted access to MDCTA in rural hospitals and shortages of interventional radiology services. Furthermore, delays in TB detection and treatment contribute to chronic cavitary disease, thereby increasing susceptibility to aneurysm formation. Improved awareness among clinicians, coupled with systematic reporting, is essential to enhance recognition and improve outcomes in this region.

In conclusion, Rasmussen aneurysm, although uncommon, represents a critical vascular complication of PTB with high risk of life‐threatening hemoptysis. MDCTA remains the gold standard for diagnosis, enabling differentiation from bronchial sources and guiding interventional planning. Endovascular embolization is increasingly favoured as the first‐line treatment due to its safety and efficacy, while surgical resection is reserved for select refractory cases. Conservative management with anti‐tuberculous therapy may be considered in small or stable aneurysms, particularly in settings where interventional radiology is lacking. This case underscores the need for heightened clinical awareness, timely imaging, and access to interventional radiology services to improve outcomes. In regions with high TB prevalence such as Southeast Asia, systematic reporting and greater awareness are essential to address the under‐recognition of this rare but serious entity.
