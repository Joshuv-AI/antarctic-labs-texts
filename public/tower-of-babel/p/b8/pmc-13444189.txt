A prospective randomized study to evaluate the effect of epidural volume extension with normal saline in combined spinal epidural anesthesia for lower limb surgeries using intrathecal hyperbaric levobupivacaine
Authors: Kavana S, Ashwini Turai, Archana Shivashankar, Subham Tripathy, Soundarya D U, Sudhir S Sherikar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCombined spinal epidural anesthesia (CSEA) offers rapid onset and effective postoperative analgesia for lower limb surgeries. Epidural volume extension (EVE), achieved by epidural saline injection following intrathecal administration, influences the cephalad spread of local anesthetic. This study examined the effect of EVE with normal saline on sensory and motor block characteristics following intrathecal hyperbaric levobupivacaine.MethodsA total of 100 American Society of Anesthesiologists I–II patients aged 18–60 years were assigned to two groups. Group A received 2.5 ml of 0.5% hyperbaric levobupivacaine intrathecally, while Group B received the same dose followed by EVE with 10 ml of normal saline. Sensory and motor block onset, peak block height, regression characteristics, and epidural supplementation requirement were compared.ResultsPeak sensory block height was T10 in Group A (42%) and T8 (46%) in Group B (P = 0.003). Sensory and motor block onset was significantly faster in Group B (P < 0.001). However, the motor block was longer in Group A (219.30 ± 22.20 min) than in Group B (206.20 ± 22.87 min; P = 0.005). Epidural top-up was more frequent in Group B (18%) compared with Group A (6%), though this difference did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.08). Adequate intraoperative anesthesia was achieved in all patients with timely epidural supplementation when required.ConclusionsEVE with normal saline in CSEA using intrathecal hyperbaric levobupivacaine results in faster onset and greater cephalad sensory blockade; however, it may be associated with earlier block regression in longer procedures.

INTRODUCTION

Epidural volume extension (EVE) is a variant of the combined spinal epidural anesthesia (CSEA) technique. It involves administering normal saline into the epidural space soon after the intrathecal injection. The injected volume temporarily compresses the dural sac. This promotes the upward spread of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and intrathecal drugs. Thus, it can enhance sensory block height and hasten onset [1-3]. This volume effect may optimize block characteristics without increasing the intrathecal drug dose. Hemodynamic effects remain comparable to conventional spinal anesthesia.

Although several studies have evaluated EVE, results remain inconsistent, with variations attributed to drug baricity, intrathecal dose, timing, and saline volume used [4-8]. However, despite extensive evaluation of EVE with bupivacaine, no studies have specifically examined its use with hyperbaric levobupivacaine, which offers comparable efficacy with a superior safety profile due to its lower cardiotoxic and neurotoxic potential [9,10]. The differing pharmacodynamic properties of levobupivacaine warrant independent evaluation of EVE’s effects when used as part of CSEA. Furthermore, understanding the interaction between epidural volume, timing, and patient positioning is essential for optimizing block characteristics, particularly in elderly or high-risk surgical patients. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess the effect of the EVE technique with normal saline in CSEA with hyperbaric levobupivacaine for lower limb surgeries.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ramaiah Medical College and Hospitals (Approval No.: MSRMC/EC/PG-82/04-2023), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. It was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2023/09/057943). One hundred and six patients aged 18–60 years, classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I–II and planned for elective lower limb surgery under neuraxial anesthesia, were enrolled in this prospective randomized controlled trial. Patients with contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia, including coagulopathy, local infection at the puncture site, allergy to local anesthetics, and patient refusal, were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included a history of lumbar spine surgery, known structural deformities of the lumbar spine (such as scoliosis or kyphosis), neurological disorders affecting sensory or motor function, and inability to cooperate or communicate intraoperatively.

Eligible patients were assigned to two groups of 50 each through computer-generated randomization, with allocation concealed using sealed opaque envelopes.

• Group A: Intrathecal 2.5 ml 0.5% hyperbaric levobupivacaine without EVE

• Group B: Intrathecal 2.5 ml 0.5% hyperbaric levobupivacaine with EVE (10 ml of normal saline)

All patients fasted for six hours before surgery and received premedication with pantoprazole 40 mg and ondansetron 4 mg on the night before and the morning of surgery. On arrival in the operating room, standard monitors were connected to record baseline vitals. An 18-G IV cannula was placed, and Ringer’s lactate (10 ml/kg) was administered.

With the patient sitting and sterilely prepped, the L3–4 intervertebral space was located. After injecting 1.5 ml of 2% lidocaine, the epidural space was accessed with an 18-G Tuohy needle using the loss-of-resistance to air technique. The epidural catheter was threaded and secured. Placement was confirmed with a 3 ml test dose of lignocaine with adrenaline (1:200,000). For patients where the L3–4 space was inaccessible, the adjacent interspace was used.

Subarachnoid block was then established at the L4–5 interspace using a 25-G Quincke spinal needle. When a midline approach was unsuccessful, a paramedian approach was adopted at the same interspace. Upon confirmation of free CSF flow, 2.5 ml of 0.5% hyperbaric levobupivacaine was injected over 20 s. Patients were immediately positioned supine. For both groups, the completion of intrathecal drug administration was taken as time zero for assessment of sensory and motor block onset. In Group B, EVE was performed by administering 10 ml of preservative-free normal saline through the epidural catheter within 5 min of spinal injection.

Hemodynamic parameters (heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and SpO2) were documented at 2-min intervals during the first 10 min, at 5-min intervals over the subsequent 20 min, and at 10-min intervals thereafter until surgery was completed. A blinded anesthesiologist (AT) assessed the block characteristics. Sensory block was examined bilaterally along the midclavicular line using an alcohol swab (loss of temperature sensation) every min until the highest level was achieved and every 10 min until regression by two dermatomes. Motor block was assessed using the modified Bromage scale every min during the block establishment phase until the onset of motor block (defined as achievement of Bromage grade 1) and attainment of maximum motor blockade. Subsequently, motor block was assessed at predefined time points, including at the end of surgery and postoperatively until complete recovery.

An effective block was defined as achieving a sensory block height up to T10 within 20 min of intrathecal injection, without the need for immediate epidural supplementation during block establishment. Patients who failed to achieve the required sensory level within this period were considered to have inadequate blocks, were managed with 10 ml of 0.5% plain bupivacaine administered via the epidural catheter, and were excluded from further analysis. During surgery, the adequacy of anesthesia was continuously assessed using sensory level testing, patient verbal response, and hemodynamic parameters. Epidural supplementation was administered when regression of sensory block below the surgical requirement or patient discomfort was noted, using incremental doses of 0.5% plain bupivacaine up to a maximum of 10 ml. Postoperative analgesia was provided through the indwelling epidural catheter. The total duration of surgery (from incision to closure) was recorded.

Adverse events such as hypotension, bradycardia, nausea, vomiting, pruritus, and shivering were documented. Hypotension was defined as a > 20% decrease in systolic blood pressure from baseline or < 90 mmHg and treated with intravenous mephentermine (6 mg increments) and fluids. Bradycardia (heart rate < 50 bpm) was treated with intravenous atropine 0.6 mg.

The following parameters were recorded:

• Onset of sensory block: time from intrathecal injection to sensory block at the L1 dermatome.

• Time to maximum sensory block: time from injection to attainment of peak sensory level.

• Two-segment sensory regression: time from peak level to regression by two dermatomes.

• Onset of motor block: time to achieve Bromage grade 1 from completion of injection.

• Duration of motor block: time to complete recovery from motor block.

As spinal anesthesia was uniformly performed at the L4–5 interspace and variation in epidural catheter insertion level was evenly distributed between groups, these variables were not considered confounding factors and were therefore not included in the statistical analysis.

Sample size calculation

Based on the study by Preethi and Ravishankar [11], the mean time to attain peak sensory level was 11.6 ± 1.565 min in the control group and 8.12 ± 1.565 min in the EVE group (P < 0.001). Accordingly, sample size estimation was performed using time to attain peak sensory level as the primary outcome variable. All other block characteristics were considered secondary outcomes. To achieve 80% power and a 95% confidence level with an effect size of 0.61, a minimum of 43 patients per group was required. Therefore, 53 patients were included in each group to account for potential dropouts.

Statistical analysis

Data were compiled using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corp.) and subsequently analyzed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Co.) and Epi Info version 7.2.1 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions and compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the independent samples t-test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

RESULTS

A total of 106 patients were assessed for eligibility and were randomized into two groups. Six patients were excluded from the final analysis due to inadequate block. All remaining patients completed the study as per protocol. Final statistical analysis was therefore performed on a per-protocol basis, including 100 (50 in each group) patients (Fig. 1). Both groups were comparable with respect to age, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, and duration of surgery; however, a statistically significant difference in sex distribution was observed between the groups (Table 1).

Block characteristics are depicted in Table 2. Group A exhibited significantly longer onset times for sensory (164.06 ± 38.70 s) and motor blocks (160.54 ± 46.39 s) compared to Group B (97.58 ± 21.21 s and 117.06 ± 71.57 s, respectively), with P values < 0.001. Time to peak sensory block was also longer in Group A (9.72 ± 1.53 min) than in Group B (7.48 ± 1.09 min, P = 0.006). The median peak sensory block height was T10 (range T6–T12) in Group A and T8 (range T2–T12) in Group B, with the EVE group achieving a significantly higher sensory block level (P = 0.003). Two-segment regression time and total motor block duration were significantly greater in Group A (163.52 ± 26.59 min and 219.30 ± 22.20 min) than in Group B (148.90 ± 25.86 min and 206.20 ± 22.87 min), both with P < 0.01. However, time to achieve maximum motor block and motor block grade at the end of surgery did not differ significantly between the groups (P = 0.732 and P = 0.178, respectively).

The requirement for epidural top-up was higher in Group B (18%) compared to Group A (6%), as depicted in Fig. 2, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.08). All patients requiring supplementation achieved satisfactory intraoperative analgesia following epidural top-up, and no patient required conversion to general anesthesia. The incidence of adverse effects such as bradycardia, hypotension, and shivering was low and comparable between groups (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Over recent years, central neuraxial blockade techniques and the range of agents used for neuraxial administration have evolved substantially. Among these, CSEA has gained popularity for its ability to combine the rapid onset and reliability of spinal anesthesia with the flexibility of epidural top-ups for prolonged procedures [12,13]. EVE, a modification of the CSEA technique, has been explored in both obstetric and non-obstetric settings for its ability to reduce the intrathecal anesthetic dose and potentially minimize spinal-induced hypotension [3-7,14,15].

Most studies evaluating EVE have employed intrathecal racemic bupivacaine and demonstrated enhanced cephalad sensory spread, with variable effects on motor block duration [4-8]. In the present study, a comparable increase in sensory block height was observed using hyperbaric levobupivacaine, suggesting that the fundamental volume-mediated effect of EVE is preserved irrespective of the intrathecal agent. However, the earlier regression of motor blockade seen in our study contrasts with some bupivacaine-based reports describing more prolonged motor effects. This difference may be attributable to the pharmacodynamic profile of levobupivacaine, which is associated with reduced motor fibre affinity and a more favorable safety margin [9,10].

Celleno et al. [16] found that the minimum effective intrathecal dose of levobupivacaine for caesarean delivery was 11.10 mg, while Parpaglioni et al. [17] reported a slightly lower dose of 10.58 mg. As our study population underwent lower limb surgeries requiring a higher level and longer duration of anesthesia compared with obstetric cases, a dose of 12.5 mg intrathecal levobupivacaine was selected.

Tyagi et al. [6] determined that 7.4 ml of normal saline was the minimum effective epidural volume required to raise the sensory block by two dermatomes during EVE. Subsequent studies have shown that the efficacy of EVE is time-dependent, with the most consistent augmentation observed when the epidural injection is administered soon after the intrathecal dose [7]. In the present study, 10 ml of normal saline was injected through the epidural catheter immediately after the patient was positioned supine. However, Trautman et al. [18] observed benefits even when EVE was delayed up to 20 min. Nevertheless, other investigators have suggested that factors beyond injection volume and timing may also contribute to the variability in block characteristics [19,20].

In the present study, EVE resulted in a significantly earlier onset of both sensory and motor block and was associated with a higher peak sensory level compared with the control group. These observations are in agreement with the findings reported by Blumgart et al. [1], who demonstrated that extradural administration of either saline or bupivacaine after spinal injection effectively extended block height in women undergoing caesarean delivery. However, Tyagi et al. [5] observed no significant enhancement in sensory levels when EVE was performed after 5 or 8 mg intrathecal bupivacaine for lower limb surgeries, suggesting that the effectiveness of EVE may depend on the intrathecal dose, baricity, and clinical context.

The rapid onset and increased cephalad spread of sensory block observed with EVE can be attributed to the volume effect or thecal compression. Injection of saline into the epidural space increases epidural pressure, which transiently compresses the dural sac, reduces CSF volume, and facilitates both earlier and more extensive cephalad distribution of the intrathecal local anesthetic. This mechanical interaction has been substantiated by both clinical and imaging studies [1,2,21]. Takiguchi et al. [22] provided myelographic evidence demonstrating a direct correlation between the volume of epidural fluid injected and the extent of thecal sac compression, thereby confirming the physiological basis of EVE-mediated block augmentation of both block onset and peak sensory level.

An important clinical observation in this study was the shorter duration of motor blockade in the EVE group compared with the control group. Consistent with this finding, a numerically higher proportion of patients in the EVE group required epidural supplementation (18% vs. 6%). All patients achieved satisfactory surgical conditions with timely epidural supplementation, highlighting the advantage of CSEA in providing flexible block extension when required.

The shorter motor block duration associated with EVE may be explained by altered intrathecal drug distribution. Volume-mediated thecal compression promotes cephalad sensory spread while reducing sustained exposure of lumbar motor nerve roots to effective local anesthetic concentrations. Loubert et al. [20] similarly reported reduced motor block intensity and faster motor recovery in obstetric patients receiving intrathecal bupivacaine with EVE, despite no significant increase in sensory block height [19]. This phenomenon has been attributed to possible caudal redistribution of intrathecal drug within the curved lumbosacral region and reduced motor fiber involvement. Earlier motor recovery may also result from pressure-mediated CSF redistribution and lower effective intrathecal concentrations at the lumbar level, which limit motor blockade duration while maintaining adequate surgical anesthesia.

Overall, EVE alters the balance between block onset, extent, and duration, offering faster and higher sensory blockade at the cost of earlier regression and increased reliance on epidural supplementation, particularly in longer surgical procedures.

This study has certain limitations. First, it was performed at a single center with a modest sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the results. Second, the study was restricted to patients undergoing lower limb surgeries, and the results may differ in other surgical populations or in obstetric settings. Third, total local anesthetic consumption, including epidural supplementation, was not quantified or compared between groups. Fourth, a statistically significant difference in sex distribution was observed between the groups. Although sex has not been shown to independently influence the onset, spread, or duration of spinal anesthesia when fixed intrathecal doses are used in non-obstetric populations, this imbalance is acknowledged as a potential limitation. Future studies employing stratified randomization may help address this issue.

Finally, hemodynamic parameters and patient satisfaction were not evaluated as primary outcomes, and future studies incorporating these variables may offer a more complete understanding of the clinical utility of EVE with levobupivacaine.

EVE using normal saline in CSEA with intrathecal hyperbaric levobupivacaine significantly hastened the onset and enhanced the cephalad spread of sensory and motor blockade compared to spinal anesthesia alone. However, it was also associated with earlier regression of block characteristics and a numerically higher, though not statistically significant, requirement for epidural supplementation. Thus, while EVE can facilitate faster and higher block levels, its use should be individualized based on anticipated surgical duration and the availability of epidural supplementation.
