The mediating roles of social support and sleep quality in the relationship between depression and cognitive function among older adults living with HIV in China
Authors: Mingdan Li, Yali Xu, Qian Liu, Zhiyan Bao, Ying Zhang, Xinyi Zhang, Chende Su, Qianqian Zhu, Huajun Wang, Ping Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to investigate the mediating effects of social support and sleep quality on the relationship between depression and cognitive function among older adults living with HIV. This cross-sectional study used convenience sampling to recruit older adults living with HIV from the outpatient department of a designated AIDS hospital in Zunyi, Guizhou Province, China, between January and December 2024. Data collection involved the use of a general data questionnaire (which included demographic and clinical information), the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Social Support Scale (SSRS). The data were organized and analyzed via SPSS version 29.0 software and process software. In this study, there was a significant negative correlation between depression and cognitive function among older adults living with HIV. In regression analyses, depression accounted for 47.7% of the variance in cognitive function after adjusting for confounders, which increased to 65.7% when social support and sleep quality variables were included. Mediation analysis indicated that depression had a direct predictive effect on cognitive function, with this direct effect accounting for 35.35% of the total effect (95% CI − 0.332, − 0.055). Furthermore, social support and sleep quality exhibited a chain mediation effect between depression and cognitive function, contributing to an overall indirect effect that comprised 64.65% of the total impact (95% CI − 0.462, − 0.254). In this study, we found that depressive symptoms in older adults living with HIV have a direct effect on cognitive function. Additionally, these depressive symptoms influence the cognitive function of older adults living with HIV through chain mediation involving social support and sleep quality. To effectively delay cognitive decline in this population, enhancing social support and improving sleep quality are key strategies for achieving significant breakthroughs in this area.Keywords: Older adults, HIV, Depression, Cognition, Chain mediating effect, Social support, Sleep quality, Healthy agingSubject terms: Health care, Geriatrics

Introduction

The advent of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has revolutionized the management of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS), transforming it from a fatal illness into a chronic and manageable condition, which has significantly increased the life expectancy of individuals living with HIV1. According to data from the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS(UNAIDS), approximately 39.9 million people were diagnosed with HIV globally in 2023, with the proportion of patients aged 50 years and older rising dramatically from 8% in 2000 to 24%, equating to approximately 9.57 million cases2. In AIDS research, HIV patients experience accelerated immune aging, resulting in age-related syndromes-such as frailty and illness-occurring up to ten years earlier than observed in the general population3–5.To differentiate them from the sexually active population aged 15–49 years, individuals aged ≥ 50 years with HIV are typically classified as older adults living with HIV3,6,7. The synergistic effects of aging, HIV, and ART drugs can activate microglia-mediated neuroinflammatory pathways, trigger calcium homeostasis through aberrant glutamate receptor activation, and induce oxidative stress leading to depletion of neurotrophic factors, resulting in significant impairments in the areas of executive functioning, speed of processing, language, recall, and motor/psychomotor domains in older adults living with HIV leading to the development of cognitive deficits, and the occurrence of cognitive impairment occurs8,9. Studies have shown that the prevalence of cognitive impairment in older adults living with HIV is as high as 87.2%9.

Depression is another common comorbidity among patients living with HIV.The incidence of older adults living with HIV is alarmingly high, reaching 53%10. Depressive symptoms serve as a significant risk factor for cognitive decline in older adults living with HIV,and the severity of depression has been identified as a predictor of cognitive function11.However, the specific pathways through which depression affects cognitive function in older adults living with HIV remain unclear.

In addition, depression can have a negative effect on social capital (personal relationships, social networks, social trust, social reciprocity, and social participation) in older adults living with HIV leading to inadequate social support12. Cognitive function also significantly improves with increased levels of social support in older adults living with HIV13. The social control hypothesis suggests that close social relationships promote health behaviors and health outcomes14.The relationship between depression and cognitive function in older adults living with HIV may be influenced by social support. Studies have confirmed that social support and sleep quality may play important mediating roles in the relationship between depression and cognitive function15. However, the path of the relationship in older adults living with HIV is unclear.

Sleep quality is a multifaceted concept that encompasses sleep efficiency, sleep latency, total sleep duration, and the latency to fall back asleep after waking16.Various factors, including age and the side effects of ART medications, can complicate sleep for individuals living with HIV. In older adults living with HIV, there exists a positive correlation between sleep quality and cognitive function; thus, sleep quality serves as an important predictor of cognitive performance17. High-quality sleep has been shown to alleviate depressive symptoms and mitigate cognitive decline18. Research has indicated that older adults living with HIV who experience low levels of social support are 1.40 times more likely to report decreased sleep quality than their counterparts with high levels of social support19.

On this basis, the present study aimed to investigate the mediating roles of social support and sleep quality in the relationship between depression and cognitive function among older adults living with HIV. Drawing from prior research, the following hypotheses have been formulated: (1) Depression in older adults living with HIV can directly predict cognitive function; (2) Social support mediates the relationship between depression and cognitive function; (3) Sleep quality mediates the relationship between depression and cognitive function; (4) Social support and sleep quality have a chain mediating effect between depression and cognitive function.

Objects and methods

Study population and ethical considerations

This cross-sectional study used convenience sampling to recruit older adults living with HIV from the outpatient department of a designated AIDS hospital in Guizhou, China, between January and December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1)met the diagnostic criteria of the China AIDS Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines (2021 edition)20, (2) were ≥ 50 years old3, and (3) exhibited no communication disorders, and were able to communicate normally while being willing to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with comorbid psychiatric diseases or serious psychological diseases, and (2) patients with comorbid tumors, or serious cardiovascular, or cerebrovascular diseases. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Zunyi Medical University Affiliated Hospital (Ethics No: KLLY-2023-714). All participants provided informed consent, and the clinical investigation was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

To calculate the sample size, we used Monte Carlo confidence interval efficacy analysis21,22. The required parameters were entered sequentially and multiple runs were performed to ensure stability of the results. When a conventional efficacy level of 0.80 was chosen, the minimum sample size required was 162. A total of 429 older adults living with HIV at the recruitment site met the initial inclusion criteria and were selected for screening. Among them, 63 patients met the exclusion criteria and were not eligible to participate in the study. To reduce the probability of error, we increased the sample size to 366 cases (power = 0.99). After excluding invalid questionnaires, we ultimately collected 351 valid questionnaires (power = 0.99), with a valid recovery rate of 95.90%.The final sample size was 351patients.More details about the study population are shown in Fig. 1.

This study was a cross-sectional study in which data were collected through the use of a general information questionnaire, the Geriatric Brief Depression Scale (GDS-15), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Social Support Scale (SSRS).

Measurement

General information questionnaire

After reviewing the literature and consulting with relevant experts, the general information questionnaire mainly included demographic information and clinical disease information. The demographic information collected included gender, age, education level, ethnicity, marital status, per capita monthly household income, smoking history, and drinking history. Clinical disease information included mainly the following: diagnosis with HIV, comorbidities, ART payment, and CD4+ T lymphocyte count. Some of the independent variables were defined as (1) smoking history: past or current smoking; (2) alcohol history: alcohol consumption in the previous year; and (3) body mass index (BMI): weight (kg)/height (m2)], underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), and overweight and above (≥ 23.0 kg/m2)23.

Depression

The Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (GDS-15)24, developed by Yesavage et al.25, was utilized to evaluate depression in older adults living with HIV. This scale serves as a specialized tool for screening depression, specifically in older adults and was revised in 198626. It consists of 15 items, with items 1, 5, 7, 11, and 13 scored as 1 point for a “no” response and the remaining items scored as 1 point for a “yes” response. The total score ranges from 0 to 15, and a score of ≥ 8 indicates the presence of depression. This scale is widely used among the older adult population in China27.In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.793.

Cognitive function

Cognitive function in older adults living with HIV was assessed via the Beijing version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), developed by Yu et al.28. The assessment includes seven dimensions: visuospatial and executive function, naming, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation, with a total score of 30 points. For individuals with ≤ 12 years of education, 1 point is added to the total score for correction,and a score of < 26 is considered indicative of cognitive impairment29. After validation in the older Chinese population.The scale has been widely used in patients living with HIV30.The Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.830.

Social support

The social support rating scale (SSRS), developed by Xiao31, was used to assess the level of social support among older adults living with HIV. This scale consists of three dimensions and a total of 10 items: objective support (3 items), subjective support (4 items), and support utilization (3 items). The total score ranges from 12 to 66, with scores of 0–22 indicating low social support, 23–44 indicating moderate social support, and 45–66 indicating satisfactory social support. Its effectiveness has been confirmed in older adults living with HIV in China32. The Cronbach’s α for the scale items in this study ranged from 0.890 to 0.940, indicating good reliability and validity.

Sleep quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse et al.33, was used to assess the sleep quality of older adults living with HIV over the past month. It was adapted into Chinese by Liu et al.34. The scale consists of seven dimensions with 19 items and five scores rated by cohabitants (these are not included in the total score). The scale uses a Likert 4-point scoring system, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21 points. A PSQI score > 7 indicates the presence of sleep disorders. The Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.83, with a test–retest Cronbach’s α of 0.85. Its sensitivity was 89.6%, and its specificity was 86.5%. After validation in the Chinese population, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.84234. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.838, demonstrating good reliability and validity.

Data collection

To ensure the quality of the questionnaire, the investigators underwent professional training. Following the acquisition of informed consent from participants, the research team distributed paper questionnaires or used Questionnaire Star to collect demographic data, and information on depression, cognitive function, sleep quality, and social support from patients. Patients answered the questions themselves, avoiding suggestive language. Clinical disease information was obtained by researchers who reviewed patients’ outpatient medical records or case management systems. For patients who were illiterate or unable to fill out the forms independently, the investigators read each survey item aloud and recorded their responses accordingly.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the data was conducted via SPSS 29.0 software (Version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, 2022).Qualitative data are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas quantitative data are expressed as medians and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. For comparisons of metric data that do not follow a normal distribution between different groups, nonparametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis H test) should be used. For further pairwise comparisons between groups, Dunn’s test was used. Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships among depression, social support, sleep quality, and cognitive function. A multi-mediation analysis approach was adopted to construct a model incorporating these variables: one independent variable, one dependent variable, and two mediating variables. In this model, depression is identified as the independent variable and cognitive function as the dependent variable. Social support and sleep quality serve as pathways linking depression to cognitive function. Process (Model 6) was used to assess total, direct, and indirect mediation effects. A bootstrap method with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was applied to evaluate the significance of regression coefficients for estimating chain mediation effects based on 5000 samples. If the 95% CI does not include zero and P < 0.001 is observed, a statiscally significant difference is indicated.

Results

Common method bias test

To mitigate potential methodological bias, the Harman single factor test was employed for the prefactor analysis of all variables in this study35. The results indicate that 10 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were identified, with the first factor accounting for an explanatory variance of 27.75%, which falls below the critical threshold of 40.00%. Consequently, it can be concluded that the findings of this study are not influenced by common methodological biases.

Among the 351 older adults living with HIV, 235 (66.95%) were male and 116 (33.05%) were female, with an average age of (65.39 ± 8.87) years. A significant proportion of the patients (57.26%) resided in rural areas, and nearly half had received only primary school education or less (50.14%). Additionally, 38.20% of the participants reported being divorced or widowed, while the average monthly family income ranged from 1000 to 3000 units of currency. Furthermore, a history of alcohol consumption was noted in 41.9% of the patients; more than half had a history of smoking. Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine the relationships among depression, social support, sleep quality, and cognitive function in older adults living with HIV. The findings indicated that depression exerted a direct predictive effect on cognitive function, accounting for 35.35% of the total effect observed. Furthermore, social support and sleep quality served as mediators in the relationship between depression and cognitive function; collectively, their mediating effects accounted for 64.65% of the total effect. Hypotheses 1 through 4 proposed in this study were supported by the results obtained.

This study demonstrated that depression in older adults living with HIV is negatively correlated with cognitive function, aligning with findings from numerous prior studies16. Depressive symptoms attributed to older adults living with HIV damage the critical cortical striatum, leading to a decrease in neuroplasticity in the brain which promotes cognitive decline, contributing to abnormalities in the brain’s signaling system, and inducing cognitive dysfunction36. Furthermore, chronic low-grade inflammation and cell-mediated immune activation represent significant mechanisms associated with both depression and cognitive decline among older adults living with HIV37. This phenomenon partly explains why the prevalence of depression and cognitive impairment is higher in patients with HIV compared to their non-infected counterparts. Therefore, it can be inferred that targeted psychological interventions may help mitigate cognitive decline in older adults living with HIV.

Social support encompasses both spiritual and material assistance provided by various societal entities, significantly influencing an individual’s physical and mental well-being38. The study revealed that social support partially mediated the relationship between depression and cognitive function in older adults living with HIV. Stigma, fatigue, worthlessness, shame, and fear of HIV exposure, isolation, and hopelessness attributed to HIV infection may further exacerbate depressive symptoms and an unwillingness to initiate help leading to inadequate social support39. A chronic lack of companionship and low social engagement in patients with low levels of social support exacerbates cognitive impairment in older adults living with HIV, whereas social interaction can mitigate the adverse effects of stress on the central nervous system and protect cognitive function40. Therefore, facilitating access to support from families, healthcare providers, communities, and other resources is essential for promoting healthy aging.

This study elucidates the critical mediating role of sleep quality in the relationship between depression and cognitive function. These findings indicate a significant positive correlation between depression and sleep quality, which aligns with the findings of previous research41. On the one hand, HIV infection can impact the central nervous system (CNS) of affected individuals through various mechanisms. For example, HIV replication within the CNS may lead to immune activation and inflammatory responses that damage CNS neurons, compromise the integrity of white matter in the brain, and result in depressive symptoms42. This neuroinflammatory environment not only exacerbates depressive symptoms directly but also disrupts circadian rhythm regulation by interfering with the hypothalamic-suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), leading to reduced deep sleep duration and increased sleep fragmentation43. Furthermore, this study revealed that sleep quality among older adults living with HIV was negatively correlated with cognitive function, corroborating earlier research findings44. Insufficient sleep is associated with decreased secretion of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which is crucial for memory consolidation and neural repair; consequently, this deficiency adversely affects cognitive ability45. In summary, heightened levels of depression may negatively influence cognitive function in older adults by impairing sleep quality. These results suggest that enhancing sleep quality could mitigate some aspects of the cognitive decline associated with depression in this population. Therefore, it is imperative for healthcare professionals to prioritize improving the sleep quality of older adults living with HIV while actively assessing their psychological well-being to prevent further deterioration of their cognitive function.

In this study, social support and sleep quality were found to play a chain mediating role in the relationship between depression and cognitive function among older adults living with HIV. Under the influence of negative emotions, the reluctance of older adults living with HIV to engage in social activities disengages patients from their core support network and weakens their ability to cope with the triple stressor of negative physical and mental conditions due to aging, and the monitoring and treatment of complications after prolonged ART46. It causes patients to experience decreased sleep quality, which further exacerbates cognitive decline. Therefore, prioritizing social support and enhancing sleep quality while addressing depressive symptoms in older adults living with HIV can significantly improve patients’ cognitive function. This approach represents a crucial strategy for promoting healthy aging within this vulnerable population.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, this study was conducted only locally in Guizhou, and the reproducibility of the results is unclear. Second, the data collected is cross-sectional; thus, conclusions can only be interpreted statistically. Longitudinal data may be necessary to further investigate this relationship. Third, the relatively small sample size raises concerns about potential bias. Therefore, future research should employ a longitudinal approach and utilize a larger multi-center sample to comprehensively examine the intricate relationships among depression, sleep quality, social support, and cognitive function in older adults living with HIV.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that depressive symptoms in older adults living with HIV not only serve as direct predictors of cognitive function status but also influence cognitive function through the mediating effects of social support and sleep quality. It is recommended that it is essential to establish a collaborative management platform utilizing blockchain technology to enhance the social support network for this population to improve the quality of sleep. This initiative aims to mitigate the stigma associated with older adults living with HIV, reduce the incidence of depression, and slow cognitive decline. Such strategies are crucial in addressing the policy requirements outlined in the “China AIDS Prevention and Control Plan (2024–2030)” and contribute significantly towards achieving the “Healthy China 2030” objectives within vulnerable groups amid an aging society. Moreover, these efforts represent an important pathway toward realizing the “95-95-95” targets by 2025.
