A multiple instance learning framework for estradiol level classification in TCT whole slide images
Authors: Yangzi Feng, Feng Shi, Yanbin Wang, Ying Gao, Wenpei Bai, Lei Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEstradiol level assessment is critical for the management of gynecological endocrine disorders. The current gold standard, serum hormone testing, is limited by its invasiveness, variability due to physiological fluctuations, and limited accessibility in primary care settings. This study investigates the feasibility of using Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) algorithms for classification of estradiol levels using routinely collected Thinprep Cytologic Test (TCT) whole slide images (WSIs), which adds no additional procedure when TCT is already indicated.MethodsThis retrospective study included 1,171 patient samples with paired TCT images and serum estradiol data. Samples were categorized into positive (n = 713) and negative (n = 458) groups using a threshold of 40 pg/mL. Each WSI was treated as a “bag,” with extracted image patches constituting “instances.” Under a weakly supervised MIL model, the binary classification performance of four models (AB-MIL, DS-MIL, Trans-MIL, and DTFD-MIL) for estradiol levels was systematically evaluated. A nested cross-validation with a fixed test set was used. We further conducted confounder analyses, multi-threshold validation, decision curve analysis, and a cross-batch generalizability test.ResultsAB-MIL achieved the highest performance: test accuracy 0.706 ± 0.048, macro-AUC 0.747 ± 0.024, macro-F1 0.692 ± 0.043. PR-AUC was 0.812. At the 0.5 operating point, sensitivity was 83.9%, specificity 64.1%. The combined clinical‑image model (age + FSH+AB‑MIL) achieved an AUC of 0.787 (NRI = 0.123, IDI = 0.0976). Multi-threshold (60,80,100 pg/mL) AUCs remained stable (0.679–0.710). Cross-batch testing revealed expected performance decay (AUC 0.611–0.631).ConclusionThis exploratory study demonstrates the technical feasibility of using an MIL-based model to infer estradiol status from cervical cell morphology in TCT WSIs. The findings provide preliminary evidence that AI analysis of routine cytology images could serve as a potential screening adjunct tool. This work presents a methodological framework for future development of endocrine assessment using routinely collected TCT samples, pending further validation and refinement, to be potentially integrated with cervical cancer screening workflows for concurrent evaluation.

Introduction

Estradiol plays a pivotal role in female physiology and the pathogenesis of various gynecological diseases. Accurate assessment of its levels is essential for managing perimenopausal syndrome, diagnosing and treating endocrine-related disorders, and monitoring estrogen-dependent tumors. Serum hormone testing remains the clinical gold standard for evaluating estradiol levels. However, this method has several drawbacks: its invasiveness can reduce patient compliance; diurnal rhythms and short-term fluctuations mean that single measurements may not fully capture longer-term trends, though they remain the clinical standard for immediate assessment [1, 2]; repeated testing imposes a significant economic burden on patients requiring long-term monitoring; and the need for sophisticated laboratory platforms limits its application in primary care settings. Consequently, there is an urgent clinical need for a convenient, reliable, and highly accessible assessment method.

The Vaginal Maturation Index (VMI) offers an alternative approach by indirectly reflecting endogenous estrogen levels through the assessment of the relative proportions of parabasal, intermediate, and superficial cells in vaginal cytology samples [3]. Compared to serum testing, VMI represents the net effect of biologically active hormones and is less susceptible to short-term variations [4]. However, the clinical utility of VMI is severely constrained by its strong dependence on manual microscopy. The process of observing and classifying at least 300 cells per sample is tedious and time-consuming, resulting in low testing efficiency. Furthermore, diagnostic consistency is highly dependent on examiner’s expertise, resulting in substantial subjectivity and poor reproducibility, which hinders large-scale clinical implementation.

Based on the anatomical continuity of the reproductive tract, we hypothesize that the cervical transformation zone shares embryonic homology with the upper vagina. Consequently, cervical epithelial cells may exhibit hormone-responsive characteristics analogous to vaginal cells. The relative proportions of epithelial cell types in cervical samples obtained from routine TCT could potentially serve to assess hormonal status. Validation of this hypothesis would significantly enhance the utility of routine TCT samples, enabling concurrent use for cervical cancer screening and hormonal status assessment, thereby offering a novel pathway for endocrine evaluation, that adds no additional procedure when TCT is already indicated for cervical cancer screening. Furthermore, the potential for patient self-sampling of TCT specimens could greatly enhance the accessibility of endocrine assessment in primary care or resource-limited regions.

Advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) provide promising solutions to the limitations of traditional cytomorphological analysis. In digital pathology, deep learning models have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in identifying and classifying malignant cells in cervical cytology [5]. Nevertheless, the intelligent analysis of endocrine-related cytomorphological features remains largely unexplored. Applying AI to TCT images for hormone level prediction poses a significant challenge: the extremely high resolution of WSIs makes precise annotation of individual cells or regions prohibitively costly and impractical. To address this challenge, we formulate the task as a binary MIL problem. MIL, as a weakly supervised learning paradigm, is particularly suited to this context. It requires only slide-level (bag-level) labels (i.e., a single estradiol level per WSI) without any fine-grained local annotations, substantially reducing labeling costs and better aligning with clinical workflows.

Therefore, this retrospective study systematically evaluates the performance of four representative MIL models (AB-MIL, DS-MIL, Trans-MIL, DTFD-MIL) in the binary classification of serum estradiol levels using a real-world clinical TCT image dataset. This exploratory research aims to validate the technical feasibility of predicting estradiol levels based on cervical cell morphology and to provide preliminary evidence for future development of clinical endocrine assessment tools that could be integrated with existing screening workflows.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

Ethical approval

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Shijitan Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No.: IIT2025-037). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the research. All patient data were anonymized and de-identified to protect privacy. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient selection

Female patients aged 18–75 years who attended the Gynecology Outpatient Department of Beijing Shijitan Hospital between January 2010 and January 2024 were eligible for inclusion if they underwent concurrent Human Papillomavirus (HPV) testing, TCT, and serum reproductive hormone profiling during the same clinical visit. Exclusion criteria comprised: HPV infection; TCT report indicating≥Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASC-US); acute reproductive tract or urinary tract infection; thyroid dysfunction; abnormal tumor markers; and current hormone replacement therapy. Postmenopausal status is defined as age ≥ 45 years and FSH ≥ 20 mIU/mL. Finally, a total of 1,171 patients were enrolled.

Data acquisition and quality control

TCT preparation protocol

Cervical samples were collected using a broom-shaped brush, placed in PreservCyt solution (Hologic), processed via the ThinPrep 2000 system to create a monolayer cell slide (4 × 2 cm), and stained with Papanicolaou stain.

WSI scanning

All TCT slides were scanned using a KF-PRO-005 digital slide scanner at 20×objective magnification (0.25 μm/pixel resolution) in brightfield mode. The scanner has a field of view of 1.5 mm×1.5 mm and a scanning time of 15s.

Quality control for WSIs

Low-quality slides were excluded based on the following criteria (assessed by two independent cytologists, with disagreements resolved by a third senior cytologist): Low cellularity: <5000 nucleated epithelial cells per slide; Heavy contamination: >50% of the slide area covered by blood, inflammation, mucus, or debris; Out-of-focus regions: >20% of the slide area with blurred cell morphology; Artifacts: air bubbles, scratch marks, or staining errors that obscure cell morphology. All TCT WSIs met the preset professional quality standards.

Data preprocessing and feature extraction

All WSIs stored in the .kfb format within this dataset were read. Subsequently, the Otsu thresholding algorithm was applied to extract tissue regions from the WSIs. This study defined the tissue foreground in cytological images through grayscale inversion preprocessing combined with 16 × 16 pixel block aggregation analysis. First, the WSI thumbnails (downsampled by a factor of 32) were converted to grayscale images and inverted. After obtaining a binary image via Otsu threshold segmentation, it is divided into 16 × 16 pixel blocks (corresponding to a 512 × 512 pixel area in the original image). Only regions where the proportion of foreground pixels exceeds 20% within a block are classified as tissue foreground. Their original image coordinates are recorded for subsequent plaque segmentation, enabling precise differentiation between foreground (tissue regions) and background (blank areas). Following this foreground filtering strategy, the average number of valid tissue foreground patches retained per TCT slide was 5,910.52. Background patch removal rates exhibited a gradient distribution: minimum 25.73%, maximum 99.79%, with an average of 71.14%. Hierarchical analysis based on background removal rates revealed: approximately 30% of slides exhibited high tissue content (background removal < 40%), 40% showed moderate tissue content (40%-70% background removal), and 30% were low-tissue/high-background slides (background removal > 70%). After foreground filtering and sliding window partitioning, all remaining valid tissue/cellular foreground patches were processed using an exhaustive strategy during both model training and inference: after background removal, every valid foreground patch from each slide was retained in its entirety, without any sampling, truncation, or weighting. All feature vectors from these patches were input in full to the MIL model for feature aggregation.

This exhaustive patch usage has important implications for both computational feasibility and feature representation. By preserving all tissue-containing patches, the approach ensures the integrity of slide‑level feature information and maximizes the retention of spatial characteristics of cells and tissue structures, enabling the model to learn representations that more faithfully reflect the true morphological features of TCT slides. Concurrently, we addressed the increased computational burden associated with exhaustive processing through hardware optimization strategies such as batch loading and GPU parallel computing, thereby ensuring experimental feasibility without compromising training or inference efficiency. To comprehensively capture image blocks containing tissue structures, a 1024 × 1024 pixel sliding window was further employed. Within identified tissue regions, grid divisions were performed with a 512-pixel stride. This stepping strategy achieves 50% overlap between image blocks, creating high coverage. Its core purpose is to ensure complete coverage of tissue regions while maximizing the spatial continuity of cells and tissue structures to avoid fragmentation of critical details (e.g., cell arrangement, gland morphology). Simultaneously, it achieves reasonable control over information redundancy through a fixed overlap ratio, thereby balancing structural integrity with data efficiency.

To obtain more discriminative feature representations, this study did not employ general models pre-trained on natural image datasets. Instead, it selected the UNI model [6], pre-trained on large-scale medical images. As an advanced foundational model for medical vision, UNI learns feature representations with an enhanced capability to capture subtle morphological variations in pathological images. Prior to feature extraction, each image patch undergoes pixel-level normalization using the mean and standard deviation from the ImageNet dataset. The normalized patches are fed into the UNI model, and after removing its classification head, a 1024-dimensional output feature vector corresponding to each patch is extracted. This study employs a stratified sampling strategy for dataset partitioning, strictly adhering to a patient-based splitting principle. This ensures that all slices from the same patient remain confined within the training/validation/test sets, fundamentally preventing data leakage that could lead to overestimation of model performance. A nested cross-validation architecture is adopted: first, 20% of the total data is fixed as an independent test set, while the remaining 80% is used for 5-fold cross-validation. Within the 5-fold cross-validation, each fold is further divided into training and validation sets at an 8:2 ratio. Ultimately, the entire dataset is partitioned into three subsets: 64% training, 16% validation, and 20% independent test. The dataset comprises 1,171 TCT slides: 713 positive samples (60.9%) with estradiol levels > 40 pg/mL and 458 negative samples (39.1%) with estradiol levels ≤ 40 pg/mL, exhibiting significant category imbalance. The number of slides and category distribution in each subset strictly matched the overall distribution: the training set comprised 749 slides (456 positive, 293 negative), the 5-fold validation set contained 187 slides (114 positive, 73 negative), and an independent test set comprising 235 slices (143 positive, 92 negative). The design of stratified sampling and nested cross-validation ensures balanced distribution of each category across all subsets, thereby guaranteeing the reliability and statistical significance of model evaluation.

Multi-instance learning model

Model selection and evaluation framework

To systematically evaluate MIL performance for estradiol prediction, we selected four representative models covering key technological paradigms: AB-MIL (Attention-Based MIL) [7],: The foundational attention mechanism model, which learns instance importance weights via a differentiable attention network, aligning well with the “key regions dominate prediction” characteristic of TCT slides.

DS-MIL (Dual-Stream MIL) [8] : Employs dual branches (key instance stream + context stream) to capture both local salient features and global contextual information.Trans-MIL [9] : Adapts the Transformer encoder to MIL, modeling long-range dependencies and pairwise associations among instances via self-attention.

DTFD-MIL (Double-Tier Feature Distillation Multiple Instance Learning) [10] : Builds on AB-MIL with a hierarchical “pseudo-bag generation – feature distillation – aggregation” framework, designed to mitigate overfitting in WSI classification.

Core model concepts

AB-MIL introduces an end-to-end trainable attention network that dynamically weights instances and aggregates them via weighted summation, focusing computational resources on key discriminative regions.

DS-MIL synergistically combines a key instance stream (max‑pooling for local saliency) and a context stream (self‑attention for global relationships), then fuses both streams to generate robust bag features.

Trans-MIL integrates a learnable class token, square‑pads the instance sequence, and feeds it into a Transformer encoder. The Nyström approximation reduces complexity from O(n²) to O(n). A Pyramid Position Encoding Generator (PPEG) captures multi‑granularity spatial information. The core attention calculation enables explicit inter‑instance modeling.

DTFD-MIL first generates random pseudo‑bags (subsets) from the original bag, then applies a two‑tier AB‑MIL: Tier‑1 learns instance weights within each pseudo‑bag; Tier‑2 aggregates distilled pseudo‑bag features to produce the final bag‑level prediction. This enhances feature capture for sparse positive regions.

Implementation details and training strategy

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch 1.12.1 and conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 Ti GPU to ensure a consistent hardware/software environment. The four MIL models (AB-MIL, DS-MIL, Trans-MIL, DTFD-MIL) were reproduced from publicly available third-party repositories. To eliminate the influence of input feature dimensionality, the feature dimension was uniformly set to 1024, matching the output of the UNI feature extractor. Core architectural parameters for each model followed the default configurations recommended in their original publications, guaranteeing a fair benchmark.

A unified training strategy was applied across all models. The Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁶ and L2 weight decay of 5 × 10⁻⁷. The learning rate was adjusted dynamically via ReduceLROnPlateau, halving the rate when validation loss did not decrease for 10 consecutive epochs. The loss function was standard cross-entropy. Training ran for a maximum of 200 epochs with early stopping (patience = 10) based on validation loss; the model checkpoint achieving the best validation performance was restored after early stopping to prevent overfitting.

To ensure rigorous and reproducible evaluation, the independent test set (20% of total data) was fixed at the beginning and evaluated only once at the end of the study, after all model training, hyperparameter tuning, and 5‑fold cross‑validation were completed. All model selection and early stopping decisions were based solely on the 5‑fold cross‑validation performed on the remaining 80% of data (training + validation). Within each cross‑validation fold, the data were split into training (64% of total) and validation (16% of total). The validation sets were used for early stopping and model selection but were not included in the final performance statistics of the independent test set. Confounder bias control and incremental value analysis

To verify whether the image model is affected by confounding bias from age and menopausal status, we designed a systematic incremental value analysis framework.

Clinical baseline model

A logistic regression model was trained using age and FSH (key confounders) as Input, both Z-score standardized. The target variable aligned with the imaging model. The model was trained on the full training set and evaluated on the same independent test set (n = 235) used for the image models to ensure direct comparability.

Combined clinical-image model

To quantify the incremental value of image features, we constructed a joint model: training a new logistic regression classifier using the predicted probabilities from both the clinical baseline model (pclinical) and the AB-MIL image model (pimage) as a two-dimensional feature vector, and a logistic regression model is trained to predict the same estradiol classification labels.

Incremental value metrics

The following statistical methods were employed to evaluate incremental value:DeLong’s test: Comparing the AUC difference between the clinical model and the combined model.Net Receiver Operating Characteristic Improvement Index (NRI): Calculating classification improvement from the clinical model to the combined model.Integrated Discrimination Improvement Index (IDI): Assessing the overall enhancement in discriminatory capability.Decision Curve Analysis (DCA): Evaluating net benefit in clinical decision-making.

Combined model performance

To quantify the incremental contribution of image-derived features, we constructed a combined model by training a logistic regression classifier using the predicted probabilities from both the clinical baseline model and the AB-MIL image model as input features.

As shown in Table 7, the combined model achieved the best overall performance (AUC = 0.787, 95% CI: 0.735–0.839). Critically, while maintaining reasonable sensitivity (0.913), it improved specificity from 0.227 (clinical baseline) to 0.443, and increased PPV from 0.634 to 0.700 (NPV = 0.782). This shift transforms the model from a “high-sensitivity but low-specificity” screening tool into a more balanced diagnostic aid, demonstrating the clinical utility of the image-derived features.

Incremental value metrics

To further quantify the incremental value contributed by image‑derived features, we employed several statistical metrics and visualization tools.

NRI

The combined model demonstrated an NRI of 0.123 compared to the clinical baseline model, indicating that 12.3% of patients were correctly reclassified into more accurate risk categories, with the improvement primarily driven by better classification of positive cases (high estradiol).

IDI

The IDI was 0.0976, confirming that the addition of image features provided independent discriminative information and enhanced the overall separation between the two outcome classes.

Discussion

This study successfully implemented an MIL model for estradiol level classification using cervical TCT WSIs, providing robust empirical support for the core hypothesis that cervical cell morphology reflects endogenous estradiol status. The results demonstrate a significant association between morphological features of cervical cells and serum estradiol levels. MIL models trained on routine TCT samples can predict serum estradiol levels, with the best-performing model achieving an AUC of 0.747, confirming the technical feasibility of this approach. While this level of discrimination is promising for an exploratory study, it is important to note that an AUC of 0.747 represents moderate diagnostic performance, underscoring its potential role as a screening adjunct or triage tool at this stage rather than a definitive diagnostic test. The principal strengths of this work lie in its clinical applicability and methodological innovation. Firstly, the use of routinely collected TCT specimens eliminates the need for additional invasive procedures, realizing the concept of “one sample, multiple uses.” Secondly, the innovative adoption of the weakly supervised MIL paradigm, which requires only slide-level labels, effectively overcoming the WSI annotation bottleneck and producing models more compatible with real-world workflows.

The selection of the 40 pg/mL serum estradiol threshold for classification is grounded in clinical rationale. This level represents a key threshold for maintaining normal physiological function in women. Estradiol levels above 40 pg/mL typically indicate sufficient ovarian function to support endometrial proliferation, vaginal mucosal health, and bone metabolism stability. Although lower thresholds (e.g., < 20–30 pg/mL) are often used to define biochemical menopause, the 40 pg/mL threshold was chosen here to potentially identify the early transition phase from reproductive age to menopause earlier, which could facilitate the timely management of perimenopausal symptoms. Importantly, we do not propose this model as a replacement for serum hormone testing; rather, we envision its future role as a low‑cost, scalable triage tool that could help clinicians decide whether serum estradiol measurement is warranted, particularly in primary care or resource‑limited settings.

In the model comparison, the outstanding performance of AB‑MIL demonstrates the effectiveness of the classical attention mechanism for this task. Its concise architecture employs adaptive weighted aggregation of instance features, which aligns well with the distribution of estradiol‑related morphological patterns in TCT slides—where key discriminative regions are likely sparse. AB‑MIL’s ability to precisely locate such key instances achieved the highest accuracy (0.706 ± 0.048), macro‑AUC (0.747 ± 0.024), and macro‑F1 (0.692 ± 0.043) among the four models. The robust performance of DTFD‑MIL (accuracy 0.677 ± 0.029, AUC 0.720 ± 0.023) highlights the value of hierarchical feature learning; although its peak metrics were slightly lower than AB‑MIL’s, its smaller standard deviations (e.g., accuracy SD 0.029, the smallest among all models) indicate greater stability across data subsets, supporting its potential for consistent real‑world application. In contrast, DS‑MIL (accuracy 0.690 ± 0.029, AUC 0.735 ± 0.018) and Trans‑MIL (accuracy 0.659 ± 0.057, AUC 0.721 ± 0.017) showed comparatively weaker performance. This likely reflects a trade‑off between model complexity and available training data: while their dual‑stream fusion and Transformer‑based global dependency modeling offer greater theoretical expressive power, under the limited data conditions of this single‑center study, such complex architectures may suffer from over‑parameterization and fail to fully leverage their advantages. From a clinical perspective, AB‑MIL’s combination of high overall accuracy and balanced class identification (sensitivity 83.9%, specificity 64.1% at the 0.5 operating point) aligns closely with the core requirements of diagnostic aids: precision and robustness.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the single-center retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our findings, underscoring the necessity for validation in multi-center, prospective cohorts. Second, the exclusion of patients with HPV infection or abnormal cytology (≥ ASC-US), while methodologically intended to reduce confounding, may restrict the model’s applicability to real-world screening populations where such conditions are prevalent. Third, the reliance on a binary classification with a fixed estradiol threshold precludes a nuanced, continuous assessment of hormone levels. Fourth, although confounder analyses were conducted, the learned morphological features likely reflect a composite of the broader endocrine environment correlated with estradiol, rather than a phenotype exclusive to estradiol itself. Fifth, the inherent biological fluctuation of serum estradiol means that a single timepoint measurement, used here as the ground truth, may not fully represent a patient’s stable endocrine state. The model therefore predicts a coarse, dichotomized proxy tied to that specific measurement. Sixth, the moderate performance (AUC 0.747) indicates substantial room for improvement, and the model’s efficacy in self-sampling scenarios remains to be systematically evaluated. Finally, the substantial performance drop in the cross-batch analysis (macro-AUC from 0.747 to 0.631) reveals that the current model is highly sensitive to domain shift (e.g., scanner, preparation batch).

Future work should focus on: prioritizing domain generalization techniques and external validation in multi-center cohorts; evaluating and optimizing the model in cohorts that include patients with HPV infection and abnormal cytology; exploring regression or multi-class modeling for more individualized monitoring; enhancing model interpretability by generating attention heatmaps for cytological expert review to validate captured morphological features; and systematically assessing the model’s effectiveness and reliability on self-sampled TCT images.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a MIL-based model for classifying estradiol levels using TCT WSIs. The findings indicate the technical feasibility of leveraging AI analysis of cervical cytology images for assessment of endogenous estradiol levels using routinely collected samples. Among the four representative MIL models evaluated, AB-MIL exhibited the best performance for this task. This research provides a novel methodological foundation and important exploratory evidence for the development of concurrently applicable auxiliary diagnostic tools for gynecological endocrine disorders alongside TCT screening.
