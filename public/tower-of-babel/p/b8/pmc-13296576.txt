Erenumab and OnabotulinumtoxinA in Chronic Migraine: A Comparative Analysis
Authors: María Garcés‐Pellejero, Marta Torres‐Ferrús, Rut Mas‐de‐les‐Valls, Edoardo Caronna, Alicia Alpuente, Eulalia Giné‐Ciprés, Patricia Pozo‐Rosich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo compare response (reduction in headache frequency and differences in patient‐reported outcomes) between Erenumab and OnabotulinumtoxinA from two phase‐IV trials after 6 months of treatment.Materials/MethodsA prospective cohort study included two parallel cohorts of chronic migraine patients treated with either Erenumab 140 mg/month or OnabotulinumtoxinA 195 U every 3 months. Participants had no additional preventive therapies or comorbid psychiatric or chronic pain conditions. Baseline data collected included demographics, monthly headache (MHD) and migraine days (MMD), impact scales (MIDAS, HIT‐6, MSQ), and anxiety/depression scores (BAI, BDI‐II). Assessments were repeated at 6 months. Baseline characteristics were balanced. Efficacy analysis (measured by reduction in MHD/MMD and impact scales) was conducted using paired t‐test, and linear regression models compared treatment‐related changes between groups.ResultsA total of 114 patients were analyzed (57 per group), with no significant baseline differences. Both treatments produced significant reductions in headache frequency at 6 months (Erenumab: −7.93 MHD and −5.36 MMD, p < 0.001; OnabotA: −6.00 MHD and −4.31 MMD, p < 0.001), without statistically significant differences between therapies. Significant improvements were observed in HIT‐6 and MSQ scores in both cohorts (p < 0.05). The Erenumab group showed significant reductions in MIDAS, BDI, and BAI scores (p < 0.05). Differences between treatments were significant for MIDAS improvements at three and 6 months (p < 0.05).ConclusionErenumab and OnabotulinumtoxinA are effective preventive treatments for chronic migraine. No significant differences were found in the magnitude of headache reduction at 3 or 6 months when comparing both treatments.Keywords: CGRP, chronic migraine, Erenumab, OnabotA, preventive treatments

Introduction

Chronic migraine (CM), with a global prevalence of 0.5%–2.4%, constitutes a significant public health challenge [1, 2]. It substantially impairs quality of life and imposes economic and social burdens due to reduced work productivity and high treatment costs [3].

Preventive treatment aims to reduce headache frequency and severity. Oral non‐specific preventives are widely used but poorly tolerated, leading to low adherence (only 14% of patients continue treatment at 1 year) [4].

OnabotulinumtoxinA (OnabotA) and Erenumab (a migraine‐specific monoclonal antibody antagonizing CGRP) have shown long‐term efficacy and safety in reducing headache frequency, analgesic use, and migraine‐related disability [5, 6]. Both outperformed topiramate in head‐to‐head randomized trials [7, 8]. At week 32, 40% of OnabotA patients achieved ≥ 50% reduction in monthly headache days (MHD) vs. 12% with topiramate, with fewer discontinuations due to side effects (1% vs. 42%) [7]. With Erenumab, 55.4% achieved a ≥ 50% reduction in monthly migraine days (MMD) vs. 31.2% with topiramate, also with fewer dropouts (10.6% vs. 38.9%) [8]. Thus, clinical and real‐world evidence supports OnabotA and Erenumab as effective and safe preventive treatments for CM, both showing better tolerability than non‐specific oral options [4].

Neither real‐world evidence nor clinical trials have directly compared the efficacy and safety of well‐tolerated CM preventive treatments, such as OnabotulinumtoxinA and CGRP‐targeting therapies, neither in randomized‐controlled clinical trials nor in real‐world. Our study aims to compare their efficacy, via reduction in headache frequency and patient‐reported outcomes (PROs), after 6 months of treatment.

Materials and Methods

This is a prospective cohort study comparing two cohorts of CM patients enrolled in independent phase IV prospective clinical trials of Erenumab (EudraCT 2019–002224‐32, 2020–003650‐77) or OnabotA (EudraCT 2020–002639‐31).

Inclusion criteria were standardized for all trials; adults 18–65 years with CM according to the ICHD‐3. Patients under concomitant preventives or with psychiatric/chronic pain comorbidities were excluded. Prior treatment with OnabotulinumtoxinA (OnabotA) in the Erenumab group was not considered an exclusion criterion, as it was required in order to meet the reimbursement criteria. Participants received Erenumab 140 mg monthly or OnabotA 195UI quarterly (PREEMPT protocol) [9] for ≥ 6 months.

Baseline demographics, headache history, frequency (MHD, MMD), prior preventives, PROs (MIDAS, HIT‐6, MSQ, BAI, BDI‐II) were collected at baseline, 3 and 6 months.

Subgroups were matched by age, sex, and baseline headache frequency. Indirect comparations were performed between cohorts. All statistical analysis was conducted in R version 4.4.2., figures created using the package ggplot2 and p‐values adjusted using FDR. Frequencies (%) described nominal variables; medians IQRs for quantitative. Normality checked with Shapiro–Wilk test; Chi‐square tested categorical variables, t‐tests for continuous and MIDAS showed unequal variances among treatments, so Welch test was used.

After three and 6 months of treatment, a paired t‐test was used to analyze the statistical significance of pre‐post reductions in headache/migraine frequency and acute medication use. Linear models evaluated inter‐group differences. A sub‐analysis was conducted on a subset of patients from each cohort, using matched‐pair comparisons to evaluate changes in PROs scores. This analysis used linear models for treatment type and duration interaction.

Discussion

The current landscape of preventive migraine treatment includes specific and non‐specific pharmacological treatments, as well as neuromodulation options, all demonstrating efficacy and safety [10]. However, there are significant differences in response profiles and tolerability. Newer treatments, such as OnabotA or anti‐CGRP therapies, are subject to regulatory restrictions on access and/or funding by healthcare systems, with recommendations typically made when other non‐specific treatments fail [11]. We lack sufficient evidence from comparative trials to prioritize therapeutic options based on efficacy and safety, which could replace current reimbursement criteria focused on pharmacoeconomics criteria.

We present the first study comparing two arms of a phase 4 trial with similar inclusion criteria, comparing advanced treatments for CM prevention. Both treatments have shown efficacy and tolerability in previous studies [4, 5, 6]. However, there are currently no studies that have evaluated differences between them. This analysis provides additional evidence to support therapeutic decision‐making.

Our results show that Erenumab and OnabotA significantly reduce headache frequency, with no significant differences in the magnitude, migraine days, or speed of onset, suggesting both could be first‐line options based on patient's factors, costs, and accessibility.

Regarding secondary outcome variables, both reduce monthly analgesic use and improve quality of life.

A noteworthy finding in our study is that patients on OnabotA did not show significant changes in the MIDAS disability scale, contrary to previous literature from clinical trials [12] or real‐world studies previously published by our group [13, 14]. Upon reviewing the characteristics of the OnabotA cohort, we found considerable deviation in the baseline MIDAS scores, which, combined with the small sample size, may explain the lack of significant differences in mean scores.

Our study has limitations. While the groups are balanced, the small sample size limits detection of subtle differences between treatments. A 6‐month follow‐up duration may not fully capture long‐term benefits. No direct adherence assessment; we estimate, based on published literature, that adherence is likely high [15, 16]. The longer disease duration in the Erenumab group could potentially influence the perception of results.

An important limitation of this study is that the comparison was conducted between cohorts derived from three independent clinical trials rather than within a single randomized head‐to‐head design. Although inclusion criterial, follow‐up, and outcome measure were comparable, this indirect comparision may introduce potential biases related to patient selection and study‐specific factors. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as they do not derive from a randomized head‐to –head comparision.

Strengths include prospective cohort design with identical inclusion criteria, allowing for systematic and comparative follow‐up, absence of comorbidities that frequently introduce bias, cohort matching ensures comparability between the groups, and comprehensive PROs evaluations including disability and depression/anxiety scales, providing a comprehensive view of the treatment impact.

Given the low likelihood of future randomized controlled trials comparing preventive treatments, the results of our study support a treatment landscape where the choices of therapy can be made more freely, as both represent first‐line therapeutic options based on the individual profile and preferences of the migraine patient. A cost‐effectiveness analysis could be useful in exploring the differences between the two treatments.

Conclusion

Erenumab and onabotulinumtoxinA are effective preventive treatments for CM, both significantly reducing headache frequency after 6 months. No significant differences were found in the magnitude of headache reduction at 3 or 6 months when comparing both treatments. This study supports the concept of personalized treatment tailored to the patient's individual needs and characteristics.
