Biodegradation of chlorpyrifos by the newly isolated Escherichia fergusonii and Clostridium bifermentans: Identification and growth optimization for bioremediation
Authors: Tesfahun Lamboro Lagiso, Adugna Abdi Woldesemayat, Zerihun Belay Gemta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chlorpyrifos, widely used in Ethiopia despite restricted official status, contaminates soil and water, posing environmental and health risks. Microbial bioremediation offers a natural, eco-friendly solution for its removal from contaminated sites. This study aimed to identify bacteria from Meki and Wonji-shoa agricultural soil, capable of degrading CP and optimize their growth conditions, such as pH, temperature, and CP concentration using enrichment technique. With a UV-VIS Spectrophotometer (600 nm), for the strains growth response and (290 nm) for degradation of chlorpyrifos were observed every two days. Based on their tolerance to high CP concentration and degradation capacity, two isolates out of eight potential strains were selected, identified using biochemical characterization and 16 S r RNA sequencing to be Escherichia fergusonii LB50 and Clostridium bifermentans PS1. The bacterial strains isolated in the current study were not previously reported in CP degradation. Their efficacy of CP degradation further confirmed by GC-MS. These isolates were able to degrade 59.34% (Escherichia fergusonii) and 70.82% (Clostridium bifermentans) of the initial concentration of chlorpyrifos (50 mg L− 1) within a period of 20 days. In both strains, glucose, as an additional carbon source, significantly increased the degradation process and dissipate > 99% of the initial CP concentration (P < 0.05). The optimum growth conditions for the isolates were determined as pH 5.0, 30 °C for Clostridium bifermentans, and 35 °C for Escherichia fergusonii, with a chlorpyrifos concentration of 50 mg L⁻¹. Under these conditions, both strains exhibited significant growth and chlorpyrifos degradation activity (p < 0.05). Although this study offers promising microbial candidates for bioremediation, further investigation is required to determine the effectiveness of these organisms and their ecological resilience within contaminated environments prior to their practical application in remediation strategies.

Introduction

Pesticides are essential in modern agriculture for protecting crops from weeds, pests, and diseases, thereby increasing yield and minimizing losses1.Their use has surged globally to meet rising demands for food and raw materials amid population growth and industrial expansion2. In Ethiopia, pesticide consumption rose from 206 tons in 2000 to 4128 ton in 2020 positioning the country among Africa’s largest users with 4.5 million hectares treated in the 2020-21 season ;3. Over 50% of registered pesticides are Highly Hazardous and widespread and often improper application threatens human health and environmental integrity4,5.

Chlorpyrifos (CP), an organophosphate acaridine with the chemical formula O, O-diethyl O-(3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridyl phosphorrothioate), accounts for 38% of global pesticide usage and is widely applied in agricultural and non-agricultural settings6,7. Its excessive application has resulted in contamination of soil, air and water, leading to residue accumulation in crops and ecosystems, which cause ecological stress and poses risk to non-target organisms and human health6,8–11. In Ethiopia, chlorpyrifos contamination is a major concern, particularly in regions with intensive agricultural use. High concentrations have been detected in surface and ground water from highland watersheds and central Ethiopian ground water sources, underscoring the threat to both environmental integrity and public health5,12.

Chlorpyrifos exhibits a higher soil absorption coefficient and lower water solubility (2 mg L− 1), leading to its detection in groundwater as far as 24 km from its application site13. The half-life of chlorpyrifos (CP) in soil typically falls between 10 and 120 days, but it can extend up to a year depending on abiotic factors like temperature, moisture, and pH14.

Various physical, chemical, and biological methods have been applied for chlorpyrifos removal15–17 Among these, biodegradation is thought to be the most successful, eco-friendly, and economically feasible method for cleaning up pesticide-polluted environments18. A Study has shown that the biodegradation of chlorpyrifos results in the accumulation of 3, 5, 6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCP) and diethyl thiophosphoric acid (DETA)19.Using catabolic and co-metabolic methods, multiple research groups have examined the capacities of different bacteria in the biodegradation of chlorpyrifos, some of these bacteria includes; Pseudomonas sp., Serratia, Enterobacter, Alcaligenes, Sphingobacterium, Gordonia, Paracoccus, and Mesorhizobium1920,2128.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have identified or characterized indigenous microorganisms capable of degrading chlorpyrifos in Ethiopian agricultural soils, despite its widespread use. The impact of biotic and abiotic factors on microbial degradation highlights the need to explore and characterize diverse biodegrading microbes22.This will enhance the development of effective bioremediation technologies suited to local environmental conditions23. Thus, this study aims to isolate and characterize native bacterial strains from chlorpyrifos-contaminated fields, assess their degradation potential under optimized abiotic conditions through repetitive enrichment and selective culturing. In addition to this, the study also examined the isolates utilization of chlorpyrifos in the presence of glucose as a co-substrate24.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Chlorpyrifos (50% EC, commercial grade insecticide) was purchased from CHEMTEX PLC via a local market in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade (≥ 99% purity) and obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA)25.

Soil sampling

Soil samples were collected from intensively farmed sugarcane and vegetable fields in the Meki (coordinates: 8°08′N, 38°49′E; elevation ~ 1640 m a.s.l.) and Wonji-Shoa (coordinates: 8°05′N, 39°01′E; elevation ~ 1550 m a.s.l.) areas of Ethiopia’s Oromia region, located in the central East African Rift Valley. These sites have received continuous pesticide applications for over a decade, representing some of the most heavily treated agricultural zones in the country. Sampling was conducted during the dry season to minimize moisture variability. At each of 12 sampling locations, triplicate soil cores from the upper 10–15 cm layer were collected using a stainless steel soil auger (5 cm diameter, AMS, Inc., American Falls, ID, USA). Cores were combined and homogenized in the field to form a 2 kg composite sample per location. Composite samples were immediately placed in a sterile, pre-labeled poly-ethylene bag (Whirl-Pak, Nasco, WI, USA) transported to the laboratory at 4 °C, air dried at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for seven days, and sieved through a 2 mm stainless steel mesh sieve (Retsch, Haan, Germany) to remove stones, roots, and debris. Prepared samples were stored at 4 °C in the dark until analysis26[,27[,28.

Determination of soil physicochemical properties

Soil physico-chemical properties were determined as follows: pH in a 1:2.5 (w/v) soil: water suspension using a glass electrode pH meter29; soil organic carbon (SOC) by wet oxidation with potassium dichromate, with organic matter calculated as SOC × 1.72430,31; total nitrogen by Kjeldahl digestion and distillation32; and available phosphorus by sodium bicarbonate (0.5 M, pH 8.5) extraction followed by ascorbic acid-molybdenum blue colorimetry . All analyses were performed in triplicate on oven-dry soil (105 °C, 24 h)33[,34[,35.

Enrichment and isolation of chlorpyrifos degrading bacteria

Soil samples from the study sites were used to enrich chlorpyrifos (CP)-degrading bacteria. Erlenmeyer flasks (250 mL) containing 50 mL mineral salts medium (MSM) supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ CP (sterilized by filtration through 0.2 μm syringe filters) were inoculated with 5 g of soil (10% w/v). The MSM pH was adjusted to 7.0 ± 0.2 with 0.1 M HCl prior to autoclaving (121 °C, 20 min, 103 kPa). The composition of mineral salt medium (g L− 1) was 1.74 g K2HPO4, 5.97 g Na2HPO4, 1 g NaCl, 1 g NH4Cl, 0.5 g MgSO4, 0.01 g CaCl2.2H2O, 0.025 g FeSO4 and 0.02 g MnSO4.4H2O. Then, the culture was incubated at 150 rpm in a shaker incubator in the dark at 30 °C temperature. Cultures were incubated in the dark at 30 °C with orbital shaking at 150 rpm for 4 weeks following Akbar et al.36,, with 10% (v/v) transfers to fresh CP-MSM every 7 days; after the final subculture, 0.1 mL aliquots were spread-plated onto MSM agar supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ CP and incubated at 30 °C for 7 days, followed by purification of distinct colonies by streaking onto CP-MSM agar (50 mg L⁻¹) until pure cultures were obtained, with colony-forming units (CFU mL⁻¹) determined by plate counting. Pure isolates were preserved at 4 °C for further analysis and screened for chlorpyrifos tolerance (20–300 mg L⁻¹) by spotting onto CP-MSM agar gradient plates, with growth assessed by CFU mL⁻¹ at 30 °C.

Morphological, biochemical, and antimicrobial characterization of isolates

All isolates were cultured on mineral salt medium agar supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ of CP for morphological and biochemical analysis. Microscopic examination included Gram staining and assessment of cell size and shape. Biochemical characterization followed standard protocols in Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology and encompassed the following tests: Klinger Iron Agar (KIA), Methyl Red (MR), Voges–Proskauer (VP), motility–indole–urease (MIU), triple sugar iron (TSI), citrate utilization, catalase, and oxidase37.

Antimicrobial susceptibility of Escherichia fergusonii (CH09) and Clostridium bifermentans (CH10) isolates was assessed by the disk diffusion method. Pure cultures from 18 to 24 h colonies were standardized to a 0.5 McFarland suspension in sterile saline and evenly swabbed onto Mueller–Hinton agar (E. coli) or Brucella agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood, hemin, and vitamin K (Clostridium bifermentans). Antibiotic disks were placed with even spacing, and plates were incubated aerobically (Escherichia fergusonii; 35–37 °C, 16–18 h) or anaerobically (Clostridium bifermentans; 35–37 °C, 24–48 h). Inhibition zone diameters were measured and interpreted using CLSI or EUCAST breakpoints, with anaerobe-specific criteria applied where available38.

Calorimetric assay for organophosphate hydrolase activity in Escherichia fergusonii CH09 and Clostridium bifermentans CH10

Organophosphate hydrolase (OPH) activity in Escherichia fergusonii CHO9 and Clostridium bifermentans CH10 was detected using a calorimetric assay. Cells expressing OPH were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with phosphate-citrate buffer (pH 8.0), and re-suspended to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1.0. Aliquots (1 mL) of the cell suspension were incubated with chromogenic substrates (1 mM methyl parathion or paraoxon) at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle shaking. The reaction was stopped by adding 0.1 mL of 10% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate, and formation of p-nitrophenol was quantified spectrophotometrically at 405–410 nm. Absorbance values were normalized to cell density and converted to p-nitrophenol concentrations using calibration curve prepared with known standards (0.01–0.5 mM). Heat killed cells (incubated at 80 °C for 15 min) used as negative control to verify enzyme specificity. All assays were conducted in triplicate, with means ± standard deviations reported39.

Bacterial growth and chlorpyrifos biodegradation in liquid culture

Seed culture of each isolate were grown in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL mineral salts medium supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos. Flasks were incubated at 30 °C and 150 rpm in a shaker incubator in the dark. After 24 h, cultures were centrifuged at 6,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Cell pellets were washed three times with sterile 0.9% (w/v) NaCl and re-suspended in 10 mL of the same solution. Cell density was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 108 CFU mL⁻¹) for biodegradation experiments.

A 100 µL aliquot of bacterial suspension (1.5 × 108 CFU mL⁻¹) was inoculated into 50 mL mineral salts medium (MSM) containing 50 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos (CP). Triplicate cultures were incubated at 30 °C and 150 rpm in a shaker incubator in the dark, with un-inoculated MSM-CP flasks as controls. Bacterial growth was monitored every 2 days over 20 days by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1 mL culture samples. At days 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, and 20, cultures from both treatments and controls were centrifuged at 6,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and 10 mL of the supernatant was extracted twice with ethyl acetate, shaken for 5 min, and dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and evaporated under reduced pressure or nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted in acetonitrile or isopropanol, and chlorpyrifos concentration was determined by UV-VIS absorbance at 290 nm against a calibration curve (1–100 µg mL− 1), ensuring linearity40. Chlorpyrifos (CP) degradation was also assessed with 1% (w/v) glucose as a supplementary carbon source. Experiments were conducted in triplicate, with un-inoculated MSM containing 50 mg L⁻¹ CP serving as controls and a killed bacterial biomass, inactivated by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15–20 min, was used as a control to differentiate between biodegradation and abiotic adsorption processes.

Chlorpyrifos extraction and GC-MS analysis

Among the isolated bacteria, those exhibiting the highest chlorpyrifos degradation capability, as determined by UV-VIS spectrophotometry, were selected for further analysis by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS).Chlorpyrifos was extracted from culture enrichments after removal of microbial biomass. In a 250 mL flask, 10 mL of sample was mixed with 50 mL deionized water and extracted with 20 mL hexane using a rotary shaker for 1 h. The organic phase was dehydrated with anhydrous Na2SO4 and dried by evaporation under nitrogen at 45 °C in a rotary evaporator. The extract was then concentrated to 2 mL by adding 10 mL hexane41.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis was performed using an Agilent 78 90B Turbo MSD 5977 A system (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) equipped with a triple quadruple mass spectrometer operating in electron impact (EI) mode and a Triple-Axis HED EM detector. Separation was achieved using an HP-5 MS column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent, San ta Clara, USA). A 2 µL sample was injected in split less mode at 180 °C, with helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.2 mL min⁻¹.The oven temperature was programmed as follows: initial temperature 60 °C (held for 1 min), increased to 120 °C at 40 °C min⁻¹ (held for 2.5 min), then rose to 310 °C at 5 °C min⁻¹, and maintained at 310 °C for 40.5 min. All analyses were conducted at the Bless Agri-Food Laboratory, an accredited facility by the Ethiopian National Accreditation Office (ENAO) (FA: T0030).

Effect of pH on bacterial growth

Optimal growth of isolates was assessed across pH 4–11 in mineral salts medium (MSM) supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos. MSM pH was adjusted using Na2HPO4 (5.8 mg L⁻¹) and KH2PO4 (3.0 mg L⁻¹) in calibrated ratio, verified with a standardized pH meter and buffer solutions. Flasks (50 mL) were inoculated with 0.5 McFarland suspensions, incubated at 30 °C with shaking (150 rpm) for 4 days; alongside un-inoculated controls to monitor abiotic degradation. Growth was quantified as optical density at 600 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Effect of temperature on bacterial growth

Growth of Escherichia fergusonii and Clostridium bifermentans isolates was evaluated at 20, 25, 30, 35, and 40 °C using 50 mL flasks of sterile mineral salt medium (MSM) supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos as sole carbon source. Flasks were inoculated with 0.5 McFarland suspensions and incubated in triplicate on an orbital shaker (150 rpm) for 4 days. Bacterial growth was quantified as optical density at 600 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Effect of different concentration of CP on bacterial growth

Sterile mineral salts medium (50 mL) supplemented with chlorpyrifos (CP; 50 mg L⁻¹) were used to assess bacterial growth responses to varying CP concentrations. Flasks were inoculated with Escherichia fergusonii and Clostridium bifermentans at 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards and amended with CP at final concentrations of 10, 50, 100, 200 or 300 mg L⁻¹. Cultures were incubated at 150 rpm on an orbital shaker42. After 4 days of incubation, samples were measured for optical density (OD) at 600 to determine bacterial growth. Un-inoculated flasks were used as control.

Molecular identification of bacterial isolates

DNA extraction and amplification by polymerase chain reaction

Following comprehensive screening for degradation efficiency, the most proficient and well-isolated bacterial strains were selected for molecular analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using the DTv3.1 cycle sequencing kit and subsequently analyzed on the ABI 3500Dx Genetic Analyzer (Hereditary Bioscience). 1µL of the supernatant was directly used for PCR amplification, targeting the bacterial 16 S r RNA. The primer set used for the amplification 16 S r RNA gene was 27f (5ʹ-GAGTTTGATCATGGCTCAG-3ʹ) and 1492R (5ʹ-TAC GG TTA CCT TGTTACGACTT-3ʹ).The primer set amplifies a ˃ 1400 bp. The concentration of each DNA sample was measured by Nano drop (Biotech instruments, USA). DNA was stored at −80 °C for further use. The ratio of absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm was used to assess the purity of the DNA.

PCR was performed in a total volume of 25 µL containing 10 pmol each of forward and reverse primers, 2.5 mM of MgCl2, 200 µM each of the four Deoxyribonucleotide triphosphate (dNTPs), 0.5 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 1 × concentration of PCR buffer (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Brazil) and 50 to 100 ng of isolated bacterial genomic DNA. The template was denatured by heating at pre-denaturation 95 °C for 5 min.This was followed by 39 cycles of denaturation 30 s at 95 °C, 45 s annealing and 1 min elongation at 72 °C. The amplicons were resolved in 1.5% agarose gel using 0.5 × Tris-acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer.

Sequencing

Briefly, the purified PCR products of the 16 S r RNA gene for our isolate was sequenced by.

Sanger sequencing platform43 and FASTA file sequence was analyzed by comparing with related gene sequences available in the Ez-Taxon and GenBank databases using BLAST-N web-based bioinformatics program.

Construction of phylogenetic tree

The 16 S r RNA gene sequences of type species were collected from EzTaxon database (http://www.ezbiocloud.net/). Before the construction of evolutionary tree, overlapping low quality regions of sequence reads were trimmed by using BioEdit online tools to generate consensus sequences through assemblies of contigs. For phylogenetic assessment of the bacterial taxa 97% similarity cutoff (3% divergence) was considered for the recognition of phylotypes44. The 16 S r RNA sequences were subjected for multiple sequence alignment with MUSCLE and phylogenetic tree was constructed using Molecular Evolutionary Genetic Analysis (MEGAX version11 software) with selection of neighbor-joining method. During tree construction, tree topologies of confidence values were evaluated by bootstrapping with 1000 repetitions.

Data management and analysis

Microsoft Excel was utilized for raw data entry. Statistical analysis of the data was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 20). Value differences were deemed statistically significant when the p-value was less than 0.05. Error bars show the standard deviation of the mean (SD), and all experimental data included in the tables and graphs are the mean values from triplicate experiments. The BLAST search program facilitated the comparison of isolated bacterial strain sequences with those available in public databases at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Sequence similarity matrices and a phylogenetic tree were generated and visualized using the Tree View feature of MEGAX software45.

Result

Soil physicochemical properties

The Soil physicochemical properties of the study sites exhibited a pH range of 6.9 to 9. Organic matter content varied from 0.54 to 4.91%. Nitrogen levels were found between 0.016 and 0.142%, while phosphorus was measured at 18.56 to 66.46 mg kg-1.

Enrichment and isolation of chlorpyrifos degrading bacteria

Eight distinct bacterial colonies were isolated and purified following four weeks of enrichment on mineral salt medium (MSM) supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos. After repeated sub-culturing, four isolates demonstrating rapid growth and high tolerance to chlorpyrifos were selected. Statistically significant differences in growth (p < 0.05) were observed among the isolates when cultured on MSM agar containing 50 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos. After 72 h of incubation, total viable counts (TVC) were 1.49 × 109 CFU mL− 1 for CHO7, 1.95 × 109 CFU mL− 1 for CHO8, 2.02 × 109 CFU mL− 1 for CHO9, and 2.17 × 109 CFU mL− 1 for CH10. No growth was detected in the un-inoculated control throughout the incubation period. (Table 1).

Morphological, biochemical, and antimicrobial characterization of isolates

Microscopic examination showed that all bacterial isolates exhibited a rod-shaped morphology. Of these, one isolate (CH10), representing 25% of the total, was Gram-positive, while the remaining were Gram-negative. Biochemical characterization revealed that isolates CH08, CH09, and CH10 were negative for gelatinase activity, whereas CH07 was positive. Urease activity was detected in isolates CH08 and CH10; all others were urease-negative. None of the isolates were positive for the methyl red test, and only CH10 tested positive for indole production. Voges-Proskauer test results were positive for CH08, CH09, and CH10, while no isolate demonstrated hydrogen sulfide (H2S) production (Table 2).

Both Escherichia fergusonii (CH09) and Clostridium bifermentans (CH10) demonstrated resistance to gentamicin, chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, and streptomycin in the antimicrobial susceptibility test. Organophosphate hydrolase activity was detected in Escherichia fergusonii. The most effective growth for isolate CHO9 was observed during the 8–12 day incubation period, with peak growth reached on the 10th day. In contrast, isolate CH10 exhibited faster growth, showing effective growth within the first 6 days of incubation. Enhanced growth of both isolates was observed when glucose was used as a co-substrate, compared to using chlorpyrifos (CP) alone as the carbon source (Table 3).

Biodegradation of chlorpyrifos (CP) was quantified by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) after 20 days of incubation in minimal salt medium (MSM) supplemented with 50 mg L⁻¹ CP (Fig. 2 A, B), confirming the degradation capability of Escherichia fergusonii CHO9 and Clostridium bifermentans CH10. By the end of the incubation period, these strains utilized 59.34% and 70.82% of the initial CP concentration, respectively (Fig. 1 A). Abiotic losses in un-inoculated MSM and heat-killed cell controls were minimal (10% and < 14%, respectively) and statistically insignificant (P > 0.05). When glucose was supplied as an additional carbon source, bacterial growth was markedly enhanced, and CP degradation increased to 99.92% (Escherichia fergusonii CHO9) and 99% (Clostridium bifermentans CH10) of the initial concentration after 20 days (Fig. 1B). GC–MS analysis indicated that chlorpyrifos was detected at a retention time of approximately 19.28 min, and both isolates converted it to 3, 5, 6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCP). However, TCP did not accumulate above the detection limit, suggesting further degradation by the isolates. Other un-identified metabolites are also produced in the degradation(Fig.3). Both isolates were able to utilize CP as the sole carbon and energy source, supporting the inducible expression of organophosphate-degrading enzymes without additional nutrients.

Isolate CHO9 (Escherichia fergusonii) and CH10 (Clostridium bifermentans) degraded 50 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos in mineral salt medium (MSM) with and without glucose, achieving 99.84% (CHO9) and 99.02% (CH10) in MSM with glucose, and 59.90% (CHO9) and 70.82% (CH10) without glucose within 20 days (Table 4). These efficiencies were comparable or higher than those reported for Pseudomonas putida, Achromobacter insuavis, Pseudomonas mendocina, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Streptomyces sp., Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Pseudomonas nitroreducens, and Bacillus cereus under similar or lower substrate concentrations.

Effect of temperature on bacterial growth

Incubation temperature is among the influential factors that can affect bacterial growth. The growth of bacterial isolates that degraded chlorpyrifos was assessed and the results were shown in the Fig. 5 below. The cell density of the isolates increased with increase in temperature from 20 to 35 °C. Escherichia fergusonii CHO9 showed its maximum growth at 35 °C, while Clostridium bifermentans CH10 showed its maximum growth at 30 °C. As we go towards higher and lower temperatures, cell densities dropped, reaching their lowest at the extremes (Fig. 5). Both strains exhibited a statistically significant difference at various temperatures, with a p-value of less than 0.05. The maximum optical density obtained was 0.66 and 0.745 for Escherichia fergusonii CHO9 and Clostridium bifermentans CH10 respectively.

Effect of initial concentration of CP on bacterial isolates

The two bacterial isolates OD600 did not significantly changed (P < 0.05) when the concentration of chlorpyrifos increased from 50 mg L− 1 to 200 mg L− 1. The OD600 of the isolates slightly dropped when the concentration of chlorpyrifos surpassed 200 mg L− 1, indicating that a concentration greater than 200 mg L− 1 chlorpyrifos could inhibit the growth of Escherichia fergusonii CHO9 and Clostridium bifermentans CH10. Maximum growth of both strains was observed when the concentration of chlorpyrifos was 50–100 mg L− 1. Although 300 mg L− 1 of chlorpyrifos would cause a significant decrease in the OD600, they could still grow. The result indicated that both strains could grow in a wide range of chlorpyrifos concentration (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study successfully isolated and characterized indigenous chlorpyrifos (CP) degrading bacteria Escherichia fergusonii CHO9 and Clostridium bifermentans CH10 from soil sample at Wonji-Shoa Sugar Plantation and Meki vegetable farm in Ethiopia’s Central Rift Valley. These strains demonstrated substantial CP degradation (59.34% and 70.82%, respectively) in minimal salt medium highlighting their bioremediation potential. Elevated pesticide level previously reported in lake Koka, Ethiopia likely stemming from intensive agricultural applications53 underscores the urgency of ecofriendly CP removal strategies, given its toxicity to humans, animals and microbes18,50. Microbial bioremediation offers a cost effective solution46, Particularly relevant in Ethiopia where CP is widely used for pest control in maize, sugarcane, onion, cabbage and garlic crops12. This is the first study investigating chlorpyrifos bioremediation using indigenous bacteria from regionally pesticide-impacted soils. It is among few reports isolating native CP-degrading strains from these genera, expanding known microbial diversity and highlighting their potential for site-specific organophosphate remediation46,54.

Soil physicochemical analysis revealed low total organic carbon and nitrogen but medium-to-high available phosphorus, consistent with prior assessment at these sites55. Intensive pesticide application likely depletes nutrients by disrupting microbial communities favouring resilient taxa like indigenous isolates identified here, which thrive without disrupting native microflora (56)24. Total viable count (TVC) of these bacterial isolates at varying chlorpyrifos concentration showed significant differences (P < 0.05), with Escherichia fergusonii and Clostridium bifermentans exhibiting optimal growth and maximum degradation. Their broad-spectrum antibiotic resistance likely stems from environmental adaptation via acquired genetic elements in contaminated soils, rather than pathogenic traits, aligning with prevalence in agricultural niches57.

Thus far, these bacterial genera roles in xenobiotic and pesticide degradation particularly chlorpyrifos have rarely been reported. However, a study by Murtaza et al. isolated an E.coli which showed maximum tolerance (11 mg/ml) and efficient degradation of chlorpyrifos34. In another study by Harishankar et al., intestinal Escherichia coli showed high tolerance (> 1400 µg/mL) 27and 16% degradation of the initial CP concentration. E.coli strains have also been studied for degradation of 81% of the initial concentration of malathion58, 66.8% reduction in the initial concentration of pendimethaliun59 and complete degradation of mesotrione in 3 h of exposure60. Clostridium strains have been described as responsible for degradation of fenpropathrin61, crude oil62, cypermethrin63 and chlorpyrifos64.

The bacterial strains isolated in this study degraded 59.34% (Escherichia fergusonii) and 70.82% (Clostridium bifermentans) of the initial concentration of CP in minimal salt medium in 20 days. In a prior study, Saengsanga & Phakratok 66 , Asamba et al.46, and Omeiri et al.,)48, observed increased degradation of chlorpyrifos (> 90%). Another study however, reported relatively lower degradation (52%) and (57.8%) of chlorpyrifos within 14 and 21 days of incubation (Ewida6566,;). Glucose supplementation boosted degradation to > 99%, likely by inducing co-metabolism without inhibition. Similarly, findings in earlier studies supported enhanced degradation of pesticides in the presence of glucose20,47,50,51. In another report, the addition of glucose resulted in an apparent increase in the organic compound removal67. This preference for CP even with alternative carbon sources suggests constitutive enzyme expression, conferring a competitive edge68, though co-substrates can sometimes hider dissipation48. Abiotic losses of chlorpyrifos (CP) in control flasks were minimal un-inoculated media (10%), killed biomass (14%) and statistically insignificant (P > 0.05). CP stability was confirmed in sterile and killed controls; however, a glucose-only control will be included in future studies to exclude abiotic effects.

Chlorpyrifos degradation produced 3, 5, 6-trichloro-2-pyridinol and unidentified metabolites. Organophosphate hydrolase activity was detected in Escherichia fergusonii but not in Clostridium bifermentans, suggesting distinct metabolic pathways. These findings highlight diverse bacterial degradation strategies and the need for further enzyme/pathway characterization, consistent with prior studies on multi-enzyme involvement20.

Both isolates exhibited optimal growth at pH 5–7 and 30–35 °C, showing statistically significant differences across tested levels (P < 0.05; OD measurements), while growth slowed under alkaline conditions, at temperature extremes, or low temperatures (which impair metabolism) versus high temperatures, which denature enzymes and increase membrane fluidity, causing cell death69. These optima align with reports from prior studies48,70,71 but differ from others thriving at pH > 772, or 25 °C73. Growth peaked at 50–100 mg L⁻¹ chlorpyrifos (CP), with tolerance from 20 to 300 mg L⁻¹ but inhibition below 20 mg L⁻¹ (possibly due to suboptimal substrate availability) and > 250 mg L⁻¹ (where CP toxicity suppresses metabolism, damages cells, disrupts membranes, impairs nutrient uptake, and reduces resilience71, mirroring patters at prior isolation at 50 mg L⁻¹71,73 and reduced degradation at low concentrations50.

The isolated Escherichia fergusonii and Clostridium bifermentans strains degrade chlorpyrifos primarily via initial hydrolytic cleavage of the P-O bond by organophosphate hydrolase (OPH) activity detected in Escherichia fergusonii but absent in Clostridium bifermentans yielding 3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCP), followed by unidentified trace products suggestive of divergent downstream oxidation pathways mediated by flavin-dependent monooxygenases or dehydrogenases. Glucose supplementation enhances degradation (> 99%) through co-metabolism, inducing constitutive enzyme expression without toxicity inhibition, which confers a competitive advantage in nutrient-limited, pesticide-contaminated soils. Optimal degradation occurs at pH 5–7 and 30–35 °C with 50–100 mg L⁻¹ CP, where moderate concentrations stimulate adaptive metabolism while higher levels (> 200 mg L⁻¹) induce toxicity via membrane disruption and impaired nutrient uptake; this reflects environmental adaptation via acquired catabolic genes, enabling these indigenous strains to thrive in phosphorus-rich but organic-poor agricultural soils of the study region.

Microbial degradation efficiently removes environmental pollutants, primarily via fungi and bacteria, though few species target complex compounds74. Bioremediation uses microbes to degrade or immobilize pesticides, which contaminate soil/water and enter food chains through livestock75. Bacteria bearing catabolic genes adapt to diverse ecosystems76.

Key limitations of this study include the inherent sensitivity constraints of gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), which can impede accurate quantification of chlorpyrifos and its metabolites at low concentrations or in complex matrices. Notably, major metabolite 3, 5, 6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCP) was undetected, possibly due to rapid transformation by isolates or levels below detection limits. Nevertheless, detailed metabolite profiling using more sensitive analytical techniques will be required in future studies to elucidate the complete chlorpyrifos degradation pathway. Degradation assays used sterile laboratory conditions to confirm isolates’ intrinsic chlorpyrifos degradation, ignoring complex environmental microbial interactions. This limits insights into real-world performance. Future studies should test efficacy under environmentally relevant conditions. Finally, resource and time constraints limited identification to genus-level 16 S r RNA gene sequencing, which often lacks resolution for closely related species (e.g., Escherichia coli and Shigella spp.) due to high sequence similarity; future studies will employ advanced methods such as multilocus sequence typing (MLST), whole-genome sequencing, or species-specific PCR for precise delineation.

Conclusion

The study isolated two CP-degrading strains, Escherichia fergusonii LB50 and Clostridium bifermentans PS1, from agricultural farms in Wonji-shoa and Meki, Ethiopia. These strains were capable of using chlorpyrifos (CP) in minimal salt medium as their sole carbon source and could enhance its degradation when glucose was also present. Optimal growth conditions for these strains were determined to be a pH of 5–7, a temperature range of 30–35 °C, and chlorpyrifos concentrations of 50–100 mg L− 1. Given that these microbes are adapted to local environments, they show promise for effective bioremediation of CP-contaminated sites. Additionally, they can serve as bio-augmentation agents for other contaminated soils. Future studies will validate these findings using actual contaminated field soils, evaluating the isolated strain’s efficacy under real-world conditions. Additional investigation is also needed to identify the specific enzymes involved in chlorpyrifos degradation by the isolates and elucidate the complete metabolic pathway through targeted genomic, proteomic, and metabolomics analyses.
