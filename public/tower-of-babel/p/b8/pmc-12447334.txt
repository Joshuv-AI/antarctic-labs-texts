Engineering human neuronal diversity: Morphogens and stem cell technologies for neurodevelopmental biology
Authors: Soraya Scuderi, Nagham Khouri-Farah, Riya Rauthan, Abhiram Natu, Hantao Wang, Alex Nelson, Alexandre Jourdon, Flora M Vaccarino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryA complex assortment of neuronal cells contributes to distinct functional circuits in the human brain. Such diversity is imposed upon pluripotent stem cells by a patterning process that begins much before the start of neurogenesis. Neural tube patterning relies on morphogens—diffusible signals that regulate transcription factor networks in progenitor cells, guiding spatial and temporal identity formation. Studying early human patterning in vivo is limited by access to embryonic tissue. Recent advances in stem cell technologies, including brain organoids, brain-on-chip systems, and assembloids, allow to explore morphogen-driven neurodevelopment. In this review, we highlight key mechanisms of neural tube patterning and how they enable insights into human brain development and neuropsychiatric disorders.Keywords: patterning, human, fetal brain development, induced pluripotent stem cells, iPSC, morphogen, gene expression, gradient, diffusion, transcription factor, gene regulatory program

Introduction

The central nervous system (CNS) is the most complex biological system of the human body, both in terms of morphological organization and cellular diversity. The neuronal diversity established during development is essential for proper brain function, as each neuronal subtype contributes to distinct parts of the neural circuits that process complex stimuli and store information (Tripathy et al., 2015). Disruption in the fine balance between the diverse neuronal populations has been linked to neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia (He et al., 2018; Sohal and Rubenstein, 2019). The generation of a variety of cell types relies on a relatively small number of molecular signals that pattern the developing neural tube. Decades of studies have established a general model of neural tube regionalization where extrinsic diffusible factors, named morphogens, trigger the coordinated activation of specific transcription factors (TFs) in uncommitted progenitor cells to establish different cell identities through changes in gene regulatory networks (GRNs). Neural tube patterning is therefore primarily dependent on the regulation of morphogens concentration in space and time and on the cell competence to respond to those signals.

This patterning process partitions the neural tube into spatial domains of gene expression within which cell-to-cell communication further define local cell identities and roles. Thus, it is crucial to study the cascade of patterning events that transform the early neural tube into the complex, intricate tissue that constitutes the brain. In this review, we present the major morphogens of the neural tube, their patterning mechanisms, and recent theories of pattern formation. Studying patterning events in vivo, which occur during early stages of embryonic development, is extremely challenging in humans. In recent years, there have been substantial efforts to reproduce embryonic development in vitro using stem cell-derived multicellular structures referred to as organoids. We summarize key milestones of the field focusing on how organoids, assembloids, and brain-on-chip technologies exploit and help elucidate the roles of morphogens during neurodevelopment. Although reproducing the entire brain in vitro remains challenging, these models undoubtedly represent important advances in the investigation and dissection of developmental processes otherwise inaccessible due to the limited availability of human embryonic and fetal brain specimens.

Control of tissue patterning by morphogens

Pattern formation occurs in developing tissues made of naive competent cells or uncommitted progenitor cells that can acquire a fate in response to exposure to a morphogen. A morphogen is a secreted signaling molecule that can diffuse from a cellular source, often referred to as an organizing center, to create a concentration gradient across the receiving tissue. In addition to diffusion in the interstitial fluid, morphogens can be actively transported via vesicles, shuttled via other proteins or delivered via cellular projections (cytonemes) (Stathopoulos and Iber, 2013). Known morphogens include members of the Hedgehog (Hh, notably Sonic Hedgehog [SHH]), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), Wingless (WNT), fibroblast growth factors (FGFs), bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), and retinoic acid (RA) families (Dessaud et al., 2010).

Morphogen signaling is controlled by the expression of genes involved in their production, their inhibitors, and their receptors. In most cases, morphogens act as a ligand and bind to a cell surface receptor that activates downstream intracellular signaling cascades (e.g., kinases and phosphatases) that then regulate the expression of target genes, eliciting and executing specific developmental programs (Cohen et al., 2010). A notable exception is RA, a membrane-permeant morphogen and an intracellular metabolite of vitamin A that directly regulates gene expression in the nucleus. For certain morphogens, there is a linear relationship between their concentration and downstream signaling activation level. However, in most cases, additional factors influence this process, including time delays (diffusion kinetic), half-lives, time scales of downstream signaling networks, feedback loops (both positive and negative), and cross-regulation by other pathways. All these elements contribute to modify the downstream transcriptional changes or stop the signaling pathway altogether (Huizar et al., 2020; Kicheva and Briscoe, 2023).

Only a handful of morphogens coordinate the entire process of embryonic development, where the same morphogen is repurposed across time and space. This ability to produce a specific developmental outcome at different times depends on the basal gene expression program in the receiving cells within different tissue contexts. This phenomenon was made evident by transplantation experiments including the CNS floor plate transplant in the limb. The floor plate secretes SHH to induce ventral fate specification in the neural tube and generate motor neurons in the ventral spinal cord. Transplanted in the limb tissue, the same floor plate cells mirrored the function of the zone of polarizing activity in the mesodermal mesenchyme, which instructs digit patterning by secreting SHH at later stages of development. This additional ectopic source of SHH in the limb resulted in polydactyly (Lettice et al., 2003; Xu et al., 2020).

Despite the early discovery of how neural induction occurs, many of the molecular mechanisms of cell fate determination by morphogens are still getting refined. In humans, neural induction begins around the end of the 3rd gestational week, when the notochord initiates the neural plate formation from the overlying ectoderm, primarily by inhibiting BMP, nodal, and TGF-β signaling by noggin, chordin, and follistatin, (Corallo et al., 2015). By the 4th week, the neural plate folds into the neural tube. In the dorso-ventral (D-V) direction, SHH secreted from the notochord ventralizes the neural tube by establishing the floor plate. On the opposite side, BMP and WNT signaling, emanating from the overlying ectoderm, contribute to dorsal patterning with the specification of the roof plate (Figure 1). SHH signaling regulates and activates GLI TFs (Gli1, Gli2, and Gli3), which then trigger a TFs cascade that prompt further specification of ventral progenitor cells in the neuroepithelium (Dessaud et al., 2010). The mutual repression between TFs induced by these opposite gradients (e.g., Gli1/Gli2 vs. Gli3R, or Nkx2.2/Nkx6.1 vs. Pax3, Pax7, Pax6, and Irx3) (Taneyhill and Bronner-Fraser, 2005; Timmer et al., 2002) (Figure 1B), helps sharpen and define boundaries between progenitor domains. Immediately after neural tube closure, secondary organizers arise. The anterior neural ridge (ANR) is one of the earliest at the rostro-ventral edge of the neural tube and it is critical for forebrain specification, secreting multiple WNT inhibitors (e.g., DKK1) to maintain low level of WNT signaling (Mourtada et al., 2023; Nikolopoulou et al., 2017). ANR also expresses FGF8 and FGF17 that will co-facilitate WNT inhibition (Echevarria et al., 2003).

At the other extreme, Wnt ligands, a family of secreted glycoproteins, are secreted by the posterior paraxial dorsolateral mesoderm to define the anterior-posterior axis and are considered caudalizing agents (Brafman and Willert, 2017). The isthmic organizer (IsO) forms at the midbrain-hindbrain boundary (MHB) and secretes FGF8 and WNT1, coordinating the development of the midbrain and anterior hindbrain on each side (Sunmonu et al., 2011). The spatial separation of morphogen’s expression is further reinforced by the antagonistic interactions between two TFs, Otx2 and Gbx2 that establishes the MHB (Gibbs et al., 2017); their crosstalk around the IsO limits the expression of FGF8 to the hindbrain (Figure 1C) (Joyner et al., 2000). Two other secondary organizing centers form in the anterior neural tube to pattern the cortical plate. The cortical hem, located in the dorsal-medial forebrain, secretes WNTs (e.g., Wnt3a) and BMPs to specify the hippocampus and choroid plexus territories. Laterally, the anti-hem forms at the boundary between cortex and future basal ganglia and secretes FGF7, epidermal growth factor, and the WNT inhibitor Secreted Frizzled-Related Protein 2 (Assimacopoulos et al., 2003; Moore et al., 2013). More caudally, SHH is secreted by the zona limitans intrathalamica (ZLI), within the diencephalon to organize thalamic patterning (Kiecker and Lumsden, 2004). By the 5th gestational week, RA activity emerges with a caudal-to-rostral gradient, refining hindbrain and spinal cord identity via homeobox (HOX) gene expression (Glover et al., 2006) (Figures 1A and 1Ci). A recent study in human brain has shown that at a later stage of development, all-trans retinoic acid, released from the meninges, plays a crucial role in regulating the timing and progression of neurogenesis in the prefrontal cortex (Como et al., 2025; Shibata et al., 2021).

Capturing the nuanced dynamics of morphogen gradients in the developing tissue demands techniques capable of high spatial and temporal resolution. Moreover, variations in cell types, extracellular matrix composition, and tissue architecture can influence morphogen distribution, making it challenging to obtain a precise map of morphogen activity. Traditional methods, such as in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry, may lack the resolution or sensitivity required to detect subtle variations in morphogen concentrations across tissues. To overcome these challenges, several advanced techniques have been recently developed. The first, RNA tomography (Tomo-seq), combines classical histological sectioning with high-sensitivity RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). By applying Tomo-seq methods is possible to reconstruct spatial gene expression patterns for thousands of genes, providing insights into morphogen activity onto downstream genes. A second technique, deterministic barcoding in tissue for spatial multi omics sequencing (DBiT-seq) (Holler and Junker, 2019) was developed to achieve near single-cell resolution and co-mapping of mRNA and protein levels within tissue sections. This technique can enhance the understanding of spatially resolved gene expression and morphogen gradients in various tissues (Liu et al., 2020). A third method, optogenetic manipulation, by integrating optogenetics with genetic modifications, allows to control signaling pathways with spatial and temporal precision. For instance, optogenetic activation of specific pathways in Drosophila’s embryos has been used to rescue developmental defects, demonstrating the potential to study morphogen functions in vivo (Johnson et al., 2020); in vitro, it has been applied to human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) differentiation toward mesoderm (Hellwarth et al., 2021) and to spatially restricted expression of SHH in organoids (for more details see the section entitled “Reproducing morphogen patterning in neural organoids”) (Legnini et al., 2023). Finally, the single-cell anchoring of key regulators to space (scANKRS) is a cost-efficient method to inspect tree-dimensional distribution of cell types identified by single-cell sequencing. The scANKRS uses cyclic immunofluorescence staining of cell type specific markers (e.g., TFs) performed on serial sections of the studied tissue to link their expression level (e.g., fluorescence intensity measurements) to gene expression on a single cell level (Khouri-Farah et al., 2025).

Transition state and gene regulatory network

An enduring notion that helps visualize how cells commit to a specific fate is the Waddington landscape (Waddington, 1959), further refined over the years (Kauffman, 1969; Wang et al., 2010). In this metaphor, a cell is depicted as a ball rolling down a hill with branching valleys, where each valley represents a different cell fate (Figure 2A). The shape of the landscape is determined by the target genes, poised to be actively induced or repressed by the morphogens that “push” the cell into one valley or another; hence the entire process of cell fate determination can be described in terms of entropy and “attractor states” (Feinberg and Levchenko, 2023). The attractor state is an emergent process where differential recruitment of cell lineage-specific TFs form transient to stable self-reinforcing GRNs. GRNs are composed of the set TFs produced in the cells that bind to specific promoters and enhancers to regulate the expression of downstream genes responsible of cell fate specification and function. A recent study in mouse radial glia stem cells, among others, demonstrated that the genetic ablation of TFs with known patterning role (e.g., EMX2 and PAX6) disrupts the regional expression of secondary TFs involved in the same patterning processes (Ypsilanti et al., 2021), corroborating the existence of a dynamic regulatory architecture behind temporal gene expression during patterning processes. GRNs are not strictly hierarchical unidirectional structures as they also encompass feedback loops that complexify the modeling of fate determination. Exploiting advances in single-cell technologies, Moris and colleagues (Moris et al., 2016) revised the epigenetic landscape theory and proposed a transition state model, where cells making fate decisions switch from one state to another, in consistence with the entropy-based model. During the transition, gene expression becomes variable, and transitioning cell exhibits a mixed identity of two or more unstable states (microstates) over time. Each of these microstates has an identical network structure, but with different gene expression levels. Indeed, single-cell transcriptomic data have shown that although a cell type can be characterized by the expression of a specific set of marker genes, individual cells would still display a certain degree of heterogeneity in terms of gene expression levels. This transition state is highly unstable, reversible, and importantly it is not necessarily a continuous sequence of events. In this view, we can envision that morphogens act on dynamic transcriptional states by selectively “activating” and reshaping the GRN to guide the unstable microstate toward a new stable state, with a release of entropy and a non-reversible decision toward a defined fate (Feinberg and Levchenko, 2023). In such model, early neural progenitor cells along the neural tube initially have a broad developmental potential—they are competent to adopt multiple regional fates (e.g., excitatory or inhibitory neurons of the forebrain) (Figure 2B). This dynamic system allows the progenitor cells to stochastically rearrange their GRN, as it is not yet locked into a specific fate, until this cellular plasticity is guided by morphogen-mediated GRN stabilization. This transition could be triggered by a specific epigenetic switch of enhancers initially in a “poised” state in progenitor cells toward an activated or repressed state in progenies (Harikumar and Meshorer, 2015; Kumar et al., 2021; Macrae et al., 2023). Subsequent self-repressing and self-reinforcing regulatory loops are then key to allow only minimal GRN changes and commit the cell in its new state. Many GRNs are characterized by feedforward and feedback loops, which help maintain robust signaling, sharp boundaries, and temporal control. Morphogens often initiate negative feedback loops by inducing the expression of their own inhibitors at a distance. A well-studied GRN is the specification by SHH signaling of the developing neural tube in a ventral to dorsal series of well-defined domains that will give rise to specific neuronal types (Cohen et al., 2013). SHH signaling induces PTCH1 that sequester the SHH ligand and downregulates SHH signaling. Similarly, WNT signaling activates AXIN2 that promote β-catenin degradation, BMPs induce chordin, noggin, and follistatin to prevent excessive dorsalization, and FGFs induce Sprouty, a family of genes that inhibit FGF receptor signaling. Instead, RA represses its own action by upregulating at a distance the enzyme responsible of its degradation, CYP26A1/C1B1, resulting in a restricted domain of RA signaling between the fetal human prefrontal and motor cortex (Shibata et al., 2021). Positive feedback loops also contribute to cell fate stability. For example, PAX3 expression in the dorsal-posterior neural tube, whose expression is initially activated by WNT signaling, is then maintained by positive autoregulation of cis-regulatory motifs (Moore et al., 2013). A further layer of regulation occurs through mutual cross-repression, where morphogen-induced TFs inhibit each other’s expression. A classic example is the MHB, where OTX2 (anterior) and GBX2 (posterior) mutually repress each other to establish a binary fate decision.

In summary, during the earliest phase of tissue development, cell type identity is fluid, and cells progressively acquire their final fate influenced by the organizing centers and in coordination with their spatial position, within a community of similar cells. Cell fate specification is very precise, coordinated, and governed by a neighborhood-watch mechanism (Abdel Fattah et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2022). This process, further modulated by region-specific TFs, ultimately shapes the distinct morphogenetic landscapes necessary for the proper delineation of neural regions and subdomains. All together these processes are crucial for proper embryo development, requiring it to be able to withstand both intrinsic and extrinsic perturbations (Mosby et al., 2024).

Morphogens and neural connectivity

Despite significant efforts devoted to investigating the molecular basis of neural connectivity and circuit formation during development, little is known about how morphogen-triggered GRNs and downstream signaling influence the precise wiring of the CNS.

Proper formation of neuronal networks requires successful cell migration, axon elongation, pathfinding, target recognition, and synaptogenesis. Several studies pointed to the involvement of morphogens in regulating the expression of adhesion molecules (e.g., cadherins) and axon guidance molecules (e.g., netrins, semaphorins, and ephrins), modulating cell-cell interactions, cytoskeletal dynamics and ultimately affecting axonal growth and direction (Charron et al., 2003; Xie et al., 2018). For instance, SHH in the ventral spinal cord acts as an attractant to guide commissural axons toward the floor plate and cross to the opposite side. Post-crossing high levels of SHH switches to repulsive and direct axons toward high expression of WNT ligands in the anterior floor plate. While SHH Smoothened-dependent attraction occurs via activation of Src family kinases, its repulsive mechanisms are mediated by 14-3-3 adaptor proteins in axon growth cone (Charron et al., 2003; Yam et al., 2012). Similar mechanisms were reported in the visual neural system formation, where SHH and Wnt3a act as guidance cues to direct retinal ganglion cell projection through the optic chiasm and into the optic nerve (Aviles et al., 2013; Kolpak et al., 2005).

In addition to direct axon guidance, morphogens can regulate the expression of genes associated with neural circuit formation. Therefore, morphogen-mediated cell-type-specific changes in GRNs first specify neuronal fates, then axon guidance, and finally help establish connectivity and synaptogenesis. This requires further actions locally upon cell adhesion and polarity (Fournier-Thibault et al., 2009).

One example is ventral high levels of SHH in the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) inducing the expression of NKX2.1 and downstream TF, LHX6, which in turn drives the expression of several genes involved in target recognition and synaptogenesis. Among these are neurexin-1, a presynaptic adhesion molecule critical for synaptic specificity, and SLIT2, involved in axon guidance and laminar targeting (Du et al., 2008; Sandberg et al., 2016; Sussel et al., 1999). Another example is WNT signaling in hippocampal development. Wnt7a, secreted by granule cells, activates the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, leading to upregulation in the expression of protocadherin-y cluster, necessary for axon targeting, synaptic adhesion, and stabilization of neuron-neuron contacts (Chen et al., 2016; Ramos-Fernandez et al., 2021; Teo and Salinas, 2021). In other midline structures, canonical WNT signaling engages the β-catenin-TCF/LEF complex and indirectly activates LHX2, which regulates the axon guidance molecules Robo1 and Robo2, essential for thalamocortical axon guidance, as well as adhesion molecules, such as netrin receptor 1 (DCC), cadherins, and integrins, to ensure correct axon pathfinding and connectivity (Herrera and Escalante, 2022; Marcos-Mondejar et al., 2012). FGF8 at the IsO induces the expression of TFs (e.g., EN1 and PAX2) in the anterior hindbrain that upregulate LHX1/5, specifying GABAergic lineages, including Purkinje cells in the cerebellum. In postmitotic Purkinje cells, the expression of the nuclear receptor RORα activates the expression of downstream adhesion molecules genes such as Cbln1 and Pcdh10, to orchestrate synaptic adhesion and connectivity (Joyner et al., 2000; Ribeiro and Sherrard, 2023). Other FGFs can affect neuronal connectivity by forming gradients to provide directional information for axon growth. FGF2 can elicit cortical pyramidal neurons’ interstitial axonal branching, leading to layer V projection neuron extension (Szebenyi et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2023).

Further investigations are needed to reach a comprehensive understanding about the role of morphogen-triggered GRNs in assuring specificity and accuracy of neural connectivity establishment during development; a significant challenge is that modeling in animals will not reproduce the complexity of human brain connections, hence the need of more sophisticated human model systems.

Reproducing morphogen patterning in neural organoids

While the action of morphogens gradients has been characterized by numerous gain- and loss-of-function studies in animal models such as Drosophila or mouse, their timing, spatial distribution, and role in human development remain poorly investigated. Human brain organoids from pluripotent stem cells—either induced (iPSC) or embryonic (ESC)—have emerged as powerful models to study the intricacies of human brain development, function, and disease. This approach was pioneered by Yoshiki Sasai and colleagues, who demonstrated that both human and mouse ESCs can be guided to generate a self-organizing 3D stratified neural structures resembling embryonic tissue, including cerebral cortex, optic cup, cerebellum, hippocampus, ventral telencephalon, and midbrain (Eiraku et al., 2008; Kadoshima et al., 2013; Kawasaki et al., 2000; Mizuseki et al., 2003; Muguruma et al., 2015; Sakaguchi et al., 2015). Sasai’s protocol was first applied to human iPSC by Mariani et al. (2012).

Human iPSC/ESC are preferred over mouse cells for studying neuronal diversity and brain development for better reflecting unique features of human neurogenesis. The human brain is markedly different from the mouse brain in both structure and developmental timeline, and can be characterized by an expanded and more complex cortex, prolonged maturation, and the presence of specialized progenitor populations such as outer radial glia, rare or absent in rodents (Pollen et al., 2015). While the cerebral cortex displays the most pronounced species-specific differences (Otani et al., 2016; Rakic, 1995) other brain regions, such as thalamus, basal ganglia, midbrain, and cerebellum show important divergences, including a protracted timeline, slower and more extended maturation, and distinct transcriptomic (Haldipur et al., 2019; La Manno et al., 2016). These differences contribute to the larger diversity of neuronal subtypes and higher neural circuit complexity of the human brain (Kanari et al., 2025). Direct comparative studies using human, primate, and mouse pluripotent stem cells revealed such species-specific timing differences in neurogenesis (Hodge et al., 2019). The growing need to study how morphogens concentration gradients can precisely pattern human neural tissue led to several technological advances to reproduce morphogen gradients in vitro in iPSC- and ESC-based human systems. This includes the design of culture devices based on microfluidic or passive diffusion to pattern brain organoids or monolayer and to generate organoid models engineered to contain organizer-like region (Figure 3; Table 1).

D-V neural tube patterning has been modeled in mouse and human ESCs using various techniques. Uzel et al. designed one of the first microfluidic platforms for simultaneous or sequential application of orthogonal gradients of RA and SAG (an SHH agonist) to mimic the spatial differentiation of motor neurons in the spinal cord (Uzel et al., 2016). Another microdevice was designed to generate gradients of SHH and BMP by passive diffusion in a mouse ESC line (Demers et al., 2016). The mechanisms underlying rostro-caudal or antero-posterior (A-P) patterning have been modeled by Rifes and colleagues (Rifes et al., 2020) who developed a microfluidic-controlled stem cell regionalization (MiSTr) device. This system exposed hESCs to a WNT-activating linear gradient, enabling their differentiation in just over two weeks into a neural monolayer patterned in the A-P dimension, from forebrain to hindbrain fates, reproducing as well the formation of the IsO marking the midbrain/hindbrain boundary (Rifes et al., 2020).

This platform was utilized by another group (Sanchís-Calleja et al., 2024) to generate dual morphogen gradients of an SHH agonist and a WNT antagonist in organoids and compare it to bathe application of the same morphogens. In the planar-monolayer MiSTr tissue, low SHH regions favored the development of cortical and lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE) progenitors and neurons, and higher SHH concentrations mainly induced MGE fates. In contrast, the lowest concentration of SHH in the organoids induced the specification of MGE progenitors and interneurons (FOXG1+ and NKX2-1+ cell), while high SHH induced a combination of hypothalamic (FOXG1− and NKX2-1+ cell) and diencephalic floor plate fates (FOXA2+ cell). The study suggested that the distinct fate outcomes may be due to differences in morphogen availability and responsiveness attributed to differences in tissue architecture and cell adhesion between the two systems (monolayer and organoids). Tissue geometry, local microenvironment, and intrinsic cellular properties also determine the magnitude of morphogen’s response.

Li et al., employed a localized passive diffusion device with crosslinked gelatin on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) film to generate a gradient of the SHH agonist purmorphamine, in order to induce D-V patterning in iPSC derived neuroepithelial sheets (Li et al., 2021). Leveraging similar principles, Pavon et al. developed a method by assembling PDMS devices capable of creating a sustained chemical gradient of purmorphamine to pattern brain organoids (MIBOs) into GE regions. Mature MIBOs were analyzed using single-cell RNA sequencing revealing LGE-like and CGE-like cell types, with less contribution of the MGE, with stable neural activity up to 5.5 months in culture (Pavon et al., 2024).

While passive diffusion can establish local morphogen gradients, long-range patterning depends on the diffusion coefficients of a morphogen and its kinetics, and it can be improved using controlled diffusion by using microfluidics or through hydrogels. A recent example is the human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-based chemical gradient system developed by Xue and colleagues to recreate neural tube-like structure known as μNTLS (Xue et al., 2024). By taking advantage of microcontact printing, human iPSC colonies were cultured on Geltrex adhesive islands to guide the formation, under controllable morphogen microfluidic gradients, of neural tube-like tissue with in vivo-like rostrocaudal cell differentiation and organization. Unfortunately, microfluidic technology has been mostly applied to adherent monolayer tissue patterning and needs to be refined for implementation in 3D neural organoids.

To achieve a higher degree of complexity, our group recently developed a novel dual orthogonal-morphogen assisted patterning system (DuoMAPS) made in PDMS consisting of a closed culture area surrounded by sources and sinks able to recreate orthogonal morphogens gradient by diffusion and trigger position-based fate acquisition (Scuderi et al., 2025). We tested a simultaneous and orthogonally oriented exposure to WNT and SHH signaling pathways to generate D-V and A-P patterning at once in human iPSC-derived embryoid bodies. Our device generates a diffusion-based gradient and allows organoid retrieval from the chip for long-term culture. After just 5 days of exposure to the oriented morphogen gradients, DuoMAPS-patterned organoids cultivated for 2 months were able to generate numerous cell lineages spanning forebrain, midbrain, to hindbrain-derived lineages, which correlated well with lineages observed in human fetal brain data. Many of the morphogen’s rules described before could be verified in DuoMAPS. Receiving cells in organoids interpreted their respective position within the culture area by sensing the concentration of morphogens, activating specific GRNs and different sets of terminal cell fate genes at specific positions in this device. Additionally, we reported a reproducible quantitative assessments of morphogen patterning on organoids from multiple iPSC lines, while also noticing a different propensity of each line to respond by activating the downstream GRNs. This pointed to the possibility of a different competence state of the initial embryoid bodies generated from each of the iPSC line studied, which might depend on several factors including genetic background, somatic mutations, reprogramming, or the intrinsic epigenetic state of the cells.

Another prominent strategy in the field involves recreating organizer structures embedded directly into organoids. Cederquist et al. pioneered this approach by generating an SHH gradient within forebrain organoids by implanting SHH-expressing cells at one pole of the organoid (Cederquist et al., 2019). D-V and A-P axes were established, leading to the formation of major forebrain subdivisions with reproducible topographic organization in human brain organoids. The SHH signaling center induced the expression of specific TFs of five topographically distinct forebrain domains (neocortex, LGE, MGE, anterior hypothalamic, and ventro-posterior hypothalamic regions) positioned according to their distance from the SHH source, similar to their arrangement in the developing brain. This approach demonstrated that a localized SHH source forming a gradient is more effective in simulating in vivo gradient than bath application for generating complex forebrain structures.

Leveraging a similar principle, Bosone et al., fused an organizer-like structure releasing FGF8 into human elongated cortical organoid engineered using PDMS molds to reproduce axial cortical patterning. In these polarized assembloids, regions closer to the FGF8 source exhibited increased expression of rostral markers (e.g., SP8, LMO4, and CPNE8), while distal regions showed higher levels of caudal markers (e.g., NR2F1, FGFR3, and EMX1), hence replicating the in vivo rostro-caudal gene expression patterns of the developing human cortex (Bosone et al., 2024). Caged photocleavable conjugated morphogens that are released upon local photo-stimulation have been used for spatiotemporal release of an SHH activator to modulate SHH signaling in forebrain organoids (Misawa et al., 2020). Additionally, organizing centers have been established within organoids using genetically engineered cells that upregulate SHH expression to model dorsal-to-ventral topographical organization (De Santis et al., 2021). These organizers can be also optogenetically activated, as illustrated in a recent article from Legnini and colleagues that proposes this strategy to study cell fates and tissue patterning in organoids in combination with spatial transcriptomics (Legnini et al., 2023). However, the application of these optogenetic approaches for orthogonal or multi-morphogen gradients application has yet to be tested.

Spatial patterning has been also attempted using morphogen-loaded beads acting as local organizers in an on-chip system (Ben-Reuven and Reiner, 2020). The researchers adapted classical developmental studies done in chick embryos to organoids and fabricated an on-chip imaging device to observe the real-time effects of WNT/BMP gradients on key genes within the organoids. By adjusting the concentrations of CHIR99021(Wnt agonist) and BMP4, they could control the expression of genes related to telencephalon, medial pallium, midbrain, and the isthmus. However, since this system is sealed, it was not possible to access the tissue for performing manipulations during the experimental timeline and the authors did not characterize long-term neuronal maturation and their functional properties upon extended culture. While RNA-seq platforms were widely adopted to determine cellular heterogeneity and regional identities in these patterned organoids, there is a lack of information on topographical organization and cell-cell interaction, important to infer cell states and state transitions within these connections and to identify the underlying organizational principles.

Neural function and dysfunction involve the crosstalk between more than two regions of the CNS. Even though simplified and of smaller size, brain organoids constitute a reductionist approach for studying complex connectivity, notably with assembloids formed by the fusion of organoids specified toward different brain regions to facilitate the study of inter-regional communication. Assembloids have been used to model functional circuits of cortico-striatal connectivity, cortex-thalamus networks, hypothalamus-pituitary signaling, and cortico-motor pathways (Bagley et al., 2017; Birey and Pasca, 2022; Jacob et al., 2020; Kim and Jiang, 2021; Miura et al., 2020; Reumann et al., 2023; Sloan et al., 2018; Xiang et al., 2018). The development of assembloids model systems promise to improve our understanding of how functional connections are first established and perturbed in complex neurodevelopmental disorders. One limitation of current assembloids models is the challenging or tedious control of spatial orientation during fusion, leading to high heterogeneity, inefficient assembly, and variable reproducibility (Ao et al., 2021). Recent attempts successfully modeled long-range inter-organoid axonal connections (Osaki et al., 2024). Advances in bioengineering, microfluidics, and bioprinting are actively being explored to enhance their physiological relevance and reproducibility (Miura et al., 2020; Reumann et al., 2023; Sloan et al., 2018).

Pluripotent stem cells quality matters

hPSCs, including ESC and iPSC, have transformed regenerative medicine and disease modeling by enabling the derivation of patient-specific cellular models of systems and organs. However, substantial variability in differentiation potential among hPSC lines raises questions regarding both the source of this variation and its significance for clinical translation.

Donor cell characteristics—primarily people’s personal genomes—is one of the major sources of variability. In addition, emerging evidence indicate that hPSC accumulate somatic mutations during lifetime (e.g., single nucleotide and copy number variants), such that different cells in the same individual do not have the same genome, a phenomenon called somatic mosaicism. Hence, tissue source and age may also be contributing to line-to-line variation, in that older donors may have higher somatic mutation burdens (Bae et al., 2022; Lodato et al., 2018), and cells and tissues may differ in somatic mutation loads, particularly those with greater exposure to environmental mutagens. For instance, fibroblasts carry hundreds to thousands of somatic SNVs (Abyzov et al., 2012, 2017) that differ among iPSC lines derived from the same donor. Maintaining genetic stability over extended passages is essential, requiring routine assessments such as karyotyping and genomic sequencing. Therefore, is crucial to confirm genome integrity and check it regularly overtime (Ludwig et al., 2023). Variability can stem also from reprogramming methods and culture conditions. Culture conditions play a critical role in maintaining iPSC quality. Variations in media composition, supplements, and laboratory practices affect growth, pluripotency, and differentiation. Standardizing protocols—such as using chemically defined media and implementing consistent handling procedures—can help minimize such variability. Such culture and batch-dependent effects are commonplace, but their magnitude appears to be relatively small, relative to donor- and line-dependent effects (Scuderi et al., 2025). For modeling disease, the best approaches to deal with variation are to use a sufficient number of genetically distinct lines to model natural human diversity, or, if variation is mostly technical, remove variable transcripts from analysis.

Conclusion

Patterning is intrinsically robust. While technical variations do exist given the fragility of in vitro systems that is subjected to noise (batch-to batch variations in culture reagents and sequencing and technical differences across preparations; cell-to-cell interactions) these variations are generally small in magnitude. Most of the variation in iPSC patterning and differentiation is intrinsic to a specific cell line and derives from the variations in the genome of the donor or from somatic mutations carried by each line, which can vary from a few hundred SNVs to large structural variations and aneuploidies causing genomic instability. There could also be permanent epigenetic changes due to environmental manipulation, causing a general change in “state” of a given line. Line-specific genetic and epigenetic variations can cause different levels of key TFs expressed by cells of a particular line, pushing cells toward one fate or another, especially during the transition state. Additionally, cells of one line may interpret morphogen gradients differently because of intrinsic differences in receptor density, which introduce a bias in signal transduction. Experimental replications in organoids systems are challenging, but also needed, because these models reflect how cells and organisms can control the self-organizing nature of development.
