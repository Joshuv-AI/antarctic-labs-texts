Prediction of non-muscle invasive bladder cancer recurrence using deep learning of pathology image
Authors: Guang-Yue Wang, Jing-Fei Zhu, Qi-Chao Wang, Jia-Xin Qin, Xin-Lei Wang, Xing Liu, Xin-Yu Liu, Jun-Zhi Chen, Jie-Fei Zhu, Shi-Chao Zhuo, Di Wu, Na Li, Liu Chao, Fan-Lai Meng, Hao Lu, Zhen-Duo Shi, Zhi-Gang Jia, Cong-Hui Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We aimed to build a deep learning-based pathomics model to predict the early recurrence of non-muscle-infiltrating bladder cancer (NMIBC) in this work. A total of 147 patients from Xuzhou Central Hospital were enrolled as the training cohort, and 63 patients from Suqian Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University were enrolled as the test cohort. Based on two consecutive phases of patch level prediction and WSI-level predictione, we built a pathomics model, with the initial model developed in the training cohort and subjected to transfer learning, and then the test cohort was validated for generalization. The features extracted from the visualization model were used for model interpretation. After migration learning, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for the deep learning-based pathomics model in the test cohort was 0.860 (95% CI 0.752–0.969), with good agreement between the migration training cohort and the test cohort in predicting recurrence, and the predicted values matched well with the observed values, with p values of 0.667766 and 0.140233 for the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, respectively. The good clinical application was observed using a decision curve analysis method. We developed a deep learning-based pathomics model showed promising performance in predicting recurrence within one year in NMIBC patients. Including 10 state prediction NMIBC recurrence group pathology features be visualized, which may be used to facilitate personalized management of NMIBC patients to avoid ineffective or unnecessary treatment for the benefit of patients.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is one of the top 10 most prevalent cancers in the world1. It begins in the bladder’s mucosal layer, with approximately three-quarters of cases confined to the mucosa or lamina propria, and is referred to as non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC)2. Although transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) is the gold standard for treating NMIBC, the disease has a 15–70% chance of relapsing within a year3 and can develop into muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC). NMIBC is one of the most demanding illnesses due to its propensity for recurrence and the necessity for many treatment sessions. As a result, determining the likelihood of recurrence is crucial to NMIBC postoperative care.

Appropriate adjuvant therapy based on the patient’s risk level is crucial in lowering the likelihood of recurrence. The Club Urologico Espanol de Tratamiento Oncologico (CUETO) score model, European Organzation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) risk tables, and the American Urology Association (AUA)/the European Association of Urology (EAU) recommendations are the three most widely utilized risk categorization techniques at the moment. These risk classification methods based on the clinical signs and pathomics characteristics of the tumor, including the depth of infiltration, tumor stage, number of tumors, size and tumor grading4–6. However, internal and external validation tests have shown that EORTC and CUETO have little discriminatory power for recurrence7. According to research, the percentages of cancer recurrence within two years of the first transurethral resection are, for example, 15–25% for low-risk patients, 30–40% for intermediate-risk patients, and 30% for high-risk patients, even with the use of various risk assessment techniques8. As the categorization of patients into risk categories is subjective, the idea of what makes a low-risk vs a high-risk patient may differ across practitioners.

To improve the low interobserver consistency in the diagnosis of cancer of the urinary tract among urologic pathologists, more research is required to uncover the specific pathologic characteristics that may correlate with an aggressive prognosis and responsiveness to intravenous treatment9. As artificial intelligence (AI) has developed recently, deep learning has done well in processing tumor-related histopathology pictures, which has motivated the continuous digitalization of histological sections. The threshold-based tumor-first label inferencing classifier performed well in identifying lung tumors and confused nonneoplastic tissues, demonstrating that deep learning can tackle challenging multi-class tissue classification in real-world histopathomics settings10. Deep learning on histological slides from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients was used to suggest a clinical predictive classifier. This classifier improves HCC prognosis prediction and identifies individuals who benefit from more intense therapy11. In addition, deep learning may predict leiomyosarcoma (LMS) prognosis and assists pathologists diagnose faster and more accurately12. Woerl et al. constructed deep learning models for predicting molecular subtypes of MIBC samples with similar or better performance than pathologists, confirming that deep learning can predict important molecular features of MIBC patients from pathomics histological sections alone, which may significantly improve the clinical management of the disease in the future13. Lucas et al. combined digital histopathology sections with clinical data to predict recurrence in NMIBC patients at 1 and 5 years using deep learning techniques. The model did not work well in predicting recurrence within 1 year in NMIBC patients, with accuracy and AUC of 65% (95% CI 52–76%), and 62% (95% CI 0.50–0.75) respectively14. The model’s applicability to Asian populations is also still debatable because of the major demographic and data preparation variations. In order to predict the recurrence of NMIBC patients within a year, it is important to develop a novel, multicenter, and better-performing deep learning model.

Using data from many centers, we created a prediction model for this study with the main goal of determining if recurrence happened within a year following the original TURBT. The model is a pathomics patch fusion-based model that integrates deep learning and traditional machine learning methods, training on the Xuzhou Central Hospital dataset, then migrating to the Suqian Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University dataset, and demonstrating good predictive accuracy. The probability of recurrence varies between variant subtypes and uroepithelial carcinoma alone, hence in this study we only chose pathomics histological sections with a pathomics diagnosis of uroepithelial carcinoma to prevent variant subtypes from affecting the prediction findings.

Materials and methods

Patients

Retrospective data was gathered on 210 patients who had TURBT at Xuzhou Central Hospital and Suqian Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University in China from 2013 to 2021. The training and test cohorts for our study were selected from Xuzhou Central Hospital and Suqian Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria included: (1) patients undergoing their first transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT); (2) availability of complete clinical data; (3) histopathologically confirmed primary urothelial carcinoma of stage Ta or T1; (4) no other tumors present or previously treated. The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients who underwent radical cystectomy within one year after the first TURBT; (2) lack of corresponding histopathological images post-TURBT; (3) cases where the tumor could not be completely resected during the first TURBT, resulting in carcinoma in situ. The 8th edition of the TNM classification of the International Union Against Cancer (UICC) was used to stage tumors, and the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) categories were used to grade tumors. After the first TURBT, patients with histologically proven Ta or T1 primary uroepithelial cell cancer were included in this study. We eliminated patients who received radical cystectomy after the initial TURBT, patients with lacking histological pictures, and cases of carcinoma in situ due to the difficulties of entirely resecting the tumor during the initial TURBT. All patients were treated with bladder instillation chemotherapy within 24 h of surgery, according to EAU risk stratification and treatment criteria, and a few intermediate and high-risk tumors were treated with Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) instillation immunotherapy. Depending on the patient’s health, all patients are monitored periodically. This includes clinical symptoms, urine habits, abdominopelvic CT scans, cystoscopies, etc. The total follow-up period is at least 1 year. Patients with recurrence of bladder cancer verified by cystoscopy and pathology were identified as the recurrence group, and the remainder as the non-recurrence group, with a total of 210 patients included in the research. This study was authorized by the Xuzhou Central Hospital Biomedical Research Ethics Review Committee (approval number: XZXY-LK-20220805-067) and was carried out at the Department of Urology. The requirement for informed con-sent was waived by the Xuzhou Central Hospital Biomedical Research Ethics Review Committee due to the retrospective nature of this study. Research involving human research participants have been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Whole slide images dataset acquisition

In patients with postoperative carcinoma tissue by 4% paraformaldehyde fixed paraffin embedding slice, slice by xylene, anhydrous ethanol and gradient alcohol dewaxing. The nucleus was stained with hematoxylin and the cytoplasm was stained with eosin. The sections were sealed by dehydration in gradient alcohol, absolute ethanol, and xylene. Microscopic examination was performed and images were collected. Before scanning the HE-stained sections of the NMIBC patient, two pathologists evaluated all histological sections under the microscope, including immunohistochemical and histochemically stained sections utilized as diagnostic assistance. They also regularly looked into the patient’s medical files. All tissue sections in this study were scanned using a full section imaging scanner (Olympus VS120 Digital Section Scanning System; Olympus Corp; Tokyo, Japan) at 20× equivalent magnification, and the whole slide images (WSIs) were obtained in vsi (scanner raw) format.

WSIs annotation and processing

Tumor regions in WSIs are considered regions of interest (ROI), but the WSIs also included fibrous connective tissue, adipose tissue, muscle tissue, inflammatory cells, hemorrhage, necrosis and other tissue types, so a pathologist used the software OlyVIA version 2.9 (https://www.olympus-sis.com/) to annotate the WSIs. Each diseased tissue type is represented by a separate irregular polygonal colored region, which is then evaluated and corrected by a second pathologist to make sure no non-tumor tissue is present in the annotated area. Because the WSIs are too large in memory to be compatible with mainstream computer vision tools, we cut all WSIs into patches (290 × 290 pixels) by non-overlapping sampling at 20× equivalent magnification (350 μm/pixel) using the software described above, resulting in 114,462 patches.

To achieve a standard normal distribution of picture intensities as inputs, all the tiny patches underwent color normalization using the Reinhard approach and were then further normalized using Z-scores on RGB channels. Our work utilizes random horizontal and vertical data flipping as an online data enhancement. For training patches, all augmentation methods and normalization were used, whereas only normalization was used for test patches.

We then divided all patches into two cohorts, with patches from Xuzhou Central Hospital as the training cohort and patches from Suqian Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University as the test cohort, and assigned an outcome label to each patch based on patient follow-up results.

Building a model based on deep learning and machine learning

Our deep learning pipeline offered two different predictions: one at the patch level, and the other at the WSI level. Due to the heterogeneity of image size and tumor, the WSI was first divided into small patches, and then the features derived from different pipelines were aggregated by an ensemble algorithm to obtain predictions of WSI levels. During the patch-level prediction, ResNet50 pretrained in ImageNet classification competition was used to compute the patch likelihood where the patches were assigned with the WSI’s label. A mini-batch gradient descent method was used to optimize the network using Softmax cross-entropy (CE) loss.

The initial parameters of the model are trained in the training cohort and transfer learning is implemented on the data in the training cohort to generalize the model across a cohort with high heterogeneity. The model weights were reused in the patch-level discriminators to perform the transfer learning, and a small amount of labeled data was used to fine-tune the weights.

The WSI-level prediction was obtained by first training a deep learning model to predict the labels and probabilities for all patches and then using a classifier to aggregate the patch likelihoods (“Supplementary Information”).

To aggregate the patch likelihoods, we used the Patch Likelihood Histogram (PALHI) pipeline and the Bag of Words (BoW) pipeline, two separate machine learning techniques15. In PALHI, the WSI was represented by a histogram of the occurrence of the patch likelihood, whereas in BoW, each patch was mapped to a TF-IDF floating-point variable, and a TF-IDF feature vector was computed to represent the WSI. Using these feature vectors, traditional machine learning classifiers were then trained to predict the recurrence status of each WSI.

Here, we fused prediction and probability histogram and TF-IDF features together. Random forest (RF), a kind of gradient-boosted decision tree, was employed in the following pipeline. Gride search was used to analyze the training cohort to determine the hyperparameters for the WSI-level classifier. Detailed integration details and techniques are described in Appendix 1.

We input the final features into the machine learning models like RF, logistic regression (LR), multilayer perceptron (MLP), support vector machine (SVM), and so on for model construction. Here, we used five cross-validations to get the ultimate radiomics model.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was conducted using Python software (version 3.7.12; http://www.python.org). Use of the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as a criterion for optimal prediction model determination. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to determine whether the expected and actual probabilities calculated by the model fit. The decision curve analysis were plotted to determine the clinical benefit of the final pathohistological model.

First, we fed patches into a ResNet50 convolutional neural network to obtain patch-level recurrence predictions. To combine various patch-level predictions into a recurrence score at the WSI level, we then trained two separate pipelines (PALHI and BoW). To select the optimal machine learning algorithm architecture model, we tested common machine learning algorithm models such as RF, LR, MLP, SVM, etc. Utilizing the test cohort, we evaluated the performance. Discussion

It has been extensively utilized in precision medicine thanks to the quick development of AI and cutting-edge image analysis techniques. It initially performed well when processing large amounts of clinical data, but over time it developed to be used in radiomics, pathomics, and even multi-omics studies, demonstrating significant advantages in the automated diagnosis and prognosis prediction of several tumors16–18. The preponderance of studies in radiomics may be explained by the greater accessibility of radiographic data compared to pathology. However, radiological images reflect more anatomical features of the tumor and ignore the important information contained in the tumor cells and extracellular matrix.

According to the study, convolutional neural networks can accurately predict patient survival by directly extracting tumor traits from histology pictures19. A deep learning-based algorithm that could predict the six most frequent genetic changes straight from the slides has also been demonstrated in certain research20. Therefore, the development of technology to digitize pathology pictures offers a crucial method of retrieving histological information. Currently, deep learning-based pathomics models of various types are more frequently used for bladder cancer subtype classification, grading, and staging with similar or even higher accuracy than pathologists, while there are fewer studies on predicting recurrence21–23. In this work, we developed the first deep learning-based pathomics model combining WSIs from various centers, enabling us to assess the likelihood of recurrence within a year for each NMIBC patient with a pathomics diagnosis. Histological assessment of the depth of bladder cancer invasion is sometimes difficult for TURBT specimens. Therefore, it is important to distinguish NMIBC from MIBC by histological AI, allowing it to automatically output recurrence risk as well as diagnosis (T-classification).

This study provided two levels of prediction: patch-level and WSI-level. We first segment the WSIs into small patches in order to ensure the integrity and realism of the model, and then we fuse the features from the patch-level predictions into the machine learning algorithm to finally obtain the WSI-level predictions. The performance of the model built by directly inputting WSIs into deep learning is not satisfactory, and the WSIs are heterogeneous among them and the image size is large. In the patch-level prediction process, to save the work of retraining the convolutional network, we chose the pre-trained ResNet50 from the ImageNet classification competition to calculate the patch likelihood. Since our tissue sections span a long period of years and come from multiple centers, with the change of staining technique different years of tissue sections may show differences in tissue structure, and tissue sections acquired from different centers may also have different tissue appearance due to different staining techniques, and such differences cannot be handled by simple color normalization alone. Therefore, we implemented a more advanced approach to migration learning and carefully set the learning rate by the cosine decay learning rate algorithm so that the model can generalize better and promote the model in different cohorts. In the process of WSI-level prediction, two independent machine-learning pipelines are used to obtain feature vectors from different perspectives, and the fused features are fed into different machine-learning algorithms to obtain the final model.

Lucas et al. were the first to apply deep learning to WSIs to predict recurrence in NMIBC patients within 1 year by constructing a deep learning model to predict recurrence in NMIBC within 1 year using pathology image data alone and clinical data combined with pathology images, respectively. The accuracy of these two models was 61% and 65%, and AUC was 0.56 (95% CI 0.43–0.68) and 0.62 (95% CI 0.50–0.75), respectively14. In contrast, Lucas et al. used only single-center data, whereas our study utilized multicenter data. The accuracy and AUC of the final pathomics model in the test cohort were 81% and 0.86 (95% CI 0.75–0.97), respectively, and the performance of the model was significantly improved. It is worth mentioning that the accuracy and AUC of our models constructed by other machine learning methods also outperform both Lucas’ models.

In our cohort, subsequent diagnostic concordance tests showed satisfactory performance. This suggests that our model has the potential as a good method for predicting relapse status in NMIBC patients. In summary, it demonstrates that our model is progressive and superior in terms of methodological selection and data processing, and that it can be applied to new data cohorts; in fact, our model beats all other typical clinical or pathomics variables in predicting recurrence. Currently, developing an individualized management program to reduce the risk of recurrence in patients with NMIBC is a great challenge. Based on the results of the current study, our pathomics model can provide individualized management for patients to develop a postoperative adjuvant treatment plan with an appropriate follow-up plan.

In addition, there are some limitations regarding our study. Although this study was a retrospective study using multi-center data, it was still not possible to avoid selection bias, no matter how strict the exclusion criteria were. And the number of included patients was small, the results were only cross-validated, and further prospective validation in a larger sample is needed. Second, we utilized manual annotation of WSIs, which may lead to bias, and opted for manual segmentation in processing WSIs into patches, a task that is labor-intensive and time-consuming, greatly increasing the burden of constructing the model, especially for clinicians, and the quality of the final resulting patches may also be biased due to the involvement of different personnel. Therefore, the development of accurate automated or semi-automated segmentation methods may help pathomics to be more easily applied to clinical practice.

The interpretability of deep learning has been a challenge, and to make the model more understandable and trustable to clinicians, we delve into the predictive mechanism of the model. We first visualized the patches with recurrence or non-recurrence labels, which can be visualized by the colors in the heat map to see the regions with a higher weight. To understand more about the factors that play a specific role in the model, we tried to visualize the features imported into the RF and found that 10 features play a major role in the model including 1 histogram feature and 9 TF-IDF features, which indicates the possibility of using the BoW pipeline to better handle the patch features. In future work, we can focus on identifying these features to improve the efficiency and accuracy of the model. In addition, this suggests that not all information in WSIs is useful for pairs, and is also consistent with the findings of Cao et al.15. Our pathomics approach also offers a number of additional benefits. The first advantage of utilizing biological specimens is that the WSIs data may be reused. Second, NMIBC recurrence prediction may be improved further by employing a range of cutting-edge strategies throughout time, such as deep learning algorithms and artificial intelligence. Future work will allow us to accumulate pathology images from around the world as a centerpiece, which will further improve the performance of current deep-learning models. Patients can benefit from the stratification of risk and guidance of treatment options, as well as the avoidance of ineffective or unnecessary treatments if predictive models can be improved so that they can be used more broadly to help support clinical decision-making and benefit patients.

Deep learning models, such as the one we employed in our study, excel in integrating diverse data types and extracting meaningful insights from unstructured data, such as histopathology slides. This facilitates a more comprehensive assessment of tumor characteristics, potentially leading to more precise recurrence predictions. However, we also acknowledge and discuss several limitations associated with these technologies. One of the primary concerns is the requirement for extensive annotated datasets to train these models effectively, which can be resource-intensive and time-consuming. There is also the challenge of model interpretability, often referred to as the ‘black box’ issue, where it becomes difficult to trace how these models arrive at their conclusions. Moreover, there are potential biases that may arise during model training if the data is not representative of the broader population. This can affect the generalizability of the models across different demographic and clinical settings.

In response to these concerns, our study makes concerted efforts to validate the model across diverse cohorts and incorporates mechanisms to mitigate bias and enhance interpretability. We have adopted techniques such as Grad-CAM for visual explanations of model predictions, which helps in understanding which features the model focuses on when making predictions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, due to the high recurrence rate that is associated with NMIBC patients, accurate prediction of disease recurrence is essential for the development of adjuvant therapy and follow-up programs that are tailored to the specific needs of individual patients. Our proposed deep learning-based pathomics model showed promising results in predicting recurrence within 1 year in NMIBC patients and tested the performance of the model in an independent cohort. With this model, we identified 10 pathomics histological imaging features to predict NMIBC recurrence status, and although not yet validated in clinical practice, this deep learning approach has the potential to enhance recurrence prediction. Since this is still an exploratory study, further work will be required in the form of a large multicenter study to refine and validate our proposed approach.
