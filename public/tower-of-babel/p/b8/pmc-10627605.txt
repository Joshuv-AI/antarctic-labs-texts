Exploration of the shared genetic biomarkers in Alzheimer’s disease and chronic kidney disease using integrated bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Junqi Li, Ying Li, Jiachang Niu, Jiacheng Zhang, Xunshu Cheng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In order to investigate the potential link between Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), we conducted a comprehensive analysis using a bioinformatics approach. We downloaded AD and CKD datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and analyzed differentially expressed genes and weighted gene co-expression networks to identify candidate genes for AD and CKD. We used a combination of the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator and random forest algorithms to select the shared genes. Subsequently, we shared genes and performed an immune infiltration analysis to investigate the association between different immune cell types and shared genes. Finally, we elucidated the relationship between the expression levels of the shared genes in disease samples and cells using single-cell analysis. Our analysis identified 150 candidate genes that may be primarily involved in immune inflammatory responses and energy metabolism pathways. We found that JunD Proto-Oncogene, ALF transcription elongation factor 1, and ZFP36 Ring Finger Protein Like 1 were the best co-diagnostic markers for AD and CKD based on the results of Least Absolute Shrinkage Selection Operator analysis and the random forest algorithm. Based on the results of immune infiltration analysis, macrophages and T-cells play a significant role in the progression of AD and CKD. Our scRNA-sequencing data showed that the 3 shared genes in AD were significantly expressed in astrocytes, excitatory neurons, oligodendrocytes, and MAIT cells. The 3 shared genes in CKD were significantly expressed in oligodendrocytes, neutrophils, fibroblasts, astrocytes, and T-cells. JunD Proto-Oncogene, ALF transcription elongation factor 1, and ZFP36 Ring Finger Protein Like 1 genes are the best diagnostic markers for AD and CKD.

1. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a cognitive disorder characterized by neurodegenerative pathology, cognitive decline, and memory loss. It is the most common cause of dementia in the elderly. Growing evidence suggests that chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant risk factor for AD, and CKD patients are at a higher risk of cognitive impairment than normal individuals.[1,2]

Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 9.1% of the world’s population had CKD in 2017.[3] In China, a national study found that the prevalence of CKD was about 10.8%.[4,5] A meta-analysis of 54,779 patients demonstrated that the risk of cognitive impairment was 39% higher in CKD patients than in non-CKD patients, and the risk of cognitive impairment in CKD patients increased progressively with declining renal function.[6] Studies have shown that for every 10 mL/min/1.73 m reduction in glomerular filtration rate, the risk of cognitive impairment increases by 11%, and for people over 65 years old, by 17%.[7,8] Nevertheless, it has been observed that cognitive dysfunction among individuals with CKD can be notably ameliorated following dialysis or kidney transplantation.[9,10] Notably, a recent investigation by Tian et al[11] discovered that the prolonged administration of furosemide reduced pathological and cognitive impairments in mice with the APP/PS1 model. Furthermore, a clinical trial conducted by Boada et al[12] demonstrated that replacing plasma and albumin could decelerate cognitive deterioration in patients with AD.

Most people with CKD have diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease as part of their development.[13] These co-occurring disorders can affect brain function and lead to cognitive impairments. Current research on AD caused by CKD focuses mainly on oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulation, and uremic toxins.[14,15] These factors may lead to disturbances in brain and peripheral amyloid-β-protein (Aβ) metabolism, resulting in brain Aβ accumulation, Tau hyperphosphorylation, glial cell activation, neuroinflammation, and neuronal loss, resulting in cognitive deficits that in turn exacerbate AD pathology.[16,17] Furthermore, some researchers believe that AD development may be related to the metabolic function of the kidneys and urinary proteins in CKD patients. A study showed that mice with 1 kidney removed had a 20% increase in Aβ in brain tissue after 6 months, accompanied by increased neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and cognitive impairment.[11] Compared to other populations, the clearance of Aβ in the blood of CKD patients was significantly impaired,[11] resulting in elevated levels of Aβ in the blood, directly affecting the function of neural progenitor cells, the cerebrovascular system, the adenosine system, and monoaminergic neurons, leading to brain dysfunction. Various cognitive functions are affected, including memory, attention, language, visuospatial skills, and executive functions.[18,19]

Many studies have shown a close relationship between AD and CKD.[20] However, most of these studies have been conducted from a clinical perspective, and the potential biogenetic markers between the two and their molecular mechanisms still need to be better understood. Given this, this study integrates and analyses genetic data related to the pathogenesis of AD and CKD from public databases to provide new insights into the biological mechanisms of the 2 diseases and to help develop preventive and curative measures against both diseases. In this study, we conducted an integrated bioinformatic analysis to explore the shared genetic link between AD and CKD. We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from GSE5281, GSE28146, GSE48350, and GSE66494 datasets to determine candidate genes associated with AD co-pathogenesis with CKD using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and DEG analysis. Functional enrichment analyses, such as Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), were performed to investigate the mechanisms involved in AD and CKD. We obtained 3 shared genes after combining various machine learning algorithms, including the Least Absolute Shrinkage Selection Operator (LASSO) and random forest (RF). We conducted a correlation analysis among the types of immune cells using CIBERSORT and an assessment of the type of immune cells and the level of infiltration. Finally, we elucidated the relationship between the expression levels of the shared genes in disease samples and cells using single-cell analysis. This study provides valuable information for future diagnosis and treatment. It is the first to investigate biomarkers associated with AD and CKD and to explore the potential genetic link between these diseases.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data collection

We retrieved gene expression datasets associated with AD and CKD from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Specifically, we downloaded 5 datasets for AD, including GSE5281, GSE28146, GSE48350, GSE122063, and GSE175814. We combined 3 GEO platforms (GPL570) GSE5281, GSE28146, and GSE48350 to form a merged dataset using the ComBat function of “sva” R package to remove batch effects.[21] This merged dataset was used as the test set (Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K390), while GSE122063 and GSE175814 were selected as validation sets. To convert probe IDs to gene symbols, we used the average value of the probe loci if a gene had multiple probe loci. We also removed probes that did not match. For CKD, we obtained 3 datasets, including GSE66494 (test), GSE115857 (validation), and GSE199711 (validation), which are summarized in Table 1.

2.2. Identification of DEGs

To identify DEGs between normal and disease samples, we utilized the GEO2R online analysis tool (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/), which is based on R packages (GEOquery and Limma). The screening criteria for DEGs were a log-fold change (logFC) > 0.5 and an adjusted P value < .05. We visualized the significantly upregulated and downregulated genes using a volcano plot and a heatmap. We conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the GSEA software and reference gene sets (c2.cp.kegg.v11.0.symbols), with significance levels determined at the q-value [false discovery rate] < 0.05 and P < .05 to identify upregulated and downregulated pathways with normalized enrichment scores > 0 and normalized enrichment scores < 0, respectively[22] (Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K391).

2.3. Network building and module identification of WGCNA

We performed a WGCNA using the R package “WGCNA” to identify potential functional modules and analyze associations between modules and specific traits or phenotypes.[23] We used the “Hculst” function for hierarchical clustering to identify apparent outliers. To ensure that the gene expression relationship conformed to the scale-free network, we used the “PickSoftThreshold” function to select an appropriate soft thresholding power β. We then used the “adjacency” function to convert the similarity matrix into an adjacency matrix based on the soft thresholding parameter β. Subsequently, we transformed the adjacency matrix into a topological overlap matrix and divided genes into modules based on the degree of dissimilarity between them in the topological overlap matrix using WGCNA. Gene importance and module membership were calculated in the next step. The Spearman method was employed to determine the Spearman correlation coefficients, their corresponding P values between the control and disease groups, and the functional modules. Finally, the key module was identified, and the corresponding genes were selected for further comprehensive analysis.

2.4. Functional analysis and pathway enrichment of candidate genes

We identified candidate genes that overlapped between WGCNA and DEGs and extracted genes with overlapping functions for further functional enrichment analysis. We used GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to understand the comprehensive information about large-scale gene data sets. We performed enrichment analyses using GO and KEGG categories with “ClusterProfiler” and “DOSE” R packages to investigate the function and pathways of overlapped candidate genes.

2.5. Screening for the potential shared diagnostic genes

We used LASSO and RF machine learning algorithms to predict disease status and identify significant prognostic factors. LASSO regression analysis selected variables and regularized them simultaneously to improve the predictive capability of statistical models. We used the RF algorithm to prevent overfitting and produce interpretable results. We obtained shared diagnostic genes by crossing the shared diagnostic genes obtained by the 2 machine learning algorithms. To evaluate the effectiveness of the diagnostic model, we plotted receiver operating characteristic curves of the diagnostic model and receiver operating characteristic curves separately for individual diagnostic genes to evaluate the effectiveness of the diagnostic genes in distinguishing between disease and control samples in the GSE122063 and GSE115857 datasets.

2.6. Correlation of immune cell infiltration with shared diagnostic genes

We used the CIBERSORT algorithm, a deconvolutional mathematical approach based on genetic expression, to identify the infiltration of 22 immune cells in normal and disease samples. We used box plots to illustrate the immune cell composition of patients with varying immune patterns. We examined the associations between the potential diagnostic biomarkers and immune cells that exhibited significant alterations using the Spearman correlation coefficient and the “reshape2” and “ggExtra” functions within the R package. We also used the ssGSEA algorithm to quantify the relative levels of the 50 hallmark gene sets (h.all.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt) in the merged dataset.

2.7. Single-cell transcriptome data processing and analysis

We obtained single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for AD (GSE175814) and CKD (GSE199711) from the GEO database and analyzed it using the software package Seurat. We eliminated cells with a percentage of mitochondrial genes higher than 15%; cell RNA features less than 3, cells expressing less than 500 and greater than 15,000 genes for AD analysis, and less than 7500 genes for CKD analysis. Following the Seurat integration pipeline, we normalized gene expression in the cells using the “NormalizeData” function and processed the data using the Seurat integration pipeline. We performed a principal component analysis to extract the top 30 principal components from the top 2000 highly variable genes. We used the “FindVariableFeatures” function to preserve these components for further analysis. We performed unsupervised and unbiased clustering of cell subpopulations using the “FindNeighbors,” “FindClusters(resolution = 0.4),” and “RunUMAP” functions. Finally, we combined the manual and The human protein atlas to annotate the cell type. [cite this: The human protein atlas—Integrated omics for single cell mapping of the human proteome.]

The CKD dataset (GSE66494) contained 8 normal and 53 CKD samples, and we removed batch effects from the dataset before conducting data analysis. A total of 1362 DEGs were then identified in the AD gene dataset. In the CKD gene dataset, 4865 DEGs were identified (Fig. 1A and B).

3.3. Functional analysis and pathway enrichment of candidate genes

The results of WGCNA and DEGs screens were crossed and overlapped to produce 150 candidate genes and potential crosstalk genes between the 2 diseases (Fig. 3A). We explored common regulatory pathways by performing GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the above 150 candidate genes. Based on the results of the GO analysis presented in Figure 3C, 3 distinct categories were identified, namely biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. The DEGs associated with biological processes and molecular functions were enriched in various functions, including ribosomal small subunit assembly, rRNA binding, growth factor binding, protein N-terminus binding, and insulin − like growth factor binding (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the KEGG analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly enriched in the HIF-1 signaling pathway, Leukocyte transendothelial migration, Notch signaling pathway, and adherens junction (Fig. 3C and D).

These findings suggest that the inflammatory response and immune response are crucial factors in the development of both AD and CKD, with significant involvement of the HIF-1 signaling pathway and Notch signaling pathway.

3.4. Screening for the shared genes

Two distinct machine-learning algorithms were utilized to identify the prospective shared genes. First, a LASSO regression algorithm was performed to identify the potential shared diagnostic genes. In AD datasets, LASSO analysis identified 53 out of the 150 core cross-genes under the most appropriate λ = 0.11 (Fig. 4A). In CKD datasets, LASSO analysis identified 5 out of the 150 core cross-genes under the most appropriate λ = 0.11 (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, in AD datasets, for the RF algorithm (Fig. 4B), the top 28 feature genes with importance > 2 were determined (Figure S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K392). In CKD datasets, for the RF algorithm (Fig. 4B), the top 10 feature genes with importance > 0.25 were determined (Figure S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K392).

4. Discussion

A growing body of research indicates that CKD patients with cognitive impairment have a significantly higher risk of developing AD if their kidney function is impaired.[18,24] Therefore, we were particularly concerned about the association between AD and CKD. Based on existing genetic databases, we used bioinformatics to explore the shared genes and underlying pathological mechanisms between AD and CKD.

In this study, we integrated the genomes of AD and CKD. For the first time, we used multiple machine learning algorithms to explore common pathological mechanisms between the two, revealing potentially shared genes, shared pathways, and associated immune cells. The intersection of critical modular genes in WGCNA with DEGs to obtain 150 candidate genes. Analysis of these 150 genes by GO and KEEG showed that the results of these genes were significantly correlated with immune response and inflammatory response.

In the GO enrichment analysis, we found that protein N-terminus binding and Insulin − like growth factor had a significant immunological profile. Protein N-terminus binding is an essential biochemical reaction that alters cellular metabolism and immune response. For example, chemotactic cytokines, such as IL-8 and IP-10/CXCL10, may undergo N-terminal proteolytic modification after secretion, which guides cellular behaviors and responses.[25] Insulin − like growth factor (includes IGF-1 and IGF-2), IGF has an essential role in the regulation of the immune response; a recent study showed that IGF-2 demonstrates its anti-inflammatory properties by inducing a preprogrammed state in maturing macrophages that favors oxidative phosphorylation. This immunological preference, instilled by IGF-2, is characterized by a decrease in IL-1b production and an increase in the expression of PD-L1.[26]

The KEGG enrichment analysis results found that the HIF-1 signaling pathway, Notch signaling pathway, Leukocyte transendothelial migration, and adherens junction were closely associated with the immune response and the inflammatory response. The HIF1 signaling pathway regulates cellular response metabolism and function that regulates gene expression of glycolytic enzymes, the glucose transporter GLUT1, and inflammatory mediators.[27,28] For example, HIF-1 signaling can promote macrophage polarization by affecting glycolysis and TCA cycling. The Notch signaling pathway is one of the critical regulators of hematopoietic and immune cell differentiation. It is closely associated with various autoimmune diseases, tumorigenesis, and tumor-induced immunosuppression.[29] It has been shown that the Notch signaling pathway is involved in the activation of macrophages and regulates a variety of biological properties of macrophages.[30,31] Leukocyte transendothelial migration is a critical immune response. For example, neutrophils can rapidly migrate across the blood endothelial cell barrier into inflamed tissues to perform effector cell functions. Neutrophil migration is also influenced by various immune factors such as adhesion molecules, chemokine drive, and endothelial cell autophagy.[32,33] Adherence junctions rely primarily on the cell-cell adhesion molecules calmodulin to function.[34] One of its major components, E-cadherin, is expressed in various leukocytes.[35,36] Research shows that E-cadherin is a marker for IL-4/IL-13-exposed alternatively activated macrophages.

LASSO analysis and RF algorithms were then performed on the candidate genes. We identified the JUND, AFF1, and ZFP36L1 genes as the most crucial shared genes between AD and CKD. JUND (one of the AP-1 transcription factor subunits) is a protein-coding gene. The main pathways associated with JUND include the IL-1 family of signaling pathways and ESR-mediated signaling pathways. Experimental studies have shown that JUND can improve neurological function and brain damage by reducing neutrophil and lymphocyte ratios by inhibiting IL-1β to improve the inflammatory state.[37] In this study, significant expression of the JUND gene was found for both AD and CKD. In AD, the transcription factor JUND promotes a shift of microglia to the M1 type and enhances Aβ clearance.[38] Several experimental and clinical observations have displayed that cell proliferation plays a vital role in the progression of CKD and is positively correlated with the severity of renal lesions.[39–41] Overexpression of TGF-α causes deterioration of renal parenchyma and cell proliferation. JUND inhibits overexpression of the renal cell mitogen TGF-α and paracrine factors involved in intercommunication between renal cells and is a critical switch to inhibit cell proliferation after reducing renal units.[42]

AFF1 acts as a positive regulator of P-TEFb kinase and regulates the transcription of numerous genes.[43] A substantial body of research suggests that AFF1 is an essential correlate of systemic diseases, such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia and FRAXE mental retardation.[36,44] AFF1 has been shown to regulate the transcription of glutaminyl transferase 2 (TGM2),[43] which has a wide range of biological functions related to cell differentiation and maturation, cell morphology and adhesion, cell death, extensive crosslinking of extracellular matrix (ECM) stabilization, and cellular lipogenesis.[45] It has been shown that TGM2 has important effects on neurodegeneration and tissue fibrosis.[46] In the brain, TGM2 is the most functionally abundant glutaminyl transferase in glial cells.[47] Studies show that deletion of TGM2 in microglia leads to impaired synaptic pruning and cognitive impairment and exacerbates pathological damage in AD in mice.[48] In the single-cell analysis conducted in this study, it was observed that AFF1 exhibited significant expression in microglia, indicating its potential role in enhancing synaptic remodeling and cognitive function through the upregulation of TGM2 levels, specifically in microglia. Furthermore, the single-cell analysis also revealed significant expression of AFF1 in immune cells and fibroblasts, primarily observed in individuals with CKD. However, it is essential to note that no experimental investigations have been identified thus far to elucidate the intricate interaction mechanism between AFF1 and these cell types.

ZFP36L1, a member of the ZFP36 family, has been strongly implicated in the regulation of autoimmunity.[49] Experimental studies have demonstrated that the absence of the ZFP36L1 gene in mice can lead to developmental lethality, accompanied by severe inflammation characterized by excessive production of TNF and IL-23, resulting in cachexia and arthritis.[50–53] In the context of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, ZFP36L1 has been identified as a crucial factor in initiating CD4+ T-cell responses by T-cells. Specifically, ZFP36L1 plays a role in regulating CD4+ T-cell activation during experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis.[49] In a model of induced neuroinflammation, ZFP36L1 facilitates the clonal amplification of CD4+ T-cell antigens.[54] In AD, the activation of pro-inflammatory CD4+ T-cells, particularly the Th17 subpopulation, results in the secretion of a substantial quantity of the inflammatory factor IL-17A. This exacerbates the neuropathological changes observed in the AD brain.[55,56] The study conducted by Tian et al[57] showed that ZFP36L1 was identified as a pathogenetic marker of AD, and a strong association between ZFP36L1 and the immune response was proposed. Drawing upon prior research, it is plausible to conjecture that ZFP36L1 may also trigger CD4+ T cell responses and govern the infiltration of pro-inflammatory cells in AD progression. Additionally, Th17 subgroups play a crucial role in the progression of CKD by promoting renal fibrosis.[58] However, the precise pathogenesis underlying the involvement of ZFP36L1 in AD and CKD remains uncertain, necessitating additional investigation and comprehensive elucidation.

We found that M1 macrophages and M0 macrophages were significantly and positively correlated with JUND and AFF1 and M2 macrophages with ZFP36L1 in AD by the CIBERSORT algorithm. In CKD, T cells CD8 was significantly and positively associated with JUND, and M2 macrophage was significantly and positively associated with AFF1 and ZFP36L1. In AD, 3 shared genes may regulate macrophages influencing disease progression, and in CKD, 3 shared genes may regulate T cells and macrophages influencing disease progression.

Studies have shown that peripheral macrophages have pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, and phagocytic effects and that these effects can have a different impact on inflammatory damage to the central nervous system damage.[59] It has been shown that peripheral macrophage dysfunction in aging mice is associated with cognitive impairment and AD pathogenesis mechanisms.[60] Our results show that in AD, M1 macrophages are significantly positively correlated with JUND and AFF1, suggesting that JUND and AFF1 may promote the polarization of M1 macrophages, which, on the one hand, can secrete pro-inflammatory and chemotactic factors, on the other hand, M1 macrophages can also activate microglia to produce reactive oxygen species, pro-inflammatory cytokines and other substances.[61] In addition, M1 macrophages are also beneficial in AD, as M1 macrophages phagocytose Aβ to reduce the pathological damage of AD. Studies have shown that erythropoietin increases the phagocytic activity of peripheral M1 macrophages via PPARγin APP/PS1-21 mice in vivo, promoting systemic Aβ clearance and alleviating AD progression.[62] For example, ZFP36L1 showed a significant positive correlation with M2 macrophages, suggesting that ZFP36L1 may promote the polarization of macrophages towards M2 macrophages and attenuate the inflammatory response. A limited study found that M2 macrophage transplantation reduced neuronal loss, improved the inflammatory response, and improved cognitive function in rats with AD, suggesting a protective effect of this subtype of macrophages against AD.[63]

In CKD, the prolonged persistence of chronic inflammation leads to altered T cell function and predisposes to T cell depletion.[64] The most obvious manifestation is a reduction in CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, characterized mainly by loss of effector functions and expression of inhibitory receptors (PD-1 and KLRG1). It has been shown that a significant reduction in T-cell subsets, particularly CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, in the CKD population is a critical factor in the exacerbation of CKD.[65,66] In the present study, JUND was shown to be significantly and positively correlated with T cell CD8+ subpopulations, suggesting that JUND may promote the expression of T cell CD8+ subpopulations to reduce the progression of CKD. In the current investigation, a significant positive correlation was observed between AFF1, ZFP36L1, and M2 macrophages, indicating that AFF1 and ZFP36L1 potentially contribute to the upregulation of M2 macrophage expression in CKD. In the context of CKD, the release of the damage-associated molecular pattern by necrotic cells triggers the differentiation of monocytes into M1 macrophages via Toll-like receptor activation. This process facilitates the secretion of substantial quantities of inflammatory factors by M1 macrophages, impairing endothelial function and renal cells. Consequently, renal function declines and renal fibrosis occurs.[67] During a limited timeframe, the presence of M2 macrophages can effectively mitigate renal inflammation and facilitate tissue repair through the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β. However, suppose there is a sustained and excessive infiltration of M2 macrophages. In that case, it can induce a continuous release of growth factors, resulting in pathological healing processes that ultimately lead to irreversible fibrosis and the progressive destruction of renal tissue. This phenomenon is exemplified in the advanced stages of diabetic nephropathy, where the predominant infiltration of M2 macrophages contributes to the development of glomerulosclerosis, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and eventual renal failure.[68] A recent study conducted by Bhatia et al[69] demonstrated that M2 macrophages in CKD possess the ability to impede the advancement of renal fibrosis through the process of autophagy mediated by putative kinase 1/Mitofusin 2 protein/parkin. Consequently, it is imperative to conduct further comprehensive investigations to elucidate the intricate mechanisms governing the impact of M2 macrophages on tissue repair and fibrosis in CKD.

The genetic analysis investigated disparities in the enrichment of hallmark gene sets between samples afflicted with disease and those that were healthy. Our findings indicate that the expression of KRAS-signaling, the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/AKT/mammalian target of the rapamycin-signaling, Hedgehog-signaling, TGF-β-signaling, and Wnt-β-Catenin-signaling was significantly elevated in individuals with AD and CKD compared to the control group.

KRAS-signaling can promote inflammation and immunosuppressive infiltration through various immunomodulatory mechanisms.[70] It has been shown that silencing Kras signaling expression in a folic acid-induced renal fibrosis model and a unilateral ureteral obstruction model, thereby reducing ERK phosphorylation, significantly inhibits renal fibrosis and impairment of renal function.[71]

The PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway plays a significant role in various cellular processes, including immune cell proliferation and differentiation, secretion of inflammatory cytokines, autophagy, and oxidative stress.[72] It has been observed that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway can either inhibit GSK-3β or activate mTOR, depending on the specific cell type. In AD, the accumulation of Aβ leads to neurotoxicity by disrupting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade in neuronal cells and neural stem cells. Additionally, Aβ inhibits the activity of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway by activating GSK-3β, thereby promoting hyperphosphorylation of tau protein.[73,74] In the context of CKD, previous studies have demonstrated that the presence of the chemokine C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) on macrophages is essential for the process of lymphangiogenesis during renal fibrosis. CCR2 exerts its influence by promoting the expression of the downstream gene HIF-1α through the activation of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway. Additionally, CCR2 directly binds to the vascular endothelial growth factor C promoter, thereby facilitating the transcription of HIF-1α and subsequently exacerbating lymphangiogenesis, contributing to the progression of renal fibrosis.[75]

The Hedgehog signaling pathway plays a significant role in regulating the immune system and repairing tissues. Research has demonstrated that in AD, the modulation of Hedgehog signaling in glial cells effectively mitigates the protein lesions induced by Aβ. Consequently, the activation of Hedgehog signaling in glial cells exhibits a favorable impact on the amelioration of neurodegenerative disorders arising from aberrant protein aggregation and misfolding.[76] A notable association has been observed between tissue fibrosis and Hedgehog-signaling in diverse tissues impacted by chronic illnesses, primarily attributable to the induction of epithelial to mesenchymal transition of cells via Hedgehog signaling, resulting in an excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix.[77,78] This finding implies that our Hedgehog-signaling pathway potentially assumes a significant function in chronic kidney disease fibrosis progression.

Alterations in the TGF-β signaling pathway have a dual effect on AD progression, with TG-β signaling normally having a neuroprotective effect on AD; for example, TGF-β1 overexpression restores synaptic plasticity and memory in the hippocampus of AD models.[79,80] However, certain studies have also identified a detrimental impact of TGF-β signaling on AD. For instance, the overexpression of TGF-β1 has been documented to induce an expedited development of cerebrovascular amyloidosis, compromised cognitive function, vascular alterations resembling those observed in AD, and diminished cerebral tissue perfusion in the AD model. In CKD, both the classical and non-classical pathways of TGF-β are stimulated, thereby facilitating the activation and proliferation of fibroblasts. The induction of phosphorylated Smad3 by TGF-β directly governs the expression of the classical marker α-SMA in fibroblasts, along with the expression of ECM proteins, including collagen and fibronectin. The upregulation of TGF-β disrupts the equilibrium between ECM synthesis and degradation, ultimately leading to heightened fibrosis.[81]

Research has demonstrated that the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in macrophages through STAT3 results in an enhanced polarization towards M2 phenotype, induced by IL-4 or TGF-β1, ultimately leading to the development of renal fibrosis.[82] In the context of AD, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the pathological progression of the disease. Notably, the disruption of this signaling pathway mediated by LRP6 has been associated with synaptic dysfunction and the formation of amyloid lesions in the brain tissue of individuals with AD. Furthermore, the activation of conventional Wnt signaling has been shown to impede the generation of Aβ and alleviate neurodegeneration and behavioral abnormalities in individuals with AD.[83,84]

By employing bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq methodologies, our study unveiled a strong association between the identified shared genes and the immune microenvironment within the disease context. Particularly in the case of AD, these genes demonstrated noteworthy expression in astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and MAIT cells. Notably, previous research has established a close relationship between glial cells, encompassing astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, and the progression of AD.[85,86] However, MAIT cells have garnered significant interest due to recent findings indicating that their absence in AD models results in the accumulation of meningeal reactive oxygen species (ROS), decreased expression of connexins, and notable meningeal leakage. Notably, the deficiency of MAIT cells has been linked to heightened neuroinflammation and impairments in learning and memory.[87]

Conversely, the introduction of MAIT cells into the AD model has demonstrated the ability to suppress ROS accumulation in brain tissue, preserve meningeal homeostasis, and maintain cognitive function. Another study has demonstrated that the MR1/MAIT cell axis is associated with numerous inflammatory diseases, including those affecting the central nervous system.[88,89] It has been observed that in the brain tissue of individuals with AD, there is an elevation in MR1 levels on the surface of microglia/macrophages, subsequently leading to increased levels of MAIT cells and the secretion of IFN-γand IL-17A. Furthermore, the study has identified that heightened levels of IL-17A contribute to cognitive deficits and synaptic alterations.[90] In summary, there is still a need for a comprehensive exploration of the significance of MAIT cells in relation to human health and disease.

The 3 genes that were found to be shared in CKD exhibited significant expression in various cell types, including oligodendrocytes, neutrophils, fibroblasts, astrocytes, and T-cells. The presence of immune cells such as neutrophils and T-cells is particularly noteworthy due to their crucial role in CKD. Specifically, during the advanced stages of CKD characterized by chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, neutrophils can migrate into renal tissue, generate ROS, secrete neutrophil serine proteases, and release neutrophil extracellular traps.[91] The differentiation and activation of T-cell subpopulations significantly influence the advancement of CKD. Notably, in cases of immune-mediated renal disease, the activation of T cells prompts a reprogramming of their metabolic pathways, shifting from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid β-oxidation to aerobic glycolysis, pentose-phosphate, and glutamine enzymes.[81] T cells exhibit varied activity patterns in CKD, influenced by distinct metabolic pathways. For instance, when exposed to IL-12 or T-bet stimulation, naïve T cells undergo differentiation into Th1 cells, characterized by heightened oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic metabolism, thereby capable of suppressing renal fibrosis.[92,93] Similarly, activated Th17 cells employ glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation to exert pro-inflammatory effects.[94]

From a more objective standpoint, this study exhibits certain limitations. The findings are contingent upon a distinct patient cohort and lack validation through clinical trials. Additionally, crucial factors such as age, gender, medication utilization, and co-existing medical conditions were not considered during the sample analysis. In order to authenticate the plausible association between AD and CKD, forthcoming models integrating both conditions will be imperative.

5. Conclusions

This study represents the inaugural attempt to investigate shared pathways and genetic diagnostic indicators for AD and CKD utilizing machine-learning methodologies. The findings of this study indicate that signaling pathways, specifically those related to immune inflammation and substance metabolism, may play a role in the development of AD and CKD. The genes JUND, AFF1, and ZFP36L1 also show promise as potential diagnostic markers for individuals with AD and concomitant CKD. This study offers novel perspectives on the potential mechanisms underlying the co-occurrence of AD and CKD. To delve deeper into the pathogenesis of AD combined with CKD, future research will integrate pertinent in vitro and in vivo experiments.
