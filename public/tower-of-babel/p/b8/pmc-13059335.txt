Targeting metabolic reprogramming to enhance adoptive immunotherapy: emerging mechanisms and translational perspectives
Authors: Liu Yang, Chengyu Zhang, Zhongxiang Zhang, Songlin Yao, Jing Shen, Zhuo Zhang, Zhangang Xiao, Shurong Wang, Zhigui Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdoptive cell therapy (ACT) has achieved breakthrough advances in the treatment of malignancies, demonstrating particularly remarkable efficacy in hematologic cancers. However, the therapeutic efficacy of ACT remains limited by multiple factors, including low tumor antigen expression, the limited in vivo persistence of infused immune cells, and the immunosuppressive characteristics of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Meanwhile, metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of cancer, plays a pivotal role in immune cells by directly regulating their differentiation, functional states, and long-term survival. Metabolic stress within the TME, including hypoxia, nutrient competition, and accumulation of metabolic byproducts, reshapes the metabolic states of T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages, thereby profoundly affecting their activation, effector functions, and in vivo persistence. Consequently, a systematic understanding of how metabolic reprogramming regulates immune cell function is crucial for overcoming the current therapeutic limitations of ACT.Main bodyThis review systematically summarizes the metabolic constraints faced by immune effector cells in ACT and the strategies to modulate them, with a particular focus on the impact of glycolysis, lipid metabolism, amino acid homeostasis, and mitochondrial function on immune cell differentiation, effector functions, and in vivo persistence. In addition, it provides a comprehensive discussion of potential approaches to target metabolic reprogramming through pharmacological interventions and genetic engineering, aiming to overcome the metabolic limitations imposed by the TME and enhance the antitumor efficacy of ACT.ConclusionWhile merely enhancing glycolysis can transiently increase the cytotoxic activity of immune effector cells, it often leads to metabolic exhaustion and reduced persistence. In contrast, optimizing mitochondrial function, boosting oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, and maintaining metabolic flexibility are more conducive to promoting memory-like phenotypes and supporting long-term survival and functional maintenance of immune cells in vivo. Future studies should integrate genetic engineering with metabolic interventions to optimize the antitumor activity of ACT in solid tumors and complex tumor microenvironments, thereby providing a theoretical foundation and translational pathway for the development of next-generation, safe and efficacious ACT therapies.Keywords: Adoptive cell therapy, Immune responses, Tumor microenvironment, Metabolic reprogramming, Immune cell metabolism

Introduction

In recent years, adoptive cell therapy (ACT) has achieved remarkable progress in cancer treatment, particularly in hematologic malignancies, where chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has demonstrated high remission rates and durable survival benefits [1, 2]. Nevertheless, ACT still faces considerable challenges. On the one hand, severe adverse events such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) remain major concerns. On the other hand, obstacles in solid tumors-including immunosuppressive characteristics, tumor antigen heterogeneity, immune evasion mechanisms, impaired adoptive immune cell infiltration, and functional exhaustion, all of which limit the therapeutic efficacy of ACT [3, 4]. In addition, issues such as high treatment costs, long manufacturing timelines, and the development of resistance also limit its widespread application. Therefore, the development of novel optimization strategies to overcome these bottlenecks is essential for advancing the clinical translation of ACT. Notably, the activation, proliferation, and effector functions of immune cells are highly dependent on dynamic metabolic reprogramming. For example, naive T cells primarily rely on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to maintain a resting state, while effector T cells rapidly increase glycolysis to fuel their cytotoxic functions [5]. However, the intense nutrient competition between tumor cells and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) induces metabolic stress conditions such as local lactate accumulation and hypoxia, which suppress the metabolic activity and effector functions of T cells, ultimately resulting in T cell exhaustion or apoptosis [6, 7]. Therefore, targeting the key metabolic pathways in immune cells, including glycolysis, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and mitochondrial function, while alleviating metabolic stress within the tumor microenvironment, can enhance their metabolic competitiveness. This approach holds the potential to substantially improve immune cell proliferation, persistence, and tumor-killing activity, thereby representing a promising strategy to enhance the antitumor efficacy of ACT. This review will provide a comprehensive analysis of the intrinsic relationship between the immune cell’s metabolism and functional regulation, systematically elucidating the key limiting effects of metabolic stress in the tumor microenvironment (including hypoxic conditions, lactate accumulation, and nutrient deprivation) on the efficacy of ACT. Furthermore, it will explore strategies to optimize immune cell metabolic adaptability through targeted metabolic reprogramming to enhance the antitumor effects of ACT, with the aim of providing new theoretical foundations and therapeutic strategies to overcome the bottlenecks in solid tumor immunotherapy.

The current state of adoptive immunotherapy

ACT, as a precise and personalized cancer immunotherapy, involves the ex vivo expansion or genetic modification of tumor-specific immune cells, which are then reintroduced into the patient to enhance antitumor effects (Fig.1). In recent years, ACT has achieved notable progress in the treatment of malignant tumors, primarily encompassing CAR-T cell therapy, CAR-natural killer (CAR-NK) cell therapy, and CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) cell therapy, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) therapy, T-cell receptor chimeric T-cell (TCR-T) therapy [8]. The therapies that are currently applied in clinical practice are primarily CAR-T cell therapy, TIL therapy, TCR-T therapy and CAR-M therapy (Table 1).

Target antigens of various types of adoptive immune therapies

CAR-T cell therapy, as a breakthrough in tumor immunotherapy, involves the genetic engineering of T cells to express specific chimeric antigen receptors, enabling them to specifically recognize and target malignant tumors for destruction. This therapy has achieved significant success in the treatment of hematologic malignancies, particularly in relapsed/refractory B-cell malignancies, such as acute B lymphoblastic leukemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and multiple myeloma [35, 36]. Currently, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved seven CAR-T cell products targeting CD19 or B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) for clinical treatment [37–39]. In addition to the approved targets, several preclinical and clinical studies are exploring other potential therapeutic targets, including B-cell-related antigens such as CD22, CD20, and CD123, as well as T-cell-specific markers such as CD7 and CD5 [10, 40]. Recent clinical experience with anti-CD5 CAR-T cells engineered with a truncated epidermal growth factor receptor (tEGFR) safety switch in relapsed/refractory T-cell lymphomas further underscores the translational potential of CD5-directed CAR-T therapy and highlights the need for robust toxicity-control mechanisms when targeting pan-T-cell antigens [40]. At the same time, clinical trials targeting solid tumor-associated antigens such as EGFRvIII [11], mesothelin [12], glypican-3 (GPC3) [13], mucin 1-C (MUC1-C)/HER2 (breast cancer/gastric cancer) [14, 15], PSMA (prostate cancer) [16], GD2 (neuroblastoma) [17], and Claudin18.2 (gastrointestinal tumors) [18] are actively underway (NCT04511871, NCT04249947, NCT03294954, NCT04404595). As of 2024, China has initiated over 850 CAR-T clinical trials [41], with studies targeting solid tumors such as neuroblastoma and malignant glioma showing promising therapeutic potential. [42, 43].

CAR-NK cell therapy, with the inherent potent cytotoxicity, non-MHC-restricted recognition mechanism, and low immunogenicity of NK cells, offers certain advantages over CAR-T cells in the treatment of solid tumors [32, 44]. The clinical application of CAR-T cell therapy is limited by adverse reactions such as CRS, off-target toxicity, and neurotoxicity, as well as the high complexity and cost associated with the personalized manufacturing process [45]. In contrast, the emergence of CAR-NK cell therapy provides a new direction to overcome the limitations and offers a novel opportunity for the advancement of tumor immunotherapy. Currently, several CAR-NK cell therapies targeting different tumor antigens have entered clinical research stages, with CD19-targeted CAR-NK cells (NCT04245722) demonstrating promising therapeutic effects in relapsed/refractory B-cell malignancies [46]. Preclinical studies have also found that CAR-NK cells targeting the pancreatic cancer-specific antigen ROBO1 and the squamous cell carcinoma-associated antigen MUC1 both exhibit significant antitumor activity [46, 47]. The clinical trial of HER2-targeted CAR-NK cells in neuroblastoma patients (NCT03383978) has confirmed the safety profile and potential therapeutic value of this therapy in solid tumors [48].

CAR-M involves the genetic engineering of macrophages, offering enhanced infiltration capacity into solid tumors and improved survival ability, making it a promising immunotherapeutic approach [34, 49]. The HER2-targeted CT-0508 and MSLN-targeted MCY-M11 CAR-M cell therapies have received clinical trial approval from the U.S. FDA, respectively for the treatment of HER2-overexpressing relapsed/metastatic solid tumors and relapsed/refractory ovarian cancer and peritoneal mesothelioma [50]. Preclinical studies of CT-0508 have shown that this therapy not only precisely induces phagocytosis of cancer cells but also cleverly protects normal cells from damage. Through the synergistic action of these dual mechanisms, it significantly reduces tumor burden and extends survival. Especially in the HER2-overexpressing ovarian cancer semi-immune mouse model, CT-0508 demonstrated exceptional safety and efficacy, highlighting its great potential in the treatment of solid tumors [51]. The CD19 CAR-M model study confirmed that this therapy possesses enhanced antigen presentation capability [52].

TCR-T cell therapy involves the genetic engineering of conventional T cells to introduce T-cell receptors (TCRs), enabling them to specifically recognize and target tumor cells for destruction. The key to this therapy lies in the engineering of TCRs, which can recognize endogenous tumor antigens presented by peptide-major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) molecules. Compared to CAR-T cells, which primarily recognize tumor surface antigens, TCR-T therapy can target intracellular antigens, overcoming the limitation of surface antigen expression, and thus has a broader application in the treatment of solid tumors [26, 53, 54]. In 2022, the FDA officially approved Kimmtrak (tebentafusp-tebn, IMCgp100) for the treatment of adult patients with HLA-A*0201-positive, unresectable or metastatic uveal melanoma [55]. TCR-T cell therapy targeting NY-ESO-1 (NCT01343043) has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in patients with synovial sarcoma [27]. Meanwhile, TCR-T constructs based on the MAGE-A4 antigen have entered phase II clinical trials to further validate their therapeutic efficacy [28]. In virus-associated tumors, TCR-T cell therapy targeting HPV E6/E7 antigens (NCT02858310) successfully induced tumor regression in cervical cancer patients [29]. Notably, the KRAS G12V mutation-specific TCR-T cell therapy (NCT03190941) has also demonstrated significant tumor regression in pancreatic cancer patients [56, 57].

TIL therapy refers to a treatment method in which TILs are isolated from the patient’s tumor tissue, cultured and expanded ex vivo, and supplemented with stimulatory factors to enhance the immune response against the tumor. TIL therapy has demonstrated outstanding efficacy in the treatment of certain solid tumors, particularly in melanoma [58]. In 2024, the representative TIL therapy product, Amtagvi (lifileucel), was approved by the U.S. FDA for the treatment of advanced melanoma, becoming the first TIL therapy product to be approved for commercialization worldwide [59]. TIL therapy primarily targets tumor-associated antigens and tumor-specific neoantigens. Key identified target antigens include melanoma-associated antigens MART-1 [19] and gp100 [20], HPV-related tumor E6/E7 oncoproteins [21], cancer-testis antigen NY-ESO-1 [22], and MAGE family proteins (MAGE-A1, MAGE-A3) [23], among others. These targets exhibit high-specific expression in immunotherapy-sensitive tumor types, such as melanoma, cervical cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and cholangiocarcinoma. Personalized TIL therapy based on next-generation sequencing technology is rapidly advancing, with treatment regimens targeting KRAS G12D mutations (NCT03745326) and TP53 mutations showing promising potential in gastrointestinal tumors [24, 25].

Against this background, ACT has made significant progress in the identification of tumor-associated targets and the engineering of immune cells. However, the overall efficacy and durability of ACT remain limited, particularly in the treatment of solid tumors. Accumulating evidence indicates that the TME is characterized by adverse metabolic conditions—including persistent hypoxia, depletion of key nutrients, accumulation of metabolic by products such as lactate, and dysregulated lipid metabolism—which not only exacerbate the metabolic stress experienced by adoptive immune cells but also induce functional exhaustion, senescence, or reduced survival, thereby severely limiting sustained and effective antitumor immunity [60, 61]. Therefore, merely increasing the number of targets or optimizing receptor structures may be insufficient to fully realize the therapeutic potential of ACT. Systematically characterizing the intrinsic metabolic features of different adoptive immune cells and elucidating how specific metabolic constraints within the tumor microenvironment suppress ACT function may guide the development of immunometabolism interventions, representing a key strategy to enhance the efficacy and durability of adoptive cell therapies.

Metabolic characteristics and metabolic limitations of immune cells in TME

Metabolic characteristics of immune cells

In recent years, metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a rapidly developing frontier in tumor research. Like the complex metabolic patterns of tumor cells, this pattern exists in various immune cells, including macrophages, neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells, lymphocytes, and natural killer cells. Under homeostatic conditions, these immune cells are mostly in a resting state, with limited energy metabolic activity. However, upon encountering infections, inflammation, or other exogenous stimuli, they are rapidly activated, initiating a series of immune response processes accompanied by significant metabolic reprogramming. The metabolic programs of immune cells exhibit differences between resting and activated states, with changes in energy demand directly driving the transformation of their functional phenotypes and the execution of immune effects (Fig.2). For example, naive T cells and memory T cells in a resting state primarily rely on OXPHOS and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) as energy sources. This metabolic mode provides a high ATP production efficiency, helping to maintain long-term cell survival and a stem-like phenotype [62]. Upon antigen stimulation, T cells rapidly undergo a metabolic program shift from a mitochondria-driven metabolism to a predominantly aerobic glycolysis-based metabolism, accompanied by enhanced glutamine catabolism and lipid synthesis, in order to rapidly generate large amounts of ATP to support cell proliferation and differentiation [63]. Activated T cells differentiate into effector T cells (such as Th1, Th2, Th17, etc.) or regulatory T cells (Tregs), with effector T cells characterized by high glycolytic activity, while Tregs primarily rely on FAO [64]. Unlike effector T cells, memory T cells do not simply revert to a single mitochondrial metabolic state after the immune response; instead, they establish a metabolic program integrating OXPHOS, FAO, and a degree of glycolysis, accompanied by enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and markedly increased spare respiratory capacity (SRC). This metabolic profile enables memory T cells to rapidly engage mitochondrial function under nutrient-limited or metabolically stressed conditions, thereby sustaining long-term survival, functional stability, and persistent immune surveillance [65]. Beyond T cells, distinct immune cell subsets also exhibit specific metabolic dependencies (Table 2). For example, activated NK cells, dendritic cells, M1 macrophages, and tumor-associated neutrophils primarily rely on glycolysis as their main energy source [75, 76]. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2 macrophages, on the other hand, rely on amino acid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation-driven oxidative phosphorylation for energy production [77].

Based on these insights, the importance of metabolic reprogramming in ACT is increasingly recognized. Various adoptive immune cell products, including CAR-T, CAR-NK, CAR-M, TCR-T, and TILs, also exhibit substantial differences in their intrinsic metabolic programs. CAR-T and TCR-T cells primarily rely on aerobic glycolysis during activation and effector phases to support rapid proliferation and cytotoxic function, whereas their long-term persistence depends on mitochondrial OXPHOS, FAO, and high SRC [65, 72]. CAR-NK cells typically exhibit a metabolic profile combining high glycolysis and mitochondrial activity, enabling rapid cytotoxic responses, but their mitochondrial function is highly sensitive to metabolic stress [68]. As innate immune cells, CAR-M cells are highly glycolysis-dependent and metabolically plastic, which confers an advantage in hypoxic environments but also renders them susceptible to metabolic reprogramming by tumor microenvironment signals [73]. TILs, derived from tumor tissues, are often metabolically exhausted and functionally impaired, relying primarily on FAO and OXPHOS to sustain long-term survival and antitumor activity [74].

Although different ACT cell products exhibit distinct metabolic profiles, they face common metabolic challenges in solid tumors, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation (glucose and amino acids), lipid dysregulation, and accumulation of acidic metabolic byproducts in the tumor microenvironment [8]. These adverse metabolic conditions exacerbate metabolic stress and induce immune cell exhaustion, markedly limiting the long-term survival and antitumor efficacy of adoptive immune cells. Therefore, systematically elucidating metabolic barriers within the tumor microenvironment and their impact on immune cell function is crucial for optimizing metabolic interventions and enhancing ACT efficacy.

The inhibitory effect of metabolic limitations in TME on the efficacy of ACT

Hypoxia, accumulation of lactic acid, and competition for glucose inhibit cellular function

Tumor cells maintain their energy metabolic demands through the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis), resulting in a characteristic hypoxic TME accompanied by lactate accumulation and glucose depletion. This immunosuppressive metabolic microenvironment not only directly impairs the metabolic adaptability and effector functions of infiltrating T cells but also promotes the recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells such as MDSCs and Tregs, thereby forming a pro-tumor immunosuppressive network [78, 79]. The combined action of these factors ultimately leads to a significant limitation in the clinical efficacy of ACT.

Glucose, as a key metabolic substrate for T cell activation and proliferation, its deficiency can lead to T cell metabolic reprogramming, significantly inhibiting proliferation, differentiation, and cytokine secretion, thereby weakening the anti-tumor immune response. Tumor cells competitively limit T cell glucose utilization by enhancing glycolytic activity and downregulating GLUT1 expression. This inhibits the Mammalian Target of Rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, reduces the expression of glycolysis-related genes, and decreases interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production, ultimately impairing T cell effector functions [80]. At the molecular level, effector T cell activation and function critically depend on sustained Ca2 + –NFAT signaling, which is finely regulated by ER Ca2 + dynamics. Glucose deprivation reduces phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) levels and inhibits enolase 1 (ENO1) activity, thereby relieving its inhibition of the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2 + -ATPase (SERCA). Enhanced SERCA activity promotes Ca2 + reuptake into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), thereby attenuating ER Ca2 + depletion and the subsequent stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1)–ORAI calcium release-activated calcium modulator 1 (ORAI1)–dependent store-operated calcium entry (SOCE). Because sustained Ca2 + influx is required for NFAT1 dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation, excessive ER Ca2 + recovery ultimately suppresses the Ca2 +/NFAT1 axis, leading to markedly reduced cytokine transcription and effector T cell function [81]. In addition, a glucose-deficient microenvironment can promote the differentiation of immunosuppressive Tregs, which further inhibit effector T cell function through cell-to-cell interactions, thereby reducing the clinical efficacy of ACT [82].

The hypoxic microenvironment significantly impairs the antitumor function of immune cells through multiple mechanisms. In T cells, hypoxia not only suppresses their proliferative capacity but also reduces the secretion of key effector cytokines, such as IFN-γ and Interleukin-2 (IL-2), which are crucial for immune cell activation and the amplification of immune responses. Chronic hypoxia promotes T cell exhaustion, characterized by impaired self-renewal capacity, diminished effector functions, and the upregulation of co-inhibitory receptors, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), ultimately contributing to the failure of CAR-T cell therapy [6, 83]. In addition, hypoxia similarly impairs the cytotoxic activity of NK cells, weakening their ability to kill tumor cells, thereby affecting the overall efficacy of ACT [84].

Lactate accumulation drives the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through multiple distinct mechanisms. Firstly, lactate-induced extracellular acidification significantly impairs T cell function by inhibiting their proliferation, survival, cytotoxicity, and cytokine secretion, thereby weakening the antitumor immune response [7]. Secondly, lactate promotes immunosuppression through dual pathways: on one hand, it directly stimulates the proliferation and functional activation of Tregs; on the other hand, under low-glucose conditions, it selectively upregulates PD-1 expression on Tregs while suppressing PD-1 expression on effector T cells, leading to resistance to immunotherapy [85, 86]. Additionally, lactic acid can also induce dendritic cells (DCs) to polarize toward an immunosuppressive phenotype, while simultaneously impairing the cytotoxic function of T cells and NK cells.

Metabolic stressors in the TME—including hypoxia, lactate, and low glucose—synergistically inhibit the function of effector T cells, thereby reducing the therapeutic efficacy of ACT. Thus, improving the metabolic state of the TME or enhancing T cell adaptability to adverse metabolic environments is a key strategy to improve the efficacy of ACT.

Amino acid deprivation induces cell exhaustion and functional inhibition

Amino acid deprivation in the tumor microenvironment is a key mechanism of immune suppression, which can significantly impair the metabolic adaptability and effector functions of ACT effector cells. Tumor cells and immunosuppressive cells (such as MDSCs and Tregs) promote the abnormal depletion of tryptophan and arginine in the microenvironment through the high expression of indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) or arginase (ARG1). This metabolic reprogramming can induce G1 phase cell cycle arrest in T cells, inhibit the mTOR signaling pathway, and downregulate the expression of effector cytokines, significantly impairing the antitumor function of T cells [87]. At the same time, the tryptophan metabolite kynurenine (Kyn) promotes Treg differentiation and induces T cell exhaustion by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), while glutamine deprivation further inhibits T cell glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, impairing their proliferation and persistence [87, 88]. It is noteworthy that reduced arginine levels significantly inhibit the proliferative capacity of antigen-specific T cells, while global or myeloid-specific knockout of Arg1 enhances the antigen-induced proliferation of adoptively transferred T cells and effectively inhibits tumor growth [89]. Additionally, amino acid deprivation can further impair T cell responses by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction. Both clinical samples and animal models have confirmed that tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells exhibit a decline in mitochondrial quality and functional defects, leading to abnormal reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Moderate levels of ROS are crucial for the acquisition of CD8+ T cell effector functions, and ROS imbalance induced by amino acid deprivation may represent another key mechanism underlying T cell dysfunction [90]. Besides, glutamine deprivation in the tumor microenvironment impairs the metabolic uptake capacity of DCs, leading to maturation defects and functional deficiencies. As DCs play a central role in tumor antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell activation, their functional impairment significantly reduces T cell infiltration and effector functions, thereby weakening the efficacy of ACT [91]. Therefore, amino acid deprivation inhibits T cell function through multiple synergistic mechanisms, becoming a key factor limiting the efficacy of ACT in solid tumors. Targeting related metabolic enzymes or supplementing essential amino acids may serve as a critical strategy to optimize ACT treatment.

Abnormal lipid metabolism interferes with the function of immune cells

Lipid metabolic dysregulation in the tumor microenvironment is a key metabolic dysfunction mechanism that limits the efficacy of ACT. In the TME, free fatty acid accumulation is driven by a combination of mechanisms, including lipolysis in cancer-associated adipocytes [92], metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells [93], upregulation of relevant enzymes such as fatty acid synthase and phospholipase A [94, 95], TME-specific conditions [93], and interactions between immune and stromal cells [96, 97]. Excessive free fatty acids and cholesterol can lead to abnormal accumulation of lipid droplets within CD8+ T cells, thereby inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS accumulation, ultimately impairing T cell proliferative activity and cytotoxic functions. Lipid overload can promote T cell exhaustion by activating the peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway and upregulating exhaustion-related transcription factors such as thymocyte selection–associated high mobility group box (TOX), characterized by the significant upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules like PD-1 and LAG-3 [98]. The cholesterol metabolism plays a dual regulatory role in T cell activation. Under physiological conditions, cholesterol specifically binds to the transmembrane domain of the TCR-β chain, promoting the formation of TCR microclusters on the cell membrane, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of TCR antigen recognition and signal transduction efficiency. However, dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism in the TME disrupts the normal TCR clustering process, interferes with immunological synapse formation, and ultimately leads to T cell activation defects and reduced tumor infiltration capacity [99, 100]. In the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) adapt to nutrient competition stress through fatty acid metabolic reprogramming, characterized by increased fatty acid uptake and intracellular lipid droplet accumulation. This metabolic reprogramming drives TAMs to switch from glycolysis to FAO as their primary energy source, promoting their polarization toward the M2 phenotype and significantly enhancing their pro-tumor functions. It is worth nothing that the dysregulation of lipid metabolism in TAMs also impairs their phagocytic capacity, leading to reduced efficiency in the clearance of tumor cells and apoptotic cells. This functional defect not only exacerbates the immunosuppressive state of the tumor microenvironment but may also further weaken the efficacy of ACT by promoting tumor cell survival and metastasis [101, 102]. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism in the tumor microenvironment impairs the efficiency of antigen uptake, processing, and presentation in DCs, leading to T cell activation defects and inducing an imbalance in DC cytokine secretion profiles (reduced pro-inflammatory factors IL-12 and IL-18, and increased immunosuppressive factor IL-10). These factors act in concert to weaken T cell function and may limit the clinical efficacy of ACT [103]. Lipid metabolic dysregulation limits the efficacy of ACT by inducing functional exhaustion, impairing mitochondrial activity, and enhancing immunosuppression. Targeting related metabolic pathways is considered a potential strategy to improve the efficacy of ACT.

The accumulation of ROS produced by mitochondrial metabolism leads to cell apoptosis.

Metabolic products generated by mitochondrial metabolism can act as signaling molecules, participating in cellular signal transduction and subsequently influencing cytokine secretion in CAR-T cells. For instance, mitochondrial-derived ROS, at certain levels, can participate in the TCR signaling pathway, promoting cytokine production [74]. However, excessive ROS can induce T cell dysfunction, DNA damage, apoptosis, and necrosis, ultimately leading to impaired effector function of CAR-T cells [74]. In the TME, persistent antigen stimulation drives T cell metabolic reprogramming, characterized by oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, which in turn promotes the formation of terminally exhausted TILs [74]. Meanwhile, insufficient mROS levels are also detrimental, as they suppress the transcriptional activity of the nuclear factor NFAT, significantly weakening the expression of key cytokines such as IL-2 [104]. Thus, the dual role of ROS in the regulation of CAR-T cell function requires further exploration and may provide new metabolic intervention targets for optimizing CAR-T cell therapy.

Metabolic abnormalities prevalent in the TME represent a major barrier to ACT efficacy. Persistent hypoxia, glucose and amino acid deprivation, accumulation of metabolic byproducts such as lactate, and dysregulated lipid metabolism not only directly impair effector functions of adoptive immune cells by limiting energy supply and mitochondrial activity, but also remodel signaling and transcriptional programs, driving immune cells toward exhaustion or dysfunctional phenotypes. Such metabolic stress is particularly pronounced in solid tumors, making it difficult for even target-specific, receptor-optimized ACT cells to maintain functional stability and sustained antitumor activity in vivo. This indicates that ACT efficacy is not solely limited by target selection or receptor engineering but largely stems from a profound mismatch between the intrinsic metabolic programs of adoptive immune cells and the metabolic conditions of the TME. Therefore, systematically modulating the metabolic profiles of immune cells, or alleviating metabolic stress by targeting inhibitory factors within the TME, may provide new theoretical rationale and intervention strategies to enhance the overall efficacy and durability of ACT.

The basis for improving the efficacy of ACT through metabolic intervention

Regulating metabolic reprogramming to enhance the function of adoptive immune cells

Adoptive cell therapy has shown remarkable preclinical potential in the treatment of malignant tumors, yet its efficacy is often limited by factors such as insufficient cell persistence, target antigen loss, and immune suppression within the TME. The unique metabolic characteristics of the TME, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and the accumulation of metabolic byproducts, can trigger metabolic dysregulation and functional exhaustion in immune cells (Fig.3). By precisely regulating various core metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, lipid metabolic networks, and amino acid metabolism, it is possible to significantly enhance the effector functions of immune cells, prolong their persistence, improve their resistance to exhaustion, and enhance their adaptability within the TME [105]. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying immune cell metabolic reprogramming will provide an important theoretical foundation and clinical translational value for optimizing ACT strategies (Fig.4).

Reprogramming of glycolysis and amino acid metabolism drives the effector functions of immune cells

Sugar metabolism reprogramming

Effector immune cells rely heavily on glycolysis during antitumor responses to meet the energy demands of rapid proliferation and cytokine production. Therefore, precise targeting of key glycolytic nodes has emerged as a crucial strategy to enhance the effector function of adoptive immune cells.

In the early phase of immune responses, enhanced glucose uptake is a metabolic prerequisite for maintaining a highly functional effector state in immune cells. Activation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–protein kinase B (AKT)–mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling pathway, or genetic engineering–mediated upregulation of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), markedly increases glucose uptake and metabolic efficiency. This metabolic enhancement supports the production of effector cytokines, including IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), ultimately potentiating the antitumor activity of immune cells [106, 107]. For example, mTOR is a central regulator of cellular metabolism and growth, existing mainly as two structurally and functionally distinct complexes, mTORC1 and mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 2 (mTORC2) [108]. Multiple studies have shown that mTORC1 enhances glycolytic flux by activating hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) and upregulating glycolysis-related genes, including GLUT1, HK2, and LDHA, thereby promoting the expression of effector molecules such as IFN-γ and Granzyme B and markedly improving both the antitumor activity and functional persistence of immune cells [109, 110]. Conversely, glucose deprivation in the tumor microenvironment reduces PEP levels in TILs, thereby impairing TCR signaling [111]. Accordingly, overexpression of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1) to elevate intracellular PEP levels effectively restores TCR-mediated Ca2 + –NFAT signaling within tumors, enhancing T cell effector function and improving the efficacy of adoptive cell therapy [111]. Therefore, enhancing glucose uptake and its downstream metabolic utilization is a key metabolic foundation for sustaining and amplifying the antitumor effector function of immune cells.

In glycolysis, remodeling the activity of key rate-limiting enzymes critically shapes the effector function of immune cells. 6-Phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3), a key member of the PFKFB family, primarily catalyzes the synthesis of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F-2,6-BP). F-2,6-BP is the most potent allosteric activator of the rate-limiting glycolytic enzyme phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1), significantly increasing its affinity for fructose-6-phosphate and thereby effectively driving glycolytic flux [112]. In activated human CD3+ T cells, PFKFB3 expression is markedly upregulated alongside increased F-2,6-BP levels, whereas inhibition of PFKFB3 kinase activity impairs T cell activation in vitro and suppresses T cell–dependent immune responses in vivo [113, 114]. Moreover, genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of PFKFB3 markedly reduces macrophage glycolysis as well as their phagocytic and antiviral functions, indicating that PFKFB3-driven glycolysis is essential for the extrinsic antiviral activity of macrophages [115]. Therefore, genetically overexpressing PFKFB3 may enhance the effector function of immune cells within glucose-limited tumor microenvironments. Moreover, pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) is a key terminal glycolytic enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of PEP to pyruvate with ATP production. PKM2 exhibits high conformational plasticity, dynamically switching between a highly active tetramer and a low-activity dimer to finely regulate glycolytic flux and the diversion of metabolic intermediates [115]. In activated effector immune cells, the low-activity conformation of PKM2 favors the diversion of glycolytic intermediates toward nucleotide, amino acid, and lipid biosynthesis to meet the demands of rapid proliferation and effector molecule production [116]. Moreover, PKM2 can translocate to the nucleus and interact with signaling molecules such as HIF-1α and STAT3, promoting the expression of glycolysis-related and effector function–related genes, thereby coupling metabolic state with immune effector programs. For example, TGF-β derived from M2 TAMs promotes nuclear translocation of the PKM2 dimer–STAT3 complex, enhancing glycolysis in bladder cancer cells and further inducing programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)–mediated immune evasion [117]. Notably, hypoxia and metabolic stress in the tumor microenvironment can induce PKM2-mediated metabolic reprogramming, but excessive inhibition of its enzymatic activity may compromise energy supply and impair immune cell function [118]. Therefore, precise regulation of PKM2 conformation and function is considered a potential strategy to optimize the effector function and metabolic adaptability of adoptive immune cells.

Conversely, limiting lactate production also helps restore immune cell effector function. LDHA catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, and its high expression is associated with poor prognosis in melanoma patients [119]. Moreover, LDHA-mediated lactate accumulation suppresses T cell and NK cell–mediated tumor immune surveillance [120]. Therefore, targeting LDHA inhibition reduces the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, alleviates intracellular acidification and lactate-mediated self-inhibition, and enhances both the cytotoxicity and functional persistence of immune cells within lactate-rich tumor microenvironments [121]. Moreover, tumor cell–surface immune checkpoint PD-L1 can enhance tumor glycolysis by activating the Akt/mTOR pathway to upregulate the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes [122]. Aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells creates a hypoxic tumor microenvironment, stabilizing HIF-1α under low oxygen and further upregulating PD-L1 expression, thereby establishing a vicious cycle that promotes both tumor metabolism and immune suppression.

These metabolic interventions enhance glycolytic flux and sustain the activation of mTORC1 and Myc signaling, with Myc further driving the transcription of key glycolytic genes, including GLUT1, HK2, PFKFB3, and LDHA, thereby establishing a positive metabolic feedback loop that supports immune cell effector function. However, when glycolysis is persistently or excessively activated, its effect on immune cells gradually shifts from supportive to inhibitory. For example, under chronic antigen stimulation or within the tumor microenvironment, immune cells exhibit impaired mitochondrial metabolism and reduced oxidative phosphorylation, accompanied by increased glycolytic dependence, a metabolic imbalance closely associated with effector T cell exhaustion. Specifically, the mitochondrial biogenesis regulator Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) is suppressed in exhausted TILs, resulting in reduced mitochondrial mass and metabolic rigidity, thereby impairing long-term functional maintenance and responsiveness [123, 124]. Concomitantly, excessive glycolysis–associated lactate accumulation directly suppresses the proliferation and cytokine production of effector T cells and NK cells by inhibiting NF-κB and MAPK signaling, interfering with TCR signaling, and lowering intracellular pH [125]. In contrast, Treg cells gain a survival and functional advantage in lactate-rich, highly glycolytic environments due to their high adaptability to lactate uptake and FAO, thereby further exacerbating immune suppression [126]. Moreover, tumor-associated macrophages and MDSCs often exhibit abnormally enhanced glycolytic activity, and their metabolic products further suppress CD8+ T cell effector function and promote tumor immune evasion [127]. Overall, glycolysis acts as a classic double-edged sword for immune cells: transient and moderate enhancement supports immune activation and effector function, whereas chronic and uncontrolled glycolysis can drive immune exhaustion and suppression, with outcomes ultimately determined by immune cell type, activation stage, and the surrounding microenvironment. Therefore, precise targeting of key regulatory nodes in glucose metabolic pathways, such as PFKFB3, PKM2, and LDHA, can enhance immune cell effector function while avoiding metabolic overload, providing a strong mechanistic rationale for optimizing the antitumor efficacy of adoptive immune cell therapies.

Amino acid metabolic reprogramming

Beyond glucose metabolism, amino acid metabolism is likewise a central determinant of maintaining and enhancing the effector function of adoptive immune cells. Among these, glutamine serves as a major carbon and nitrogen source for rapidly proliferating T and NK cells, fueling the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle via α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) to provide energy and substrates for nucleotide, lipid, and protein biosynthesis [128]. Glutaminase 1 (GLS1) is the rate-limiting enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of glutamine (Gln) to glutamate (Glu) with ammonia (NH₃) production. In activated effector T cells and NK cells, GLS1 expression is upregulated to enhance glutamine metabolism, thereby supporting cell proliferation, cytokine secretion, and effector function; conversely, GLS1 inhibition reduces α-ketoglutarate generation, impairs TCA cycle activity and biosynthetic capacity, and ultimately suppresses immune cell effector function [129, 130]. Moreover, regulation of glutamine transporters is critical for immune cell function. For example, in CD4+ T cells, overexpression of SLC38A1 enhances mitochondrial function [131], whereas SLC38A2 contributes to T cell development and memory formation by modulating mTORC1 activity [132]. However, in the esophageal squamous cell carcinoma microenvironment, high SLC1A5 expression is significantly negatively correlated with effector CD8+ T cell infiltration [133]. During innate immune responses, glutamine markedly enhances NK cell antitumor activity through activation of the SLC7A5-mediated mTORC1 signaling pathway [133]. Therefore, precise modulation of glutamine metabolism in distinct immune cell subsets, or supplementation with α-ketoglutarate, can effectively enhance immune cell effector function, providing a mechanistic basis for optimizing adoptive immune cell therapies. Notably, adequate availability of amino acids such as arginine is closely linked to mTORC1 activation and is essential for T cell proliferation, survival, and memory phenotype formation [5]. Arginine also plays a central role in regulating the T cell cycle; its deficiency suppresses mTORC1 activity while enhancing mTORC2 signaling, leading to cell-cycle arrest, whereas exogenous arginine supplementation can reverse this effect. In addition, arginine deprivation inhibits T cell proliferation by activating the GCN2 signaling pathway, causing arrest at the G0/G1 phase [132]. Tryptophan metabolism also plays a critical role in immune cell effector function. In the tumor microenvironment, high expression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 and 2 (IDO1/IDO2) metabolizes tryptophan (Trp) into Kyn, which suppresses CD8+ T cells and promotes Treg differentiation via AhR signaling, thereby weakening antitumor immunity. [134]. Therefore, inhibiting IDO activity or supplementing tryptophan may restore T cell proliferation and effector function, thereby improving the efficacy of adoptive cell therapy. Finally, the serine/glycine one-carbon (SGOC) metabolism pathway provides rapidly proliferating immune cells with nucleotides, proteins, and methylation substrates such as SAM, while maintaining NADPH and glutathione levels, thereby supporting IFN-γ and Granzyme B expression, enhancing antioxidant capacity, and ultimately boosting immune effector function [135, 136].

Targeting key nodes in glutamine, arginine, tryptophan, and SGOC metabolism not only supplies immune cells with energy and biosynthetic support but also modulates epigenetic and signaling pathways, establishing a positive metabolic feedback network and providing a solid mechanistic basis for optimizing the efficacy of adoptive immune cell therapies.

Mitochondrial functional remodeling and lipid metabolic reprogramming enhance immune cell persistence and resistance to exhaustion

Mitochondrial functional remodeling

Immune cell persistence and resistance to exhaustion are key determinants of durable ACT efficacy, with mitochondrial function serving as a central regulatory axis in this process. Enhanced mitochondrial function constitutes a key molecular basis for maintaining T cell stemness and the central memory phenotype (T_CM). Compared with adoptively transferred T cells derived from effector T cells, T_CM-derived adoptive T cells exhibit markedly prolonged in vivo persistence and more durable and potent antitumor responses [137–139]. This difference may arise because memory T cells and partially activated NK cells enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and increase mitochondrial SRC, enabling them to maintain sufficient energy supply and functional stability within nutrient-deprived and metabolically constrained tumor microenvironments [140]. Therefore, metabolic modulation targeting mitochondrial function—particularly strategies that enhance SRC—has emerged as a key approach to improve the in vivo persistence and resistance to exhaustion of adoptive immune cells. Moreover, enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis is typically accompanied by upregulation of key transcriptional coactivators, such as PGC-1α.PGC-1α coordinately regulates the expression of electron transport chain (ETC) components, activation of FAO, and the establishment of antioxidant programs; activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling or direct overexpression of PGC-1α markedly promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and functional maturation, thereby significantly extending T cell persistence in vivo [141, 142]. Upregulation of PGC-1α or treatment with PPAR agonists enhances FAO, promotes the formation of early memory T cells, and significantly augments their antitumor effector function [143, 144]. Moreover, the deep mechanisms underlying immune cell functional exhaustion involve a vicious positive feedback loop between metabolic constraints and epigenetic reprogramming [74]. Under chronic antigen stimulation or within the tumor microenvironment, immune cells often exhibit marked mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by reduced oxidative phosphorylation, impaired SRC, and decreased nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) levels [145, 146]. These metabolic abnormalities compromise mitochondrial energy supply and redox homeostasis, leading to sustained ROS accumulation, which in turn induces and stabilizes the expression of exhaustion-associated core transcription factors such as TOX, nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A (NR4A), and eomesodermin (EOMES). These transcription factors suppress the transcription of effector function genes, driving immune cells from a reversible dysfunctional state toward a terminally exhausted phenotype [147, 148]. Moreover, NAD+, a key coenzyme for maintaining mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis and redox balance, is closely linked to mitochondrial integrity. Its depletion further exacerbates ROS accumulation and energy deficiency, thereby accelerating the establishment of immune cell exhaustion programs [149]. In contrast, supplementation with NAD+ precursors (e.g., nicotinamide riboside, NR) or antioxidant interventions can effectively restore mitochondrial membrane potential and reduce ROS levels, thereby inhibiting or delaying the progression of immune cells toward an irreversible exhausted state. These approaches are considered important metabolic strategies to enhance immune cell persistence and resistance to exhaustion [150].

Thus, mitochondrial functional remodeling not only provides immune cells with sustained and stable energy supply but also disrupts the vicious cycle between metabolic defects and epigenetic reprogramming, enabling them to maintain effector function and long-term survival within highly suppressive tumor microenvironments. This establishes a solid metabolic foundation for durable antitumor efficacy in ACT.

Lipid metabolic reprogramming

Beyond mitochondrial functional remodeling, lipid metabolic reprogramming is also a crucial metabolic foundation determining the persistence and exhaustion resistance of adoptive immune cells. Lipid uptake represents the initial step of lipid metabolic regulation and is primarily mediated by fatty acid transport proteins—such as cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) and fatty acid transport proteins (FATPs)—as well as fatty acid–binding proteins (FABPs). These molecules facilitate the uptake of exogenous fatty acids into cells, where they serve as energy sources or biosynthetic substrates to meet the metabolic demands of immune cells under stress conditions [151]. However, in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, alterations in lipid uptake are closely associated with their functional status and exhaustion phenotype [152]. Moreover, lipid metabolism does not unidirectionally promote antitumor immunity. Tregs also rely heavily on lipid metabolism to maintain their immunosuppressive function [153] and can further shape an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by modulating the metabolic programs of TAMs [153]. Specifically, in TAMs, CD36-mediated lipid uptake enhances FAO and, through activation of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) signaling pathway, upregulates M2-associated genes such as mannose receptor C-type 1 (Mrc1), transglutaminase 2 (Tgm2), and Arg1, thereby maintaining their pro-tumorigenic and immunosuppressive phenotype [154].

At the level of energy metabolism, FAO serves as a critical alternative energy pathway for immune cells to maintain function under nutrient-limited and chronic antigen–stimulation conditions. Unlike terminally differentiated effector cells that rely heavily on aerobic glycolysis, memory T cells, stem-like T cells, and partially mature NK cells preferentially utilize FAO coupled with oxidative phosphorylation as their primary energy source, thereby maintaining metabolic homeostasis and functional integrity in glucose-deprived and persistently stimulated tumor microenvironments [155]. Mechanistically, fatty acids transported into mitochondria via carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) undergo FAO, continuously supplying reducing equivalents to the TCA cycle and electron transport chain, thereby supporting ATP production and maintaining elevated mitochondrial SRC. Studies have shown that enhancing CPT1A-dependent FAO markedly improves the in vivo persistence of CAR-T cells and their antitumor efficacy [156]. Moreover, the PPARα signaling pathway transcriptionally upregulates FAO-related genes, further enhancing the metabolic adaptability of immune cells and supporting their long-term functionality and sustained antitumor activity within the tumor microenvironment [157]. Notably, lipid synthesis also plays a critical regulatory role in maintaining immune cell function, particularly under conditions of rapid proliferation or limited exogenous lipid supply. Key enzymes, such as fatty acid synthase (FASN) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), catalyze the conversion of acetyl-CoA into fatty acids, providing essential substrates for membrane bilayer construction, energy storage, and signaling molecule synthesis. Concurrently, active lipid synthesis can influence the transcriptional programs of immune-related genes by regulating acetyl-CoA availability and histone acetylation levels, thereby supporting long-term survival and functional maintenance of immune cells [151]. However, excessive lipid synthesis can accelerate terminal differentiation of T cells and promote the development of an exhausted phenotype. Conversely, inhibition of ACC or FASN activity helps maintain early T cell differentiation and stem-like features, thereby delaying the onset of functional exhaustion [155, 158]. In innate immune cells, lipid metabolism also exhibits a bidirectional effect on functional regulation. For example, activated DCs partially rely on lipid oxidation for energy, and moderate lipid accumulation can enhance their proinflammatory cytokine secretion and antigen cross-presentation capacity, thereby promoting the activation of NK cells and effector T cells [159]. However, excessive lipid oxidation or abnormal lipid droplet (LD) accumulation can disrupt the formation and stability of MHC I complexes, ultimately impairing the antigen-presenting function of DCs [159, 160]. Moreover, cholesterol metabolism, mediated through pathways such as the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), facilitates exogenous cholesterol uptake, which is critical for maintaining membrane fluidity, lipid raft stability, and immunological synapse formation, thereby enhancing immune cell effector function [161]. However, in the tumor microenvironment, abnormal cholesterol accumulation can induce lipid peroxidation and ROS generation, thereby driving CD8+ T cell functional exhaustion and impairing their antitumor efficacy, indicating that cholesterol metabolism in immune cells requires precise and dynamic balance [162].

Lipid uptake, fatty acid oxidation, lipid synthesis, and cholesterol metabolism together constitute a highly interconnected metabolic network that supports immune cell long-term survival, functional maintenance, and resistance to exhaustion by providing sustained energy, maintaining membrane dynamics, and regulating signaling and gene expression programs. Accordingly, rational targeting of these metabolic pathways has emerged as a potential strategy to enhance the persistence and functional stability of ACT. However, regulation of lipid metabolism exhibits a pronounced bidirectional nature, as abnormal lipid accumulation or metabolic imbalance can induce functional impairment and exhaustion phenotypes. Moreover, immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs and TAMs also heavily rely on lipid metabolism to sustain their suppressive functions. Therefore, precise modulation of lipid metabolism in the context of ACT is crucial for maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing immunosuppressive side effects.

Strategies for targeting immune cell metabolic reprogramming to enhance ACT efficacy

Based on existing evidence, we propose a multi-targeted, synergistic metabolic intervention strategy: precise modulation of key glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes (e.g., PFKFB3, PKM2) and core regulators of amino acid metabolism (e.g., GLS1, IDO1) can markedly enhance the effector functions of adoptive immune cells; concurrently, targeting key pathways of mitochondrial biogenesis (e.g., the PGC-1α signaling axis) and critical enzymes of fatty acid oxidation (e.g., CPT1A) can effectively maintain metabolic homeostasis and prolong in vivo persistence (Table 3, Fig.5). This multi-layered metabolic reprogramming strategy can synergistically address metabolic challenges in the tumor microenvironment, including nutrient competition, hypoxic stress, and the accumulation of immunosuppressive metabolites. From a technical perspective, these interventions can be implemented either pharmacologically using small-molecule inhibitors or genetically via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing. This integrated metabolic modulation approach holds promise for enhancing the antitumor efficacy and persistence of ACT, offering an innovative strategy to overcome immune cell dysfunction in solid tumor therapy and demonstrating significant potential for clinical translation.

CAR-T, TCR-T, and TIL cell therapies

Targeting glycolytic metabolism

Glycolysis is a crucial metabolic pathway supporting rapid proliferation and effector function of T cells. However, under sustained or excessive activation, heavy reliance on glycolysis often drives metabolic exhaustion and terminal effector differentiation in ACT products such as CAR-T, TCR-T, and TILs, thereby limiting their long-term persistence and durable antitumor efficacy in vivo. Consequently, metabolic reprogramming targeting key glycolytic nodes has emerged as an important strategy to enhance ACT effectiveness.

In terms of pharmacological interventions, small-molecule compounds optimize ACT cell metabolic fitness by inhibiting or modulating the activity of key glycolytic enzymes. Among them, LDHA-mediated lactate production represents a critical metabolic checkpoint driving immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment. Inhibiting LDHA reduces lactate accumulation, which may alleviate TME-induced functional suppression of effector T cells and thereby enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. FX11, a commonly used small-molecule LDHA inhibitor, has demonstrated the potential to reduce lactate production, improve immune status, and suppress tumor progression in various preclinical models of solid tumors [163, 193]. In preclinical xenograft models, other LDHA inhibitors, such as oxamate, have been shown to relieve immunosuppression and enhance CAR-T cell functionality when combined with CAR-T therapy [164]. Monocarboxylate transporters 1 and 4 (MCT1 and MCT4) mediate transmembrane transport of lactate and other monocarboxylates in coordination with proton flux, playing a critical role in regulating cellular energy metabolism, pH homeostasis, and the tumor immune microenvironment. Targeting MCT1/MCT4 can enhance ACT cell tolerance to metabolically suppressive environments by reducing intracellular acid toxicity or improving lactate reutilization. For example, combining the MCT1 small-molecule inhibitors AZD3965 or AR-C155858 with CAR-T cell therapy markedly enhances their cytotoxic activity in vitro and improves tumor control in mouse models [165]. These studies provide strong theoretical and experimental evidence supporting the targeting of LDHA/lactate metabolism to enhance adoptive cell therapies. Beyond lactate metabolism, PKM2, as the rate-limiting enzyme at the terminal step of glycolysis, serves as a key metabolic hub linking glycolytic flux to T cell differentiation fate. Preclinical studies have shown that pharmacological activation of PKM2 combined with anti–PD-1 therapy not only induces CD8+ T cell–dependent antitumor immune responses but also enhances mitochondrial metabolic capacity that supports cytotoxic function, suggesting that targeting PKM2 holds potential for augmenting CAR-T cell effector activity [166]. Notably, 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG), a prototypical glycolytic inhibitor targeting hexokinase, can promote the formation of long-lived memory CD8+ T cells and enhance their antitumor responses by suppressing glycolysis, a mechanism that may contribute to improving the efficacy of adoptive cell therapies such as CAR-T cells [167]. Moreover, the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier complex (MPC1/MPC2), which mediates the transport of pyruvate into mitochondria for entry into the tricarboxylic acid cycle, plays a central role in regulating the balance between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation [168]. In T cells and CAR-T immunotherapy, regulation of the MPC not only influences CD8+ T cell memory differentiation but is also critical for lactate oxidation and overall antitumor function within the tumor microenvironment. CAR-T cells generated in the presence of the MPC inhibitor UK5099 exhibit more sustained antitumor activity [169], providing an experimental basis for enhancing ACT cell resilience and effector function in nutrient-deprived and hypoxic environments.

Genetic engineering strategies aim to achieve long-term metabolic optimization of ACT cells by directly modulating the expression of metabolic genes, addressing the challenges of glucose deprivation and metabolic competition within the tumor microenvironment. For example, overexpression of the glucose transporter GLUT1 in CAR-T cells markedly enhances glucose uptake and glycolytic capacity, while simultaneously increasing oxidative phosphorylation and promoting the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines. This metabolic reprogramming enables CAR-T cells to exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity in vitro and superior tumor control and persistence in mouse models, a mechanism associated with reduced T cell exhaustion and promotion of Th17 differentiation [170]. Furthermore, Valentić et al. demonstrated that enhancing GLUT5 expression increases the metabolic competitiveness of CAR-T cells under physiological glucose conditions, thereby further improving their antitumor efficacy [171]. Introducing a mutant form of PFKFB3 resistant to metabolic inhibitory signals significantly enhances glycolytic flux, thereby improving CAR-T cell survival, proliferation, and effector function under low-glucose conditions, and augmenting their antitumor activity in vivo. PFKFB3 directly regulates glycolytic flux mediated by downstream PFK1 through controlling the synthesis of fructose 2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP), and its activity is critical for cellular metabolic adaptation and functional performance in glucose-restricted microenvironments. Studies have shown that co-expression of PFK and the high-affinity glucose transporter GLUT3 significantly enhances T cell glycolytic capacity, cytokine secretion, and antitumor efficacy against human melanoma in mouse models, providing strong experimental support for improving ACT therapy [194]. Furthermore, PFKFB3 plays a critical role in T cell metabolic adaptation and functional maintenance, with its inhibition markedly reducing glycolytic activity and suppressing immune responses [172, 173], further highlighting the potential of targeting PFKFB3 to enhance ACT cell metabolic fitness and antitumor efficacy.

In summary, combining pharmacological interventions with genetic engineering to precisely reprogram key glycolytic regulatory nodes can effectively mitigate the adverse effects of nutrient deprivation and metabolic competition in the tumor microenvironment, significantly enhancing the survival, proliferative potential, and effector functions of ACT cells, particularly CAR-T cells, under metabolically restricted conditions. Such metabolic optimization strategies not only enhance the functional persistence of ACT cells but also provide a translationally relevant theoretical framework and practical approach for improving the overall efficacy of ACT therapies in solid tumors.

Targeting lipid metabolism

Lipid metabolism plays a critical regulatory role in T cell differentiation, survival, and maintenance of effector functions. In particular, the coupling of FAO with mitochondrial function is considered a central metabolic feature for sustaining the memory-like phenotype of T cells and the in vivo persistence of CAR-T, TIL, and TCR-T cells. However, within the tumor microenvironment, aberrant lipid composition and dysregulated homeostasis of free fatty acids and cholesterol often lead to lipid overload, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial dysfunction in immune cells, thereby inducing metabolic stress and functional exhaustion. Accordingly, precise modulation of lipid uptake, lipid biosynthesis, FAO, and cholesterol metabolism has emerged as a key metabolic intervention strategy to enhance the efficacy of ACT therapies.

At the pharmacological level, small molecules targeting lipid metabolism–related pathways can rapidly reprogram immune cell metabolic states without altering their genetic background. Enhancing the FAO pathway can markedly improve the metabolic adaptability of CAR-T cells, promote their long-term survival, and boost antitumor functions, thereby providing a novel metabolic intervention strategy for optimizing CAR-T cell therapy. For example, agonists of PPARα and its associated complexes, such as bezafibrate, can markedly enhance mitochondrial FAO capacity and SRC in CD8+ T cells, thereby improving their metabolic adaptability in nutrient-deprived and hypoxic microenvironments. Treatment with bezafibrate upregulates the expression of the key FAO rate-limiting enzyme CPT1, promotes oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, and significantly increases both the number and antitumor activity of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells. In ACT models, this metabolic intervention strategy confers markedly enhanced in vivo persistence and improved antitumor efficacy of T cells [143, 174]. Furthermore, activation of AMPK or inhibition of the mTOR signaling pathway can effectively promote FAO while suppressing excessive lipid synthesis, thereby driving the establishment of memory-like T cell metabolic programs. Representative agents include metformin and rapamycin: metformin enhances mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation via activation of the AMPK–ACC–CPT1A axis, whereas rapamycin binds to FK506-binding protein 12 (FKBP12) to form a complex that interacts with the FRB domain of mTORC1, thereby inhibiting its kinase activity [195–197]. Multiple studies consistently demonstrate that such metabolic interventions markedly increase the SRC of CD8+ T cells and ACT cells, enhancing their survival, in vivo persistence, and antitumor efficacy in nutrient-deprived and hypoxic tumor microenvironments [175–177]. Beyond FAO, cholesterol metabolism also plays a unique role in regulating T cell effector functions. Cholesterol not only serves as a critical structural component of cellular membranes but also directly participates in the formation of TCR signaling microdomains (lipid rafts) and the amplification of TCR signaling. Stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) is a key lipid-metabolizing enzyme localized to the endoplasmic reticulum, responsible for converting saturated fatty acids into monounsaturated fatty acids. In vitro and in MCA205-bearing mouse models (a murine fibrosarcoma cell line), treatment of CD8+ T cells with SCD1 inhibitors markedly enhances IFN-γ secretion and cytotoxic activity, while reducing oleic acid production and cholesterol ester accumulation mediated by the cholesterol acyltransferase ACAT1. Furthermore, SCD1 or ACAT1 inhibitors can synergistically enhance the antitumor efficacy of anti–PD-1 antibodies or CAR-T cell therapy in mouse tumor models [178]. Another in vitro study also demonstrated that ACAT1 knockdown significantly enhances CD8+ T cell function and increases the cytotoxic activity of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells [179].

At the genetic engineering level, directly enhancing fatty acid uptake and oxidation has been shown to markedly improve the in vivo persistence of ACT cells. However, different immune cell subsets exhibit distinct dependencies on lipid metabolism and divergent functional outcomes, indicating that lipid metabolic reprogramming must strike a precise balance between “enhanced utilization” and “avoiding overload.” Among TIL subsets, tissue-resident memory T (Trm) cells display unique metabolic preferences, relying primarily on FAO rather than glycolysis for long-term survival. In the gastric adenocarcinoma TME, fatty acid deprivation induces apoptosis of Trm cells, whereas PD-L1 blockade downregulates and upregulates the expression of fatty acid–binding proteins Fabp4/5 in tumor cells and Trm cells, respectively, markedly enhancing lipid uptake by Trm cells and thereby improving their survival both in vitro and in vivo [198]. These findings indicate that enhancing lipid uptake and FAO in specific T cell subsets serves as a crucial metabolic support for maintaining their survival and function. However, excessive or nonselective lipid uptake in the tumor microenvironment is also a key mechanism driving immune cell dysfunction. For example, high expression of the lipid transporter CD36 in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells promote the uptake of oxidized lipids, such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein (OxLDL), leading to lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial damage, and suppression of effector functions. Conversely, CD36 deficiency markedly alleviates lipid overload and restores T cell antitumor activity [152]. Moreover, persistent activation of ACC1 in the tumor microenvironment drives lipid biosynthesis and storage, creating metabolic competition with FAO and thereby impairing the energy metabolism and effector functions of TILs. Inhibition of ACC activity can redirect lipid utilization pathways, enhancing T cell metabolic adaptability and survival [199]. Beyond fatty acid metabolism, cholesterol homeostasis also exhibits a complex “double-edged sword” effect in regulating the efficacy of ACT therapy. On one hand, tumor cell membrane stiffening induced by cholesterol depletion in the tumor microenvironment has been shown to markedly enhance the antitumor activity of adoptive T cells. The underlying mechanisms may involve increased mechanotransduction capacity of T cells [200] and improved perforin insertion efficiency [201], thereby promoting tumor cell lysis [202]. On the other hand, excessively low cholesterol levels may suppress CAR-T cell proliferation and induce autophagy-mediated apoptosis, ultimately impairing their antitumor efficacy. To address this issue, CRISPR-Cas9–mediated specific knockout of liver X receptor β (LXRβ) in CAR-T cells effectively blocks cholesterol depletion–induced metabolic suppression and exhaustion signaling, thereby restoring cellular metabolic homeostasis. This strategy has demonstrated markedly enhanced CAR-T antitumor activity across multiple solid tumor models [161, 180].

Precise modulation of lipid metabolism through pharmacological interventions or genetic engineering, by enhancing FAO while limiting aberrant lipid accumulation, helps maintain a memory-like metabolic phenotype in ACT cells, improves mitochondrial function and in vivo persistence, and consequently markedly enhances their antitumor efficacy.

Targeting amino acid metabolism

Amino acid depletion and metabolic competition are prevalent in the TME, affecting key substrates such as glutamine, arginine, tryptophan, and branched-chain amino acids. Such nutrient restrictions suppress T cell mTORC1 signaling, mitochondrial metabolism, and protein synthesis, thereby limiting the proliferation and effector functions of CAR-T, TIL, and TCR-T cells. Consequently, precise targeting of amino acid metabolic pathways has emerged as a critical strategy to enhance the efficacy of ACT therapy.

In terms of pharmacological intervention, the primary approach is to alleviate abnormal amino acid depletion and inhibitory metabolic signaling in the tumor microenvironment, thereby improving the metabolic milieu for ACT cells. In the tryptophan metabolic pathway, IDO1 and tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO) are the key rate-limiting enzymes catalyzing the conversion of tryptophan to Kyn. IDO1-mediated Kyn production markedly suppresses cytokine secretion and cytotoxicity of CAR-T cells while promoting the development of an exhausted phenotype. Treatment with IDO1 inhibitors, such as epacadostat or 1-methyl-tryptophan (1-MT), reduces Kyn production, restores T cell proliferation and proinflammatory cytokine release, and significantly enhances the antitumor activity of CAR-T and TIL cells in multiple solid tumor mouse models [180]. In gastric and pancreatic tumor models, fludarabine and cyclophosphamide were used to suppress IDO1 expression in the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing CAR-T cell infiltration, inhibiting tumor growth, and prolonging survival [181]. In addition, Huang et al. demonstrated that microRNA-153 (miR-153) specifically binds to the 3’-UTR of IDO1 mRNA, suppressing its expression in colorectal cancer cells. This mechanism significantly enhances the antitumor activity of CAR-T cells both in vitro and in vivo [203]. In terms of arginine metabolism, MDSCs and other myeloid immunosuppressive cells deplete local arginine through high expression of ARG1, thereby suppressing the proliferation and effector functions of T cells. Pharmacological inhibition of ARG1, using agents such as nor-NOHA or CB-1158, can relieve this metabolic suppression and significantly restore and enhance T cell proliferation and proinflammatory cytokine secretion in vitro [182], providing strong evidence for alleviating arginine depletion and improving ACT function. Regarding glutamine metabolism, GLS1 inhibitors, such as CB-839, can reprogram the tumor microenvironment to enhance the activation of tumor antigen–specific T cells and demonstrate synergistic effects in various adoptive T cell therapy models. Notably, when combined with PD-1 or CTLA-4 checkpoint inhibitors, CB-839 significantly promotes effector T cell infiltration into tumors, markedly improving therapeutic responses in high–tumor mutation burden melanoma models [183].

In terms of genetic engineering strategies, enhancing ACT cells’ amino acid biosynthesis, transport, and resistance to metabolic suppression can achieve long-term metabolic optimization and improve their functionality within the tumor microenvironment. Taking arginine metabolism as an example, the low-arginine tumor microenvironment markedly suppresses CAR-T cell proliferation and effector functions, largely because T cells naturally lack the key enzymes, Argininosuccinate Synthase 1(ASS1) and Ornithine Transcarbamylase (OTC), required for endogenous arginine synthesis. Co-expression of ASS1 or OTC in CAR-T cells via genetic engineering markedly enhances their proliferative capacity under low-arginine conditions and improves their antitumor activity in both solid tumor and leukemia models, providing an effective strategy to overcome amino acid–mediated metabolic suppression [184]. In terms of tryptophan metabolism, highly expressed IDO1 catalyzes tryptophan degradation to generate the immunosuppressive metabolite kynurenine (Kyn), depleting an essential substrate while suppressing the proliferation and function of effector T cells and CAR-T cells. Genetic engineering to overexpress kynureninase (KYNU) in CAR-T cells can degrade accumulated Kyn, preserving their cytotoxic activity. Animal models further demonstrate that such metabolically optimized CAR-T cells exhibit markedly enhanced antitumor efficacy within Kyn-high tumor microenvironments [185]. In addition, upregulating the expression of key amino acid transporters, such as SLC7A5 (LAT1) and SLC1A5 (ASCT2), enhances T cell uptake of leucine, tryptophan, and glutamine, thereby promoting mTORC1 signaling, protein synthesis, and cytokine production. For example, Panetti and colleagues engineered CAR-T cells to co-express SLC7A5 and SLC7A11, significantly enhancing intracellular arginine metabolic flux. These metabolically optimized CAR-T cells not only retained their original cytotoxic function but also delayed the onset of exhaustion, while exhibiting superior in vitro proliferative capacity and more durable in vivo antitumor activity [186].

Pharmacological interventions primarily provide metabolic support to ACT cells by alleviating amino acid deprivation and inhibitory metabolic signaling within the tumor microenvironment, whereas genetic engineering strategies fundamentally enhance metabolic adaptability by endowing cells with autonomous amino acid synthesis, transport, and resistance to metabolic suppression. The combined application of these approaches can maximally restore and sustain the antitumor functions of CAR-T, TIL, and TCR-T cells in amino acid–restricted tumor microenvironments, offering a promising synergistic strategy to enhance ACT efficacy.

Targeting mitochondrial metabolism

In the solid tumor microenvironment, persistent hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and accumulation of metabolic byproducts such as lactate and reactive oxygen species can markedly impair mitochondrial structure and function in ACT cells, manifested by restricted OXPHOS, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, and suppressed mitochondrial biogenesis. These mitochondrial metabolic impairments not only compromise the energy supply of CAR-T, TIL, and TCR-T cells but also induce sustained activation of exhaustion-related transcriptional programs, such as TOX and NR4A, ultimately leading to diminished effector function and reduced in vivo persistence. Consequently, targeting mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a key strategy to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ACT in solid tumors.

In terms of pharmacological intervention, under hypoxic and nutrient-restricted conditions, the excessive reliance of CAR-T cells on glycolysis may temporarily meet energy demands but often results in mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced metabolic flexibility, ultimately triggering metabolic exhaustion and functional decline. Rapamycin, a specific mTORC1 inhibitor, can suppress effector glycolysis–driven terminal differentiation and promote the maintenance of a memory-like metabolic phenotype in T cells, thereby enhancing the in vivo persistence and antitumor efficacy of ACT cells. Further studies have shown that metformin-mediated AMPK activation synergizes with rapamycin-mediated mTORC1 inhibition to globally reprogram the metabolic state of CAR-T cells. AMPK activation enhances cellular energy sensing and mitochondrial biogenesis, while mTORC1 inhibition redistributes nutrient signaling and metabolic flux. Their combined action activates the mitochondrial coactivator PGC-1α, significantly increasing SRC and effectively improving CAR-T cell metabolic homeostasis and functional persistence [177]. In addition, prolonged hypoxia and nutrient competition in the tumor microenvironment drive effector T cells to rely excessively on glycolysis. This is accompanied by pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) phosphorylation mediated by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK), which inhibits pyruvate entry into mitochondria, thereby impairing OXPHOS and restricting metabolic flexibility. Dichloroacetate (DCA), a classical PDK inhibitor, relieves PDH inhibition, redirecting pyruvate from glycolysis into the TCA cycle and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism [187]. Metabolic reprogramming of T cells with DCA during in vitro expansion effectively reduces their reliance on glycolysis, enhances mitochondrial metabolic capacity and stem-like phenotype, and significantly improves in vivo engraftment efficiency and antitumor activity in both TCR-T mouse models and human CAR-T models [187]. Beyond energy metabolism remodeling, oxidative stress is another critical factor limiting mitochondrial function in ACT cells. Persistently elevated ROS in the tumor microenvironment can directly cause mitochondrial membrane potential loss and dysfunction, thereby compromising immune cell survival and effector activity. Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, including mitoquinone (MitoQ) and SkQ1, specifically reduce mitochondrial ROS levels and alleviate oxidative damage–induced cellular injury, thereby enhancing mitochondrial homeostasis and promoting cell survival [188]. In addition, MitoQ and other mitochondria-targeted antioxidants have been shown to reduce ROS levels in exhausted T cells and restore mitochondrial function, providing important mechanistic support for applying such strategies in ACT cells to improve their functionality within hypoxic, high-ROS tumor microenvironments [188]. At the genetic engineering level, directly reprogramming the mitochondrial metabolic program of ACT cells has been shown to significantly enhance their metabolic adaptability and in vivo persistence within the solid tumor microenvironment. Among these strategies, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis is one of the most representative approaches. Overexpression of the key transcriptional coactivator PGC-1α and its upstream regulators, such as TFAM, increases mitochondrial content in CAR-T, TIL, and TCR-T cells, enhances OXPHOS capacity and SRC, thereby maintaining a memory-like metabolic phenotype, delaying the activation of exhaustion-related transcriptional programs, and markedly improving in vivo persistence and long-term tumor control [189]. Beyond enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, relieving endogenous inhibitory signals of mitochondrial respiration can also effectively improve ACT cell function. Specific knockout of NR4A family transcription factors in CAR-T cells via CRISPR/Cas9 markedly enhances mitochondrial OXPHOS activity and significantly boosts antitumor efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo models [190]. In addition, MCJ, an endogenously expressed negative regulator of mitochondrial respiration in CD8+ T cells, inhibits the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Gene knockout studies have shown that MCJ-deficient CD8+ T cells exhibit markedly enhanced mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. In a B-cell leukemia mouse model, MCJ-deficient CD8+ CAR-T cells demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy compared with wild-type cells in both in vitro cytotoxicity assays and in vivo therapeutic experiments [191]. Notably, metabolic reprogramming of mitochondrial substrate utilization also demonstrates significant potential for functional enhancement. The MPC plays a central role in regulating mitochondrial substrate metabolism in T cells, and its intact function is critical for maintaining lactate oxidation capacity and antitumor efficacy in CD8+ T cells. Regulation of MPC can markedly alter substrate utilization patterns in T cells. MPC inhibition shifts cellular metabolism toward greater reliance on fatty acid and glutamine oxidation to support a memory-like phenotype, whereas maintaining moderate mitochondrial pyruvate influx in the tumor microenvironment favors OXPHOS and energy supply, thereby enhancing in vivo persistence and antitumor activity of CAR-T cells [169]. age-associated declines in NAD+ levels are considered a key metabolic basis for CAR-T cell functional deterioration. As a critical coenzyme for mitochondrial energy metabolism and redox reactions, NAD+ depletion markedly impairs mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and diminishes antitumor efficacy. Restoration of NAD+ levels can effectively reverse metabolic defects in aged CAR-T cells, significantly improving mitochondrial function and enhancing therapeutic outcomes [192].

Whether through pharmacological modulation of mitochondrial metabolic pathways or direct genetic reprogramming of mitochondrial function in ACT cells, these approaches can significantly enhance the metabolic adaptability, in vivo persistence, and antitumor efficacy of CAR-T, TIL, and TCR-T cells within the solid tumor microenvironment. In the future, combining mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming strategies with immune checkpoint blockade, targeted metabolic therapies, or next-generation CAR/TCR designs holds promise for further overcoming critical bottlenecks in ACT-based solid tumor therapy.

CAR-NK and CAR-M cell therapies

Although CAR-NK and CAR-M cells have demonstrated favorable safety profiles and preliminary efficacy in tumor immunotherapy, limited in vivo persistence, metabolic exhaustion, and TME-mediated suppression remain major barriers to their broader application. Under steady-state conditions, CAR-NK cells rely heavily on OXPHOS and FAO, whereas during activation or execution of tumor-killing functions, glycolysis is rapidly upregulated to meet energy demands. However, persistent hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction, and multiple inhibitory metabolic signals still substantially limit their in vivo persistence and antitumor efficacy. CAR-M inherently possess tumor-infiltrating capacity, and their functional phenotypes are similarly dependent on metabolic states: pro-inflammatory (M1) macrophages primarily rely on glycolysis, whereas immunosuppressive (M2) macrophages depend on FAO and OXPHOS to maintain energy homeostasis and phenotypic functions. Factors in the tumor microenvironment, such as lactate accumulation, nutrient deprivation, and acidification, can markedly impair the antitumor activity of CAR-M cells. Therefore, metabolic reprogramming tailored to the metabolic features of CAR-NK and CAR-M cells has emerged as a crucial strategy to enhance their therapeutic efficacy.

At the pharmacological intervention level, various metabolic modulators have been employed to improve the energy homeostasis and antitumor function of CAR-NK and CAR-M cells. For example, AMPK activators, such as metformin, and mTORC1 inhibitors, such as rapamycin, can enhance mitochondrial metabolism and suppress excessive glycolysis, thereby increasing metabolic flexibility and functional persistence. Experimental evidence supporting these effects has been demonstrated in NK cells [204, 205]. In addition, aberrant lactate accumulation and the resulting acidification in the tumor microenvironment represent key mechanisms of immune suppression, directly impairing the function of various effector immune cells, including NK cells. In a murine breast cancer model, genetic silencing or pharmacological inhibition of the monocarboxylate transporter MCT4 (e.g., 7acc1) blocked tumor lactate efflux, alleviated local acidification, and increased tissue pH, thereby significantly enhancing NK cell cytotoxicity, perforin release, and CD107a expression, ultimately suppressing tumor growth [120, 206]. For CAR-M cells, etomoxir, an inhibitor of the FAO rate-limiting enzyme CPT1, effectively blocks FAO-dependent metabolic reprogramming, thereby inhibiting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and promoting pro-inflammatory M1 polarization. In hepatocellular carcinoma models, etomoxir treatment significantly reduced TAM infiltration [207, 208]. Although these findings are primarily derived from TAM metabolic studies, they provide important insights for CAR-M cell engineering.

At the genetic engineering level, systematic metabolic engineering strategies specifically targeting CAR-NK cells remain limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on enhancing tumor-targeting recognition and improving cytokine support. For example, sustained activation of the IL-2 receptor signaling pathway induces upregulation of c-Myc and NRF1, which enhances mitochondrial metabolic adaptability and ATP production in NK cells, thereby improving CAR-NK cytotoxicity, resistance to exhaustion, and in vivo persistence within the solid tumor microenvironment [209]. However, direct genetic engineering approaches targeting metabolic pathways—such as enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, promoting fatty acid oxidation, or reducing reliance on glycolysis—to systematically improve CAR-NK metabolic homeostasis remain largely exploratory, with clear experimental evidence still lacking [210]. Similarly, genetic engineering studies on CAR-M cells have primarily focused on enhancing antigen-specific recognition, phagocytic capacity, and cytokine secretion. Direct targeting of metabolic pathways—such as mitochondrial biogenesis, FAO/OXPHOS, or glycolytic regulation—remains largely unreported experimentally [211].

Therefore, future studies could draw on metabolic engineering experiences from CAR-T and other immune cells, employing genetic engineering to specifically modulate key metabolic regulators (such as PGC-1α, TFAM, or lipid metabolism–related molecules) to enhance the persistence and antitumor efficacy of CAR-NK and CAR-M cells within nutrient-deprived, hypoxic, and metabolically competitive tumor microenvironments. Such metabolic engineering strategies hold promises to complement existing pharmacological interventions, thereby advancing CAR-NK and CAR-M immunotherapies toward higher efficiency and improved microenvironmental adaptability.

Conclusions and perspectives

This review systematically summarizes recent advances in ACT, providing a comprehensive analysis of its clinical advantages, existing challenges, and potential solutions across various malignancies. Although ACT has achieved remarkable clinical success in hematologic malignancies and shows broad potential in solid tumor therapy, its overall efficacy remains constrained by multiple factors. Metabolic stress in the TME—including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, accumulation of metabolic byproducts such as lactate, infiltration of immunosuppressive cell populations (e.g., Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, MDSCs), tumor antigen heterogeneity, and immune evasion—collectively limit tissue infiltration, induce functional exhaustion, and shorten in vivo persistence of adoptively transferred immune effector cells, thereby significantly compromising the clinical efficacy of ACT. Among various ACT approaches, CAR-T cell therapy has emerged as the most rapidly advancing and widely applied form of cellular immunotherapy due to its potent antitumor activity and high degree of engineerability. However, the application of CAR-T cells in solid tumor therapy still faces multiple challenges, including a lack of tumor-specific antigens, inefficient trafficking and infiltration of CAR-T cells into tumor sites, and the suppressive effects of the TME on their activation, functional maintenance, and long-term persistence. To overcome these limitations, CAR technology has been extended to other immune cell types with antitumor potential, such as CAR-NK and CAR-M cells. Although these novel ACT strategies show promise in solid tumor therapy, they remain constrained by limited effector cell sources, restricted in vivo persistence, and limitations in cell numbers and functional capacity, further highlighting the importance of elucidating the biological characteristics of immune cells and the mechanisms governing their functional regulation. Increasing evidence indicates that intense metabolic competition exists between immune cells and tumor cells within the TME, involving the reprogramming of key pathways such as glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism. This metabolic remodeling not only directly impairs the effector functions of T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells but also promotes polarization of TAMs toward the M2 phenotype and induces the expansion of Tregs and MDSCs, thereby exacerbating immunosuppression and significantly limiting the therapeutic efficacy of ACT. Based on this, the present review systematically analyzes key metabolic factors influencing ACT efficacy, with a particular focus on strategies aimed at optimizing the effector functions and in vivo persistence of adoptively transferred immune cells. These strategies include modulation of glycolytic pathways, lipid metabolic networks, amino acid homeostasis, and enhancement of mitochondrial biogenesis. With an increasing understanding of the regulatory mechanisms governing immune cell metabolism, targeting metabolic reprogramming has gradually emerged as a key strategy to enhance the antitumor activity of ACT, including CAR-T, CAR-NK, CAR-M, TIL, and TCR-T therapies. Current evidence suggests that solely enhancing glycolysis may transiently increase the cytotoxic activity of immune effector cells but is often accompanied by metabolic exhaustion and reduced persistence. In contrast, optimizing mitochondrial function, enhancing oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, and maintaining metabolic flexibility are more conducive to the formation of memory-like phenotypes and support long-term in vivo persistence of immune cells. In addition, targeting amino acid metabolic pathways, such as glutamine and arginine metabolism, has shown potential advantages in enhancing immune cell adaptability within the TME. These findings suggest that comprehensive metabolic regulation strategies centered on mitochondrial homeostasis and metabolic plasticity may offer more pronounced and durable therapeutic benefits than short-term enhancement of individual metabolic pathways. Future studies could leverage genetic engineering technologies such as CRISPR-Cas9 to precisely modulate key metabolism-related genes in immune cells (e.g., PD-1, LDHA, CPT1A), enabling dynamic reprogramming in response to metabolic stress within the TME. Beyond metabolic rewiring, recent preclinical and translational work has shown that eliminating intrinsic inhibitory regulators such as CD5 via CRISPR-Cas9 markedly enhances T-cell activation, mitigates exhaustion, and improves in vivo persistence and antitumor efficacy of engineered T cells, highlighting CD5 ablation as a complementary strategy to optimize ACT functional durability [212]. In addition, combining ACT with specific metabolic modulators—such as IDO inhibitors, metformin, or FASN inhibitors—offers a translationally promising approach to enhance clinical efficacy in tumor therapy. Nevertheless, several key issues remain unresolved in the field. The optimal metabolic states of adoptively transferred immune cells, such as CAR-T cells, during ex vivo preparation, tumor infiltration, and long-term in vivo maintenance have yet to be clearly defined. Moreover, some metabolic intervention strategies, while enhancing immune effector functions, may inadvertently promote tumor metabolic adaptation or exacerbate immunosuppression, and their long-term safety and systemic effects require further evaluation. Therefore, a clinically validated, coherent, and reliable metabolic reprogramming framework spanning the entire lifecycle of ACT has yet to be established. Future studies should systematically compare the efficacy and durability of different metabolic modulation strategies at various stages of ACT and validate their safety and feasibility in both preclinical and clinical settings. Overall, systematically elucidating and optimizing metabolic reprogramming strategies in immune cells, in combination with multimodal therapeutic approaches, will provide a crucial translational pathway to overcome treatment barriers in solid tumors and advance the clinical translation of ACT.
