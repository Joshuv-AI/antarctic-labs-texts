Perceived stigma among lung cancer patients: a cross-sectional study with latent profile analysis
Authors: Renmei Yang, Xiaoling Liu, Fengli Zuo, Maria F Jimenez-Herrera, Yi Ren, Xu Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerceived stigma is a significant psychosocial issue among lung cancer patients. Understanding the characteristics of lung cancer stigma, along with its influencing factors, is crucial for providing effective support.ObjectivesWe aimed to identify stigma profiles, examine associated demographic/clinical factors, and propose a clinically usable Short-form Cataldo Lung Cancer Stigma Scale (CLCSS-C-SF) cut-off for screening in Chinese lung cancer patients.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of data from 441 lung cancer patients who completed the CLCSS-C-SF. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was applied to analyze stigma. Both univariate analysis and multinominal logistic regression were adopted to investigate the influencing factors. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was utilized finally to establish the cut-off value of the CLCSS-C-SF.ResultsThe LPA analysis revealed three stigma profiles: low (6.09%), moderate (44.31%), and severe (49.60%). Factors associated with lower perceived stigma included primary education (odds ratio [OR] = 0.273, p = 0.007), annual household income over 100,000 Chinese yuan (OR = 6.642, p = 0.019), and absence of metastasis (OR = 8.231, p = 0.028). Those with diagnosis duration longer than 12 months (OR = 0.156, p = 0.043), retirees (OR = 1.268, p < 0.001) and those with two or more children (OR = 0.716, p = 0.011) were more likely to belong to the moderate and severe stigma profiles. The ROC curve analysis identified a 49 as the optimal cut-off value of the CLCSS-C-SF.ConclusionThis study highlights the heterogeneity and influencing factors of perceived stigma in lung cancer patients. The findings support the need for targeted psychological interventions to address stigma in this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16253-2.Keywords: Lung neoplasms, Perceived stigma, Latent profile analysis, Cut-off value, Chinese

Background

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality around the world, with approximately 2,500,000 new cases and 1,800,000 deaths reported in Global cancer statistics 2022, based on the latest GLOBOCAN estimates of cancer burden across 36 cancer types in 185 countries [1]. Despite progress in prevention, screening, and advanced treatments of lung cancer [2, 3], the overall survival rates and survival times for lung cancer have gradually improved [3]. As a result, there is an increasing focus in improving lung cancer patients’ quality of life and well-being [4].

Stigma is defined as negative beliefs, attitudes, and stereotypes associated with specific attributes or characteristics that results in discrimination and social exclusion [5]. This stigma affects various cancer patients, but lung cancer is considered the most stigmatized due to its association with smoking and the perception that it is self-inflicted [6–10]. Research suggests that lung cancer stigma contributes to multifaceted adverse consequences, such as lower participation in the screening programs, poor patient-clinician communication, delays in medical diagnosis and seeking medical assistance [11, 12], skepticism about treatment [8, 13], and reluctance to participate in clinical trials and social activities [11]. Moreover, lung cancer stigma will negatively affect psychosocial status of lung cancer patients [9, 11, 14, 15], physical health [16], and overall quality of life (QoL) [8, 11, 15, 17]. Therefore, recognizing stigma through appropriate measurement is crucial for improving QoL, especially in China, which has the highest population of lung cancer patients worldwide [1].

The Cataldo Lung Cancer Stigma Scale (CLCSS) was the first comprehensive tool developed to assess lung cancer stigma [9]. The original scale consists of 31 items across four factors: stigma and shame, social isolation, discrimination and smoking [9]. This scale demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties [9] but was burdensome in length for patients [18]. The original CLCSS has also been translated into Chinese and achieved satisfactory psychometric metrics in Chinese lung cancer patients, resulting in 31-item scale with the same four-factor structure [19]. To reduce response burden, Hong et al. developed and validated a 22-item Chinese version (CLCSS-C-SF) with a large sample of Chinese lung cancer patients, reporting satisfactory validity and reliability [20].

However, CLCSS-C-SF lacks a cut-off value, which makes it difficult to accurately assess the stigma of Chinese lung cancer patients hard. Furthermore, the characteristics of perceived stigma and its demographic and clinical influencing factors remain elusive among lung cancer patients. Therefore, it is necessary to: (1) clarify the characteristics of perceived stigma among Chinese lung cancer patients using LPA), (2) investigate the demographic and clinical factors that influence perceived stigma, and (3) determine the cut-off value for CLCSS-C-SF using receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis. Identifying different stigma profiles is crucial as it goes beyond a variable-centered approach and reveals meaningful patient subgroups, which can provide information for developing targeted, profile-specific interventions. Examining influencing factors helps identify high-risk populations that require priority support, while also establishing a cut-off value for CLCSS-C-SF, providing a practical tool for early stigma screening in clinical settings. Collectively, this will help healthcare professionals and policymakers effectively address the increasingly serious issue of stigma. Therefore, this study aims to clarify the characteristics and influencing factors of perceived stigma among Chinese lung cancer patients, and determine the cut-off value of CLCSS-C-SF.

Methods

Study design

The present study was designed as a cross-sectional study, and all results were reported strictly following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for observational research. We have attached the completed STROBE checklist in Supplementary Table 1.

Study participants

This study used data initially collected to develop instrument for predicting the risk of psychological distress of Chinese patients of lung cancer. Both Chinese versions of the full-length CLCSS and the CLCSS-C-SF were used to measure perceived stigma in the parent study. In the parent study, participants were recruited from seven hospitals in Chongqing, China, using convenience sampling following ethical approval from the respective hospital committees. Inclusion criteria included: (1) a lung cancer diagnosis, (2) self-reported awareness of their lung cancer diagnosis, as confirmed by an affirmative response to the question, “Are you aware that you have been diagnosed with lung cancer?” during screening., (3) age 18 or older, and (4) fluency in reading, speaking, and understanding Chinese. Patients with other types of cancer or psychiatric disorders were excluded.

Data collection and sample size

Eligible participants were selected and referred by oncologists or primary nurses. All participants returned their informed written consent before answering all questionnaires. Researchers conducted face-to-face interviews using standardized instructions, and questionnaire items were explained when necessary. Participants independently completed the questionnaires between September 2018 and August 2019. Based on the number of events per predictive variable, which indicates ≥ 10 participants per variable [21], a maximum sample size of 144 participants was estimated after accounting for a 20% invalid response rate because this study would investigated 12 potential factors. Moreover, 300 participants are generally sufficient for the LPA [22]. Our study ultimately included 441 valid respondents from an initial 450 participants to meet analysis criteria.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

The lead investigator (X.T.) developed a demographic information sheet based on variables identified from published literature as potential influencing factors. The study included 17 demographic and clinical variables, including age, gender, education experience, occupational status, marital status, residence, annual household income, family history of lung cancer (yes or no), smoking history (yes or no), drinking history (yes or no), surgery history (yes or no), pain severity, and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage.

Stigma

Lung cancer stigma was assessed using the CLCSS-C-SF, a validated abbreviated instrument derived from the original CLCSS [20]. The 22-item scale measures stigma across four distinct dimensions: stigma and shame (6 items), social isolation (8 items), discrimination (4 items) and smoking (4 items). Each item was answered on a 4-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating strongly disagree) and 4 indicating strongly agree. The total score of the scale is the sum of the scores of all items, with higher scores representing more severe perceived stigma. The CLCSS-C-SF demonstrated high reliability, reporting a Cronbach coefficient of 0.918 for the overall scale and 0.788 to 0.927 for the four subscales, confirming its validity in measuring stigma among patients with lung cancer in validation study [20]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s coefficient for the overall scale was 0.917, while the Cronbach’s coefficients for four subdimensions were 0.775, 0.853, 0.668, and 0.769, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Reporting of all results strictly followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for reporting observational studies [23]. Participants with any missing data were excluded from the analysis, resulting in the inclusion of only complete cases. Descriptive analyses detailed participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics and perceived stigma.

LPA was commonly used to identify symptom features and determine the optimal cut-off point for measurement instruments when accurate reference cannot be obtained [24, 25]. LPA is a person-centered method that mainly used for identifying latent sub-populations within a population based on continuous variables measured at a single time point, making it appropriate for cross-sectional data [26–28]. Despite some arbitrariness in determining profile members due to its semi-subjective properties, LPA has a relatively low misclassification rate and often yields more reasonable results than other classification approaches [29, 30]. Typically, individuals who were categorized into the latent profile with the lowest symptom or risk levels are designed “non-cases,” while others are deemed “cases” [25]. Hence, we conducted LPA to investigate the potential profiles of perceived stigma among lung cancer, using each item of the CLCSS-C-SF as the input variable, with robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimation for parameter estimation. The model fitting improvement between k-classes and k-1 classes models was compared using the Lo–Mendell–Rubin (LMR) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and a significant p-value indicates better fitting. We used several metrics to determine the optimal number of profiles, including entropy, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), and combined with the interpretability and definition of profiles. Specifically, an entropy value exceeding 0.80 indicates acceptable classification, while lower values in AIC, BIC, and aBIC indicates good model fit. The “turning point” on the scree plot of aBIC indicates an appropriate number of profiles. In addition, we determined the optimal number of profile based on a combination of statistical criteria, simplicity, and interpretability of results, for example, a probability of more than 5% of group members indicates the validity of each model [31]. Ultimately, the combinations of these fit criteria determined the final number of latent profiles.

Following optimal model selection and classification definition, Chi-square and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to evaluate associations between demographic and clinical characteristics and perceived stigma profiles. Statistically significant variables from univariate analysis were further analyzed using multinomial logistic regression. Multinomial logistic regression enables the simultaneous comparison of all latent classes against a designated reference category, offering comprehensive insights into how each predictor differentially influences the probability of belonging to specific profiles. This approach is statistically more robust and interpretable than conducting a series of pairwise binary logistic regressions, as it models all class memberships within a unified framework while preserving the overall structure of the latent class solution. Odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to express the regression results.

We further used the ROC analysis to create the optimal cut-off point of the CLCSS-C-SF. Several metrics, such as the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and Youden’s index, were adopted to assess classifier performance, with Youden’s index identifying the optimal cut-off value. We used SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3 to perform statistical analysis, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Ethical consideration

The protocol of the present study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chongqing University Cancer Hospital (Approval No: CZLS2021183-A). All procedures conducted in this study complied with the ethical requirements of the institutional committee, as well as the ethical principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. All participants returned their written informed consents before formally answering all questionnaires. To ensure data confidentiality and anonymity, all participant information was de- immediately identified by assigning a unique research code after collection. The data were analyzed and reported in aggregate form to prevent the identification of individual participants.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The median age of all participants was 60.0, with the interquartile range of 52.0 to 67.0. The majority were male (71.4%) and had limited education experience (68.0%). A significant portion were unemployed (44.9%). Nearly all were married (99.3%) and used medical insurance for balancing medical expenditures (97.3%). Over half reported no history of alcohol consumption (53.7%), but were current or former smokers (63.9%), and had been diagnosed within the past six months (53.1%). Additionally, most of the participants resided in urban areas (69.4%) and had one or two children (99.4%), but lacked family history of lung cancer (87.8%) and comorbidities (74.1%). However, the majority were in an advanced stage of illness (85.7%) and have experienced metastasis (62.6%). A minority suffered from moderate-to-severe pain (19.0%), and most had not undergone surgery (61.9%). Detailed characteristics are provided in Table 1.

Latent profile analysis

We estimated latent profile models with one-to-four-class solutions, and all results are summarized in Table 2. Moreover, all detailed materials for running LPA in Mplus software, including the complete input code and output, are presented in the supplementary dataset. All models demonstrated high entropy values (> 0.9). Although the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased with the increasing of classes numbers, the BLRT test remained significant for the 4-class solution, whereas the LMR test became non-significant. Moreover, as shown in the supplementary Fig. 1, the scree plot of aBIC exhibited an elbow at the 3-class model, indicating a point of diminishing returns in model fit improvement beyond this solution. Consequently, we determined the 3-classsolution as the final model. The classification probabilities for the most likely latent class membership were between 0.979 and 0.999, demonstrating high classification accuracy and reliability. Although the “low” stigma group comprised only 27 patients, this proportion (6.09%) exceeds the 5% threshold established in our methodology for assessing model plausibility. This confirms that the category remains interpretable and meaningful despite its limited size. The distribution of items across profiles is further illustrated in Fig. 1.

Scores of CLCSS-C-SF

The mean total CLCSS-C-SF score was (66.240 ± 11.145). The mean scores for each dimension were as follows: stigma and shame (18.055 ± 3.653), social isolation (25.143 ± 4.594), discrimination (11.633 ± 2.643), and smoking (11.361 ± 2.338). Additionally, the mean total scores for the “low”, “moderate”, and “severe” stigma groups were (40.000 ± 6.552), (59.800 ± 4.800), and (75.123 ± 4.756), respectively.

Influencing factors of perceived stigma of lung cancer patients

The results of the univariate analysis revealed significant differences in stigma scores across levels of educational level (χ2 = 23.158, p < 0.001), occupational status (χ2 = 31.908, p < 0.001), quantity of children (χ2 = 13.423, p = 0.009), household annual income (χ2 = 83.719, p < 0.001), diagnosis duration (χ2 = 17.457, p = 0.001), drinking history (χ2 = 19.837, p < 0.001), and TNM stage (χ2 = 77.477, p < 0.001), metastasis (χ2 = 16.264, p < 0.001) among three stigma profiles (shown in Table 4). Therefore, we further included the eight variables as independent variables for subsequent multinominal logistic regression analysis with the “low stigma” group defined as the reference category. The specific coding for these eight variables is detailed in supplementary Table 2. The parallel lines test (χ2 = 193.888, p < 0.001) revealed that the hypothesis of proportional odds assumption was violated, providing that the use of multinomial logistic regression is reasonable. The collinearity diagnosis revealed that the model tolerance values were between 0.555 and 0.919, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) values were between 1.088 and 1.802, indicating that there is no multicollinearity between the independent variables. The model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke’s pseudo-R², with a value of 0.517 indicating a good fit. As shown in Table 5, the result of multinomial logistic regression analysis indicated that, compared to participants with university education, those with primary education (odds ratio [OR] = 0.273, p = 0.007) were less likely to belong to the low stigma group. Compare to those with an annual household income of ≤ 100,000 Chinese yuan, participants with an income over 100,000 Chinese yuan (OR = 6.642, p = 0.019) were more likely to belong to the low stigma group. Compared to participants with metastasis, those with no metastasis (OR = 8.231, p = 0.028) were more likely to belong to the low stigma group. Compared to those with a diagnosis duration of ≤ 12 months, participants with a diagnosis duration longer than 12 months (OR = 0.156, p = 0.043) were less likely to belong to the low stigma group. Compare to employed participants, retirees (OR = 1.268, p < 0.001) were more likely to belong to the low stigma group. Compare to participants with no children or one child, those with two or more children (OR = 0.716, p = 0.011) were less likely to belong to the low stigma group.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis

The ROC curve of CLCSS-C-SF score yielded an AUC value of 0.998 (95% CI: 0.995 to 1.000, p < 0.001), indicating excellent predictive ability for perceived stigma (as shown in Fig. 2). The optimal cut-off value was ≥ 49. At this threshold, the sensitivity was 0.986 (95% CI: 0.969 to 0.995), the specificity was 1.000 (95% CI: 0.872 to 1.000), and Youden’s index was 0.993. The confusion matrix is presented in Supplementary Table 3. In a proposed clinical workflow, screening for stigma using the CLCSS-C-SF would be administered by trained nursing staff during routine outpatient visits or initial hospital admissions for lung cancer patients. Patients screening positive would be referred for a comprehensive psychosocial assessment conducted by a clinical psychologist. Based on this assessment, appropriate follow-up interventions, such as counseling, support group enrollment, or psychiatric consultation, would be initiated.

Discussion

The present study employs LPA to characterize the stigma profiles of Chinese lung cancer patients and investigates possible demographic and clinical influencing factors. The study categorizes stigma into three latent profiles, including low, moderate, and severe. The main demographic and clinical characteristics associated with lung cancer stigma were measured. Generally speaking, people with higher levels of education, annual household incomes exceeding 100,000 Chinese yuan, diagnosis duration exceeding 12 months, and no metastasis are more likely to experience low stigma. Conversely, retired individuals and those with two or more children are more likely to experience moderate and severe stigma. The cut-off value of the CLCSS-C-SV is determined to be 49.

This study identified three different stigma profiles: “low stigma,” “moderate stigma,” and “severe stigma” groups. This finding is consistent with the latent profile analysis observed in breast cancer patients [32]. However, a significant divergence lies in the distribution of patients, highlighting the unique nature of the stigma associated with lung cancer. Although 21.9% of breast cancer patients belonging to the “low stigma” group, this situation only accounts for 6.09% of the lung cancer cohort, and the vast majority (93.91%) of patients experience moderate to severe stigma. This stark contrast is further reflected in the high mean total stigma score (66.240 ± 11.145) of our sample. The highest score on the social isolation sub-scale indicates that alienation may be a core experience. The clear gradient of scores from “low” to “sever” strongly validates the latent classification and underscores the profound distress of high-risk groups. These findings collectively indicate that the burden of stigma on lung cancer patients is not only more prevalent, but also more severe, possibly exacerbated by smoking-related blame [20]. Based on breast cancer research, which identified resilience as a key mediator [32], we propose that interventions should be targeted [33]. The “severe stigma” group may require intensive therapy to address shame and social isolation, while the “moderate stigma” group can benefit from resilience-building and peer support programs to prevent the condition from worsening. The small size of the “low stigma” group underscores the need for public health departments to incorporate routine stigma assessments into clinical practice in order to achieve early and personalized interventions.

Educational level was a significant factor influencing stigma. Contrary to what might be intuitively expected, the present study revealed that participants with lower educational levels (e.g., primary education) had significantly lower odds of belonging to the low stigma profile compared to those with university education, suggesting they experienced higher levels of stigma. This finding contrasts with some previous literature [34–36], which suggested that higher education might increase stigma through greater knowledge and fear. The counter-intuitive link between lower education and lower stigma may reflect reduced illness awareness, different coping styles, or lower internalized blame and merits replication and qualitative follow-up. Our study found that higher annual household income was associated with lower perceived stigma. We are not surprised by this finding because a higher annual household income can significantly reduce the psychological and financial stress associated with a cancer diagnosis and treatment [5, 36]. Additionally, the absence of metastasis was another factor linked to lower perceived stigma, possibly due to fewer symptoms and complications, resulting in better QoL and reduced psychological distress [37]. Conversely, the duration since diagnosis, retirement status, and having two or more children were associated with moderate to severe perceived stigma. The length of time since diagnosis significantly influenced perceived stigma. Notably, those diagnosed more than 12 months ago reported more serious perceived stigma. This can be understood that in the context that patients may face increased treatment-related side effects and a higher risk of complications over time [38], which can intensify their experience of stigma. Retirement may be linked to reduced social engagement and increased feelings of dependency [38], while responsibility of supporting multiple children can amplify perceived stigma due to a greater number of children mean more responsibility, a greater family burden and an increased financial burden [34].

The determination of the cut-off value of the CLCSS-C-SF at 49 through ROC analysis is an important contribution to the assessment tool that can be used to measure stigma in lung cancer patients. Specifically, CLCSS-C-SF captures perceived stigma, which refers to patients’ subjective awareness and expectation of public stereotypes and discrimination, distinct from public stigma (societal attitudes and beliefs) and self-stigma (internalization of negative beliefs) [39]. This cut-off value improves the screening accuracy of the scale, enabling healthcare practitioners to identify patients who suffer from significant stigma and require intervention. Using ROC analysis to determine this cut-off value ensures that the scale maintains a balance between sensitivity and specificity, making it a reliable tool for clinical practice and research. However, the prevalence beyond the threshold of ≥ 49 in this Chinese cohort may be limited, as stigma perception is influenced by cultural norms, social contexts, and healthcare systems [39]. Future cross-sectional studies are needed to validate its applicability in other populations.

Undoubtedly, the results should be interpreted cautiously due to several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, it inherently lacks a temporal dimension, which restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between perceived stigma and its influencing factors. Second, convenience sampling will inevitably limit the generalizability of the findings. Although this sampling strategy is practical and often necessary in clinical settings, it may not accurately represent a wider population of lung cancer patients. Third, stigma is a multifaceted construct encompassing both public stigma and self-perceived stigma. Our study focused exclusively on self-perceived stigma, potentially overlooking the influence and impact of public stigma. Fourth, in the logistic regression analysis, complete separation occurred for certain variables due to the limited sample size in the non-stigma group, which prevented the model from converging and obtaining reliable odds ratio estimates. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies are warranted to determine potential predictors of stigma in Chinese lung cancer patients. Fifth, although we included key demographic and clinical variables in this study, we cannot rule out the possibility of residual confounding factors due to unmeasured factors (e.g., psychological traits, social support, or specific treatment modalities). Future studies will benefit from incorporating these potential confounding factors to further validate the relationships identified in our analysis. Finally, our goal is to establish a cut-off value for CLCSS-C-SF; however, due to the fact that the threshold comes from the same variables used in LPA classification and ROC analysis, there is a potential risk of overfitting. This may lead to overly optimistic estimates of the accuracy of the determined cut-off value. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct external validation in an independent queue to confirm the generalizability and stability of the determined cut-off value.

Conclusion

This study emphasizes the complexity of perceived stigma among lung cancer patients and the multifaceted influences that shape it. The three-profile model provides a comprehensive framework for understanding stigma of this population, while the identified demographic and clinical factors provide actionable insights for targeted interventions. The validated threshold of CLCSS-C-SF further enhances the clinical practicality of the scale. These insights pave the way for more detailed and effective strategies to reduce stigma, ultimately improving the psychosocial well-being of lung cancer patients.

Relevance to clinical practice

Identifying different stigma profiles and their related factors is of great significance for tailored interventions. Understanding that lower educational level, higher household income, and lack of metastasis are protective factors against stigma can inform for developing targeted support programs. Conversely, recognizing that individuals diagnosed more than 12 months ago, retirees, and those with more family members have a higher degree of stigma highlights the need for specific psychosocial interventions in these subgroups. Moreover, the established cut-off value of CLCSS-C-SF enhances its practicality in clinical settings, enabling healthcare providers to efficiently identify patients at risk of significant stigma and facilitate timely interventions.

Relevance to future research

Future research should explore longitudinal changes in stigma perceptions and investigate the efficacy of tailored interventions in mitigating stigma among lung cancer patients. Additionally, expanding the research to diverse cultural contexts can further validate the generalizability of these findings and the applicability of the CLCSS-C-SF cut-off value.
