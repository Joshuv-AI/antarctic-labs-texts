A dataset of heart sound regurgitation of patients with heart valve disorders
Authors: Mohammad Fraiwan, Ali Ibnian, Nishi Shahnaj Haider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart regurgitation is a cardiac condition characterized by the backward flow of blood, producing audible murmur sounds detectable during auscultation. If left untreated, it can lead to serious complications affecting cardiac function. This article presents a comprehensive dataset of heart sound recordings, including aortic regurgitation (AR), mitral regurgitation (MR), tricuspid regurgitation (TR), and healthy heart sounds, collected from patients at a single hospital using an electronic stethoscope. For each participant, recordings were obtained from three standard chest locations, and all diagnoses were confirmed by an experienced cardiologist. The dataset provides high-quality, labeled recordings that capture the variability of regurgitation sounds across different types and locations. It is intended to support the development and evaluation of automated algorithms for detecting cardiac abnormalities, including machine learning and signal processing approaches. Additionally, this dataset offers an educational resource for medical students and trainee clinicians to practice auscultation skills, recognize different types of regurgitation murmurs, and improve diagnostic proficiency. By making these recordings publicly available, the dataset can serve as a benchmark resource for both research and clinical training in cardiac auscultation.Keywords: Auscultation, Diagnosis, Electronic stethoscope, Heart valve disease, Heart Regurgitation

Background

Heart valve regurgitation is a condition in which a heart valve fails to close properly, causing backward blood flow. This can result in serious complications affecting both cardiac and pulmonary function [6]. Depending on the severity and progression of the disease, regurgitation may require medical management or surgical intervention, such as valve repair or replacement, to prevent long-term damage. Clinical assessment of regurgitation severity relies on multiple staging mechanisms, including echocardiographic grading (mild, moderate, or severe), functional assessment of cardiac output, and evaluation of symptoms, which help guide treatment decisions and surgical planning [7]. Over the past decades, diagnostic methods for identifying heart regurgitation have advanced significantly, including auscultation, echocardiography, and other clinical tools. In addition to these traditional approaches, computerized analysis of heart sounds has shown substantial potential in detecting and classifying heart valve disorders [1,2].

This article presents a comprehensive Heart Sound Regurgitation dataset, including recordings of aortic regurgitation (AR), mitral regurgitation (MR), tricuspid regurgitation (TR), and healthy heart sounds. The data were collected from diagnosed patients under the supervision of an experienced cardiologist, with recordings obtained from multiple chest locations and controlled body positions to ensure clinical relevance.

The dataset is intended to support multiple research and educational applications. For researchers, it provides high-quality labeled recordings suitable for developing and evaluating machine learning and deep learning algorithms for automated detection and classification of heart regurgitation [[3], [4], [5]]. For medical students and trainee clinicians, the dataset serves as an educational resource to practice auscultation, recognize different types of regurgitant murmurs, and improve diagnostic proficiency. By providing access to standardized, high-quality heart sound recordings, this dataset aims to accelerate both AI-based innovations and medical education in the field of cardiac diagnostics.

Data Description

The article presents heart sound recordings from the aortic (A), mitral (M), and tricuspid (T) regions of the human chest wall [8], including three types of regurgitation— aortic regurgitation, mitral regurgitation, tricuspid regurgitation, and normal heart sounds. Recordings were obtained from 104 participants with informed consent, following ethical committee guidelines. The dataset includes 17 AR, 68 MR, 84 TR, and 230 N recordings. All recordings are provided in.wav format with clear annotations. For example, the file E_H_1_M.wav indicates participant ID ‘1′, mitral region ‘M’, heart sound ‘H’, and extended filtration mode ‘E’ used during recording. The dataset is organized into four folders—AR, MR, TR, and N—each containing the corresponding heart sound recordings. Additionally, the “Heart Sound Annotation.xlsx” file provides comprehensive metadata, including participant age, gender, type of regurgitation, echocardiographic grade, and the number of recordings per category.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The presence of heart regurgitation was initially confirmed for all enrolled participants using echocardiography, ensuring that each case in the dataset reflects clinically validated findings. Following this confirmation, heart sound recordings were obtained from three primary valve regions on the chest wall: aortic (A), mitral (M), and tricuspid (T), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Recordings were performed with participants in a sitting position to standardize thoracic expansion, minimize motion artifacts, and optimize auscultation conditions.

Anatomical locations on the chest wall for heart sound recordings: aortic valve (AV) at the second intercostal space along the right sternal border, mitral valve (MV) at the apex near the fifth intercostal space along the midclavicular line, tricuspid valve (TV) at the lower left sternal border, and pulmonary valve (PV).

Each valve region was selected to maximize the audibility of the corresponding regurgitation type. Aortic regurgitation was recorded at the second intercostal space along the right sternal border, where diastolic murmurs are most pronounced. Mitral regurgitation recordings were obtained at the apex of the heart near the fifth intercostal space along the midclavicular line, the location where systolic murmurs are most distinct. Tricuspid regurgitation was recorded at the lower left sternal border, where tricuspid murmurs are best heard. These standard chosen anatomical positions ensure that the recorded sounds accurately represent the underlying valve pathology.

Heart sounds were captured using the extended filtration mode of the 3 M Littmann Electronic Stethoscope Model 3200, which provides a wide frequency range that preserves subtle murmur components while minimizing ambient noise. Due to the stethoscope’s limited memory capacity, recordings were transferred via Bluetooth to a nearby computer using StethAssist software for secure storage and immediate backup.

Fig. 2 shows amplitude-time plots for each regurgitation type—AR, MR, TR—as well as healthy heart sounds. They provide a visual representation of heart sound signals, showing the timing and intensity of each beat, which help to correlate visual signal patterns with auscultation findings. These high-quality recordings provide a valuable resource for multiple applications, including the development and evaluation of AI algorithms for automated murmur detection, signal processing research such as denoising and feature extraction, and medical education, enabling students and clinicians to practice auscultation and enhance diagnostic proficiency.

Limitations

While this dataset provides high-quality, clinically validated heart sound recordings, several limitations should be considered:

1.The number of recordings for certain regurgitation types, particularly aortic regurgitation (AR), is relatively small, which may limit the robustness of AI-based models and prevent reliable staging of severity. To enable comprehensive analyses that include disease grading, additional recordings across all regurgitation types would be beneficial.

The number of recordings for certain regurgitation types, particularly aortic regurgitation (AR), is relatively small, which may limit the robustness of AI-based models and prevent reliable staging of severity. To enable comprehensive analyses that include disease grading, additional recordings across all regurgitation types would be beneficial.

2.A few recordings contain audible echoes or artifacts, which reflect either physiological variations or recording conditions; these should be taken into account when performing signal analysis.

A few recordings contain audible echoes or artifacts, which reflect either physiological variations or recording conditions; these should be taken into account when performing signal analysis.

3.Because the dataset captures a wide frequency range, specialized electronic stethoscopes or high-quality speakers are recommended for listening on a computer, as low-end speakers or standard headphones may not reproduce the low-frequency components accurately. This limitation, however, is unlikely to affect clinical auscultation where trained professionals use standard stethoscopes.

Because the dataset captures a wide frequency range, specialized electronic stethoscopes or high-quality speakers are recommended for listening on a computer, as low-end speakers or standard headphones may not reproduce the low-frequency components accurately. This limitation, however, is unlikely to affect clinical auscultation where trained professionals use standard stethoscopes.

4.While the dataset originates from a single hospital, and the demographic diversity is limited, the acoustic characteristics of heart regurgitation are largely determined by the underlying pathophysiology, making the dataset broadly applicable for both research and educational purposes. These considerations should guide proper utilization of the dataset and inform future efforts to expand and diversify it.
