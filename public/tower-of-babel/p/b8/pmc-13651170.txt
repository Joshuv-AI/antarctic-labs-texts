Surgical outcomes of percutaneous pedicle screw fixation for unstable traumatic thoracolumbar fractures: a meta-analysis focused on randomized controlled trials from the past 15 years
Authors: Jianfeng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrevious meta-analyses often lacked explicit time and included heterogeneous case types and surgical strategies. This led to substantial heterogeneity and limited the clinical applicability of these meta-analyses. This study focuses on evidence from the past 15 years, systematically comparing percutaneous pedicle screw fixation (PPSF) with open pedicle screw fixation (OPSF) for unstable traumatic thoracolumbar fractures (TTF). We aim to evaluate perioperative outcomes and radiographic correction based on contemporary practice.MethodsRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English between January 2011 and January 2026 were retrieved from PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and the Web of Science Core Collection. Studies were screened using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Six RCTs involving 305 patients were included, with 151 patients in the experimental group and 154 in the control group. Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4. Mean difference (MD) was used as the effect size. Heterogeneity was assessed with the I2 statistic. Sensitivity analysis was used to identify the main source of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test.ResultsThe random-effects meta-analysis showed that the experimental group had significantly less intraoperative blood loss (MD = − 32.51 mL, 95% CI − 57.52 to − 7.51, P = 0.01), shorter postoperative hospital stay (MD = − 2.09 d, 95% CI − 3.89 to − 0.29, P = 0.02), and smaller postoperative Cobb angle (MD = − 1.06°, 95% CI − 1.67 to − 0.46, P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in operative time (MD = − 10.15 min, 95% CI − 28.75 to 8.46, P = 0.29) or postoperative vertebral wedging angle (MD = 0.25°, 95% CI − 1.73 to 2.24, P = 0.80) between groups. Sensitivity analyses identified the study by Yang et al. as the main source of heterogeneity for operative time. After excluding this study, the fixed-effects model showed a shorter operative time in the experimental group (MD = − 8.85 min, 95% CI − 12.74 to − 4.95, P < 0.001). Perioperative outcomes showed high heterogeneity, whereas radiographic outcomes showed low to moderate heterogeneity. Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias. Risk-of-bias assessment classified four RCTs as having a low risk of bias and two as having some concerns; no study was judged to have a high risk of bias.ConclusionPPSF may offer perioperative advantages over OPSF in selected unstable traumatic thoracolumbar fractures, mainly by reducing visible intraoperative blood loss and shortening postoperative hospital stay. PPSF was also associated with a modestly smaller postoperative segmental Cobb angle, although its clinical relevance and long-term durability remain uncertain. The operative-time finding should be interpreted cautiously because it became significant only in exploratory sensitivity analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07094-7.Keywords: Traumatic thoracolumbar fracture, Percutaneous pedicle screw fixation, Open pedicle screw fixation, Unstable spinal fracture, Meta-analysis, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Traumatic thoracolumbar fracture (TTF) is a common musculoskeletal injury caused by high-energy trauma. In this meta-analysis, TTF refers to acute traumatic fractures involving T10–L5, a region that includes the thoracolumbar junction and therefore may be affected by differences in local sagittal alignment during reduction and follow-up assessment. The annual global incidence is approximately 38.6 per 100,000 population. These injuries account for 62–70% of all spinal fractures, with 35–45% cases classified as unstable [1, 2]. In the included trials, instability was defined according to the original study criteria, mainly AO/Magerl or AO Spine type B/C injury, translational or rotational instability, or Denis two- or three-column involvement. In addition, 18–28% of patients present with spinal cord or nerve root injury. These factors increase the risk of chronic low back pain, progressive deformity, neurological deterioration, and long-term disability. As a result, TTF imposes a considerable burden on both patients’ quality of life and healthcare systems worldwide [3]. Surgical treatment remains the first-line approach for unstable TTF. Its central aims are to achieve accurate fracture reduction, restore spinal stability, and reduce the risk of long-term complications [4]. Percutaneous pedicle screw fixation (PPSF) is a key technique in minimally invasive spine surgery. It has been widely adopted due to its reduced soft-tissue damage, lower intraoperative blood loss, less postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stay compared with open surgery [5, 6].

Several meta-analyses have compared PPSF with traditional open pedicle screw fixation (OPSF). However, important limitations remain. Many studies did not restrict the publication period, which led to the inclusion of early-stage, lower-quality studies alongside more recent, standardized research [7, 8]. In addition, different fracture types such as osteoporotic and pathological fractures were often combined. Studies involving additional procedures such as spinal canal decompression or bone graft fusion were also included, which greatly increased between-study heterogeneity [9, 10].

The period since 2011 represents a key stage in the development of PPSF. During this time, surgical techniques became more standardized, indications more precisely defined, and technologies such as intraoperative navigation and robotic assistance more widely applied. Study design and outcome selection during this period also align more consistently with current clinical practice [11]. Therefore, earlier pooled evidence may not accurately reflect the present efficacy and safety of PPSF. To date, no meta-analysis has focused specifically on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from the past 15 years. Moreover, high-quality comparisons that isolate the surgical approach itself remain limited, because previous reviews often mixed percutaneous and open fixation studies with different fracture types, decompression procedures, bone grafting, fusion, or other adjunctive interventions. As a result, current clinical decision-making still lacks robust evidence aligned with modern surgical practice.

Against this background, the present study strictly limited inclusion to RCTs published in English between 2011 and 2026. The analysis was restricted to studies directly comparing PPSF with conventional open surgery alone. Studies involving planned spinal canal decompression, laminectomy, anterior reconstruction, bone grafting, fusion, vertebroplasty, or kyphoplasty were not included, so the pooled comparison focused on posterior pedicle screw fixation alone. Six eligible studies were ultimately included. We evaluated outcomes from two core perspectives—perioperative indicators (operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative hospital stay) and radiographic reduction outcomes (postoperative Cobb angle and postoperative vertebral wedging angle). By applying strict inclusion criteria, this study sought to minimize heterogeneity, fill the gap in meta-analytic evidence limited to the past 15 years in this field, and provide higher-level evidence better suited to contemporary surgical decision-making for unstable TTF. It may also help refine the clinical decision-making and operative workflow of PPSF and, in turn, improve patient prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. It was designed as a secondary analysis of publicly available English-language literature. Eligible studies were restricted to prospective parallel-group RCTs published between January 1, 2011 and January 31, 2026 (the past 15 years).

Literature search strategy

The final literature search was completed on February 15, 2026. Four major international academic databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and the Web of Science Core Collection. All searches were strictly limited to full-text articles published in English and aligned with the predefined 15-year time window. Search strategies combined medical subject headings (MeSH/Emtree) with free-text keywords. To ensure completeness, reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were also screened manually. The complete PubMed search strategy is shown in Table 1. Detailed strategies for the other databases are provided in Supplementary File 1, adapted to each database’s specific indexing rules and search syntax.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study design: publicly available prospective, parallel-group RCTs, regardless of whether blinding of outcome assessors was implemented; (2) study population: patients with fresh unstable TTF confirmed by combined X-ray, CT, and MRI, with fracture levels limited to T10–L5, age 18–75 years, and an interval from injury to surgery of ≤ 3 weeks. Unstable fracture met at least one of the following criteria: AO spinal fracture type B (tension band injury), type C (translational/rotational unstable injury), or Denis classification involving ≥ 2 columns; (3) interventions: the experimental group received PPSF and the control group received OPSF. No planned adjunctive procedures, including vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty, spinal canal decompression, laminectomy, anterior reconstruction, bone grafting, or fusion, were permitted; (4) outcomes: each article reported at least 1 of the 5 core outcome indicators prespecified in this study, with complete extractable data; (5) publication requirements: full-text articles in English published within the predefined 2011–2026 period, neurological deficit was not used as a separate pooling variable because neurological status and decompression indications were not reported consistently across the eligible RCTs.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-RCTs, including retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, case series, reviews, expert commentaries, conference abstracts, letters, animal experiments, or cadaveric biomechanical studies; (2) studies involving pathological or osteoporotic vertebral fractures, old fractures (> 3 weeks after injury), congenital spinal deformity, previous spinal surgery, severe multiorgan failure, coagulation disorders, or malignancy; (3) non-English articles, unavailable full texts, or duplicate reports (studies with the largest sample size and the most complete follow-up data were retained); (4) studies with non-comparable interventions or additional procedures introducing confounding; (5) studies with incomplete core outcome data, from which complete means and standard deviations could not be obtained or converted.

Study selection

Study screening was completed independently by two investigators who had received standardized training. A pilot consistency test was performed, and the inter-rater Kappa value was ≥ 0.85, indicating good agreement. Duplicate records were removed using EndNote 21. Titles and abstracts were then screened to exclude studies that were clearly ineligible, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved by a third senior investigator with over 12 years of experience in evidence-based spine research. Ultimately, 6 RCTs in English met all inclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Two investigators independently extracted the data under double-blind conditions, and all entries were subsequently cross-checked to avoid transcription errors. The definitions and raw data of the 5 prespecified core outcome indicators are as follows: operative time (time from skin incision to wound closure, min), intraoperative blood loss (suction volume plus gauze-derived estimates, mL; this outcome represented visible intraoperative blood loss as reported in the original RCTs. Hidden blood loss was not calculated because the included trials did not provide sufficient perioperative hemoglobin, hematocrit, or blood-volume data for formula-based estimation), postoperative hospital stay (time from surgery to discharge, d), postoperative Cobb angle (the angle between the superior endplate of the proximal vertebra and the inferior endplate of the distal vertebra at the fractured segment measured at the final postoperative follow-up°; for pooling, Cobb angle was treated as the local segmental angle at the fractured level rather than as global thoracic kyphosis, lumbar lordosis, or whole-spine sagittal alignment), and postoperative vertebral wedging angle (the wedge angle corresponding to the difference between the anterior and posterior heights of the injured vertebra measured at the final postoperative follow-up, °). If a study reported multiple follow-up time points, the value recorded at the longest available follow-up was used for radiographic outcomes.

For all continuous variables, means and standard deviations were extracted directly when available. If only medians and interquartile ranges were reported, values were converted using the Hozo method. Missing or unclear data were requested from study authors via emails. If no valid reply was received within 14 days, the data were considered unobtainable, and the corresponding outcome from that study was excluded from analysis.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the 6 included English-language RCTs were assessed using the Cochrane-recommended Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool. Two independent investigators performed the assessment under double-blind conditions, and disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer. After completing the domain-level assessments, each study was assigned an overall risk-of-bias grade according to the following criteria: low risk of bias if all domains were rated as low risk; some concerns if at least one domain raised some concerns but none was rated as high risk; and high risk of bias if one or more domains were rated as high risk.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration). A two-sided significance level of α of 0.05 was used as the significance threshold, and a Z-test with P < 0.05 for the pooled effect size was considered statistically significant. All outcomes were continuous variables, and mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was used as the pooled effect measure, as the units for each outcome were consistent across the included studies, requiring no additional standardization. A random-effects model was used for all primary meta-analyses, given its preference in medical meta-analyses where between-study heterogeneity is anticipated. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic and interpreted as low (< 30%), moderate (30–60%), substantial (60–90%), or considerable (> 90%). Fixed-effect models were used only in sensitivity analyses to explore the robustness of the primary random-effects results and were not used to replace the primary estimates. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially removing individual studies and recalculating the pooled effect size. By comparing the pooled estimates and heterogeneity before and after each removal, the stability and reliability of the combined results were assessed. If heterogeneity decreased markedly after exclusion of one study, that study was regarded as the principal source of heterogeneity, and the reasons for its influence were further examined. As only 6 RCTs were ultimately included, publication bias was evaluated with the Egger’s test, with P < 0.05 indicating the presence of publication bias.

Results

Study selection process

A total of 141 records were identified through database searching, and no additional records were identified through other sources. After duplicate records were removed, 65 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 47 records were excluded because they clearly did not meet the eligibility criteria. The remaining 18 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Twelve full-text articles were excluded, mainly because complete outcome data could not be obtained after contacting the authors or because the follow-up duration was shorter than 3 months. Ultimately, 6 prospective RCTs met all inclusion criteria [12–17] (Fig. 1).

The 6 included studies enrolled 305 participants. Of these, 151 patients underwent PPSF (experimental group) and 154 underwent OPSF (control group). These studies were published between 2012 and 2026, providing good coverage of the 15-year study period (Table 2).

Methodological quality assessment of the included studies

All 6 studies used either computer-generated random number tables or a central randomization system and were therefore judged to be at low risk of bias for sequence generation. Allocation concealment was adequate in 4 studies, whereas the remaining 2 did not clearly describe the implementation process and were therefore judged as having some concerns. Blinding of surgeons and patients was not feasible due to the nature of the intervention. Therefore, assessment focused on outcome evaluation. Five studies reported blinded assessment of radiographic outcomes and were rated as low risk. One study did not clearly report blinding and was classified as having some concerns. All 6 studies had low attrition rates (< 8%) and provided complete outcome data; these domains were therefore rated as low risk. All studies completely reported the prespecified outcomes, with no evidence of selective reporting of positive findings. In addition, all studies performed sample size estimation in advance, baseline characteristics were comparable between groups, and no other obvious source of bias was identified. Overall, four studies were classified as having a low risk of bias and two as having some concerns; no study was classified as having a high risk of bias. The main concerns were incomplete reporting of allocation concealment and outcome-assessor blinding in selected trials. Thus, the overall study quality was considered adequate for quantitative synthesis, although not uniformly low risk across all domains (Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis

Comparison of operative time

All included studies reported operative time. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 97%), so a random-effects model was applied. No statistically significant difference in operative time was found between the two groups (P = 0.29). Sensitivity analysis showed that after exclusion of the study by Yang et al., heterogeneity decreased substantially (I2 = 37%), suggesting that this study was the main source of heterogeneity. The analysis was then repeated using a fixed-effect model as an exploratory sensitivity analysis, which suggested a shorter operative time in the experimental group than in the control group (MD = − 8.85 min, 95% CI − 12.74 to − 4.95, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot comparing operative time between PPSF and OPSF

Comparison of intraoperative blood loss

All studies reported intraoperative blood loss. As with operative time, between-study heterogeneity remained considerable (I2=96%). Unlike operative time, however, the random-effects model showed that intraoperative blood loss in the experimental group was 32.51 mL lower than that in the control group (95% CI − 57.52 to − 7.51, P = 0.01). Sensitivity analysis showed that heterogeneity disappeared after excluding three studies (Jiang et al., Yang et al., and Zhang et al.) (I2 = 0%). The fixed-effects model still showed lower intraoperative blood loss in the experimental group than in the control group (95% CI − 11.83 to − 2.26, P = 0.04). However, this result should be interpreted cautiously due to the exclusion of multiple studies (Fig. 4).

Forest plot comparing intraoperative blood loss between PPSF and OPSF

Comparison of postoperative length of hospital stay

Considerable heterogeneity was observed for postoperative hospital stay (I2 = 93%). The random-effects model showed that hospital stay in the experimental group was 2.09 d shorter than in the control group (95% CI − 3.89 to − 0.29, P = 0.02). Sensitivity analysis identified that the studies by Yang et al. and Zhang et al. were the main sources of heterogeneity. After their exclusion, heterogeneity decreased (I2 = 33%). When the model was switched to a fixed-effects model, the between-group difference remained significant (P < 0.001), and postoperative hospital stay in the experimental group was 1.05 d shorter than in the control group (95% CI − 1.55 to − 0.54) (Fig. 5).

Forest plot comparing postoperative length of hospital stay between PPSF and OPSF

Comparison of postoperative Cobb angle

Five studies reported postoperative Cobb angle. Because follow-up durations were not uniform across studies, the value recorded at the final follow-up was used in all cases. No heterogeneity was observed among studies for postoperative Cobb angle (I2 = 0%). The pooled analysis showed that the postoperative Cobb angle was lower in the experimental group than in the control group (95% CI − 1.67 to − 0.46, P < 0.001). The result remained unchanged under a fixed-effects model (Fig. 6).

Forest plot comparing postoperative Cobb angle between PPSF and OPSF

Comparison of postoperative vertebral wedging angle

Four studies reported vertebral wedging angle, heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 41%). The random effects and fixed-effects models showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups in postoperative vertebral wedging angle (P = 0.08) (Fig. 7).

Forest plot comparing postoperative vertebral wedging angle between PPSF and OPSF

Sensitivity analysis restricted to studies with ≥ 12 months of follow-up

To assess the impact of follow-up duration on radiographic outcomes, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 5 studies with ≥ 12 months of follow-up (excluding Jiang XZ 2012, which had 3 months of follow-up).

For postoperative Cobb angle, the random-effects analysis showed consistent results with the primary analysis (MD = − 1.08°, 95% CI − 1.70 to − 0.45, P = 0.0007). For postoperative vertebral wedging angle, the analysis of 4 studies with ≥ 12 months of follow-up also showed no significant difference between groups (P = 0.36). These findings indicate that radiographic outcomes were robust to variations in follow-up duration (Fig. 8).

Discussion

This meta-analysis focused on evidence published between 2011 and 2026 and included 6 eligible RCTs involving 305 patients with unstable TTF. Overall, PPSF showed advantages over OPSF in reducing intraoperative blood loss, shortening postoperative hospital stay, and improving postoperative Cobb angle. No statistically significant difference in operative time was observed in the primary analysis. However, after removal of the main source of heterogeneity, PPSF also showed a shorter operative time. No between-group difference was found for postoperative vertebral wedging angle.

A notable finding is the contrast in heterogeneity across outcomes. Perioperative indicators (operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative hospital stay) showed very high heterogeneity, whereas radiographic outcomes (postoperative Cobb angle and vertebral wedging angle) showed low to moderate heterogeneity. Egger’s test did not detect statistical evidence of small-study effects, but the small number of included RCTs and the considerable heterogeneity in perioperative outcomes limit the certainty of the pooled estimates.

In the analysis of operative time, heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 97%), and this largely explains why the random-effects model did not show a statistically significant between-group difference. Sensitivity analysis based on sequential exclusion of individual studies showed that the 2019 study by Yang et al. was the main source of heterogeneity. In that study, operative time in the experimental group was only 51.55 ± 7.10 min, far shorter than in other included studies. After its exclusion, heterogeneity decreased (I2 = 37%), and the fixed-effects model clearly showed that PPSF shortened operative time by about 8.85 min compared with OPSF. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in surgeon experience and case complexity, although these explanations remain clinically plausible rather than definitive because the original trials did not provide sufficient data for subgroup analysis or meta-regression. The study by Yang et al. was conducted in a specialized minimally invasive spine center with highly experienced surgeons and a predominance of AO type B fractures. These fractures are less complex and require fewer adjustments during screw placement. By contrast, other studies were conducted in general hospitals with variable surgical experience and a higher proportion of AO type C fractures, which are more technically demanding. From a technical standpoint, PPSF avoids extensive paraspinal muscle dissection and laminar exposure. This simplifies the procedure and can reduce operative time [18, 19]. Still, limited technical proficiency and mixed case selection may offset this advantage. This is very much in line with the rationale of the present study, namely to focus on the more mature technical era of the past 15 years. Our findings are also consistent with the dedicated RCTs reported during the same period by Li, Jiang, and others, all of which suggest that once surgeons have moved beyond the learning curve of minimally invasive techniques, PPSF becomes more time-efficient than OPSF.

Intraoperative blood loss is a key indicator of surgical trauma. However, this meta-analysis evaluated visible intraoperative blood loss only. Hidden blood loss, which may occur after minimally invasive spine surgery through bleeding into paraspinal tissues or the fracture zone, could not be assessed because the original RCTs did not provide the data required for formula-based calculation. In the present study, the random-effects model showed that PPSF reduced intraoperative blood loss by 32.51 mL compared with OPSF, although heterogeneity again remained very high (I2 = 96%). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the studies by Jiang, Yang, and Zhang were the main contributors to heterogeneity. After these studies were removed, heterogeneity disappeared (I2 = 0%), and the blood-sparing advantage of PPSF remained significant (P = 0.04). Previous studies suggest that the lower blood loss associated with PPSF is mainly related to the smaller surgical exposure. It avoids extensive muscle dissection, thereby reducing vascular injury and bleeding from exposed bone surfaces [20, 21]. In addition, the wider use of intraoperative 3D navigation and robotic assistance over the past 15 years has substantially improved screw placement accuracy, reduced bleeding caused by repeated punctures, and further limited soft-tissue injury [22]. The observed heterogeneity may be related to fracture severity. In the study by Zhang et al., more than 60% of patients had AO type C fractures, which involve translational and rotational instability of the spine. In such cases, repeated puncture and adjustment of the screw trajectory are often required to achieve satisfactory reduction, and this inevitably increases bleeding along the puncture tract. Earlier meta-analyses concluded that PPSF and OPSF did not differ in intraoperative blood loss [23, 24]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in study periods. Earlier studies included the initial learning phase of PPSF, when techniques were less standardized. In contrast, the present analysis reflects a more mature stage of minimally invasive spine surgery.

In the analysis of postoperative hospital stay, the random-effects model showed that hospitalization was 2.09 d shorter in the experimental group than in the control group, with heterogeneity reaching I2 = 93%. Sensitivity analysis identified the studies by Yang and Zhang as the main sources of heterogeneity. After these studies were excluded, heterogeneity declined to 33%, and the fixed-effects model further confirmed that PPSF shortened hospital stay by 1.05 d. This variability may not be explained solely by surgical factors, and differences in postoperative care may also have contributed. For instance, in the study by Zhang et al., more than 40% of patients had comorbid conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, which slowed wound healing and functional recovery and, in turn, prolonged hospitalization. Another study by Yang et al. implemented enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) principles in spinal surgery, including preoperative optimization, multimodal analgesia, and early mobilization, all of which substantially shortened hospital stay. The shorter hospital stay associated with PPSF is consistent with its lower surgical trauma and reduced postoperative pain. Patients can mobilize earlier and are less likely to experience complications related to prolonged bed rest [25]. This provides a more favorable clinical setting for ERAS, and the broader adoption of ERAS in spine surgery over the past 15 years [26] may further enhance the advantage of PPSF.

Unlike the pronounced heterogeneity seen in perioperative indicators, both radiographic outcomes showed low to moderate heterogeneity. PPSF was associated with a statistically smaller postoperative local segmental Cobb angle, while no difference was observed in vertebral wedging angle; however, the magnitude of the Cobb angle difference was modest, and its clinical relevance remains uncertain. The postoperative Cobb angle is highly standardized in spine surgery. All included studies measured the angle at the final follow-up between the superior endplate of the vertebra above the fractured segment and the inferior endplate of the vertebra below it, and all enrolled patients had unstable fractures at T10–L5, which kept case selection fairly homogeneous. The advantage of PPSF in correcting Cobb angle probably stems from the fact that the minimally invasive approach preserves the posterior ligamentous complex more completely. This structure is crucial for maintaining posterior column stability and correcting kyphotic deformity, whereas OPSF requires wider paraspinal muscle dissection and may cause iatrogenic injury to the posterior ligamentous complex, thereby compromising the long-term maintenance of deformity correction [27]. By contrast, the lack of a difference in postoperative vertebral wedging angle is mainly related to the nature of posterior fixation itself. Vertebral wedging angle primarily reflects the extent of trabecular damage in the anterior column of the injured vertebra. Whether PPSF or OPSF is used, both are posterior fixation procedures. They restore vertebral height only indirectly through distraction generated by the screws and do not directly repair structural injury to the anterior column. Therefore, their ability to correct vertebral wedging is limited [28, 29].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only 6 studies were ultimately included, and complete data for postoperative vertebral wedging angle were available in only 4 studies. The overall statistical power is therefore limited, and the present conclusions still require confirmation in larger multicenter RCTs. Second, all three perioperative indicators showed very high heterogeneity. Although sensitivity analysis helped identify the principal sources, excluding those studies further reduced the sample size, so the findings should still be interpreted cautiously. Third, follow-up duration varied considerably across studies, ranging from 3 to 16 months, and long-term data beyond 2 years were unavailable. As a result, we were unable to compare PPSF and OPSF with respect to more distant endpoints such as recurrence of spinal deformity or chronic low back pain. Fourth, no subgroup analysis was performed according to AO fracture classification, and the present study therefore cannot determine whether PPSF performs differently across fractures with different degrees of instability. Additionally, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons across the five primary outcome analyses, resulting in a family-wise error rate of approximately 22.6% at an α level of 0.05. This increases the risk of type I errors (false positives). Therefore, statistically significant findings should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the included trials did not provide sufficiently consistent data on postoperative pain, neurological recovery, return to function, quality of life, reoperation, implant failure, pseudarthrosis, or other implant-related complications. Therefore, this meta-analysis cannot determine whether the modest difference in postoperative Cobb angle translates into clinically meaningful patient-centered benefit. The restriction to English-language full-text articles may also have introduced language bias.

Conclusion

Based on contemporary RCT evidence, PPSF may offer perioperative advantages over OPSF for selected unstable traumatic T10–L5 fractures treated with posterior pedicle screw fixation alone, particularly by reducing visible intraoperative blood loss and shortening postoperative hospital stay. PPSF was also associated with a modestly smaller postoperative local segmental Cobb angle, but this should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of clinically superior kyphosis correction. Given the considerable heterogeneity in perioperative outcomes and the lack of consistent data on hidden blood loss, neurological recovery, patient-centered outcomes, complications, and long-term maintenance of correction, these findings should be applied cautiously.
