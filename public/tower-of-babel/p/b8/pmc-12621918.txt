Single-port versus multiport robotic surgery in head and neck procedures: a systematic review and meta-analysis of surgical parameters
Authors: Turki Bin Mahfoz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSingle-port robotic platforms represent an emerging approach in head and neck surgery, yet their comparative effectiveness against traditional multiport systems remains unclear.MethodsA systematic review of PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov was conducted from inception to January 17, 2025. From 93 potentially relevant articles, 5 retrospective comparative studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 1,000 patients (single-port, 453; multiport, 547). Random-effects meta-analysis was performed for operative parameters.ResultsSingle-port surgery demonstrated shorter docking times (pooled effect size, –1.05; 95% confidence interval [CI], –1.47 to –0.64) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 74.27%). Console times showed no significant difference between approaches (effect size, –0.05; 95% CI, –0.35 to 0.25). Total operative times, reported in 2 studies, indicated modest reductions with the single-port approach (295 ± 92 minutes vs. 315 ± 101 minutes; 101.9 ± 23.6 minutes vs. 114.9 ± 32.8 minutes). Major complication rates were comparable (single-port, 1.8%–9.7%; multiport, 2.7%–4.3%). Negative margin rates favored single-port procedures (57%–100% vs. 48%–92.6%).ConclusionThis systematic review suggests that single-port robotic surgery offers advantages in docking time while maintaining comparable safety profiles to multiport approaches in head and neck procedures. However, the quality of evidence remains low due to the observational nature of available studies and heterogeneity in outcome reporting.Keywords: Laparoscopy, Minimally invasive surgical procedures, Operative time, Treatment outcome, Robotic surgical procedures

INTRODUCTION

The evolution of minimally invasive surgery in head and neck procedures has been characterized by progressive technological advancement, with robotic surgical systems representing the most significant innovation in contemporary surgical practice. Since the inaugural robotic thyroidectomy and subsequent development of transoral robotic surgery (TORS), these platforms have demonstrated substantial improvements in surgical precision, 3-dimensional visualization, and patient outcomes [1,2]. However, traditional multiport (MP) robotic systems, despite their revolutionary impact, present inherent limitations including multiple incision requirements, potential instrument collision in confined anatomical spaces, and suboptimal ergonomics characteristic of head and neck surgical environments [3].

The development of single-port (SP) robotic platforms represents a significant technological advancement specifically engineered to address these fundamental limitations [4,5,6]. This innovative approach employs a unified entry point for the deployment of all robotic instruments, theoretically offering superior advantages in surgical accessibility, enhanced cosmetic outcomes, and improved operative efficiency. The integration of flexible articulating cameras and instruments within SP systems constitutes a substantial engineering advancement over conventional MP architectures, potentially transforming surgical approaches in anatomically constrained regions.

Initial clinical experiences with SP robotic surgery in head and neck procedures have generated considerable interest within the surgical community. Preliminary investigations suggest potential benefits including enhanced maneuverability within confined anatomical spaces, elimination of instrument interference, and superior cosmetic results. Nevertheless, the clinical adoption of this technology necessitates rigorous evaluation of its comparative effectiveness, comprehensive safety profile, and economic implications relative to established MP methodologies [5,7,8].

Despite accumulating clinical experience, the evidence base comparing SP and MP robotic approaches in head and neck surgery remains fragmented and methodologically heterogeneous [3]. While numerous institutions have documented their preliminary experiences, systematic evaluations of comparative outcomes have been inadequately addressed [9]. Critical questions persist regarding operative efficiency metrics, oncological adequacy, perioperative morbidity, and learning curve dynamics. Furthermore, the substantial capital investment required for novel robotic platforms mandates robust evidence-based justification for the adoption and optimization of patient selection criteria. The economic implications of SP technology implementation are particularly relevant within contemporary healthcare resource constraints [10,11]. Although potential benefits in operative efficiency and reduced hospitalization duration may offset initial expenditures, comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses remain deficient. Additionally, the implications for surgical education, multidisciplinary team coordination, and institutional protocol modifications require systematic evaluation [12].

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively synthesize the current evidence comparing SP and MP robotic approaches in head and neck surgical procedures. Through rigorous evaluation of available data encompassing operative outcomes, safety profiles, and technical considerations, this investigation seeks to provide evidence-based guidance for surgical decision-making while identifying critical knowledge gaps requiring future research investigation.

METHODS

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and ClinicalTrials.gov. The search was conducted on January 17, 2025, using a combination of Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords related to SP robotic surgery, head and neck procedures, and study designs. The search encompassed terms related to surgical approach (“Single-port,” “single site,” “single-incision”), robotic procedures (“robot-assisted,” “robotic surgery,” “da Vinci”), and head and neck interventions (“thyroidectomy,” “neck dissection,” “transoral robotic surgery”). Additionally, grey literature sources were searched to minimize publication bias. The complete search strategy for each database is detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, observational studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses that compared SP robotic surgery with MP robotic techniques in adult patients (≥18 years) undergoing head and neck procedures. We excluded case reports, expert opinions, non–peer-reviewed studies, and single-arm studies. Only studies published in English or those with available translations were included. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Study selection

The author screened the titles and abstracts of the identified articles using the predetermined eligibility criteria. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were then assessed. The selection process was documented using the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow diagram [13,14,15].

Data extraction

The extracted information included study characteristics, including author, year, country, and design; patient demographics and clinical characteristics; surgical details and technical specifications; operative outcomes, including conversion rate, operative time, and blood loss; postoperative outcomes, including complications, length of stay, and functional outcomes; and quality indicators and follow-up information. To facilitate meaningful safety profile comparisons while preserving original data integrity, complications requiring surgical intervention, resulting in permanent sequelae, or causing mortality have been considered as major events across all studies.

Data synthesis and analysis

The synthesis focused on a detailed tabulation of study characteristics and outcomes, qualitative analysis of surgical techniques and their evolution, structured comparison of operative and postoperative outcomes, assessment of trends in complications and functional results, and critical evaluation of learning curves and technical considerations. The results were organized thematically to address the surgical outcomes, safety profiles, and technical feasibility. Where appropriate, forest plots were generated for visual representation of individual study results without pooling data to maintain transparency while avoiding potentially misleading combined estimates. The risk of bias in individual studies was assessed using a risk of bias assessment tool appropriate for the study design. The overall strength of the evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, considering study limitations, consistency of results across studies, directness of evidence, precision of estimates, and risk of bias [16]. This assessment provided a framework for interpreting the reliability and applicability of the findings while acknowledging the limitations of the available evidence.

RESULTS

Study selection and characteristics

A systematic search of databases on January 17, 2025, revealed 93 potentially relevant articles (34 from PubMed, 45 from Scopus, 7 from CENTRAL, and 7 from ClinicalTrials. gov). After eliminating duplicates and conducting initial screening, 23 articles were selected for full-text review (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 3). Eighteen of these were subsequently excluded for the following reasons: 8 single-arm studies lacking MP comparison, 2 non-head and neck procedures, 1 study comparing different MP systems, 3 non-comparative studies, and 4 studies comparing other surgical approaches. The final analysis incorporated 5 studies that satisfied all inclusion criteria. These studies directly compared SP and MP robotic techniques in head and neck surgery, specifically examining thyroidectomy, TORS, oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and hypopharyngeal cancer procedures.

Study demographics and baseline characteristics

Five studies included in the analysis were all retrospective and published between 2020 and 2024, as shown in Table 1 [9,17,18,19,20]. The research originated from various locations: 2 studies from the United States [18,20], and 3 from Korea or involving Korean collaborations [9,17,19]. Across all studies, the combined sample size was 1,000 participants, with 453 in SP groups and 547 in other robotic system groups (da Vinci Si system [Si], MP, da Vinci Xi system [Xi]).

Among the studies reporting demographic data, the mean patient age ranged from 42.9 ± 11.3 years in thyroid surgery patients [9] to 66.6 ± 6.8 years in patients undergoing surgery for laryngeal or hypopharyngeal cancer [19] (Table 2). Surgical parameters

The SP group consistently demonstrated shorter docking times across the studies reporting this metric (Table 3). Costantino et al. [17] reported the briefest docking times (SP, 5.7 ± 2.5 minutes vs. Xi, 10.0 ± 4.4 minutes), while Lee et al. [9] showed the largest difference (SP, 12.1 ± 4.0 minutes vs. MP, 18.3 ± 5.8 minutes). Sampieri et al. [19] reported intermediate values (SP, 6.5 ± 3.1 minutes vs. MP, 8.8 ± 4.7 minutes). Console time patterns varied among the studies, with Sampieri et al. [19] reporting the shortest durations (SP, 42.7 ± 13.7 minutes vs. MP, 53.9 ± 29.4 minutes), followed by Lee et al. [9] (SP, 45.5 ± 12.9 minutes vs. MP, 43.7 ± 12.4 minutes). Van Abel et al. [20] and Costantino et al. [17] reported longer console times (SP, 87.9 ± 29.0 minutes vs. Si, 85.6 ± 40.5 minutes and SP, 76.3 ± 30.7 vs. Xi, 88.1 ± 36.9 minutes, respectively). Total operative times were reported in 2 studies: Van Abel et al. [20] reported 295 ± 92 minutes for SP vs. 315 ± 101 minutes for Si, and Lee et al. [9] reported 101.9 ± 23.6 minutes for SP vs. 114.9 ± 32.8 minutes for MP.

Clinical outcomes

Intraoperative blood loss was reported only by Lee et al. [9], showing minimal differences between groups (SP, 20.8 ± 27.7 mL vs. MP, 24.8 ± 48.5 mL) (Table 4). Length of stay demonstrated considerable variation across studies, with Lee et al. [9] reporting 3.0 ± 0.5 days for SP vs. 3.4 ± 0.7 days for MP, Van Abel et al. [20] noting 2.1 ± 0.6 days for SP vs. 3.2 ± 1.4 days for Si, and Sampieri et al. [19] documenting longer stays (SP, 23.5 ± 11.2 days vs. MP, 21.6 ± 7.4 days).

Major complications were consistently low across all groups. Van Abel et al. [20] reported rates of 9.7% for SP and 4.3% for Si groups, while Sampieri et al. [19] documented identical rates of 3.6% in both arms. Lee et al. [9] noted rates of 1.8% vs. 3.1%, and Costantino et al. [17] reported 3.7% vs. 2.7% for SP and Xi groups, respectively. Notably, there were no conversions to open surgery reported in any study.

Margin status showed varying results across studies, with Van Abel et al. [20] achieving the highest negative margin rates (SP, 100% vs. Si, 92.6%). Costantino et al.[17] reported lower but still favorable rates for SP procedures (SP, 71% vs. Xi, 56%), while Sampieri et al. [19] demonstrated similar patterns (SP, 57% vs. MP, 48%).

Meta-analysis

Random effects modeling for console time yielded a pooled effect size of –1.05 (95% confidence interval [CI], –1.47 to –0.64) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 74.27%) (Fig. 2A). Docking time analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference (effect size, –0.05; 95% CI, –0.35 to 0.25) (Fig. 2B). Meta-analysis of total operative time from 2 studies revealed statistically significant reduction favoring SP approaches (mean difference, –13.26 minutes; 95% CI, –19.9 to –6.6; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1) with minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.680). The thyroidectomy study of Lee et al. [9] contributed 95.8% of the analysis weight, demonstrating significant operative time reduction (–12.96 minutes; 95% CI, –19.7 to –6.2), while the TORS study of Van Abel et al. [20] showed a nonsignificant trend toward reduced operative time (–20.0 minutes; 95% CI, –52.1 to 12.1). These findings indicate consistent treatment effects across different head and neck procedures.

Quality of evidence

The GRADE approach was used to evaluate evidence quality across 4 key outcomes (Table 5). Despite the observational nature of the included studies, the quality rating was maintained due to well-designed protocols, standardized reporting, and consistent findings across centers with adequate sample sizes. The quality of evidence for major complications was rated as low, primarily due to serious imprecision arising from low event rates, despite standardized reporting protocols. Both operative time and length of stay outcomes received low-quality ratings, reflecting inconsistencies and significant heterogeneity in reporting methods. The studies demonstrated a moderate risk of bias with consistent patterns across all publications (Table 6). Selection bias was minimal, supported by clear documentation of consecutive patient enrolment and well-defined inclusion criteria. While performance bias was consistently high due to the inherent limitations of surgical intervention studies, detection bias was assessed as low, attributed to standardized protocols for evaluating surgical results and complications.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 1000 patients evaluates the comparative effectiveness of SP vs. MP robotic surgery in head and neck procedures [9,17,18,19,20]. The findings demonstrate non-inferiority of SP platforms with respect to safety and efficacy, while revealing nuanced operational differences that merit careful interpretation.

The meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in docking time favoring SP systems (pooled effect size, –1.05; 95% CI, –1.47 to –0.64), consistent with streamlined setup requirements. This aligns with contemporary reports from Ninomiya et al. [21], who achieved median docking times of 3 minutes using single-site plus-2 ports methodology, and corroborates observations by Sarin et al. [22] regarding consistent technical demands across platforms. However, console time analysis demonstrated equipoise (effect size, –0.05; 95% CI, –0.35 to 0.25), suggesting that fundamental surgical execution remains equally challenging regardless of platform selection.

The observed moderate heterogeneity in operational efficiency (I2 = 74.27%) reflects institutional factors, surgeon experience, and learning curve effects. Rottoli et al. [23] documented substantial efficiency improvements with experience, while Hoshino et al. [24] established consistent technical proficiency requirements across platforms. These observations underscore the paramount importance of surgical expertise over technological platform selection.

Major complication rates demonstrated comparable safety profiles, with rates consistently below 10% across all studies. Complication patterns reflected procedure-specific risks rather than platform-dependent differences, with Jeong et al. [25] reporting hemorrhage as the predominant concern in TORS, while Soputro et al. [26] documented equivalent perioperative complications in robotic prostatectomy. Kim et al. [27] achieved complete resection without conversion in mediastinal procedures, supporting safety equivalence across diverse applications. Emerging evidence suggests procedure-specific advantages extending beyond technical metrics. Chavali et al. [28] documented reduced blood loss, shorter hospitalizations, and decreased opioid requirements in robotic prostatectomy, while Lee et al. [9] demonstrated superior outcomes in thyroid surgery. These findings indicate that SP advantages may manifest through enhanced recovery parameters rather than solely operative efficiency.

The assessment of oncologic adequacy represents a fundamental limitation in current comparative literature. Comprehensive evaluation encompasses surgical margins, lymph node yield, and long-term recurrence patterns—parameters inadequately addressed in existing comparisons. Only Sampieri et al. [19] provided lymph node retrieval data, demonstrating equivalent yields between platforms. The absence of recurrence data across all studies reflects both recent SP introduction and insufficient follow-up duration. This evidence gap requires prospective investigation with standardized protocols and extended surveillance to establish definitive oncologic equivalence. Several other constraints also warrant consideration. The exclusively retrospective nature introduces selection bias despite matching attempts. Temporal bias in historical control designs—where SP systems were introduced after MP proficiency—confounds interpretation through accumulated experience and evolving protocols. The observed trend toward superior margin rates in SP procedures requires cautious interpretation, given these temporal confounders and population differences.

Substantial procedural heterogeneity represents the most significant analytical limitation. The pooled analysis encompasses procedures from thyroidectomy to complex hypopharyngeal resections, which differ fundamentally in complexity, complication profiles, and technical demands. This heterogeneity challenges pooled estimate validity and limits applicability to specific procedures. Technical reporting inconsistencies further complicate interpretation, with variable operative time definitions and non-standardized complication classifications. Learning curve effects, while acknowledged, were not uniformly quantified across studies [29]. Current evidence suggests SP robotic systems achieve non-inferiority to MP approaches in head and neck surgery, with modest setup efficiency advantages but equivalent technical execution requirements. However, limited evidence necessitates prospective randomized trials with procedure-specific stratification, standardized learning curve documentation, and comprehensive long-term oncologic follow-up.

The similar safety profiles and oncologic outcomes suggest that surgeon experience, patient selection, and tumor characteristics remain more influential determinants of surgical success than platform selection. Future research should emphasize prospective comparative studies with contemporary controls, standardized outcome reporting [30], and adequate oncologic follow-up. Until such evidence emerges, platform selection should be guided by surgeon expertise, institutional resources, and procedure-specific considerations rather than presumed technological superiority.

This systematic review suggests that SP robotic surgery is a viable alternative to MP approaches for head and neck procedures, offering potential advantages in operative efficiency while maintaining similar safety and oncological outcomes. These findings advocate for the ongoing development of minimally invasive platforms, highlighting the significance of surgeon experience and appropriate patient selection over specific platform choice. The analysis of 5 retrospective studies demonstrated consistent patterns of reduced docking times, comparable safety profiles, and promising outcomes in SP procedures. Additionally, the trend toward shorter hospital stays and improved surgical margin status, though needing validation through prospective studies, indicates potential benefits that extend beyond mere technical feasibility. However, several important considerations arose from this analysis that warrant attention from the surgical community.
