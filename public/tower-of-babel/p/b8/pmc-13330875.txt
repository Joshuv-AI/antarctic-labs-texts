Efficacy of combined nutrition and exercise interventions on muscle health and performance in patients after a stroke: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jie Zhuang, Jinyan Wang, Yinhu Hu, Lei Song, Juan Jin, Zekai Hu, Jiawei Tong, Cong Fu, Lei Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStroke survivors experience profound skeletal muscle deterioration, typically losing 15%–20% of skeletal muscle mass in the affected limb within 4–6 months post-stroke. This deterioration accelerates functional decline, falls and secondary sarcopenia. While combined nutrition and exercise interventions have shown synergistic effects in other muscle-wasting conditions, the evidence for stroke remains fragmented. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the efficacy of combined nutrition and exercise interventions on muscle health and performance in patients after stroke.Methods and analysisWe will systematically search PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection and CNKI from inception without language or date restrictions. Randomised controlled trials evaluating combined structured exercise and nutritional interventions in adults (aged ≥18 years) with stroke will be included. Primary outcomes are muscle strength and muscle mass; secondary outcomes include functional performance, balance, daily living habits, neurological function and muscle composition. Two independent reviewers will conduct screening, data extraction and outcome-level risk-of-bias assessment using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomised trials. Hierarchical decision rules and robust variance estimation will be applied to address dependent effect sizes arising from multiple time points or concurrent outcome measures. Meta-analyses will be performed using random-effects models, with predefined criteria for switching to narrative synthesis under irreconcilable clinical or statistical heterogeneity. Pre-planned subgroup analyses will explore key moderators (eg, stroke chronicity, baseline nutritional status, exercise modality and supplement type). Certainty of evidence will be graded using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required as this review involves secondary analysis of published data. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations.Trial registration numberPROSPERO, CRD420261335163.

Introduction

Rationale

Stroke remains a devastating neurological condition with profound global impact, accounting for approximately 12.2 million new cases annually as the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide.1 Beyond primary neurological insults, survivors experience striking secondary skeletal muscle deterioration that fundamentally impairs health and performance. Longitudinal studies demonstrate that stroke survivors can lose 15%–20% of skeletal muscle mass in the affected limb within the first 4–6 months post-stroke, with atrophy progressing most rapidly during the acute and subacute phases.2 3 This loss is not confined to the paretic side; significant wasting also occurs contralaterally, suggesting systemic catabolic processes. Furthermore, stroke survivors exhibit 30%–40% lower knee extensor strength on the paretic side compared with age-matched controls, alongside a 20%–25% reduction in muscle cross-sectional area.4 These deficits carry severe functional consequences. Knee extensor strength accounts for 50%–70% of the variance in gait speed among stroke survivors,5 and 40%–70% of community-dwelling stroke survivors fall annually.6 Sarcopenia affects 30%–50% of stroke survivors, depending on diagnostic criteria and is independently associated with prolonged hospitalisation, increased dependency and higher mortality.7 8

The synergistic potential of combining exercise and nutrition to counteract this deterioration is grounded in robust physiological mechanisms. Exercise, particularly resistance training, stimulates muscle protein synthesis (MPS) by activating the mammalian target of rapamycin pathway and sensitising muscle to anabolic stimuli for 24–48 hours post-exercise.9 However, this response is substrate-dependent, as the exercise-induced increase in MPS cannot be fully realised without adequate amino acid availability, particularly leucine.10 This synergy is critically relevant post-stroke. First, stroke survivors frequently exhibit ‘anabolic resistance’, where the muscle’s synthetic response to protein is blunted due to reduced blood flow, inflammation and neural disruption, a barrier that exercise can help overcome.11 Second, patients who have had a stroke are at high risk of malnutrition (prevalence 16%–49%), with inadequate protein intake common due to dysphagia, reduced appetite and functional limitations.12 13 Finally, post-stroke inflammation, with elevated interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-α, promotes catabolism that nutritional interventions may counteract.14

Evidence from other wasting conditions strongly supports these combined approaches. In older adults with sarcopenia, a meta-analysis of 37 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (>2500 participants) demonstrated that combining exercise and protein supplementation, particularly doses exceeding 20 g consumed within 2 hours post-exercise, produced significantly greater improvements in muscle mass and strength than exercise alone.15 In patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, nutritional supplementation combined with exercise training yielded greater gains in fat-free mass than exercise alone.16 Similarly, a Cochrane review in patients with hip fractures found that protein and energy supplementation with physiotherapy reduced hospital stays by 5.6 days and improved functional recovery.17 Further, research in healthy older adults establishes clear dose–response relationships, showing that total daily protein intakes ≥1.6 g/day and targeted leucine intake (2–3 g per meal) are particularly important for optimal MPS stimulation.18 19

Despite this compelling rationale, evidence regarding stroke rehabilitation remains fragmented, with individual studies yielding variable findings. For instance, an RCT involving 20 patients with chronic stroke showed that 8 weeks of aerobic training combined with protein supplementation (20 g per session) led to favourable trends in body composition, cardiopulmonary fitness and functional performance.20 Conversely, a study of 62 patients with subacute stroke reported that protein supplementation enhanced handgrip strength and gait speed but failed to improve muscle mass, suggesting distinct outcome-specific effects.21 Such heterogeneity across existing stroke literature, particularly regarding stroke chronicity, supplement composition, exercise modality and outcome measures, currently precludes definitive clinical conclusions. Consequently, critical questions remain unresolved regarding the optimal timing of nutrient delivery, the moderating effects of baseline nutritional status and the translation of surrogate physiological outcomes to meaningful functional improvement.

Current clinical guidelines provide limited guidance; the 2020 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines note that ‘optimal protein intake and the role of nutritional supplements remain uncertain’,22 highlighting a critical evidence gap. A comprehensive synthesis is urgently needed to aggregate data from multiple trials, increase statistical power, resolve discrepancies, identify clinical effect modifiers and establish evidence-based rehabilitation protocols. By providing the first systematic synthesis of combined nutrition and exercise interventions for post-stroke muscle health and performance outcomes, this review will yield direct implications for clinical practice, guideline development and future research prioritisation.

Objectives

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the efficacy of combined nutrition and exercise interventions, compared with control conditions, on skeletal muscle outcomes in adults post-stroke.

Specific objectives are (1) to determine the efficacy of combined nutrition and exercise interventions on muscle strength in patients after stroke; (2) to evaluate the effects of these interventions on muscle mass and muscle composition; (3) to assess secondary outcomes, including functional performance, balance and mobility and neurological function and (4) to identify potential moderators of treatment effect, including stroke chronicity, baseline nutritional status, nutritional supplement type, exercise modality and intervention duration.

Methods and analysis

This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol was designed in accordance with the Cochrane Back Review Group Guidelines,23 24 the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol guidelines25 26 and the methodology outlined in a previously published protocol in this field.27 This study commenced on 1 April 2026 and is anticipated to be ready for publication by 1 April 2027.

Eligibility criteria

Population

Inclusion criteria

Adults aged 18 years and older with a clinical diagnosis of stroke (ischaemic or haemorrhagic) and at any stage post-stroke (acute, subacute or chronic). There will be no restrictions based on gender, ethnicity or setting (inpatient, outpatient or community-based).

Exclusion criteria

Participants with other progressive neurological conditions (eg, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis or motor neuron disease) will be excluded, as will those with significant concurrent musculoskeletal comorbidities that would confound or invalidate objective muscle assessments. Animal studies will also be excluded.

Intervention

Included studies must evaluate a combined intervention that incorporates both a structured exercise programme and a concurrent nutritional component.

Exercise component

Must involve a structured, explicitly prescribed and supervised or semisupervised exercise programme designed to improve muscle outcomes. Eligible modalities include resistance training, aerobic training, functional task training, balance training or any multi-component combination thereof.

Nutritional component

Must involve the administration of a specific nutritional supplement (eg, protein/amino acid formulae, multi-nutrient compounds, creatine, vitamin D or omega-3 fatty acids) or a structured dietary modification programme intended to specifically augment muscle health. The co-administration of an isolated single meal without an accompanying structured supplement or formal dietary modification programme is insufficient for inclusion.

Comparison

The comparator or control group must meet at least one of the following criteria: (1) usual care for patients after stroke or no active intervention; (2) exercise intervention alone delivered concurrently with a nutritional placebo or no nutritional supplement; (3) nutritional intervention alone or dietary modification delivered alongside sham exercise or no exercise programme4 and (4) alternative combined intervention comparing different supplement types or exercise protocols.

Studies

RCTs will exclusively be included, encompassing parallel-group, cluster-randomised and crossover designs. Conversely, quasi-randomised trials, observational cohort studies, case reports and other review types will be excluded.

Information sources

To ensure a comprehensive and unrestricted literature retrieval, a systematic search of the following bibliographic databases will be conducted from their inception: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection and the CNKI. The conduct and reporting of the search strategy will be guided by the PRISMA-S (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses literature search extension).28 To identify unpublished, grey literature or ongoing clinical trials, we will search clinical trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP). Furthermore, supplementary search methods will encompass both backward citation searching by manually screening the reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews and forward citation searching using tools such as Scopus or Web of Science. Where necessary, corresponding authors of primary studies and prominent experts in the field will be contacted via email to obtain missing data, study clarifications or unpublished datasets. No language or date restrictions will be applied to any phase of the search process.

Search strategy

The search strategy will be developed in collaboration with an information specialist (if available) and will combine terms related to the target population (eg, stroke, cerebrovascular accident and hemiplegia), the combined intervention (eg, exercise, resistance training, physical therapy, nutrition, dietary supplements and protein) and study design (RCTs). A representative draft search strategy for PubMed is provided in online supplemental file 1. Final search strings will be tailored to each database using appropriate controlled vocabulary (eg, Medical Subject Headings for PubMed and Emtree for Scopus) and relevant free-text keywords. All databases will be searched from their inception, and no language or date restrictions will be applied for any of the database searches.

Data management

The complete record for each eligible study, including citation and abstract, will be imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics). Study citations will be aggregated during the search process, and duplicate records will be systematically identified and removed prior to screening. Within EndNote, distinct folder hierarchies will be configured for each reviewer using the ‘group set’ feature, containing individual subfolders under ‘groups’ that correspond to each database. The full-text versions of all potentially eligible and final included studies will be retrieved and stored securely within the EndNote X9 library.

Selection process

The study selection process will be conducted in strict accordance with the Cochrane Back Review Group for systematic reviews of interventions to ensure methodological consistency and rigour. Following deduplication, two reviewers (JZ and ZH) will independently screen the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the predefined eligibility criteria. The full texts of potentially eligible articles will then be retrieved and independently assessed for inclusion by the same two reviewers. Any disagreements arising at any stage of the screening process will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (LF). The specific reasons for exclusion of full-text articles will be documented and reported in the final PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction process

Two independent reviewers (JZ and ZH) will extract data using a standardised, pre-piloted data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel. The form will be pilot-tested on a sample of three to five included studies to ensure completeness and consistency, with refinements executed as necessary prior to full data extraction. For each eligible study, the comprehensive information detailed in table 2 will be extracted. To minimise attrition bias and preserve the benefits of randomisation, intention-to-treat analysis results will be strictly prioritised over per-protocol data. Any disagreements between the two reviewers during the data extraction process will be resolved through discussion or, if consensus cannot be reached, by consulting a third reviewer (LF). In cases where data are missing, unclear or insufficiently reported, the corresponding authors of the primary studies will be contacted via email to request the necessary information. If no response is obtained after two contact attempts within a 3-week period, or if the authors are unable to provide the requested data, the outcome will be noted as missing in the risk-of-bias assessment, and the study will be analysed using the available data only. All extracted data will be stored and managed in a secure spreadsheet, and a completed extraction form for each included study will be maintained for transparency and audit purposes.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The risk of bias of the included RCTs will be evaluated independently by two reviewers (JZ and ZH) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2), in strict accordance with the guidance provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.29 Rather than assigning a single global rating to an entire study, assessments will be conducted at the individual-outcome level. Bias will be evaluated across the five core domains specified by RoB 2: (1) bias arising from the randomisation process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome and (5) bias in selection of the reported result.

Each domain will be evaluated using a structured signalling-question approach, culminating in a judgement of ‘low risk of bias’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk of bias’ for each domain. An overall risk-of-bias judgement will then be derived for each specific outcome based on these criteria. Any disagreements between the primary reviewers will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, via consultation with a third independent reviewer (LF). The final risk-of-bias assessment will be visually integrated using summary tables and standard graphical formats and will be formally incorporated into sensitivity analyses by systematically excluding studies judged to have an overall high risk of bias.

Data synthesis

If sufficient clinical and methodological homogeneity exists across the included studies, a quantitative synthesis will be performed using Review Manager (RevMan V.5.4) software. For continuous outcomes, the treatment effect will be calculated as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs when the same measurement scale is used across the trials. Conversely, when different instruments or modalities assess the same underlying construct (eg, different muscle mass assessment tools), standardised MDs (SMDs) with 95% CIs will be employed. For any dichotomous outcomes, effect sizes will be expressed as ORs or risk ratios with corresponding 95% CIs. When primary studies report both unadjusted and covariate-adjusted effect estimates, adjusted estimates, particularly those accounting for baseline outcome values or key prognostic factors, will be strictly prioritised to optimise statistical precision and minimise confounding bias. Given the anticipated clinical and methodological diversity regarding participant demographics, intervention protocols and outcome measures, a random-effects model will be pre-specified and applied for all meta-analyses.

To address statistical dependence between effect sizes from multiple time points or outcome measures, a standardised hierarchical decision framework will be used. For multiple measures of the same core construct (eg, muscle strength or mass), we will prioritise the original trial’s pre-specified primary endpoint; if none exists, we will select the most clinically relevant and methodologically rigorous measure (eg, knee extensor strength over handgrip strength, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry over bioelectrical impedance analysis). For equally valid measures with no clear hierarchy, robust variance estimation (RVE) with small-sample correction will be applied to pool effect sizes, accounting for within-study correlations to preserve statistical power and avoid Type I error inflation.

With respect to multiple follow-up time points, the primary meta-analysis will focus exclusively on the immediate post-intervention assessment, aligning with our pre-specified primary endpoint timing. To evaluate the sustainability and durability of treatment effects over time, we will conduct separate stratified meta-analyses for predefined follow-up intervals, specifically categorised as short-term (3 months), medium-term (6 months) and long-term (12 months) post-intervention. To handle the correlated effect sizes resulting from the same study cohort across these multiple longitudinal time points, RVE will be applied again. In scenarios where RVE is mathematically unfeasible due to an insufficient number of primary studies, a pre-specified decision rule will be employed to select a single effect size per construct per study, systematically prioritising the time point with the largest sample size and most standardised measurement modality. Finally, to confirm the stability of our primary pooled estimates, sensitivity analyses including alternative outcomes or time points will be conducted to assess the robustness of our findings.

For cluster RCTs, effect sizes and SEs will be adjusted to account for clustering effects using the design effect formula 1+ (m−1)×intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (m=average cluster size). For trials that omit reporting ICCs, typical estimates derived from published stroke rehabilitation literature (ranging from 0.05 to 0.15) will be used, or the corresponding authors will be contacted to obtain the raw dataset. Cluster RCTs for which clustering adjustments are mathematically unfeasible will be excluded from the primary meta-analysis and restricted to a narrative synthesis, with this methodological limitation acknowledged in the discussion. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses will be performed to compare estimates with and without these trials to evaluate their impact on overall effect estimates.

For crossover RCTs, data from the first intervention period will be strictly prioritised to eliminate potential carryover effects. We will extract paired effect sizes and SEs if appropriate within-subject, correlation-adjusted paired analyses are explicitly reported in the primary text. Crossover trials lacking both first-period data and paired analysis results will be excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis and relegated to the narrative synthesis, with their potential impact discussed. Additionally, we will critically evaluate the adequacy of the washout period, pre-specifying a minimum threshold of ≥4 weeks as sufficient for muscle-related outcomes; trials presenting an inadequate or undeclared washout period will be restricted strictly to first-period data or excluded entirely from the quantitative synthesis.

The eligibility criteria intentionally encompass a broad spectrum of stroke populations, including acute, subacute and chronic stroke, as well as a wide range of exercise modalities and nutritional interventions. While this approach maximises the comprehensiveness of the review, it may also generate substantial clinical heterogeneity across included studies. To address this anticipated challenge, a sequential multi-dimensional heterogeneity management framework will be implemented, initiating the process with a qualitative clinical heterogeneity assessment to systematically compare included studies across three core axes: intervention design, participant demographics and outcome measurement parameters. Variations across these domains will be visually mapped in a dedicated matrix to identify potential effect modifiers prior to statistical pooling.

Statistical heterogeneity will be quantified using the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50% and 75% defining low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively. For substantial heterogeneity (I2>50%), we will first conduct stratified meta-analyses by pre-specified clinical moderators (stroke phase, exercise modality and supplement type) to identify homogeneous subsets. If heterogeneity remains, sensitivity analyses will be performed to detect outlier studies driving the variation.

Meta-analysis will be deemed inappropriate and replaced with structured narrative synthesis when qualitative assessment confirms irreconcilable clinical heterogeneity (including fundamental differences in intervention intensity/type, non-standardised outcome measures that cannot be combined via SMDs or divergent core population eligibility criteria unresolvable by subgrouping), when extreme statistical heterogeneity (I2>75%) remains unexplained after subgroup and sensitivity analyses, when fewer than three eligible studies are available for a specific outcome or when severe missing outcome data prevents reliable pooling. Narrative synthesis will be structured by primary and secondary outcomes, further stratified by key clinical characteristics, and will describe the direction and magnitude of individual study effects while exploring potential explanations for divergent results.

To systematically explore potential sources of clinical or methodological heterogeneity and to assess whether treatment effects differ across distinct clinical characteristics, pre-planned subgroup analyses will be conducted if sufficient data are available. Subgroup stratification will be performed across the following six axes: (1) stroke phase (acute≤1 month, subacute 1–6 months and chronic>6 months); (2) baseline nutritional status (sarcopenic vs non-sarcopenic); (3) type of exercise (resistance training, aerobic training or comprehensive rehabilitation); (4) type of nutritional supplement (protein/amino acids, vitamins/minerals or multi-nutrient formulas); (5) intervention duration (≤8 weeks vs >8 weeks) and (6) type of control group (usual care/no intervention, exercise alone or nutrition alone). Furthermore, if an adequate number of primary studies are retrieved, adhering to the standard methodological threshold of ≥10 studies per continuous covariate, random-effects meta-regression will be performed. This will allow for the quantitative evaluation of continuous moderators, such as mean participant age, exact dosage of protein supplementation and cumulative hours of intervention, on the overall pooled effect sizes. It is acknowledged that this threshold may not be reached for most continuous covariates in the present review, and thus meta-regression analyses may be largely or entirely unfeasible in practice.

To evaluate the stability and overall robustness of our primary pooled estimates, comprehensive sensitivity analyses will be performed. The impact of specific clinical and methodological choices will be tested by sequentially excluding the following subsets of data: (1) studies with a high risk of bias; (2) studies that used different measurement tools for the same outcome; (3) studies where missing data were imputed; (4) cluster RCTs to evaluate the impact of clustering adjustment and (5) crossover RCTs that only provided full-period data without paired analysis results.

If a sufficient number of trials (≥10) are included within a specific meta-analysis, publication bias and small-study effect will be evaluated visually using funnel plots and quantified statistically using Egger’s linear regression test. However, it is acknowledged that this statistical threshold may not be reached for several outcomes in this field, given the relatively limited number of stroke-specific combined nutrition and exercise intervention trials currently available in the literature. If visual or statistical asymmetry is detected, possible causes will be explored, including publication bias, small-study effects or methodological heterogeneity.

For meta-analyses containing fewer than 10 studies, formal statistical testing for publication bias will be omitted to avoid underpowered and misleading interpretations. Instead, a qualitative assessment of publication bias risk will be performed by reviewing trial registry records to identify unpublished literature and compare the direction of effect sizes between small and large studies. The potential confounding impact of unassessed publication bias will be transparently addressed within the limitations section of the final manuscript.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The overall quality and certainty of the body of evidence for the main outcomes will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, in strict accordance with the methodology outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.30 Two reviewers (JZ and ZH) will independently perform the GRADE assessment, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (LF).

‘Summary of Findings’ tables using GRADEpro GDT software for all pre-specified primary outcomes (muscle strength, muscle mass and muscle composition) and key secondary outcomes (functional performance, balance and activities of daily living) will be generated. The certainty of the evidence for each outcome will be classified into one of four levels: high, moderate, low or very low.

As this review strictly includes RCTs, the evidence for each outcome will initially be rated as high certainty. However, the rating may be sequentially downgraded by one or two levels if serious or very serious limitations are identified across the five core GRADE domains: (1) risk of bias: methodological limitations identified via the RoB 2 tool; (2) inconsistency: unexplained statistical heterogeneity or highly divergent point estimates across trials; (3) indirectness: deviations or mismatches regarding the target population, combined interventions or specific outcome measures; (4) imprecision: wide CIs or small cumulative sample sizes that fall short of the optimal information size and (5) publication bias: strong evidence or high qualitative risk of missing, unpublished datasets.

Conversely, although rare in a body of RCT evidence, the certainty of evidence may be upgraded if specific criteria are met, including exceptionally large effect sizes, a clear dose–response gradient or scenarios where all plausible confounders would have mathematically diminished the observed effect.31

The potential for non-reporting bias will be systematically minimised through a comprehensive, multi-tiered search strategy that encompasses major clinical trial registries, specifically ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO ICTRP, alongside proactive communication with primary authors to obtain unpublished datasets or clarify incomplete outcome reporting. To rigorously evaluate the risk of selective outcome reporting, we will cross-reference and map the pre-specified outcomes declared in trial registries or published protocols against the final metrics presented in the peer-reviewed publications.

The findings from both our quantitative (for meta-analyses with ≥10 studies) and qualitative (for meta-analyses with <10 studies) publication bias assessments will be directly integrated into the final GRADE certainty matrix. The certainty of the evidence for any given outcome will be formally downgraded if robust evidence of non-reporting bias is identified. This operational threshold for downgrading includes statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry via Egger’s regression, the identification of multiple completed or discontinued registered trials that lack corresponding published results or a clear, systematic pattern of positive or statistically significant findings being exclusively selected for publication.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required for this systematic review and meta-analysis, as it will involve the synthesis and analysis of data from previously published studies. No primary data collection will be undertaken and there will be no direct contact with patients or participants. Therefore, formal ethical review and institutional review board approval are not applicable. The findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal, adhering to PRISMA guidelines and presentation at national and international conferences.
