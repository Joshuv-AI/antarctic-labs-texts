Occupational heat risk perceptions and behavioral adaptation strategies among construction and welding workers in Bangladesh
Authors: Ashiqur Rahman Tamim, Muhammad Mainuddin Patwary, Mondira Bardhan, Md Ismay Azam Badhon, Md Shahinur Rahman, Imran Chowdhury Sakib, Afif Iftikhar, Md Pervez Kabir, Md Najmus Sayadat Pitol, Chameli Saha, Matthew HEM Browning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing frequency and intensity of extreme heat events pose severe health risks to outdoor workers. Despite growing global recognition of occupational heat illness, evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains limited. This cross-sectional study surveyed 320 construction and welding workers to assess perceived heat-related health risk and behavioral adaptation in Bangladesh. Multinomial logistic regression examined factors associated with adaptive behaviors. Over 80% of workers perceived themselves as vulnerable, commonly reporting excessive sweating, thirst, cramps, irritability, and emotional instability. Construction workers were more likely to increase adaptive behaviors such as taking regular breaks (OR = 9.49, 95%CI: 2.45–36.74), wearing loose clothing (OR = 4.26, 95%CI: 1.14–15.90), and using electric fans (OR = 2.84, 95%CI: 1.12–7.22). However, they were also more likely to report a decrease in slowing their work pace (OR = 14.20, 95%CI: 2.03–99.21) and in planning work during cooler hours (OR = 34.81, 95%CI: 2.22–546.80). Long work experience was associated with increased use of electric fans as a cooling option (OR = 6.97, 95%CI: 1.97–24.68) and greater attention to weather forecasts (OR = 3.81, 95%CI: 1.01–14.37). Workers who experienced burns or memory decline adopted specific protective measures. Surprisingly, higher education was linked to lower participation in heat-safety training. These findings highlight the urgent need for occupational heat-safety policies, awareness campaigns, and targeted interventions to safeguard vulnerable outdoor workers in Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53118-x.Keywords: Occupational Heat Stress, Heat-Related Illness, Heatwave Adaptation, Climate Change, LMICsSubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Risk factors

Introduction

Climate change and rising global temperatures are causing increasingly frequent and devastating heatwaves1, with global heat-related mortality projected to rise by 0.5–2.5% under 1.5–3 °C warming scenarios2. By 2100, working-age populations in high-emission regions may experience a dramatic surge—up to 16 times higher exposure to extreme heat compared to recent decades3; this exposure may affect half of the world’s population by the same timeframe, even with stringent mitigation measures4. Furthermore, extreme heat threatens the economy, ecology, and human health4,5, with workplace productivity declining by 30% during heat stress and a 2.6% loss per °C above 24 °C5. These losses were exacerbated by reduced worktime policies6, heat-induced sick leave7, and workforce attrition8, collectively slowing economic development, altering migratory patterns, aggravating poverty, and widening social inequality9–11.

Heat vulnerability in individuals has been shaped by socioeconomic, demographic, health, and environmental factors12, with lower socioeconomic status, poverty, and limited education correlating strongly with heat-related mortality13,14. This risk is amplified in elderly populations, who experience higher hospitalization and mortality rates during extreme heat events15, and in workers with preexisting health conditions such as cardiovascular disease16, diabetes17, or mental health disorders18. These vulnerabilities are compounded by occupational settings; workers in strenuous outdoor roles (e.g., construction, welding) or poorly ventilated indoor environments face heightened risks due to prolonged heat exposure, inadequate cooling systems, and proximity to heat sources19,20. Construction workers endure extreme temperatures without access to drinking water, shade, or workplace policies addressing heat safety21,22, exacerbating heat-related illness risks such as cramps, fatigue, and stroke23. Such conditions impair mental and physical performance, increasing the likelihood of occupational accidents, fatalities, and chronic health outcomes like skin cancer and immunological dysfunction24.

Heat adaptive capacity (HAC) is a key factor in determining heat stress vulnerability. The outdoor workers with low HAC often suffer more with health issues and work efficiency due to heat exposure25. A study on Australian workers indicated that they have strong heat adaptability, largely due to their use of personal adaptive practices26. Chinese construction workers adopted some behavioral strategies like having cold water, changing work schedules, taking rest in shaded areas, putting on protective headwear, and stopping work when temperatures get very high19. Research has also suggested that a 40-minute break can enable 94% recovery from heat-related impacts27. A study with Bangladeshi workers revealed that higher water consumption, allowing air flow, and avoiding outdoor exposure were the main behavioral adaptations during hot weather28. Additionally, favorable clothing can help alleviate heat stress19,27.

Importantly, these two occupational groups also differ in their workplace environments and constraints, which may shape their adaptive capacity in distinct ways. Construction work is typically conducted in open outdoor settings with exposure to sunlight but some flexibility for rest breaks and airflow19, while welding tasks are often tied to fixed locations, equipment, and safety requirements that may limit movement and adaptive options29. These contrasts suggest that workers may adopt different coping strategies, such as reliance on rest breaks, schedule adjustments, or cooling practices, depending on the nature of their work environment and heat exposure30. Furthermore, variations in occupational safety practices, access to training, and workplace support across these sectors may further influence adaptive behaviors, making their comparison particularly relevant for identifying context-specific intervention needs19.

Bangladesh is highly vulnerable to climate change, with heatwaves emerging as a growing concern31. From 2003 to 2007, heatwaves were associated with an annual mortality rate of approximately 1,500 deaths32. Beyond health impacts, occupational heat stress carries profound economic implications, with the International Labor Organization (2019) projecting global productivity losses equivalent to 80 million full-time jobs and an estimated economic cost of USD 2.4 trillion33. While heat risk perception plays a role in shaping responses, evidence suggests that heightened awareness alone is insufficient to prevent adverse outcomes such as heat stroke in the absence of effective adaptive behaviors34. Importantly, adaptive practices are shaped by local climatic conditions, socioeconomic realities, and workplace settings, with factors such as age, income, access to cooling, and marital status influencing how individuals respond to heat stress35. Despite these realities, research on occupational heat exposure in Bangladesh remains limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on risk perceptions in specific industries—for instance, garment workers36 and petroleum workers37, without addressing heat-related risks or adaptive strategies. More recently, Shahrujjaman et al.28 examined adaptation among informal workers in Dhaka, but the findings were constrained by their focus on a single urban sector and potential recall biases from post-summer data collection. As such, there is still a lack of comprehensive, multi-sector evidence on how outdoor workers in Bangladesh perceive heat risks and adopt behavioral strategies to cope with them. To address this gap, the present study investigated the climate change awareness, occupational heat risk perception, heat-illness experiences, and self-regulated adaptive behaviors among construction and welding workers across multiple cities in Bangladesh. By identifying existing strategies and barriers, the study aimed to generate actionable insights for policymakers and the scientific community, ultimately supporting interventions to reduce health burdens and improve worker productivity.

Methods

Study area

The study was carried out in Bangladesh, a rapidly developing country in South Asia38. The country is characterized by a tropical monsoon climate with three distinct seasons: hot summer (March to early June), rainy season (June to early October), and dry winter (mid-October to late February). However, climatic conditions vary across the country. During the hot summer, average minimum temperatures range from 23.0 to 25.8 °C, with peaks reaching 31.3 to 35.3 °C 31. The current study was conducted on three divisional cities of Bangladesh (Dhaka, Khulna, Barishal). These cities were selected to represent diverse geographic regions and demographic characteristics, encompassing coastal and inland areas, commercial centers, and regions with significant migration and population growth. Notably, heatwaves are most prevalent between April and June, peaking in May and occasionally extending into the monsoon season until September31. Relative humidity follows a similar pattern, peaking at around 90% during the early stages of the monsoon season and gradually decreasing towards the end of the rainy season, following the peak in maximum temperature31.

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among construction and welding workers at 3 different cities (Dhaka, Khulna, & Barishal) in Bangladesh from May 2 to July 5, 2024. This study period was deliberately selected to coincide with the peak hot season in Bangladesh (Fig. 1), thereby capturing workers’ experiences under heightened thermal stress and make it easier for respondents to remember and connect to the problems and consequences of urban heat waves during this period. Recently, Bangladesh experienced unprecedented heatwaves during this timeframe, marking it as one of the most intense periods of heat in the country’s history. The record-breaking heatwave in April 2024 was the longest continuous heatwave since 1948, lasting 26 consecutive days39. ​Elevated temperatures continued into May and June, affecting large parts of the country, including the Dhaka, Khulna, and Barishal divisions.

Participants were selected using a purposive snowball sampling technique. This approach was necessitated by the absence of a centralized registry or employer database for construction and welding workers in Bangladesh, who are predominantly engaged in informal, temporary, or mobile work arrangements, making probability sampling impractical. Participant recruitment took place in two individual industries (welding and construction) with a focus on outdoor work environments from Dhaka, Khulna, and Barishal. The questionnaire was developed through the following sequential steps: (1) Systematic literature review: items were developed based on a careful review of validated and widely-used instruments in occupational heat stress and environmental health literature40–43. Item domains and response scales were aligned with conceptual frameworks from these precedents to ensure construct coverage, providing the foundation for content validity. (2) Expert review: the draft questionnaire was reviewed by all co-authors, including occupational health researchers, environmental scientists, and public health specialists with field experience in Bangladesh, to assess the completeness of content coverage, internal consistency of item groupings, and appropriateness of response formats. (3) Translation and back-translation: the questionnaire was translated into Bengali (the native language of all participants) by a bilingual member of the research team and independently back-translated into English by a second bilingual researcher to verify conceptual equivalence. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. This step addressed cultural and linguistic appropriateness. (4) Cognitive pretesting: the Bengali instrument was cognitively pretested with 10 workers (5 from each occupation) in a separate urban area. Workers were asked to respond to items and then explain, in their own words, what they understood each question to mean. This method identified items that were unclear, ambiguous, or culturally inappropriate, allowing targeted revision of wording, response options, and skip logic. No major structural changes were required, confirming overall face validity. (5) Enumerator training: six trained enumerators underwent standardized training including practice interviews, role-play exercises, and a training session on ethical conduct and standardized administration, further supporting consistent and reliable data collection. Data were collected digitally via Google Forms and subsequently reviewed by independent team members for completeness and consistency, reducing data entry error and interviewer-induced bias. Any discrepancies or missing values were resolved through follow-up with the enumerators. Notably, participants were made aware that their involvement in the study was entirely voluntary and that they might leave at any moment without facing any consequences.

The sample size for this study was determined using a single population proportion formula, appropriate for cross-sectional surveys assessing perception and preparedness levels in a defined population44. In the absence of prior evidence on occupational heat perception among outdoor workers in Bangladesh, a conservative prevalence of 50% was assumed to maximize the required sample size. A 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a 5.5% margin of error were applied in the calculation. This resulted in a minimum required sample of 318 participants. Given the very large size of the national working (employed) population (70.5 million)45, the finite population correction was negligible and did not alter the estimate. In total, 320 participants were recruited. The inclusion criteria to be a participant were construction and welding workers who must be 18 years or older.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire was created after a thorough evaluation of relevant studies on heat exposure and occupational health40–43. The final questionnaire consists of five parts and 37 questions about demographic and working information, climate change risk perceptions, occupational heat stress risks, perceived heat stress symptoms and adaptation strategies. The detailed questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

The first section of the questionnaire included screening questions such as the respondent’s current place of residence and age. The second section evaluated climate change risk perceptions, which consisted of six questions related to urban heat and climate change knowledge. The first and second questions assessed the respondents’ knowledge of climate change by asking, “Are you aware of climate change?” and “What are the signs of climate change?” The third question asked “Do you think climate change could affect the frequency or severity of heat-related stress in your workplace?” In addition, the fourth and fifth questions asked about the environmental and work-related factors that influenced heat exposure in the workplace. The last question evaluated their concern about the potential impacts of climate change on occupational heat stress risks. Respondents were asked to rate the severity on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all concerned) to 5 (extremely concerned).

The third section focused on workers’ perceptions of occupational heat risks. Participants were asked about their concerns regarding workplace heat exposure and the likelihood of developing heat-related illnesses, with responses ranging from 1 (not at all concerned) to 5 (extremely concerned). Workers were also asked whether they had personally experienced heat-related symptoms, including heavy sweating, excessive thirst, dry cough, muscle cramps, fatigue or weakness, headache, dizziness, cold or clammy skin, nausea or vomiting, feelings of excessive heat, and fainting. In addition, participants were asked to report self-perceived cognitive and emotional experiences associated with heat stress, including emotional irritability, difficulty controlling temper, low mood, memory decline, insomnia, trouble concentrating, lack of interest, and poor appetite, using single-item binary responses adapted from previously published literatures46,47. These items were not validated against established clinical psychiatric scales and should therefore be interpreted as indicators of self-perceived, heat-associated cognitive and emotional disturbances rather than clinically diagnosed conditions. Finally, participants were asked about workplace preparedness, including the availability of heat safety guidelines, training on heat safety, and whether they had received information or warnings from employers or health organizations regarding protection against extreme heat exposure. Heat-prevention training was captured using a single binary item asking whether participants had received any training on the prevention of heat-related injuries at their workplace (yes/no)19. No information was collected on training content, duration, delivery format, quality, or provider identity. This variable should therefore be interpreted only as self-reported exposure to any form of employer- or organization-led heat safety communication, not as a standardized or rigorously evaluated training intervention.

The fourth section of the questionnaire focused on the workers’ adaptation strategies to occupational heat exposure. Participants were asked about the measures they used to cope with heat stress. The items assessed the frequency of specific adaptive behaviors, including following weather forecasts, drinking cool water, wearing loose and light-colored clothing, taking regular breaks, and planning and carrying out heavy routine outdoor work in the early morning or evening hours or in shaded areas. Additionally, participants were asked about their involvement in training programs on working safety in the heat, slowing down work rates, use of personal protective equipment (PPE), and cooling systems like electric fans in the workplace. The variable “use of cooling options (e.g., electric fans)” was based on self-reported changes in fan use during hot weather as a behavioral adaptation strategy, without distinguishing between personal or employer-provided devices. As access to such cooling options likely varies across worksites, this measure should be interpreted as a general indicator of adaptive behavior rather than standardized availability. Responses were measured on a six-point (1 − 6) scale: never do it, decreases a lot, decreases, unchanged, increases, and increases a lot.

The final section asked about respondents’ demographic characteristics, such as gender, age, education, occupation (working sector), work experience and monthly income. Additionally, this section inquired about daily working hours, working environment, proximity to heat sources, time spent outdoors, and frequency of work performed near heat sources to understand their working conditions more comprehensively.

All questionnaire items included in the study, namely gender, age group, education level, working sector, years of work experience, daily working hours, monthly family income, outdoor time, workplace environment type, proximity to heat source, heat exposure frequency, and heat-prevention training, were operationalized as individual categorical or ordinal items. The ten heat adaptation behaviors were similarly analyzed as separate individual outcomes rather than aggregated into a composite scale. As no multi-item composite scale was constructed, internal consistency statistics such as Cronbach’s alpha and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin values were not applicable in this analytical context. Content validity was ensured through systematic review of prior validated instruments, and face validity was assessed through a pilot study conducted prior to full data collection.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and occupational factors. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations. Normality tests were performed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. For non-normally distributed continuous or ordinal variables, Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used where appropriate.

To identify the factors associated with workers’ adaptation strategies to occupational heat exposure, we estimated a series of multinomial logit (MNL) models. The MNL model is an extension of the binary logit framework that allows analysis when the dependent variable has more than two unordered and mutually exclusive outcomes48. In this study, the dependent variables were the extent to which respondents reported adopting behaviours that mitigated high workplace heat exposure. For each behavior, respondents initially selected from six options: never do it, decreases a lot, decreases, no change, increases, or increases a lot. Participants who selected never do it were excluded from the analysis. For interpretability, the two “decreases” categories were combined into a single decreases outcome, and the two “increases” categories were combined into a single increases outcome, resulting in three final options: decreases, no change, and increases42,43. Each adaptive behavior was modeled separately to assess the determinants of perceived changes in adaptive behaviors. Independent variables encompassed a wide range of sociodemographic and occupational characteristics, including gender, age group, education level, income category, years of work experience, daily working hours, outdoor work duration during hot weather, workplace environment (indoor/outdoor), the presence and frequency of working around heat sources. Additional covariates captured perceptions and concerns, including concern about heat exposure, concern about heat-related illness and injury, prior injury experience, perceived heat-related symptoms, having heat-prevention training and the availability of workplace guidelines or warnings. Given the extremely small number of female participants (n = 3; 0.99% of the total sample), these observations were excluded from the regression analyses to ensure model stability and avoid unreliable coefficient estimation. Consequently, all multinomial logistic regression models were conducted on male workers only (analytical sample: n = 317). Descriptive analyses, however, retained the full sample (n = 320) for completeness and transparency. The gender variable was not included in the regression models due to near-complete homogeneity in the analytical sample.

Given that the dependent variables consisted of three unordered categories, the MNL model was used to estimate the probability of workers selecting increase or decrease relative to the reference outcome (no change). Model estimation was performed using maximum likelihood methods implemented in the multinom function of the R package nnet. To address potential multicollinearity among independent variables, we examined pairwise correlation coefficients and visualized them using the R package corrplot. Variables with correlation coefficients greater than 0.7 were not simultaneously included in the models. The variables heat-prevention training, heat warnings, and heat guidelines were highly correlated (r > 0.7); thus, only heat-prevention training was retained for the final models. All other retained variable pairs fell below the threshold and were included simultaneously in the models. The coefficients from the MNL models represent the log odds of selecting a particular outcome relative to the reference category (no change). The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Separate ORs were estimated for ‘increase’ and ‘decrease’ relative to ‘no change’. An OR greater than 1 indicates a higher likelihood of reporting either increase or decrease in a behavior relative to no change, whereas an OR less than 1 indicates a lower likelihood, holding all other variables constant.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethical Clearance Committee of Khulna University, Bangladesh (KUECC − 2023/09/51). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were free to withdraw at any time. No personal identifiers were collected during the survey to ensure confidentiality.

Results

Characteristics of respondents

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and working characteristics of the study participants. The study included 320 workers, predominantly male (99.1%). A statistically significant difference was observed in daily working hours (p < 0.001), with 48.1% of all workers exceeding 8 h per day and welding workers (68.1%) working longer hours than construction workers (33.5%). Outdoor exposure during hot weather also differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001), with 87.5% of all workers spending more than 5 h outdoors, and construction workers (94.6%) reporting greater outdoor exposure than welding workers (77.8%). Workplace environment showed the significant difference between groups (p < 0.001), with 96.8% of construction workers working in predominantly outdoor settings (completely or mainly outdoors), compared to a more mixed indoor–outdoor distribution among welding workers. No statistically significant differences were observed between groups in gender, age, education, income, years of work experience, heat source exposure, or frequency of working around heat sources.

Climate change risk perceptions

Table 2 summarizes workers’ awareness and concerns regarding climate change. The majority (75.6%) of workers were aware of climate change, however, no significant difference (p = 0.615) was observed between construction (74.6%) and welding workers (77%). The distribution of perceived signs of climate change differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001). The most reported signs were increased temperature (71.3%) and irregular rainfall (67.2%), with welding workers more likely to note both (83.7% and 74.8%) compared to construction workers (62.2% and 61.6%). The majority of workers (92.5%) believed that climate change affects heat-related stress in the workplace, with no difference between groups (p = 0.707). However, environmental factors influencing heat exposure differed significantly (p = 0.003), with welding workers more likely to report hot air (77.0%) and high humidity (60.0%), while construction workers more frequently cited direct sunlight exposure (78.4%).

Work-related factors influencing heat exposure also differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001). Most workers identified physical workload (95.3%), working hours (91.3%), duration of rest breaks (71.9%), and access to cooling systems (54.1%) as key contributors to heat stress. Welding workers more frequently reported longer working hours (97.8% vs. 86.5%), but less access to rest breaks (69.6% vs. 73.5%) and cooling systems (42.2% vs. 62.7%). Levels of concern regarding the impact of climate change on workplace heat stress also differed significantly (p = 0.004). Overall, 74.7% of workers reported high or extreme concern, with welding workers more likely to be extremely concerned (39.3%) compared to construction workers (21.1%).

Workplace heat risk perceptions

Table 3 presents workers’ concerns and preparedness regarding heat exposure and heat-related injuries in the workplace. Most workers (80.6%) were concerned about heat exposure, with no significant difference between construction (78.4%) and welding (83.7%) workers (p = 0.234). However, concern about heat illness risk differed significantly between groups (p = 0.047), with welding workers (32.6%) more likely to report extreme concern compared to construction workers (22.2%). Regarding heat-related injuries, 72.5% of workers expressed concern, although differences between groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.066). The most commonly reported injury types included hitting objects, burns, and falls, trips, and slips. The distribution of reported injury types did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.111). Significant disparities were observed in workplace preparedness indicators. Construction workers were more likely to report the availability of workplace heat guidelines (42.7% vs. 20.7%, p < 0.001), receipt of heatwave warnings (42.7% vs. 16.3%, p < 0.001), and training on heat-related injury prevention (40.0% vs. 10.4%, p < 0.001) compared to welding workers.

Heat-related illness symptoms

Figure 2 & Table S1 summarizes the self-reported physical illness symptoms among the workers during hot weather. The most commonly reported symptoms included excessive sweating, frequent thirst, tiredness or weakness, and headaches. Significant differences were observed in heat-related physical illness symptoms (p < 0.05), with excessive sweating being more prevalent among welding workers (70.7%) than construction workers (51.3%). Frequent thirst was also higher among welding workers (57.8%) compared to construction workers (45.5%). Muscle cramps were reported more frequently by welding workers (27.0%) than by construction workers (14.3%). Nausea or vomiting was more common among construction workers (7.2%) than welding workers (2.6%). Fainting was significantly less frequent among welding workers (93.0% reported never experienced) than construction workers (70.9%, p < 0.001). No significant differences were found for tiredness or weakness, headache, or dizziness (Fig. 2 & Table S1).

Regarding the self-reported mental health symptoms, emotional irritability (63.7%) and difficulty controlling temper (66.9%) were the most commonly reported issues. However, no significant differences were observed in mental health symptoms between groups. Emotional irritability (74.1%) and difficulty controlling temper (80.7%) were more common among welding workers than construction workers (56.2% and 56.8%, respectively). Low mood was reported at similar rates (43.7% vs. 37.8%). Insomnia was more frequent among construction workers (53.0%) than welding workers (40.7%). Behavioral adaptations during hot weather

Most workers reported an increase in adaptive behaviors to mitigate heat exposure. The most commonly increased behaviors included drinking cool water before feeling thirsty (80%), taking regular breaks in shaded or cooler areas (69%), wearing loose and light-colored clothing (58%), slowing down the work rate to accommodate hot weather conditions (54%), and planning outdoor work during cooler times of the day (52%). However, a considerable proportion of workers indicated that they had not previously engaged in such adaptive behaviors, including participation in heat safety training programs (68%) and the use of PPE (60%) (Fig. 4). Significant differences between construction and welding workers were observed for taking regular breaks in shaded or cooler areas, planning outdoor work during cooler times of the day, participation in heat safety training programs, and use of personal protective equipment (all p < 0.05). However, no signifcant differences were observed regarding following weather forecasts, drinking water before feeling thirsty, wearing light-colored clothing, slowing down the work rate, or using electric fans as a cooling option (Fig. 4 & Table S2).

Factors influencing the changes in heat adaptation behavior in workplace

Demographic and occupational differences in adaptation behavior

Figure 5 & Table S4 summarizes the factors influencing the behavioral adaptation by workers with odds ratios (ORs) (Fig. 5) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (Table S4). Workers with a college or higher level of education were less likely to increase participation in heat-related safety training (OR = 0.22, CI = 0.05–0.93). Compared with welding workers, construction workers were more likely to adopt several adaptive behaviors, including taking regular breaks (OR = 9.49, CI = 2.45–36.74), wearing loose clothing (OR = 4.26, CI = 1.14–15.90), and using electric fans for cooling (OR = 2.84, CI = 1.12–7.22). However, they were also more likely to report a decrease in planning work during cooler periods (OR = 34.81, CI = 2.22–546.80) and in slowing down their work rate (OR = 14.20, CI = 2.03–99.21). Workers with greater work experience (9 years or more) were seven times more likely to increase the use of cooling options, such as electric fans, (OR = 6.97, CI = 1.97 − 24.68) and nearly four times more likely to follow weather forecasts regularly (OR = 3.81, CI = 1.01 − 14.37), although they were nearly seven times more likely to report a decrease in slowing their work rate (OR = 6.96, CI = 1.31 − 9.45) than those having lower work experience. Those working more than eight hours per day showed six times more increased tendency to drink cool water more frequently before feeling thirsty (OR = 6.41, CI = 1.45 − 28.35) and nearly ten times more willingness to follow weather forecasts to manage heat exposure (OR = 9.76, CI = 1.39 − 68.59) than their counterparts. In contrast, workers in mainly outdoor settings were less likely to take regular breaks during hot weather, while workers in indoor settings were less likely to wear loose clothing as an adaptive strategy.

Occupational risk perceptions and behavioral adaptation

Perceived heat risk significantly influenced adaptive behaviors. Workers concerned about heat exposure were less likely to increase wearing loose clothing (OR = 0.11, CI = 0.02–0.74). Those slightly concerned about heat illness risks were more likely to increase water intake (OR = 31.85, CI = 1.28–793.83) and use electric fans as cooling systems (OR = 7.14, CI = 1.05–48.47). Moderately concerned workers were less likely to slow down their work rate (OR = 0.20, CI = 0.04–0.93), whereas very concerned workers were more likely to follow weather forecasts (OR = 5.21, CI = 1.20–22.61) and slow down work, but less likely to wear loose clothing (OR = 0.06, CI = 0.01–0.34). Extremely concerned workers were more likely to slow down their work rate (OR = 6.73, CI = 1.12–40.49) (Fig. 5 & Table S4).

Physical injuries, heat−stress symptoms and behavioral adaptation

Workplace injuries and health symptoms were also associated with adaptive behaviors. Workers who experienced injuries from hitting objects were more likely to take regular breaks (OR = 3.49, CI = 1.04–11.77), while those injured by moving objects were more likely to use cooling systems (OR = 7.90, CI = 1.81–34.56). Workers who suffered burns were more likely to plan work during cooler periods (OR = 6.55, CI = 1.17–36.74). Participation in heat-related training was associated with increased likelihood of planning work during cooler hours (OR = 6.50, CI = 1.00–42.21).

Workers reporting excessive sweating and tiredness were more likely to increase water intake (OR = 6.35 and 3.19, respectively). Muscle cramps and fainting were associated with increased likelihood of wearing loose clothing (OR = 2.42 and 2.64, respectively), while muscle cramps were also associated with following weather forecasts (OR = 1.95, CI = 1.09–3.47). Workers reporting memory decline were more likely to use electric fans as cooling systems (OR = 11.72, CI = 2.04–67.38) and slow down work (OR = 4.28, CI = 1.01–18.14). Insomnia was associated with increased water intake (OR = 8.67, CI = 1.30–57.77), while reduced interest in activities was associated with planning work during cooler hours (OR = 9.90, CI = 1.60–61.29) (Fig. 5 & Table S4).

Discussion

Climate change risk perceptions

In our study of construction and welding workers in Bangladesh, we found that these heat−stressed workers showed reasonably high levels of awareness of climate change, consistent with previous studies reporting strong recognition of climate risks among outdoor workers in diverse settings21. Such awareness was likely reinforced by their occupational exposure to changing weather conditions. However, variations in perceived signs of climate change indicated the influence of job−specific environments. For instance, welding workers were more likely to report increased temperature and irregular rainfall, possibly due to combined exposure to ambient and process−generated heat, which may heighten their sensitivity to environmental change. This finding supported evidence that climate change perceptions often reflect lived occupational and environmental experiences49. Workers widely recognized the impact of climate change on heat−related stress, consistent with climatological trends of rising temperature, irregular rainfall, and extreme weather in the region50. Yet significant differences were observed in their identification of environmental heat factors. Welding workers emphasized hot air and humidity, while construction workers cited direct sunlight exposure, reflecting how task environments shape risk perceptions51. These variations are critical for policy considerations, as they point to differentiated vulnerabilities across occupational groups. Perceptions of work−related factors further revealed welding workers’ elevated risk: they more frequently reported longer working hours, fewer rest breaks, and limited access to cooling compared to construction workers. Such conditions explain their greater concern about workplace heat stress. Prior studies highlight rest regimes, shade, cooling systems, and hydration as essential for adaptation20.

Occupational risk perceptions

The majority of workers expressed concern about workplace heat exposure, aligning with prior studies20,52. While overall concern did not differ significantly between construction and welding workers, welding workers were more likely to be extremely concerned about heat illness. This may reflect their more continuous exposure to process−generated heat, which amplifies vulnerability perceptions, echoing findings from other heat−intensive occupations41,52. Workers’ concerns about heat−related injuries were consistent with patterns reported elsewhere21. Although group differences were not statistically significant, welding workers more frequently reported burns, reflecting dual risks of radiant heat and direct contact with hot surfaces, whereas construction workers mentioned falls, slips, and being struck by objects, often exacerbated by fatigue or dizziness. These task−specific differences parallel findings among mining and outdoor industrial workers, where exposure shaped diverse morbidity profiles21,41. An important disparity emerged in access to workplace heat guidelines, early warnings, and training, with construction workers significantly more likely to report such provisions. This suggests structural inequities in institutional support, echoing evidence that informal or small−enterprise workers often lack occupational health programs53. Prior research emphasizes that guidelines, training, and warning systems are critical for reducing heat stress and injuries51.

Perceived heat−related illness symptoms

Physical symptoms such as excessive sweating, frequent thirst, and muscle cramps were commonly reported, with welding workers experiencing higher rates than construction workers, aligning with prior studies reported in other similar working settings19,20,52. This may result from intense thermal exposure, heavy physical exertion, and the use of protective clothing, which reduces sweat evaporation, traps heat, and elevates body temperature even under moderate conditions52,54–56. Symptoms such as headaches and dizziness did not significantly differ between groups, indicating general heat exposure effects.

Psychological symptoms were also observed with no differences between welding workers and construction workers. However, welding workers reported higher irritability, potentially linked to both heat exposure and occupational manganese exposure, which has been associated with cognitive and mood disturbances57,58. Construction workers reported more insomnia, possibly related to irregular working hours or stress from outdoor work59,60. Welding workers experienced slightly more cases of poor appetite, likely due to dehydration, physical exhaustion, and prolonged exposure to high temperatures61.

Factors explaining differences in behavioral adaptation

Changes in hydration behavior

Workers engaged in longer working hours (> 8 h) were more likely to increase water intake, which likely reflects increased cumulative heat exposure and fluid loss during prolonged physical activity. Evidence suggests that fluid requirements rise substantially with work intensity and duration due to increased sweating rates, which can reach up to 2–3 L per hour in hot environments62. Workers who were slightly concerned about heat-related illness were substantially more likely to increase water intake, indicating that awareness of heat risk can strongly motivate preventive actions. This aligns with previous evidence suggesting that hydration behavior is not only physiologically driven but also influenced by behavioral awareness and workplace education63. Workers reporting excessive sweating, fatigue, or weakness were more likely to adopt anticipatory hydration. Studies from other occupational settings reported that exposure to extreme heat encourages self−regulatory behaviors, including proactive hydration, to prevent performance decline or illness23,64. Sweating is the primary thermoregulatory mechanism during strenuous outdoor work, but heavy fluid loss can impair cardiovascular and muscular function23,52. Early−onset fatigue, often a precursor to heat exhaustion, has been frequently observed among outdoor workers exposed to prolonged solar radiation and physically strenuous labor19,65. In contrast, muscle cramps were associated with a reduced likelihood of increased hydration. Evidence suggests that fluid intake immediately after dehydration without adequate electrolyte replacement can increase susceptibility to muscle cramps66. Workers with insomnia were also more inclined to hydrate, possibly due to dysregulation of thermoregulatory and hormonal systems induced by poor sleep67,68, which aligns with evidence linking sleep deprivation to higher perceived heat stress69.

Changes in working schedule

Construction workers were more likely to take regular breaks during hot weather compared to welding workers, likely due to the physically demanding nature of their tasks and awareness of heat−related risks70–72. Work break and rest regimens were commonly employed to prevent heat stress20–22, and globally, similar practices have been formalized, such as mandatory ten−minute breaks every four hours in Austin73. In Bangladesh, construction sites are typically exposed to the full intensity of the tropical sun74, making breaks an essential physiological coping mechanism, consistent with patterns observed in other tropical climates75. Despite this, construction workers were more likely to report a decrease in planning work during cooler hours and in slowing their work pace compared to welding workers in this study. Bangladesh’s informal economy is among the most extensive in South Asia, with over 87% of the workforce engaged without formal contracts, employer contributions to social protection, or enforceable occupational health and safety coverage76. Within this context, construction and welding workers often operate under piece-rate or daily-wage payment systems where income is directly tied to output volume or hours completed—meaning that taking additional breaks, slowing work pace, or leaving due to heat-related illness carries an immediate and severe financial penalty. This structural reality transforms what might appear to be individual behavioral choices into structurally constrained responses to competing survival pressures.

Fear of job loss may further compound this dynamic: in labor markets characterized by excess supply of unskilled and semi-skilled workers, the threat of replacement is a powerful disincentive against asserting the right to rest or safety compliance. Workers may also internalize productivity norms imposed by supervisors or labor contractors, particularly in output-driven construction settings with contractual deadlines77–79. Economic pressures, especially in settings with informal labor arrangements and piece−rate payments, further discourage adjustments to work schedules, as reduced pace or breaks directly affect wages80,81. Similar patterns have been reported in mining industries and other low−income country contexts, highlighting how economic vulnerability can override heat safety considerations22,82.

Prior experience of workplace injuries were associated with changes in scheduling behaviors, such as workers who had burns or injuries were more likely to take breaks or plan work during cooler hours. Similar observations were reported among construction workers in China19. Burns, particularly common among welding workers due to intense radiant heat and sparks, are a severe and often traumatic form of injury83,84. Increased heat stress results in reduced job productivity and leads to occupational illnesses and injuries if enough breaks have not been maintained during work activities85. A direct and painful experience with severe heat can foster a deep concern for thermal dangers (e.g., burn), prompting workers to implement further preventive measures86, such as smart work scheduling.

Workers who reported extreme concern about heat illness were more likely to slow their work pace, indicating that high perceived vulnerability serves as a strong motivator for adaptive behavior, consistent with previous occupational health studies87. Similarly, workers who received heat−related training were more likely to adjust their schedules, as training enhances understanding of heat risks, symptom recognition, and the rationale for adaptive practices such as shifting work hours19,88–90. It should be noted, however, that heat-prevention training was operationalized as a single binary item capturing self-reported exposure to any form of heat safety communication, with no information collected on training content, duration, delivery format, or quality. The observed association should therefore be interpreted as a preliminary signal that any form of heat safety communication may influence behavioral adaptation, rather than as evidence for a specific training program’s efficacy. Future research should adopt more granular measures of training quality and content to better characterize dose-response relationships between heat safety training and adaptive behavioral outcomes. In contrast, workers with greater work experience (≥ 9 years) were more likely to report a decrease in slowing their work pace, possibly due to physiological acclimatization, psychological desensitization25, or a strong sense of duty and resilience compelling them to maintain productivity91. Likewise, workers with only moderate concern about heat−related illness tended not to slow their work, aligning with the Health Belief Model92, as they may rely on other preventive strategies, such as hydration or rest breaks, while perceiving they can manage risk without compromising output.

Psychological factors were also related to workplace heat adaptation, with workers reporting reduced motivation or memory decline being more likely to adjust work schedules and slow their work pace. Heat stress is well−documented to impair attention, concentration, and short−term memory, and workers experiencing such cognitive effects may consciously or subconsciously slow their work to prevent errors, accidents, or performance decline93–95. Evidence indicated that elevated temperatures exacerbate pre−existing mental health challenges, increasing irritability, fatigue, and impairing coping capacity96,97. Consequently, these workers proactively modify their schedules to cooler periods as a form of self−preservation. These findings underscore the importance of recognizing cognitive impairment as an early indicator of heat stress and integrating mental health considerations into occupational safety strategies, particularly in heat−exposed settings like Bangladesh, to support both physical and psychological well−being98.

Changes in personal comfort adaptation

Construction workers commonly adopt adaptive clothing and cooling strategies to cope with extreme heat, with loose−fitting garments being the most prevalent. Loose clothing facilitates heat dissipation, reduces thermal discomfort, and lowers the risk of heat−related illness, a practice widely observed among physically demanding outdoor workers in tropical and subtropical climates99,100. In contrast, workers primarily indoors exhibited little adjustment in clothing, which may reflect a tendency among indoor employees to neglect the possibility of overheating, even when ventilation or climate control is inadequate101,102, despite evidence linking elevated indoor temperatures to reduced cognitive and physical performance, fatigue, and heat morbidity103.

Personal cooling methods, particularly electric fans, were frequently reported among construction workers exposed to high solar radiation. Fans improve thermal comfort and mitigate heat stress when used alongside hydration and breaks, reflecting growing awareness of occupational heat risks104,105. Adoption of these practices correlated with work experience; employees with over five years of experience reported actively following adaptive measures, while those exceeding nine years often treated them as routine. Similarly, earlier studies found that workers with more years of experience demonstrated more consistent use of cooling strategies, including electric fans and rest breaks106.

Perceived vulnerability was also related to personal adaptive behavior. Workers expressing even slight concern about heat−related illness were more likely to adopt cooling strategies, including shade seeking and adjusting work pace, supporting evidence that moderate risk perception promotes preventive actions25,107. Similarly, workers with a history of occupational injuries demonstrated higher engagement in heat mitigation, likely due to heightened risk awareness and sensitivity to environmental hazards108,109.

Psychological factors were further related to personal thermal adaptation. Outdoor workers reporting memory decline or reduced attention were more likely to implement cooling measures, reflecting a behavioral response to the cognitive strain imposed by heat stress. Elevated temperatures were known to impair attention, working memory, and executive function, and workers perceiving these effects appear to proactively adopt strategies to mitigate physical and cognitive risks93,96,110.

Environmental awareness and preparedness

Experienced construction workers demonstrated greater attentiveness to weather forecasts than younger, less experienced colleagues. Previous research supports this pattern, showing that age and work experience positively influence safety attitudes and behaviors, whereas early−career workers tend to be more risk−taking and less cautious regarding occupational hazards111–113. Similarly, those working more than nine hours daily were more likely to monitor weather conditions, consistent with studies indicating that prolonged outdoor exposure fosters recognition of heat risks and motivates preventive behaviors41. Workers who experienced heat−related illnesses (e.g., muscle cramps) were also more inclined to follow weather forecasts. Such experiences reinforce the importance of proactive measures, including adjusting work hours, hydrating adequately, and seeking shade during peak heat19,72,114. Interestingly, workers with college or higher education were less likely to participate in heat−related training, diverging from prior studies showing greater engagement among educated employees41,115. This finding may reflect differences in the perceived relevance or usefulness of heat safety training among workers with higher educational attainment. It is possible that more educated workers rely on prior knowledge or informal sources of information, which could reduce their engagement in structured training programs. Recent studies have shown that higher education links to greater awareness of health risks and more active adoption of preventive behaviors, suggesting that educated workers may self-regulate heat exposure independently, without requiring structured training116,117. Locally, this may reflect prioritization of career−focused training or financial incentives over occupational health programs, suggesting that perceived practical value strongly influences participation in preventive initiatives.

Policy and practice implications

Our study suggests that effective heat adaptation among Bangladeshi outdoor workers requires behavioral, educational, and structural interventions. Policies should ensure regular work breaks, shaded rest areas, and access to cooling tools such as electric fans, particularly at high−exposure outdoor sites. Targeted training is needed for younger and less experienced workers to improve recognition of heat stress, adoption of safe work practices, and practical adaptation strategies. Workplace interventions should consider informal labor arrangements and economic pressures that prevent workers from slowing their pace or attending safety training. Integrating heat awareness into routine occupational health programs, with incentives, may improve engagement among educated workers who prioritize career−oriented training. Heat stress prevention must be included in workplace risk assessments and occupational health systems, with direct worker input. Guidelines should address both outdoor (construction) and indoor/high−heat (welding) environments, reflecting sector−specific challenges. Low participation in heat safety training highlights the need for accessible and practical programs. Policies should empower workers to self−pace and take breaks without fear of penalty. Monitoring systems tracking environmental conditions and worker health, combined with proactive scheduling based on weather forecasts, can reduce risks. Multi−sector collaboration among government, employers, labor organizations, and public health authorities is essential for the effective implementation and evaluation of interventions.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The use of purposive snowball sampling may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, as participants were not randomly selected and may over-represent specific social networks; this approach was necessary due to the lack of a formal sampling frame for informal workers. The sample included only male workers, reflecting sectoral norms but limiting gender perspectives. Heat-related symptoms were self-reported and not objectively verified, and environmental measurements such as temperature, wind speed, and radiant heat were not collected, restricting dose–response assessment and comparability with instrument-based studies. Although proxies such as workplace setting, perceived airflow, and fan use were included, the absence of standardized indices such as Wet Bulb Globe Temperature limits precise characterization of heat exposure. Physiological heat acclimatization was not assessed and may act as an unmeasured confounder, as more experienced workers could exhibit lower symptom perception due to long-term exposure rather than behavioral adaptation alone. As no composite scale was developed, internal consistency and sampling adequacy could not be evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha or the KMO statistic. Key structural factors such as employment formality, payment systems, and social protection were not measured, limiting deeper political economy interpretation. Additionally, some regression estimates showed wide confidence intervals, likely reflecting sparse observations within certain outcome categories. Finally, the study focused on two occupational groups in selected urban areas, which may not represent all outdoor workers across diverse climatic regions of Bangladesh. Future research should incorporate objective environmental monitoring, physiological acclimatization measures, validated composite scales, and broader structural variables to better disentangle behavioral, physiological, and economic determinants of heat adaptation.

Conclusions

This study showed that Bangladeshi construction and welding workers were generally aware of climate change and its impact on heat stress at work, although their adaptive capacity varied. Most workers recognized factors like high temperature, humidity, and heavy workload as contributors to heat exposure. However, access to protective measures such as shade, cooling devices, and heat−related training was limited, especially among welding workers. Workers’ adaptive behaviors, such as drinking more cool water, taking breaks, using fans, wearing loose clothing, or adjusting work schedules, were influenced by a combination of heat-related symptoms, injuries, and risk perception. More experienced workers and those working longer hours tended to take more preventive actions, while workers in indoor settings adapted less. Furthermore, changes in working schedule and personal comfort were also used to cope with rising heat events. These outcomes highlight the urgent need for targeted policy interventions, occupational heat standards, and context−specific awareness programs to address urban heat exposure among outdoor workers in Bangladesh. The study also emphasizes the importance of integrating heat risk management into labor and urban planning policies, particularly for outdoor workers who are often excluded from mainstream protection frameworks.
