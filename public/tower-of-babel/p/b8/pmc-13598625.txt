Occlusive versus non-occlusive CPAP interfaces in neonatal respiratory care: a comparative review of efficacy and safety
Authors: Aleksandra Hibner, Samantha Hietalati, Sandra Lawrynowicz Leibel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContinuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is first-line therapy for respiratory distress in preterm infants, but optimal interface selection remains controversial. Occlusive interfaces (nasal prongs and masks with tight seals) provide accurate pressure delivery but can cause significant nasal trauma, while non-occlusive interfaces (loosely fitting nasal cannulae) reduce trauma but may compromise therapeutic efficacy.ObjectiveTo synthesise current evidence comparing occlusive with non-occlusive CPAP interfaces regarding pressure delivery accuracy, clinical efficacy, safety outcomes and practical implementation in neonatal intensive care.MethodsComprehensive review of randomised controlled trials, systematic reviews, bench studies and observational studies comparing CPAP interface types in preterm neonates. Evidence quality assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation criteria where applicable.ResultsOcclusive interfaces deliver pressure within 0.5–1.0 cmH2O of set values and demonstrate superior efficacy in landmark trials (COIN, SUPPORT), particularly in extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks’ gestation). However, nasal trauma rates reach 20–60%. Non-occlusive systems reduce trauma dramatically (0–5.4% injury rates) but consistently underdeliver pressure. Non-occlusive systems have failed to meet non-inferiority criteria in recent trials, with CPAP failure rates of 19.7% versus 17.3% for occlusive systems. This increased to failure rates >50% in extremely preterm infants.ConclusionsCurrent evidence supports occlusive CPAP as first-line therapy for moderate-to-severe respiratory distress in preterm neonates, particularly those of <32 weeks’ gestation. Non-occlusive interfaces may be appropriate for stable infants, weaning support or apnoea of prematurity management. Adequately powered randomised trials stratified by gestational age and respiratory severity are urgently needed to define optimal interface selection strategies.

Introduction

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the established first-line therapy for respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) [1–3] and apnoea of prematurity (AOP) [4] in preterm infants, with efficacy established in landmark trials [5, 6]. CPAP maintains functional residual capacity (FRC), prevents atelectasis and reduces work of breathing by applying continuous distending pressure throughout the respiratory cycle [7–9]. Modern surfactant administration via less invasive methods permits concurrent CPAP use. This has further reduced the need for mechanical ventilation [10–13].

The effectiveness of CPAP therapy depends critically on consistent delivery of intended distending pressures. This has led to two distinct interface design philosophies: 1) occlusive systems that create relatively airtight seals to minimise pressure loss (Hudson nasal prongs, Medline; bi-nasal prongs and nasal masks, Fisher & Paykel, CareFusion); and 2) non-occlusive systems that employ loosely fitting interfaces accepting controlled air leakage (respiratory assist multifunctional (RAM) cannulae, Neotech; Nioflo devices, Flexicare) [14–17]. These design differences fundamentally affect pressure transmission accuracy, clinical efficacy, complication profiles and practical implementation (table 1).

Occlusive interfaces transmit pharyngeal pressures within 0.5–1.0 cmH2O of set values [20, 21]. However, the seal required for effective pressure transmission creates pressure points on delicate nasal tissues, with reported injury rates of 20–60% [26]. Non-occlusive systems emerged to address these complications, with the RAM cannula reducing nasal injury rates to 5–14% [27, 28]. However, bench studies demonstrate that non-occlusive interfaces underdeliver pressure by 2–3 cmH2O, with delivered pressures dropping below 2 cmH2O with mouth opening [20, 21, 32]. This review examines the comparative benefits and limitations of occlusive versus non-occlusive CPAP delivery systems in neonatal intensive care, synthesising current evidence to guide clinical decision-making regarding interface selection for different patient populations and clinical scenarios. We searched PubMed, MEDLINE, the Cochrane Library and EMBASE from inception through January 2026 using search terms including: “continuous positive airway pressure”, “CPAP”, “nasal prongs”, “RAM cannula”, “non-invasive ventilation”, “preterm infant”, “neonate” and “respiratory distress syndrome”. We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs), systematic reviews, meta-analyses, bench studies and observational studies comparing CPAP interface types in neonatal populations. Reference lists of included articles were reviewed to identify additional relevant studies.

CPAP mechanisms and interface classification

Modern CPAP systems generate continuous positive pressure through either flow-dependent or flow-independent mechanisms, each with distinct characteristics that influence clinical application. Flow-dependent systems, exemplified by bubble CPAP, create positive pressure by directing gas flow through water; the depth of submersion determines the CPAP level through hydrostatic principles [22, 33]. Flow-independent mechanisms employ demand valves or other mechanisms to maintain consistent pressure regardless of breathing patterns [14, 34]. Interface design critically determines CPAP effectiveness.

CPAP addresses the unique physiological challenges of preterm infants through multiple mechanisms. In the upper airway, preterm infants exhibit increased epiglottal flexibility and decreased tissue support, predisposing to obstruction. The compliant chest wall and inward rib cage motion during respiration contribute to airway collapse, alveolar atelectasis, reduced FRC and ventilation–perfusion mismatch. CPAP counteracts these deficits by applying continuous distending pressure throughout the respiratory cycle, maintaining positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) that stabilises both upper and lower airways [8, 35]. The sustained positive pressure provides structural support to the pharynx, prevents complete alveolar collapse, maintains FRC, reduces airway resistance and stabilises the chest wall. These mechanisms collectively improve oxygenation and reduce work of breathing [7–9, 36, 37].

Beyond preterm infants, CPAP is increasingly used to support late preterm (34–36 weeks) and term infants with various respiratory conditions. Transient tachypnoea of the newborn (TTN), caused by delayed clearance of fetal lung fluid, is the most common indication for respiratory support in term and late preterm infants, affecting 0.5–4% of this population [38, 39]. TTN typically manifests within the first 2 h of life with tachypnoea, mild retractions and oxygen requirement. Most cases resolve within 24–72 h. CPAP facilitates lung fluid reabsorption by preventing alveolar collapse and reducing respiratory effort [40, 41]. Meconium aspiration syndrome (MAS) represents another major indication for CPAP in term infants, occurring in 2–9% of meconium-stained deliveries [42]. While approximately one third of infants with MAS require mechanical ventilation, 10–20% can be successfully managed with CPAP alone, provided that air trapping is not severe [34, 43]. CPAP addresses the pathophysiology of MAS by maintaining alveolar recruitment in areas of patchy atelectasis and counteracting surfactant dysfunction. Tolerance may be limited in more mature infants. Early neonatal pneumonia in term infants similarly benefits from CPAP as a bridge to more intensive support when needed.

Occlusive interfaces create near-complete seals within the nares, directing gas flow through the patient's respiratory system. These include bi-nasal short prongs (Hudson-style), nasal masks with cushioned seals and bi-nasal prongs with integrated stabilisation [14, 29, 44, 45]. The occlusive nature ensures set CPAP pressure correlates closely with delivered pressure, typically within 0.5–1.0 cmH2O of target values [20, 46].

Non-occlusive interfaces, exemplified by the RAM cannula, utilise thin, flexible prongs occupying only 60–80% of the nares diameter, permitting gas escape around the prongs [21, 27]. This design accepts pressure loss in exchange for reduced tissue pressure. When gas escapes around loosely fitted prongs, effective airway pressure may fall 2–3 cmH2O below set values [20, 21].

Evidence base for clinical efficacy

The evidence for occlusive CPAP derives from landmark trials establishing nasal CPAP as standard of care (table 2). The COIN trial [5] randomised 610 infants of 25–28 weeks’ gestation to nasal CPAP versus intubation, demonstrating reduced mechanical ventilation duration. The SUPPORT trial showed that early CPAP in 1316 extremely preterm infants (24–27 weeks’ gestation) reduced intubation rates [6]. Both used occlusive bi-nasal prongs. A Cochrane meta-analysis by Subramaniam et al. [3] further confirmed that prophylactic or very early CPAP reduces the risk of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), with the greatest benefit in extremely low birth weight infants. Additional RCTs and systematic reviews have reaffirmed that early occlusive CPAP promotes lung recruitment, reduces oxygen and ventilator exposure, and shortens duration of respiratory support [49, 50]. Despite these substantial benefits, nasal trauma remains a significant limitation of occlusive CPAP. The pathophysiology involves direct pressure injury, shear forces from interface movement, moisture-related maceration and ischaemia from impaired tissue perfusion. Preterm skin, particularly in the first week of therapy, is highly susceptible to pressure-related damage; risk correlates with interface size, contact pressure, duration of use and frequency of repositioning [18].

Effective prevention requires expert fitting and maintenance protocols that balance an adequate seal with tissue protection [51]. Strategies include careful interface selection based on patient anatomy, scheduled rotation between prongs and mask interfaces, use of hydrocolloid or silicone barriers, and prompt identification of early skin compromise [19, 29, 52]. The need for skilled nursing care and continuous monitoring may challenge feasibility in resource-limited settings. Additionally, infant discomfort, parental distress and interference with feeding or oral care (especially when mouth closure or chin straps are required) represent practical limitations of occlusive systems [53, 54].

The reduction in nasal trauma remains the most compelling advantage of non-occlusive systems. The loose-fit of devices such as the RAM cannula minimises pressure points and shear forces that cause tissue breakdown with occlusive systems. Several recent RCTs have confirmed substantial reductions in nasal injury rates. Maram et al. [27] reported nasal injury rates in 5.4% of infants using a RAM cannula plus Cannulaide compared with 26.4% with Hudson prongs (p<0.001) in 229 neonates. Samim et al. [28] found moderate-to-severe nasal trauma in 2.4% of infants supported by the RAM cannula versus 8.7% with short bi-nasal prongs. Al-Lawama et al. [47], in the ProRAM trial, reported no nasal injuries in the RAM cannula group versus 20% with bi-nasal prongs. The simpler application of non-occlusive interfaces reduces setup time and technical expertise requirements. This may improve workflow efficiency in busy neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) [27]. Clinicians frequently report easier positioning, fewer readjustments and decreased need for skin checks compared with occlusive systems. In addition, reduced visible trauma may alleviate parental distress and improve acceptance of CPAP therapy. This may strengthen family-caregiver engagement during neonatal intensive care [29].

The major limitation of non-occlusive CPAP lies in the inconsistent pressure delivery. Bench and clinical studies consistently demonstrate that these systems underdeliver pressure relative to set values. Singh et al. [20] measured direct pharyngeal pressures and found that RAM cannulae underdelivered by 2–3 cmH2O, whereas Hudson prongs delivered within 0.5 cmH2O of target pressures. Gerdes et al. [21] identified key determinants of delivered pressure, including mouth opening, prong size and insertion depth, to underscore the variability in pressure delivery across infants. Sharma et al. [32] similarly reported significant pressure losses across multiple non-occlusive systems; delivered pressures often fell below therapeutic thresholds of 5–8 cmH2O. These discrepancies are clinically meaningful. In preterm infants with surfactant-deficient RDS, a 2–3 cmH2O pressure loss can substantially reduce FRC and impair gas exchange [55].

Recent randomised trials have yielded mixed results regarding the clinical non-inferiority of non-occlusive systems. Samim et al. [28] found CPAP failure rates of 19.7% with RAM cannula versus 17.3% with short bi-nasal prongs in infants of 28–34 weeks’ gestation, failing to meet the prespecified non-inferiority criteria (risk difference −2.36%, 95% confidence interval −11.9 to 7.2%) because the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval crossed the non-inferiority margin of 10%. By contrast, Al-Lawama et al. [47] reported comparable respiratory outcomes with RAM cannula versus bi-nasal prongs in more mature infants (≥32 weeks’ gestation), suggesting that gestational age and disease severity may critically influence outcomes. Kumar et al. [48] observed CPAP failure rates of 52.1% in infants of 28–30 weeks’ gestation versus 18.2% in those of 31–32 weeks’ gestation when supported by RAM cannulae. These results add to evidence of reduced efficacy in the most immature infants. Claassen et al. [56] demonstrated that higher set CPAP pressures (6.8±1.1 cmH2O) were required with RAM cannulae to achieve equivalent clinical stability compared with 5.3±0.9 cmH2O for occlusive prongs, potentially offsetting safety or comfort gains.

Pressure delivery accuracy

Bench studies consistently demonstrate significant pressure attenuation with non-occlusive interfaces. Singh et al. [20] found that RAM cannula underdelivered pressure by 2.45 cmH2O while Hudson prongs remained within 0.40 cmH2O (p=0.0002). Gerdes et al. [21] demonstrated that with 60–80% nares occlusion, mean airway pressure was 60±17% less than the set CPAP; open mouth reduced delivered pressure to <1.7 cmH2O. Because optimal CPAP pressures range from 5 to 8 cmH2O, a 2–3 cmH2O reduction could drop the delivered pressure below the therapeutic threshold. Mouth opening represents a major source of pressure loss even with occlusive interfaces. MacDonald et al. [23] demonstrated an approximately 20% reduction in transpulmonary pressure when chin straps were removed, even with the mouth appearing passively closed. Visual observation of mouth closure was inadequate; active support via a chin strap was required to maintain the prescribed pressure. Bachour et al. [57] confirmed that chin straps significantly reduce mouth leak in adult patients receiving CPAP. These findings support the use of chin straps in infants with clinical signs of inadequate pressure delivery, such as persistent bubbling failure or increasing oxygen requirements. Routine use of chin straps in all neonates on CPAP is not universally practiced and should be guided by clinical assessment. The chin strap should keep the mouth passively closed with the baby being able to yawn or cry.

CPAP delivery systems differ in leak detection capability. Bubble CPAP offers a distinct advantage: air leaks are immediately apparent through changes in bubbling pattern, allowing rapid identification and correction [8, 24, 58–60]. Ventilator-derived CPAP may not provide obvious visual cues when leaks occur. Pressure loss may therefore go undetected until clinical instability manifests [8, 61]. This underscores the critical importance of bedside nurses and respiratory therapists in maintaining effective CPAP delivery through vigilant monitoring and systematic interface assessments [62, 63]. Quality improvement initiatives standardising leak detection protocols have demonstrated significant reductions in extubation failure rates [64].

For extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks’ gestation) with moderate-to-severe RDS, evidence strongly supports initial management with occlusive CPAP to optimise pressure delivery and maximise the likelihood of avoiding intubation (table 4) [3, 6, 65]. These infants have the most severe surfactant deficiency, greatest chest wall instability and lowest tolerance for suboptimal respiratory support [8]. Infants of 28–32 weeks’ gestation represent an intermediate population in whom clinical judgement guides interface selection [66, 67]. More mature infants (>32 weeks’ gestation) or those with mild respiratory distress may be suitable candidates for non-occlusive systems, especially when preventing nasal trauma is a priority [2, 40]. Expert opinion and clinical experience suggest that initial stabilisation with occlusive systems followed by transition to non-occlusive for longer term support may optimise both effectiveness and comfort. Prospective trials are needed to confirm this approach (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06123143).

The physiological rationale for this staged approach centres on the “open lung” strategy [67, 68]. In acute respiratory distress, particularly in extremely preterm infants with surfactant deficiency and highly compliant chest walls, precise pressure delivery is essential to establish and maintain FRC. Once FRC has been established, evidenced by decreasing oxygen requirements and clinical stability, transition to non-occlusive interfaces may be considered, although emerging evidence suggests that the duration of occlusive CPAP therapy may have important implications for lung development. McEvoy et al. [25] conducted a RCT in which stable preterm infants were assigned to either extended CPAP for 2 weeks beyond achieving respiratory stability or standard discontinuation to room air. At 6 months corrected age, infants in the extended CPAP group demonstrated significantly greater alveolar volume, improved lung diffusion capacity and enhanced forced expiratory flows compared to controls. Preclinical studies in animal models support these findings. Chronic mechanical stretch from CPAP not only distends the lungs but actively stimulates lung growth and development [69]. These observations suggest that the continuous distending pressure provided by CPAP may promote alveolar and airway development during a critical window of postnatal lung maturation. This has important implications for interface selection: premature weaning of preterm infants to non-occlusive CPAP, with its inherent pressure attenuation of 2–3 cmH2O, may not only compromise immediate respiratory support but could also deprive developing lungs of the sustained mechanical stretch that promotes optimal growth. Clinicians should therefore carefully consider whether the infant has received an adequate duration of effective distending pressure before transitioning to interfaces that deliver lower pressures, particularly in extremely preterm infants at highest risk for impaired lung development and BPD.

Staffing expertise represents a significant practical consideration. Occlusive systems require specialised knowledge for optimal fitting and assessment that may not be available in all settings. Non-occlusive systems generally require less specialised training but staff must recognise clinical signs of ineffective support and understand when escalation is necessary [30, 31, 47].

Clinical scenarios and practical management

The clinical indicators in table 3 provide objective parameters for assessing CPAP adequacy, while table 4 offers population-specific guidance.

Scenario 1: respiratory instability on non-occlusive CPAP

When an infant demonstrates signs of inadequate pressure delivery (increased work of breathing, rising oxygen requirements, frequent desaturations), the initial intervention should be to increase the set CPAP pressure. Given that non-occlusive systems underdeliver by 2–3 cmH2O, clinicians should not hesitate to increase PEEP beyond traditional thresholds; pressures of 9–10 cmH2O may be necessary to achieve equivalent delivered pressure of 6–8 cmH2O. If instability persists despite PEEP optimisation (e.g. set PEEP of 10 cmH2O without improvement), transition to an occlusive interface should be strongly considered.

Optimising CPAP pressure delivery is critical regardless of interface. Anderson et al. [70] demonstrated that implementing protocols for higher bubble CPAP pressures during neonatal transport reduced intubation rates without increasing complications, underscoring the importance of adequate distending pressure.

Scenario 2: transitioning from occlusive to non-occlusive CPAP

When transitioning a stable infant, clinicians must account for anticipated pressure attenuation to avoid inadvertent derecruitment. A direct 1:1 pressure conversion effectively represents a significant pressure wean. Instead, when transitioning to a non-occlusive interface, set PEEP should be increased by 1–2 cmH2O to compensate for expected pressure loss. For example, an infant stable on occlusive CPAP at 6 cmH2O should transition to non-occlusive at 7–8 cmH2O to maintain the equivalent delivered pressure.

Scenario 3: interface selection at extubation

The post-extubation period is high-risk and adequate CPAP is critical. As outlined in table 4, extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks’ gestation) should be extubated to occlusive CPAP. More mature infants (>32 weeks’ gestation) with mild respiratory distress may be appropriate candidates for non-occlusive interfaces with higher set pressures (8–10 cmH2O). Systematic assessment using table 3 parameters should guide management, with early escalation to occlusive support if clinical indicators suggest inadequate pressure delivery.

Future directions and research needs

The most pressing research need is for adequately powered RCTs directly comparing occlusive with non-occlusive systems in defined patient populations with clinically relevant outcomes. Future trials should stratify by gestational age and disease severity, with particular attention to extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks’ gestation) who face the highest risks. Long-term outcome studies examining neurodevelopmental effects and chronic lung disease progression are vital. Standardisation of pressure monitoring methods and development of hybrid interfaces combining accurate pressure delivery with improved comfort offer promising paths for innovation [33]. Implementation science research examining barriers to adopting evidence-based protocols could significantly affect outcomes regardless of interface approach.

Conclusions

Occlusive CPAP interfaces deliver accurate, predictable distending pressures with proven efficacy in landmark trials. Nasal trauma rates of 20–60% remain a significant limitation. Non-occlusive systems substantially reduce trauma (0–5.4%) but with inconsistent pressure delivery, including 2–3 cmH2O potential losses, raising concerns about therapeutic effectiveness in critically ill neonates. Current evidence supports occlusive CPAP as the first-line therapy for moderate-to-severe RDS in preterm neonates, particularly those of <32 weeks’ gestation, in whom accurate pressure delivery is crucial. Non-occlusive approaches may be appropriate for weaning, managing stable infants with AOP, supporting more mature infants with mild respiratory distress, or when nasal preservation is especially important. An “open lung” strategy using occlusive CPAP to establish FRC, along with a chin strap to reduce pressure loss through the open mouth, followed by transition to non-occlusive systems once stability is achieved in older infants with less compliant chest walls, represents a physiologically rational approach. However, lung growth from positive pressure should also be considered in stable preterm infants from an occlusive interface, with careful assessment for nasal trauma. Individual patient assessment and appropriate interface selection are key principles: successful outcomes depend on matching technology to patient needs and the clinical context.
