Day-to-day variability in resting metabolic rate in strength athletes
Authors: Claire Buechel, Kate Pumpa, Naroa Etxebarria, Luke Ashton, Michelle Minehan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResting Metabolic Rate (RMR) is measured in sport settings for health monitoring and nutrition planning; however, interpreting repeated measures requires an understanding of typical day-to-day variability under habitual, free-living conditions. This study quantified intra-individual variability in RMR among weight-stable strength-trained athletes, examined the impact of data processing approaches, and established protocol-specific least significant change (LSC) thresholds under ecological conditions.MethodsA repeated-measures design was used to quantify intra-individual variability in RMR in 13 weight-stable strength-trained athletes (8 male, 5 female). RMR was measured on five consecutive mornings using the Parvo Medics TrueOne 2400 metabolic cart with a Hans Rudolph mouthpiece under standardized conditions. Breath-by-breath data were processed using multiple cleaning approaches to evaluate their impact on RMR estimates, with the most reliable method retained for analysis. Intra-individual variability was quantified and used to derive protocol-specific LSC thresholds.ResultsSession averaging produced the lowest intra-individual variability in RMR (CV: 3.4 ± 1.4%) compared with alternative data cleaning approaches. Mean RMR was 26.9 ± 1.4 and 29.1 ± 1.7 kcal·kgFFM−1·day−1 in males and females, respectively. The group-level LSC was 2.9 ± 1.2 kcal·kgFFM−1·day−1 (10.4 ± 4.2%), representing the magnitude of change required to exceed expected within-subject variability under the conditions of this protocol. Fat-free mass (FFM) was positively associated with both absolute RMR and day-to-day variability, whereas prior-day training load and protein intake showed no clear association with RMR variability.ConclusionsNotable intra-individual variability occurs in repeated RMR measurements in strength-trained athletes under applied conditions. Meaningful interpretation of change requires protocol-specific reliability estimates to distinguish true physiological change from expected day-to-day variation. Session averaging improves measurement consistency and is recommended for mouthpiece-based indirect calorimetry systems. RMR should be interpreted relative to individual and context-specific variability rather than universal thresholds.

Introduction

Resting metabolic rate (RMR) represents the energy required for basic physiological function at rest and is used in clinical and applied settings to assess energy requirements, guide nutritional prescription, and monitor risk of Low Energy Availability (LEA) and consequently risk of Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDS) [1,2]. Accurate and reproducible measurement of RMR is relevant in strength-trained athletes who frequently cycle through phases of energy restriction and refeeding for body composition and competition goals. These dietary phases can induce alterations in RMR, driven by changes in fat-free mass (FFM), endocrine adaptations, and metabolic efficiency, increasing risk of metabolic suppression [3,4]. Metabolic suppression is suspected when measured RMR is below predicted RMR, or RMR expressed per kilogram of FFM is below expected levels [1]. Alternatively, longitudinal monitoring can provide insight into an athlete’s individual metabolic profile, however there is no consensus on what constitutes a “meaningful” change in RMR.

RMR is typically measured via indirect calorimetry, where oxygen consumption (VO₂) and carbon dioxide production (VCO₂) are measured in a fasted, rested state to estimate energy expenditure [5]. While the Parvo Medics TrueOne 2400 system with Hans Rudolph mouthpiece is widely used in applied sport science, limited published data describe its expected day-to-day variability. This is important as indirect calorimetry is highly sensitive to equipment configurations, sampling interfaces (e.g. canopy vs. mouthpiece), testing conditions, and data processing strategies [6,7]. For example, prior research using the Deltatrac II metabolic cart and hood reported a within-individual coefficient of variation (CV) of 7.29% + 2.26% in healthy sedentary adults, suggesting that RMR would need to change by at least ~8% to exceed normal measurement error under those conditions [8]. However, while CV provides a summary of relative within-subject variability across repeated trials, it does not directly translate into a decision threshold for determining whether an observed change exceeds expected short-term variation, limiting interpretation of meaningful physiological change at the individual level.

In repeated RMR assessments, variability arises from multiple sources, including technical measurement error, biological day-to-day fluctuation, and behavioural influences such as prior exercise, diet, sleep, and circadian factors [6]. Least significant change thresholds (LSC) can be used to derive an individualised threshold for within-subject variability, representing the minimum change required to exceed expected within-subject variation arising from both biological fluctuation and residual measurement noise under specific testing conditions [9]. This positions LSC as a potentially useful tool for monitoring longitudinal metabolic change in athletes under habitual, free-living conditions and may better represent the range of variation encountered in practice, rather than isolated instrument error.

Deriving an accurate LSC requires consistent processing of metabolic cart data, yet there is no consensus on best practice. This is particularly challenging with the Hans Rudolph mouthpiece where steady-state criteria commonly used to define RMR (e.g. 3–5-minutes with <10% CV in VO₂ or VCO₂) are often unattainable due to greater breath-by-breath variability [10,11]. To manage this, practitioners often use fixed time-interval averaging across the session, though this may inflate RMR estimates compared to filter-based methods that exclude outlier breaths [12]. Beyond data processing, most studies examining day-to-day RMR variability assume constant variance across individuals and do not distinguish technical error from biological noise, limiting practical utility [8,13]. Although these sources of variability cannot be fully separated in applied settings, statistical models accounting for heterogeneity in within-subject variation may improve interpretation of repeated RMR measurements.

Multilevel modelling provides a framework to account for within- and between-subject variability [14], while exploring the influence of covariates such as body composition, prior-day training, and nutrition. These factors may be associated with observed variability and can provide context for interpreting expected within-subject fluctuation. FFM is a key covariate in this context, as it is a primary determinant of absolute RMR, reflecting the combined metabolic activity of organ tissues and skeletal muscle [15]. Further, skeletal muscle is metabolically dynamic and responsive to recent exercise, nutrient availability, physiological stress, and inflammation [16,17], which may contribute to greater day-to-day variability in RMR in individuals with higher FFM. This provides a physiological rationale for examining FFM not only as a determinant of absolute RMR, but also as a potential contributor to within-subject variability in repeated measurements.

Therefore, this study aimed to quantify intra-individual day-to-day RMR variability among weight-stable strength-trained athletes using the Parvo Medics TrueOne 2400 system with Hans Rudolph mouthpiece, and to determine a protocol-specific LSC threshold under ecological conditions. To do this, we sought to address existing methodological gaps by comparing data cleaning strategies and exploring the influence of selected behavioural and physiological influences on RMR fluctuations.

Methods

Participants and study design

A repeated-measures study was conducted to quantify intra-individual variability in RMR in strength-trained athletes when living under habitual, free-living conditions. Fourteen participants (9 male, 5 female) were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling from local strength gyms, competitive organisations such as the Australian Powerlifting Union, and professional networks. The study sought a sample size of 10–23 participants, based on methods used in previous studies validating day-to-day RMR variations in healthy subjects [8,18,19]. Eligibility criteria included: (i) age 18–45 years; (ii) declare as a natural athlete; (iii) weight stable (no active weight loss or gain), (iv) current resistance training of at least 4 days per week, and (v) prior competition experience in strength sports (e.g. powerlifting, Olympic weightlifting, bodybuilding) [20]. All participants provided written informed consent.

Resting metabolic rate

RMR was measured on 5 consecutive mornings (5:30 am–9 am) using the Parvo Medics TrueOne 2400 metabolic cart (Parvo Medics, Sandy, UT), with a Hans Rudolph mouthpiece and nose-clip interface. Consecutive-day testing was selected to isolate short-term within-subject variability in RMR under habitual, free-living conditions, while minimising the influence of longer-term physiological adaptations, training periodisation, or changes in energy balance that may occur with less frequent measurement intervals. Participants were tested at the same time each morning (±30 minutes) to minimise the influence of circadian variation on RMR [6]. Calibration of gas analysers and flow sensors was performed prior to each session using certified gas mixtures and a 3 L syringe. Testing was performed in a quiet, dimly lit, and temperature-controlled environment (20–24 °C). Participants were instructed to fast overnight and refrain from caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, and exercise prior to testing (>10 h) in accordance with the Compher protocol [6]. Upon arrival, participants were weighed and asked to rest supine for 10 minutes. This was followed by a 15-minute habituation period with the mouthpiece (without nose clip), during which calibration was performed. RMR was then recorded for 25 minutes, with the first 6 minutes discarded to exclude artifacts and the remaining 20 minutes retained for analysis [7]. Inspired VO₂ and expired VCO₂ data were collected continuously, and energy expenditure was calculated using the Weir equation [21]. For female participants, testing was conducted during the early follicular phase of the menstrual cycle based on self-reported timing to reduce hormonal influences on RMR [22]. Non-menstruating females were measured at any stage of their cycle (n = 2).

Body composition

Body composition was assessed as a single measurement using Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA, GE Lunar Prodigy), operated by a certified densitometrist, and analysed using GE Encore Software (Encore Version 17 GE Healthcare). Participants were measured in the morning after a 10-hour fast with positioning according to the Nana protocol [23]. DXA reliability was independently assessed in our laboratory prior to data collection [24]. Per prior testing, lean body mass CV was 0.7%, with a LSC of 1.9% (95% CI). Fat mass CV was 1.3%, with a LSC of 3.7% (95% CI).

Diet and training

Diet and training were monitored rather than standardised to preserve ecological validity and reflect the applied context in which repeated RMR measurements are typically interpreted in applied sport settings. At the end of each laboratory session, a 24-hour diet and training recall was conducted by an accredited sports dietitian to monitor consistency in nutritional intake and training behaviours across the testing period, and to identify any potential factors that might influence day-to-day RMR fluctuations. Participants were asked to report all food and fluid intake, training type, training timing, duration, and perceived intensity (Borg’s CR-10) for the previous 24 h at each visit [25]. Dietary data were analysed using FoodWorks software (Version 8, Xyris, Queensland). Energy intake was expressed as kcal·day−1 and kcal·kgFFM−1·day−1. Macronutrient intake was expressed as g·kg·day−1. Training was quantified as an internal training load using session RPE × session duration (min) [26].

Data analysis

Raw data were exported to Microsoft Excel (v16.98) and analysed in R (v4.4.1, R Core Team). Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD) were used to summarise participant characteristics. To evaluate how breath-by-breath data cleaning influenced RMR estimates, four commonly used cleaning approaches were compared after excluding the first 5 minutes of each trial (acclimation period): (i) Session Average (mean RMR across the trial), (ii) Freire-low Filter (data retained within ±15% of trial mean), (iii) Freire-med Filter (data retained within ±10% of trial mean), and (iv) Steady-state Filter (5-minute window with lowest coefficient of variation (CV) in VO₂) [12]. Group-level variability was assessed using coefficient of variation (CV%) and typical error (TE). The Session Average method was retained for further analysis due to low coefficient of variation (CV%) and typical error (TE), as well as its practical ease of implementation in applied sport settings.

Intra-individual variability in RMR was quantified using participant-level standard deviation (SD) and expressed as least significant change (LSC, kcal∙day−1 and %). LSC was calculated using the formula: LSC=t×SD×1+1n where t is the critical t-value in a student’s t-distribution (df = 4; t = 2.776), SD is the participant's standard deviation across repeated trials, and n is the number of measurements for each participant (n = 5). This formula reflects the minimal detectable change required to exceed observed measurement noise with 95% confidence.

Bayesian hierarchical models were used to estimate the predictors of RMR (kcal∙day−1) and its variability. RMR was modelled with a Student’s t-distribution, selected after visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots indicated slight skew and moderately heavy tails. This approach was appropriate given the sample size (n = 13) and the t-distribution’s robustness to outliers. Participant-level random intercepts were included to account for repeated measures across models. The mean (location) component of the model included only an intercept and random effect for participant, allowing individual differences in baseline RMR to be accounted for without overparameterization. Predictors were applied to the standard deviation (scale; σ) component of the model to examine factors influencing within-subject variability in RMR. Predictors were informed by physiological plausibility and exploratory visualisation. Between-subject FFM (z-scored) was included due to its established role as a determinant of total RMR and its observed patterns of association with variability in this sample [27]. Within-subject predictors, prior-day training load and protein intake (within-centred and z-scored), were included based on theoretical links to acute metabolic effects, such as excess post-exercise oxygen consumption and the thermogenic effect of protein [28,29].

Three models were fitted: (1) an FFM-only model, (2) an FFM plus prior-day within-individual training load model, and (3) an FFM plus prior-day within-individual protein intake model. This stepwise approach was used to examine the robustness of associations while limiting model complexity, given the modest sample size and potential overlap between body composition, dietary intake, and training behaviours. Weakly informative priors were used. For FFM, a normal (0.3, 0.5) reflected the plausible magnitude of association between FFM and RMR variability, while remaining broad to allow for uncertainty in both direction and size of effect. Training load and protein intake were assigned broad normal (0, 1) priors. Sensitivity analyses using default weakly informative priors were conducted to assess the influence of prior specification on model estimates. Convergence was assessed using Rhat, effective sample sizes, leave-one-out information criterion, and posterior predictive checks [30]. Results are reported as posterior means with 90% credible intervals (CrI), indicating a 90% probability that the estimate is within the interval, given the observed data, model, and priors. A 90% CrI was chosen to improve computational stability [31].

Results

Participant characteristics

Fourteen strength-trained athletes completed this study, and 13 (8 male, 5 female) were included in the final analysis (Table 1). One participant was excluded post hoc after it was identified that they were 12 weeks post-bodybuilding competition and still experiencing symptoms of recovery, including fluid retention and psychological restrictions around food intake. Despite weight stability, the participant exhibited high RMR variability and suppressed RMR relative to FFM, indicating ongoing physiological disturbance inconsistent with the study’s requirements for healthy, weight-stable athletes. Participants were classified according to the McKay framework as Tier 2–4 athletes, representing trained to elite strength-trained individuals [20]. The majority of participants were classified as Tier 3 (highly trained) (n = 7), with four meeting criteria for Tier 4 (elite) based on national-to-international competition level and performance metrics (Supplementary Table S1).

Predictors of RMR variability

Bayesian models were fitted to examine predictors of within-subject variability in RMR. Three models were specified; FFM-only, FFM plus prior-day within-individual training load, and FFM plus prior-day within-individual protein intake. In the FFM-only model, the estimated mean RMR was 1748.3 ± 108.4 kcal·day−1 (90% CrI: 1574.3–1930.4). Between-subject variability (random intercept SD) was 409.6 kcal·day−1 (90% CrI: 291.8–573.2). Within subject variability was 58.9 kcal∙day−1 (90% CrI: 46.2–71.8). FFM was positively associated with within-subject variability (β = 0.32, 90% CrI: 0.12–0.52), indicating greater day-to-day variability in RMR with higher FFM (Figure 2).

In the model including prior-day training load, the estimated mean RMR (1747.1 ± 107.3 kcal·day−1) and both between- and within-subject variability were unchanged relative to the FFM-only model (409.1 and 58.4 kcal·day−1, respectively). The association between FFM and within-subject variability remained consistent (β = 0.31, 90% CrI: 0.11–0.50), while prior-day training load showed no clear association with variability (β = 0.04, 90% CrI: −0.24–0.34).

In the model including prior-day protein intake, mean RMR (1747.8 ± 110.2 kcal·day−1) and both between- and within-subject variability were unchanged relative to the FFM-only model (408.8 and 58.8 kcal·day−1, respectively). FFM remained positively associated with within-subject variability (β = 0.32, 90% CrI: 0.13–0.51), whereas prior-day protein intake showed no meaningful association (β = −0.07, 90% CrI: −0.30–0.16). Posterior estimates were consistent when models were refit using default priors, indicating that results were not sensitive to prior specification.

Discussion

Accurate and reproducible RMR measurement is important in sport nutrition practice for monitoring metabolic adaptation, informing energy prescription, and identifying potential metabolic suppression associated with LEA and REDS. Our research shows that RMR measured by the Parvo Medics TrueOne 2400 system with Hans Rudolph mouthpiece typically varies day-to-day by 3.4% in weight-stable, strength-trained athletes. This variability reflects the combined influence of technical measurement error and residual biological and behavioural fluctuation under standardised testing conditions when athletes are living under habitual, free-living conditions. Our group-level LSC calculation indicates that RMR would need to change by 2.9 kcal·kgFFM−1·day−1 (10.4%) to exceed this combined variability within our protocol. Importantly, this threshold should not be interpreted as a universal benchmark for meaningful physiological change, but rather as a context- and method-specific estimate derived from this cohort. For sports dietitians, practitioners, and researchers, who often seek practical methods to identify potential metabolic suppression in the context of LEA/REDS, protocol-specific CV and LSC thresholds may strengthen interpretation of repeated RMR assessments by distinguishing expected day-to-day variability from likely physiological shifts that warrant further investigation.

When managing RMR variability in practice, data processing approaches play a key role in improving the reliability of RMR assessment. The session averaging method produced the most reliable estimates (CV = 3.4 ± 1.4%, TE = 61.2 kcal). This represents one of the most practically relevant findings of the present study. Our results align with Alcántara et al. who compared cleaning methods across 4 ventilated-hood carts on two repeat days, finding that 20–30 minute session averages produced the most consistent RMR estimates (CV = 3.6–5.7%, day-to-day differences = 7–33 kcal∙day−1) [32]. In contrast, steady-state filtering approaches, particularly those relying on short-duration CV thresholds, may artificially reduce within-test variability without improving reproducibility across days [12]. In mouthpiece configurations where breath-by-breath noise is inherently greater, filtering approaches were less effective for reliability, as evidenced by the inflated variability in our steady-state filter method (CV = 6%, TE 108 kcal). This likely reflects the inability of short steady-state windows to capture representative metabolic data in the presence of increased ventilatory noise. Together, these results indicate that while filtering may improve apparent within-test precision, session averaging provides a more robust and ecologically valid approach for longitudinal monitoring using mouthpiece systems.

Reported variability in repeated RMR measures differs across laboratories, systems, and populations. For example, Woods et al. reported a CV of 6.6% with a Parvo Medics TrueOne system in endurance-trained athletes [33]. Bader et al. reported a CV of 5.2% in healthy, non-trained males using a ventilated hood and SensorMedics system across 4 sessions spread over 6 months, equating to ~130–160 kcal∙day−1 of variation [18]. Roffey et al. used a Deltatrac II system in overweight adults and reported day-to-day variability using a mouthpiece (CV 7.3%) compared to a hood (CV 5.2%) [8]. These comparisons highlight that RMR variability is influenced by participant characteristics, equipment configuration, sampling interface, and standardisation procedures. A systematic review of 16 studies assessing RMR in healthy, untrained adults using portable gas analysers found considerable variability in measurement across devices and recommended more rigorous validation of portable gas analysers across diverse populations and metabolic assessments [34]. These findings reinforce the importance of establishing laboratory-, protocol-, and population-specific reliability metrics, such as LSC or minimal detectable change (MDC) benchmarks to enable accurate interpretation of longitudinal RMR changes and minimise misclassification arising from methodological and biological variability.

To reinforce this recommendation, we point to recent longitudinal studies in team-sport athletes. Hudson et al. measured RMR across a match week in elite rugby players, finding a ~231 kcal increase (~10% change, p = 0.005) the morning after competition compared to pre-match levels [35]. Similarly, Carter et al. observed a post-match increase in RMR measures of male soccer players the day after competition of ~261 kcal∙day−1 (~13% change, p = 0.0004) [36]. Both studies identified significant group-level change, however whether these changes represented meaningful shifts in underlying metabolic status at the individual level, as opposed to transient physiological responses to competition, remains unclear. In the context of athlete monitoring, meaningful change is generally interpreted as a sustained alteration in metabolic rate (e.g. suppression associated with low energy availability or increases reflecting changes in body composition), rather than short-term elevations driven by acute exercise stress. Using our lab-specific LSC as an illustrative benchmark, Hudson’s observed increase would likely fall within normal variation, whereas Carter’s exceeds this threshold. Importantly, exceeding the LSC in this context may reflect an acute physiological response rather than a longer-term metabolic adaptation, highlighting the need to consider both magnitude and temporal context when interpreting RMR data. For practitioners, these comparisons highlight that RMR measured shortly after competition or heavy training may reflect acute recovery demands rather than chronic metabolic adaptation, supporting the need to consider testing timing when using RMR to guide nutrition decisions. In contrast, Scantlebury et al. measured RMR in women’s rugby players at three timepoints over a 10-month period, finding no statistical difference in absolute mean RMR across timepoints, with the largest group change of ~130 kcal (~7% change) [37]. Although less frequent sampling may have failed to capture short-term fluctuations similar to those observed by Hudson and Carter, these findings further illustrate how differences in sampling frequency and methodological context influence the interpretation of RMR change. These studies are therefore useful examples of the challenges involved in distinguishing transient variability from physiologically meaningful adaptation in applied settings.

Our findings also question the utility of comparing measured and predicted RMR to infer metabolic suppression [1]. The combined impact of prediction error and inherent day-to-day variability substantially limits the interpretability of RMR ratios. A 2023 systematic review found that no single predictive equation is universally accurate for athletes, with the most precise (Ten Haaf equation) correctly predicting only ~80% of athletes’ RMR within ±10% of measured values, while others achieved 41–64% accuracy [38]. When considered alongside the magnitude of within-subject variability observed in our cohort, deviations of this magnitude may occur in the absence of true metabolic suppression. As such, reliance on predicted-to-measured comparisons may lead to misclassification. This is supported by previous research demonstrating limited utility of RMR ratios for detecting low energy availability in athletes [39,40], and highlights the value of repeated, individualised monitoring approaches. In applied sport nutrition practice, repeated RMR measures interpreted alongside protocol-specific variability thresholds may reduce risk of over-diagnosing metabolic suppression from a single measured-to-predicted ratio, and support more precise individualised nutrition prescription.

FFM was the strongest predictor of both absolute RMR and day-to-day variability in our cohort, consistent with literature identifying FFM as a primary determinant of RMR [41–43]. Athletes with greater FFM (>70 kg) exhibited higher within-subject variability, with LSC values exceeding 300 kcal∙day−1. This pattern suggests that variability in RMR scales with the amount of metabolically active tissue, resulting in greater absolute fluctuation in larger individuals. While high-metabolic-rate organs contribute a large share of resting energy expenditure and remain relatively stable, a greater proportion of skeletal muscle may increase the responsiveness of whole-body metabolism to short-term physiological fluctuations [16,17]. Consequently, larger athletes may exhibit greater absolute day-to-day variation in RMR, even under standardised conditions. These findings indicate that RMR variability is not uniform across individuals, but scales with body composition. This distinction is practically important when monitoring larger strength-trained athletes, as a change that appears substantial in absolute terms may still fall within expected variability, whereas smaller absolute change in a lower-FFM athlete may be more meaningful. Therefore, our group-level LSC of 10.4% may provide an initial benchmark for repeated RMR monitoring in similar strength-trained athletes; however, best practice should be to interpret longitudinal changes relative to individual LSC values and athlete characteristics such as FFM, rather than relying on fixed group-level thresholds.

While RMR variability scaled with FFM, a proportion remained unexplained, suggesting that additional factors may contribute to day-to-day fluctuations. The inclusion of prior-day behavioural variables did not meaningfully reduce this variability, suggesting limited explanatory value of these factors within the present context. When examining prior-day training load, no clear association with RMR variability was observed. While acute exercise is known to elevate RMR via excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC) [17], the magnitude and duration of this response depends on exercise intensity, timing, and individual recovery status. Greer et al. demonstrated that strenuous resistance training can elevate RMR ~14 h post-exercise, returning to baseline within ~24 h [44], whereas moderate endurance exercise may have minimal impact [45]. Despite standardising pre-test conditions (≥10 h exercise abstinence), residual effects from prior training may still contribute to variability, particularly following high-load or evening training sessions. In strength-trained athletes, there may also be exercise-induced muscle damage and associated inflammatory responses, which can persist for up to 72 h and increase metabolic demand independent of baseline physiological status [46]. However, the absence of a clear association in our cohort suggests that the influence of prior-day training on RMR variability may be small, inconsistent across individuals, or not adequately captured by session-level training load metrics, particularly given variability in exercise intensity, timing, and recovery. Additionally, prior-day protein intake showed no meaningful association, indicating that overnight fasting conditions likely attenuate acute dietary thermogenesis in this context.

The following limitations of our research should be considered. While our sample size (n = 13) falls within the range used in previous studies examining day-to-day RMR variability in healthy individuals [8,18], the relatively small cohort limits the generalisability of our findings. Participants were weight-stable strength-trained athletes, and therefore the derived LSC thresholds may not directly apply to other athlete types (e.g. endurance, team-sport, etc.) or sedentary adults. Future studies should conduct similar repeated-measures analyses in broader athletic and nonathletic populations to determine whether day-to-day RMR variability and protocol-specific LSC thresholds differ across groups. Among female participants, menstrual phase was standardised to the early follicular phase based on self-reported history; however, this was not biochemically verified. Although testing conditions were standardised, variability related to prior training stress, recovery status, sleep duration or quality, and autonomic regulation was not controlled. This was intentional to reflect ecological conditions under which RMR is typically assessed in practice. Accordingly, system-level measurement error was not independently isolated, and the observed variability reflects the combined influence of residual technical error and biological day-to-day fluctuation under these applied conditions. Therefore, the derived LSC should be interpreted as a protocol-specific threshold for detecting meaningful within-athlete change rather than a pure estimate of instrument precision. Compliance with pre-test instructions (e.g. fasting and exercise abstinence) relied on self-report, introducing potential recall or reporting bias. Additionally, participants were unable to consistently recall precise external training loads (e.g. kg lifted), and therefore internal load was used as a proxy, which may not fully capture variability in training stimulus. Finally, while we compared four commonly used breath-by-breath data cleaning strategies to address inconsistencies in RMR processing, alternative methods exist, and researchers and practitioners are encouraged to replicate these approaches within their own populations and laboratory settings to ensure method suitability and consistency in interpretation.

Conclusion

This study quantifies day-to-day RMR variability and establishes LSC thresholds in strength-trained athletes using the Parvo Medics TrueOne 2400 system with a Hans Rudolph mouthpiece. A session average approach to data cleaning produced the most consistent RMR estimates, representing a key practical finding and supporting its use in mouthpiece-based systems for applied athlete monitoring. Under the conditions of this protocol, RMR would need to change by approximately 2.9 kcal·kgFFM−1·day−1 (10.4%) to exceed expected within-athlete variability. This threshold reflects the combined influence of technical measurement error and biological day-to-day fluctuation under habitual, free-living testing conditions, and should be interpreted as protocol-specific rather than a universal benchmark for meaningful physiological change. Individualised LSC thresholds revealed substantial inter-individual variation, with athletes possessing higher FFM requiring larger absolute changes to exceed normal day-to-day variability. Prior-day training load and protein intake showed no clear evidence of an association with within-subject variability. These findings emphasise that meaningful interpretation of RMR change requires laboratory-specific reliability estimates and context-specific LSC thresholds. Future research should validate LSC thresholds across diverse athletic populations and measurement systems.

Practical recommendations

Determining LSC provides a practical framework for interpreting longitudinal changes in repeated RMR measurements, as it defines the magnitude of change required to exceed expected within-subject variability under specific testing conditions. In practice, protocol- and population-specific LSC thresholds may help practitioners determine whether observed changes in an athlete’s RMR exceed normal day-to-day variability. This can support more informed nutrition decisions during energy restriction, weight manipulation, or suspected LEA/REDS, by helping practitioners assess whether a reduction in RMR is greater than expected variability and may reflect metabolic suppression following weight-loss interventions or states of problematic LEA. Importantly, RMR changes should be interpreted alongside broader clinical and performance contexts rather than used as a standalone indicator of LEA/REDS.
