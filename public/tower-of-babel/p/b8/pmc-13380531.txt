Correlation of type 3 secretion system genes with antimicrobial resistance in Pseudomonas aeruginosa: A unique clinical signature
Authors: Farkhanda Afaque, Aliya Hayat, Somia Gul, Ayaz Ahmed, Mahmooda Kazmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPseudomonas aeruginosa is a well-known opportunistic pathogen with intrinsic multidrug resistance, extensive drug resistance, and multiple virulence factors, making hospital-acquired infections therapy resistant. The type 3 secretion system (T3SS) and its four effector proteins, i.e., ExoS, ExoT, ExoU, and ExoY, have invasive and cytotoxic effects that lead to host cell death. The significant correlations of exo genes with the resistance attributes of P. aeruginosa in disease progression were evaluated in the current study.MethodsIn total, 102 random clinical strains of Pseudomonas were collected from local pathological settings in Karachi, Pakistan. The strains were conventionally characterized using Pseudomonas isolation agar. Biochemical identification was conducted by using QTS-24 strips to confirm P. aeruginosa, and molecular confirmation based on species-specific 16S rRNA genes. The strains were categorized based on antimicrobial sensitivity testing in accordance with CLSI guidelines. The virulence profiles of the selected multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains were obtained using the specific T3SS exo gene by PCR.ResultsAmong 102 strains, 44 strains were identified as P. aeruginosa. According to the drug resistance profiles for the P. aeruginosa strains, 80.6% (25/31) were MDR and 16.1% (6/31) were extremely drug resistant (XDR) based on multiple antibiotic resistance index (MARi) evaluations. The highest sensitivity was recorded for meropenem with 84%, followed by piperacillin with 66% and ertapenem with 64%. The maximum resistance was to colistin with 100% antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) where the resistance was characterized by a minimum inhibitory concentration value ≥ 4 μg/mL, and tigecycline and aztreonam with 82%. The most prevalent exo genes were exoS at 43%, followed by exoT at 30%, exoU at 13%, and exoY at 9%. Statistically significant correlations were found between exo genes and MARi resistotype groups, with a p-value <0.05 and Carmer's value of 1.00.ConclusionsIn this study, significant correlations were detected between drug resistance and T3SS exo genes, which are a severe threat to the healthcare sector, and systemic surveillance is necessary to ensure good health and well-being.Keywords: Antibiotic resistance, MARi, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, T3SS exo genes, Virulence

Introduction

The global emergency of antimicrobial resistance is intensifying over time. The Centers for Disease Control (CDC) Antibiotic Resistant Threats Report indicated that ∼2.8 million infections and >35,000 deaths occurred per annum in the United States,1,2 and 700,000 globally.3 Pseudomonas aeruginosa is found in 30–50% of opportunistic nosocomial infections, with an increase in mortality of up to 48%.4 The CDC reported significant findings on multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains of P. aeruginosa, which pose public health concerns and contribute to 13% of hospital-acquired infections, resulting in a global crisis.5 P. aeruginosa is a particular risk due to its drug-resistant behavior, with multiple types of intrinsic and acquired resistance that can inactivate various classes of antibiotics. The common antibiotic resistance modes include alteration of target sites, attenuation of membrane permeability, efflux pumps, biofilm formation, and quorum sensing.6 The treatment barrier is primarily due to the high prevalence of multiple drug resistance in commonly encountered strains of hospital-acquired P. aeruginosa.2,4 P. aeruginosa strains that harbor genes encoding numerous virulence factors are prone to requiring higher generations of antibiotics, disseminating MDR organisms,7 and further issues such as aggravating the spread of extremely drug resistant (XDR) strains.8 The difficulty of treating Pseudomonas-associated infections by using antipseudomonal drugs has increased the economic burden, mortality, morbidity, and long-term critical hospitalization, and only a limited class of drugs are treatment options for MDR and XDR infections.9

Pseudomonas infections involve a complex interplay among diverse virulence traits, including structural virulence factors that contribute to adherence to host cells, where P. aeruginosa utilizes Type IV pili, flagella, and lipopolysaccharides.10 This interplay among virulence factors facilitates escape from host defenses and triggers various systemic diseases.11 A notable virulence trait that co-occurs with other virulence factors is the type 3 secretion system (T3SS), which produces toxins, including four distinct effectors that are injected into the host cell via T3SS: ExoS, ExoT, ExoU, and ExoY. P. aeruginosa outcompetes other common hospital-acquired pathogens due to its ability to evade the host immune system and cause tissue damage via T3SS effectors, including the exoU gene, which facilitates active tissue damage. The exoS and exoT genes cause cell death, leading to colonization and dissemination, posing a significant infection risk. Moreover, exoY escalates intracellular signal messengers, resulting in their propagation and leading to the risk of septic shock.12, 13, 14

Several previous studies focused on molecular screening for virulotyping different P. aeruginosa strains, but the extent of screening varies among different demographic territories,15 and limited data are available for Asian countries.5 Moreover, the epidemiological surveillance of MDR P. aeruginosa as well as drug resistance and the interplay of T3SS proteins requires complete genotypic evaluation due to the poor prognosis caused by MDR P. aeruginosa infection-related comorbidities.16

In the present study, we aimed to determine the distribution and correlations between T3SS profiles with different combinations of exoS, exoT, exoU, and exoY, and drug resistance to evaluate their relationships with P. aeruginosa susceptibility in order to facilitate the identification of effective treatment regimens for these infections. This study meets the objective of Goal 3 of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) to improve and promote appropriate health and well-being for global impacts.

Materials and Methods

Sampling and phenotypic characterization

In total, 102 Pseudomonas isolates were collected from wounds, deep tissues, and blood samples at a local pathological laboratory in Karachi, Pakistan, between January 2024 and October 2024. P. aeruginosa strains were confirmed by Gram staining and conventional methods based on colonial morphology on Pseudomonas isolation agar (Oxoid). Pigment production, motility, and biochemical tests were performed according to the standard microbiological protocol.17 Among 102 strains, 44 strains were phenotypically confirmed by using QTS-24 rapid test strips (DESTO).18 These strains were further confirmed by molecular characterization using species-specific 16S rRNA. The species-specific primers were: forward 5′-TGCCTGGTAGTGGGGGATAA-3′ and reverse 5′-GGATGCAGTTCCCAGGTTGA-3′.19 The PCR mixes contained DreamTaq green master mix (12.5 μL), DNA sample (5 μL), reverse and forward primers (1 μL each), and the volume was made up using nuclease-free water to 25 μL.20 The PCR reactions conditions in the thermal cycler were as described by Shaebth et al.19 with some modifications, i.e., initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The products were separated and observed on 1.5% agarose gels with a 100 bp reference ladder. The DNA amplified bands were visualized under a benchtop ultraviolet transilluminator (Uvitec, Cambridge, UK).19

Antibiotic efficacy profile

Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method

The Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion standardized method21 was employed for AST using Muller–Hinton agar (OXOID), with an exponential phase growth culture of P. aeruginosa.22 The antipseudomonal antibiotics from different classes of antimicrobial agents were selected for Pseudomonas aeruginosa according to previous studies. The antibiotic disks (OXOID) used were: aminoglycoside (gentamicin, CN), carbapenems (imipenem, IPM), meropenem (MEM), ertapenem (ETP), penicillin (piperacillin, PRL), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, CIP), third generation cephalosporin (ceftazidime, CAZ), fourth generation cephalosporin (cefepime, FEP), monobactam (aztreonam, AZT), tetracycline/glycylcyclines (tigecycline, TGC), polymyxin (colistin, CT) and sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, SXT).23 AST was evaluated based on categories of sensitive (S), intermediate (I), and resistant (R) according to the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines 2021.24 Furthermore, zone of inhibition measurements were performed to categorize as MDR and XDR.25

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of CT

The MIC of CT was assessed for P. aeruginosa according to interpretative standards in the CLSI guidelines, where a reported MIC breakpoint of ≥4 μg/mL indicates resistant and ≤2 μg/mL is considered intermediate. However, there are no criteria for interpreting the sensitivity to CT in the guidelines. Briefly, a log-phase culture of P. aeruginosa in Muller–Hinton Broth (OXOID) was adjusted to McFarland standard 0.5. Four serial dilutions of CT sulfate salt (Sigma, Aldrich) were prepared at concentrations of 2, 4, 8, and 16 μg/mL in sterile distilled water. MIC testing was conducted in triplicate using 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates. After incubation, the results were interpreted using an ELISA reader (DiaSource) at a wavelength of 600 nm.26,27

Multiple antibiotic resistance index (MARi) evaluation

MDR and XDR strains of P. aeruginosa were evaluated by using the rapid, comprehensive, and economical MARi method to aid the identification of high-risk resistant strains.28 The results were assessed using the formula: MARi = a/b, which is the ratio of the number antibiotics that the isolates resisted (a) relative to the total number of antibiotics evaluated (b). A significant MARI value >0.2 reflected the high risk of resistant bacteria evaluated in the present study.25 Furthermore, strains were classified into subgroups based on the number of resistant antibiotics included in a category and the calculated ratios.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from P. aeruginosa strains by using the boiling method, with some minor changes compared with previous studies. Briefly, a few colonies were collected from an overnight-grown culture and suspended in 500 μL of nuclease-free water, before heating at 100 °C in a preset water bath for 10 min, followed by immediate cooling at −20 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was collected as the purified DNA sample. The yield was evaluated by using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).17

Molecular characterization of exo virulotypes

PCR was implemented for virulotyping the exoS, exoT, exoU, and exoY genes.29 The details of the primer sequences are shown in Table 1. The PCR mixture of 25 μL comprised 1 μL each of the forward and reverse primers, 12.5 μL master mix, 5 μL of template DNA, and the final volume was made up by adding nuclease-free water. The thermal cycle conditions for all exo genes were as reported by Khodayary et al.,29 with 30 repeated cycles consisting of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, and annealing at 68 °C for 1 min, followed by 72 °C for 1 min and 7 min for primer extension and final extension, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data were collated using Microsoft Excel version 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive analyses of AST results and exo gene prevalence quantitative values were reported as frequencies and percentage ratios. Furthermore, MARi occurrences were evaluated based on calculations and respective percentages. Correlations between exo gene virulotypes and MARi incidence rates were assessed based on bivariate analyses (cross-tabulations) using SPSS software version 20 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The Cramér's coefficient test (V2) was used to identify significant differences between two categorical variables, with values between 0 and 1, where 0 indicates no association and 1 denotes a potential association. The interpretations were categorized as weak ≤0.2, moderate 0.2 ≤ 0.6, or strong >0.6.30 Data were visualized using sunburst and box and whisker plots with Microsoft Office Excel version 2019 (Microsoft Corporation).

AST results

The resistance ratios among the P. aeruginosa isolates were high against the antibiotics tested in the present study, as shown in Figure 4A–C. Drug resistance was significant and up to 100% for CT, as well as 82% for TGC and AZT, and 73% for SXT. CT and TGC are the last resort treatments for P. aeruginosa infections, and we observed high levels of resistance to these drugs. High resistance was found to the β-lactam antipseudomonal antibiotics FEP and CAZ with 68% and 50%, respectively.

The carbapenem MEM had the highest sensitivity of 84%, followed by PRL with 66%, ETP with 64%, and IPM with 59%. Moderate sensitivity was recorded for CIP and CN, with 57% and 45%, respectively. Moreover, resistance to CT was high, i.e., 73% in P. aeruginosa strains at concentrations ≥4 μg/mL. However, some strains had intermediate sensitivity, with 27% at ≤ 2 μg/mL, where the sensitivity was interpreted in accordance with the CLSI guidelines,26,27 as shown in Figure 4C.

MARi evaluations

Among the 102 phenotypic resistotypes of P. aeruginosa tested in this study, 44 resistant strains were evaluated by MARi to assess the presence of MDR and XDR. Strains with resistance to ≥1 agent in ≥3 antimicrobial categories were classified as MDR, and XDR strains were susceptible to ≤2 categories.25,31

Ten major MAR groups were categorized based on the number of antibiotics with resistance various patterns. These groups were further classified into subgroups according to the different combinations of antibiotic resistance in that group. Overall, 31 groups and subgroups were included in the study, as shown in Table 2 and Figure 5A,B. High incidences of >0.2 to 0.92 were observed, and identified as MDR for 80.6% (25/31) and XDR for 16% (6/31), and 3% (1/31) were recorded as non-MDR with a MAR index value <0.2 at 0.16.

Discussion

In recent decades, the virulent behavior of P. aeruginosa has been closely linked to its drug resistance.32 Emerging MDR/XDR clones are frequently likely to cause prolonged hospitalization, where it is difficult to treat infections, leading to significant mortality and morbidity.33 Pakistan is a low and middle-income country (LMIC) with high levels of antibiotic abuse due to noncompliance and unjustified drug usage by doctors and patients, leading to the increased spread of resistance.34

Our results showed that CT resistance was recorded in up to 100% (AST) of strains and MIC was 73% at ≥ 4 μg/mL, indicating a high level of emerging CT resistance. Previous studies have suggested that increasing resistance to last resort drugs such as CT has emerged in different groups of Gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli35 and P. aeruginosa, leading to treatment failure, and up to 97% resistance was reported in a study conducted in Pakistan.36 Increasing resistance has been observed during the past decade in Acinetobacter baumannii and P. aeruginosa.37 All of these increases are significant indicators of irrational drug usage. However, contrasting findings have also been reported by Jafari et al.38, highlighting lower CT resistance, i.e., levels of 9%, 21.3%, and 6.3%, with varying results in different global regions. Resistance to TGC was reported as 90% in India,39 and we obtained a similar result of 82%. We found high resistance to CT and TGC in Pakistan, which is the third largest consumer of antibiotics, and this high consumption has exacerbated resistance to the last line of treatment drug options. AZT resistance was found in 82% and higher than that in other studies, with 45.2% in China40 and 34% in Egypt41 The rates for resistance to FEP and CAZ were similar at 68% and 50%, respectively. Similar percentages were reported in a study conducted in KSA, with 64–78% resistance to FEP and 47–68% to CAZ, and 87% in Iran.42 The rate of resistance to PRL was 34% in the present study, and another study found resistance in 23.8% of P. aeruginosa isolates.43 Roulová et al.44 found resistance to MEM in 27.2% of strains, and we found resistance in 16%, with a maximum optimistic sensitivity of 84% for a successful treatment option.

The inclusion of ETP, SXT, and TGC was based on routine laboratory testing panels, even though intrinsic resistance is found to these drugs in P. aeruginosa. Our inclusion of these drugs with other classes of antibiotics may have been a limitation of this study. ETP resistance profiles are available in few previous studies of ETP in P. aeruginosa, although Santos et al.45 reported increased susceptibility in 80%, and we observed sensitivity in 64%. Their result for IPM was similar to our finding of resistance in 41%.46 We found SXT resistance in 74% in the study group, and a previous study found resistance in 67.74%. The findings for CIP and CN of 43% and 55% resistance, respectively, support those obtained by Ramsay et al.47 in China with 37.5% and 45.2% resistance to CIP and CN. Comparable results were observed by Santella et al.48 (2019) in Italy with resistance to CIP in 41.8% and CN in 12.3%.

The MAR index evaluation results were similar to those reported in previous studies, with around 80.5% MDR and a high MAR index in the range of 0.33–0.86.49 According to Woh et al.,25 higher incidences of MARi >0.2. were observed with 0.2–0.92. These MARi data clearly indicate that the illegitimate use of antibiotics has exacerbated the incidence of resistance in Pakistan, especially due to easy access to over-the-counter medications.

The relationships between MDR and T3SS virulotypes have been evaluated in previous studies due to their significant roles in pathogenicity and the dissemination of infections.50 The four known potential effector proteins that act as virulence factors associated with T3SS are encoded by exoS, exoT, exoU, and exoY. These proteins are involved in maintaining host cytoskeletal organization and signal transduction-type functions in cells.13

The T3SS profiles obtained in our study showed that the predominance of exoS genes was 43% in the study strains, followed by exoT in 30%, exoU in 13%, and exoY in 9%. In contrast to our findings, a study in Peshawar, Pakistan, found a higher incidence of exoT in 100%, as well as exoY in 88.46%, exoU in 57.68%, and exoS in 38.46%.51 However, a similar study in Iran found that the prevalence was highest for exoS at 55% and then exoU at 56%.17 Another study conducted in Portugal (2022) showed that the prevalence was 100% for exoS, exoT, and exoY, and the prevalence of exoU was minimal among the study strains.52

Moreover, comparison of the relationships between T3SS exo genes and MARi groups with other studies showed that exoT was most prevalent in various regions at 86%, followed by exoY at 61%, and exoU+ and exoS + coexistence at 64%.53 Similar study reported a high prevalence of exoS genes, as found in our results (64%), with rates of 67.60% and 62.4% in Iran and Bulgaria, respectively.17 Variations have been observed in the existence and coexistence of T3SS effector proteins, including exoS, exoT, exoY, and exoU genes. This diversity is due to geographical and regional heterogeneity, and could readily lead to dissemination across the world through humans, water sources, and animal husbandry.

Conclusions

In this study, we found significant associations between T3SS-associated virulence factors and multidrug resistance to antibiotics, specifically against P. aeruginosa. These data reflect the critical rises in MDR strains in the community and excessive antibiotic use. We found significant correlations between the virulence and resistance profiles in Pakistan, and this geographic distribution may be important for the spread of MDR/XDR P. aeruginosa strains across borders to neighboring countries, and possibly also affect the Middle East region. Our findings support the objective of Sustainable Development Goal (SDG-3) to improve overall health and well-being, and to strengthen the quality of life globally.
