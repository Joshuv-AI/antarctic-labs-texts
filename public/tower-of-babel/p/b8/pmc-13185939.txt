Real-time eco-driving strategy for intelligent connected vehicles under mixed traffic environments
Authors: Likang Fan, Yuhong Cai, Bin Zhou, Hongqian Wei, Bo Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEco-driving in mixed traffic environments faces challenges in real-time multi-objective optimization. This article proposes a hierarchical adaptive multi-intersection eco-approach and departure (AM-EAD) strategy. The upper layer generates a speed profile for multiple intersections by integrating information of intersections and preceding vehicles constraints. Especially, for car-following, a safe distance is determined using a variable time headway (VTH) model with a Kalman filter. The lower layer employs model predictive control (MPC) to coordinate speed tracking, car-following, and parking control via predictive horizon adaptation, achieving multi-objective optimization while improving computational efficiency. Validation is performed using a co-simulation platform integrated with real-world road data. Hundred randomized simulations show that compared to isolated intersection EAD (I-EAD) and rule-based EAD (R-EAD) benchmarks, AM-EAD reduces energy consumption (EC) by 8.39% and 19.55% and shortens travel time by 1.89% and 8.00%. Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) experiments confirm real-time performance with an average computation time of 8.21 ms.Subject areas: computer simulation, industrial ecology

Introduction

Due to the presence of signalized intersections, the driving status of vehicle needs to be frequently adjusted in mixed traffic environments.1 Eco-driving utilizes the vehicle road coordination technology of intelligent connected vehicles (ICVs) to optimize driving routes and speed profiles based on real-time traffic data, reducing travel time and EC.2 Eco-driving at signalized intersections, also termed eco-approach and departure (EAD), has been extensively studied by numerous researchers in recent years.3,4 To assess ecological driving, Zhang et al.5 proposed an innovative analytical method based on driving behavior profiles. Without considering traffic interference, the EAD strategy can be classified based on the number of signal lights and intersections, namely isolated intersection EAD (I-EAD) and multiple intersections EAD (M-EAD).

The I-EAD strategy which optimizes vehicle approach and departure maneuvers employs vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication to acquire signal phase and timing (SPaT) information. This leads to the elimination of unnecessary stops and speed variations, thereby enhancing overall vehicle energy efficiency. For example, Rakha et al.6 utilized V2X to obtain SPaT information and then proposed an optimization algorithm to determine the speed trajectory. Zhang et al.7 planned acceleration/decel-eration strategy based on the NSGA-II algorithm to obtain speed profiles and used real-world driving data to verify the effectiveness of the strategy. Similarly, a risk-based adaptive cruise control was presented in Ma et al.8 to reduce speed fluctuations caused by traffic signals. Additionally, based on reinforcement learning (RL), Jayawardena et al.9 proposed the I-EAD, which has generality in different traffic scenes while reducing EC. Building upon these developments, I-EAD strategies can be broadly categorized into single-vehicle optimization and multi-vehicle cooperative optimization. Single-vehicle optimization relies solely on the state of ego vehicle and roadside constraints, whereas multi-vehicle coordination entails cooperative optimization among multiple vehicles.

Within single-vehicle I-EAD, two scenes are noteworthy: the intelligent transportation systems (ITSs) scene and the wireless charging lane (WCL) scene. In ITS, the I-EAD strategy not only optimizes vehicle states but also controls traffic signals, thereby facilitating smooth intersection traversal and reduced EC. To simultaneously optimize traffic signals and vehicle speeds, the authors of Xu et al.10 developed a bi-level collaborative optimization algorithm based on enumeration and pseudospectral methods. Sun et al.11 proposed an eco-driving strategy that combined a motion wave model with a car-following model. This strategy resulted in reduced energy consumption and improved both traffic flow and average travel speed at signalized intersections. Correspondingly, to improve intersection traffic efficiency, Ahmad et al.12 adopted a dual-layer framework to achieve eco-driving in mixed traffic scenes. The authors of Li et al.13 designed an adaptive signal control and ICVs coordination system based on RL, achieving a maximum reduction of travel time by 37.33% and EC by 15.95% compared with existing methods. In the studies mentioned above, both SPaT and vehicle are controlled simultaneously. However, with the emergence of WCL, I-EAD needs to extend the stay of vehicles at intersections to utilize WCL for charging. In response, Zhang et al.14 proposed a wireless eco-driving strategy, results showed that the travel cost can be saved by up to 16%. Ren et al.15 employed the twin delayed deep deterministic policy gradient to plan real-time speed profiles, which enhanced traffic efficiency and enabled vehicles to fully utilize WCL to extend their driving range. Meanwhile, Xiao et al.16 developed a cooperative hybrid platoon eco-driving strategy that incorporated WCL, providing theoretical support for the future deployment of WCL systems. Nonetheless, the aforementioned I-EAD strategies primarily tend to focus on static influencing factors from the roadside, yet in real-world environments, the interference from human-driven vehicles (HVs) should not be overlooked.

In multi-vehicle I-EAD, a conventional approach to handling HV interference is to maintain a safe distance. For example, Bae et al.17 designed a velocity tracking MPC to avoid frontal collisions and violations, ensuring driving safety while reducing EC. Nie et al.18 developed a predictive cruise control system based on a dual-layer MPC, which separately handles intersection traffic and maintains a safe car-following distance. In simulations, the system achieved energy saving rates of 8.5%–15.6%. Besides, Dong et al.19 developed an event-triggered energy-efficient driving control strategy that explicitly incorporates constraints from both preceding vehicles and traffic signals, thereby enhancing its adaptability to diverse traffic scenarios. Although these methods ensure safety, the ego vehicle may exhibit poor energy consumption performance. By fusing predictions of queue release times, Dong et al.20 designed an enhanced eco-approach control strategy that considers the preceding vehicle queue. Yu et al.21 predicted the trajectory of HV and then optimized the trajectory of ego vehicle using MPC and Pontryagin’s minimum principle. Directly incorporating the uncertainty of the preceding vehicles into the constraints, Li et al.22 proposed an eco-driving algorithm based on stochastic MPC, and confirmed its effectiveness through simulations and road tests. Bai et al.23 developed an eco-driving approach based on rules and deep reinforcement learning. Compared to model-based methods, this approach reduced EC by 12.70% and travel time by 11.75%. Zhang et al.24 proposed a platoon control framework based on hybrid MPC, which effectively addressed the issues of traffic efficiency and EC in hybrid platoons. Yang et al.25 proposed an autonomous ecological driving strategy based on three popular continuous-action RL algorithms, optimizing the overall driving performance of vehicles. In addition, numerous researchers have also explored intersection queues, such as Dong et al.26 proposed integrating queue constraints with virtual traffic lights; Li et al.27 considered traffic uncertainty through a queue flow model; and Xiao et al.28 predicted the impact of HVs to optimize speed trajectories; Liu et al.29 used improved K-means method to classify driving intention. While the ego vehicle is in a congested environment, Guo et al.30 simultaneously considered longitudinal speed optimization and lateral lane-change selection to avoid congested lanes. Similarly, Dong et al.31 presented an overtaking-enabled approach control strategy to minimize the EC and travel delays of vehicles, with a reported 20.91% reduction in EC compared with the constant speed control strategy.

I-EAD has demonstrated significant effectiveness in isolated intersection scenes by leveraging V2X information. However, its optimization capabilities face challenges in complex urban road networks with dense traffic signals, thereby spurring a critical need for M-EAD which offers broader coverage and a higher level of coordination. Unlike I-EAD, M-EAD focuses on the overall complexity of multi-intersection systems but can similarly be categorized into single-vehicle and multi-vehicle.

In single-vehicle M-EAD, the core objective is to plan a globally optimal speed trajectory across a network of multiple intersections, maximizing the use of SPaT information for energy savings. For example, Dong et al.32 utilized the A∗ algorithm to implement green window planning for multiple intersections, and then employed iterative dynamic programming (DP) to obtain the optimal speed trajectory. Li et al.27 used RL to optimize speed trajectories at multi-intersection after assessing the impact of traffic uncertainty. Similarly, Liu et al.33 introduced a dual-layer ecological driving algorithm, which performs global optimization of speed and power systems at the strategic level and locally employs an MPC framework to achieve precise speed tracking and powertrain regulation, exhibiting significantly enhanced energy consumption performance over benchmark algorithms. Zhang et al.34 designed a hierarchical speed optimization strategy based on hybrid MPC utilizing collaborative V2X technology to reduce EC of vehicles at consecutive intersections. In addition, Pan et al.35 proposed an M-EAD that took bus stops into account and evaluated the effectiveness of the strategy based on actual driving data, achieving a maximum energy consumption reduction of 31.19%. Jia et al.36 proposed an ecological driving strategy for continuous signal intersections that supports lane changing, which not only reduces energy consumption but also improves traffic efficiency by 1.68%. Although the single-vehicle M-EAD improves the EC performance of vehicles under multi-intersection conditions, it does not consider the interference from random traffic flows, resulting in limited universality.

Building upon single-vehicle M-EAD, multi-vehicle M-EAD addresses traffic flows interference through methods such as prediction, constrained optimization or cooperative control, seeking a balance between EC and safety in different scenes. Zhang et al.37 demonstrated a strategy based on the explicit dynamic traffic model to achieve a balance between EC and efficiency. Ding et al.38 developed an M-EAD strategy based on constrained RL, which outperformed traditional methods in random scenes at multi-intersection. Ma et al.39 constructed an eco-cooperative adaptive cruise control that integrates eco-driving and vehicle following, enhancing the energy efficiency of ICVs. By extending the optimization target from a single vehicle to a vehicle platoon, coordinated cooperation within the fleet can yield systemic energy-saving benefits far exceeding single-vehicle optimization. Ma et al.40 exhibited an eco-cooperative adaptive cruise control based on nonlinear MPC (NMPC) to minimize the EC of the CAVs platoon. Wang et al.41 proposed a collaborative ecological driving system based on the CAVs role transformation protocol model to optimize fleet EC. Nie et al.42 used an ecological collaborative optimization strategy based on gradient MPC using the fast projection gradient method and conventional MPC, generating a safe and optimal speed trajectory for the vehicle fleet. Taking into account factors such as dynamic models, spacing strategies and traffic timing, Wang et al.43 proposed an eco-driving strategy for mixed-fleet vehicles at multiple intersections. During the testing process, all vehicles passed through intersections with the lowest energy consumption, achieving a 9.4% reduction in energy consumption. M-EAD is designed for optimization and application in multi-intersection scenes. Although its research coverage remains limited in specific contexts, it demonstrates notably superior energy efficiency over I-EAD in dense signalized urban traffic environments.

Both I-EAD and M-EAD involve processing a substantial number of constraints, posing severe challenges to the real-time performance of the algorithms. To enhance the real-time performance of the I-EAD algorithm, Jia et al.44 proposed an ecological driving method based on spatial-domain NMPC. The simulation results aligned with receding horizon DP (RH-DP) and enabled real-time plan execution. Zhang et al.45 designed a lightweight regression model and an approximate optimization algorithm to enhance the real-time performance of I-EAD. Meng et al.46 derived a real-time analytical solution to optimize vehicle speed based on a dual-objective optimal control model, which enable vehicles to pass through intersections without stopping. Moreover, Hu et al.47 applied an eco-drive strategy based on optimal control to predict feasible time windows for dynamic traffic intersections, which enhanced fuel economy and yielded an average computation time of 12 ms. For M-EAD real-time issues, Yang et al.48 introduced a modular and scalable system that optimized vehicle trajectories through multiple intersections, which enabled deployment in large-scale networks without a rise in computational complexity. Liu et al.49 proposed a bi-level convex optimization method to decouple speed planning and energy management, which produced results similar to DP but at a much lower computational cost. Li et al.50 employed a dual-delay deep deterministic policy gradient agent to plan vehicle speed in real-time, which significantly reduced EC. Lei et al.51 studied dynamic inverse hierarchical optimization M-EAD which integrated SPaT data and road data to plan vehicle speed, resulting in a 7.43% improvement in fuel economy and a reduction in computation time. Through the integration of MPC and DRL, Leng et al.52 conducted an eco-driving strategy that accounted for the global reference trajectory and achieved up to 23.6% energy savings with high computational efficiency.

In summary, current EAD research predominantly focuses on isolated signalized intersections or short-duration continuous signalized intersections within specific scenes. When extended to complex traffic environments, the increased nonlinear constraints impose a significant computational burden, thus hindering practical implementation. To address these limitations, this paper proposes an AM-EAD algorithm, which considers the SPaT information, the state of the ego-vehicle, the preceding vehicle and road speed limits, achieves a balanced trade-off among vehicle EC, travel time, driving comfort and safety with minimal computational overhead.

The proposed method differs from existing studies in the following aspects: compared to M-EAD and eco-CACC, it targets the relatively complex scenario of consecutive multi-intersection mixed traffic flow; compared to conventional NMPC, it adopts a scenario-driven lightweight predictive control architecture to balance accuracy and efficiency; and compared to RL-based methods, its deterministic optimization mechanism based on an analytical model avoids dependence on data training, enhancing reliability and deployment convenience.

Problem formulation and control framework

Problem formulation

When applied in urban traffic scenes, EAD faces three challenges: (1) non-uniform distribution of signalized intersections; (2) interference from stochastic traffic flow; and (3) real-time algorithmic requirements.

As illustrated in Figure 2, the R-EAD exhibits a pronounced stop-and-go pattern between signalized intersections P1 (signalized intersection 1) and P3 (signalized intersection 3), leading to increased EC and reduced ride comfort. The I-EAD incorporates constraints from the upcoming signalized intersection, enabling smoother transit through the intersection. However, the speed profile between P1 and P2 still presents opportunities for further optimization.

Typical EAD and AM-EAD vehicle trajectories

In contrast, the AM-EAD simultaneously considers multi-intersection scenes and interference from surrounding vehicles. Compared to the I-EAD algorithm, AM-EAD yields a smoother speed profile for the ego vehicle between intersection P1 and intersection P3, while mitigating the impact of other vehicles on its velocity trajectory. And through the design of a lightweight algorithm, AM-EAD can meet the real-time requirements.

Detail of control strategy

Definition of traffic scenes

This section elaborates on the rule-based driving scene segmentation algorithm in Figure 3B, encompassing the segmentation flowchart (as illustrated in Figure 4A) and driving scene diagram (as illustrated in Figure 4B).

Six typical urban driving scenes

Due to the significant differences in characteristics across different scenarios, it is easy to use rule-based methods for classification, and the computational burden of rule-based methods is minimal. Based on the characteristics of the six aforementioned driving scenes, this paper extracts five unique parameters to divide the scenes, including dpre, dlimit, D1, vpre, v0 (for details, refer to nomenclature). The processing of pass will be introduced in section optimal speed window. Then, customized optimization strategies are applied for different types of traffic scenes.

The traffic scenes encountered by vehicles in urban road driving can be categorized as follows:

Scene A: dpre ≤ dlimit, D1 < 0, and vpre ≥ v0; or dpre > dlimit and D1 < 0;

Scene B: dpre ≤ dlimit, D1 < 0, and vpre < v0;

Scene C: dpre > dlimit and D1 > 0;

Scene D: dpre ≤ dlimit, D1 > 0, and D1 ≤ dpre;

Scene E: dpre ≤ dlimit, D1 > 0, D1 > dpre and the preceding vehicles can pass intersection no stop;

Scene F: dpre ≤ dlimit, D1 > 0, D1 > dpre, and the preceding vehicles are required to stop at intersection.

During experiments, velocity and position weights were adjusted dynamically according to the tracking mode, while the remaining weights were determined during model tuning.

Algorithm verification

To evaluate the performance of the proposed AM-EAD algorithm, a simulation environment was constructed using MATLAB/Simulink + CarSim + Prescan on a PC equipped with an Intel Core i5-12600KF processor and 16 GB RAM for simulation testing. Subsequently, HIL experiments were conducted by replacing CarSim with physical hardware. The experiments simulated a real urban traffic environment based on Tianfu Third Street in Wuhou District, Chengdu, featuring a 3,500-meter road section containing nine non-uniformly distributed signalized intersections. The maximum spacing between intersections was 660 m, while the minimum was 276 m (Figure 6A). Intersection spacing, timing and phase information were acquired from map data (Table 1). Each intersection was equipped with V2X devices transmitting SPaT information to the ego vehicle, with an effective communication range of 800 m. The algorithm’s considered signalized intersection range Dc was set to 800 m, the maximum number of considered intersections was three, and the ego vehicle’s MMW radar detection range was 200 m.

Baseline algorithm

To evaluate the performance of the proposed AM-EAD algorithm, a comparative analysis was conducted against two benchmark algorithms: R-EAD and I-EAD. In the R-EAD strategy, the ego vehicle approaches and departs intersections at a constant cruising speed, set equivalent to the average speed under the AM-EAD strategy. When encountering a red light, the vehicle decelerates at a predefined rate until complete stop; upon signal transition to green, it accelerates at a preset rate to traverse the intersection. The I-EAD strategy makes decisions solely based on traffic signal information at the immediate intersection, while maintaining identical control logic to AM-EAD for other operations. Both R-EAD and I-EAD strategies instantly activate car-following mode upon detecting preceding vehicle spacing below the safety threshold to maintain safe distances, utilizing the same control algorithm as AM-EAD. Across all three algorithms, the autonomous vehicle’s initial and terminal speeds were zero, requiring acceleration from standstill and deceleration to stop at designated locations.

100 random simulation experiments for EC and travel time reduction

As shown in Figures 7A and 7B, the energy efficiency improvements and travel time reductions achieved by AM-EAD exhibit dispersion under randomized traffic conditions. This aligns with practical observations where eco-driving potential varies across different traffic scenes. In certain specific scenes, the R-EAD strategy coincidentally traversed all intersections without stopping, yet still exhibited higher EC than AM-EAD. This indicates that gradual acceleration and deceleration of AM-EAD strategy effectively reduce energy expenditure.

When benchmarked against the I-EAD strategy, AM-EAD demonstrated maximum and minimum energy efficiency improvements of 17% and 0% (very few scenes) respectively in randomized simulations. This reveals that while AM-EAD shows no efficiency gain over I-EAD in limited scenes, it delivers superior performance enhancements in most operational contexts.

Regarding travel time, AM-EAD generally reduces journey duration compared to I-EAD. This stems from its ability to incorporate multi-intersection information for proactive speed adjustments, enabling efficient traversal of signalized intersections. The reduction is more pronounced versus R-EAD, where frequent stop-and-go patterns inherently prolong travel time.

The energy consumption variance of AM-EAD (1,804.03) is notably lower than that of I-EAD (2,242.39) and R-EAD (7,315.88), indicating more stable performance across 100 randomized experiments.

Based on the t-distribution, the 95% confidence intervals for energy reduction are [7.76%, 9.01%] (vs. I-EAD) and [18.29%, 20.81%] (vs. R-EAD). The narrow intervals suggest high estimation precision. A one-tailed paired t test confirms statistical significance (p < 0.05), verifying that the improvement is due to the algorithm design rather than random variation.

In summary, the proposed AM-EAD algorithm leverages environmental information to generate smooth speed trajectories through planning and control. This approach enhances energy efficiency and ride comfort without extending travel duration.

Hardware in the loop experiment

To further validate the performance of AM-EAD, HIL experiments were conducted. The connection relationships and operational logic among components are illustrated in Figure 8. The traffic scene and AM-EAD algorithm were implemented on a PC, with computational results transmitted to an industrial control computer via ROS topic communication within SIMULINK. During operation, it connected with the chassis domain controller (CDC) via CAN bus to transmit control commands for vehicle actuation.

Hardware in the loop experiment

The experimental procedure initiated by launching the ROS master node on the industrial control computer, followed by registering the ROS node of PC to the master node. This established a data exchange link between the two devices, enabling hardware-in-loop experimentation upon algorithm execution on the PC.

The experimental scene replicated the co-simulation setup. AM-EAD algorithm results demonstrated substantial alignment between simulation and experimental outcomes (Figure 9). Calculation time

The computational duration of AM-EAD varies with environmental complexity: the upper-layer speed planning algorithm dynamically considers diverse constraints, while the lower-layer MPC algorithm adapts its computation step size and prediction horizon based on vehicle state. Nevertheless, the overall computation time remains low, with AM-EAD accounting for only 20.56% of the total, demonstrating good real-time performance and meeting the requirements for real-time control.

Discussion

This paper designs a real-time eco-driving strategy for handling mixed traffic scenes at consecutive signalized intersections, conducting extensive randomized simulations and HIL validation. The strategy enhances vehicle energy economy and driving comfort while ensuring traffic efficiency and safety. In hardware-in-loop experiments, the algorithm demonstrated favorable real-time performance with an average computation time of 8.21 ms.

Through hierarchical algorithm design, precise driving scene classification triggers distinct planning algorithms, improving adaptability. Incorporating relevant intersection data based on relative positioning between vehicles and traffic signals enhances throughput efficiency and speed profile smoothness at consecutive signalized intersections. Results indicate that the proposed AM-EAD strategy reduces average EC by 8.39% and decreases average travel time by 1.89% compared to the existing I-EAD strategy.

Limitations of the study

Although consideration of queue effects prevents car-following behavior at signalized intersections, vehicles still encounter car-following scenes in non-signalized areas. Experimental data analysis revealed significant EC during car-following operations due to suboptimal speed profiles.

Furthermore, the experimental scenarios in this study remain limited in terms of the number and behavioral randomness of interfering vehicles. Future research will focus on integrating the strategy with path planning to further optimize energy consumption, while also striving to extend validation to higher traffic density and more complex mixed traffic environments to verify the algorithm’s generalizability and robustness.

Method details

Method framework

The AM-EAD framework proposed in this paper is illustrated in Figures 3, 4 and 5. The framework begins by acquiring data through V2X, millimeter-wave radar, and GPS. A rule-based algorithm then classifies mixed traffic into six basic scenes. For each scene, tailored optimization algorithms define control objectives and constraints. Finally, MPC refines the velocity trajectory to deliver real-time eco-driving commands.

Experiments

To evaluate the proposed AM-EAD algorithm, simulation and hardware-in-the-loop experiments were conducted. The simulation platform was built using MATLAB/Simulink, CarSim, and PreScan, based on real road data from Tianfu Third Street in Chengdu, featuring a 3500 m section with nine signalized intersections (spacing ranging from 276 m to 660 m). Each intersection broadcasts SPaT information via V2X within an 800 m communication range. The ego vehicle considers up to three intersections ahead and has a radar detection range of 200 m. Zero to four random interfering vehicles (speed 10–20 m/s) are placed ahead, with some programmed to ignore traffic signals to test the ego vehicle’s compliance. The ego vehicle maintains its lane, and vehicle parameters follow the Borgward BX-i7 model. Comparisons are made with R-EAD and I-EAD algorithms.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantifications were performed using data from 100 randomized simulations. Energy consumption and travel time were measured to evaluate algorithm performance. Results are presented as mean ± SD, with variance reported to assess stability. Statistical significance was determined using one-tailed paired t-tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The 95% confidence intervals for energy reduction were calculated based on the t-distribution: [7.76%, 9.01%] versus I-EAD and [18.29%, 20.81%] versus R-EAD. The narrow intervals and low p-values confirm that the observed improvements are attributable to algorithm design rather than random variation.
