Relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception via pathological evidence confirms
Authors: Lung-Huang Lin, Chi-Jung Huang, Cheng-Yu Lo, Yu-Hsien Lee, Yung-Chuan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAlthough some cases of intussusception in older children are associated with pathological changes such as lymphoma or polyps, the cause of most cases in infants is unknown. Several reports have identified an association between adenovirus infection and intussusception in children. However, much of the evidence is indirect, such as stool samples or throat swab data. Our study analysed intestinal tissue, which may be more direct evidence of the relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed children<6 years of age with intussusception who underwent surgery for failed reduction. The pathological tissue was processed into formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections. Adenovirus immunohistochemistry (IHC) and PCR testing were performed to obtain direct evidence of the relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception.ResultsOur study included 29 patients, 27 appendiceal and 8 intestinal tissues. Only eight appendix specimens were successfully processed into FFPE tissue. IHC testing was positive in three cases (37.5%), and PCR testing was positive for adenovirus type C in four cases (50%). The control group consisted of eight children <6 years who underwent incidental appendectomies, and all control subjects had negative IHC and PCR analyses. PCR is as useful and reliable as IHC in diagnosing adenovirus in intussusception and has greater sensitivity than IHC.ConclusionWe directly confirmed the relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception through IHC analysis and PCR detection of pathological evidence. PCR is more sensitive than IHC for diagnosing adenovirus in intussusception.

Introduction

Intussusception is the most common cause of bowel obstruction in children aged 3 months to 6 years. It is a life-threatening condition that occurs when part of the intestine folds like a telescope, with one part slipping into another. This can cause a blockage that prevents digested food from passing through the intestines. If left untreated, it can lead to severe damage to the intestines, intestinal infections, internal bleeding and even peritonitis.1 2

Although some cases of intussusception in older children are associated with pathological changes such as lymphoma or polyps, the cause of most cases in infants is unknown. However, many previous studies have observed that intussusception is related to lymphoid tissue hyperplasia, which may be caused by infections such as adenovirus.3 In addition, there are no studies in Taiwan to date that reveal adenovirus infection and intussusception from a pathological perspective.

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed children <6 years of age with intussusception who underwent surgery for failed reduction. We included 29 patients and obtained 35 pathological specimens. There were 27 appendiceal tissues and 8 intestinal tissues, including 21 patients underwent successful manual reduction of intussusception (‘milking’), only 19 patients had appendiceal tissues preserved. Eight cases underwent intestinal resection due to failure of manual reduction, of which eight patients retained both intestinal tissues and appendiceal tissues. However, only eight cases of appendiceal specimens from incidental appendectomies were successfully processed because most specimens had been stored for many years. We cut the pathological tissue into FFPE sections and performed adenovirus IHC and PCR detection to obtain direct evidence of the relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception.

IHC testing using antiadenoviral antibodies identified three positive cases (37.5%). PCR testing revealed adenovirus type C in four cases (50%). The control group consisted of eight children under 6 years of age who underwent incidental appendectomies without intussusception or appendicitis. None of the control subjects was positive in IHC with antiadenovirus antibody and qPCR analyses.

Thus, in this study, we directly confirmed the relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception by pathological evidence. IHC analysis and PCR testing confirmed evidence of adenovirus infection in patients with intussusception. PCR is as useful and reliable as IHC and has greater sensitivity than IHC in diagnosing adenovirus in intussusception.

Methods

Study participant disposition

We conducted a retrospective analysis of the pathological tissues of children under 6 years old with intussusception who were admitted to Taipei Cathay Hospital from December 1998 to September 2020 due to failed manual reduction. A total of 35 pathological specimens were recorded in the surgical reports. There were 27 appendix tissues and 8 intestinal tissues in the pathological specimens. Due to the long storage time of most specimens, only eight appendix specimens were successfully processed into FFPE tissue. Adenovirus IHC analysis and PCR detection were performed on these specimens. The control group consisted of eight children younger than 6 years of age who underwent incidental appendectomies without intussusception or appendicitis and whose tissues were derived from neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour, lymphoma and hepatoblastoma surgeries. This study analysed the positive rate of adenovirus in intussusception from pathological tissues using IHC and PCR. The pathological tissues were processed into FFPE: 4 µm sections for IHC and 10 µm sections for PCR.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Serial 4 µm paraffin sections were deparaffinised using EZ Prep (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, Arizona). Slides were incubated with antiadenovirus (Bio SB, Santa Barbara, California) at a 1:50 titration for 32 min using the automated Ventana Benchmark XT (Ventana Medical Systems). Markers were detected using the Optiview DAB detection kit (Ventana Medical Systems) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.4 All sections were counterstained with haematoxylin using Ventana reagent. Immunohistochemically stained slides were reviewed and analysed by a pathologist for correlation with histological interpretation.

Deparaffinised FFPE sections (4 µm) were incubated with the diluted antiadenovirus antibody (1:50) (Bio SB). Labelling was detected using the OptiView DAB detection kit (Ventana Medical Systems) according to our previous report.4 5 All sections were counterstained with haematoxylin using Ventana reagent. Immunohistochemically stained slides were reviewed and analysed by a pathologist to determine the correlation with histological interpretation.

Nested PCR for adenovirus species C hexon DNA

After sequence comparison, six subgenus-specific primer pairs HsgA1/HsgA2, HsgB1/HsgB2, HsgC1/HsgC2, HsgD1/HsgD2, HsgE1/HsgE2 and HsgF1/HsgF2 were evaluated, and nested PCR was performed. The subgenus-specific primer pairs used in this study were prepared from formalin-fixed paraffin-packed DNA using the GeneRead DNA FFPE kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, as reported by Pring-Akerblom et al,6 DNA was extracted from 4 µm-thick sections of buried tissue samples.

To amplify viral DNA obtained from FFPE samples, we used a 10 µL reaction mixture containing 2 µL of viral DNA, 0.5 µM of each primer, 1 µL of AmpliTaq Gold 360 Buffer, 2.0 µM of MgCl2 and each dNTP AmpliTaq Gold 360 DNA Polymerase prepared at a concentration of 200 µM and 0.25 units. Thermal cycling was performed for a total of 50 cycles. One cycle consisted of denaturation at 95°C for 60 s, annealing at 40°C for 60 s and primer extension at 72°C for 60 s. In the first cycle, the denaturation step lasted for 600 s at 95°C; in the last cycle, the extension step lasted for 420 s at 72°C. 2 µL of the PCR product from the subgenus-specific primer pair was used as a template for the second step of a nested PCR run using the same subgenus-specific primer pair. This PCR amplification was performed following the same protocol as the first reaction. 10 µL of the final reaction product was analysed on an ethidium bromide-stained 2% agarose gel.

Patient and public involvement

No patient involved.

Statistical analysis

Adenovirus IHC testing using antiadenoviral antibodies identified three positive cases (37.5%) and PCR testing identified adenovirus type C in four cases (50%), compared with eight non-intussusception controls using χ2 test. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (V.22.0). P<0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Discussion

Human adenoviruses are the most common pathogens isolated from cases of paediatric intussusception. However, the specific clinical characteristics of paediatric intussusception associated with adenovirus infection are unclear. Fifty-one recognised human adenovirus serotypes are classified into six types (A–F) based on common organisms, morphological and genetic characteristics, and serotype composition. A given species also tends to be associated with a specific disease syndrome. For example, serotypes 40 and 41 of type F are most commonly associated with acute gastroenteritis in children and adults. By contrast, serotype species C (1, 2, 5 and 6) are most commonly associated with respiratory diseases and conjunctivitis in young children. Research conducted in the USA shows that adenovirus C causes less than 15% of intussusception cases.2 7

Adenoviruses are an important cause of respiratory infections in infants and young children and are associated with a large proportion of intussusception cases. Intussusception is a common cause of bowel obstruction in children younger than 3 years old. Although the incidence of intussusception varies among countries and years, a retrospective study based on data from 1995 to 1964 and 1999 to 2001 found that the incidence of intussusception in Taiwan is approximately 0.35–0.77 per 1000 live births, and the highest incidence is between 12 and 24 months of age.8 9 Most cases of intussusception in children remain idiopathic, and many previous studies have discussed possible causes of intussusception in children. Once a child is infected with adenovirus, swollen lymph nodes and abnormal gastrointestinal motility may develop, leading to intussusception. The most common sites are usually in the small intestine and terminal ileum.10

Since the 1960s, there has been an increasing number of studies on adenovirus infection and intussusception. In 1969, Clarke et al described adenovirus infection in Taiwanese children with intussusception.11 They observed that intussusception in infancy is often associated with adenovirus infection, which tends to support the hypothesis that early intestinal spread of adenovirus in Taipei causes intussusception. In 1992, Bhisitkul et al discovered adenovirus infection and intussusception in children.12 In 2003, Guana et al discovered intussusception associated with adenovirus infection in Mexican children. Using IHC analysis, they found that adenovirus antigen was present in epithelial cells in tissue specimens from 4 of 12 Mexican children (33%) who underwent surgical resection for intussusception. In addition, species C (non-enteric) adenovirus was the most dominant type.13 In 2010, Chen et al studied the epidemiology of childhood intussusception and determinants of recurrence and operation: analysis of national health insurance data between 1998 and 2007 in Taiwan.14 They observed that the family Adenoviridae has been considered the predominant viral pathogen involved in intussusception in recent decades. In 2011, Okimoto et al reported an association of viral isolates from stool samples with intussusception in children.15 In 2016, Ukrapol et al noted adenovirus infection as a potential risk for intussusception in paediatric patients. They pointed out that molecular analysis of adenovirus genomes showed that the adenoviruses detected in patients with intussusception were all type C adenoviruses.16 In 2017, Jang et al discovered an epidemiological correlation between faecal adenovirus subgroups and paediatric intussusception in South Korea.17 In 2023, Tseng et al noted that adenovirus infection is a risk factor for recurrent intussusception in paediatric patients.18 Most of the abovementioned studies have demonstrated an association between adenovirus infection and intussusception in children. However, much of the evidence is indirect, such as throat swab data or stool samples. Our study analysed intestinal tissue, which may provide more direct evidence of the relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception. A team at our hospital demonstrated this relationship by analysing the annual incidence of these two diseases from January 2008 to June 2011, showing similarities in the peak months of both diseases.19 Another study demonstrated the co-occurrence of intussusception and adenovirus infection in identical twins.20

Although storage of paraffin-embedded tissues at 4°C did not protect against nucleic acid degradation, both RT-qPCR and qPCR efficiency were not affected by storage time in both human and rat tissues. For RNA and DNA from PFPE tissue stored at 4°C for up to 9 years, performance was close to or even equivalent to that of nucleic acids from cryopreserved controls, regardless of amplicon length.21 Histopathological characteristics observed in routine H&E staining studies cannot be used to differentiate adenoviral infection from other causes of appendicitis, but H&E staining is often used to predict treatment response, diagnose cancer and determine the likely outcome of the disease.22 Our study revealed approximately the same proportion of stained cells.

Although lymphoid hyperplasia is a non-specific reactive change that may occur anywhere in the intestine, it is more commonly found in the ileum or the distal portion of the rectum. Lymphoid tissue found throughout the submucosa of the small intestine often appears as aggregates of lymphoid follicles called Peyer’s patches. The number of these Peyer’s patches increases in response to stimuli such as viral infection. Lymphatic responses to various stimuli are most active during childhood. Adenoviruses and enteroviruses have been documented as examples of such stimuli.23

We noted that, in addition to mononuclear inflammatory cells and dendritic cells in follicles beneath the lamina propria, adenoviral antigens were also found in epithelial cells (figures13). In addition to being used to detect pathogen antigens in fixed tissue, IHC using monoclonal and multiclonal antibodies can complement fresh tissue PCR and culture for the direct diagnosis of infectious diseases.24 Children without intussusception had no evidence of adenovirus immunostaining or PCR positivity in their tissues. This suggests that positive immunostaining or PCR results occur only in children with active adenovirus infection. Comparison of the extent of lymphoid hyperplasia in affected and unaffected individuals revealed no evidence of lymphoid hyperplasia in the control group.

Intussusception most commonly affects children between 3 months and 3 years of age, with a peak incidence at 5–6 months. Male children are more likely to be affected with a ratio of 2:1.25 Two globally licensed rotavirus vaccines have been associated with a low risk of intussusception in several studies.26 In Taiwan, rotavirus vaccine has been included in the National Immunization Program since 2017. Our study was conducted from 1998 to 2020, and from the medical records, we found that none of our patients had received rotavirus vaccine. Approximately 30% of paediatric patients with intussusception experience a viral illness prior to the onset of intussusception. A close relationship between intussusception and adenovirus infection has been noted in some populations.18 Because our study was so long ago, we could not find out whether the patients had adenovirus infection from the medical records. Previous studies from Asia that examined the use of ultrasound-guided pneumatic reduction achieved a success rate of up to 95%.27 28

In current data, less than 1% of children undergo emergency surgery after failed reduction of intussusception.29 If manual reduction fails during surgery, tissue samples can only be obtained through surgical resection. Therefore, it was not easy to obtain tissue samples for this study. Our study is exploratory PCR, not qPCR, and is qualitative rather than quantitative. For convincing histologic evidence of active infection, we found two cases showing epithelial hyperplasia and viral inclusions as well as lymphoid hyperplasia that may alert the pathologist to the possibility of adenovirus infection on H&E staining (figure 3). Serological testing plays a supportive role in diagnosing adenovirus infection. This test is more suitable for prospective studies, but our study was retrospective and, therefore, this test was not performed.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report in Taiwan to observe adenovirus infection and intussusception from a pathological perspective. Our study included 29 patients who underwent intussusception surgery, and 35 pathological specimens were recorded in the surgical reports. There were 27 appendix tissues and eight intestinal tissues in the pathological specimens. Nevertheless, since most of the specimens had been preserved for many years, only eight appendix specimens from incidental appendectomies were successfully processed into FFPE tissue. IHC testing using antiadenoviral antibodies identified three positive cases (37.5%). PCR testing revealed adenovirus type C in four cases (50%). The control group consisted of eight children under 6 years of age who underwent incidental appendectomies without intussusception or appendicitis. None of the control subjects was positive in IHC with antiadenovirus antibody and qPCR analyses. Compared with the control group, the p value for IHC was greater than 0.05, showing no statistical difference, while the p value for PCR was less than 0.05, showing a statistical difference.

Conclusions

Our study directly confirmed the relationship between adenovirus infection and intussusception through pathological evidence. Very few patients undergo surgery for intussusception, and it is not easy to obtain eight tissue specimens in this research. We discovered FFPE tissue sample from intestinal tissue confirming evidence that adenovirus, specifically type C, may cause intussusception in Taiwan. IHC analysis and PCR testing confirmed evidence of adenoviral infection in patients with intussusception. PCR is as useful and reliable as IHC in diagnosing adenovirus in intussusception and has greater sensitivity than IHC. This requires a large enough sample to confirm, which is our limitation.
