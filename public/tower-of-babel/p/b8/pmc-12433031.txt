Parental readiness for discharge in day surgery children: current status and associated factors
Authors: Huiling Wang, Qinqin Ding, Ting Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnhancing parental readiness for hospital discharge is critical for optimizing postoperative outcomes in pediatric day surgery. However, current evidence regarding the discharge preparedness of parents in this context remains insufficient. This study aimed to assess the status of parental readiness for discharge of day surgery children and identify its key influencing factors.MethodsParents of the day surgery children at our hospital from February 2024 to February 2025 were included in this study. We utilized the Discharge Readiness Scale to assess the study participants, examining their discharge readiness scores and identifying associated influencing factors.ResultsA total of 252 parents of day surgery children were included. The overall discharge readiness score among these parents was 235.18±30.66. Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between parental discharge readiness and several factors: the child’s previous history of having day surgery (r=0.590), the parent’s educational level (r=0.632), place of residence (r=0.592), and personal monthly income (r=0.606) (all P<0.05). Multiple linear regression found that the child’s previous history of having day surgery, the parent’s educational level, place of residence, and personal monthly income were influencing factors of parental discharge readiness in day surgery children (all P<0.05).ConclusionsThe discharge readiness of parents of day surgery children is at a moderate level. Healthcare providers should implement targeted pre-discharge guidance and continuity of care to enhance parental confidence and improve their discharge readiness.

Introduction

Day surgery, as a modern healthcare service model, is characterized by its cost-effectiveness, high quality, and efficiency (1). Patients can complete the entire process of admission, surgery, and discharge within a single day, which effectively reduces hospitalization duration and significantly lowers medical costs while optimizing the utilization of healthcare resources (2,3). Although day surgery has stringent eligibility criteria and standardized procedures in patient selection, types of surgery, and postoperative care, patients are discharged within 24 hours after surgery, leaving the professional medical care environment (4,5). This may expose them to unforeseen and self-management challenging issues. Previous studies (6,7) have indicated that the relatively short postoperative observation period in day surgery hampers the effective implementation of postoperative care. Patients and their families often lack the necessary caregiving knowledge and skills, which increases the risk of postoperative complications and the likelihood of negative emotions (8).

Among day surgery patients, pediatric patients represent a unique subgroup. Due to their physiological and psychological characteristics, children require more meticulous and specialized care after surgery (9,10). Parents of pediatric day surgery patients serve as the primary caregivers after discharge, and their level of discharge preparedness directly affects the child’s home recovery outcomes (11,12). Parental preparedness encompasses not only the knowledge of postoperative care but also the psychological readiness and coping abilities for potential issues (13). Therefore, understanding the discharge preparedness of parents of pediatric day surgery patients is of great significance for improving postoperative recovery quality and reducing postoperative risks in children. This study investigates the discharge preparedness of parents of pediatric day surgery patients to comprehensively understand their readiness at the time of discharge, analyze the factors influencing their preparedness, and provide a scientific basis for developing targeted nursing interventions. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-265/rc).

Methods

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval No. 202505010-1) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all pediatric participants, who voluntarily signed a paper-based consent form. All collected information was used exclusively for the purpose of reporting this study.

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design and was conducted from February 2024 to February 2025 at the Day Surgery Center of a tertiary children’s hospital in Jiangsu Province, China. The study population consisted of pediatric patients who met the criteria for day surgery and their parents. The inclusion criteria required that the pediatric patients have a clear disease diagnosis and meet the eligibility criteria for day surgery, successfully completing the entire treatment process of admission, surgery, and discharge within 24 hours, with an age range of 1 to 18 years. Additionally, the primary caregivers of the pediatric patients were required to have normal communication and comprehension abilities, be willing to complete the questionnaire survey. The exclusion criteria included pediatric patients who needed to be transferred to a specialized ward for continued inpatient treatment due to complications or changes in their condition after surgery, as well as caregivers who had experienced a major stressful event in the recent past (within 3 months), such as a serious illness or death of a family member, which might affect their caregiving capacity and psychological state.

Demographic data were collected from pediatric patients and their parents, including the child’s gender, age, body mass index (BMI), diagnosis, history of previous day surgery, as well as the parent’s gender, age, educational level, place of residence, and personal monthly income. The discharge preparedness of parents was evaluated using the previously reported Discharge Preparedness Scale. Originally developed by Weiss et al. (14), this scale was subsequently translated and validated by Chen et al. (15). The scale has been extensively employed in clinical practice and has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity across various studies. This scale is a comprehensive tool designed to assess the readiness of parents to take care of their children after discharge from day surgery. It comprises 29 items that are distributed across five critical dimensions, which together provide a holistic view of the parents’ preparedness. These dimensions include available social support, which examines the extent of help and resources parents can access; post-discharge coping ability, which evaluates parents’ confidence and strategies for managing their child’s care at home; knowledge of the disease, which assesses parents’ understanding of their child’s condition and treatment; parental personal condition, which looks at the parents’ own health and well-being; and the child’s personal condition, which considers the child’s specific needs and characteristics. Each item on the scale is rated on a scale from 0 to 10, allowing for a nuanced and detailed assessment of each dimension. The total score, which ranges from 0 to 290, is calculated by summing the scores of all 29 items. A higher total score indicates a higher level of discharge preparedness among parents, suggesting that they are better equipped to manage the responsibilities and challenges associated with their child’s post-discharge care (16). The reliability of the scale has been confirmed through rigorous statistical analysis, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.886, indicating a high level of internal consistency and reliability (17). This reliability ensures that the scale consistently measures what it is intended to measure, making it a valuable tool for healthcare providers to assess and enhance parental readiness for discharge.

On the day of the child’s discharge, the researchers conducted a survey among the parents of pediatric patients who met the inclusion criteria. Prior to the survey, the researchers proactively explained the purpose and significance of the study to the parents, providing detailed clarifications on the meaning of each item in the questionnaire. This step was crucial to ensure that the parents fully understood the study and participated voluntarily. After obtaining the parents’ consent, the researchers distributed the questionnaires and provided guidance on how to complete them. The questionnaire was designed to be concise and straightforward, with an estimated completion time of approximately 10 minutes. Once the parents had completed the questionnaire, the researchers collected them on the spot to ensure the integrity and accuracy of the data.

Statistical analysis

In this study, data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0. For quantitative data that followed a normal distribution, the results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical data were described using frequency counts (n) and percentages (%). For comparisons between groups, either independent samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, with the choice of statistical method determined based on the results of homogeneity of variance tests. To explore the correlations between the discharge readiness of parents of pediatric patients and various characteristic variables, Pearson correlation analysis was used for normally distributed data, while Spearman rank correlation analysis was applied for non-normally distributed data. To further identify influencing factors, a multiple linear regression model (stepwise regression method) was utilized to analyze the independent determinants of discharge readiness among parents of pediatric patients. Prior to regression analysis, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with a threshold of VIF <10 considered acceptable. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

There is substantial room for improvement in discharge readiness of parents of day surgery children. This phenomenon may be closely related to the unique nature of day surgery. Given that children are discharged shortly after surgery, parents are required to assume primary responsibility for postoperative care (18). However, the current postoperative guidance and support systems provided by hospitals may not fully meet the actual needs of parents. Insufficient ability of parents to recognize and manage postoperative complications, as well as differences in the adaptability of the home care environment, are significant factors affecting readiness (19,20). To address these issues, hospitals should enhance preoperative education for parents, employing a variety of educational methods such as videos, brochures, and simulation exercises. Additionally, establishing a 24-hour emergency consultation hotline could improve parents’ coping abilities and alleviate their anxiety (21).

Among the various dimensions scored, “available social support” received the highest score, indicating that family and community support networks play a positive role in postoperative care. However, the efficiency of utilizing social support can still be improved, as some parents may lack sufficient knowledge of community nursing services or online support platforms. To further optimize the social support system, it is recommended that hospitals collaborate with community healthcare institutions to form dedicated follow-up teams for families of day surgery children, providing home care assessment services. Concurrently, the development of intelligent follow-up systems, such as mobile apps or WeChat mini-programs, could offer personalized care reminders and online expert consultations, thereby enhancing the availability and timeliness of social support and assisting parents in better managing postoperative care challenges.

It is noteworthy that the dimensions of “child’s personal condition” and “parent’s personal condition” received relatively lower scores, reflecting a knowledge gap among parents regarding postoperative pain management, wound care, and psychological adaptation. This phenomenon is particularly evident in families with lower educational levels or lower incomes, possibly due to limited access to information. In response to this situation, nursing interventions should focus on individualized education, employing illustrated brochures or dialect-based explanations according to the parents’ educational level (22). Strengthening skill training by arranging for parents to participate in simulated nursing operations such as wound disinfection and pain assessment before discharge is also essential. Moreover, providing professional psychological counseling services for parents with higher levels of anxiety can help reduce their caregiving stress and improve overall care quality (23).

The multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the child’s previous history of having day surgery, the parent’s educational level, place of residence, and personal monthly income are independent factors influencing discharge readiness. This finding suggests that parents with lower economic status or from rural areas face greater caregiving challenges. To reduce health disparities, it is recommended that, from a policy perspective, postoperative home care costs for day surgery be included in the scope of medical insurance reimbursement to alleviate the financial burden on families (24,25). Additionally, providing continuous care support to families in remote areas through telemedicine or in collaboration with rural health clinics is crucial (26). Implementing stratified management for low-income or low-education families, with increased frequency of telephone follow-ups or home visits, can ensure that these families receive necessary care guidance and support (27).

This study systematically reveals the current status and influencing factors of discharge readiness among parents of day surgery children, providing important insights for future research and clinical practice. Subsequent studies could focus on exploring the practical effects of digital nursing tools, such as the role of wearable devices in monitoring children’s vital signs and providing automatic alerts. Additionally, the development of standardized home care capability assessment tools is needed to more accurately identify high-risk families (28). From a policy standpoint, incorporating discharge readiness into the quality evaluation system for day surgery in hospitals could motivate healthcare institutions to continuously optimize their care processes (29). Through multidimensional and systematic interventions, it is expected that significant improvements in parental discharge readiness can be achieved, ultimately enhancing the postoperative recovery quality and overall health outcomes for children.

The present study acknowledges several limitations that may influence the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. Firstly, this study was conducted as a single-center investigation, which inherently restricts the sample size and population diversity. The limited sample and population may introduce biases related to the specific characteristics of the study site, potentially affecting the external validity of the results. Consequently, the findings may not be fully representative of broader populations or different geographical regions. Secondly, while the study identified several factors influencing parental discharge readiness, it is plausible that additional variables not examined in this study may also play a significant role. For instance, cultural factors, family dynamics could all impact parental preparedness for discharge. Future research should aim to conduct larger-scale, multi-center studies with more diverse populations to provide a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing discharge readiness. Thirdly, in our study exploring parental readiness for discharge in day surgery children, we acknowledge a potential limitation related to the Hawthorne Effect. As participants were aware that a readiness survey would be conducted, it is possible that their behavior and level of attention during the study period may have been influenced. This awareness could have led to increased vigilance or altered responses, which may not fully reflect their natural state in a non-study setting. Future research might consider implementing more observational or less intrusive methods to mitigate this effect and gain a more accurate understanding of parental readiness in real-world scenarios.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings of this study indicate that there remains considerable room for improvement in the discharge readiness of parents whose children undergo daytime surgery. Among the assessed dimensions, standardized scores were highest to lowest as follows: available social support, postoperative coping ability, disease knowledge, parental preparedness, and the child’s individual condition. Multivariate analysis identified several independent factors influencing parental discharge readiness, including the child’s history of previous daytime surgeries, parental education level, residential location (urban vs. rural), and individual monthly income. These results underscore the need for targeted interventions to enhance discharge preparedness, particularly in addressing modifiable factors such as health education and socioeconomic support systems. These findings highlight the multifaceted nature of discharge readiness and underscore the need for targeted interventions to enhance parental preparedness. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating comprehensive educational and support programs tailored to address these factors, with the aim of improving discharge outcomes and overall patient care.
