Genome-wide analysis of the DnaJ gene family and their expression profiles under salt stress in potato
Authors: Qi Fu, Yanan Pu, Jintao Liu, Xuemei Wang, Dianjue Li, Guangtao Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSaline stress is one of the primary environmental factors affecting global food security, significantly jeopardizing crop quality and yield. DnaJ proteins (DnaJs) are a type of protein that plays an important role in dealing with salt stress for plants. Nevertheless, the comprehensive exploration of the DnaJ gene family and its role in salt stress in potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is rare.ResultsA total of 113 StDnaJs were identified from the potato genome, and they were categorized into three distinct types, with the same type members possessing similar structures. Gene duplication analysis identified twenty-five pairs of duplicated genes, potentially contributing to the expansion of the StDnaJ family in potato. The promoters of StDnaJ genes contained numerous cis-acting regulatory elements linked to light response, plant growth, hormone response, and stress response. A heat map was generated using RNA-seq data, suggesting that they were expressed in roots or leaves under salt stress. We also selected 22 highly responsive members with continuous induction to validate their expression patterns using RT-qPCR, finding that they were significantly induced by salt stress in leaves or roots, with StDnaJ10, StDnaJ21, StDnaJ23, StDnaJ64, StDnaJ94, and StDnaJ107 being induced in both roots and leaves, indicating that these genes may play important roles in salt stress.ConclusionsThis study presents an extensive analysis of StDnaJs and provides targeted members for further salt-resistant research in potato.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11961-8.

Introduction

Soil salinity poses a significant threat to agricultural productivity, affecting approximately 45 million hectares of irrigated land [1]. Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), as the world’s third-largest food crop after rice and wheat, is highly valued for its rich nutrition, high yield, and adaptability [2]. However, potato is relatively sensitive to salt stress during its growth and development stages. Elevated soil salinity diminishes photosynthetic efficiency, disrupts ionic equilibrium, and hinders osmotic regulation, leading to substantial declines in both the yield and quality of potato tubers [3]. Thus, precise regulation of genes involved in salt stress response represents a promising strategy to enhance potato salt tolerance and ensure sustainable production.

Heat shock proteins (Hsps), first identified in Drosophila melanogaster in response to heat shock [4], play a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [4]. They facilitate the proper folding of nascent proteins and preserve the structural integrity and functionality of existing proteins under stress conditions [5–10]. Hsps are classified into six major families based on molecular weight and sequence homology: Hsp100, Hsp90, Hsp70, Hsp60, Hsp40, and Hsp20 [11–13]. Among these, Hsp40 proteins are the most prevalent in eukaryotic organisms. For example, yeast is reported to have 22 members, while Arabidopsis and rice contain 109 and 104 members, respectively [7, 14, 15].

Hsp40s, also known as DnaJs, were first identified in Escherichia coli, and typically have a molecular weight of 41-kDa [16]. DnaJs play essential roles in protein folding and unfolding, translocation, translation, and degradation via stimulation of the ATPase activity of chaperone Hsp70 proteins [16, 17]. Based on their domain architecture, DnaJ proteins are categorized into three groups [18]. Group I J-proteins are characterized by the J-domain, G/F-domain, and zinc finger domain. Group II J-proteins contain the J-domain plus either a G/F-domain or a zinc finger domain. Group III J-proteins only contain the J-domain [19]. The different types of DnaJ proteins reflect their functional diversity.

DnaJ proteins are integral to plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses, including pest infestations, pathogenic infections, drought, salinity, and heat stress. For instance, the overexpression of a tobacco J-domain protein has been demonstrated to enhance drought tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis [20]. The tomato chloroplast-targeted J-protein, LeCDJ1, can improve tolerance to heat, cold, and drought stress, as well as resistance to Pseudomonas solanacearum [21–23]. The AtJ3 in Arabidopsis regulates pH homeostasis and influences stress sensitivity, particularly under alkaline conditions and high salt [24]. OsDNAJ15 encodes a plasma membrane-localized heat shock protein 40 (Hsp40) as well as the chaperone regulator OsBAG4, which was involved in transcriptional regulation under salinity stress tolerance [25]. Furthermore, GmDnaJA6 has been identified as a potential regulator of resistance to saline and alkaline stress, with its overexpression leading to increased tolerance to these conditions [26]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of DnaJ proteins in plant abiotic stress responses.

To date, DnaJ proteins have been extensively characterized in several plant species, including Arabidopsis, rice, maize, cucumber, and pepper [7, 15, 27–29]. However, the identification and characterization of DnaJ proteins in potato remain limited. In this work, the members of the DnaJ gene family in potato were identified through bioinformatics. Subsequently, a comprehensive analysis was conducted, incorporating phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, conserved motifs, cis-regulating elements in the promoter region, and expression patterns under salt treatment. This research establishes the groundwork for a deeper understanding of the role of StDnaJs in salt stress.

Results

Identification and characterization of DnaJ genes in potato

A total of 113 StDnaJ genes were identified in the potato genome using the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) of the DnaJ structural domain (PF00226). Based on the Pfam and NCBI CDD databases, we confirmed that all the members contained the full DnaJ domain. These genes were systematically named from StDnaJ1 to StDnaJ113 based on their chromosomal positions (Table S1).

The physicochemical properties of these genes were further analyzed, including protein length, molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and subcellular localization predictions. The protein lengths ranged from 99 to 991 amino acids (aa), with molecular weights varying between 11.41 and 280.69 kDa. The theoretical pI values spanned from 4.59 to 10.52, reflecting a broad spectrum of protein charge distributions. Subcellular localization predictions revealed that the majority of StDnaJ proteins were localized in chloroplast or cytoplasm (Table S1). This diverse subcellular distribution suggests that the StDnaJ gene family may play multiple functional roles across different tissues and cellular compartments.

Phylogenetic analysis of the StDnaJ gene family

To understand the phylogenetic relationships of the StDnaJ gene family, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on 113 StDnaJ and 115 Arabidopsis DnaJ protein sequences using MEGA-X with the maximum likelihood (ML) method. The phylogenetic tree indicated that the StDnaJ members could be divided into three distinct types: type I, type II, and type III. Type I comprised 20 StDnaJs, while type II contained 22 StDnaJs, with these two types exhibiting a close evolutionary relationship. The remaining members were clustered into type III, constituting the largest group (Fig. 1). Overall, the phylogenetic analysis indicates that the StDnaJ gene family is highly conserved in Arabidopsis and potato, suggesting shared evolutionary origins and functional importance.

Chromosomal distribution and gene duplication of StDnaJs

The 113 StDnaJ genes were unevenly distributed across all 12 chromosomes of potato, with each chromosome containing between four and seventeen genes (Fig. 2, Table S1). Notably, the majority of StDnaJ genes were concentrated on chromosomes 1, 4, 5, and 11, which contained 17, 14, 14, and 10 genes. Chromosomes 6, 7, and 9 each had eight members, while only four genes were found on chromosome 10. To investigate gene duplication events, we examined tandem and segmental duplications among the StDnaJ genes. Tandem duplications occur when two or more genes are located within a 200 Kb range on the same chromosome. Circos analysis revealed that six pairs of tandem duplicated genes (StDnaJ15/StDnaJ16, StDnaJ31/StDnaJ32, StDnaJ51/StDnaJ52/StDnaJ53, StDnaJ78/StDnaJ79/StDnaJ80, StDnaJ83/StDnaJ84, and StDnaJ100/StDnaJ101/StDnaJ102) were located on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, and 11, respectively (Fig. 2). Additionally, 19 pairs of segmental duplication events (StDnaJ4/StDnaJ63, StDnaJ4/StDnaJ113, StDnaJ16/StDnaJ99, StDnaJ19/StDnaJ23, StDnaJ21/StDnaJ76, StDnaJ23/StDnaJ48, StDnaJ24/StDnaJ49, StDnaJ34/StDnaJ67, StDnaJ37/StDnaJ56, StDnaJ39/StDnaJ103, StDnaJ39/StDnaJ64, StDnaJ42/StDnaJ61, StDnaJ42/StDnaJ99, StDnaJ55/StDnaJ38, StDnaJ56/StDnaJ68, StDnaJ64/StDnaJ113, StDnaJ64/StDnaJ103, StDnaJ70/StDnaJ108, and StDnaJ88/StDnaJ95) were identified on different chromosomes (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that gene duplication events, including both tandem and segmental duplications, have contributed to the expansion and diversification of the StDnaJ gene family.

Subsequently, we further analyzed the distribution of introns and exons within the StDnaJ to explore their structural characteristics. The results revealed significant variation in the number of exons (ranging from 1 to 23) and introns (ranging from 0 to 22) in the gene family (Fig. 3). Most StDnaJ genes within the same group typically exhibited similar exon numbers and structures. However, there were significant differences in intron lengths, as exemplified in StDnaJ25 and StDnaJ82 in type I, StDnaJ24 and StDnaJ32 in type II.

Cis-acting regulatory elements in StDnaJ promoters

Cis-acting regulatory elements in gene promoters serve as binding sites for transcription factors, enabling the integration of external stimuli through the regulation of gene expression. To analyze the upstream regulatory mechanisms of the StDnaJ gene family, we identified conserved cis-acting regulatory elements within the 2000 bp promoter regions of StDnaJs using PlantCARE [30]. A total of 202 conserved cis-acting regulatory elements were identified, which were classified into four main functional categories: light response, plant growth and development, plant hormone response, and stress-related elements (Fig. 4). Within the light-responsive category, several elements were identified, including the CAAT-box, Box4, G-box, GT1-motif, and G-Box. Notably, the CAAT-box was present in all StDnaJ genes, with StDnaJ25 and StDnaJ113 containing the highest number. The findings suggest that the expression of StDnaJ genes is likely regulated by light. Five cis-elements were associated with plant growth and development: TATA-box, ERE (Estrogen response element), AAGAA-motif, CAT-box, and O2-site motif. In the phytohormone-responsive category, the ABRE element (ABA-responsive element) involved in ABA responsiveness was the most prominent, followed by two MeJA responsive elements (the CGTCA motif and TGACG motif), as well as the TCA-element involved in SA response and the P-box involved in GA response. Furthermore, we observed that 78.8%, 66.3%, and 47.8% of StDnaJ family members contained ARE (Anaerobic-responsive element), As-1 (Activation sequence-1), and WUN-motif (Wound-responsive motif) respectively. These results indicate that StDnaJs may play important roles in regulating light response, plant growth and development, plant hormone response, and stress response.

Expression patterns of StDnaJ members under salt stress

To investigate the potential function of the StDnaJ members in response to salt stress, we analyzed their expression patterns in leaves and roots under 200 mM NaCl treatment based on published transcriptome data [31]. The results revealed that the expression levels of 46 StDnaJs were significantly changed in roots or leaves. In roots, 11 StDnaJs were significantly upregulated, with three of them (StDnaJ78, StDnaJ79, and StDnaJ 61) being specifically induced during the early stage of salt stress, and two of them (StDnaJ39 and StDnaJ64) induced during the late stage. Conversely, 12 StDnaJs were significantly downregulated in roots, including StDnaJ2, StDnaJ6, StDnaJ15, StDnaJ17, StDnaJ28, StDnaJ36, StDnaJ37, StDnaJ40, StDnaJ66, StDnaJ68, StDnaJ97, and StDnaJ107 (Fig. 6). In leaves, the expression of 26 StDnaJ genes was significantly upregulated, with distinct temporal and tissue-specific response patterns. For example, StDnaJ13, StDnaJ18, StDnaJ25, StDnaJ43, StDnaJ55, and StDnaJ69 exhibited an upregulation followed by a downregulation pattern during the treatment period. Meanwhile, StDnaJ5, StDnaJ21, StDnaJ39, StDnaJ64, and StDnaJ107 peaked after 24–48 h of NaCl treatment, suggesting their potential involvement in late responsive signaling pathways. Notably, StDnaJ39, StDnaJ64, and StDnaJ107 were induced in both roots and leaves, but with differing expression trends. StDnaJ39 and StDnaJ64 showed upregulation in both tissues, while StDnaJ107 was downregulated in roots but upregulated in leaves, indicating distinct regulatory mechanisms for maintaining cellular stability under stress. Overall, these findings suggest that StDnaJ genes play diverse roles in potato’s response to salt stress, with tissue-specific and time-dependent expression patterns contributing to their functional complexity.

Quantitative expression analysis of StDnaJs under salt stress

To further verify the response of StDnaJ genes under salt stress, we analyzed their expression by RT-qPCR. We selected 12 highly responsive members (StDnaJ10, StDnaJ15, StDnaJ21, StDnaJ23, StDnaJ29, StDnaJ45, StDnaJ64, StDnaJ73, StDnaJ80, StDnaJ94, StDnaJ98, and StDnaJ107) that exhibited substantial induction in roots, as well as 15 StDnaJ genes (StDnaJ10, StDnaJ18, StDnaJ21, StDnaJ22, StDnaJ23, StDnaJ60, StDnaJ62, StDnaJ64, StDnaJ67, StDnaJ69, StDnaJ76, StDnaJ87, StDnaJ90, StDnaJ94, and StDnaJ107) with continuous induction in leaves (FPKM fold change > 2 compared to 0 h in roots or leaves). In roots, the expression level of StDnaJ94 induced by NaCl stress was the highest (> 5-fold at 6 h), followed by StDnaJ45 (> 4-fold at 6 h). The expression of the remaining genes was induced 2- to 3-fold (Fig. 6). Notably, StDnaJ64 and StDnaJ98 were significantly upregulated and peaked after 48 h of NaCl treatment. In leaves, StDnaJ18, StDnaJ23, StDnaJ69, StDnaJ87, and StDnaJ94 displayed a pattern of upregulation followed by downregulation. StDnaJ21, StDnaJ62, StDnaJ76, StDnaJ90, and StDnaJ107 were not induced early under salt stress but were upregulated at 24–48 h, peaking at 48 h. Among them, StDnaJ107 showed the highest upregulation at 48 h (> 15-fold). Furthermore, we observed significant induction of StDnaJ10, StDnaJ21, StDnaJ23, StDnaJ64, and StDnaJ94 with a similar expression pattern in leaves and roots, while StDnaJ107 showed downregulation in roots and the opposite pattern in leaves. These experimental results were largely consistent with the transcriptomic analysis, supporting the notion that StDnaJ genes play tissue-specific roles in response to salt stress, contributing to the plant’s adaptive mechanisms.

Discussion

DnaJ proteins, one type of heat shock protein, are widely present in plants that respond to both biotic and abiotic stresses. Up to now, the mechanisms by which DnaJ responds to salt stress have been reported in many species, such as Arabidopsis, rice, and soybean [23, 29, 32–34]. For instance, BIL2 encodes a member of the DnaJ protein family that localizes to mitochondria, promoting mitochondrial ATP synthesis and increasing resistance to salt stress conditions [19]. Similarly, overexpression of GmDnaJC7 in soybeans improves salt tolerance, increasing germination rates and root lengths under stress conditions [23]. However, the identification of DnaJ proteins in potato has not yet been reported. In this study, we identified 113 StDnaJ genes in potato through bioinformatics approaches, which exhibited diverse physicochemical properties and significant variations in size, gene structure, and expression patterns under salt stress.

The location of proteins within cells is closely associated with their function. Therefore, understanding the subcellular localization of proteins is crucial for functional research. In plants, DnaJ proteins localize to various compartments, including the cytoplasm, nucleus, chloroplasts, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER). For example, chloroplast-localized DnaJ proteins, such as AtJ11 and AtJ20 in Arabidopsis, protect the photosynthetic apparatus under heat and high-light stress [35, 36]. Similarly, the mitochondrial protein BIL2 modulates brassinosteroid signaling, enhancing plant growth and stress resistance [32]. Our subcellular localization predictions for StDnaJ proteins revealed that most members were localized in the chloroplast or cytoplasm, suggesting their potential roles in potato’s response to abiotic stresses (Table S1).

The phylogenetic relationship of gene family members helps elucidate their evolutionary history, classification, functional prediction, and the adaptation and speciation processes in both organisms and genes [27]. J-proteins have traditionally been classified into three types (type I, type II, and type III) based on the bacterial DnaJ classification [18]. Similar to other studies, 113 StDnaJ proteins were identified and divided into three types according to phylogenetic analysis, among which Type III members were the most numerous (Fig. 1). Type I and II exhibit a close evolutionary relationship, suggesting that they are relatively conserved in their evolution and functions. Ten conserved motifs were found in 113 StDnaJs (Fig. 3), the vast majority of DnaJ proteins contain motif 1 and motif 2, while motif 7 is only present in a few members of type III. Additionally, StDnaJ members were divided into several subgroups based on conserved domains, suggesting they may exhibit diverse functions. For example, the Fer4 domain in the Fer4_8 subfamily, which was first identified in a sulfate-reducing bacterium, plays an important role in the biosynthesis of ferrichrome A [37–39]. The Jiv90 domain in the Jiv90 subfamily interacts with viral protein in the N-terminal region of the polyprotein [40, 41]. The phylogenetic classification and the various motifs of StDnaJs collectively offer valuable insights into their evolution and functional diversification.

Gene duplication is a key evolutionary process that enables plants to adapt to environmental stresses by providing additional genetic material for functional innovation. Following gene duplication, duplicated copies can evolve new functions or specialize in specific stress responses, providing plants with a broader range of adaptive strategies. NAC transcription factors such as ANAC019, ANAC055, and ANAC072 regulate drought and salinity responses following gene duplication [42]. Similarly, in rice, the DREB (Dehydration-Responsive Element Binding) gene family has expanded through duplication, with paralogs such as OsDREB1A and OsDREB1B showing differential expression under drought and cold stress [43]. In our study, chromosome location showed that all these StDnaJ genes were mapped unevenly across 12 potato chromosomes (Fig. 2). 42 genes were involved in gene duplication, including 6 tandem duplication events and 19 segmental duplication events that were identified. These duplications likely enhance gene dosage and functional diversification, enabling adaptation to diverse environmental challenges.

Promoters can control the start time and expression degree of gene transcription, the analysis of promoter is the premise and basis of expression regulation research [44]. We analyzed the cis-acting regulatory elements of the promoter of the StDnaJ family genes (Fig. 4). The results showed that 77% of StDnaJ members had the ABRE element, which is a class of elements capable of binding to conserved abscisic acid (ABA)-dependent transcription factors [45]. As a key stress hormone, ABA can bind to the ABRE element in the promoter of the StDnaJ gene, activate the expression of a series of downstream stress resistance-related genes, and thus initiate the stress response in cells [9, 46, 47]. Similarly, the jasmonic acid signaling pathway is involved in the regulation of plant salt stress responses [48, 49]. The distribution of two jasmonic acid-related elements, CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif, on the promoter of the StDnaJ genes suggested their potential involvement in the salt stress response. Additionally, we observed that 78.8%, 66.3%, and 47.8% of StDnaJ family members contained ARE (Anaerobic-responsive element), As-1 (Activation sequence-1), and WUN-motif (Wound-responsive motif), which were various stress-related elements. These elements suggest that the StDnaJ genes can sensitively perceive salt stress signals and rapidly initiate the gene expression regulatory program through binding with corresponding transcription factors.

Gene expression profiles can provide important clues to reveal gene functions [50, 51]. We analyzed the expression of all 113 StDnaJ genes under NaCl treatments based on RNA-seq analysis [31], and selected 22 highly responsive members with continuous induction in roots or leaves to validate their expression patterns using RT-qPCR. We found that the expression of 12 StDnaJs was significantly upregulated, while StDnaJ107 was significantly downregulated in roots under NaCl stress (Fig. 5). In leaves, many StDnaJ genes responded to salt stress, especially StDnaJ18 and StDnaJ94, showing particularly high upregulation of more than 20-fold. Additionally, we found that StDnaJ10, StDnaJ21, StDnaJ23, StDnaJ64, and StDnaJ94 exhibited similar expression patterns in leaves and roots. Among these, StDnaJ94 showed a higher level of induction in leaves than in roots, while StDnaJ107 showed downregulation in roots, which was the opposite pattern in leaves, indicating distinct regulatory mechanisms for maintaining cellular stability under stress (Fig. 6). Upon further integration with the analysis results of cis-acting regulatory elements, we found that StDnaJ107 contained the largest number of ABRE elements. It is hypothesized that StDnaJ107 is strongly activated by the ABA signal, thereby participating in the response to salt stress. In summary, this study conducted a comprehensive genome-wide identification and fundamental functional analysis of the StDnaJ gene family in potato. The findings offer new insights into the StDnaJ homoeologs and present valuable information for further functional characterization. Future research will involve the validation of StDnaJ functions through gene overexpression or gene knockout techniques in potato.

Conclusions

In this study, 113 StDnaJ genes were identified and systematically characterized through bioinformatics analyses and expression profiling. Phylogenetic analysis showed that these StDnaJ proteins were clustered into three types, with similar gene structures in each type, reflecting functional conservation. Cis-acting regulatory elements analysis indicated that this gene family could respond to multiple signaling pathways. Finally, gene expression analysis revealed that 22 StDnaJ genes exhibited substantial induction in roots or leaves under salt stress. Taken together, these results present an extensive analysis of StDnaJs and provide potential targets for potato salt resistance studies.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification of the StDnaJ gene family in potato

The Arabidopsis DnaJ protein sequences were obtained from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp) [52]. The potato genome and amino acid sequences were retrieved from the National Genome Science Data Center (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/) [53], BioProject: accession no. PRJCA011810. The hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the J-domain (PF00226) was downloaded from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) to search for a potential protein sequence containing the DnaJ domain in potato (e-value < 10−2). To validate the accuracy of these DnaJ proteins, redundant protein sequences were manually removed, and the remaining sequences were confirmed as the final StDnaJ protein sequences based on the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) of NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/). The ExPASy website (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was utilized to predict the molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point, and amino acid number of the StDnaJ proteins [54]. The WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) was utilized to predict protein subcellular localization [55].

Phylogenetic tree analysis of the StDnaJ gene family

The StDnaJ protein family sequences of potato were used to construct a neighbor-joining tree. First, MAFFT v7.427 was used to perform multiple sequence comparisons, and second, MEGA-X software [56] was employed to construct a phylogenetic tree with the maximum likelihood (ML) method, followed by annotation of the evolutionary tree using iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de/).

Chromosome localization and gene duplication analysis of the StDnaJ gene family

The potato genome sequence and general feature format (GFF) files were used to extract chromosome length and location information of StDnaJ family members through TBtools software [57]. Collinearity analysis was performed using MCScanX software with the following parameters: match score of 50, match size of 5, gap penalty of −1, overlap window of 5, e-value of 1e-5, and maximum gaps of 25. Analysis of segment and tandem duplications resulting from gene duplication events was conducted.

Analysis of protein motif and gene structure of the StDnaJ gene family

The potato StDnaJ gene superfamily sequence information was consistent with the above method. The structural information related to the StDnaJ genes was visualized using TBtools. The conserved motifs were identified using the online MEME tool (http://meme.nbcr.net/meme/tools/meme/) [58].

Cis-acting regulatory elements analysis of the StDnaJ gene family

The upstream regulatory regions (approximately 2000 bp) of StDnaJ gene sequences were extracted from the DMv8.1 genome and submitted to the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to predict the distribution of cis-acting regulatory elements on the promoter [29]. Subsequently, all cis-acting regulatory elements were classified and extracted for visual analysis using the TBtools software.

Expression of StDnaJ genes based on RNA-Seq in potato

In this paper, RNA-seq data reported by Liu et al. [31] were used to investigate the expression patterns of putative StDnaJ genes in potato. A heat map of StDnaJ genes under NaCl stress was generated using the TBtools software.

RNA extraction, and quantitative real-time PCR analysis

RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent method and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) from TaKaRa. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Code No. RR820A/B) from TaKaRa on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR Instrument (Applied Biosystems). Tubulin served as an internal control for sample normalization. Primer design was performed using Premier 5 software, with details outlined in Supplementary Table S2. The EF1α (Elongation factor 1α) gene was used as an internal control [59]. The relative expression values of genes were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method [60]. All experiments were conducted with biological triplicates.
