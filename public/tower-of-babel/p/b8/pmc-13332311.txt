Assessment of Dementia Patients Caregivers’ Home Safety Knowledge in the Outpatient Setting of Jeddah, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Asim Muhammed Alshanberi, Houriah Yasir Nukaly, Ahmed Hafez Mousa, Mahinar Mamdouh Alhartani, Jumanah Yaser Nassar, Ruqayyah Ali Ahmed, Terad Adnan Talmesany, Mohammed Shaikhomer, Ahmad A Imam, Ehab A Abo-Ali, Fatma E Hassan, Shakeel Ahmed Ansari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHome safety is a major issue for individuals involved in caring for dementia patients due to a lack of practice and awareness, as it accounts for more than 90% of the needs of patients with dementia. Such caregivers face constant challenges in preventing accidents while trying to maintain the dementia patients’ independence and quality of life.ObjectiveThe objective of this unicentric study was to assess the correlates of dementia patients’ caregivers’ home safety practices.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in an outpatient setting of a private hospital in Jeddah (Saudi Arabia) from November 2022 until January 2023 by assessing the possible correlation between the caregivers’ demographics among 128 dementia patients’ caregivers. It also evaluates their service experience including the type of care, assistance duration, duration of caregiving, average time spent with persons living with dementia, dementia care training experience and their home safety practice quality. A questionnaire utilizing selected items from the validated “Alzheimer’s Association Home Safety Checklist” was employed to assess the caregivers’ home safety practices. Besides, the caregivers’ psychological well-being and depression were assessed through a validated “Patient Health Questionnaire-2.ResultsAlthough 66% of caregivers showed good home safety practices, 29% of caregivers reported depressive symptoms. Caregivers with extended caregiving duration (more than 5 years) demonstrated significantly better home safety practices as compared to those with shorter duration (AOR: 5.68, 95% CI: 1.76–18.33, p = 0.004). Further, depressed caregivers provided poor service as compared to non-depressed caregivers (AOR: 2.94, 95% CI: 1.19–7.24, p = 0.019).ConclusionShort-term and long-term dementia training aligning with cultural values and traditions of Saudi Arabia are required to ensure the care of such persons living with dementia is achieved in an effective and respectful way. Moreover, trained caregivers can improve quality of life, delay institutionalization and reduce healthcare costs.

Introduction

Dementia represents a group of diseases that affect the patient’s thinking capacity. This level of decline causes cognitive disabilities reaching up to a level of severity indicating interference with the patient’s capacity to function independently.1–4 According to recent Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) facts and figures, the most common cause of progressive dementia in older adults is AD which accounts for 50–75% of all cases5 followed by vascular dementia, accounting for approximately 17–30% of all cases. Dementia with Lewy bodies accounts for nearly 10–15% while fronto-temporal dementias are relatively rare.6

AD accounts for more than two-thirds of cases of progressive cognitive decline in the elderly population of Saudi Arabia, with an estimated total prevalence of approximately 130,000 individuals affected by the condition [7]. Additionally, the prevalence of AD in KSA was estimated to be approximately 5%, with a noticeable rise in cases among individuals over the age of 65. Moreover, a significant association was observed between diabetes and AD with type 2 diabetes mellitus acting as a major risk factor for cognitive decline. Studies indicate a greater prevalence of mild cognitive impairment among diabetic Saudi individuals.7,8

Islamic values emphasize respect, honor, and care for older family members. Furthermore, the oldest members are considered to represent wisdom, love, faith and blessings, and their opinions are predominant within the family. Therefore, family members provide care for older members with disabilities or those who are vulnerable. In addition, the autonomy and dignity of older adults are important aspects to consider. Owing to the influence of the social beliefs and cultural norms of the families, if older adults require assistance, family members are likely to hire a caregiver at home; if they cannot afford one, a relative provides care for the older adults, as it is considered unacceptable to send older adults to care facilities. However, caring for older adults with dementia is challenging as it may lead to stress and fear in family members.8,9

Dementia’s behavioral and psychological symptoms are also linked to poor caregiver outcomes, including reduced income, poor health, and poor quality of life. As noted earlier, caregivers managing these symptoms experience more depression than caregivers of people with dementia alone or with other chronic diseases. The most challenging aspects of providing care include sleep disruptions, recurrent vocalizations, managing wandering, restlessness, and refusal of care.10,11

This necessitates the requirement of professional dementia caregivers in home-based and in other relevant healthcare settings which makes them crucial for individuals needing assistance with daily living, personal care and emotional support. Due to their involvement in day-to-day care in addition to coordinating a variety of health services, it is crucial to assess the level of practice of caregivers about home safety tips to optimize healthcare and quality of service that they provide to persons living with dementia.12,13 Researchers have earlier proved that caregivers with better understanding of the disease and persons living with dementia needs are more likely to identify it early, hence reflecting on better clinical outcomes.14,15 It was earlier demonstrated that caregivers may become depressed for a variety of reasons, including pain, discomfort, hunger, dim lighting and sleep deprivation. Additionally, the expenses of caring for persons living with dementia and the occurrence of behavioral and psychological symptoms in them can be extremely stressful for the caregiver, which can result in depressive symptoms.16

A detailed home safety checklist (removing tripping hazards, improving lighting, securing dangerous items like medications, cleaning products, and firearms; installation of grab holders in the bathroom for additional support, providing seating near the bed to help with dressing and ensuring closet shelves are installed at an accessible height for easy access and to avoid climbing or objects falling overhead) can increase the awareness of dementia specific impairments to caregivers, who can establish a safe environment for caring persons living with dementia. It includes home safety tips that help in coping with some of the more hazardous behaviors that may occur as the disease progresses.17,18 It has been analyzed before that the caregiver’s role in caring for persons living with dementia can be potentially improved if the correlations of good practices like engagement in meaningful activities, using positive communication, maintaining routines, and providing specialized support for both individuals and their families are identified.19

Dementia caregivers often lack adequate training, poor communication skills that lead to high levels of stress, anxiety and burnout that affect their lives physically and mentally. Moreover, the caregivers may follow a rigid time and task- oriented approach instead of a person-centered approach that limits proper care to persons living with dementia.20 Hence, the main objective of this unicentric study was to assess the dementia patients’ caregivers’ home safety practices. Further, we evaluated the possible impact of the caregivers’ demographics, their relationship to the dementia patient as well as their service experience on their home safety practice and other related attributes. Additionally, the caregivers’ psychological status was assessed by Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) tool to observe its impact on the outcome of their practice.

Methodology

Design

A cross-sectional study was conducted among caregivers who cared for people with dementia. This non-probability method serves as a practical and cost-effective approach that allows researchers to quickly access stressed caregivers for data on caregiver burden and needs.

Sample and Setting

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in an outpatient private polyclinic (Jeddah, Saudi Arabia) from November 2022 until January 2023. The study population includes older adults (above 60 years) living with dementia which constituted AD, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal and mixed dementia. Male and female caregivers aged 18 and above, taking care of persons living with dementia with a confirmed diagnosis of dementia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria for establishing the diagnosis, were eligible to participate in the study. Caregivers of persons living with dementia diagnosed with other types of neurological disorders, and persons living with dementia without registered caregivers or who did not consent to the study were excluded from this study. All male and female individuals below 18 who considered themselves as caregivers were also excluded from the study. Based on the inclusion criteria, 128 persons living with dementia caregivers were enrolled in this study. Rationale focuses on reducing caregiver burden, improving caregiver quality of life, and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions in real-world settings. The convenient sampling technique was adopted during the recruitment of the study sample. The ethical approval for this study was obtained by the Research Unit of Batterjee Medical College, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (RES-2023-0008). Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained from the study participants prior to study commencement, and they had the right to withdraw at any time. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975 (revised in 2013).

Study Tool

The questionnaire used in this research involves a face-to-face survey based on the validated “Alzheimer’s Association Home Safety Checklist” and “Patient Health Questionnaire-2”. The Alzheimer’s Association 2023 Home Safety Checklist provides comprehensive, proactive measures to create a secure environment for individuals with dementia. Key actions include securing hazardous materials, installing extra locks, improving lighting to prevent falls, removing clutter, etc. The checklist consists of 16 Practice statements. “Yes” indicates knowledge (1 point) and “No” indicates a lack of knowledge (0 points). A score of more than 10 points indicates good practice while a score of less than 10 shows poor practice. Moreover, a PHQ-2 was used as a short item screening tool for detecting potential depression in clinical settings. It consists of a two-question scale answered on a Likert scale ranging between 0 and 3. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). A total score of 3 or higher indicated the presence of depressive symptoms.

This cross-sectional survey was conducted using a community-engaged research approach, partnering with community members, specifically, caregivers of individuals who were personally and professionally impacted by dementia. The questionnaire was available in English and Arabic. Cronbach’s alpha testing was obtained as 0.84 for the 16-item Home Safety Checklist which determines the reliability of the study tool.

Study Variables

The study variables were divided into four main sections as I: Dementia caregivers’ socio-demographic characteristics section including age group, gender, marital status, educational level, employment status and relationship to dementia patient, II: Dementia caregivers’ service characteristics section including type of care, assistance duration, duration of care-giving (in years), average time spent with persons living with dementia and dementia care training experience, III: Home safety assessment section and IV: Dementia caregivers’ psychological assessment (depression) section. All the variables were independent. The outcome variable was the level of practice that was interpreted by achieving at least 60% correct of the practice as mentioned in the assessment checklist.

“Alzheimer’s Association Home Safety Checklist” adapted from the Alzheimer’s Association Home Safety Checklist21 was provided to the caregiver, aiming to create a safe environment to prevent injury and serious consequences from occurring and help maximize the person’s independence. Several items were used to evaluate the caregivers’ practice of some necessary home safety precautions, as shown in Table S1. The level of home safety practice was recorded as “Good” or “Poor” based on the computed score from the Home Safety Checklist (score ≥10 = Good; score <10 = Poor). The presence of depression among caregivers was also assessed using the PHQ-222 as shown in Table S2.

Data Analysis

Data was analyzed by employing Statistical Package for Social Science version 26.0 software (SPSS Incorporation, Chicago). Data was checked for missing information, and none was found to be missing. Data was summarized using mean values of age and standard deviation and median values for Likert scores. Results were expressed in percentages for socio-demographic variables. Chi-square test was used to compare the variables. Logistic regression analysis was performed, considering the outcome as the level of practices, namely Good or Poor. For statistical interpretation of data, p-values less than 0.05 were adopted as significant. Variables were selected for inclusion in the logistic regression model based on theoretical relevance and statistical significance in the bivariate analysis. Specifically, variables that demonstrated a statistically significant association (p<0.05) with the outcome (home safety practice level) in the Chi-square analyses were eligible for entry into the multivariable model. Additionally, variables considered theoretically important based on prior literature—such as employment status and relationship to the person living with dementia—were included even where bivariate p-values were borderline. The Hosmer-Leme show goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the calibration of the model and the overall model chi-square with associated degrees of freedom and p-value.

Association Between Dementia Caregivers’ Home Safety Practices and Caregivers’ Service Characteristics

Table 3 showed an insignificant effect on the type of care (paid or unpaid), assistance duration (full/part time), and average time spent with dementia patient (<6 hours, 6–12 hours or >12 hours) on the quality of caregivers’ home safety practices. However, there was significant association between home safety practices and other caregiver service variables. The long duration of caregiving (more than 5 years) showed 42 (82.4%) caregivers providing good home safety practice as compared to 9 (17.6%) caregivers that exhibited poor practice (p=0.005). Twenty-five (83.3%) caregivers with dementia care training practice showed good home safety practice while 5 (16.7%) showed poor practice. Moreover, 39 (39.8%) caregivers without dementia care training experience showed good practice and 59 (60.2%) showed poor practice (p=0.02) thereby indicating that caregivers with dementia care training experience provided good care to persons living with dementia. Eighteen (48.6%) caregivers who felt depressed showed good home safety practice while 19 (51.4%) showed poor practice. It was also recorded that 66 (72.5%) caregivers not feeling depressed showed good practice while 25 (27.5%) showed poor practice (p=0.010), indicating that caregivers feeling depressed provided poor care to persons living with dementia.

As displayed in Table 4, the logistic regression analysis examined the significant factors influencing caregivers’ practices in home care for persons living with dementia. The occupation status of caregivers did not significantly affect practices, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 1.87 (95% CI: 0.79–4.39, p = 0.154) for employed caregivers as compared to unemployed caregivers. The relationship with the dementia patient (child and spouse vs. others) also showed insignificant impact (AOR: 0.28, 95% CI: 0.21–1.56, p = 0.275). Moreover, caregivers’ training experience indicated a non-significant trend towards better practices (AOR: 0.41, 95% CI: 0.133–1.24, p = 0.113). However, the duration of caregiving demonstrated significant results. It was noted that caregivers with 1–5 years of experience had significantly better practices (AOR: 5.68, 95% CI: 1.76–18.33, p = 0.004). Additionally, caregivers reporting feelings of depression were more likely to engage in poor practices (AOR: 3.49, 95% CI: 1.19–7.24, p = 0.019). Discussion

There is an information gap in studies from Saudi Arabia regarding caregivers’ home safety practices, home modifications, and how to handle safety issues or accidents in persons living with dementia.8,14,23,24 Hence, efforts were raised to evaluate the home safety practices and service expertise of dementia patient caregivers in an outpatient setting in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Furthermore, the psychological well-being of dementia patient caregivers was evaluated because it has a significant and direct impact on the quality of care they provide. Our study revealed that most caregivers were female (68%). A greater percentage of female caregivers has been reported earlier by other investigators as well25,26 which could be due to the increased female participation in the workforce and changing societal norms regarding gender roles. Although few studies have examined gender differences among dementia caregivers27–29 according to stress-coping theories, females are more likely to cope with caregiving stressors.30 Moreover, the current study reported that most caregivers were married and showed good home safety practices. Unmarried caregivers experience a severe and faster decline in mental health due to reduced economic stability, high burden of providing care alone and lack of social support, as compared to their married counterparts. A recent study conducted by Park et al (2023) found that unmarried caregivers experienced faster worsening of depressive symptoms compared to their married counterparts.31 The association between caregiver burden and depressive symptoms in family caregivers of older adults was also emphasized.

The caregivers exhibited a diverse profile, consisting of males with an average age of 42.3 years old and females averaging 44.5 years old. The age of dementia caregivers significantly influences their experience. It was revealed that younger caregivers (less than 45 years) are more prone to suffer from modifiable risk factors like poor sleep, obesity and smoking. However, older caregivers experience higher physical risks of mortality, cardiac disorders and increased inflammation. Majority of the caregivers who participated in this study were educated (either college or above). Mean age of the persons living with dementia caregivers was reported as 48.04 years and most of them were having the matric level education.32 Knowledge and awareness of the educated dementia caregivers reduce their anxiety, stress and burden significantly. It enhances the caregiver-patient relationship through better communication, promotes person-centered care and improves the quality of life.

Additionally, most caregivers were immediate family members representing either offspring or spouses of persons living with dementia and were not paid for their caregiving efforts. This could be explained by the potent family bonding, sense of love or reciprocity, spiritual fulfillment or cultural norms. Majority of the dementia caregivers in this study offered unpaid service with 74 caregivers being employed. Similarly, a study conducted by Parveen and coworkers demonstrated that nearly half of the caregivers were children of persons living with dementia, and about one-third were the spouses of the persons living with dementia.32 It was proposed earlier that positive outcomes are associated with closer relationships between caregivers and persons living with dementia.27 The closer relationships in our study included offspring/spouses and showed better safety adherence practices in terms of positive experiences such as feeling a reciprocal bond, personal and spiritual growth, sharing activities, increased faith, enjoying togetherness and feelings of task accomplishments.

Regarding the duration of caregiving, half of the caregivers provided full-time assistance, with 40% caring for more than 5 years. Moreover, a considerable number of caregivers spent extensive time with persons living with dementia, with half of them devoting more than 12 hours to caring the persons living with dementia. Janssen and coworkers (2020) have reported mean duration as 0.7 years, 1.7 years and 1.1 years for elderly persons living with dementia without formal care, home care and institutional care, respectively.33 Parveen et al (2022) also recorded that the average time spent for daily caregiving was 7.4 ± 2.9 hours per day, while the role of caregivers’ mean duration was 3.6 ± 3.0 years.32 Notably, caregivers with extended caregiving durations (more than 5 years) demonstrated significantly better home safety practices as compared to those with shorter durations.

Recent review has witnessed the importance that caregivers who received specific dementia care training exhibited superior home safety practices.34 Our study showed that majority of caregivers having training experience demonstrated good practice. This implies that formal training in dementia care positively influenced caregivers’ ability to provide adequate home safety measures for persons living with dementia. According to a recent study, most caregivers were aware of aspects related to dementia care in terms of a safety practice scale.35 Such training reduces caregiver burnout by promoting understanding of the disease, helps manage daily activities and fosters safer environments.

Caregivers experiencing feelings of depression were associated with poor home safety practices as compared to those not feeling depressed. This suggests a potential impact of caregivers’ mental well-being on their capacity to maintain a safe environment for persons living with dementia. The previous study reported 40% prevalence of depression among dementia caregivers.36 Earlier investigators have also demonstrated that depression among caregivers was highly correlated with poor practice as compared to mentally stable caregivers37–40 as has been depicted in our findings. It has been further explored that depression affecting the caregiver can be raised due to higher severity of dementia and lower support from others. Therefore, the support of other family members has a strong effect on the caregiver’s level of depression. Moreover, caregiver burden correlates with symptoms of anxiety and depression, which were directly or indirectly associated with poorer psychological and physical health outcomes. As a result, the healthcare team should encourage the caregivers and their family members to cooperate as a team in providing care and support as much as possible.8 Linear regression results further emphasized the multifaceted nature of caregiving for persons living with dementia, highlighting the significance of factors such as the duration of caregiving, and the emotional state of caregivers in influencing the quality of home safety practices provided to persons living with dementia. The importance of reducing cognitive load and modifying emotional regulation and environmental demands on dementia caregiver behavior has been explained by earlier investigators.41–43

Understanding these correlations is crucial for developing targeted interventions and support systems to enhance caregivers’ abilities in providing safe and effective care for individuals affected by dementia. Due to the increased reliance on informal caregivers, advanced measures like digital interventions, behavioral management and employment of robots can help to lessen the burden on caregivers. This technological advancement can provide mental and emotional healthcare support to persons living with dementia as well as caregivers and could help in filling a gap in current healthcare practices.44–47 Moreover, devising a policy for dementia caregivers should focus on receiving professional training to manage behaviors, understand the disease process and improve their own well-being, and a person-centered approach. Integrated care approaches should encourage collaboration between health and social services to provide comprehensive support.

Limitations

Limitations of the current study include the adoption of a survey method study design and the convenient sampling technique in a single-center private outpatient setting. The sample size was relatively small, especially for the planned logistic regression analysis which may impact the statistical power and precision of the results, and suggests the need for larger, multi-center studies in the future. Given the constraints of the convenient sampling approach and the modest sample size, the study can be considered as exploratory in nature. Hence, a larger-scale multi-center national study is recommended. Also, the assessment of the caregivers’ well-being by a detailed Patient Health Questionnaire-9 is recommended to obtain more reliable results.

Conclusion

This study found that caregivers who work extended hours and have completed dementia training demonstrate improved home safety practices because their caregiving experience helps them manage depressive symptoms. The study showed that caregiver skill development occurs through experience, educational programs and mental state. It was also observed that caregiver training programs need to include mental health support, as it helps create safe environments that benefit people living with dementia. Existing caregiver training was evaluated immediately after completion in the current study, failing to track whether skills are maintained over time (months or years later). Hence, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm their impact on long-term caregiver depression. The study further recommends conducting extensive multi-center studies to determine how structured training and psychological treatment methods can affect dementia caregiver practices over time. Future longitudinal and multi-center research are needed to better assess temporal patterns and causal pathways influencing caregivers’ home safety behaviors, and the findings found should not be taken as causal linkages.
