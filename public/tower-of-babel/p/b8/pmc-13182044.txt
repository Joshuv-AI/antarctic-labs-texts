Anemia and risk of ischemic heart disease among adults with hypertension in Thailand
Authors: Boonsub Sakboonyarat, Jaturon Poovieng, Naphat Wongkliawrian, Kanlaya Jongcherdchootrakul, Mathirut Mungthin, Ram Rangsin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe examined the association between anemia and ischemic heart disease (IHD) risk in adults with hypertension in Thailand.ResultsA total of 14,744 participants were analyzed. The mean hemoglobin (Hb) level was 12.6 ± 1.8 g/dL, and 5,867 participants (39.8%) had anemia. During follow-up, 88 incident IHD cases were recorded, corresponding to an incidence of 6.0 per 1,000 persons. The adjusted risk ratio (aRR) for IHD corresponding to a one g/dL decrease in Hb level was 1.16 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02–1.32). Compared with participants without anemia, those with mild and moderate-to-severe anemia were associated with increased IHD risk, with aRRs of 1.73 (95% CI 1.02–2.92) and 1.82 (95% CI 1.02–3.26), respectively. Participants with anemia had an aRR of 1.76 (95% CI 1.09–2.84) for IHD compared to those without. Considering anemia and hypertension control status, individuals with anemia and uncontrolled hypertension were observed to have a higher risk of IHD (aRR 1.98; 95% CI 1.02–3.82) compared to those with no anemia and controlled hypertension. Anemia is a significant, independent risk marker for IHD in Thai adults with hypertension. Screening for anemia could help identify a high-risk subgroup for more intensive cardiovascular monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07807-5.

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) is a pathological condition that occurs when the cardiac muscle does not receive enough blood supply to meet its oxygen requirements. It is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, especially in high-risk populations like those with hypertension [1–3].

Anemia is a global health issue, with disparities based on age, sex, and geography. It occurs when there are abnormal red blood cells or low hemoglobin (Hb) concentrations, crucial for oxygen transport. Existing studies revealed that anemia is associated with an increased risk of IHD and poorer outcomes in coronary artery disease (CAD) patients [4–6]. For example, the ARIC study found a 41% higher risk of IHD related to anemia (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.41, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01–1.95) [5]. Similarly, a study of Chinese patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention identified anemia as a predictor of ischemic events, with aHR of 2.20 (95% CI 1.61–3.00) [7]. However, data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey indicated that men with higher hematocrit (Hct) levels had a lower risk for CAD mortality (adjusted risk ratio (aRR) 0.90, 95% CI 0.70–1.20). This underscores the complex relationship between anemia and CAD mortality, revealing challenges in adjusting for confounding factors in patients with anemia [8, 9].

Hypertension is a significant risk factor for IHD [10]. This increased risk occurs through two primary mechanisms: oxidative stress and mechanical stress, both of which impact the arterial walls [11, 12]. The 2019 GBD study revealed that 54.6% of IHD disability-adjusted life years are due to high systolic blood pressure (BP) globally and 58.2% in Southeast Asia, including Thailand [13].

The 2019 National Health Examination (NHES) in Thailand revealed that 25.4% of adults have hypertension [14]. Among those receiving continuous care, 66.6% had controlled hypertension (BP < 140/90 mmHg) during their most recent visit, and 50% achieved this control at their two latest visits [15]. A recent study found that 3.6% of Thai adults with hypertension also have a history of IHD [15]. The 2019 NHES revealed that 16.2% of Thai adults had anemia, with 21.0% in women and 11.0% in men [14]. However, limited information exists on anemia prevalence among adults with hypertension and its relation to IHD risk in Thailand. We aimed to evaluate the association between anemia and IHD risk in Thai adults with hypertension and assess the effect of hypertension control measure modification.

Methods

Study designs and subjects

A retrospective cohort study with a short follow-up period was conducted to investigate the association between anemia and the new onset of IHD. We used data from the 2018 Thailand DM/HT study database, with permission from the National Health Security Office [16]. The Thailand DM/HT study assessed clinical outcomes, including hypertension control status and CVD complications in patients with hypertension, as previously described by Sakboonyarat et al. [15, 16]. The study involved individuals aged 20 and above with hypertension who had been receiving continuous care at outpatient clinics in 1072 hospitals nationwide.

The 2018 Thailand DM/HT study, which spanned from January 2018 to June 2018, initially included 36,557 patients with hypertension in its database [16]. The primary analysis included 14,744 hypertensive patients without baseline IHD and available Hb information after excluding 1,198 with IHD history before 12 months and 20,615 without Hb data (Supplementary Table S1).

The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board, the Royal Thai Army Medical Department (approval number S055h/65_Exp). A waiver of informed consent was granted because of the use of secondary data.

Outcome and exposure

The risk of IHD in people with hypertension was determined by new onset within 12 months, based on ICD-10 codes (I20–I22 and I25) [15]. The details of the case assertion are provided in the supplementary methods. The study analyzed baseline Hb levels, focusing on anemia, defined by the World Health Organization as an Hb level below 13 g/dl for men and below 12 g/dl for women [17]. Anemia status was classified into three categories: no anemia, mild anemia, and moderate-to-severe anemia. Mild anemia was defined as Hb levels of 11 to < 13 g/dl for men and 11 to < 12 g/dl for women, while moderate-to-severe anemia was defined as Hb levels < 11 g/dl for both sexes [17]. Hypertension control was defined as BP < 140/90 mmHg for two consecutive times at the latest visit and one time before the latest visit [15]. The details of data collection and covariates are provided in the supplementary methods.

Statistical analysis

We conducted all data analysis using StataCorp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC. Statistical significance was considered with a two-sided p-value less than 0.05. The risk of IHD was calculated and presented as per 1000 people and a 95% CI. We used log-binomial regression analysis to evaluate the association between anemia and the risk of IHD. The multivariable analysis was adjusted for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and medications, and the results are presented as aRR and 95% CI. We also conducted a subgroup analysis among individuals with controlled hypertension and those with uncontrolled hypertension, as well as tested the interaction to explore the effect modification of hypertension control status. We also calculated the average adjusted prediction of the risk of IHD stratified by anemia and hypertension control status, comparing it to the group with no anemia and controlled hypertension as the reference group.

For the sensitivity analysis, we used marginal structural models (MSMs) to determine the association between anemia and the risk of IHD among the study participants. In this analysis, we constructed stabilized inverse probability attrition weights (IPAW) for the total population (with and without Hb information). Multivariable logistic regression was used to model the absence of Hb information based on age, sex, health insurance scheme, geographic region, location of outpatient clinic, occupation, smoking status, alcohol use, T2D, DLP, renal insufficiency, insulin use, statin use, ACEI/ARB use, antiplatelet use, obesity, BP control status to predict IPAW. Additionally, logistic regression was used to predict the absence of an Hb record and estimate the stabilized constant for IPAW. The stabilized constant divided by IPAW was used to calculate the stabilized IPAW for MSMs. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding using E-values estimated by the evalue package, as detailed in the Supplementary methods [18].

Association between anemia and the risk of IHD

Within a year, 88 new-onset IHD events were observed, corresponding to 6.0 events per 1,000 people (95% CI 4.7–7.2). The age- and sex-adjusted risk of IHD was found to be 3.8 (95% CI 2.5–5.2), 7.7 (95% CI 4.9–10.5), and 10.2 (95% CI 6.2–14.2) per 1,000 people in individuals with no anemia, with mild anemia, and with moderate-to-severe anemia, respectively (Fig. 1). Decreased Hb level was associated with an increased risk of IHD (aRR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.32) (Fig. 2). Individuals with mild and moderate-to-severe anemia were observed to have a higher risk of IHD compared to those without anemia, with aRRs of 1.73 (95% CI 1.02–2.97) and 1.82 (95% CI 1.02–3.26), respectively. The sensitivity analysis used MSMs to account for patients with hypertension without Hb information in their medical records, yielding results similar to those of the primary analysis (Table 2). Hb measurements in routine care may be obtained preferentially in individuals perceived by clinicians to be at higher clinical risk, potentially biasing associations despite MSM adjustment. The E-value for the aRR = 1.76, which indicates the association between the unmeasured confounder and factors related to the risk of IHD, was 2.91 (see Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

Among Thai adults with hypertension, lower Hb levels were associated with a higher risk of IHD; each one g/dL decrease corresponded to a 16% higher risk. Mild and moderate-to-severe anemia were also associated with a 73% and 82% higher risk of IHD, respectively, although these estimates were relatively imprecise. Overall, individuals with anemia were observed to have a 1.76-fold risk of IHD compared to those without, a finding consistent with the sensitivity analysis showing a 77% higher risk. The short follow-up limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between anemia and IHD.

The study revealed that 39.8% of participants had anemia, consistent with Asian countries' prevalence of 17% to 56% among individuals with hypertension [19, 20]. In Thai adults with hypertension, anemia was associated with IHD, consistent with previous research, despite limited evidence from Asian countries. The ARIC study found that anemia was associated with increased risk of IHD (aHR 1.41, 95% CI 1.01–1.95) [5]. A study of 6,116 patients with percutaneous coronary intervention found that severe anemia was linked to increased CVD mortality, with 30-day event rates of 4.3% for severe anemia, 3.4% for mild anemia, and 1.2% for no anemia (p = 0.002) [4]. Another study in the U.S. found no significant association between Hct tertiles and CAD mortality in men, but women in the upper tertile had a 1.3 times higher mortality risk [8].

In the subgroup analysis of hypertension control status, the point estimates indicate a higher risk in both subgroups; however, these findings were not statistically significant, likely due to smaller sample sizes. The non-significant interaction term (p = 0.695) suggests there is no statistical evidence to show that the effect of anemia varies based on hypertension control status.

When considering anemia and hypertension control status simultaneously, we found that individuals with anemia and uncontrolled hypertension were observed to have a 98% higher risk of IHD than those with no anemia and controlled hypertension (Fig. 3). Although this result is statistically significant, it is based on a small number of cases, which may affect its stability.

The Thai hypertension Guidelines recommend annual anemia screening for all people with hypertension [21]. Screening coverage should be improved at treatment initiation and annually thereafter. Patients with both anemia and uncontrolled hypertension warrant close monitoring to mitigate future IHD risk.

Anemia is linked to IHD through systemic hypoxia and myocardial ischemia. While acute or chronic anemia is typically associated with these conditions [9], research has shown that reducing Hb to 5 g/dL in healthy individuals does not induce hypoxia or ischemia [22]. This suggests that the cardiovascular impact of anemia is likely modulated by the presence of pre-existing conditions that compromise compensatory mechanisms. For example, in individuals with hypertension [23] and comorbidities such as severe coronary artery lesions, acute anemia can result in transient hypoxia and ischemia [9]. Those with mild coronary lesions may also experience transient ischemia during episodes of severe acute anemia, such as those caused by gastrointestinal bleeding [24].

Due to hemodynamic changes, chronic anemia can result in LVH [9, 25–27]. Reduced tissue oxygenation in chronic anemia can lead to hemodynamic changes like increased systemic arterial dilation, decreased systemic vascular resistance, reduced afterload, and increased stroke volume [9, 28]. The sympathetic nervous system is activated in the anemic state, leading to an increased heart rate [9, 28]. All these changes result in an increased cardiac workload, leading to LVH [24, 26, 27]. Over time, LVH can result in the development of IHD [29–31].

While some anemias, such as normocytic anemia associated with chronic disease, are linked to CVD [9], others, like macrocytic anemia resulting from nutritional deficiencies, may not be [32]. It is essential to determine the cause of anemia to assess its potential influence on IHD. Our findings support the routine screening for anemia in this population. Identifying anemia should prompt clinicians to investigate its underlying cause (e.g., nutritional deficiencies, chronic kidney disease), as these conditions are themselves potent cardiovascular risk factors and may represent actionable therapeutic targets.

Limitations

The short follow-up limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between anemia and IHD. About 60% of people with hypertension lacked Hb data, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting the generalizability, though we used MSMs to minimize this, with consistent sensitivity analysis results. Hypertension control relied on two consecutive assessments, which may lead to misclassification bias in subgroup analyses. Unavailable data on physical activity, dietary behavior, and inflammatory markers could lead to residual confounding in our multivariable model. However, our sensitivity analysis suggests that substantial unmeasured confounding (E-value = 2.91) would be required to change the association between anemia and IHD risk.
