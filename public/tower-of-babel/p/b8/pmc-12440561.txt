Association between dietary index for gut microbiota and kidney stones with body mass index as a potential mediator: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Wen Wei, Zhixin Li, Yunjie Yang, Yan Deng, Jing Wu, Kaiying He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dietary habits are associated with kidney stones, yet the association between the newly proposed dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM), which assesses the impact of diet on gut microbiota health, and kidney stones remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate the association between DI-GM and kidney stones. This cross-sectional study utilized data from the 2007 to 2020 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, involving adults aged 20 years and older. DI-GM was calculated using dietary recall data, while the kidney stone status was determined based on self-reported questionnaire responses. The associations between DI-GM and kidney stones were explored using multivariable logistic regression, with mediation analysis conducted to assess the potential mediating effect of body mass index (BMI). A total of 26,712 participants were included, with a kidney stones prevalence of 9.91%. In the fully adjusted logistic regression model, higher DI-GM scores and beneficial gut microbiota scores were associated with a lower prevalence of kidney stones (DI-GM, odds ratio: 0.94, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.90–0.98; beneficial gut microbiota, odds ratio: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.85–0.98). Furthermore, BMI mediated the association between DI-GM and kidney stones (mediation proportion: 12.32%, 95% CI: 7.39%–22.4%, P < .001). DI-GM was negatively associated with kidney stones prevalence, with BMI mediating this association. These findings suggest a potential role of diet in kidney stone risk reduction.

1. Introduction

Kidney stones are a common condition in the urinary system, characterized by the deposition of crystalline substances in the kidneys, which gradually form hardened crystals leading to stone formation. In recent years, the prevalence of kidney stones has been rising globally,[1] becoming an important public health issue. In the United States, the prevalence of kidney stones in adults ranges from 9% to 11%.[2,3] Kidney stones not only significantly affect the quality of life but can also lead to severe complications such as renal dysfunction and urinary tract infections. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms behind kidney stone formation, as well as effective prevention and treatment strategies, is of great importance. Therefore, exploring the risk factors for kidney stones and finding effective prevention strategies becomes especially important.

Studies have shown that dietary habits are associated with the occurrence and recurrence of kidney stones,[4] and dietary recommendations and nutritional adjustments play an important role in the treatment and prevention of kidney stone.[5,6] Moreover, research suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis may serve as a significant risk factor for kidney stones,[7,8] and modulating the gut microbiome could be beneficial in preventing both the formation and recurrence of kidney stones.[9–11] And dietary factors are crucial in regulating the composition of the gut microbiome.[12–14] Therefore, adopting a diet that benefits gut microbiota health could be a novel strategy for kidney stone prevention.

Recently, Kase et al conducted a systematic review of interventional and longitudinal studies on the association between diet and gut microbiota. They identified 14 dietary components, noting that some are beneficial while others may have adverse effects on the gut microbiota.[15] Based on this, they developed the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM), which reflects the association between dietary quality and gut microbiota health. Further study has demonstrated a significant positive association between DI-GM and the indirect biomarker of gut microbiota diversity, urinary enterodiol and enterolactone. This suggests that DI-GM is positively associated with gut microbiota diversity. Thus, DI-GM provides a powerful tool for assessing dietary habits that promote or harm gut microbiota health, ultimately helping to quantify and optimize dietary quality to improve gut health.

Body mass index (BMI) is a commonly used measure for assessing obesity and disease risks, and has been confirmed to be associated with the risk of kidney stones.[16] Additionally, obesity is closely associated with gut microbiome imbalance.[17] A study has shown that dietary components such as DL-norvaline can target the gut microbiota to alleviate obesity-related diseases in mice.[18]

Compared to existing dietary indices such as the oxidative balance score, alternative Mediterranean diet score, and healthy eating index-2015, the DI-GM focuses more on the regulation of the overall gut microbial environment by diet. It provides a unique perspective to study the interaction between microbiota and the host, rather than directly measuring specific microbial species or their abundance.[15] Previous studies generally suggest that a higher DI-GM score is associated with more favorable health outcomes, such as lower prevalence of metabolic syndrome, constipation, and hyperlipidemia,[19–21] as well as higher serum albumin levels in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD).[22] However, the association between DI-GM and kidney stones remains underexplored, particularly with regard to the potential mediating role of BMI.

This study aims to explore the association between the DI-GM and kidney stones using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Additionally, it seeks to examine the potential mediating role of BMI in this association. We hypothesize that higher DI-GM scores, indicating diets beneficial to gut microbiota, are associated with a lower risk of kidney stones, with BMI potentially serving as a partial mediator in this association. Through this research, we hope to provide new insights into the prevention of kidney stones.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This study analyzed data from NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics, which is designed to assess the health and nutrition of adults and children in the United States. NHANES utilizes a complex, stratified, multistage probability sampling design to ensure representative national samples. The survey collects interview data on health, diet, and personal, social, and economic characteristics, as well as physical measurement data from visits to the mobile examination center, and laboratory test data conducted by highly trained medical professionals. The research protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. As a secondary analysis of de-identified public data, no additional ethical review was required. All procedures followed the ethical principles set forth in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions, or comparable ethical standards. More details can be found on the official NHANES website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/) (accessed on January 10, 2025).

This study included adults aged 20 years and older from the 2007 to 2020 NHANES cycles, comprising a total of 38,433 participants. Exclusion criteria included participants with missing self-reported kidney stones data (N = 1839), missing DI-GM component data (N = 2926), missing BMI data (N = 360), pregnant women (N = 375), and those with missing covariate data (N = 6221). After applying these exclusion criteria, the final sample consisted of 26,712 participants (Fig. 1).

Flowchart depicting the screening process of NHANES 2007 to 2020 participants. BMI = body mass index, DI-GM = dietary index for gut microbiota, NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Assessment of DI-GM

According to the study by Kase et al,[15] the DI-GM scoring system includes 14 food items or nutrients. DI-GM scores were calculated based on the average intake from 2 independent 24-hour dietary recall interviews for each participant. Beneficial components include chickpeas, avocados, cranberries, coffee, fermented dairy products, green tea (not available in NHANES due to the lack of specific tea consumption data), broccoli, fiber, soybean, and whole grains. In contrast, harmful components include processed meats, red meats, refined grains, and a high-fat diet (≥40% of energy from fat). During the scoring process, foods beneficial to gut health are assigned a score of 1 if their intake exceeds the gender-specific median, and a score of 0 if below the median. For foods detrimental to the gut microbiome, a score of 0 is given when their consumption is ≥gender-specific median or when fat intake is ≥40% (i.e., high-fat diet), otherwise a score of 1 is assigned. The gender-specific medians for each DI-GM component were calculated based on the distribution among participants in the NHANES sample who had complete data for all DI-GM components. The total DI-GM score ranges from 0 to 13, with participants grouped into 0 to 3, 4, 5, and ≥6 categories based on existing literature.[23–25] A higher DI-GM score indicates a greater benefit to the gut microbiome and reflects the positive role of diet in promoting gut health. A higher DI-GM score indicates a more beneficial impact on gut microbiota, reflecting the positive role of diet in supporting gut health. For detailed information on the components and scoring standards of the DI-GM system, refer Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P961.

2.3. Definition of kidney stones and BMI

Participants were asked, “Have you ever had kidney stones?” If they answered “Yes,” they were classified as having kidney stones. BMI was calculated as the ratio of weight (kg) to the square of height (m).

2.4. Covariates

Covariates were selected based on the following 3 criteria: the variable changed the matched odds ratio (OR) by at least 10% when added to the multivariable logistic regression model; the variable had a P-value <0.1 in univariate analysis; and the variable was considered clinically meaningful based on prior literature and clinical knowledge.[23,26,27] Based on these criteria, the following potential confounders were included: age, sex, marital status, race, poverty income ratio (PIR), education level, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, total energy intake, protein intake, dietary fiber, calcium intake, phosphorus intake, vitamin D intake, vitamin C intake, water intake, uric acid, CKD, cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, and diabetes. Marital status was categorized as married/living with a partner, and other (widowed, divorced, separated, or never married). Race/ethnicity categories included non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican-American, and others. PIR was divided into 3 groups: ≤1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, and >3.5.[28] Education level was classified into 3 groups: less than high school, high school or equivalent, and above high school. Smoking and drinking status were categorized as never, former, and current, according to prior studies.[29,30] Physical activity was measured in MET-minutes per week.[31] CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/g.[32] Estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation and albumin-to-creatinine ratio (mg/g) was calculated by dividing urinary protein concentration (mg/L) by urinary creatinine concentration (mg/dL). A history of CVD was defined as self-reported diagnosis by a doctor or other health professional of any of the following conditions: coronary heart disease, angina, stroke, heart attack, or congestive heart failure. Hypertension was defined as meeting any of the following diagnostic criteria: mean systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, mean diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of antihypertensive medication.[33] The diagnostic criteria for diabetes was based on any one of the following conditions: doctor told you have diabetes, glycohemoglobin (%) ≥6.5, fasting glucose (mmol/L) ≥7.0, random blood glucose (mmol/L) ≥11.1, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test blood glucose (mmol/L) ≥11.1, or use of diabetes medication or insulin.[29]

2.5. Statistical analysis

Following the NHANES analysis guidelines, our study accounted for the complex sampling design and dietary sample weights.[34] Based on the DI-GM scores, participants were divided into 4 groups: group 1 (scores 0–3), group 2 (score 4), group 3 (score 5), and group 4 (score ≥6), for the description of the study population characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as weighted means and standard errors, while categorical variables are reported as unweighted frequencies and weighted percentages. Group differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

We employed multivariable weighted logistic regression models to estimate the association between DI-GM scores and kidney stones. Crude model was an unadjusted model. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, marital status, race/ethnicity, education level, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity. Model 2 further adjusted for energy, protein, dietary fiber, water intake, vitamin C, vitamin D, calcium intake, phosphorus intake, and uric acid. Model 3 built upon Model 2, additionally adjusting for disease history, including CKD, CVD, hypertension, and diabetes. We also performed restricted cubic spline curve fitting analysis to evaluate whether there is a potential nonlinear relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones, adjusting for all covariates in Model 3.

To explore the mediating role of BM in the association between DI-GM and kidney stones, we conducted mediation analysis using the Sobel test, Bootstrap method, and a quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo method with 1000 simulations based on normal approximation. Additionally, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted based on sex, age (<60 or ≥60 years), drinking status (yes or no), hypertension status (yes or no), diabetes status (yes or no), CVD status (yes or no), and CKD status (yes or no) to assess the association between DI-GM and kidney stones in different population subgroups. Finally, to evaluate the robustness of the results, we performed sensitivity analyses. First, to mitigate the impact of missing covariates, we conducted multiple imputation (5 imputations) using chained equations and reported pooled results. Then, individuals with extreme energy intake (<500 kcal/day or >5000 kcal/day) were excluded from the analysis.[35]

All data analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Free Software Foundation statistics software (version 2.1; Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The “survey” package (version 4.4.2) was used for survey sample analysis, the “mediation” package (version 4.5.0) for mediation analysis, and the “mice” package (version 3.15.0) for multivariate imputation. Statistical significance was determined using 2-sided P-values <.05.

3.2. Association between DI-GM and kidney stones

As shown in Table 2, the results of the multivariable logistic regression indicated a modest inverse association between DI-GM scores and the risk of kidney stones. In the unadjusted model, each point increase in DI-GM score was associated with a 6% reduction in the prevalence of kidney stones (OR: 0.94, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.91–0.98). This modest association remained statistically significant in Model 3 (OR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98), after fully adjusting for covariates. Further grouping of DI-GM scores revealed that, compared to the DI-GM 0 to 3 group, participants with DI-GM ≥ 6 had about a 24% lower risk of kidney stones (OR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.63–0.93). Additionally, as beneficial to gut microbiota scores increased, the risk of kidney stones tended to decrease (OR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.85–0.98), while the association between unfavorable to gut microbiota scores and kidney stones was not significant. After adjusting for all covariates in Model 3, restricted cubic spline analysis showed that DI-GM, as well as beneficial and unfavorable gut microbiota scores, showed linear associations with kidney stones (all P for nonlinearity > 0.05) (Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P960).

3.3. Mediation analyses

Figure 2 illustrates the potential mediating role of BMI in the association between DI-GM and kidney stones. We found that a higher DI-GM was associated with a lower BMI (β: −0.38, 95% CI: −0.44 to −0.32). Meanwhile, a higher BMI was associated with an increased risk of kidney stones (OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03). Further analysis revealed that BMI partially mediated the association between DI-GM and kidney stones (mediation proportion: 12.32%, 95% CI: 7.39%–22.4%, P < .001).

4. Discussion

This study demonstrated that DI-GM and beneficial to gut microbiota scores were negatively associated with the prevalence of kidney stones. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness and reliability of these findings. Additionally, BMI was found to play a mediating role in this association.

Dietary factors play a significant role in kidney stone formation. Both high carbohydrate and animal protein intake have been associated with an increased risk of kidney stones.[36–38] In contrast, higher dietary fiber intake has been shown to reduce this risk.[39] Additionally, dietary patterns significantly influence the gut microbiome. A high-protein diet was reported to decrease the abundance of beneficial gut microbes, such as Akkermansia and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, in rats.[40] Conversely, Vegan diets, regular vegetable consumption, and higher fiber intake have all been associated with increased gut microbiome diversity and abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Clostridium species.[12,41,42] Furthermore, dietary patterns rich in fermented foods have been demonstrated to enhance gut microbiome diversity and reduce inflammatory markers.[43]

In addition, reduced gut microbiome diversity has been proven to increase the risk of urinary tract stones.[44,45] Chen et al identified several potential biomarkers for calcium oxalate stones, including Bacteroides, Phascolarctobacterium, Faecalibacterium, Akkermansia, and Lactobacillus, with Phascolarctobacterium and Faecalibacterium being positively associated with short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production that may help reduce kidney stone risk.[46] Gut microbiota dysbiosis may promote stones by disrupting oxalate metabolism, intestinal barrier function, and inducing renal inflammation.[7,45] Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis pointed out that kidney stone patients commonly exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis, and that adjusting dietary patterns or supplementing with microbes, probiotics, or synbiotics may help prevent stone formation.[8]

Our findings are consistent with these studies, showing that DI-GM and beneficial gut microbiota scores are negatively associated with kidney stones. This suggests that dietary modulation of gut microbiota may help prevention. Zheng and Cao also found higher dietary live microbe intake was associated with a decreased risk of kidney stones, aligning with our results.[9]

Moreover, significant differences in the composition of gut microbiota between obese and lean individuals have been reported.[47,48] Modulation of gut microbiota with prebiotics and probiotics offers multiple benefits, including restoring microbial balance, reducing inflammation, and lowering body fat.[49,50] Additionally, it improved metabolic parameters such as alleviating insulin resistance and facilitating weight loss.[51] Furthermore, a multicenter, double-blind, randomized placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that fermented milk containing Lactobacillus gasseri SBT2055 significantly reduced body weight, waist circumference, BMI, and abdominal fat.[52] Another randomized controlled trial confirmed that probiotic supplementation not only reduced body weight and visceral fat but also alleviated systemic inflammation associated with fat accumulation.[53]

Our study also found an association between DI-GM and BMI, with higher DI-GM potentially contributing to metabolic health maintenance and reducing obesity risk. A randomized controlled trial involving overweight premenopausal women showed that consuming fermented dairy products (foods linked to beneficial microbes in the DI-GM) significantly reduced blood lipid levels.[54] Conversely, lower DI-GM often reflects poor dietary habits, such as high-fat intake and excessive consumption of processed meats, which may trigger inflammation and disrupt fat metabolism, leading to increased BMI.[55–57]

Cohort studies conducted in Japan and China, together with our study, have consistently found that higher BMI is associated with increased kidney stone risk.[16,58] However, a meta-analysis reported that overweight and obesity increased the risk of calcium oxalate stones, but showed no association with calcium phosphate stones.[59] Due to the lack of detailed stone type data in the NHANES database, we are unable to specify the association between DI-GM and different types of kidney stones, which remains a limitation of our study. Future research should address this gap.

Although the underlying mechanisms linking the gut microbiota, BMI, and kidney stones have not been fully elucidated, there are some possible biological explanations. SCFAs, such as butyrate (key metabolites produced by the gut microbiota) help maintain the gut barrier by regulating the expression and distribution of tight junction proteins including claudin-1 and zonula occludens-1.[60,61] A decrease in SCFA levels leads to increased intestinal permeability, facilitating the paracellular absorption of oxalate and other relevant compounds.[62] Butyrate can also upregulate the expression and promoter activity of SLC26A3 via modulation of specific transcription factors, influencing oxalate transporter function.[63] Moreover, SCFAs regulate lipid metabolism and adipose tissue homeostasis, enhancing energy expenditure and inhibiting adipogenesis.[64] Metabolically active visceral fat secretes proinflammatory cytokines that increase urinary oxalate concentration and induce systemic inflammation.[65] SCFAs further suppress the NF-κB signaling pathway, reducing local and systemic levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.[66] Activation of inflammasomes and upregulation of inflammation-related molecules are closely associated with kidney stone formation.[67]

The main strength of this study lies in its use of a large, nationally representative sample to comprehensively analyze the association between DI-GM, which reflects gut microbiota diversity, and kidney stones, thereby ensuring the reliability and robustness of the findings. Moreover, we observed a mediating effect of BMI in this association. Overall, our study indicated that higher DI-GM was associated with a reduced risk of kidney stones, offering potential new strategies for prevention. However, there are several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causality. Future longitudinal studies and intervention trials are needed to clarify the causal association between DI-GM and kidney stones and assess their long-term effects. Second, DI-GM was based on self-reported 24-hour dietary recall data, which may introduce recall bias. Although averaging 2 independent recalls helps estimate usual intake more accurately, recall variability remains, possibly leading to misclassification of habitual dietary patterns. Third, although we controlled for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding due to unknown or unmeasured factors may still affect the results. Specifically, factors such as genetic predisposition, for example, a family history of kidney stones, and environmental exposures, like water quality, could contribute to this residual confounding. Finally, the DI-GM was developed based on dietary and gut microbiota data from US adults, and its applicability to populations with different dietary patterns and microbiota profiles is uncertain. Therefore, external validation in non-US cohorts is necessary to determine its generalizability.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort studies and randomized controlled trials to clarify the temporal sequence and causal pathways linking diet, gut microbiota, BMI, and kidney stone risk. Intervention studies incorporating dietary modifications or gut microbiota-targeted therapies, coupled with longitudinal microbiome profiling and metabolic phenotyping, would provide more robust mechanistic evidence and inform personalized prevention strategies.

5. Conclusions

These findings suggest that diets promoting gut microbial health may be associated with reduced kidney stone risk, with BMI as a partial mediator.
