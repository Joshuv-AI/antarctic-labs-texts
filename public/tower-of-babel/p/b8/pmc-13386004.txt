Anthropometry-adjusted TyG indices predict diabetes, graft failure, and mortality in kidney transplant recipients
Authors: Jiachuan Xiong, Tamas Szili-Torok, Ömer Tarik Özyilmaz, Weiwei Xu, Juul S Daamen, Jip Jonker, Dion Groothof, Daan Kremer, Antonio Gomes Neto, Robert A Pol, Martin H de Borst, Stephan J L Bakker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a widely used surrogate marker of insulin resistance and associated with cardiometabolic outcomes. However, the comparative prognostic value of TyG-derived indices, particularly those incorporating anthropometric measures or long-term variability, for adverse outcomes after kidney transplantation remains unclear.ObjectiveTo compare the prognostic performance of 15 TyG-derived indices for predicting new-onset diabetes after transplantation (NODAT), graft failure, and all-cause mortality in kidney transplant recipients (KTRs).DesignThis prospective observational study was conducted within the TransplantLines Biobank and Cohort study and included 464 KTRs (median age, 56.9 years; 65.1% male) followed for a median of 71 months. Fifteen TyG-derived indices were classified into metabolic, anthropometry-adjusted, and variability-based domains. Their prognostic performance was assessed using an analytical framework integrating machine learning with conventional statistical methods.ResultsComparative analyses identified 4 indices, TyG-WWI, TyG-WHR, TyG mean, and TyG-BMI, as having superior predictive performance. Among them, anthropometry-adjusted indices (TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI) showed the most stable associations across 3 outcomes. In multivariable analyses, TyG-WHR independently predicted NODAT (HR 2.30, 95% CI 1.21-4.39), graft failure (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.18-4.18), and mortality (HR 1.87, 95% CI 1.19-2.96), while TyG-WWI independently predicted mortality (HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.11-3.86). Cross-validation showed no consistent advantage of combined models over single well-performing indices.ConclusionAmong adult KTRs from the TransplantLines cohort with a functioning graft, TyG-WWI and TyG-WHR showed the most consistent associations with adverse post-transplant outcomes. Their potential role as complementary metabolic risk markers requires validation in larger and more diverse transplant cohorts.Keywords: kidney recipient, triglyceride-glucose derived index, diabetes, graft failure, mortality, machine learning

Kidney transplantation is widely recognized as the optimal renal replacement therapy for patients with kidney failure, providing substantial improvements in survival and quality of life compared with long-term dialysis [1-4]. Despite advances in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive regimens, and post-transplant care, kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) remain vulnerable to a broad spectrum of complications and late adverse outcomes [5-7]. These include graft function decline and allograft loss, as well as metabolic complications such as new-onset diabetes after transplantation (NODAT), which in turn are associated with worse long-term patient and graft outcomes [8, 9].

Insulin resistance is a central mechanism underlying cardiometabolic disorders [10]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, derived from fasting triglycerides (TGs) and glucose, has been proposed and widely used as an easy-to-measure surrogate marker of insulin resistance [11]. TyG has been linked to cardiovascular events and mortality in multiple populations, supporting its prognostic relevance beyond glycemic control alone [12, 13]. Recently, several TyG-derived indices incorporate anthropometric parameters to better capture body fat distribution and metabolic risk [14, 15]. For example, TyG multiplied by waist-to-hip ratio (TyG-WHR) and TyG multiplied by a body-shape index (TyG-ABSI) reflect central adiposity [16, 17], TyG multiplied by the hypertriglyceridemic waist phenotype (TyG-HTGW) approximates visceral fat dysfunction [18], and TyG multiplied by weight-adjusted waist index (TyG-WWI) adjusts metabolic burden to body-composition independent of body mass index (BMI) [19]. These indices integrate metabolic and body-shape information and are strongly associated with cardiometabolic complications [20]. However, evidence suggests that the prognostic performance of these TyG-derived indices can differ across disease contexts and outcomes, indicating that the optimal TyG-derived metric may be population- and outcome-specific [21].

In KTRs, cardiometabolic disturbances are common, often manifesting as NODAT, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity [22, 23]. Several studies have begun to evaluate the clinical implications of TyG in kidney transplant populations, including associations with adverse graft outcomes and composite endpoints [24]. However, existing evidence remains fragmented, typically focusing on single indices or single outcomes. To date, a comprehensive head-to-head evaluation of a broad set of TyG and its derived indices for multiple key adverse outcomes after kidney transplantation, including NODAT, graft failure, and mortality, has been lacking. Therefore, this study aims to integrate machine-learning methods with traditional statistical approaches to systematically compare the prognostic value of 15 TyG-derived indices for adverse outcomes in KTRs.

Methods

Study population

This prospective observational study was conducted within the TransplantLines Biobank and Cohort Study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03272841), an ongoing cohort at the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG, Groningen, The Netherlands), which has been described in detail elsewhere [25]. We included adult KTRs aged ≥18 years who had a functioning graft for at least 1 year and completed at least 2 outpatient clinic visits between June 2015 and October 2025. Participants were excluded if data required for the calculation of TyG and TyG-derived indices were unavailable. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the UMCG (Medical Ethical Committee No. 2008/186), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul.

Clinical parameters

Participants’ health status, medical history, and medication use were extracted from medical records. Smoking status and alcohol consumption were evaluated using a standardized questionnaire, classifying individuals as current or noncurrent smokers and drinkers. Body weight and height were measured with participants in light indoor clothing and without shoes. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), and body surface area was estimated using the Du Bois formula [26]. Blood samples were collected, and all laboratory analyses were conducted on the same day as the study visit, during a morning appointment following an overnight fast of 8-12 hours. Blood pressure was measured using a semiautomatic device (Dinamap 1846, Critikon, Tampa, FL, USA) according to a standardized protocol. Diabetes mellitus was defined by the 2017 American Diabetes Association criteria as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L or the use of antidiabetic medication [27]. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP > 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP > 90 mmHg. Estimated glomerular filtration rates based on creatinine (eGFRcr) and cystatin C (eGFRcys) were calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equations [28].

Calculation of TyG-derived indices

In this study, a total of 15 TyG-derived indices were systematically calculated and categorized into 3 domains: (1) traditional and metabolic TyG-derived indices, (2) fat-distribution and body-shape-derived TyG indices, and (3) TyG variability indices reflecting long-term fluctuation. The base TyG index was calculated using fasting TG and fasting glucose (FG) levels according to the following formula: TyG = ln[TG (mg/dL) × FG (mg/dL)/2]. When laboratory values were reported in mmol/L, they were converted to mg/dL prior to calculation using standard conversion factors. Traditional and metabolic TyG-derived indices included the TyG multiplied by body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG multiplied by waist circumference (TyG-WC) [18], TyG-WHR [16], TyG-ABSI was defined as waist circumference divided by BMI2/3 and height1/2 (TyG-ABSI) [17], and the TyG-HTGW was defined as the coexistence of elevated TG levels (≥1.7 mmol/L) and increased waist circumference (≥90 cm in men and ≥85 cm in women) (TyG-HTGW) [29]. Fat-distribution and body-shape TyG indices comprised TyG multiplied by waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR) [30], TyG-WWI was defined as waist circumference divided by the square root of body weight [19], TyG multiplied by body roundness index (BRI), derived from waist circumference and height to reflect body fat distribution (TyG-BRI) [31], TyG multiplied by lipid accumulation product (LAP), calculated as (waist circumference [cm]-65) × TGs (mmol/L) in men and (waist circumference [cm]-58) × TGs (mmol/L) in women (TyG-LAP) [32] and TyG multiplied by visceral adiposity index (VAI), calculated using established sex-specific formulas incorporating waist circumference, BMI, TGs, and HDL cholesterol (TyG-VAI) [33, 34]. For assessment of long-term TyG variability, repeated measurements of TG and FG obtained during follow-up were used to construct individual TyG trajectories. Based on these trajectories, 4 variability metrics were calculated: TyG_mean (mean TyG value), TyG_sd (standard deviation), TyG_CV (coefficient of variation, calculated as standard deviation (SD) divided by the mean multiplied by 100), and TyG_VIM (variability independent of the mean), defined as VIM = SD/mean^β × 100, where β was derived from the regression coefficient of ln(SD) on ln(mean). Detailed formulas for each of the 15 TyG-derived indices are provided in Table S1 [35]. To enable consistent comparison across indices, the analytical cohort included only participants with sufficient metabolic and anthropometric data to calculate the full index set. Baseline TyG and anthropometry-adjusted TyG-derived indices were calculated using fasting TG, FG, and anthropometric measurements obtained at the TransplantLines study inclusion visit after kidney transplantation. Variability-based indices additionally required repeated TG and glucose measurements during follow-up. These repeated measurements were used to derive TyG_mean, TyG_sd, TyG_CV, and TyG_VIM, which were analyzed as separate TyG-derived indices rather than as time-varying covariates. Missing values were not imputed because several variables had substantial missingness and imputation could distort within-person TyG variability.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes were NODAT, graft failure, and all-cause mortality. NODAT was identified if any of the following conditions occurred during follow-up: (1) fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) on at least 2 separate occasions; (2) random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) in the presence of classic symptoms of hyperglycemia; (3) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% measured at least 3 months after transplantation; or (4) initiation of glucose-lowering therapy (including oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin) for persistent hyperglycemia after transplantation [36]. Graft failure was defined as initiation of chronic dialysis or retransplantation, whichever occurred first, and included both rejection-related and death-censored graft failure. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period. For survival analyses, participants were followed from the index date until the occurrence of the outcome of interest or the end of follow-up. Follow-up was censored at the time of graft failure, death, or the last available clinical assessment for participants who remained alive with a functioning graft.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), according to distribution as determined by Shapiro–Wilk tests. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons for continuous variables utilized the Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess pairwise correlations between 15 TyG-derived indices. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to estimate cumulative event-free survival for each endpoint, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to evaluate associations between TyG-derived indices and clinical outcomes. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. To account for multiple testing across interrelated TyG-derived indices, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to Cox regression analyses using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, separately within each outcome and model. Prognostic performance across age metrics was compared using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves and integrated area under the curve (iAUC) values, calculated with inverse probability of censoring weighting. To evaluate the relative importance of 15 TyG-derived indices for each outcome, Random Survival Forest (RSF) models were constructed separately for NODAT, graft failure, and all-cause mortality. Variable importance was quantified using permutation-based importance measures, reflecting the decrease in prediction accuracy after random permutation of each variable. Survival-based XGBoost models were fitted using the Cox proportional hazards loss function. Gradient boosting trees were trained to rank the importance of TyG-derived indices for each outcome. Feature importance was assessed using gain-based importance scores. To further enhance model interpretability and reduce collinearity among TyG-derived indices, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were applied. A SHAPBoost-based feature selection strategy was used to identify the most informative subset of TyG-derived indices for each endpoint (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=SHAPBoost). This approach iteratively evaluates candidate features while accounting for feature interactions and collinearity, selecting subsets that maximize the concordance index (C-index). Separate SHAPBoost selection was done for NODAT, graft failure, and mortality. The SHAP summary plots were generated to visualize the direction, magnitude, and relative importance of selected TyG-derived indices.

Finally, combined prediction models based on TyG-derived indices were developed using 2 selection strategies: conventional statistical analyses and machine learning-based SHAPBoost screening. The top-performing indices identified by each approach were incorporated into multivariable models. Model discrimination was evaluated using repeated 5-fold cross-validation (50 repetitions) with out-of-fold (OOF) risk prediction. In each repetition, models were trained on 4-fold and predictions were generated exclusively for individuals in the held-out fold. Single-index models were evaluated using cross-validated Cox regression to ensure fair comparison. iAUC was calculated from aggregated OOF predictions across repetitions, and the performance of combined models was compared against individual indices across the 3 clinical outcomes. All analyses were conducted using R software (version ≥4.3.0). A two-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 464 KTRs were included in the final analyses (Fig. S1 [35]). The median age was 56.9 (47.5-65.7) years, and 65.1% of the participants were male (Table 1 and Table S2 [35]). During the median follow-up of 71 months (53-89), 43 participants developed NODAT, 88 experienced graft failure, and 78 died. Participants had a median of 5 [IQR 5-6] outpatient visits, and the median number of paired TG and glucose measurements available for calculating TyG variability indices was 5 [IQR 4-5]. A total of 15 TyG-derived indices were calculated and categorized into 3 predefined domains based on their biological and methodological characteristics (Fig. 1A and Table S3 [35]). Furthermore, the ridgeline density plot illustrates the standardized distributions (z-scores) of the 15 TyG-derived indices across the study population (Fig. 1B). The traditional and metabolic TyG indices exhibited relatively symmetric and centrally distributed patterns, whereas fat-distribution and body-shape indices showed greater dispersion. In addition, the correlation heatmap showed that TyG variability indices were highly intercorrelated but largely independent of other TyG measures, whereas body-shape-adjusted indices, particularly TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI, showed strong correlations with traditional TyG (Fig. S2 [35]).

Machine learning-based variable importance analysis

Next, to compare the prognostic ability of the 15 TyG-derived indices, RSF and XGBoost survival models were applied to evaluate the relative importance of those indices across 3 outcomes: NODAT, graft failure, and all-cause mortality. In the RSF analysis, TyG-WHR, TyG-BMI, TyG_sd, TyG-CV, and TyG_mean were among the top variables of importance across the 3 outcomes (Fig. 2A). XGBoost survival analysis with SHAP-based importance ranking identified that TyG-HTGW, TyG-VIM, TyG-WWI, and TyG-CV ranked among the most influential features across outcomes (Fig. 2B). The detailed outcome-specific importance patterns of TyG-derived indices for NODAT, graft failure, and all-cause mortality based on SHAP-based XGBoost analysis are shown in Fig. S3 [35]. In these plots, each point represents an individual patient; positive SHAP values indicate that the index contributes to a higher model-predicted risk, whereas negative values indicate a contribution to lower predicted risk, with the color gradient reflecting the observed index value. Taken together, based on the consistent importance identified by both RSF and XGBoost models, 8 TyG-derived indices, including TyG_mean, TyG_sd, TyG_CV, TyG_VIM, TyG-WWI, TyG-BMI, TyG-HTGW, and TyG-WHR, were retained for subsequent analyses.

Traditional statistical approaches

Firstly, we used time-dependent AUC analyses to compare the predictive performance of the 8 TyG-derived indices selected by the machine-learning approach. Across 3 outcomes, TyG-WWI and TyG-WHR consistently demonstrated superior and more stable discrimination over the follow-up (Fig. 2C-2E). TyG_mean also showed moderate but sustained predictive ability for NODAT across time (Fig. 2C). Based on their performance, 4 indices, TyG-WWI, TyG-WHR, TyG_mean, and TyG-BMI, were selected for subsequent analyses. Then, Kaplan–Meier analyses showed that TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI achieved statistically significant risk stratification for incidence of NODAT (log-rank P < .05) (Fig. 3A). For graft failure and all-cause mortality, all 4 indices, TyG-WHR, TyG_mean, TyG-WWI, and TyG-BMI, demonstrated significant separation of survival curves (log-rank P < .05) (Fig. 3B and 3C). Moreover, the RCS analyses were performed to explore potential dose-response relationships between the 4 selected TyG-derived indices and adverse outcomes (Fig. S4A-C [35]). For NODAT, none of the 4 indices exhibited a statistically significant linear or nonlinear association with incident NODAT. For graft failure, TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI were significantly associated with graft failure risk (P for trend <.01). For all-cause mortality, TyG_mean, TyG-WHR, and TyG-WWI demonstrated significant positive linear associations with mortality risk (P for trend <.01). In addition, Cox proportional hazards models were performed (Table 2). In univariable analyses, when modeled as continuous variables, higher TyG_mean, TyG-WWI, and TyG-WHR were each significantly associated with increased risks of graft failure and all-cause mortality. When categorized by optimal cutoff values, both TyG-WWI (high vs low) and TyG-WHR (high vs low) demonstrated strong associations with all 3 outcomes. After adjustment for potential confounders, TyG-WHR remained independently associated with NODAT (HR 2.30, 95% CI 1.21-4.39, P = .011), graft failure (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.18-4.18, P = .014), and all-cause mortality (HR 1.87, 95% CI 1.19-2.96, P = .007). Similarly, TyG-WWI independently predicted all-cause mortality (HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.11-3.86, P = .022) and showed a borderline association with NODAT (HR 1.89, 95% CI 0.99-3.60, P = .053) and graft failure (HR 1.69, 95% CI 0.98-2.92, P = .061). After FDR correction, the associations of TyG-WHR with graft failure and all-cause mortality remained statistically significant, whereas its association with NODAT was attenuated. TyG-WWI showed an attenuated but suggestive association with all-cause mortality after correction. In sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for baseline FG, the association between TyG-WHR and NODAT remained robust (high vs low: HR 2.70, 95% CI 1.42-5.15; P = .003; FDR-adjusted P = .020). High TyG-WWI also showed a nominal association with NODAT after glucose adjustment (HR 2.15, 95% CI 1.13-4.10; P = .020; FDR-adjusted P = .053), suggesting that these associations were not solely driven by baseline FG (Table S4 [35]).

SHAPBoost analysis and combined prediction modeling

Finally, to explore the potential synergistic effects of these TyG-derived indices, SHAPBoost analysis identified that the combination of 4 indices (TyG-HTGW, TyG-ABSI, TyG-WWI, and TyG-VAI) achieved the highest C-index for NODAT. In contrast, the combination of TyG-BMI, TyG-WWI, and TyG_CV was most predictive of graft failure. For predicting all-cause mortality, a combination of TyG-WWI, TyG-VAI, and TyG_CV yielded the highest C-index (Fig. 4A). Detailed outcome-specific importance patterns for each index are presented in Fig. S5 [35]. Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the prognostic value of 15 TyG-derived indices in KTRs using a rigorous, multistage analytical framework. By integrating machine learning and traditional statistical approaches, we identified TyG-WWI and TyG-WHR as the most important indices for predicting post-transplant NODAT, graft failure, and mortality.

Cardiometabolic disorders are common after kidney transplantation and may adversely affect both graft and patient outcomes [37]. Insulin resistance, abdominal adiposity, dyslipidemia, chronic inflammation and immunosuppressive therapy may jointly contribute to NODAT, cardiovascular risk and long-term graft dysfunction [38-40]. These mechanisms support the need for simple and routinely available markers that capture metabolic risk burden in KTRs. The TyG index has been widely used as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance and has been associated with kidney function decline and cardiovascular outcomes in both general and transplant populations [41-43]. However, the conventional TyG index does not account for body composition, fat distribution or long-term metabolic variability [44]. Therefore, TyG-derived indices incorporating anthropometric or variability-based information may provide additional insight into post-transplant metabolic risk [45]. Our findings suggest that TyG-derived indices are not interchangeable. Indices reflecting long-term TyG variability tended to show greater importance in some machine-learning analyses, whereas body-shape-adjusted indices, particularly TyG-WWI and TyG-WHR, showed more consistent associations in conventional survival models and across clinical outcomes. This heterogeneity indicates that different TyG-derived indices may capture overlapping but distinct aspects of metabolic risk, and that their prognostic relevance may depend on the outcome and analytical approach. The stronger associations observed in high-vs-low analyses may reflect nonlinear or threshold-like risk patterns, as continuous Cox models estimate an average linear effect across the full distribution. These categorical results should therefore be interpreted as complementary risk-stratification analyses rather than as replacements for continuous models. Such distinctions are important for the appropriate selection and interpretation of TyG-based markers in future research and clinical risk stratification.

Previous studies comparing TyG and TyG-derived indices in kidney transplant populations have reported inconsistent findings. One study suggested that TyG-BMI may be useful for identifying individuals at high risk of NODAT [46], whereas another reported that the conventional TyG index, rather than other TyG-derived indices, was associated with cardiovascular events, graft loss and NODAT in KTRs [47]. Evidence from nontransplant CKD populations has also suggested potential differences among TyG-derived indices [48]. Our study extends this evidence by evaluating a broader panel of 15 TyG-derived indices across 3 clinically relevant post-transplant outcomes. The relatively consistent performance of TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI may be explained by their integration of insulin resistance-related information with anthropometric markers of central adiposity or body shape. TyG-WHR combines the TyG index with WHR, thereby capturing both impaired glucose–lipid metabolism and fat distribution [49, 50]. TyG-WWI similarly integrates TyG with weight-adjusted waist index and has been linked to diabetes, cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in previous studies [51, 52].

Compared with BMI-based measures alone, body-shape-adjusted indices may be particularly relevant in KTRs because body weight and composition can change substantially after transplantation [53]. BMI may be influenced by fluid status, post-transplant weight gain and changes in fat and lean mass, whereas WHR and weight-adjusted waist index may better reflect abdominal adiposity and body-shape-related metabolic risk [54, 55]. The inconsistent pattern observed for TyG-BMI, including its apparent inverse association with graft failure, should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of a protective effect. BMI-based indices may be influenced by muscle mass, fluid status, nutritional reserve and reverse epidemiology in KTRs, whereas TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI may better capture central adiposity and body-shape-related metabolic risk. Nevertheless, our study did not directly assess longitudinal body-composition changes after transplantation. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as evidence that baseline or summarized body-shape-adjusted TyG indices may capture clinically relevant metabolic risk, rather than as direct evidence of post-transplant body-composition dynamics. Future studies incorporating repeated body-composition assessments are needed to clarify these mechanisms.

Finally, we compared the predictive performance of combined TyG-based models with individual TyG-derived indices using repeated cross-validated evaluation. Although machine-learning-selected combinations showed high apparent discrimination, their advantage diminished after OOF validation and did not consistently exceed that of single indices. This suggests that multiple TyG-derived metrics may capture largely overlapping metabolic information, and that increasing model complexity may introduce noise rather than clinically meaningful incremental signal.

In the present study, the AUCs of individual TyG-derived indices were modest, indicating that these measures should not be interpreted as stand-alone clinical decision tools. Rather, their potential value may lie in providing complementary information on cardiometabolic risk burden, given that they can be derived from routinely available laboratory and anthropometric variables. The lack of consistent improvement from multi-index models in cross-validated analyses further supports the use of simple, interpretable indices such as TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI rather than more complex TyG-based combinations. These findings suggest that TyG-WHR and TyG-WWI may help enrich risk assessment during post-transplant follow-up, but external validation and evaluation within established clinical risk models are required before clinical implementation.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively modest. Because our analyses required simultaneous evaluation of multiple TyG-derived indices, including anthropometry-adjusted and variability-based metrics derived from repeated measurements, the number of participants with complete data across all variables was reduced. This may have limited statistical power and introduced potential selection bias, thereby restricting the generalizability of our findings to the broader kidney transplant population. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require external validation in larger, multicenter, and ethnically diverse cohorts. Second, this was a single-center study involving predominantly Caucasian KTRs. Differences in immunosuppressive regimens, metabolic phenotypes and clinical practice patterns across regions and ethnic groups may affect the generalizability of our findings. Third, although baseline medication use was presented in baseline characteristics table, detailed longitudinal medication data, including treatment initiation, discontinuation, dose changes and adherence, were not consistently available. Glucose- and lipid-lowering therapies may influence FG, TGs, and TyG-derived indices, while postbaseline treatment changes may also be affected by evolving metabolic risk. Therefore, time-varying treatment effects could not be fully addressed, and future studies with detailed longitudinal medication data are needed. Finally, from a methodological perspective, although multiple machine-learning approaches were applied, different algorithms may differentially evaluate static and dynamic predictors, and concordance across methods cannot be assumed. Future studies with detailed longitudinal medication data, repeated body-composition assessment and additional analytical frameworks are needed to further validate and refine the prognostic value of TyG-derived indices after kidney transplantation.

Conclusion

In this cohort of adult KTRs from the TransplantLines Biobank and Cohort Study with a functioning graft for at least 1 year, body-shape-adjusted TyG indices, particularly TyG-WWI and TyG-WHR, showed the most consistent associations with NODAT, graft failure and all-cause mortality among the 15 TyG-derived indices evaluated. These findings suggest that incorporating anthropometric measures into TyG-based assessments may provide complementary metabolic risk information during post-transplant follow-up in similar KTR populations. Future studies should validate these findings in larger, multicenter and ethnically diverse transplant cohorts, assess longitudinal changes in TyG-derived indices and body composition, and determine whether these markers improve existing clinical risk models or guide targeted metabolic interventions after kidney transplantation.
