A functional genomics-pharmacotranscriptomics framework identifies host-directed anti-influenza agents
Authors: Jianfa Qiu, Xuecong Xing, Jingfeng Wang, Weijing Yuan, Xiaorong Li, Aiping Wu, Zhimin Gu, Zhuo Zhou, Jianwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInfluenza A virus (IAV) remains a major threat to human and animal health, while the emergence of drug-resistant strains necessitates new antiviral strategies. Here, we developed an integrative host-directed drug discovery framework combining functional genomics and pharmacotranscriptomics. By aggregating published genome-wide screens, we assigned host genes functional scores reflecting their effects on IAV replication and used these scores to estimate the antiviral status of host cells. Screening nearly 20,000 drug-induced transcriptional signatures identified compounds that shift host gene expression toward an antiviral state. Among 54 selected hits, 18 showed anti-IAV activity. Notably, lithocholic acid and ALW-II-49-7 inhibited viral replication in vitro and protected mice from lethal infection in vivo. This host-targeted framework provides a systematic and scalable strategy for discovering antivirals that are less susceptible to resistance and potentially applicable to other rapidly evolving pathogens.

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) is a persistent threat to public health, causing annual epidemics and sporadic pandemics that result in significant morbidity and mortality worldwide. The segmented nature of the IAV genome facilitates genetic reassortment between strains, especially during co-infection in avian or swine hosts. This process generates novel viral genotypes and alters antigenic properties, driving the rapid evolution of circulating strains and complicating control efforts. Recent events, such as the sustained spread of highly pathogenic H5N1 in cattle and associated human infections, have heightened concerns over the zoonotic and pandemic potential of IAV.1,2,3 Therefore, the development of effective agents against IAV infection is crucial for addressing the challenges posed by its complex and rapidly evolving epidemiology.

Currently, therapeutic options for IAV are limited.4 Almost all FDA-approved antiviral drugs, including oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir, baloxavir, and amantadine, target the virus directly.5,6 However, the rapid accumulation of resistance mutations in circulating IAV strains has severely reduced the effectiveness of these agents. Resistance to amantadine is now widespread,7,8 and similar trends are emerging for neuraminidase inhibitors such as oseltamivir.9,10 More concerningly, resistant variants to the newly approved baloxavir have already appeared in clinical trials.11,12 These situations underscore the need to develop more effective, broad-spectrum anti-IAV therapeutic agents.

A promising alternative is to target host-cell factors, which are essential for viral replication and, being evolutionarily conserved, are less prone to resistance. However, a critical bottleneck has limited this approach. While functional genomics can identify host dependency factors, many are considered “undruggable” or lack available targeting compounds, making the path from target identification to drug discovery long and uncertain. However, host targets without directing drugs can be indirectly perturbed by drugs’ treatment with various mechanisms. Focusing on a single target may overlook the effects of these drugs’ potential in regulating indirect targets. To overcome this, we developed a computational framework that bypasses single-target development by directly bridging functional genomics with pharmacotranscriptomics, enabling us to evaluate the transcriptome-wide drug efficacy. Firstly, we integrated data from numerous IAV-related genome-wide screens to assign a “functional score” to every host gene, quantifying its pro- or anti-viral role. Following this, we leveraged the functional score and the cMAP (Connectivity Map) database for virtual drug screening. The cMAP database contains vast transcriptomic profiles from cells treated with nearly 20,000 small molecules. By weighting each drug’s transcriptional signature with our functional scores, we computed a “drug score” that predicts the compound’s ability to shift the cell into an overall antiviral state. By this approach, we identified and validated a plethora of compounds that exert potent anti-IAV activity.

As such, our screening system prioritizes drug-induced functional alterations at the genome-wide level, allowing for a comprehensive and quantitative assessment of drug efficacy, thereby providing an effective pipeline for host-directed drug discovery. Beyond IAV, this framework is applicable to other pathogens and complex diseases where a functional understanding of host genes can guide therapeutic selection.

Results

General framework for host-directed anti-IAV discovery

We established a two-step virtual screening method for host-directed anti-IAV compound discovery, with the first functional genomic data integration, and a following gene set scoring system based on the integrated functional genomics and pharmacotranscriptomics.

At the first step, we constructed a genome-wide functional landscape of host factors involved in IAV replication. To achieve this, we curated published high-throughput screening datasets (employing CRISPR, RNAi, or other genetic perturbations) and integrated them using an aggregation method. Within each dataset, the MAGeCK algorithm was applied to statistically measure the cell survival changes induced by genetic perturbations under viral infection using a negative binominal model. This allowed us to determine the functional importance of each gene that directly revealed their pro- or antiviral strength.13 Some dataset conducted multiple rounds or repeats of screening. In this process, each screening round or biological repeat within an independent dataset were individually measured and ranked, rather than comprehensively generating a single unique ranking with the robust rank aggregation (RRA) algorithm included in MAGeCK. We next ranked genes for each screening round or biological repeat from antiviral functions to pro-viral, and subsequently used the RRA (with the R package) to extract the gene ranking enrichment information to generate a unique consensus functional score for every host gene. Second, we leveraged this functional landscape to conduct a virtual drug screen using the cMAP database, which contains the transcriptional profiles of ∼20,000 compounds based on the L1000 platform. For each compound, we weighted its induced gene expression changes with our corresponding functional scores by multiplying the functional scores with the corresponding gene expression values, which identified the antiviral functional contribution of each gene. The sum of gene contributions quantifies a compound’s predicted ability to shift the host cell into an anti-IAV state. This strategy effectively transforms vast pharmacotranscriptomic data into a prioritized list of drug candidates for experimental validation (Figure 1B).

Integration of genome-wide screens delineates host’s functional factor landscape

To establish a comprehensive portrait of host functional involvement in IAV infection, we systematically aggregated functional genomics datasets from 11 independent studies, investigating host dependency or restriction factors. As detailed in Table S1, these datasets were generated under diverse experimental conditions, encompassing various virus strains, cell lines, multiplicity of infection (MOI), times after infection, perturbing methods, and evaluating systems (Table S1). Of note, a meta-analysis dataset included in our study14 is a conclusive list of four independent RNAi screening studies15,16,17,18 (Figure 2A). To reconcile this inter-study variability and extract biologically conserved signals, we employed a robust integration pipeline. First, each dataset was analyzed using the MAGeCK algorithm, ranking genes based on their antiviral or pro-viral effects (see STAR Methods). Then, we integrated gene rankings from all studies through the RRA algorithm, which generates a significance probability for each gene’s functional enrichment.19 We then defined a functional score as the negative logarithm of the RRA-derived p value, with positive scores indicating antiviral genes and negative scores marking proviral factors (Figure 2A). Importantly, the RRA framework penalizes condition-specific outliers, thereby mitigating the impact of experimental heterogeneity and prioritizing genes with consistent functional relevance across diverse infection contexts. To ensure that our integrated rankings were not biased by any individual dataset, we performed a bootstrap procedure for quality control. We systematically omitted one dataset at a time and regenerated functional score rankings using the remaining datasets, iterating through all datasets. As a randomized control, we introduced a randomly permuted dataset with the other datasets for aggregated ranking in each iteration to produce a randomly perturbed ranking. By comparing the perturbed rankings (leave-one-out or randomized control groups) with the original ranking (incorporating all datasets), we calculated total rank changes to assess the impact of including or excluding each dataset. If each dataset contributes robust ranking information, the total rank changes in the leave-one-out group should be significantly smaller than those in the randomized controls. As expected, all leave-one-out rank changes were significantly smaller than the randomized controls (Figures S1A and S1B), indicating that our integrated rankings are stable and reflect biologically consistent signals across the aggregated studies, rather than being skewed by noise from any single data source.

Our integrated analysis produced a detailed functional map of the host-virus interface, stratifying key factors by their roles across the viral life cycle. Among the top hits, SLC35A1, required for receptor sialylation,20 was the leading pro-viral factor, while known IAV restriction factors like OAS3 and DDX6 topped the antiviral lists (Figure 2B). Functional annotation and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) enrichment analyses revealed that pro-viral processes were dominated by biological functions essential for early infection, such as neuraminate transport, lysosomal acidification (v-ATPases),21 and negative regulation of the TLR signaling pathway. Conversely, anti-viral factors were primarily associated with host defense and immune activation, including interferon signaling, inflammation, and specific entry inhibitors (e.g., B4GALNT2, APOE) or polymerase repressors (SERTAD3)22,23,24,25,26 (Figures 2C, 2D, and S1C–S1E). Together, these results recapitulated known biological functions and tested the reliability of our functional scoring landscape.

Multiscale validation of functional score identifying host’s anti-IAV state

Having established the consensus genome-wide functional landscape, we next validated its ability to quantify the host’s global defense against IAV. We defined an anti-IAV score for each biological sample as the sum of its gene expression values weighted by our functional scores, which characterized the antiviral state of the samples. To do this, we tested this concept across two biological scales.

First, at the cellular level, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from IAV-infected samples, where natural transcriptional heterogeneity of the A549 cell line provides a spectrum of antiviral states. In this dataset, cells were infected with an IAV at a low MOI of 0.01, allowing each infected cell to receive a single viral particle, and NH4Cl was added to prevent intercellular viral spread before the first round of replication was complete. This ensured that all infected cells experienced the same infection conditions, and the variation in viral replication could be attributed solely to the host cell antiviral status during infection.27 By calculating the anti-IAV score for each infected cell, we observed that cells with higher anti-IAV scores consistently contained fewer viral transcripts (Figures 3A and 3B). This negative correlation is stably shown in all eight viral genes (Figure 3C). Second, we extended this validation to the individual level using a public dataset from a controlled human IAV challenge. By analyzing a longitudinal bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset from peripheral blood, we found that participants who developed clinical symptoms did not show significant differences in baseline anti-IAV scores compared to asymptomatic individuals (Figure 3D). However, at subsequent time points post infection, asymptomatic group maintained a sustained higher average anti-IAV scores than the symptomatic group, indicating a correlation between symptom development and anti-IAV scores during infection (Figure 3E). These results demonstrate that anti-IAV score effectively identified host antiviral status in time course resolution at the individual level.

Collectively, the in vitro and in vivo data indicate that our functional score incorporating infection-related transcriptomic datasets successfully quantifies host antiviral status across biological scales and sequencing platforms.

Combining functional scores and drug transcriptomes to identify anti-IAV drugs

Small-molecule compounds can reprogram host gene expression, and their modulation of functional genes can lead to diverse infection outcomes. Some compounds bidirectionally affect both proviral and antiviral genes, simultaneously upregulating and downregulating genes within the same category. These indiscriminate perturbations tend to offset each other in function, resulting in little or no net antiviral effect (Figure S2A). Antiviral compounds tend to display directed transcriptional effects by selectively enhancing the expression of antiviral genes while suppressing proviral factors, shifting gene expression patterns toward host defense, and blocking the viral replication that relies on host functions (Figure S2B). Conversely, proviral compounds promote virus-supporting mechanisms by upregulating proviral genes or weakening host defenses by downregulating antiviral genes (Figure S2C). This principle of the directionality of gene expression changes predicts a drug’s effect, which forms the basis of our drug screening strategy.

Based on this hypothesis, we developed a computational pipeline to identify anti-IAV compounds by coupling functional scores with pharmacotranscriptomic profiles. Specifically, drug-induced gene expression changes can be profiled by transcriptomics, and the gene functions were determined as functional scores. We applied this principle to the drug-perturbed A549 cells’ transcriptomic data from the cMAP database. Our pipeline successfully identified a large number of known antiviral agents with high drug score, including probenecid,28,29 isoquercetin,30,31 tetracycline,32 and vitexin,33 suggesting our screening system effectively highlights antiviral compounds (Figure S3A). In addition, the distribution of the drug score across all compounds formed an approximately bell-shaped curve (Figure S3B). This result aligns with the expectation that only a small portion of compounds exert potent antiviral or proviral effects, and most of them have limited viral regulation potential.

Compounds with similar antiviral functions probably induce a common gene expression pattern. To explore this, we performed principal-component analysis (PCA) on Z score-normalized transcriptomic profiles of all compounds. We found that drug treatment induced several distinct transcriptional signatures, but the number of these signatures was far fewer than the number of drugs (Figure 4B). This indicates that compound-induced expression patterns are constrained and that antiviral compounds act through common biological pathways. We further performed k-means clustering and optimized the k value, ultimately setting k = 6 that properly aligned with the PCA-derived signatures (Figures 4B and 4C). Notably, cluster 1 was characterized as highly antiviral with the highest average drug score, and accordingly, clusters with further distance from cluster 1 exhibit lower average scores (Figure 4C). By linear regression analysis, we further confirmed that the coordination distance was negatively related to the mean drug score of the clusters, suggesting a link between anti-IAV activity and a specific expression pattern (Figure 4D).

We further examined whether high-scoring antiviral compounds act through particular, well-defined mechanisms of action (MOAs). Analysis of the associations between drug scores and annotated MOAs revealed that many of the top 20 MOAs have been previously linked to broad-spectrum antiviral effects,34,35,36 highlighting the effectiveness of our screening. For example, the pregnane X receptor (PXR), a nuclear receptor that regulates innate immune pathways such as NF-κB and TLR signaling, inhibits various viral infections, including vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and dengue virus (DENV).37 Our screening also revealed that PXR-activating compounds exhibit potent anti-IAV activity, indicating that PXR mediates antiviral effects through conserved, host-directed mechanisms (Figure 4E). Additionally, our MOA analysis identified overlooked or underexplored mechanisms. For instance, bile acids, the natural products synthesized by the human body with diverse physiological roles, showed highly anti-IAV potential (Figure 4E). This suggests that bile acid-mediated processes can enhance host resistance to IAV,38,39 making bile acids and their derivatives promising candidates for anti-IAV drug development.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that our virtual screening pipeline effectively identifies antiviral compounds by quantifying their systematic impact on host factors, thereby connecting drug-induced transcriptional changes to functional outcomes and actionable therapeutic strategies.

In vitro and in vivo validation of virtual screened anti-IAV compounds

We next sought to validate our virtual screening results and the core effectiveness of our framework using a western blot assay (Figure 5A). Given that a large number of compounds in the cMAP database lack commercial availability, we searched the top 800 candidates by drug score and identified 54 commercially accessible compounds (Figure 5B). Western blot validation showed that 38 of these candidates inhibited the expression of at least one viral protein (Figure 5C). Specifically, 23 reduced M1 levels (>20% inhibition; Figure S3C) and 32 reduced nucleoprotein (NP) levels (>15% inhibition; Figure S3D), with 18 compounds significantly suppressing both viral proteins (defined as inhibition of IAV replication). This corresponds to a hit rate of 33.33% (18/54), which is considered a favorable rate, as virtual screening studies typically achieve hit rates between 1% and 40%, with 29.01% of studies reporting a hit rate higher than 25%.40 Seven compounds that inhibited both proteins and showed potential for drug development were further tested for cytotoxicity and showed no significant reduction in cell viability after 48 h, indicating their antiviral effects were not due to cytotoxicity (Figure S3E). Encouraged by these in vitro results, we evaluated the in vivo efficacy of two top candidates, lithocholic acid (a natural bile acid) and ALW-II-49-7 (a synthetic inhibitor), which were selected for their in vitro antiviral potency and development potential (Figures 5B, 5C, S3C, and S3D). Plaque assays confirmed that both compounds significantly reduced infectious viral titers (Figure 6A). The low IC50 and the high CC50 of the compounds resulted in favorable selective indexes (Figures 6B, 6C, and S3F). In a murine IAV infection model, mice received these compounds intraperitoneally (i.p.) either before (prophylactic) or after (therapeutic) viral challenge (Figure 6D). Oseltamivir, a clinically used drug for treating influenza, was included as a positive control. Both treatments reduced the body weight loss velocity to a degree comparable to oseltamivir phosphate (Figures S4A and S4B), and significantly reduced cumulative weight loss at the typical decreasing window (Figure 6E). Survival analysis revealed a significant reduction in mortality and prolonged survival in the treated groups, with lithocholic acid exhibiting superior efficacy to oseltamivir phosphate under our experimental treatment regimen (Figure 6F). At 7 days post-infection, lung viral RNA levels were significantly reduced in the drug-treated mice, with the inhibitory efficacy of ALW-II-49-7 comparable to that of oseltamivir phosphate (Figure 6G). These findings indicate that the protective effects are mediated through viral suppression. Histological analysis confirmed reduced inflammatory infiltration, lung consolidation, and tissue damage in treated animals, demonstrating preserved respiratory function during infection (Figures 6H and 6I). Notably, although lithocholic acid exhibited a more modest effect on reducing viral load compared to ALW-II-49-7 (Figures 6A, 6G, and S3F), it conferred greater protective benefits in mitigating weight loss and improving overall survival (Figures 6E and 6F). Mechanisms and pharmacokinetics of candidate compounds

Following the validation of the two anti-IAV candidate compounds, we sought to explore their host-directed mechanisms. Given that these two compounds possess distinct established targets, with lithocholic acid interacting with multiple receptors, including the PXR, farnesoid X receptor (FXR), and TGR5,41,43 and ALW-II-49-7 acting as an ephrin type-B receptor 2 (EphB2) kinase inhibitor,44 we are interested whether they inhibit IAV through independent pathways or share convergent molecular patterns.

First, we evaluated the drug-induced transcriptomic signatures from the cMAP database. By quantifying the functional contributions of expression-perturbed genes, we found that both compounds establish an antiviral state primarily by downregulating host dependency (proviral) factors. Specifically, the antiviral contribution of the downregulated gene sets (score >3,000) markedly outweighed the proviral impact of the upregulated sets (score ∼ −2,000) (Figure 7A). Notably, although the two compounds exhibited distinct, drug-specific regulatory profiles, they convergently repressed several high-contribution functional genes, including CCDC86, PAX3, and AJAP1 (Figures 7B and 7C). Crucially, both drugs downregulated top-ranked proviral genes identified in our initial meta-analysis. For instance, lithocholic acid robustly suppressed SLC35A1 (critical for the synthesis of the influenza virus receptor), whereas ALW-II-49-7 targeted ATP6V0C (essential for endosomal acidification during virus uncoating) (Figure 7B). To further investigate the systemic mechanisms of drug action in vivo, we performed bulk RNA-seq on lung tissues from drug-treated mice (Figure S4C). As expected, because bulk lung tissue encompasses a highly heterogeneous mixture of immune and stromal cells, its transcriptomic profile diverged from the monoculture-based cMAP data. Despite this cellular complexity naturally diluting cell-autonomous signatures, we still observed a consistent downregulation trend for the key proviral genes identified in vitro, such as Slc35a1, Atp6v0c, Actg2, and Ccdc86 (Figure S4D).

Beyond the repression of cellular targets, the in vivo transcriptomes revealed that both drugs induced broad immune gene activation. Differential expression and functional enrichment analyses demonstrated a significant upregulation of immune-related genes, including interferon-stimulated genes (e.g., Oasl1) and various interleukins (Figures 7E, S4D, and S4E). Interaction network analysis further highlighted a convergent regulatory response for both treatments, featuring shared hub genes such as Foxp3 (a master regulator of immune homeostasis) and Tnf, alongside the downregulation of multiple histone families (Figures S5A–S5C). Given its prediction as a central hub and its known potency as an anti-IAV cytokine,45 TNF likely plays a pivotal role in mediating the in vivo efficacy of these treatments. Additionally, the alteration of histone families may globally affect host transcription, thereby playing a role by further shifting gene expression patterns toward an antiviral profile.

Taken together, these results suggest that lithocholic acid and ALW-II-49-7 exert their in vivo protective effects through a multi-layered mechanism: the targeted downregulation of critical proviral factors coupled with the orchestration of host immune responses.

Finally, we assessed the pharmacokinetics of the two compounds in mice to identify their in vivo dynamic signatures (Figure 7F). Within 24 h post-treatment, ALW-II-49-7 showed an adequate serum plasma retention time (T1/2), but showed insufficient bioavailability with a low Cmax. In contrast, while lithocholic acid exhibited high bioavailability, its short peak time (Tmax) and fast clearance (T1/2) restrained its area under the curve (AUC) (Figure 7G). Notably, although the serum concentration of both drugs dropped below their IC50 within 2 h post-treatment (Figures 7F and S3F), the immune activation sustained at 24 h post-treatment (Figures S4D and S4E), exhibiting the decoupling of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. This decoupling effect may overcome the small therapeutic window that requires the shortened dosing interval and sufficient dosing concentration such as virus-directed therapeutics.

Discussion

In our study, we aim to address a key challenge in antiviral drug discovery: systematically leveraging functional genomic data to identify host-directed therapeutics. By integrating IAV screening datasets with the cMAP pharmacotranscriptomic database, we developed a drug scoring framework that successfully identified novel antiviral compounds. In vitro and in vivo validation confirmed this approach as an effective strategy for host-targeted antiviral development.

Several studies have employed the cMAP database for drug discovery in complex diseases.46,47 However, these methods may not be suitable for viral infections due to inherent differences between viral and complex diseases, such as cancer and autoimmune disorders. A key limitation of existing approaches is their reliance on reversing gene expression profiles associated with disease.48,49 This strategy is less effective for viral infections, as infection activates both antiviral and pro-viral genes, making it difficult to achieve selective antiviral effects simply by reversing the infected-state expression profile. Some methods use pathways or co-expression networks to infer gene functions,50,51 but such inference can be misleading in antiviral drug discovery, as viruses are known to hijack host signaling pathways, leading to unpredictable outcomes. In contrast, our study emphasizes the importance of functional analysis by employing experimentally validated data to accurately define gene functions. This approach provides a more reliable foundation for identifying antiviral compounds and is better suited for addressing viral infections.

Functional genomic screening effectively identifies host genes for regulating viral infection. However, inherent experimental deviation and low signal-to-noise ratios obscure the extraction of effective information compared to other omics data. More importantly, the diverse experimental conditions across different screening studies (Figure 2A and Table S1) often lead to the identification of condition-specific factors. To overcome these limitations, we applied the RRA algorithm to extract consensus information from multiple IAV infection-related functional genomic datasets, which reduced false positives and negatives, thereby enhancing gene functional ranking reliability. The aggregated functional ranking uncovered previously overlooked functional genes and enabled multi-dimensional characterization of host antiviral states, including cellular and organismal systems. Notably, while our consensus-based algorithm effectively minimizes false positives by aggregating data across multiple screens, this stringency inherently compromises comprehensiveness. Consequently, our compiled list of functional host factors is not exhaustive. Biologically relevant genes that were not consistently enriched in the original datasets were excluded from our framework and therefore lacked a meaningful functional score. This limitation likely explains why certain candidate compounds misclassified by our computational pipeline failed to exhibit antiviral efficacy, or paradoxically displayed proviral effects, during our experimental validation (Figures 5C, S3C, and S3D).

In developing our methodological approach, we drew inspiration from the L1000 platform, which infers genome-wide expression from approximately 1,000 directly measured “landmark” genes. Similarly, we applied RRA to highlight genes with consistently robust effects across multiple datasets, which could serve as “landmarks” while down-weighting those with unstable rankings. Although this strategy may miss some genuinely functional but variably ranked genes, it increases the relative importance of robust genes, allowing them to serve as reliable marker genes. These genes encompass the major biological processes during viral infection, enabling inference of genome-wide functional states by the robust functional genes (Figure 2D). In addition to the L1000 platform, our approach builds upon the convergent patterns shared across the continuum of established virtual screening methodologies,52 utilizing them as a robust foundation while integrating a design tailored to viral infection.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. Genome-wide pooled screens are inherently biased toward early infection events, with limited resolution for late-stage viral processes. Additionally, the scarcity of gain-of-function screening data risks underrepresenting genes whose critical roles emerge only through overexpression studies rather than knockout approaches. A particularly significant challenge involves the dynamic nature of host-virus interactions. For example, IFIT2 exhibits stage-specific roles, acting antivirally during early infection but supporting viral translation later.53 Such temporal complexity was observed in both published datasets and our integrated scores, highlighting that our current genome-wide scoring provides only a cumulative effect during pooled screening. Similarly, our drug screening lacks the experimental depth (time-course, dose-response data) needed to fully resolve dynamic therapeutic effects.

The validated efficacy of our platform underscores its potential as a robust anti-IAV development strategy. Moving forward, evaluating this approach across a broader spectrum of viral families will be essential to establish its generalizability. Beyond virology, these data-driven framework holds broad translational potential, offering a versatile pipeline to accelerate drug repurposing and discovery in other complex therapeutic areas, such as oncology and degenerative diseases. To address current limitations, future iterations should incorporate temporally resolved screens and multi-dimensional datasets to better capture dynamic host-pathogen interactions. Such enhancements would extend the platform’s utility not only to acute infections but also to complex, chronic diseases.

Conclusions

In this study, we established a virtual drug screening pipeline for host-targeted anti-IAV agents through evaluating drug reprogramming genome-wide host antiviral states. The pipeline systematically quantified the host genes’ function on pro- or antiviral with a functional score, and then scored the drugs’ antiviral ability using drug-induced gene perturbation data and the functional score. Unlike the current strategies, this approach avoided the concept of drug-target interaction, providing a more comprehensive and quantitative strategy of host-targeting drug discovery. Eighteen out of 54 top-ranked hits exhibit significant anti-IAV activity, demonstrating the efficacy of our drug screening strategy. Two selected compounds, lithocholic acid and ALW-II-49-7, exhibited potent protective efficacy against IAV in vivo, and altered TNF or histone expression may underlie this protective effect. Overall, this study unified the host target functional quantification and antiviral drug identification, providing a comprehensive strategy, speeding up the antiviral agent discovery.

Limitations of the study

Although we present a framework for antiviral drug prioritization, several limitations should be noted. First, our analysis relies largely on transcriptomic readouts from public datasets obtained under single drug concentrations or specific time points, which does not fully capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of host-targeted drug responses. Second, our in vitro antiviral assessment was performed at a single time point, and a multicycle replication curve would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of antiviral kinetics. Similarly, in vivo viral loads were measured only at day 7 post-infection, limiting our ability to distinguish early viral suppression from later effects on pathogenesis. Third, i.p. administration of oseltamivir, rather than the clinical oral route, may have led to an underestimation of its protective effect, potentially affecting the relative comparison with test compounds. Fourth, because our framework evaluates the antiviral state based on cumulative, systems-level transcriptomic shifts, isolating the specific mechanistic contribution or threshold of any individual proviral gene (such as SLC35A1) within this complex network remains challenging. Finally, although lithocholic acid showed relatively weaker direct antiviral activity in vivo, it exhibited strong efficacy in maintaining body weight. We speculate that this protective effect may be driven by additional immunomodulatory effects that alleviate immunopathology; however, robust experimental evidence is required to substantiate this hypothesis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Viruses

The H1N1 Influenza A virus strain A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 and A/WSN/1933 were kindly provided by Dr. T Deng (Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences). Viruses were cultured and growth using MDCK cells or chicken embryo. For IAV infection, the cells’ culture medium was removed, and cells were washed with PBS two times, then cells were infected with virus of corresponding MOI with infection medium for 1 hour. Method details

Genome-wide screening data integration

In our study, previously published IAV-related genome-wide screening datasets were curated for integrated analysis. We selected high-quality datasets from 11 independent studies that cover most of the similar studies (Table S1).14,23,25,53,54,55,56,57,58,59,60 Given the variability in data formats among different sources, we implemented a harmonization process to ensure consistency across the analysis workflow. To do this, we primarily employed the MAGeCK13 algorithm to functionally score and rank genes from each independent screening round or biological repeats. For CRISPR-KO or RNAi-based loss-of-function screens, genes were ranked in ascending order based on negative enrichment scores, whereas for gain-of-function screens using CRISPRa or similar methods, genes were ranked in descending order based on their enrichment scores. Specifically, for studies that provided raw sequencing data, we recalculated the MAGeCK scores for each gene from scratch, regardless of whether the original study included MAGeCK score lists. For papers lacking raw sequencing data but providing functional scores, we adopted the scores reported by the original investigations, which encompassed both MAGeCK-based metrics and those derived from alternative algorithms. For a small number of studies that did not provide a full list of genome-wide scoring, we elected to use only the significantly enriched gene subsets from non-full lists.

Note that Tripathi S (2015) presented data derived from meta-analysis across multiple independent screens. The functional scores provided by the study represent an integrated evaluation of 4 independent screening papers.15,16,17,18 Unfortunately, none of these four original studies provided full lists. Instead, Tripathi S's scores were generated based on accessing the raw sequencing data from all four studies and performing additional analyses. While the list provided by Tripathi S is also incomplete, we chose to use this non-comprehensive list in our workflow rather than deriving scores from the raw data of the original studies individually.

R package RobustRankAggreg was applied for RRA ranking of the independent screening rounds and biological repeats, that ordered as described above. The functional score of the gene can be described as above:vdis(gsi)=−log10(RRAgsi)Where the gsi refers to the i-th gene of the RRA aggregated gene list from multiple functional screening; vdis(gsi) refers to the functional score of the i-th gene; RRAgsi refers to the RobustRankAggreg outputted p-value of the i-th gene.61

Antiviral status assessment

To quantify the contribution of genome-wide functional gene expression patterns to the global host antiviral state, we considered both the functional importance (functional score) and expression level of each gene. For individual genes, functional contribution was defined as the product of their functional score and expression level. Additionally, transcriptome-wide antiviral function was calculated as the sum of functional contributions across all genes. Thus, the antiviral capacity of a host transcriptomic sample is represented by an anti-IAV score:anti−IAVscorej=∑k=1n(functionalscorek×expressionvaluek)Where j refers to the j-th sample; k refers to the k-th gene; n refers to the number of genes in the transcriptomic dataset; functional score refers to the gene functional score as determined above; and the expression value refers to read counts for RNA-Seq sample or the normalized signal value for transcriptomic chip samples or L1000 samples. The antiviral status of the host mentioned in this study is quantified with the anti-IAV score.

To identify the anti-IAV compounds, the drug-treated host’s anti-IAV score were considered as compounds’ host-directed anti-IAV ability that renamed as “drug score” to characterize the drug anti-IAV activity:vdrg(R)=∑gri=1NR(vdis(gri)×Rgri)Where the vdrg(R) refers to the drug score from a full set of genes with a drug perturbation transcriptomic data (R); the gri refers to the i-th gene of R; NR refers to the number of genes in gene sets R; and the vdis(gri) refers to the functional score of the i-th gene.61 The R in our study specifically used the L1000 compound-treated A549 cell datasets from the cMAP database, including a subset of GSE92742 and a subset of GSE70138.

Western blot analysis

Cells after infection were washed twice with PBS and then lysed on ice for 20 minutes with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, #P0013C) supplemented with 1% (vol/vol) proteinase and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (MCE, #HY-K0010, #HY-K0021, #HY-K0022). The total protein was quantified using the BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23227) and then denaturation by adding SDS-PAGE loading buffer (TAKARA, #9173) and boiling for 10 minutes. The protein was separated in SDS-PAGE and then transferred to the PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk dissolved in TBST buffer at room temperature for 1 hour and then incubated with the primary antibody at 4°C overnight. The primary antibodies used in this study were: Rabbit polyclonal anti-influenza A nucleoprotein (NP) (GeneTex, #GTX125989), Rabbit monoclonal anti-influenza A matrix protein (M1) (GeneTex, #GTX636675), Rabbit polyclonal anti-ISG15 (Proteintech, #15981-1-AP), and Mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH (Abclonal, #AC035). Following washed three times with TBST, the second antibody against the corresponding species was applied for membrane incubation, including HRP Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) (ABclonal, #AE003), and HRP Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (ABclonal, #AS014). TBST washed the membrane three times, and imaging was performed using Clinx ChemiScope S6 and ECL kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #34577).

ImageJ software was used for Western blot data quantification. Relative protein expression was determined as the gray value ratio of the target protein to the internal control (GAPDH). For inter-gel normalization, the relative expression of the negative control (DMSO group) on each gel was set to 1.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay

CCK8 assay was used to assess cytotoxicity. A549 cells were plated in a 96-well plate for 2×104 per well and incubated overnight for attachment. Compounds for testing were added to wells with corresponding concentrations, and cells were then cultured for 48 hours. Following drug treatment, the CCK8 reagent was added to the culture medium with 10 μL/well and kept incubated in the culture environment for 1 hour. Then the plate was submitted to test the OD450 absorbance, and the background was deducted by the treatment-free well, and the results were normalized by the wells that were treated with DMSO in the corresponding concentration.

Plaque assay

A549 cells were pretreated with 10 μM lithocholic acid or ALW-II-49-7 for 6 hours, followed by infection with influenza A virus A/WSN/33 at a MOI of 0.01 for 1 hour. After viral adsorption, cells were maintained in viral growth medium containing 10 μM of the respective compounds for an additional 47 hours. Supernatants were subsequently collected and subjected to 10-fold serial dilutions. Aliquots of each dilution were added to confluent MDCK cell monolayers in 12-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow viral attachment. The inoculum was then removed, and cells were overlaid with 2×DMEM supplemented with 1 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin, 0.5% fetal bovine serum, and low-melting-point agarose. The overlay was allowed to solidify at room temperature before incubation at 37 °C for 72 hours. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, after which the overlay was removed and the cell monolayer was stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Plaques were enumerated, and viral titers were calculated based on plaque counts and the corresponding dilution factors.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

To extract total RNA, TRIzol was applied for tissue lysis. Specimens were cut into small pieces and ground within TRIzol until complete lysis. RNA was then extracted from the TRIzol phase with chloroform and precipitated with isopropanol. The RNA pellet was washed with ethanol twice and dissolved in DEPC H2O. A reverse transcription kit (accurate biology, #AG11705) was applied for host cDNA synthesis, and the viral RNA was reverse transcribed with specific primers and followed the kit’s procedures. The quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green premix reagent (Accurate Biology, #AG11701), and the program was performed with the Bio-Rad CFX Connect. All RNA expressions were normalized by reference gene 18S rRNA.

Animal infection model

C57BL/6J mice aged from 6 to 8 weeks and body weight ranging from 18 to 22 grams were used for the IAV infection model. The solvent group contained 9 males and 7 females, the Lithocholic acid group contained 8 males and 7 females, and the ALW-II-49-7 group of 8 males and 8 females. Before infection, mice were intraperitoneally injected with solvent or corresponding drugs 25 mg/kg at -6 hours (6 hours before infection). Then, each mouse was inoculated intranasally with 40 PFU of the A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 in a 30 μL volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 0 hours. After infection for 24 hours, mice were treated again, identical to -6 hours. The body weight and survival of mice were monitored for 2 weeks starting on the day of infection. Two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons was used for statistical analysis of changes in mouse body weight, and the Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test was applied to assess the statistical significance of mouse survival.

At 7 days post-infection, 3 mice of each group were humanely sacrificed to harvest lung tissue for qRT-PCR and hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. Tissue damage was quantified with the Smith lung damage scoring system. For each HE staining slide, picture was zoomed in for 10× and randomly selected 5 fields for scoring. The average score of the selected fields was considered as the score of the sample. virus replication was assessed by qRT-PCR, quantifying the viral NP RNA segment, and the results were normalized with the host internal reference 18S rRNA. The HE staining is applied for inflammation, lesions, and lung damage.

RNA-seq library construction and data analysis

Total RNA extracted from mouse lung tissue was used for RNA-Seq library construction. First, poly(A)-containing RNA was enriched from the total RNA, followed by reverse transcription of the enriched RNA into cDNA. The cDNA was fragmented prior to index ligation, and the constructed library was subsequently amplified via PCR. The final libraries were submitted to an Illumina HiSeq PE150 platform for high-throughput sequencing.

For upstream RNA-Seq data analysis, FastQC was used for sample quality control. First, 15-nucleotide adapters were removed using Trim Galore. Subsequently, reads were aligned to the mouse genome GRCm38 via HISAT2. SAM files generated from alignment were converted to BAM format using SAMtools, and FeatureCounts was then applied to quantify read counts from the BAM files.

The downstream analysis included differential expression analysis, functional enrichment analysis, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis. DESeq2 was used to identify differential gene expression between drug-treated and negative control groups. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs identified by DESeq2 was performed using ClusterProfiler with data visualization, and the Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process (BP) subset served as the background database. PPIs among DEG-encoded proteins were retrieved from the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). The interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape, while network analysis was conducted with CentiScaPe.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8. For cell experiments and molecular experiments, n=3, error bar indicates SD, student’s t-test was used for statistical analysis, except other mentioned. For mouse body weight comparison, n was denoted in the figure legends, error bar indicates SEM, two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons was used for statistical analysis.
