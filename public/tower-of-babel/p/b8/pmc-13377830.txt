CTHRC1 drives megakaryocyte-mediated immunotherapy resistance in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma via the integrin αIIbβ3/Rap1 pathway and is targetable by mRNA vaccination
Authors: Yixin Liu, Jianfeng Zhou, Mengyuan Song, Jian Wang, Qixin Shang, Jiajia Du, Yanxin Li, Xiangzheng Chen, Jing Wang, Xuerui Liao, Longqi Chen, Weimin Li, Yong Yuan, Yushang Yang, Yangping Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The clinical efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains suboptimal, as most patients eventually develop drug resistance and experience disease progression. Here, we identify Collagen Triple Helix Repeat Containing 1 (CTHRC1) as a critical mediator of immunotherapy resistance in ESCC. Elevated CTHRC1 expression was observed in tumors unresponsive to immune checkpoint blockade and was associated with enhanced platelet activity and infiltration of megakaryocytes (MKs) into the tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, CTHRC1 facilitated MK activation and recruitment, fostering an immunosuppressive niche that impaired cytotoxic T-cell activity and promoted cell exhaustion. To therapeutically target this axis, we developed a lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-encapsulated mRNA vaccine encoding CTHRC1. In preclinical ESCC models, the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine elicited robust antitumor immunity. Notably, the combination of CTHRC1-mRNA vaccination with anti–PD-1 therapy induced synergistic intratumoral T-cell infiltration, depletion of MKs, reversal of immunosuppression, and durable tumor regression. Collectively, these findings uncover an unrecognized immunoregulatory function of CTHRC1 in ESCC and highlight its therapeutic targeting as a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.Graphical abstractCTHRC1 directly engages integrin αIIbβ3 and to drive megakaryocyte and activates the TLR4–NF-κB-IL-8 axis to promote megakaryocyte/platelet infiltration, promoting an immunosuppressive ESCC TME and T-cell dysfunction. Administration of a CTHRC1 mRNA–LNP vaccine elicits potent antigen-specific humoral and cellular immunity, dismantles megakaryocyte-mediated immune exclusion, converts a cold TME into an inflamed state, and restores sensitivity to PD-1 blockade. Introduction

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), the leading histological subtype of esophageal cancer, is one of the most lethal malignancies, predominantly affecting populations in East Asia and Africa [1]. In recent years, neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy (nICT) has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for advanced ESCC, as evidenced by accumulating prospective clinical trials [2]. However, despite improved response rates with first-line nICT in advanced ESCC, over half of patients develop primary or acquired resistance [3]. This challenge has limited the five-year survival rate of ESCC patients to approximately 40%, resulting in a survival plateau [4, 5]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying immunotherapy resistance and identifying novel therapeutic strategies to overcome it remain critical for advancing ESCC treatment.

Platelets and megakaryocytes (MKs) are key components of the hematologic system involved in hemostasis and thrombosis [6, 7]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that cancer patients receiving chemotherapy or immunotherapy have a significantly increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) [8]. Conversely, platelets and MKs may contribute to the regulation of treatment efficacy [9]. Levin and Conley first confirmed in 1964 that platelet counts in patients with solid tumors were higher than the normal reference, a condition termed thrombocytosis associated with malignancy [10]. Recent studies have also reported platelet-mediated treatment resistance in solid tumors with vitro and clinical cohort validation [11]. However, the cancer-promoting role of MKs in solid tumors has only recently begun to be investigated, as they were traditionally considered to predominantly reside in the bone marrow [12]. Notably, Huang et al. demonstrated the presence of MKs within non-small cell lung cancer tumors, which correlated with poor prognostic outcomes [13]. Recent research has further shown that MKs suppress B cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity and facilitate hyperglycemia-induced tumor metastasis, underscoring their significant role in tumor immune modulation [13, 14]. Despite these findings, the role of MKs in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and their involvement in immune resistance remains poorly understood.

Collagen triple helix repeat-containing 1 (CTHRC1) is highly expressed in various malignant gastrointestinal tumors and plays a pivotal role in cancer progression [15, 16]. Emerging evidence indicates that CTHRC1 contributes to the regulation of cell polarity and the development of tumor resistance [17]. As a critical modulator of multiple signaling pathways, its function in platelets and MKs remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we identified that CTHRC1 drives resistance to to nICT in ESCC by facilitating platelets/MKs infiltration into TME. CTHRC1 directly binds to integrin αIIbβ3 and promotes cytokine secretion, thereby enhancing MK-ESCC chemotactic adhesion and inducing T-cell exhaustion, ultimately fostering an immunosuppressive TME. To counteract, we developed a CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine and demonstrated that its combination with anti-PD-1 antibody effectively reversed immunotherapy resistance in vivo. Our findings not only underscore the previously underexplored role of MKs and platelets in modulating the immune landscape of solid tumors but also propose a promising combinatorial strategy to overcome resistance to immune checkpoint blockade.

Materials and methods

Cohort study and clinical specimens

This retrospective study included 140 patients with ESCC who were hospitalized and treated at the Department of Thoracic Surgery and Department of Oncology, West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between January 2020 and December 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) histopathologically confirmed thoracic ESCC without prior treatment; (3) locally advanced disease at enrollment (clinical stage II, III, or IVA, without distant metastasis) and eligible for immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy; (4) normal function of major organs; and (5) voluntary participation with signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) contraindications to ICI therapy, such as intestinal obstruction; and (2) incomplete clinical records. All eligible patients received nICT, consisting of two cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy.

We retrospectively collected and analyzed the clinical information of these patients. For cohort 1 (n = 23), we collected hematological parameters, including platelet count and mean platelet volume (MPV), from ESCC patients with either nICT-sensitive (TRG 0–1) or nICT-resistant (TRG 2–3) responses, and performed comparative analyses (Supplementary Table 1) [18]. For cohort 2 (n = 8), tumor tissue samples from 4 pairs of ESCC patients showing differential responses to nICT were collected and subjected to RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) analysis. For cohort 3 (n = 109), formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded postoperative tumor specimens from eligible patients were collected and re-arrayed into a tissue microarray (TMA), in which representative tumor cores from multiple cases were integrated into a single slide for high-throughput analysis.

Hematoxylin-eosin and immunohistochemical staining

Section (4 μm thick) were obtained from tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subjected to HE and IHC staining. For HE staining, standard procedures were followed, including xylene dewaxing, ethanol rehydration, hematoxylin staining (5–10 min), acid alcohol differentiation, eosin counterstaining (2–3 min), dehydration, and mounting. Patients were dichotomized into high- and low-CTHRC1 expression groups using the median H-score as the cutoff.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry analysis

To investigate the role of CTHRC1 in the tumor microenvironment of ESCC, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) was performed using the PANO 6-plex IHC kit (Panovue, Beijing) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequential staining with different primary antibodies was conducted, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) was used to enhance signal intensity, with microwave-mediated antigen retrieval applied after each amplification cycle.

The primary antibodies included: CTHRC1 (h), CD3 (h), CD4 (h), CD8 (h), CD42b (h), CD19 (h), CD20 (h), CD68 (h), CD86 (h), CD206 (h), CTHRC1 (m), CD42b (m), CD3 (m), and CD8 (m). After all rounds of antigen staining, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Slides were scanned using the Mantra imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA), and quantitative and spatial analyses were performed using inForm image analysis software (version 2.4, PerkinElmer, USA). For quantitative analysis, StrataQuest software (TissueGnostics) was utilized to measure cell density based on nuclear area, protein expression per cell, and cell area. Spatial distribution analysis of immune cells was performed by quantifying CD3 + CD4+/CD3 + CD8+ T cells、CD42b+ cells、CD19 + CD20+ B cells、CD68 + CD86+ cells、CD68 + CD206+ cells at defined distance intervals (0–25 μm, 25–50 μm, 50–100 μm, 100–200 μm and >200 μm) from CTHRC1⁺ cells using the same software.

Identification of MKs in ESCC tissue

MKs were identified using complementary morphological, protein, and transcriptomic criteria across experimental platforms. For HE, MKs were initially recognized based on their characteristic large size, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and multilobulated or polyploid nuclei, and were independently confirmed by experienced pathologists through microscopic evaluation. For lineage-specific validation in tissue sections, MKs were further defined as large CD42b+ nucleated (DAPI+) cells, whereas small CD42b+ anucleate fragments were classified as platelets and excluded from analysis. To corroborate MK infiltration at the transcriptional level, publicly available ESCC single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets (GSE188900 and GSE196756) were analyzed. Because MKs are nucleated and transcriptionally active, they form stable cell clusters and express canonical megakaryocyte lineage genes (ITGA2B/CD41, GP1BA/CD42b, PF4, and VWF) together with megakaryocyte-associated transcription factors (GATA1 and GATA2), whereas platelet aggregates lack nuclei and transcriptional activity and therefore do not generate coherent transcriptomic profiles. Clusters lacking these nuclear transcriptional features were excluded as platelet debris.

Transwell assay

A 6-well Transwell chamber (8 μm pore size) was used to assess cell migration. KYSE-180 cells (1 × 10⁶) were seeded in the lower chamber and MEG-01 cells (5 × 10⁵) in the upper chamber, with both chambers containing RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Recombinant human CTHRC1 protein was added to the lower chamber at a final concentration of 100 ng/ml. After 12 h of incubation, migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Migrated cell numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA-Seq processing and analysis

Whole-transcriptome analysis was conducted using RNA-seq on ESCC tumor samples previously collected in this study to identify genes differentially expressed in immune-resistant versus sensitive tumors. The ribosomal RNA (rRNA) in the sample was removed using the GenSeq® rRNA Removal Kit (GenSeq, Inc.). After removing rRNA, the sample was used to construct a sequencing library using the GenSeq® Low Input RNA Library Prep Kit (GenSeq, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The constructed sequencing library was quality controlled and quantified using the BioAnalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, USA), and then 150 bp double-end sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument. After sequencing with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer, paired-end reads were obtained. Q30 was used for quality control, and cutadapt software (v1.9.3) was used to remove adapters and low-quality reads to obtain high-quality reads. The hisat2 software (v2.0.4) was used to align high-quality reads to the reference genome. Then, HTSeq software (v0.9.1) was applied to obtain the original counts at the gene level as the mRNA expression profile, and the original counts at the transcript level as the lncRNA expression profile [19]. We use R package edgeR, Pheatmap and ggplot2 to standardize and calculate the fold change and p-value between the two groups of samples, screen differentially expressed mRNA, display heatmap and volcano plots. The CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm and R package xCell was used to infer the infiltration levels of platelets and MKs in the TME. Further RNA-seq analysis was conducted on MKs and KYSE-180 cells treated with rCTHRC1. Enrichment analyses were subsequently carried out using R packages clusterProfiler, GOplot, and enrichplot. R package Seurat was used to analyze the single-cell RNAseq data from public database (GSE196756) to display the expression of CTHRC1 in tumor and normal epithelial cells.

Co-culture of MK and ESCC cells

To investigate MK adhesion to ESCC cells, a co-culture system was established using the MEG-01 cell line (suspension) and KYSE-180 cell line (adherent). Both cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% (FBS. Recombinant human CTHRC1 (100 ng/ml) was added to the MEG-01 culture system for 12 h prior to co-culture. Subsequently, MEG-01 cells (5 × 10⁵) were added to a 6-well plate containing pre-adhered KYSE-180 cells (1 × 106) and co-cultured for an additional 12 h. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were fixed with glutaraldehyde. The adhesion of MEG-01 cells to KYSE-180 cells was then observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

PBMC isolation and cytotoxicity assay

Peripheral blood samples anticoagulated with heparin were collected from two healthy donors. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using density gradient centrifugation with a human PBMC isolation solution. The cells were then resuspended at a concentration of 2 × 10⁶ cells/ml and seeded into T25 flasks in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 25% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Half of the culture medium was refreshed every 48 h, and recombinant IL-2 (100 U/ml) and IFN-γ (100 ng/ml) were added every 48 h to support PBMC growth, proliferation, and activation.

Once the PBMCs reached a density of 5 × 10⁶ cells/ml, they were introduced into the established MK–ESCC co-culture system in 6-well plates. After 12 h of co-incubation, the supernatant was collected for mass cytometry analysis (CyTOF) to assess immune cell composition and surface marker expression [20]. Adherent cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged for quantification of PBMC-mediated tumor cell killing using ImageJ software.

CyTOF staining and data analysis

The cryovials containing PBMCs samples were taken out from the liquid nitrogen tank and quickly transferred to a 37℃ water bath for rapid thawing. After 1 min, they were taken out and the cell suspension was completely transferred into pre-warmed RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) at 37℃ for cell washing. Then, LunaStain cell staining buffer was used to repeat the washing process. According to cell counting results, 310^6 cells were collected and stained with 10 µL of Cisplatin reagent (Polaris Biology, China) for 5 min, and washed once with LunaStain cell staining solution. Subsequently, Fc Receptor Blocking Solution (Biolegend, USA) was used for staining for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed and incubated with heavy metal-labeled membrane antibodies mix (Polaris Biology, China) for 30 min at room temperature (Supplementary Table 2). Cells were then washed twice with LunaStain cell staining buffer and stained with Ir-DNA intercalator Reagent (Polaris Biology, China) for 10 min. After staining, the cells were washed and adjusted to 5*10^5 cells per milliliter with LunaAcq acquisition solution (Polaris Biology, China). Cell acquisition was performed on a mass cytometer (Lunarion, Polaris Biology, China).

After acquisition, mass cytometry data was converted to standard FSC 3.0 file (Lunarion, Polaris Biology, China). Manual gating was performed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, US). T-SNE was used to get an overview of the immune compartment. To identify different cell subtypes, FlowSOM clustering and metaclustering was performed.

CTL killing assay

To evaluate whether MKs enhance resistance to antigen-specific cytotoxicity by CD8⁺ T cells after adhering to ESCC, mouse ESCC organoid cells were transduced with gga-OVAL lentivirus (UBIGENE, China) to stably express ovalbumin (OVA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Successfully transduced cells were selected and expanded for subsequent experiments. These OVA-expressing ESCC organoid cells were co-cultured with rCTHRC1(m)-pretreated L8057 cells (mouse MK cells) for 12 h to facilitate the formation of MKs-ESCC complexes. The resulting complexes were then incubated with OVA-specific CD8⁺ T cells at various effector-to-target (E: T) ratios (1:2, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, and 30:1) for 24 h. OT-1 T cells were isolated from the spleens of OT-1 transgenic C57BL/6 mice (IMSR_JAX:000664) using a negative selection kit (STEMCELL, Canada). Cytotoxicity was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using the CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

For each sample, 5 × 10⁶ cells were used for staining. To distinguish live and dead cells, a non-fixable viability dye, 7-AAD (eBioscience), was applied. Cells were then blocked with Fc-block (BioLegend) to reduce non-specific binding and subsequently stained with the following antibodies: CD8a, IFN-γ, PD-1, CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD42b, and CD41. To assess intracellular IFN-γ production in CD8⁺ T cells, cells were re-stimulated with 0.5 ng/ml PMA and 0.5 ng/ml ionomycin in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS for 1 h to promote cytokine accumulation. This was followed by the addition of a protein transport inhibitor, and incubation continued for 3–5 h with intermittent shaking. Flow cytometry data were acquired using Agilent flow cytometer and analyzed using appropriate flow cytometry software.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

SPR assays were conducted using a Biacore T100 instrument equipped with a Sensor Chip CM5 (GE Healthcare, USA) at 25 °C to assess the interactions between compounds and target proteins. Purified integrin αIIbβ3 proteins were immobilized onto the CM5 chip surface. Sensorgrams were obtained by injecting varying concentrations of the test compounds. Binding kinetics and dissociation constants (Kd) were determined by fitting the sensorgram data using BIA evaluation software version 4.1.

Amplification and linearization of plasmid templates

The CTHRC1 protein sequence was retrieved from NCBI (UniProt: Q9D1D6). The extracellular domain of CTHRC1 was repeated five times, with each repeat connected by glycine-serine (G-S) linkers. The specific mRNA-encoded protein sequences of CTHRC1 are provided in Supplementary Table 3. The pVAX1-CTHRC1 plasmid was transformed into DH5α competent cells using the heat shock method. Following transformation, bacterial cells were cultured on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates containing ampicillin and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was then selected and inoculated into an Erlenmeyer flask containing LB medium supplemented with ampicillin, and cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 250 rpm for 12–15 h. Plasmid DNA was extracted from the collected bacterial cells using a plasmid extraction kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To linearize the plasmid, the XhoI restriction enzyme was used to digest the amplified plasmid at a site adjacent to the poly(A) tail. The degree of linearization was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The linearized plasmid DNA was purified using a PCR product recovery kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US), and its concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US).

In vitro transcription and purification of CTHRC1 mRNA

mRNA was synthesized in vitro using DNA-dependent transcription with T7 RNA polymerase. The process involved sequential steps: RNase-free water and NTPs were first mixed, followed by the addition of CleanCap® Reagent AG (3’ OMe), which was mixed and centrifuged. Next, 10× Transcription Buffer was added, and the mixture was centrifuged again to collect the supernatant. The DNA template was then introduced. Enzymes including Murine RNase Inhibitor, Yeast Inorganic Pyrophosphatase, and T7 RNA Polymerase were added, mixed, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The transcription reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 2–3 h. To remove residual DNA, DNase I was added, and the resulting RNA product was purified using the Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). mRNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, respectively.

Preparation of CTHRC1 mRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles

To prepare lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) encapsulating mRNA, an ethanol-based lipid mixture was first formulated by dissolving ALC-0315, cholesterol, DSPC, and DMG-PEG2000 at a molar ratio of 50:38.5:10:1.5, with a final lipid concentration of 10 mg/mL. Meanwhile, the mRNA encoding the antigenic protein was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL in 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.0). These two phases were combined at a 1:3 ratio using a microfluidic mixing device (Inano E, Micro&Nano, Shanghai, China) at a total flow rate of 12 mL/min to produce the mRNA-LNP formulation. The product was subsequently diluted 40-fold with sterile PBS (10 mM, pH 7.2) and transferred into Amicon® Ultra-15 centrifugal filters (10 kDa cutoff) for purification and buffer exchange. Samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 15–30 min at 4 °C, and the PBS wash step was repeated three times to ensure purity. The final mRNA-LNP solution was adjusted to 2 mg/mL, stored at 4 °C, and utilized within one week to ensure stability. The particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of the LNPs were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a Mastersizer 3000 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK). The mRNA encapsulation efficiency was determined using the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, R11490).

mRNA and mRNA-LNPs transfection

mRNA-LNP and mRNA-Lipo transfections were performed using LNPs and Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US), respectively. HEK293T cells at approximately 70% confluency were transfected with mRNA-CTHRC1-LNP or mRNA-CTHRC1-Lipo at a final mRNA concentration of 0.5 µg/cm². Lipofectamine-mediated transfection was carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Expression differences following transfection were evaluated by Western blot analysis.

Evaluation of mouse-specific antibody titers and isotype profiling using ELISA

The levels of antigen-specific antibodies in mouse serum were quantified by indirect ELISA. ELISA plates were first coated with rCTHRC1 protein (expressed in E. coli) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBST, wells were blocked with 1% BSA at 37 °C for 2 h. Following blocking, 100 µl of serially diluted serum samples were added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with gentle shaking. After washing, 100 µl of HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG (1:1000) was added and incubated at 37 °C for another hour. Plates were washed, and 100 µl of TMB substrate was added for color development in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. The reaction was stopped with 100 µl of 2% H₂SO₄, and absorbance was read at 450 nm. For isotype analysis, primary antibodies against IgG1, IgM, and IgG2a were used in combination with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies.

Western blot analysis

For Western blot analysis, cells and tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6; 150 mM NaCl; 1% NP-40; 1% sodium deoxycholate; 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Kangwei Biotechnology, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CTHRC1, CD31, CD41, P-selectin, CD36, β-actin, ITGA2B, ITGB3, ITGA2, CXCR4, P2Y1, AKT, p-AKT, Rap1, Rap1 GTP, GAPDH, TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, TLR6, MYD88, TRAF6, IκBα, p-IκBα, P65, IL-8, CXCR1/2 and p-P65. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Millipore, USA) and analyzed by densitometry using ImageJ software. Information regarding the antibodies utilized is presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Establishment of ESCC mouse models

An ESCC mouse model was established using Trp53^-/-; Cas9 C57BL/6 mice. Esophageal epithelial tissues were isolated and cultured in vitro to generate organoids. CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing was employed to induce Pten and Smad4 loss-of-function mutations, along with overexpression of Myc and Kras^G12D within the organoids (TPSKM organoid). Organoids were enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions, mixed with Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio, and transplanted into anesthetized mice aged 6–8 weeks. A small incision was made in the left upper abdomen along the pararectal line to expose the stomach and esophagus. Tumor cells were then injected at the gastroesophageal junction. After injection, the peritoneum and skin were sutured. Approximately one week post-surgery, orthotopic ESCC tumors were successfully established in the mice.

Cytokine antibody arrays

Cytokines present in the conditioned medium were analyzed using the RayBio® Mouse Interleukin Array C1, C2 and Human Cytokine Array C6, C7 (RayBiotech, USA), which enables the simultaneous detection of multiple secreted proteins. Briefly, 2 mL of blocking buffer was added to each well of the array and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Following antigen blocking, 1 mL of sample (cell culture supernatant or mouse serum) was added to each well. The arrays were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by thorough washing. Subsequently, biotin-labeled detection antibodies were applied and incubated for 90 min at RT. After additional washes to remove unbound antibodies, streptavidin was added and incubated for 1 h at RT. Signal detection was performed using the ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA), and data were extracted accordingly. For in vivo therapeutic comparison, tumor-bearing mice received anti-PD-1 alone or in combination with anti-CTHRC1 neutralizing antibody or anti-CD42b drug (R300) according to the indicated schedule, whereas the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine was administered as described above. Platelet depletion efficiency, tumor growth, and body weight were monitored throughout the experiment.

Platelet isolation

To investigate the inhibitory effect of the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine on platelet migration and adhesion toward TME, an in vitro platelet adhesion assessment system was established. Platelets were isolated from peripheral blood of mice in each experimental group using mouse platelet isolation kit (Solarbio, China), and the resulting platelet suspensions (1 × 10⁶/mL) were co-cultured with ESCC organoids for 24 h. After incubation, cell complexes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and sequentially stained with anti-cytokeratin 5 antibodies to label tumor cell membranes and anti-CD42b antibodies to label platelet membranes. Colocalization analysis was performed using laser scanning confocal microscopy. Parallel samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, subjected to critical point drying, and examined using SEM to visualize platelet–tumor cell adhesion at high resolution. The proportion or number of adherent platelets was quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with biological replicates, and repeated experiments yielded consistent results. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.5.1) and GraphPad Prism. For comparisons between two groups with equal variances, unpaired Student’s t-tests were applied; Welch’s t-test was used when variances were unequal. Paired t-tests were employed for matched data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among three or more groups, followed by Holm-Sidak or Tukey’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data in contingency tables. Correlation analyses were performed to assess relationships between continuous variables. Covariates were first screened by univariate analysis, and variables with P < 0.10 were subsequently entered into multivariate regression models. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method with statistical significance evaluated by the log-rank test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ns, not significant.

Results

ESCC patients exhibiting resistance to nICT display elevated platelet levels, along with notable infiltration of MKs within the TME

Thrombocytosis is a frequently observed hematologic abnormality in patients with advanced or recurrent malignancies and is primarily driven by the dysregulated proliferation and activation of platelets and MKs [21, 22]. To investigate this, we collected peripheral blood and tumor tissue samples from clinically classified immunotherapy-sensitive (TRG0–1) and -resistant (TRG2–3) ESCC patients and performed comprehensive analyses (Fig. 1A; Table 1). Compared to immunotherapy-sensitive ESCC patients, those with TRG2–3 exhibited significantly reduced peripheral white blood cells, lymphocytes, and red blood cells, alongside abnormally elevated platelet counts and decreased mean platelet volume (MPV) (Fig. 1B-C; Supplementary Fig. 1A-D).

CTHRC1 directly binds to and potentiates integrin αIIbβ3 activation

In previous work, we observed that tumors can secrete CTHRC1 to enhance their own progression and recruit immunosuppressive cells via cytokine-dependent pathways [23, 24]. To investigate how ESCC cells recruit MKs into the tumor microenvironment via CTHRC1, we performed transwell coculture assays using MKs and ESCC cells. The addition of recombinant CTHRC1 (rCTHRC1) protein significantly enhanced the chemotaxis of MKs toward ESCC cells (Fig. 2A-C). RNA-sequencing of MKs from the rCTHRC1-treated group revealed substantial upregulation of gene expression programs associated with cell migration and adhesion (Fig. 2D-E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further indicated activation of multiple MK activation and adhesion-related pathways (Fig. 2F). Utilizing the STRING database, we constructed a potential CTHRC1-centered protein–protein interaction network, and local clustering analysis revealed that CTHRC1-interacting proteins were enriched in ECM–receptor interaction, cell adhesion, platelet activation, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). Given the central role of membrane receptors such as integrin α2β1, integrin αIIbβ3, P2Y1, and CXCR4 in downstream signal transduction in MKs, we hypothesized that CTHRC1 may directly bind and activate one or more of these receptors (Fig. 2G). To test this hypothesis, we knocked down the genes encoding key ligand-binding subunits of these receptors—ITGA2 (integrin α2β1), ITGA2B and ITGB3 (integrin αIIbβ3), CXCR4, and P2RY1 (P2Y1)—in HEK-293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Loss of ITGA2B or ITGB3 abrogated CTHRC1-induced AKT phosphorylation, whereas knockdown of the other subunits did not (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Pharmacological inhibition studies confirmed these findings: only eptifibatide, a selective integrin αIIbβ3 inhibitor, blocked CTHRC1-induced platelet aggregation, while inhibitors of integrin α2β1 (BTT 3033), CXCR4 (plerixafor), or P2Y1 (BPTU) had no effect (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these findings indicate that integrin αIIbβ3 may function as a dominant receptor mediating CTHRC1-induced AKT activation and MK hyperreactivity.

To further verify whether CTHRC1 directly binds to integrin αIIbβ3, we performed in silico molecular docking simulations, which suggested that CTHRC1 may interact at the interface between the ITGA2B and ITGB3 subunits. The docking model indicated that CTHRC1 binds to the ligand-binding site of integrin αIIbβ3, specifically engaging the β-propeller domain of the αIIb subunit and the βI domain of the β3 subunit, constituting a potential activation interface (Fig. 2I). SPR assays confirmed direct binding between CTHRC1 and purified recombinant integrin αIIbβ3, and this interaction was competitively inhibited by eptifibatide, supporting the specificity of the CTHRC1-integrin αIIbβ3 binding (Fig. 2J-K; Supplementary Fig. 2E-F). Co-immunoprecipitation assays further revealed a potential interaction between CTHRC1 and integrin subunits ITGB3/ITGA2B (Fig. 2L). We then performed protein and small-molecule docking analyses, which indicated that eptifibatide binds to the integrin αIIbβ3 site in a manner that competitively overlaps with CTHRC1 (Supplementary Fig. 2G). Co-immunoprecipitation assays further demonstrated that eptifibatide competitively attenuated the interaction between CTHRC1 and ITGA2B/ITGB3 in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 2H). Mechanistically, CTHRC1 markedly enhanced downstream AKT phosphorylation and RAP1 activation in MKs, both of which were largely blocked by eptifibatide (Fig. 2M-N). Functionally, pretreating MEG-01 cells with rCTHRC1 for 6–12 h enhanced their adhesion to KYSE-180 ESCC cells in coculture assays (Supplementary Fig. 2I-J). Eptifibatide treatment significantly impaired MK adhesion to ESCC cell lines (Fig. 2O-P). Collectively, these findings strongly support a model in which CTHRC1 directly binds to and activates integrin αIIbβ3, promoting MK hyperreactivity and their recruitment into the TME of ESCC.

CTHRC1 indirectly regulates MKs chemotaxis toward ESCC via the TLR4/NF-κB/IL-8 axis

To further comprehensively evaluate the role of CTHRC1 in ESCC, we confirmed that knockdown of CTHRC1 in ESCC resulted in reduced cell proliferation, invasion, and migration capacities (Supplementary Fig. 3A-E). Furthermore, transcriptome sequencing analysis on KYSE180 cell line samples supplemented with rCTHRC1 revealed that CTHRC1 was significantly associated with intercellular interaction GO terms, as well as the Toll-like receptor (TLR) and NF-κB signaling pathways (Fig. 3A-C; Supplementary Fig. 3F-G). Given that CTHRC1 functions as a secreted protein and the TLR and NF-κB pathways are hierarchically related in an upstream-downstream configuration, we hypothesize that CTHRC1 may activate the NF-κB pathway by binding to TLR receptors on the tumor cell membrane surface (Fig. 3D). To validate this hypothesis, we performed immunoprecipitation assay to investigate the interactions between CTHRC1 and membrane-associated TLR pathway proteins (TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, and TLR6) (Fig. 3E). The IP results suggested a potential interaction specifically between CTHRC1 and TLR4, which was subsequently confirmed by Co- immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence assay (Fig. 3F-G; Supplementary Fig. 3H). Through the application of the TLR4-specific inhibitor Tak242, we confirmed the protein level that CTHRC1, upon interacting with TLR4, triggers the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in KYSE180 cells (Fig. 3H-I).

TLR/NF-κB signaling serves as a critical regulator of cytokine induction and expression [25]. Accordingly, we employed a cytokine array to evaluate alterations in the levels of secreted cytokines (Fig. 3J). We found that the secretion of IL-8, TIMP-1, uPAR, RANTES, and angiogenin was reduced following the administration of Tak242 and restored upon supplementation with rCTHRC1 (Fig. 3K; Supplementary Fig. 3I). We then replicated the transwell migration assay with co-cultured MKs and KYSE180 cells, supplementing the system with pertinent cytokines (Fig. 3L). We identified IL-8 as a strongly correlated cytokine in promoting MK chemotaxis toward ESCC (Fig. 3M). In addition, western blot analysis of ESCC tumor tissues from patients with differential responses to immunotherapy revealed broad upregulation of the TLR4/NF-κB/IL-8 axis in resistant tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3J-K). To further determine whether IL-8 receptor signaling mediates CTHRC1-induced MK recruitment, we performed rescue experiments by knocking down CXCR1 and CXCR2, the two canonical IL-8 receptors, in MKs (Supplementary Fig. 3L). We found that depletion of either receptor attenuated IL-8-induced migratory activation of MKs, with CXCR2 knockdown showing a more pronounced inhibitory effect, indicating that the IL-8/CXCR1/2 axis contributes to the chemotactic response of MKs toward ESCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 3M-N).

MKs adhesion enhances the resistance of ESCC cells to immune cytotoxicity

Previous studies have shown that neoadjuvant therapy is an independent risk factor for the development of VTE in patients with ESCC [26]. Conversely, platelets and MKs may contribute to the regulation of ESCC treatment efficacy. Platelet-mediated resistance mechanisms have been progressively validated in both in vitro models and clinical cohorts [11]. However, as MKs have traditionally been regarded as residing primarily in the bone marrow, their potential involvement in cancer therapy has received limited investigation. To further investigate the impact of ESCC adhesion to infiltrating MKs on immunotherapy efficacy, we treated MEG-01 cells with rCTHRC1 and co-cultured them with KYSE180 cells for 6 h to establish an ESCC-MK adhesion model. PBMCs were isolated from two independent healthy donors and activated using interleukin-2 (IL-2) and interferon-gamma (IFNγ) to enhance immune function. The activated PBMCs were then introduced into the co-culture system (Fig. 4A). Our findings indicate that MKs adhering to ESCC cells exhibit significantly reduced susceptibility to immune-mediated cytotoxicity, suggesting that MK adhesion provides a protective effect against immune cell attack (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. 4B).

We then collected the supernatant from the cytotoxicity assay and conducted mass cytometry (CyTOF) analysis. Our findings indicate that MK adhering to ESCC cells not only exhibited resistance to immune-mediated cytotoxicity but also contributed to the accelerated exhaustion of cytotoxic immune cells (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. 4A-B). Specifically, we observed a marked depletion of naïve CD8⁺ T cells, effector memory CD8⁺ T cells (EMCD8), double-negative (DN) T cells, γδ T cells, and natural killer T (NKT) cells (Fig. 4C-E). Furthermore, there was a significant expansion of terminally differentiated effector memory CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells (EMRA), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and senescent CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell populations, suggesting an immunosuppressive shift in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 4F-G). To evaluate whether MKs enhance the resistance of ESCC cells to antigen-specific CD8⁺ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity, we assessed the in vitro cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-dependent killing of OVA-presenting ESCC tumor cells by OT-I transgenic T cells, which specifically recognize the OVA peptide (Fig. 4H). We found that the MKs-ESCC adhesion complex significantly reduced the sensitivity of ESCC cells to antigen-specific T cell-mediated killing (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis of the supernatants collected post-CTL engagement revealed a significant reduction in IFN-γ and IL-2 secretion, indicating that the antigen-specific T cells had entered an exhausted state (Fig. 4J-K; Supplementary Fig. 4C).

After synthesizing CTHRC1-mRNA using a one-step method, capillary electrophoresis confirmed both high purity and efficient 5’-end capping of the CTHRC1-mRNA (Fig. 5C-D). The mRNA was subsequently encapsulated into LNPs via a microfluidic system. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNPs exhibited a uniform spherical morphology and dynamic light scattering measurements indicated an average particle size of 81.12 nm (Fig. 5E-F; Supplementary Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis using the RiboGreen-RNA assay, combined with agarose gel electrophoresis, confirmed the high encapsulation efficiency of CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP (Fig. 5G; Supplementary Fig. 5B). In HEK293T cell transfection, CTHRC1-mRNA-LNPs achieved significantly higher CTHRC1 transfection efficiency compared to CTHRC1-mRNA-Lipo2000 (Fig. 5H).

We initially immunized in vivo with 10 ug CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine on days 0, 6, and 12, and monitored the humoral immune response. Through ELISA analysis, we observed that IgM levels induced by immunization with 10 µg of CTHRC1-mRNA-LNPs after 6 days showed no significant difference compared to the LNP control group (Supplementary Fig. 5C). However, CTHRC1-specific IgG titers demonstrated a marked elevation following the initial immunization, indicating a rapid immune response triggered by a single dose (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Notably, after completing the vaccination course (day 24), sustained high levels of antigen-specific IgG titers were observed (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Generally, Th1-mediated immune responses predominantly drive IgG2a production, while pronounced IgG1 alterations are associated with Th2 responses. ELISA quantification confirmed substantial levels of both IgG1 and IgG2a in the serum of CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP-immunized mice, with IgG2a slightly surpassing IgG1 (Supplementary Fig. 5F). These findings suggest that the vaccine induced a mixed Th1/Th2 immune response, with a modest predominance of Th1 response.

CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine reversed immunotherapy resistance and abrogated platelet/MK infiltration within the TME of ESCC

To evaluate the potential of targeting CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP for immunotherapy in ESCC, we established an orthotopic organoid ESCC model in C57BL/6 mice, with in situ tumors forming approximately one week after surgery (Supplementary Fig. 6A-E). We categorized the in vivo models into four groups: control, nivolumab, CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine, and combined therapy group, nivolumab was administered at intervals of 3 days (for a total of 5 doses), while the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine was given at intervals of 6 days (for a total of 3 doses) (Fig. 5I). Encouragingly, the combination therapy group receiving both the vaccine and nivolumab exhibited the most favorable therapeutic outcomes in the in vivo model, with the slowest tumor growth rate (Fig. 5J-N). While nivolumab monotherapy and CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP monotherapy each displayed moderate tumor-suppressive effects during the initial phase, the combination therapy group exhibited a more sustained and pronounced tumor control effect after 20 days.

Furthermore, H&E staining revealed pronounced tumor necrosis in the combination therapy group (Fig. 5O-Q). Immunohistochemical analysis of Ki-67 revealed that treatment with either the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine or the combination therapy significantly suppressed tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 5P-R). Compared to the control group, no significant body weight reduction was observed in the other treatment groups, confirming the biosafety of the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine (Fig. 5S). Moreover, immunization with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine induced a persistent generation of antigen-specific IgG antibodies (Fig. 5T).

The CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine and nivolumab demonstrated a synergistic effect. To further confirm whether the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine activated an immune response in vivo, we employed a cytokine array to detect differentially expressed and highly secreted cytokines following immunization with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine (Fig. 6A). We found that immunostimulatory cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-17 A, IL-12 p40, and TNF-α) were significantly elevated in the serum of mice following immunization with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine (Fig. 6B). Then, we performed flow cytometry analysis on tumor tissues. Although the proportion of CD8 + T cell infiltration in the tumor tissues of the vaccine group and the nivolumab group exhibited no difference, the frequency of IFN-γ + CD8 + T cells in mice receiving combination therapy was significantly elevated compared to the other treatment groups (Fig. 6C-F; Supplementary Fig. 7A). Additionally, we observed that immunization with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine led to a decreased proportion of MK infiltration within the tumor tissue, whereas no significant differences in MK infiltration were observed in the bone marrow (Fig. 6D-G; Supplementary Fig. 7B). MKs are the precursors of platelets, and the physiological behavior of platelets can partially represent MKs. We collected peripheral blood from the mice and isolated platelets, which were then co-cultured with murine ESCC organoids. IF analysis and scanning electron microscopy demonstrated that platelets derived from the control and nivolumab monotherapy groups displayed enhanced adhesion to ESCC organoids, whereas platelets from mice immunized with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine exhibited a diminished tumor adhesion capacity (Fig. 6I-J; Supplementary Fig. 7C-E). mIHC demonstrated that, following immunization with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine, the infiltration of CD42b+ cells in the tumor tissue was eliminated, accompanied by an increase in CD8 + T cell infiltration (Fig. 6K; Supplementary Fig. 7F). Notably, immunization with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine led to a reduction in CTHRC1 expression in the tumor tissue and effectively elicited CTHRC1-specific cytotoxic immune responses (Supplementary Fig. 7F). Moreover, mIHC further revealed that the combination of CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccination and nivolumab significantly increased the intratumoral infiltration of CD4⁺ T cells and CD20⁺ B cells, indicating a broadly activated adaptive TME associated with the enhanced antitumor efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 7G-H).

To further determine whether direct depletion of platelets or therapeutic blockade of CTHRC1 could phenocopy the antitumor effect of CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccination, we established additional in vivo treatment groups including nivolumab monotherapy, nivolumab combined with R300-mediated platelet depletion, nivolumab combined with anti-CTHRC1 neutralizing antibody, and nivolumab combined with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine. We first confirmed the efficiency of R300-mediated platelet depletion in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 7I). In ESCC mouse model, both platelet depletion plus nivolumab and anti-CTHRC1 antibody plus nivolumab showed partial antitumor activity compared with nivolumab monotherapy (Fig. 6L). Notably, the frequency of IFN-γ⁺ CD8⁺ T cells was also significantly increased in the R300 plus nivolumab group, whereas only a modest increase was observed in the anti-CTHRC1 antibody plus nivolumab group (Fig. 6M; Supplementary Fig. 7J). However, long-term platelet depletion combined with nivolumab was associated with detectable body weight fluctuations, suggesting limited tolerability (Fig. 6N). The combination of CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccination and nivolumab produced the strongest tumor control among all treatment groups, while maintaining a more favorable safety profile (Fig. 6L-N). In addition, mice receiving the vaccine combination exhibited the highest serum titers of CTHRC1-specific IgG at the experimental endpoint, supporting durable humoral immune activation induced by active immunization (Fig. 6O).

Discussion

As fundamental components of the hematopoietic system, platelets and their precursors, MKs, are increasingly recognized as active participants in a wide range of physiological and pathological processes, including hemostasis, inflammation, and cancer progression [27, 28]. The potential link between thrombocytosis and malignancy was first proposed by Levin and Conley in 1964 [10]. Since then, numerous studies have demonstrated that elevated platelet counts are frequently associated with poor prognosis across various solid tumors [29]. However, the functional contributions of platelets/MKs within the TME, as well as their potential to modulate responses to immunotherapy, remains largely unexplored.

Here, we found that patients with ESCC who exhibited resistance to nICT had significantly higher peripheral platelet counts compared to responders. Moreover, MKs, typically confined to the bone marrow, lungs, and spleen, were aberrantly enriched within the tumor tissue of nICT non-responders. CTHRC1 was identified as a critical mediator driving the recruitment and infiltration of MKs into the TME. Mechanistically, CTHRC1 directly binds to integrin αIIbβ3 on MKs, thereby enhancing PI3K/Rap1 signaling and promoting their migratory and adhesive capabilities. Additionally, CTHRC1 interacts with TLR4 on ESCC cells, activating NF-κB pathway and inducing IL-8 production, which in turn indirectly facilitates MKs recruitment and adhesion. Furthermore, we identified CTHRC1 as a promising immunotherapeutic target. Combination treatment with CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP and anti-PD-1 antibody enhanced antitumor immune responses, effectively abrogated MK infiltration, and overcame therapeutic resistance.

CTHRC1 is a secreted glycoprotein initially identified in the context of vascular injury and repair, where it was found to regulate cell motility and tissue remodeling [30]. Recent studies have revealed that aberrant expression of CTHRC1 is widely observed in human solid cancers, where it plays a crucial role in the shaping and regulation of an immunosuppressive TME [31–33]. Although direct evidence linking CTHRC1 to platelet regulation remains limited, CTHRC1 has been implicated in promoting angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling—processes that theoretically endow it with the capacity to influence platelet adhesion and activation. Integrins are key surface receptors mediating cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, playing essential roles in the biological behavior and signaling of platelets/MKs. In this study, we found that CTHRC1 directly binds to integrin αIIbβ3, functioning as a ligand-receptor pair and triggering downstream signaling cascades. Furthermore, CTHRC1 stimulated ESCC cells to secrete IL-8, which in turn activates MKs. Previous studies have shown that elevated levels of IL-8 enhance MKs migratory capacity and promote their rapid expansion and maturation within the bone marrow, leading to the generation of inflammatory platelets [34, 35]. These platelets are frequently enriched in immunomodulatory molecules such as PD-L1 and proinflammatory cytokines [36]. Therefore, aberrantly activated and chemotactically adhesive MKs may actively participate in shaping an immunosuppressive TME, further contributing to immune escape in solid tumors. Consistently, direct disruption of the platelet/MK axis by R300-mediated depletion partially restored antitumor immunity, as evidenced by increased IFN-γ⁺ CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, supporting the functional importance of platelet/MK-dependent immune suppression in this model.

Previous studies have demonstrated that platelets suppress CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and IFN-γ secretion via TGF-β release [37–39]. In addition, platelets can impair T cell recognition either by expressing PD-L1 or by forming complexes with tumor cells [40, 41]. As the progenitors of platelets, MKs may possess even greater secretory capacity and immunosuppressive potential. Notably, our study revealed that upon adhesion to ESCC cells, MKs conferred enhanced resistance to both nonspecific immune attack and antigen-specific T cell–mediated cytotoxicity. In vitro co-culture of MKs with ESCC cells induced rapid exhaustion of effector T cells, leading to a shift toward terminally differentiated and aging T cell phenotypes, accompanied by a marked suppression of IFN-γ production. These findings suggested that MKs–tumor interactions may contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive niche that facilitates immune evasion.

To counteract the therapeutic resistance driven by MKs infiltration in ESCC, we developed a novel CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine to remodel the immunosuppressive TME and enhance antitumor immunity. Messenger RNA vaccines have emerged as a promising strategy in cancer immunotherapy due to their safety, rapid production, and ability to induce both cellular and humoral immune responses [42–44]. Recent advances in LNP technology have further improved the stability, biodistribution, and immunogenicity of mRNA-based platforms [45, 46]. In our preclinical model, the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine showed limited efficacy as monotherapy but exhibited synergistic antitumor activity when combined with nivolumab. While the anti-PD-1 monotherapy demonstrated initial tumor suppression, therapeutic effects waned after 20 days, recapitulating clinical observations of acquired resistance to PD-1 inhibitors. In contrast, the combination of nivolumab with the CTHRC1-mRNA-LNP vaccine sustained long-term tumor control in mouse model and markedly increased the infiltration of IFN-γ–producing CD8⁺ T cells within the TME. Furthermore, platelet adhesion assays confirmed a notable reduction in platelet–tumor cell adhesion in the vaccine group, supporting the hypothesis that CTHRC1-neutralizing immunity may contribute to the disruption of MK-driven immune barriers. Compared with CTHRC1 blockade, active vaccination achieved stronger antitumor efficacy and elicited the highest CTHRC1-specific IgG titers at the treatment endpoint, suggesting that sustained humoral immune activation may represent an important advantage of the vaccine strategy. In addition, although platelet depletion plus anti-PD-1 partially phenocopied the immune-restorative effect of the vaccine, prolonged depletion was accompanied by body weight fluctuations, whereas the vaccine combination maintained a more favorable safety profile. Therefore, our findings identified CTHRC1 as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming immunotherapy resistance in ESCC.

In summary, the current study demonstrates a previously unrecognized role of MKs/platelets in mediating immunotherapy resistance. We demonstrate that CTHRC1 promotes MKs infiltration and immune evasion in ESCC by activating integrin αIIbβ3 and inducing IL-8 secretion. The TME infiltrated MKs contribute to immune evasion through direct suppression of cytotoxic T cell function. Targeting CTHRC1 with an mRNA-LNP vaccine restores antitumor immunity and offers a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy for ESCC treatment.
