Water–energy nexus analysis of floating PV systems: impact of footprint design on evaporation control and energy yield
Authors: Ceyda Aksoy Tırmıkçı, Ercüment Sancar Adıyaman, Hilal Çapkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid deployment of floating photovoltaics (PV) offers a dual-modality solution for energy and water security by preserving land, enhancing energy yield through cooling, and mitigating evaporation. This study utilizes a commercially calibrated simulation framework to evaluate the performance of three PV configurations—rooftop, small-footprint (SF) FPV, and large-footprint (LF) FPV—under the specific climatic conditions of Türkiye. Comparative results indicate that the SF FPV configuration achieves the highest annual grid-delivered energy (Egrid = 37,320.0 kWh) and the most favorable greenhouse gas payback time (GPBT ≈ 0.80 years), closely followed by LF FPV (≈ 0.81 years), while the rooftop TOPCon system yields a GPBT of ≈ 1.15 years. Beyond energy metrics, volumetric water savings were quantified, revealing that open-water evaporation from the reservoir (513,268 m³/year) can be reduced by 295,919 m³/year under 80% LF FPV coverage. This saving represents 14.47% of the total storage capacity, equivalent to the annual irrigation demand of approximately 148 hectares of cropland. Seasonal analysis further highlights that evaporation reductions are most significant during summer months, with daily evaporation in July decreasing from 5.422 to 2.612 mm/day under maximum coverage. Ultimately, while SF FPV optimizes energy generation and carbon recovery, LF FPV maximizes water preservation, demonstrating that integrated water-energy management strategies can be tailored to the specific resource priorities of arid and semi-arid regions.Keywords: Floating photovoltaic system, Water conservation, Evaporation mitigation, Large‑footprint floating PV, Small‑footprint floating PV, Rooftop PVSubject terms: Climate sciences, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Due to the significant energy potential offered by renewable energy technologies, the deployment of these systems in offshore and floating configurations has been rapidly expanding. Offshore wind and floating photovoltaic (FPV) systems have garnered increasing attention as key components of surface-based renewable energy infrastructure1, 2. Following the completion of a 20 kW research-oriented FPV project in Japan in 20073, the construction and deployment of FPV power plants accelerated worldwide. Since then, FPV systems have been deployed in numerous countries, including the United States, Italy, Spain, France, South Korea, and Singapore4. More recently, China commissioned the Dezhou Dingzhuang FPV project, currently the largest FPV installation globally, with an installed capacity of 320 MW (YSG Solar5. In addition, India has initiated the commissioning of the Omkareshwar Dam FPV project, which is expected to reach 600 MW by 2025 (Economic Times6. At present, the FPV market is dominated by Asia, accounting for approximately 87% of global installed capacity, and is projected to grow to 62 GW by 20307.

Floating photovoltaic (FPV) systems present both notable advantages and certain challenges when compared to conventional ground-mounted photovoltaic (PV) systems. On the one hand, FPV systems generally achieve higher conversion efficiency due to the natural cooling effect of the water surface and reduced dust accumulation. In addition, their minimal land-use requirement eliminates competition with land-intensive sectors such as agriculture, mining, and tourism, while also avoiding challenges related to land acquisition, permitting, and site development. FPV systems have also been reported to generate up to 10.2% more power than equivalent ground-mounted systems for a 10 MW capacity plant. Furthermore, FPV installations provide additional environmental and resource-related benefits, including the reduction of water evaporation in reservoirs, suppression of algae growth, and potential improvements in water quality. These combined advantages may also translate into economic gains, particularly through the elimination of land-related costs and enhanced system performance. On the other hand, FPV systems involve certain limitations, including higher initial capital costs due to the need for floating structures, anchoring, and mooring systems, as well as more complex installation and maintenance requirements. Long-term material degradation, such as corrosion and biofouling, may also affect system reliability. In addition, potential ecological impacts—such as changes in light penetration and dissolved oxygen levels—must be carefully evaluated through site-specific assessments. Despite these challenges, FPV systems offer significant environmental benefits, including reduced reliance on fossil fuels and mitigation of CO₂ emissions8.

Recent nationwide assessments highlighted the vast potential for FPV deployment in Türkiye, a country situated in a semi-arid climate zone increasingly vulnerable to severe droughts and declining per capita water availability9. Specifically, Kulat et al.10, demonstrated that covering only 10% of Türkiye’s suitable water reservoirs could generate 125 TWh of energy annually—meeting roughly 41% of the national electricity demand—while conserving 1,242.1 hm³ of freshwater. Complementing this, Ateş11, identified that FPV installations on hydroelectric power plant (HEPP) reservoirs could satisfy 7.3% of the surface water needed for agricultural irrigation nationwide through evaporation reduction. On a more localized scale, Temiz and Javani12, showcased the feasibility of small-scale FPV systems in the Aegean region, illustrating that such systems can meet up to 86% of decentralized energy needs while preserving terrestrial footprints.

However, despite these large-scale assessments and projections, a critical gap remains in high-resolution, site-specific comparative analyses that bridge the transition from theoretical modelling to practical implementation across different FPV configurations. Existing studies often focus either on utility-scale deployments or purely simulation-based approaches, with limited direct comparison of alternative FPV layouts under identical environmental conditions.

FPV systems are commonly categorized into three main groups: (i) pedestal-type systems, (ii) small-footprint (SF) systems, and (iii) large-footprint (LF) systems. SF systems are typically installed closer to the water surface and can achieve enhanced cooling, while LF systems cover a larger portion of the water surface, resulting in greater evaporation reduction but reduced convective heat transfer. Accordingly, FPV configurations are often grouped into SF and LF categories based on surface coverage13, 14. Comparative studies generally indicate that SF systems achieve higher electrical efficiency due to improved heat dissipation15, 16.

While several studies have established the general benefits of floating photovoltaic (FPV) systems in terms of enhanced energy yield and water conservation17, a critical gap remains in high-resolution comparative analyses that validate simulation-based predictions against real-world implementations across different floating configurations. In particular, existing studies predominantly focus on either large-scale utility projects or purely theoretical simulations, often overlooking direct, site-specific comparisons between alternative FPV layouts under identical environmental conditions.

This study addresses these limitations by conducting a comprehensive comparative analysis of rooftop PV, small-footprint (SF) FPV, and large-footprint (LF) FPV systems using experimentally informed simulations for a representative agricultural reservoir in Türkiye. By bridging simulation-based modelling with practical implementation, the study provides both numerical validation and a decision-support framework tailored for water-stressed regions.

Methodology

The research methodology is structured around a multi-dimensional framework designed to evaluate the technical, environmental, and volumetric performance of diverse PV configurations. This approach, illustrated in Fig. 1, follows an integrated four-phase process. Initially, site-specific meteorological and reservoir data from Türkiye were utilized to calibrate the simulation tools. In the second phase, three distinct configurations—Rooftop PV, Small-Footprint (SF) FPV, and Large-Footprint (LF) FPV—were established within the modelling environment (Fig. 2). The third phase involved a comprehensive assessment of key performance metrics, including annual energy yield, Greenhouse Gas Payback Time (GPBT), and evaporation mitigation potential. Finally, these results were synthesized into a decision-support output, categorizing the systems based on their strategic advantages in either energy-carbon optimization or maximum water preservation.

Study area

This study was conducted at the Reisler Okçular Pond, located in the Kaynarca district of Sakarya, a region where agriculture accounts for approximately 17% of the local economy. Sakarya possesses a significant irrigable potential of 930,000 decares, of which 728,080 decares are categorized as economically irrigable (Sakarya Provincial Directorate of Agriculture and Forestry18. As a result, small-to-medium scale irrigation ponds like Reisler Okçular are vital infrastructure for regional agricultural productivity.

Reisler Okçular Pond serves as a critical water resource for the surrounding agricultural activities and local biodiversity. The site-specific meteorological conditions are characterized by a semi-arid climate profile, influenced by the increasing frequency of severe drought events in the Marmara region. According to the State Meteorological Service, the region has experienced significant deviations from long-term averages, with precipitation levels dropping approximately 12.1% below historical means in recent periods9.

The specific selection of this site was based on its representative nature for the ‘water-energy-food nexus’ in Turkey, where per capita water availability is projected to decline to the ‘water stress’ threshold of 1,120 m³ by 203019. The pond’s surface area and operational depth facilitate a comparative assessment of different floating PV configurations. The primary objectives for the FPV design at this specific site are to mitigate thermal evaporation losses from the irrigation reservoir and to provide a decentralized electricity supply for the pond’s localized lighting and operational infrastructure.

System configurations

Floating photovoltaic (FPV) technology consists of a floating platform that supports photovoltaic (PV) modules, a mooring system to ensure structural stability, and an underwater transmission system that transfers the generated electricity to an onshore substation14.

Due to the early developmental stage of the technology, a wide variety of FPV system designs have emerged. However, these configurations are commonly categorized into two primary groups14:

Small Footprint (SF): In this configuration, PV modules are elevated above the water surface, allowing enhanced convective cooling while minimizing the covered water area.

Small Footprint (SF): In this configuration, PV modules are elevated above the water surface, allowing enhanced convective cooling while minimizing the covered water area.

Large Footprint (LF): In this configuration, floating structures cover a larger portion of the water surface, leading to reduced convective cooling but significantly greater evaporation suppression.

Large Footprint (LF): In this configuration, floating structures cover a larger portion of the water surface, leading to reduced convective cooling but significantly greater evaporation suppression.

Despite these well-established classifications, there is still limited understanding of how these configurations perform under identical real-world conditions, particularly in small-scale irrigation reservoirs.

In this context, the present study offers a novel contribution by modelling FPV systems specifically designed for an irrigation reservoir, with the dual objective of reducing water evaporation and generating low-carbon electricity. Furthermore, a comparative analysis is conducted between SF FPV, LF FPV, and conventional rooftop PV systems to evaluate their relative performance. Based on the findings, the most suitable FPV configuration is selected for implementation, and its performance is subsequently monitored and analyzed in real-time in comparison with an existing rooftop PV system located in the same region.

Simulation tool and models

In photovoltaic (PV) systems, the solar energy output serves as the dependent variable, while meteorological conditions, temporal factors, and module characteristics constitute the primary independent variables. The performance of these systems is typically evaluated using various simulation tools, such as PVsyst, PVsol, TRNSYS, Solar GIS, PVGIS, PVWatts, and SISIFO20, 21. Although numerous software options offer similar functionalities, they employ distinct computational methods to calculate PV variables. Understanding these diverse computational approaches and their relative accuracy compared to empirical data is essential for selecting the most appropriate tool for precise system analysis.

To achieve high-fidelity simulation results, it is imperative to account for the specific technology (e.g., monocrystalline, polycrystalline, thin-film), module configurations (e.g., bifacial, glass-glass, glass-backsheet), and installation environments (e.g., rooftop, floating, or integrated). Under Standard Test Conditions (STC) (25 °C), monocrystalline modules typically exhibit power temperature coefficients ranging from 0.003 °C− 1 and 0.005 °C− 1, while polycrystalline and amorphous modules show coefficients of approximately 0.004 °C− 1 and 0.0011 °C− 1 and 0.0026 °C−1respectively22. Despite the availability of multiple modelling tools23, specialized software dedicated to floating PV (FPV) remains limited. Consequently, adapting current simulation environments to accurately reflect water-based applications is necessary to improve the reliability of performance predictions.

Floating PV systems differ fundamentally from ground-mounted or rooftop installations, requiring specialized consideration of the following factors:

Albedo: Albedo represents the dimensionless fraction of solar energy reflected by a surface, ranging from 0 (perfect absorber) to 1 (perfect reflector)24. While land-based surfaces exhibit significant albedo variations depending on soil moisture and vegetation—ranging from 0.12 for moist soil to 0.40 for textured agricultural areas25, 26—water surfaces generally possess much lower albedo values. Consequently, the albedo effect in FPV systems is often considered negligible compared to terrestrial installations27.

However, it should be emphasized that this simplification represents an idealized scenario. In real-world FPV systems, wind speed and water temperature dynamically influence convective heat transfer and module operating temperature. The wind-dependent term (Uv), when included, typically enhances heat dissipation under higher wind conditions, while the presence of water beneath FPV systems introduces additional cooling through lower ambient temperatures and evaporative effects. Consequently, neglecting Uv may lead to deviations in absolute temperature and performance predictions under field conditions. Nevertheless, this assumption allows for isolating configuration-driven thermal differences, and thus the relative performance comparison between rooftop, SF FPV, and LF FPV systems is expected to remain consistent. Future work will incorporate dynamic wind interactions and water–air coupling mechanisms to improve model realism and predictive accuracy.

Greenhouse gas payback time

As solar PV systems do not emit greenhouse gases during operation, solar energy is recognized as a clean energy source. However, there are significant pre-operational emissions linked to the production, transportation, and installation phases. Given the rising concerns regarding climate change, evaluating the environmental and economic profiles of energy technologies is of paramount importance. The Greenhouse Gas Payback Time (GPBT) is the most prevalent metric for this purpose, representing the time required for a PV system to offset the emissions generated during its manufacturing and deployment40:

In Eq. 18, GHGE represents the embodied greenhouse gas emissions of the PV system, whereas GHG0 denotes the annual greenhouse gas savings achieved. GHGE is calculated as the sum of emissions generated throughout the production, transportation, and installation of the PV modules and the balance of system (BOS) components40:

To parameterize embodied GHG emissions for the rooftop system using mono-cSi modules manufactured in China, recent life-cycle assessment (LCA) syntheses reporting installation-specific emission intensities were adopted. Average life-cycle GHG intensities of 48.5 g CO₂eq/kWh for rooftop-mounted PV and 34.5 g CO₂eq/kWh for ground-mounted PV have been reported based on statistical analyses of qualified studies41. For floating PV (FPV), embodied GHG emissions of 137.73 kg CO₂eq/m² of installed area were estimated by Redón Santafé et al.42,.

To enable a transparent payback analysis, these intensity metrics were translated into an embodied GHG basis for the rooftop configuration. To reflect current manufacturing practices in China and maintain conservative system-level accounting, cradle-to-gate module footprints for mono PERC modules (approximately 412–517 kg CO₂eq/kWp, depending on design and grid assumptions) were harmonized with installation-type benchmarks and typical balance-of-system (BOS) contributions. Accordingly, a conservative central value of 850 kg CO₂-eq/kWp (with a sensitivity range of 750–950 kg CO₂eq/kWp) was adopted for mono-cSi systems manufactured in China43.

For FPV scenarios, system-level embodied GHG emissions were derived from the area-based factor of 137.73 kg CO₂-eq/m² reported by Redón Santafé et al.42,. This factor encompasses the PV modules, floating HDPE structures, metallic mounting frames, anchoring and mooring components, and balance-of-system (BOS) elements (cabling, connectors, junction boxes). Applying this intensity to the active system area of 124 m² yields GHGE≈ 17,079 kg CO₂-eq for both SF-FPV and LF-FPV configurations. It should be noted that Redón Santafé et al.42, characterised a specific pontoon-based FPV design; differences in flotation material, anchoring depth, or mooring length at other sites may alter the embodied intensity. Accordingly, the GPBT values reported here should be interpreted as indicative estimates rather than definitive figures, and site-specific LCA data are recommended for future deployments.

According to Table 6; Fig. 5, all three systems—SF-FPV, LF-FPV, and Rooftop PV—exhibit pronounced seasonal variability, with energy output peaking between May and August and typically reaching a maximum in June–July. This behavior is primarily driven by the seasonal variation in global solar radiation (HT), which directly determines the available irradiance input to the PV modules. The observed dip in March, followed by a sharp increase in April, reflects the transition from winter to spring conditions, where both solar altitude and day length increase rapidly, leading to a nonlinear rise in incident energy.

Earray and Egrid trends across the SF FPV, LF FPV, and Rooftop PV systems.

Throughout the year, Earray remains consistently higher than Egrid, which can be attributed to conversion and system-level losses, including inverter inefficiencies and thermal losses within the modules. Among the configurations, SF-FPV delivers the highest energy yield, followed by LF-FPV, with Rooftop PV producing slightly lower values. This difference is primarily governed by thermal behavior: floating systems benefit from enhanced convective heat transfer due to proximity to water, which reduces module operating temperature (Tcell) and improves electrical conversion efficiency.

The superiority of the SF-FPV system can be further explained by its higher effective heat loss coefficient (Uc), which enhances heat dissipation and reduces temperature-induced efficiency losses. As PV efficiency decreases with increasing temperature, even moderate reductions in Tcell can lead to measurable gains in energy output. Therefore, the observed performance hierarchy (SF > LF > Rooftop) is a direct consequence of improved thermal regulation rather than differences in solar resource availability.

Comparative simulations isolating the thermal loss coefficient (Uc) confirm the thermal advantage of floating systems. Increasing Uc from 29 W·m⁻²·K⁻¹ (Rooftop PV) to 46 W·m⁻²·K⁻¹ (SF-FPV) results in approximately a 1.7% increase in annual Egrid. This improvement is physically explained by enhanced convective cooling, which lowers the module operating temperature and mitigates temperature-induced efficiency losses.

Correspondingly, the annual performance ratio (PR) increases from 0.892 (Rooftop) to 0.908 (SF-FPV), representing an approximate 1.8% relative improvement (Fig. 6). The seasonal PR decline observed during high-irradiance summer months is mainly due to elevated module temperatures; however, SF-FPV exhibits a smaller reduction, indicating stronger thermal resilience. This confirms that systems with higher Uc values are less sensitive to thermal stress, maintaining more stable performance under peak solar conditions.

Overall, small-footprint FPV emerges as the most efficient configuration for hot, high-irradiance regions, balancing superior energy yield with robust thermal stability. Although LF-FPV shows slightly lower electrical performance than SF-FPV, it confers environmental co-benefits by reducing surface water evaporation. Notably, the modeled gains reflect controlled assumptions—holding wind-dependent transfer (Uv), albedo, and soiling constant, and using configuration-averaged Uc values that can vary with wind, water temperature, and humidity. Hence, the reported figures should be interpreted as theoretical benchmarks isolating thermal effects rather than precise operational forecasts.

Environmental impact

The model presented in Eq. 17, which estimates evaporation under conditions with and without floating PV, indicates that the large-footprint FPV configuration yields the greatest reduction in evaporation. Figure 6 shows the monthly average daily evaporation rates for three cases: no PV, small-footprint FPV (SF), and large-footprint FPV (LF). The no-PV case exhibits the highest evaporation due to full exposure to solar radiation, while the SF configuration achieves a moderate reduction through partial surface coverage. In contrast, the LF configuration results in the lowest evaporation rates, as its extensive coverage provides greater shading and substantially limits water loss. These differences become more pronounced during high-evaporation periods, with the LF case consistently delivering the largest reduction relative to the no-PV baseline.

Figure 7; Table 8 detail the evaporation rates for large-footprint floating photovoltaic (LF-FPV) systems under varying coverage ratios across the year. The data reveal a clear seasonal pattern: evaporation peaks during the summer months (June–August) due to elevated temperatures and solar radiation, while it reaches minimum levels in winter (December–February). The results indicate that LF-FPV systems significantly mitigate evaporation compared to open water surfaces; this effect becomes more pronounced as the coverage area expands, particularly for larger-scale configurations. The observed reduction in evaporation with increasing FPV coverage is primarily driven by the combined effects of solar shading and reduced aerodynamic exchange at the water surface. The floating structures limit direct solar radiation reaching the water, thereby decreasing surface temperature and suppressing evaporation rates. Simultaneously, partial coverage disrupts wind flow across the water surface, reducing turbulent heat and mass transfer, which further contributes to evaporation suppression. These coupled mechanisms explain why evaporation reduction becomes more pronounced at higher coverage ratios, particularly under high-temperature summer conditions.

Greenhouse payback time

Using Eqs. (18, 19, 20), embodied GHG emissions () and annual GHG savings () were calculated for each system. An active area of 124 m² was assumed for all configurations. For floating PV, an embodied intensity of 137.73 kg CO₂-eq/m² was adopted, yielding kg CO₂-eq for both SF-FPV and LF-FPV systems.

For the rooftop PV system (PROWATT 590 Wp N-type TOPCon; cells manufactured in China; module assembly and BOS in Türkiye), a system-level embodied factor of 850 kg CO₂-eq/kWp—consistent with recent IEA PVPS Task 12, Fraunhofer ISE, NREL, and JRC analyses—was used, resulting in kg CO₂-eq. A sensitivity range of 750–950 kg CO₂-eq/kWp corresponds to 21,240–26,904 kg CO₂-eq.

Annual GHG savings were derived from the measured annual delivered energy () reported in Table 6, combined with Türkiye’s electricity emission factor ( g CO₂-eq/kWh) and transmission/distribution losses (), yielding an effective factor of 0.555632 kg CO₂-eq/kWh.

The resulting GPBT values (Eq. 18) are 0.80 years (≈ 9.6 months) for SF-FPV, 0.81 years (≈ 9.8 months) for LF-FPV, and 1.15 years (≈ 13.8 months) for the rooftop system. For the rooftop case, GPBT varies between 1.02 and 1.29 years when the embodied factor range of 750–950 kg CO₂-eq/kWp is considered. Overall, sub-1-year GPBT values are obtained for both FPV configurations, while the rooftop TOPCon system achieves a GPBT of approximately 1.15 years, indicating that embodied emissions are offset within the early years of operation under Türkiye’s grid conditions.

The calculated Carbon Payback Time (GPBT) of less than 1.2 years is consistent with recent life cycle assessments (LCA) for high-efficiency silicon PV systems in high-irradiance zones. When compared to recent LCA benchmarks for high-efficiency c-Si PV, these findings indicate that both SF and LF FPV configurations function as rapid carbon-offsetting assets, despite the additional material requirements associated with floating structures. The shorter GPBT observed for FPV systems compared to rooftop PV is primarily attributable to their higher annual energy yield, which accelerates the offset of embodied emissions.

Conclusion and future work

Integrated water–energy optimization framework

The selection between Small-Footprint (SF) and Large-Footprint (LF) configurations involves a direct trade-off between electrical efficiency and hydrologic conservation. Based on our results, SF FPV provides an approximate 1.7% increase in annual energy yield compared to LF systems due to superior convective cooling ( vs. ). Conversely, LF FPV at 80% coverage saves an additional 149,887 /year of water compared to SF FPV at the same coverage ratio.

Optimization of design parameters such as tilt angle, inter-row spacing, and inverter sizing can further differentiate the performance of FPV and rooftop systems. For instance, lower tilt angles in FPV systems can reduce wind loads on floating structures while potential yields are maintained through enhanced convective cooling, whereas rooftop designs are often constrained by structural orientations and shading from surrounding buildings.

To guide this choice, we propose a qualitative, target-based decision matrix informed by local water-scarcity conditions (referred to here as the Local Scarcity context). This matrix does not constitute a formal mathematical optimization index; rather, it provides scenario-based guidance for selecting the appropriate FPV configuration:

Energy-Priority Scenario: In regions where grid stability or carbon offset speed is the primary constraint, SF FPV is the preferred configuration, achieving a GPBT of approximately 0.80 years.

Energy-Priority Scenario: In regions where grid stability or carbon offset speed is the primary constraint, SF FPV is the preferred configuration, achieving a GPBT of approximately 0.80 years.

Water-Priority Scenario: In arid regions or for dedicated irrigation reservoirs during drought-prone years, LF FPV is the preferred design. The 1.7% reduction in energy yield is substantially offset by the 14.47% increase in total reservoir storage retention.

This trade-off can be simplified by a target-based approach: if the objective is to secure irrigation for more than 100 hectares, LF FPV becomes the strongly favored configuration despite the slight reduction in electrical Performance Ratio (PR). Furthermore, the integration of energy storage systems (ESS) or grid-interactive demand-side management could enhance the reliability of large-scale FPV deployments, particularly in off-grid or weak-grid agricultural regions.

Building upon these findings, the comparative analysis revealed that the SF FPV configuration outperforms the LF FPV and rooftop systems in terms of electrical performance and carbon payback time. While LF FPV systems offer superior evaporation reduction, the overall techno-environmental balance favors the SF FPV configuration. Therefore, the SF FPV system was selected and implemented in this study as the most suitable configuration under the given site conditions (Fig. 8).

Socio-economic implications and ecological considerations

Beyond technical performance, the socio-economic implications of FPV systems are substantial. The ability to preserve sufficient water to support the irrigation of approximately 148 hectares of cropland annually represents a direct contribution to regional food security and enhances the resilience of rural livelihoods against extreme drought conditions. In addition, from a public health perspective, maintaining reservoir water levels contributes to improved water quality stability by reducing the relative concentration of pollutants and ensuring reliable access to water resources for community use.

However, high coverage ratios (e.g., 80%) also introduce ecological complexities. Potential impacts such as light attenuation and restricted gas exchange must be considered, particularly in sensitive ecosystems where dissolved oxygen (DO) levels are critical. In such cases, adopting partial coverage configurations or specific “ecological corridors” within the FPV array is recommended to maintain aquatic health.

Methodological limitations and future research

It should be noted that the optimization framework and sensitivity analysis presented in this study are based on a one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach, which allows clear identification of individual parameter effects such as temperature and heat transfer coefficients. However, in real-world applications, these parameters may interact in a coupled and non-linear manner. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of multi-parameter interactions—such as the coupling between irradiance, temperature, and CAPEX—in shaping system performance and uncertainty ranges.

While the present study leverages high-resolution regional data and confirms results through cross-case consistency, certain modeling simplifications were necessary. To support the evaporation modeling, regional climatic and hydrological parameters were adopted from recorded measurements for a representative agricultural reservoir located within the same province in the Marmara Region of Türkiye. These data, indicating an average evaporation rate of approximately 837.9 mm/year and a relative humidity of 72%47, served as a reference baseline to characterize local conditions. The calculated open-water evaporation values ($513,268 m^3/year$) are consistent with findings reported for comparable irrigation reservoirs in the region, such as the Turgutlu Pond case study47. This agreement supports the reliability of the applied modeling approach, despite the simplified assumptions inherent in the initial model structure.

Although this work provides a practical comparison of three PV configurations, the methodology can be extended in future studies by introducing more complex micro-climatic and system-level parameters. Future iterations should focus on developing sophisticated micro-climate models—such as the ‘moisture-tunnel’ concept—to describe the thin air layer between the water surface and PV modules where humidity and airflow differ from ambient conditions. Furthermore, incorporating ‘wind-sheltering coefficients’ to account for local topography and vegetation would further refine the predictive accuracy of convective heat transfer and evaporation rates.

Beyond thermal dynamics, future work should incorporate ecological feedback mechanisms, particularly light attenuation coefficients and gas exchange limitations, to quantify the long-term impact of FPV shading on dissolved oxygen levels and aquatic thermal stratification. From a system-level perspective, the inclusion of degradation-related factors—such as biofouling on floating structures or moisture-induced performance losses—and integration constraints, including storage coupling and grid interaction stability, would enable a more comprehensive assessment. While the incorporation of such spatial and systemic parameters would enhance the physical realism of the model, their detailed formulation requires dedicated experimental datasets. Therefore, future work will focus on in-situ experimental validation and the development of these extended, multi-variable sensitivity and uncertainty analysis frameworks.

Conclusion

This study investigated a comprehensive comparative analysis of three photovoltaic (PV) system configurations—rooftop, small-footprint (SF) floating, and large-footprint (LF) floating—under real-time operating conditions at an irrigation pond in Sakarya, Türkiye. The systems were evaluated using a commercial PV simulation tool, calibrated with in-situ measured data, to assess annual energy yields, greenhouse gas payback times (GPBT), and volumetric water conservation benefits.

The results reveal distinct performance characteristics among the configurations. The SF-FPV configuration achieved the highest annual grid-delivered energy (Egrid = 37,320.0 kWh) and the most favorable GPBT (0.80 years), indicating remarkably rapid mitigation of embedded greenhouse gas emissions. While the LF-FPV system exhibited a slightly longer GPBT (0.81 years), it provided substantial water conservation benefits, the most pronounced among all scenarios due to its extensive surface coverage. In comparison, the rooftop PV system (28.32 kWp) yielded a GPBT of approximately 1.15 years, with embodied GHG values consistent with modern manufacturing standards.

A critical contribution of this work is the first-time quantification of volumetric water savings within this comparative framework. Open-water evaporation from the pond (374,600 m² surface area; 2.045 million m³ capacity) totals 513,268 m³/year, accounting for 25.1% of the total storage. Deployment of LF-FPV systems at 80% coverage reduces evaporation to 217,349 m³/year, effectively saving 295,919 m³/year (14.47% of storage capacity)—volume sufficient to irrigate approximately 148 hectares of cropland annually. Under varying coverage ratios, SF-FPV systems saved between 45,635 and 146,032 m³/year (2.23–7.14% of capacity). These reductions are most significant during summer months, specifically in July, when daily evaporation decreases from 5.422 to 2.612 mm/day under 80% LF-FPV coverage, precisely when water stress is most acute.

These findings underscore the dual environmental advantages of FPV systems—emission mitigation and water preservation—positioning them as vital solutions for regions facing simultaneous energy and water scarcity. Given Türkiye’s semi-arid climate, LF-FPV systems emerge as a strategic tool for integrated water-energy management, enabling reservoir operators to harmonize renewable energy generation with rigorous water conservation objectives.

In conclusion, this study indicates that floating PV systems represent a promising alternative to land-based PV from energy-yield and environmental perspectives, combining rapid carbon payback with high energy efficiency and ecosystem preservation. While SF-FPV systems are optimal for maximizing energy and carbon performance, LF-FPV systems maximize water storage stability, offering complementary pathways for sustainable development in water-limited environments. While this study focuses on a specific reservoir in Türkiye, the proposed multi-dimensional evaluation framework is highly scalable. By adjusting climatic and hydrological inputs, the findings and the underlying methodology can be adapted to other Mediterranean and semi-arid regions, providing a standardized approach for regional FPV potential assessments.
