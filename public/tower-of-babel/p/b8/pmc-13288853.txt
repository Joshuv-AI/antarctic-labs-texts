Understanding contemporary challenges to safe abortion access in Africa through a health systems lens: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Caesar Agula, Edmund Wedam Kanmiki, Charlotte Abra Esime Ofori, Oladimeji Ogunoye, Kathryn Church, Ramatou Ouedraogo, Ayaga A Bawah

Abstract AbstractBackgroundUnsafe abortion remains a major public health concern in Africa, contributing substantially to maternal morbidity and mortality across the continent. Although legal restrictions, stigma and socio-cultural factors are well-documented barriers to safe abortion access, health systems-related barriers are not adequately explored. This systematic review aims to examine how health systems-related barriers hinder access to safe abortion in clinic settings in Africa over the past decade.Methods and analysisA comprehensive search strategy will be employed to retrieve relevant studies from electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science and African Journals Online. Background

Although access to and use of contraceptives have increased over time, unintended pregnancies remain relatively high in most low- and middle-income countries, including Africa.1 Individuals with unintended pregnancies who do not wish to continue their pregnancies may choose to have an abortion.1 Approximately 73 million abortions occur each year globally, with 45% being unsafe,-either performed using non-recommended methods or by an untrained provider or both.2 Most of the unsafe terminations are performed in Africa, contributing substantially to maternal morbidities and fatalities on the continent.2 3 Estimates from the Guttmacher Institute indicate that 8.3 million of the 11.1 million abortions that occur in Africa are not safe.4 Notably, about 15 000 women in Africa die annually due to complications from unsafe abortions, and these fatalities contribute 7% of the maternal deaths on the continent.4

Legal restrictions and stigma are notable for significantly impeding access to safe abortion services in African countries.5,7 In most parts of the continent, terminating pregnancies is either prohibited or permitted only on the grounds of preserving a woman’s health or life.8 9 As of 2019, about 90% of countries in the sub-Saharan region alone had highly to moderately restrictive laws for abortion.5 For example, while in Ghana and Kenya abortion is only permitted on the grounds of saving women’s health, other countries such as Sudan, South Sudan, Uganda, Gabon and Congo (Brazzaville) have much stricter laws.9 Additionally, the sociocultural and religious stigma associated with abortion further hinders access to safe abortion services in most African countries.10 11 Stigma operates at multiple levels (individual, community and institutional) across the continent, affecting abortion service provision, access and quality.10 11

Beyond these well-known constraints, health system challenges continue to hinder access to essential healthcare services, including high-quality abortion services.12 13 In particular, barriers such as the limited availability of abortion services in some communities, weak referral systems, shortages of trained providers, high service costs, provider conscientious objection and insufficient health information frequently restrict women’s access to safe and quality abortion services.13,17

A growing body of literature has examined abortion access in Africa.518,20 However, there is little attention on how health system-related factors shape abortion service accessibility, availability and quality. The landscape of abortion access in Africa has changed considerably over the past decade, with the increasing availability of medication abortion (MA) pills, which can be obtained in some private pharmacies even in contexts where pharmacists/pharmacy providers are not legally sanctioned to offer abortion services.14 21 Women’s preference for pharmacies over clinics may be driven by health system-related factors or structural barriers, highlighting the significance of this study.22

This review seeks to systematically synthesise recent literature on health system-related barriers to safe abortion access in clinic settings in Africa. Specifically, the review will examine how challenges linked to the core health system building blocks affect abortion access. Understanding these health system dynamics is crucial for informing the programming of initiatives and strategies aimed at strengthening African health systems for improving access and quality of abortion services.

Methodology

Protocol registration and reporting

This protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database under registration number CRD420251156948. It was designed in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) (see online supplemental additional file 1 for details).23 Findings of the completed review will be reported using the updated guidelines of the PRISMA.24

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility of studies will be assessed using the Population-Exposure-Outcome (PEO) framework and other criteria, as this review focuses on health system-related barriers to safe abortion access in clinics (eg, hospitals, clinics and polyclinics) in all African countries. The details of studies that will be included and excluded from this review are provided below.

Population

This review will include all empirical studies and reports from international organisations that examined health system-related challenges affecting women and girls seeking abortion in clinics (either public or private) in Africa. Additionally, studies focusing on health providers and other stakeholders will be included if they address health system-related barriers to abortion service delivery. However, studies conducted outside the 54 countries in Africa, as well as those that do not address health system-related challenges in clinics, will be excluded. Additionally, studies solely on postabortion care will not be considered.

Exposure

Only studies highlighting barriers to accessing safe abortion in clinics, which are linked to the WHO health system building blocks, will be considered. These health system blocks are service delivery, health workforce, financing, governance and leadership, health information systems and medical products and technologies.25 Studies on abortion barriers not related to any of the health system blocks will not be considered. Additionally, studies focusing exclusively on contraception, maternal health or general sexual and reproductive health and rights challenges without addressing abortion access will be excluded.

Outcome

The primary outcome of interest is access to safe abortion. Abortion studies reporting on health system-related barriers that influence this outcome, such as service availability, affordability, provider capacity, governance or health information, will be included. However, those that do not report on the stated outcome of interest will be excluded.

Timing

To capture contemporary evidence, only studies published from 2015 to 2025 will be included. Studies outside this period will not be considered.

Language

Only studies published in the English language will be considered while excluding those in other languages.

Study design

This review will adopt a comprehensive approach, encompassing a diverse range of study designs to ensure a thorough synthesis of the evidence. Both qualitative and quantitative studies will be considered as well as mixed-methods research. Specifically, the search will include empirical studies with designs such as cross-sectional, longitudinal, experimental and exploratory research. However, non-empirical studies such as literature reviews, commentaries, perspectives, editorials or letters to the editor will be excluded. This approach will allow us to map the full breadth of available empirical evidence on health systems barriers to safe abortion care.

Information sources

The main source of information will be electronic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest and African Journals Online. Grey literature, such as fact sheets and reports from international organisations such as the Guttmacher Institute and WHO, will also be considered.

Search strategy

A systematic search of the electronic databases will be conducted to retrieve all relevant citations using keywords and synonyms related to abortion and health system-related barriers/challenges to safe abortion in Africa. To ensure a thorough search, we will combine these terms using Boolean operators “AND” and incorporate appropriate MeSH terms where relevant to maximise the retrieval of relevant literature. Online supplemental additional file 2 provides a draft of the search and MeSH terms, organised using the PEO framework, and how the terms will be used in PubMed.

Selection process

To select eligible studies for analysis, a three-stage approach will be followed. At the first stage, all retrieved citations from the search strategies will be imported into Covidence for duplicate removal. At the second stage, the de-duplicated titles and abstracts will subsequently be screened by independent reviewers (CA, EWK, CAEO and OO) based on the study’s eligibility criteria. At the final stage, all studies that passed the title and abstract screening will then undergo a full-text assessment by independent reviewers based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies at all stages of the screening will be mainly resolved through discussion among the research team to build a consensus. If necessary, RO or AAB (health systems experts) will act as tiebreakers. The selection process will be shown by the PRISMA 2020 flow chart (see online supplemental additional file 3 for the flow chart).24

Quality assessment

The 2018 version of the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) will be employed to assess the quality of each included study.26 The MMAT is designed to enable critical appraisal of diverse study designs (including quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods, randomised controlled and non-randomised), making it suitable for reviews addressing complex topics such as health systems.26

Each study will be evaluated independently by two reviewers (CA and EWK) using the five main domains of MMAT: (1) Clarity of the research question, (2) Appropriateness of the design employed by the study, (3) Relevance of the sampling strategy and data sources, (4) Quality of measurements or data analysis and (5) Coherence and validity of interpretations or conclusions.

Each indicator will be rated as “Yes”, “No” or “Can’t tell”. The proportion of indicators met will be calculated to generate an overall quality score for descriptive purposes. No study will be excluded solely based on quality; instead, the appraisal results will be used to guide the interpretation of findings and highlight the strength of the evidence base.

Data synthesis and analysis

Selected studies will be analysed separately under two levels of grouping. First, studies will be grouped based on the legal conditions for ending pregnancy in the country/countries of focus. Specifically, studies will be categorised under the following legal conditions for abortion: (1) Permitted based on request, (2) Permitted based on broad social or economic grounds, (3) Permitted to preserve health, (4) Permitted to save a person’s life and (5) Prohibited altogether.9 Studies with a scope that encompasses different countries and varied legal conditions will be captured in all applicable groups. Second, studies in each of the initial groups will be structured based on the WHO health system building blocks. This approach is employed to comprehensively understand the diversity of health systems challenges across different legal contexts for abortion on the continent.

A narrative method will be used to synthesise multiple studies, focusing on summarising text and critically interpreting the text.27 This method is appropriate since it enables interpretative synthesis of multiple studies with different designs.27

Ethics and dissemination

This work does not require ethical approval, as it involves analysis of data from published literature and does not include any primary data collection. The results for the main review will be submitted for publication in a journal. In addition, the African Coalition for Research and Communication on Abortion (ACORCA) will disseminate the findings.

Discussion

Unsafe abortions substantially contribute to maternal morbidities and fatalities in Africa, with about 15 000 women on the continent dying each year from unsafe abortion complications.4 Restrictive legal conditions and stigma are well-known to hinder access to safe abortion in most parts of the continent.5 7 However, health system-related challenges significantly impede access to safe and high-quality abortion services in clinic settings in some African countries.14 22 The landscape for abortion access has also evolved on the continent, with many women opting to self-manage abortions with MA pills from pharmacies and likely not to benefit from comprehensive abortion care.20 28 The reasons for this shift in preference to access abortion in pharmacies over clinics may include health system-related challenges in clinics, underscoring the importance of this study.

This review seeks to fill a critical gap in the abortion literature by synthesising contemporary studies that examined how barriers linked to the WHO’s health system building blocks affect access to safe abortion in clinic settings in Africa. Findings from this review could enhance understanding of the complex health system dynamics on abortion provision and access in Africa to inform targeted initiatives and strategies to strengthen the continent’s health systems for improving access to safe abortion services.
