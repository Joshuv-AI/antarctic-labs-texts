Vaccines as a psychological shield: the role of vaccine acceptance in mitigating COVID-19 obsession, burnout, and death anxiety among medical personnel
Authors: Preeda Sansakorn, Iqra Mushtaque, Muhammad Awais-E-Yazdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthcare workers experienced psychological distress during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in the form of intrusive thoughts and heightened death anxiety. Vaccination is expected not only reduce infection risk but also reduce psychological burden. The aim of the present study was to examine the relationship among COVID-19 obsessions, emotional exhaustion, and death anxiety and examined whether vaccine acceptance moderates these association among medical staff. Cross-sectional designs were adopted in this study, and data was collected from 200 members of the medical staff (doctors, nurses, and paramedical personnel). All the study’s participants were vaccinated at the time of data collection. PLS-SEM was used to analyze the data. The sample had a high exposure of COVID-19, with 63% previously infected, and female staff reported high level of obsession, emotional exhaustion and death anxiety. Vaccine acceptance was high across the sample (86%). Moreover, hypothesis testing results showed that COVID-19 obsession was found to be associated with death anxiety (β = 0.271; t = 5.120; p = 0.000). Vaccine acceptance also influences the relationship between COVID-19 obsession and death anxiety (β= -0.263; t = 4.393; p = 0.000). Vaccine acceptance is a psychosocial protective factor for frontline healthcare professionals by diminishing their experiences of obsessive thoughts and fear of dying related to COVID-19. Enhancing vaccination campaigns, while incorporating psychological support into workplace health programs, is likely to improve the psychological health and resilience of frontline healthcare workers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14338-z.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has been described as the worst national crisis in recent history, with medical staff positioned on the frontlines of the fight against the virus. Due to its high transmission rates and prevalence of severe illness and death, COVID-19 has placed a unprecedented pressure on healthcare systems [1]. In addition to causing considerable physiological harm, infectious diseases also have a negative impact on mental health. Working in COVID-19 isolation wards and providing continuous care to infected patients placed medical staff at heightened risk, and the rising incidence of infections and deaths among healthcare personnel became a major concern [2].

Healthcare practitioners are among the most vulnerable group not only due to their direct exposure to infected individuals, but also because many witness their peers suffer severe complications or even lose their lives due to virus [3]. The COVID-19 related death rate among healthcare workers was reported to be 1.5% [4]. It is widely acknowledged that the pandemic has had a detrimental effect on the mental health and overall well-being of medical staff. Multiple reports indicate that healthcare workers experienced elevated level of psychological burden compared to others occupational groups, and the medical staff has been reported to face the highest rate of burnout syndrome in recent years [5, 6]. The rapid rise in COVID-19 cases and deaths, shortage of human and material, resources [7] limited availability effective therapies, and an increase in job demands have collectively contributed to emotional exhaustion and mental health deterioration among medical staff [8, 9].

Though there are many concerns surrounding the effects of the pandemic on healthcare workers [10], little research has been done to better understand the specific mechanisms of how healthcare workers experience the psychological effects of COVID-19 (i.e. through obsessive thoughts, fear of contamination and death anxiety). The psychological responses to the pandemic are important from a clinical perspective as they may lead to increased obsessive-compulsive tendencies, increased levels of perceived threat, and increased levels of emotional exhaustion and burnout.

Researchers and pharmaceutical companies have collaborated to develop safe and effective vaccines to help and stop the spread of COVID-19 and prevent further loss of life. Vaccination is considered one of the most effective approaches to controlling infectious diseases [11]. For decades, vaccines have been regarded as a corner stone in combating rapidly spreading infectious illnesses. In this context, healthcare workers play a critical role as their acceptance or hesitancy towards COVID-19 vaccines can slightly influence public’s adoption of COVID-19 vaccines, as they are a trusted source of health information. Several vaccines have been approved for global use, including Sinovac and Sinopharm, Pfizer, Moderna, Janssen, Oxford/AstraZeneca, and Sputnik V [12, 13, 14]. Due to an insufficient supply of COVID-19 vaccinations internationally, governments have prioritized vaccine distribution to high-risk groups such as frontline healthcare workers. In Pakistan front-line healthcare workers and elderly citizens were among the first to receive Sinopharm doses, donated by China [15].

Not only might these pandemic-related psychological factors impact healthcare workers’ willingness to get vaccinated, but they may also promote their reluctance to do so. The examination of psychological influences resulting from the pandemic is critical to understanding healthcare worker overall health and behavior in high-risk environments, as well as the mental health consequences of such factors.

The need to examine COVID-19 related obsessions, fears of contamination and anxiety about death have impacted the amount of emotional exhaustion and behavior of Pakistani healthcare professionals has become critical because of these gaps in knowledge. The findings from this study will be essential for developing clinical, psychological, and organizational solutions to support the resilience of healthcare professionals and improve the effectiveness of national public health strategies.

Therefore, the purpose of the study is to examine the association among COVID-19 obsession, emotional exhaustion, and death anxiety with the moderating role of vaccination acceptance in Pakistani medical professionals working in COVID-19 isolation wards (Fig. 1).

Research gap

Despite the increasing amount of data available about COVID-19 obsession, death anxiety, emotional fatigue, and behavior (like, for instance, acceptance of vaccines), little research has been conducted concerning the relationship between these four areas within the context of lower-income and middle-income countries. Most prior research has concentrated only on one psychological variable or outcome at a time; consequently, we know very little about how these various mental health issues interact with each other to shape healthcare workers’ mental health statuses and their patterns of pro-social behavior towards protection during the pandemic period. Also, research carried out in Pakistan is limited and due to Pakistan having high rates of infection among health care workers and its unique health care system challenges, there is a lot of need for extensive research on pandemic related obsessed cognitions and fear of dying.

Literature review

COVID-19 obsession

The COVID-19 pandemic has created a substantial global psychological crisis, and healthcare providers have been identified as one of the most at-risk groups due to their continued exposure to infacted individuals. Historically, infectious disease outbreaks have increased the distress, anxiety and irrational thought processes of those affected [16]. Consequently, when the COVID-19 outbreak occurred, healthcare providers had to implement social-distancing measures, contend with large numbers of deaths and endure ongoing uncertainty. Thus, healthcare providers were particularly prone to developing anxiety-related conditions.

The American Psychiatric Association defines obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) as a persistent mental disorder characterized by intrusive, unwelcome, time-consuming, painful thoughts, images, or desires. People with compulsions engage in repetitive mental or behavioral actions to alleviate distress. Studies have found that repeated exposure to health alerts (i.e., constant health warnings), the usage of media that heightens anxiety, and having prolonged exposure to a contaminated patient are common triggers for developing obsessive compulsive behaviors due to contamination anxiety [17, 18]. Some of the behaviors associated with obsessive-compulsive behaviors due to contamination anxiety include excessive hand hygiene, excessive bathing, social distancing, and performing ritualistic sanitization practices [5, 19, 20]. This obsession primarily consists of dysfunctional thoughts and behaviors that develop from fear of the Coronavirus (COVID-19) [21]. Medical Staff reported a higher prevalence of obsessive-compulsive symptoms associated with contamination fears and excessive cleaning rituals than the general population during the COVID-19 pandemic [22]. Another study revealed that COVID-19 obsessions that persist in medical staff have a negative impact on their mental health [23]. Medical employees had a higher prevalence of sleeplessness, anxiety, depression, somatization, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms than non-medical workers. Similarly, psychological distress was shown to be greater in nurses and technicians than in physicians [6]. These obsessive thoughts may negatively affect one’s ability to perform his/her daily activities, make choices, or have a healthy emotional state, indicating the importance of understanding the clinical issues concerning OCD behaviors amongst health care professionals.

Emotional exhaustion and death anxiety

In the current pandemic context, death anxiety may possibly be a major motivating factor in human activity [24]. Another study revealed that 5% of medical staff suffer from severe death anxiety [25]. Furthermore, many participants who are working in COVID-19 isolation wards reported having a greater fear of death than the general population, and nurses reported having a greater fear of death than physicians [26]. COVID-19 pandemic, emerging studies have indicated that health professionals experienced distress, fear, clinical symptoms of anxiety, and burnout (emotional exhaustion) [27–30]. Enea et al. [26] found that COVID-19 obsession altered the relationship between death anxiety and emotional exhaustion. Since the COVID-19’arrival, several of the health care professionals got sick or died left them to consider their mortality [9, 31]. As suggested by TMT, when mortality becomes more important (i.e., when people are aware that they will die), the way they feel emotionally, think cognitively, and deal with life (i.e. resort to avoidance) is altered. In other words, when someone is faced with the reality of impending death, they are more likely to cope with the stressors of a pandemic through “death anxiety.”

Vaccine acceptance

The research focus on vaccine acceptance in healthcare workers has become an important topic considering their position as trusted sources of health information. In the early periods of vaccine distribution, there were great differences in COVID-19 vaccine acceptance between countries. For example, in the U.S. the acceptance level was 36% [32], while acceptance rates were 30% in Saudi Arabia [33], and 27.7% in the Democratic Republic of the Congo [34]. Initial respondents are medical staffs who were involved in the first phase of vaccination distribution [35], with rates as high as 70% in some countries [36]. High-risk groups include health care professionals, older people, particularly those with chronic co-morbid conditions, and those who provide critical services [37]. Fully immunizing over 82% of the population will be crucial to achieve the herd immunity for controlling virus transmission and restricting the pandemic. This will take major public acceptance, and very low levels of hesitancy [38]. The World Health Organization states that because of the pandemic, life-saving routine vaccinations have been delayed, and vaccine hesitancy is a global concern.

Hypothesis

H1

COVID-19 obsession has a positive association with death anxiety among medical staff.

H2

Emotional exhaustion has a positive association with death anxiety among medical staff.

H3

Vaccine acceptance negatively moderates the relationship between COVID-19 obsession and death anxiety among medical staff.

H4

Acceptance of vaccines has a negative effect on the link between emotional exhaustion and death anxiety among medical staff.

Methodology

Research design

The cross-sectional research design was used to investigate the relationship between COVID-19-related obsessions, emotional exhaustion, and death anxiety among medical staff, as well as the moderating role of vaccine acceptance. The survey sampled medical workers, including physicians, nurses, and paramedical staff, who performed their jobs in the COVID-19 wards. The participants were recruited for the study by using a purposive sampling technique. The inclusion criteria of the study were all medical staff doctors, nurses, and paramedical staff who performed their duties in isolation wards with an age range of 20 to 60 years. Participants who have any psychological illness (severe anxiety or depression) or disease like hypertension or cardiovascular disease were excluded.

Using this information, the total number of participants needed was approximately N = 150. However, out of the 300 questionnaires distributed, of which 200 valid responses were analyzed. The response rate was 66.6%.

Procedure

The data was gathered from different hospitals in Punjab, Pakistan; Nishtar Hospital Multan, Mayo Hospital Lahore, Combined Military Hospital Okara, and Combined Military Hospital Lahore between June 15th and December 15th, 2021. Permission was obtained from the hospital’s higher authorities. Pakistan is presently immunizing front-line medical workers and patients with Sinopharm’s suggested dosages. All medical workers in Pakistan are required by the government to have the COVID-19 vaccination. All voluntary participated medical staff complete an informed consent form, a socio-demographic information sheet, and self-reported questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

The partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was conducted using SmartPLS 3.2.6 for the analysis of the direct and moderating relationships within the model, as PLS-SEM was determined as being appropriate for use with complex models, medium size sample datasets and non-normality of data. The analysis was conducted using the standard two-step process: the measurement model was evaluated for reliability and validity, and then the structural paths were estimated via bootstrapping (5,000 replications). Missing values (< 3%) were addressed using the mean substitution method, and the presence of multicollinearity was identified using variance inflation factors (VIF). Quality of the model was evaluated through both effect sizes and R² values, and moderation was assessed using interaction terms that were created in SmartPLS.

Background information of the study participant (N = 200)

Table 1 summarizes the respondent’s demographic characteristics. 68% of the 200 responses were female, while 32% were male. The data were taken from medical staff, of which 40% were physicians, 46% were nurses, and 13% were paramedical personnel who conducted their tasks with COVID-19 patients. Each respondent carried out their responsibilities in the COVID-19 isolation wards. in which 63% of medical staff were contaminated by COVID-19. Furthermore, female medical staff has the highest COVID-19 obsession compared to male staff (66.2% vs. 37.5%). Similarly, female medical staff suffers from emotional exhaustion as compared to male staff (74.2% vs. 70.3%). On the scale of death anxiety, females have a higher score than males (47.8% vs. 23.4%). Male and female respondents have the same level of vaccine acceptance (86.9% vs. 86.7%).

Measurement model

The model was evaluated in two parts: convergent validity and discriminant validity. The first step in the measurement model (Fig. 2) is to determine its convergent validity [43, 44]. Hair et al. [43] suggest the minimum factor loading, particularly for a construct, is 0.30, which required removing any items from the original sample that were less than this loading. To establish adequate convergent validity, AVE value should have been equal to or greater than 0.50 [45, 46, 47]. The convergent validity of this study is affirmed with all the AVE values in the presidential range, as presented in Table 2.

The next step in the evaluation of the measurement model is to assess discriminant validity, which refers to the degree of separation among constructs, empirically indicating the degree to which constructs differ [43]. Table 3, demonstrates that discriminant validity is achieved, as all diagonal values are higher than the off-diagonal correlations among other constructs in their rows and columns. Discriminant validity was also assessed through indicators cross-loadings [48], Table 4, the cross-loading matrix indicated that all indicators loaded higher on their respective constructs than they did on the other constructs. Table 5, indicates that all the HTMT values for the pairs of constructs were below the thresholds. The composite reliability (CR) [43] values for all the constructs were above the recommended threshold of 0.70 as well as all the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were above the recommended threshold of 0.50. Overall, the analysis of the factor loadings showed all the indicator loadings were above 0.50 (to as much as 0.900).

Structural model

The analytical path was taken to the structural model (inner model), which included the hypothesized relationships between latent constructs. To statistically test these relationships, a bootstrapping procedure (n = 5,000 subsamples) was performed. The structural model that was estimated is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Bootstrapping was employed to evaluate the structural model and to summarize the hypothesized relationships (Fig. 3; Table 6). There was a strong positive correlation observed between covid-19 obsession and death anxiety (β = 0.271, t = 5.120, p < 0.001) supporting H1. However, emotional exhaustion did not significantly predict death anxiety (H2) (β= -0.005, t = 0.097, p = 0.923), suggesting that emotional strain alone does not lead to increased fear of death among healthcare workers in this sample. Moreover, the results showed that vaccine acceptance moderated the relationship between COVID-19 obsession and death anxiety (β = -0.263, t = 4.393, p < 0.001), thus supporting H3. This suggests that people who have higher levels of vaccine acceptance are likely to have lower levels of death anxiety if they have obsessive thoughts about COVID-19. Conversely, there was no evidence that vaccine acceptance moderated the relationship between emotional exhaustion and death anxiety (β = -0.018, t = 0.293, p = 0.769), therefore providing no support for H4. The structural model showed that cognitive responses toward the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., obsessive thinking) significantly contributed to participants’ levels of anxiety surrounding death, whereas emotional exhaustion was less predictive of these levels of anxiety. Furthermore, vaccination acceptance played an important role as a psychological ‘shield’ protecting individuals from experiencing high levels of fear related to death.

Discussion

This study emphasizes the psychological impact of caring for COVID-19 patients in dedicated isolation wards on frontline HCWs. The study examines the following psychological burdens that were experienced by these professionals: distress, obsession, emotional exhaustion, death anxiety and vaccine acceptance. The overall infection rate was found significantly high (Table 1), infection rate of 63% among the HCWs within the current study supports many international studies that have shown 40–70% of frontline workers have contracted COVID-19 in-hospital due to continuous patient contact especially when there is PPE unavailability [3, 49].

A clear disparity between the gender was found in psychological outcomes. Obsession related to COVID-19, males had significantly lower (37.5%) than females (66.2%). In terms of emotional exhaustion, males reported 70.3% while females reported 74.2%. regarding death anxiety, males reported just 23.4%, whereas females reported 47.8%. Findings from studies show that women have experienced greater psychological pressure than men during the coronavirus pandemic [50].

Vaccine acceptance (86.9% females and 86.7% males) was same for men and women in this high-risk HCW group. Research supports that professional identity, clinical experience, and scientific knowledge reduce the risk of women being hesitant to get vaccinated based on gender [51].

In this study, results revealed that medical staff who have COVID-19 vaccine acceptance has less COVID-19 obsession and death anxiety. Early research showed that medical staff working with COVID-19 patients experienced psychological distress and burnout, largely due to increased occupational exposure [5]. This aligns with evidence showing that frontline exposure increased death anxiety among healthcare workers [24, 52].

It stands to reason that the heightened levels of anxiety may have been due to the prolonged exposure to critically ill patients, higher rates of contact with infected individuals, and exposure as a participant in end-of-life care, compounded with a perceived lack of control about patient outcome. Our results confirms that death anxiety remained a prominent psychological burden during the pandemic [24]. It is important to note that 63% of the medical staff in our health region reported having contracted COVID-19. Additionally, there are many well-documented occupational issues to further complicate this risk: psychological distress and work overload is only amplified by inadequate resources across human and material domains, and ultimately the lack of access to adequate safety equipment and PPE [53].

This also accounts for the higher expressions of COVID-19-related obsession and death anxiety for the participants of this study. The present research investigates the role of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance as a moderator in the relationship between COVID-19 obsession and death anxiety. The results show that vaccine acceptance has a statistically significant negative moderating effect that weakens the relationship that COVID-19 obsession has with death anxiety for medical staff (see Table 6). This pattern is consistent with prior evidence that vaccination improves short-term mental wellbeing [49]. According to another survey, most health care medical staff in Pakistan are willing to be vaccinated early in a vaccination program, with only a tiny percentage of participants refusing to be vaccinated [5, 54]. Nurses in critical and high exposure settings consistently show elevated psychological distress [55], including death anxiety and obsessive thoughts [56].

Furthermore, results revealed that emotional exhaustion has a non-significant association with death anxiety among medical staff (Table 6). Emotional exhaustion is not a significant predictor of death anxiety. Emotional exhaustion is one of the key areas of occupational burnout and is seen as a major cause of death anxiety. However, from the current study it was found that emotional exhaustion does not have a significant relationship with death anxiety in health professionals. Therefore, these findings suggest that the psychological processes associated with death anxiety due to the covid-19 pandemic are more directly related to disease specific fear than to generalized work related depletion (Özgüç et al. [57]). While emotional exhaustion is associated with a pattern of chronic fatigue, low to moderate levels of energy, and an overwhelming sense of being overwhelmed, it does not instigate the thoughts related to mortality unless there is significant concern about personal safety threats [58].

As research established the impact of vaccination on the Corona virus, the vaccine became more widely available. According to one study, COVID-19 vaccines with 95% success in illness prevention could dramatically lower future attack rates, hospitalizations, and fatalities, even if they provided little protection against infection [59]. Current study findings indicate that COVID-19 vaccine acceptance is substantially higher among medical professionals than it is globally [38, 60, 61]. Vaccination is still an important preventive technique for lowering disease burden and preventing new outbreaks. Our results are aligning with Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices’ guidelines, demonstrating that a targeted immunization strategy can successfully lower the disease burden among high-risk group [62].

Strength and limitations of study

The study has several advantages. First, only few studies examine the effects of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance on the psychological state of medical staff working on the front lines in Pakistan, especially regarding the correlation between COVID-19 obsession and anxiety over death. Second, the authors used reliable and established psychological measurement tools for all constructs. Thus, they built their study on a sound psychometric foundation, as evidenced through PLS-SEM. Third; by employing an advanced analytic technique (PLS-SEM), they were able to develop and test complex models of relationship among latent constructs and the impact of the relationships on one another. In addition, the response rate was very good (66%) due to the difficult conditions of collecting data during a pandemic, which indicates that the authors were successful in reaching out to and engaging with participants. Finally, this research contributes valuable evidence from the perspective of a lower-middle-income country to further develop our understanding of the psychological well-being of healthcare workers on the front lines during COVID-19.

Our study has some limitations: first, Self-reporting is used to measure both the COVID-19 related obsession and effects of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance, which could be biased. Secondly, the study was conducted only in one region (Punjab) in one country. Third, the study considered only two sub-dimensions of the vaccine acceptance scale even though its inclusion of additional components would have theoretically added to knowledge of respondent’s attitudes towards vaccines.

Implications

The results of the study suggest that facilitating vaccine acceptance among medical staff provides them with a measure of protection against infection and alleviates their death anxiety and COVID-19 related obsessions. Healthcare institutions should facilitate discussions about vaccine hesitancy and promote vaccination as part of a mental health strategy. Mental health intervention programs need to provide interventions for intrusive pandemic-related thoughts and residual anxiety - especially for those who have vaccine hesitancy. Policy makers should embrace vaccination programs as an equal effort to protect health care workers from both physical and psychological harm in the occupational health programs. Promoting vaccine acceptance can ultimately be one means to assist in shielding frontline medical staff from psychological harm during pandemics and to improve their mental health and resilience.

Conclusion

According to the findings of this study, vaccination effectiveness appears to lessen COVID-19-related obsessions and death anxiety in medical practitioners who are working in COVID-19 isolation wards. Our research adds to the increasing body of evidence suggesting that COVID-19 immunization has a positive impact on mental health for healthcare workers. The findings provide important information for policy and public health by reaffirming expert assertions that vaccination against COVID-19 is related to an increase in positive psychological benefits for healthcare workers. A representative sample of medical personnel in Pakistan demonstrated that having received the COVID-19 vaccination was associated with reduced COVID-19 related anxiety and obsessive behavior, which demonstrates that the COVID-19 vaccination reduces the psychological discomfort associated with the COVID-19 pandemic.
