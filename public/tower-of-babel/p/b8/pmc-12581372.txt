Exploring collaborative practice between conventional and Korean medicine: a qualitative study of Korean medicine doctors’ experienced barriers and strategies
Authors: Dasol Park, Moon Joo Cheong, Hyungsun Jun, Jungah Uhm, Inae Youn, Jungtae Leem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn South Korea, collaborative practice between conventional medicine (CM) and traditional Korean medicine (CPCK) is expanding under consumer demand and supportive national policies in line with global trend. Yet studies of Korean Medicine Doctors’ (KMDs) experiences of CPCK, especially in relation to policy and institutional contexts, remain scarce. This study reconstructed KMDs’ hospital-based CPCK experiences, focusing on processes across institutions, perceived barriers and challenges that disrupt seamless operation, and strategies adopted to address them.MethodsThrough literature review and team discussions, a framework for data collection and analysis was pre-developed with three domains: CPCK process, barriers and challenges, and actions to address them. Focus groups and in-depth interviews were conducted with 26 licensed KMDs engaged in patient care at 10 South Korean hospitals (university-affiliated, public, and private). Participants (residents and specialists in internal medicine, rehabilitation, acupuncture and moxibustion, gynecology, neuropsychiatry, and other clinical fields) each had ≥ 1 month of direct CPCK experience. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using qualitative content analysis; two researchers iteratively coded the data guided by the three domains.ResultsCPCK typically relied on referral-based consultations. Collaboration initially centered on stroke and musculoskeletal disorders but has gradually expanded. KMDs frequently sought CM collaboration for inpatient prescription renewal and diagnostic testing, whereas Western medical doctor (WMD)-to-KMD requests were infrequent. From KMDs’ accounts of barriers and coping strategies, three themes emerged: insufficient institutional support for seamless CPCK, physician indifference toward collaboration with KMDs, and fragmented CPCK owing to limitations in national policy. Participants regarded CPCK as promising but reported fatigue from limited support and negative attitudes among WMDs, with burdens falling disproportionately on KMDs.ConclusionQualitative content analysis of KMD interviews at Korean hospitals explored CPCK processes, identified barriers and facilitators, and examined KMDs’ perspectives and attitudes toward collaboration. KMD-only sampling limits generalizability. Nonetheless, by detailing day-to-day CPCK procedures and challenges, the study highlights that true partnership and preservation of professional identity remain major concerns for KMDs striving to advance CPCK. Future studies should include diverse participants, test CPCK protocols, and address broader policy measures for sustainable integrative care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05117-0.Keywords: Integrative medicine, Collaborative care, Korean and western collaborative care, Traditional and complementary medicine, Traditional korean medicine, Qualitative content analysis, Qualitative research

Background

Integrative medicine (IM) is an approach that selectively integrates practices from conventional medicine (CM) and traditional and complementary medicine (T&CM) to create and implement comprehensive treatment plans [1]. Growing interest in T&CM’s holistic approach has induced the global expansion of IM, including in the United States and Europe, as a response to the limitations of disease-focused CM, which often depends heavily on expensive advanced diagnostics, therapeutic technologies, and pharmacotherapy [2–4].

East Asian traditional medicine, centered on acupuncture, moxibustion, herbal medicine, and tuina, is practiced alongside conventional medicine under different national policies in countries such as Korea, China, Japan, and Vietnam [5]. In South Korea, the 1951 Medical Services Act established a dual system, in which traditional Korean medicine (TKM) and CM each operate independently with distinct educational, licensing, and specialty systems [6]. However, in line with global trends, there is a growing demand in Korea for IM that combines the strengths of TKM and CM while compensating for their respective weaknesses [7]. Substantial evidence exists regarding the benefits of integrating CM and T&CM for conditions including cancer, musculoskeletal disorders, and cardiovascular disease, with high patient satisfaction reported in these settings [8–12]. Consequently, in Korean healthcare, collaborative practice between CM and TKM (CPCK) has assumed multiple forms, ranging from fully independent parallel treatments to collaborative clinical decision-making at the individual or team level [13]. In 2009, the Korean government legalized cross-employment, thereby establishing a legal basis for CPCK [14], and launched pilot programs led by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in 2016. The fifth phase of these programs is slated for 2025 [15].

Despite IM’s beneficial effects and growing demand in Korea, along with some policy and institutional changes, many challenges remain regarding its successful implementation at policy, clinical, academic, and educational levels [16]. Particularly, academic conflicts and competing interests among healthcare practitioners, who are key participants in collaboration, can hinder seamless integrative care [17, 18]. It is, thus, necessary to investigate the perspectives of the public, patients, policymakers, and, especially, practitioners who already engage in CPCK [19]. Related studies in Korea have examined views of the general public [20], patients [21], government officials [22], and healthcare professionals [17, 18, 23], though most provider-focused research involved quantitative surveys of Western medicine doctors (WMDs). Additionally, previous qualitative studies on CPCK in Korea were criticized for failing to consider institution-specific characteristics [16]. To advance more efficient and synergistic IM, it is important to explore the perspectives of practitioners, especially Korean medicine doctors (KMDs) who have practiced CPCK under Korea’s evolving policy environment since 2009.

Accordingly, this study aimed to reconstruct how CPCK unfolds in hospital settings from the perspective of KMDs, analyzing the typical procedures and interactional flows, the structural and institutional barriers shaping these processes, and the relational, practical, and psychological challenges encountered along the way. We also examined the strategies KMDs adopted to overcome such barriers and challenges, situating these efforts within their unique professional position, legally recognized and highly trained, yet institutionally marginalized, and provide foundational data that may guide future policy decisions and clinical practice changes.

Methods

All methods, including researcher characteristics, framework development, data management, IRB approval, and obtaining written informed consent from participants, as well as statements on ethics considerations and patient/public involvement, are presented in the supplementary file (see pages 1–5 in Supplementary file 1).

Research design

To explore KMDs’ perceptions and experiences with CPCK and to identify obstacles and coping strategies, we conducted semi-structured, open-ended interviews. Interviews were grouped based on each participant’s institution, recognizing that CPCK experiences could significantly vary depending on the hospital setting. However, we also considered factors such as workplace environment, time constraints, and participants’ attitudes toward CPCK, adjusting our methods (group or one-on-one) and occasionally subdividing interview groups by institution [24]. All data collection and analysis were conducted in Korean, while the manuscript was written in English.

Framework development

Prior to data collection, theoretical and conceptual frameworks commonly used in inter-professional and integrative care research [25–29] were reviewed but deemed unsuitable for capturing the episodic, multi-level, and policy-embedded nature of CPCK in Korea, as their direct application risked narrowing the scope and limiting reconstruction of processes involving national policies, hospital administration, and interprofessional partnerships. Therefore, drawing on Glaser and Laudel’s guidance on category construction [30], Hughes’ review of integrated care as an emergent, context-shaped practice [31], and our team’s prior review of CPCK in Korea identifying research gaps in on-site demand and activation strategies [16], we developed a generalized analytic framework with three domains: CPCK process, barriers and challenges, and actions to address them. This framework guided data collection and analysis and was iteratively reviewed to ensure consistency with the principles of maximizing data capture, participant-centeredness, and analytic flexibility.

Sampling and recruitment

KMDs who had participated in CPCK and were willing to join the study were eligible; those with less than one month of CPCK experience were excluded. We applied convenience sampling [32] to initially recruit KMDs, who were easily accessible to the research team, from university hospitals, public hospitals, and private hospitals. Potential participants were contacted via phone and email, and they received information about the study before consenting to participate. Subsequently, additional KMDs who were not directly accessible to the researchers were recruited through snowball sampling [33], based on recommendations from the initial participants.

Recruitment ended when sufficient data were gathered and no new information emerged in further interviews, indicating data saturation [34]. In total, 26 KMDs participated. None refused to take part or dropped out during the study. DP had no prior or subsequent relationship with the participants. All participants received compensation. Setting

All interviews were conducted in Korean and in person. They took place in quiet, private, and comfortable settings, such as meeting rooms at the participants’ hospitals. Each interview lasted 90–120 min, and each participant was interviewed only once.

Data collection

After the 11th interview, no new themes emerged, indicating data saturation. In total, we interviewed 26 participants from 10 institutions. From October 2019 to January 2020, JL (the interviewer) conducted six group interviews and five single-person interviews. Each interview began with a brief self-introduction round encompassing participants’ current workplace and position, the institution and position they held when participating in CPCK, their length of CPCK experience, and any additional clinical or non-clinical background in TKM.

We developed an interview guide [35] based on a literature review on CPCK in Korea [16], discussions among the research team, and a pilot test. sTable 1 summarizes the key questions that the interviewer asked in each session. The interviewer encouraged participation from all members, occasionally prompting individuals and adjusting questions to delve into experiences.

All interviews were audio-recorded by JL and transcribed verbatim by JL and DP. DP reviewed the transcripts to ensure they matched the audio recordings. Participants did not review or amend the transcripts. Non-verbal and social cues, including gestures, facial expressions, and certain behaviors, were noted during the interviews. Only the participants and researchers were present during the interview sessions.

Data analysis

Qualitative content analysis (QCA) [30, 36] was performed to reconstruct and analyze participants’ experiences with CPCK, using ATLAS.ti software (Version 9.11.4; ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2025).

Before coding, three preliminary categories based on a pre-developed framework were reviewed in light of the research questions, forming the basis for analyzing KMDs’ experiences, including: (1) how they engaged in CPCK processes, (2) the barriers and challenges they encountered, and (3) the efforts they described to address these barriers and challenges and expand CPCK. Two researchers (JL, DP) independently coded the first interview transcript to confirm whether these categories reflected the key elements of participants’ experiences, then applied them to all transcripts.

After each interview, the codes that emerged were reviewed to ensure they aligned with the research questions, with obvious errors corrected and duplicate codes merged. The two researchers re-read the transcripts to verify that the codes accurately reflected the original text, refining, deleting, merging, or subdividing codes as required. Similar codes were grouped by shared meanings, examined for relevance to the research questions, and applied to derive key themes representing participants’ core experiences and perceptions. This iterative process continued until no new themes appeared after the 11th interview, indicating data saturation. Subsequently, a process diagram was created by connecting the key themes to the structural elements influencing CPCK, namely national policies, hospital policies and administrative support, and the partnership between healthcare practitioners involved, and the resulting themes and their core codes were then presented.

This study drew on Boon et al.’s criteria for team-based collaboration from parallel care to integrative care [26], Gaboury et al.’s key components of inter-professional collaboration in integrative healthcare clinics [28], and Grant et al.’s qualitative study themes on the integrative health care process [37]. This was performed to examine whether each interview round involved the following CPCK process elements: shared patient files, independent practice aligned with each practitioner’s expertise, simultaneous patient interviews or rounding, clinical meetings for clinical purposes, academic discussions among providers, CPCK protocol development and use, employment of a CPCK coordinator, and the presence of a mediator or mediating agency for provider conflicts.

Any coding or thematic discrepancies between the two researchers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. A non-medical researcher (MJ) conducted an audit trail to reduce possible bias in the analysis. The final codes, themes, and process diagram were informed by participants’ feedback and subsequently reviewed and approved by all authors holding KMD licenses.

General CPCK process

Regardless of their institution type, participants noted that CPCK generally proceeded through a consultation-based mechanism. Practitioners requested assistance from another healthcare practitioner when they recognized that a test or treatment required by the patient exceeded their scope of practice or specialization. Most participants reported that these consultations mostly served inpatients. Within an institution, clinical notes and referral requests were communicated via an electronic medical record (EMR) system accessible to both CM and TKM. If no qualified practitioner was available on-site, external referrals were made via a written request that the patient carried to the receiving institution. This CM–TKM consult workflow mirrored typical CM–CM consultations.

Participants perceived that the range of CPCK indications is expanding over time, moving beyond stroke and musculoskeletal conditions to include cancer, dermatological diseases, obstetric/gynecological disorders, major trauma, and post-operative care. According to participants, KMDs frequently consulted CM to renew medications that inpatients had originally been prescribed under CM care, requesting diagnostic tests for new admissions, treating emergency or severe cases, and fulfilling patient requests. Some participants noted that these frequent referrals stemmed from ongoing disputes and vague regulations that effectively limit KMDs’ access to diagnostic tools. Conversely, the most common reason for CM to consult TKM was to accommodate patients who requested TKM therapy, particularly acupuncture for comorbid conditions, although a few referrals were also made when standard treatments proved insufficient. Except at one private TKM hospital, where practitioners reported near-equal referral rates between TKM and CM, most KMDs observed that requests from TKM to CM far outnumbered those from CM to TKM.

At one private TKM hospital and one CM hospital, a few participants described joint patient interviews or rounds by KMDs and CM physicians. At other institutions, however, each practitioner typically conducted patient interviews independently. Patient files were shared via an integrated EMR system. CPCK that occurred between university-affiliated CM and TKM hospitals could share clinical data only if their EMR systems were integrated; otherwise, patient information was transferred solely by referral letters carried by patients. Clinical findings and treatment summaries for referred patients were returned via the EMR system or written reports.

In a few cases, practitioners held meetings, whether between individuals, departments, or at the institution level, to facilitate clinical decision-making for certain patients referred for CPCK. During these discussions, test results, clinical opinions, and treatment plans were comprehensively shared, and disagreements in the clinical decision-making process were resolved collaboratively.

At a few institutions, collaborative care guidelines were developed through inter-departmental discussions and pilot testing. These guidelines were applied to cases that, according to internal consensus, would benefit from CPCK. A few institutions also designated administrative staff to serve as CPCK coordinators, tasked with scheduling patient appointments, supporting communication among collaborating practitioners, and educating patients about CPCK [38].

Barriers experienced by KMDs during CPCK and tackling strategies

Participants reported encountering various challenges, both directly and indirectly, throughout the CPCK process. These challenges can be categorized into three main themes: (1) insufficient institutional support, (2) difficulties forming partnerships with collaborating CM physicians, and (3) limitations arising from national policies. They addressed these issues individually or collectively, with or without institutional backing.

Theme 1. Insufficient institutional support for a seamless CPCK process

Participants consistently emphasized that administrative backing at the institutional level significantly influences the smooth delivery of CPCK. Additionally, they noted that a hospital’s specific characteristics, including its administration, director, personal philosophies, and commitment to CPCK, impact the level and extent of support. Consequently, participants identified several areas requiring institutional support: clinical information sharing among practitioners, development and application of CPCK protocols, mediation of inter-professional conflicts, hiring and training CPCK support staff, and compensating the extra workload and contributions of involved practitioners.

Subtheme 1–1. Support for information sharing among practitioners

All institutions employed an EMR system, and intra-institutional referrals were typically conducted via EMR. In two university-affiliated KMD hospitals, EMR integration with their counterpart CM hospitals enabled more efficient communication. Most participants recognized a unified EMR as crucial for facilitating CPCK. However, in one university-affiliated TKM hospital, although all technical requirements for EMR integration had been met, opposition from the linked CM hospital prevented the integration, resulting in CPCK never happening and thus presenting a major hurdle. Additionally, participants indicated that even when EMR systems were integrated, the interface usually focused on CM-specific chart formats. One institution addressed this by involving KMDs in updating the EMR to better accommodate TKM data and the more frequent CPCK requirements in KMD practice.

Beyond merely exchanging referral letters or EMR data, participants viewed more comprehensive clinical information sharing as essential for decision-making. In institutions without policies promoting additional communication, a few KMDs had to contact physicians informally to discuss specific cases; elsewhere, no contact occurred beyond referral and reply notes in the EMR. In contrast, when hospitals officially supported staff meetings for clinical discussions, where KMD participation was mandated as an internal policy despite not being required for insurance coverage, participants perceived it improved not only the understanding of cases but also mutual appreciation of each profession’s role. However, if institutions demanded multi-disciplinary meetings without offering extra resources or adjusting workloads, these sessions often became formalities or never occurred at all, not because a lack of partnership but simply due to limited time.

Subtheme 1–2. Institutional support for developing and applying CPCK protocols, and for mediating Inter-professional conflicts

Eight of the 11 interview rounds highlighted the need for dedicated clinical guidelines and pathways for CPCK, especially in complex cases where multiple experts are involved. Such protocols not only standardize clinical decisions but can also optimize hospital workflow. When no CPCK protocols were in place, physicians, nurses, and administrators often lacked clarity on how and when to involve TKM, resulting in conflicts, and, sometimes, decreasing patient flow to the KMD service. However, participants acknowledged that developing CPCK guidelines is “tough”, as it requires not only reflection on each institution’s unique context and additional time and resources, but also a “paradigm shift” grounded in mutual understanding among practitioners. Where hospital leadership or a proactive director supported the development and implementation of CPCK protocols, participants observed more explicit role delineation, decrease in conflicts, and broader acceptance of each other’s expertise, ultimately benefiting both patients and the hospital.

Participants added that even with CPCK protocols, disputes may arise from differing clinical opinions between KMDs and WMDs. They believed that while trust can help resolve conflicts case by case, a formal institutional mechanism would be more reliable:

Let’s say I look at an X-ray and see a fracture, but the orthopedist doesn’t. That’s easy to resolve clinically, but the patient could perceive a huge difference. If there’s trust, like in our hospital, we’ll just say, ‘We’ll consult again and get a second opinion,’ and things go smoothly. But if there’s no trust and no institutional support, it becomes very tough. A neutral third party could help, but our real-world system doesn’t have that.

At one institution, a third-party administrator, not subordinate to either CM or TKM, sometimes mediated disagreements. However, participants noted that such a setup is not widely feasible; therefore, hospitals require more systematic conflict-resolution policies:

We need a mediator for CPCK. Even just hearing both sides helps. But in most hospitals, everyone answers to the director, so a true mediator isn’t possible, and there’s no policy support for it. If we had a formal system for that, it would be great.

Subtheme 1–3. Hiring and training CPCK coordinators

A few participants expressed a need for CPCK coordinators. Most hospitals had limited clinical staff in each specialty and were already busy with regular duties, leaving little capacity for extra CPCK-related tasks. Additionally, schedules were not optimized for joint consultations, resulting in delays:

In a large hospital like ours, we can’t collaborate unless both practitioners’ schedules match. But each department chair has their own commitments. Sometimes, by the time a KMD is available, the CM physician is busy this afternoon or tomorrow, and the patient might just give up and go elsewhere.

No institution had specifically hired a CPCK coordinator. Instead, in a few hospitals, TKM residents or nurses unofficially filled that role, augmenting their existing workload. Even where a coordinator existed, they rarely received formal CPCK-specific training.

Subtheme 1–4. Compensation policies for the increased workload and contributions of CPCK participating practitioners

In most interviews, participants stated that CPCK increases their workload without offering any direct financial incentive. KMDs often rely on CM physicians for diagnostic tests, but those physicians, especially those open to TKM, remain over-burdened by their own clinical duties. Furthermore, inter-disciplinary meetings or academic discussions to enhance CPCK demand additional time and effort. However, many hospitals lacked policies or payment structures to compensate practitioners for the additional work. A few participants believed that introducing incentives for WMDs could foster more robust CPCK:

It might help if we clarified incentives for WMDs who collaborate with KMDs. If a WMD performs a procedure based on a KMD referral, they’re contributing financially to the hospital. But in our institution, there’s no direct reward for that. The WMD may feel like they’re ‘doing all the work for someone else’s benefit.’ Actually, WMDs who take these referrals are more collaborative than those who refuse them, so we need a way to acknowledge that contribution.

Theme 2. Challenges of partnership in CPCK: between wmds’ indifference and kmds’ endeavors

Participants held positive views about CPCK experiences with WMDs who displayed clinical and academic interest in TKM. Such WMDs often recognized the potential of TKM in cases where standard treatments reached their limits, such as managing persistent pain after thoracotomy or stroke-related appetite loss, and showed interest in evidence supporting TKM. Additionally, they tended to become more open to TKM when they observed favorable outcomes. Participants noted that these supportive WMDs generally understood that not all conventional medical practices are fully evidence-based, indicating a sense of shared ground with TKM. Furthermore, personal connections or shared work experiences from a hospital’s formative period fostered collegial trust, particularly when team members were of a similar age or had overlapping interests. However, across all interview rounds, participants most frequently cited WMDs’ apathy, misconceptions, or hostility toward TKM as a significant barrier to CPCK. KMDs responded by pursuing various strategies, both clinically and academically, to build trust with both WMDs and patients.

Subtheme 2 − 1. WMDs who show little interest in working with KMDs

Participants encountered conflict with WMDs who were uninterested in the potential benefits of TKM at the boundaries of standard treatment, or who harbored biases and distrust, sometimes even open antagonism, toward TKM. These tensions impacted not only the CPCK process but also patient care. Conflicts of this nature were particularly pronounced in public or university hospitals, where no secondary incentives such as financial gain existed. Participants perceived that hostile policies from certain professional associations or societies could cut-off any prior academic exchange between WMDs and KMDs, thus fueling unfavorable perceptions among WMDs in positions such as residency or society leadership, especially in university-affiliated hospitals.

Subtheme 2-1-1. Patients’ treatment preferences not respected

A few participants witnessed WMDs neglect or refuse to treat patients solely because they were receiving TKM therapy. Others described situations in which WMDs discontinued a patient’s TKM treatment without consulting the collaborating KMD or openly expressed personal distrust of TKM to the patient, rather than offering a medical opinion. In these scenarios, patients often lost trust in the healthcare team, stopped visiting the KMD, or even stopped visiting that hospital:

Collaboration still requires the patient’s choice too. But if the patient hears overwhelmingly negative comments about TKM from their WMD, of course they won’t want TKM anymore. I’ve had a patient come to me in tears, saying, ‘My WMD told me to stop TKM therapy. I have no idea why.’ It happens a lot.

Additionally, patients often missed opportunities to receive TKM because of WMDs’ reluctance or outright refusal to issue referrals:

Residents often won’t honor a patient’s request for TKM. They might say, ‘Why bother with something useless?’ or complain that writing a TKM referral is a waste of time. As a result, many patients just give up on TKM.

Subtheme 2-1-2. KMDs not recognized as competent professionals

All interview rounds conveyed a common thread: KMDs perceived they were not adequately respected as medical professionals by WMDs. Participants believed WMDs often clung to distorted stereotypes of KMDs, stereotypes that did not match any real KMDs but nevertheless fueled conflicts during CPCK:

WMDs have such a limited grasp of East Asian traditional medicine. They imagine us like some local moxibustion parlors or a cupping service in a public bath. There’s no real KMD like that, but they hate that imaginary KMD anyway. It’s a constant cycle of misunderstanding.

When adverse events occurred, even if unrelated to TKM, WMDs often placed disproportionate blame on KMDs. This pressure led a few KMDs to practice defensively or become reluctant to prescribe herbal medicine.

On a new CPCK admission, I always check for any preexisting wounds and document them: ‘This wound isn’t due to TKM treatments.’

A few physicians disregarded the legitimacy of a KMD’s clinical judgment or referral rationale, refusing to acknowledge KMDs as independent medical experts:

Sometimes we send a referral just to get necessary tests done, because we lack access to diagnostic tools, and then we hear back (through the patient) that the WMD said, ‘Why are they asking for a referral for something so trivial?’

After such incidents, several KMDs avoided sending referrals to certain specialties or reduced direct communication, opting to gather information about an WMD’s treatment only through the EMR or via the patient.

Subtheme 2-1-3. CPCK Reduced to a Perfunctory Referral Owing to a Lack of Mutual Trust Among Practitioners

Participants noted that CPCK without mutual trust usually amounted to perfunctory referrals. While KMDs, restricted from using diagnostic tools, must refer patients to WMDs, WMDs often viewed TKM referrals as “optional if the patient insists,” rather than a legitimate clinical need. In these passive collaborations, minimal information was exchanged, and CPCK seemed more like an administrative formality or task delegation than true teamwork.

Several participants perceived that passive CPCK leads to suboptimal patient care:

In the U.S., if a pregnant patient has headaches, a physician explains that medications are limited, so acupuncture or massage could be an option, and the patient chooses what fits best. Then the physician refers them accordingly. Here, a passive referral is purely to satisfy the patient’s request, so the WMD takes no actual responsibility for that decision.

Subtheme 2–2. KMDs’ efforts to obtain Understanding and trust from WMDs and patients

All participants saw WMDs’ lack of understanding and indifference toward TKM as the primary barrier to CPCK and engaged in various individual or group-level initiatives to overcome it. They noted that although evidence-based research in TKM is expanding, the field remains under-developed, with numerous knowledge gaps.

Subtheme 2-2-1. Presenting TKM in the Language and methods of modern science

Participants unanimously perceived it was necessary to explain TKM in ways consistent with modern scientific frameworks so that both WMDs and patients could recognize its value. In every interview round, they mentioned reinterpreting TKM in biomedical terms to facilitate mutual understanding:

I’ve never really obsessed over my identity per se. I’m just a healthcare practitioner. … I believe that to promote collaboration, I should offer WMDs and patients clear, concise information they can understand and accept. We may use different tools and frameworks, but we share the same ultimate goal. If we exchange the right information, we’ll get along fine, and CPCK can thrive.

Additionally, participants recognized the importance of academic discussions and research exchanges between WMDs and KMDs. Many had successfully introduced research findings on TKM to their WMD colleagues:

We’ve done conferences with emergency department and rehab medicine. We have a plan to invite pain medicine next. I want to investigate how intermittent acupuncture after nerve blocks compares to no acupuncture. It seems highly relevant, but no one’s studied it. So we’re planning a conference with pain medicine about doing that.

Several participants reported that co-conducting research with WMDs substantially boosted collaboration. Working intimately on projects fostered personal rapport and demonstrated KMDs’ grasp of research methodologies, which enhanced mutual respect and furthered CPCK:

I think it’s crucial to invest time in collaborative studies on mild, chronic conditions where TKM may help. That way, WMDs gradually see that the KMD team is really trying and doing research, which establishes a foundation of trust, even if it’s small. With that basis, they’re less likely to dismiss TKM out of hand.

Subtheme 2-2-2. KMDs showing empathy and respect for wmds’ concerns

Several KMDs navigated conflicts by acknowledging and respecting WMDs’ positions, thereby facilitating smoother collaboration. All participants recognized that WMDs experience uncertainties about TKM, particularly regarding safety, drug interactions, and acute care, and attempted to address these concerns empathetically:

Ultimately, the primary doctor bears final responsibility for a patient’s clinical course in CPCK. We understand that. So we need to tackle any safety issues or drug interactions in a way that minimizes risk, in order to address their concerns.

For instance, a few KMDs thoroughly explained their treatment plans, monitored patient progress, and proactively identified potential problems to mitigate WMDs’ worries. Others adopted a methodical approach, focusing CPCK first on specific conditions that presented fewer safety concerns, then gradually expanding collaboration once trust was established. This led to the creation of pilot protocols:

In the emergency department, the biggest concern was ‘What if we miss a fracture or a bleed in a mild trauma patient, and they present later?’ So we drafted a protocol requiring KMDs to consult family medicine. We’ve caught missed fractures this way. Over time, you build trust through those examples.

In a few institutions, KMDs assumed the primary responsibility for organizing staff meetings and tailoring the agenda to busy clinical schedules, thus fostering more efficient communication:

We don’t just drop in and say, ‘Help us with this’ or ‘Do that for us.’ We organize conferences on particular topics or follow-up cases with each department. If people are really busy, we schedule it at lunchtime and order something to eat. That’s the quickest way.

Several participants maintained a consistently courteous attitude toward uncooperative WMDs, hoping to create a more favorable environment for future collaboration:

Even if they ignore me, I still say hello. Some WMDs have never once responded. It makes me wonder if it’s because I’m a KMD. But even then, I greet them anyway. Over the years, about half will eventually greet me back, and that opens a small door.

Subtheme 2-2-3. Prioritizing rapport with patients

Other participants believed that preserving a strong patient–KMD relationship was paramount. They took steps such as exploring reimbursable treatment options to reduce the patient’s out-of-pocket costs, coordinating schedules for minimal waiting times, and guiding them through the CPCK process.

Additionally, they managed patients’ expectations by clarifying realistic treatment goals and the scope of TKM. This was especially helpful when patients, unsuccessful with prior standard treatments, viewed TKM as their last resort:

When someone’s had paralysis for 3–4 years, I don’t promise a cure. I focus on pain relief or easing spasticity. If that aligns with the patient’s needs, they tend to be more satisfied.

Theme 3. Fragmented CPCK owing to limitations in National policy

All interview rounds revealed that systemic constraints hinder the effectiveness of CPCK. Even when clinicians agreed on the need for and value of CPCK, national policy limitations often restricted its scope or prevented sustained collaboration.

Subtheme 3 − 1. Narrow policy recognition of CPCK

Several participants noted that current regulations set the scope of insurance coverage narrowly, thereby limiting which conditions and treatments qualify as CPCK and discouraging practitioners from using it more broadly. They believed these restrictions should be relaxed to better reflect clinical realities. Additionally, a few participants criticized rules requiring patients to receive all their treatments in a single day for the collaboration to count:

Subtheme 3 − 2. Inefficiency stemming from differences in legal authority between WMDs and KMDs

All interview rounds identified the legal disparity in diagnostic privileges between WMDs and KMDs as a primary obstacle to CPCK. It created asymmetric access to clinical information, driving KMDs to consult WMDs mainly for tests rather than for broader clinical collaboration. Furthermore, even treatments that KMDs were fully capable of performing required a prescription from an WMD, which decreased potential revenue and undermined efficient collaboration:

KMDs have a high level of education, yet they can’t legally order even basic blood tests, and there’s no insurance coverage for that. This causes a major information gap between WMDs and KMDs, which makes genuine collaboration for best clinical outcomes impossible.

Under current policies, CPCK doesn’t generate revenue for KMDs. For instance, KMDs can judge that hyperthermia treatment is appropriate for a cancer patient, but a radiation oncologist is still needed to order it.

Subtheme 3–3. Inefficient Inter-Institution collaboration

Participants perceived a lack of support for CPCK among different institutions as a barrier. A few of them indicated that hospitals lacking certain specialties could offer better care to their inpatients by collaborating with external facilities, but the system provides no meaningful support beyond a billing code for inter-institution referrals:

When we send a patient to a CM hospital, that patient basically has to register as an outpatient there. Even if the hospitals are physically close, the CM side might have many people waiting, and our patient ends up in a time-consuming process where we can’t just jump the line.

Subtheme 3–4. Low uptake of CPCK clinical research findings

Participants noted that even successful CPCK studies often fail to translate into day-to-day clinical practice. They believed that proving CPCK’s effectiveness and proposing new protocols through research were only the preliminary steps; system-level follow-up was required to integrate the findings. A few participants mentioned that even if research identified an effective herbal remedy during CPCK, no corresponding pharmaceutical company or insurance coverage existed to facilitate its incorporation into conventional clinical practice:

“If we have strong research on CPCK, that definitely helps. We get referrals for similar cases, like irritable bowel syndrome or chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, but for collaboration to continue, we need a system. Plus, if we discover a promising herbal product, there’s no company to produce it, no insurance coverage—so it never moves forward.”

Theme 4. KMDs’ attitudes toward CPCK: between hope and exhaustion

Participants believed CPCK could provide broader, more effective treatment, especially in cases where standard therapies induce significant side effects or have limited efficacy, such as complex conditions requiring multi-faceted care:

From my experience, patient satisfaction with CPCK is high, and outcomes seem good. I didn’t measure it with an objective tool, but I see a difference when patients get CPCK versus not, how well they follow up, and whether they recommend us to others. CPCK clearly has benefits in both narrow and broad senses.

At the same time, participants experienced considerable physical and mental strain while navigating institutional and policy constraints and confronting WMDs’ skepticism about TKM. They often experienced the burden of having to develop protocols, negotiate with stakeholders, and address misconceptions on their own. Additionally, they perceived a certain helplessness when WMDs dismissed TKM evidence or delayed referral requests, particularly when KMDs were obliged to request even basic tests from WMDs:

For every CPCK effort, I have to convince nurses of TKM and CM hospital, residents, WMD professors, administrators, everyone. Doing all that alone, while already busy, is exhausting.

Over time, residents absorb negative attitudes toward TKM from their seniors. Patients tell me all the negative things their WMDs said. I tried challenging it each time, but it’s so draining. If collaboration doesn’t happen anyway, it can kill your motivation to keep pushing for it.

Sometimes I propose new ideas for patients at the limits of standard care, but I get zero response from the WMDs. After a few times, I think, ‘Fine, no one’s interested. Why bother?’

Despite these challenges, many participants continued to take pride in TKM and persisted in CPCK to demonstrate its clinical value, which they believed was obscured by policy restrictions and uninformed criticism. They believed that CM and TKM, grounded in different paradigms, could achieve synergy across prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation, provided that legal and systemic barriers were addressed. Additionally, they perceived a need to systematically identify and implement TKM’s most valuable roles within CPCK:

In Korea, WMDs and KMDs hold equal medical licenses. We view or interpret diseases somewhat differently, but that can be advantageous, giving us a broader picture of patients and better solutions.

Participants agreed that optimal CPCK should be truly complementary, with both professions adopting a rational and flexible approach for the patient’s benefit:

Medicine definitely has limitations. Sometimes TKM can do better. If WMDs took a moment to think, ‘Isn’t TKM more beneficial here?’ and recommended it, patients wouldn’t have to suffer or spend as much. I’m a KMD, but I don’t insist on TKM alone. I hope WMDs will think more rationally like this.

One participant noted that current CPCK models seldom include the patient’s perspective and suggested that including patients into discussions could yield a more integrative model:

We don’t yet incorporate the patient’s point of view into CPCK. If we captured feedback on how CPCK helped from their perspective, that might improve models of integrative collaboration. Maybe we could have a forum where WMDs, KMDs, and patients all join in discussing how CPCK can help.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study examined the experiences of KMDs who participated in CPCK at hospital-level institutions in Korea. Through QCA of interview data, we explored the typical CPCK process, the factors that hinder its smooth implementation, and the ways in which KMDs attempt to overcome these barriers. We identified that CPCK generally follows a referral-based process in which KMDs rely heavily on WMDs, partly because KMDs have limited access to diagnostic devices and because secondary or tertiary care facilities often treat more complex conditions. Owing to this imbalance, KMDs often led initiatives to develop and implement CPCK protocols, persuade WMDs and hospital administrators to support these protocols, communicate with WMDs via academic and social channels, and build trust with patients. In a few cases, these efforts succeeded in boosting collaboration. However, many participants reported fatigue and a sense of futility, citing the one-sided responsibility that often falls on KMDs, along with insufficient policy and resource support, challenges that individual KMDs alone could not resolve. Nevertheless, most KMDs recognized CPCK’s clinical value and expressed willingness to continue pursuing value-based complementary collaboration by highlighting TKM’s clinical utility and advocating for policy reforms. Additionally, they proposed improvements at the levels of education, academic research, and institutional and national policies.

Information sharing in CPCK

Several reports have emphasized that truly patient-centered, multi-disciplinary collaboration requires a shared format for exchanging clinical information among professionals [39]. In our study, most participating institutions employed an integrated EMR system, enabling referrals and patient-file sharing between CM and TKM. However, even when EMR integration existed, the record formats largely reflected the perspective of CM. Similar tendencies, where integrated health systems adopt templates centered on biomedical norms, have been noted in the United States and Canada [29, 37]. In Korea, though, KMDs typically have sufficient familiarity with biomedical knowledge and apply the same disease-coding system (Korean Standard Classification of Diseases) as WMDs. Thus, the biomedical orientation of EMR systems was not a major concern for most participants, given that a few institutions had not integrated EMRs at all, rendering any form of CPCK challenging. We identified that such non-integration issues often stemmed less from technical or policy barriers and more from the reluctance of CM hospitals. Hence, before tackling any technical fixes, it appears more critical to build a partnership culture founded on patient-centered values and mutual trust among practitioners.

Importance of partnership among CPCK participants

Our findings showed that KMDs frequently attempted, and often struggled, to form a true partnership with WMDs as equal healthcare professionals to achieve synergistic, complementary care. A trusting, cooperative relationship is fundamental to successful integrative practice. Studies have repeatedly demonstrated that when WMDs lack trust in or familiarity with T&CM practitioners, referrals and other aspects of collaboration decline [28, 37, 40]. In this study, KMDs attempted to obtain WMDs’ trust through various strategies: sharing evidence on TKM therapies, focusing on conditions that might respond particularly well to TKM, developing data-driven CPCK protocols, and building evidence on clinical effectiveness. These attitudes reflect a high regard for evidence-based practice. Indeed, TKM professionals have increasingly acknowledged the importance of evidence-based medicine and attempted to adapt it to T&CM research and clinical care [41–43]. However, TKM research often faces methodological and infrastructural challenges, and these gaps can induce skepticism about TKM’s validity. Addressing this issue requires not only deeper mutual understanding of both medical paradigms but also the development of appropriate research designs for evaluating TKM. Furthermore, institutions, industry, and stakeholders must assume an active role in broadening research capacity and resources for TKM [44].

CPCK protocols as a tool for implementing partnership, and the need for support

CPCK protocols can serve as key tools for realizing partnerships in day-to-day clinical practice. In collaborative settings with multiple interventions and practitioners, protocols provide a systematic framework that defines each party’s roles and clarifies the overall treatment plan. However, universal CPCK guidelines are lacking, and each institution must tailor its protocols not only to relevant evidence but also to its unique circumstances. If these protocols fail to reflect local clinical needs, they can hinder rather than help, limiting each practitioner’s capacity and stifling a sense of partnership [45]. Our results showed that few institutions in this study had successfully developed and implemented such protocols. Even when they did, the process was protracted, requiring significant cooperation among practitioners and extensive institutional support. Lack of institutional resources often increased the practitioners’ workload to an unsustainable level, even when the clinical outcomes of CPCK were positive [46]. Hence, institutional involvement is crucial for sustaining CPCK built on true partnerships [47].

Preserving professional identity and Securing equal clinical authority

Although Korean KMDs receive extensive training and possess legal licenses, they face practical restrictions compared to WMDs, such as limited access to certain medical devices and differential insurance policies. This power imbalance can restrict collaborative outcomes and lock in unequal roles, as noted in prior studies [28, 29]. Nevertheless, we identified that participants firmly believed it was crucial to maintain TKM’s unique identity, particularly when treating illnesses that may benefit from an alternative clinical strategy. They also stressed that achieving a “true partnership” requires equal institutional and policy-based authority, echoing previous proposals suggesting that T&CM can maximize its clinical value in integrative care if it retains its identity while also narrowing the power gap with biomedical professionals [47, 48]. In other words, KMDs want clinical authority on par with WMDs, but not necessarily by merging into the biomedical profession. They see equal authority as a prerequisite for preserving TKM’s identity and for extending and sustaining CPCK’s clinical impact. This perspective resembles the experience in the United States, where doctors of osteopathy, amid debates that integration within the biomedical system risked eroding osteopathy’s distinctive value, responded by strengthening education to raise clinical standards and by retaining a distinct academic and philosophical heritage to preserve identity [49]. Nevertheless, cultural, institutional, and historical factors differ substantially, and additional discussion is required to assess feasibility and relevance in Korea.

Limitations and strengths

First, because this qualitative study explored experiences within a specific context, the themes we identified and the institutional realities they reflect may not be generalizable to all KMDs or healthcare settings. To address this concern, we continued interviews until data saturation and included diverse types of hospitals. Second, we recruited participants through convenience and snowball sampling, but we did not include other stakeholders (e.g., hospital directors, administrators, nurses, WMDs, physical therapists, patients, etc.). Although this restricted our ability to incorporate multiple perspectives, our goal was to fill the gap in the literature regarding KMDs’ views on CPCK; we, therefore, focused on KMDs with substantial collaborative experience across various settings. Future research might employ multi-layered interviews and participant observation that include different healthcare professionals and patient groups. Third, we combined group and individual interviews, which could have introduced variations in participants’ responses owing to group dynamics. To address this, we applied a semi-structured format, and an interviewer experienced in qualitative methods maintained detailed field notes and performed audit trails to ensure data quality. Fourth, many members of our research team were practicing KMDs who hold positive attitudes toward CPCK, which may introduce interpretive bias. We mitigated this by maintaining reflexivity, inviting reviews by an outside expert, and frequently cross-checking codes and themes.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. We investigated real-world CPCK experiences among KMDs in diverse hospital environments. By conducting focus groups to explore collective experiences and in-depth interviews for candid accounts, we gathered rich insights into participants’ experiences and attitudes. Our research team’s clinical background in TKM gave us insight into practical and contextual issues in CPCK, strengthening the interpretation of findings. Through QCA, we captured specific aspects of CPCK’s actual processes, as well as facilitators and barriers at various levels, and proposed elements required to maximize clinical outcomes and maintain sustainability. Notably, our findings underscore the importance of preserving TKM identity while ensuring equal clinical authority—an observation consistent with those of previous studies highlighting the need to reduce inter-professional power imbalances and enhance synergy in integrative care. Finally, our study provides a concrete example of how CPCK is practiced in Korea, contributing to broader discussions on ideal CPCK models worldwide. Institutions and policymakers may find our results useful when devising new support strategies or refining existing CPCK frameworks.

Conclusion

Through QCA of interviews with KMDs involved in CPCK at Korean hospitals, this study explored the CPCK process, identified barriers and facilitators, and examined KMDs’ perspectives and attitudes toward collaboration. Our research underscores that KMDs face systemic and institutional barriers, often coupled with unsupportive WMDs, and respond by engaging in academic exchanges, developing CPCK protocols, and implementing various collaborative measures, while also illuminating how institutional and policy contexts may either promote or hinder CPCK. Although our research, as a qualitative study focusing exclusively on KMDs, has limited generalizability, its close examination of daily CPCK practices and obstacles underscores that forging true partnerships and maintaining professional identity remain central concerns for KMDs seeking to advance CPCK. Future work should include diverse participants and might test the effectiveness of specific CPCK protocols. Broader institutional and national support measures also warrant further investigation to improve and sustain CPCK in real clinical contexts.
