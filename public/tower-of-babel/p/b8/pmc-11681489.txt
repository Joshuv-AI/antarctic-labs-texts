Cardiomyopathy‐Associated Chronic Heart Failure in Infants Aged <1 Year: A Prospective Observational Cohort Over 5 Years in Northern China
Authors: Wenhong Ding, Benzhen Wang, Jeff Hong, Lingeng Lu, Yanyan Xiao, Guangsong Shan, Connor Myers, Qing Yu, Christopher Spurney, Zipu Li, Ling Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere have been few large‐scale studies on the outcomes of cardiomyopathy‐associated heart failure (HF) in infants aged <1 year. This study aimed to assess longitudinal echocardiographic outcomes of infants with HF secondary to cardiomyopathy who survived for >1 year.Methods and ResultsA prospective observational study following 327 infant patients up to 5 years in 2 large pediatric heart centers in Northern China between January 2010 and December 2018. A total of 236 (72.2%) patients had reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (HF with reduced ejection fraction group; LVEF <40%), 91 (27.8%) patients had midrange LVEF (HF with midrange ejection fraction group; LVEF ≥40% but <55%). LVEF improved significantly within the first year and remained stable in years 2 through 5 for both groups. The HF with midrange ejection fraction group had a higher rate of LVEF normalization (hazard ratio, 1.65; P<0.001). Baseline LVEF ≥40%, baseline left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter Z score <7.8, the absence of left bundle‐branch block, and the absence of β‐blocker use were 4 independent favorable predictors for future LVEF normalization. A total of 62.4% of enrolled patients were diagnosed with left ventricular noncompaction. No significant difference in LVEF normalization was found among the different types of cardiomyopathy studied.ConclusionsA significant number of infants with cardiomyopathy who survived >1 year were found to improve with medical therapies during the first year of diagnosis. Poorer outcomes were associated with decreased LVEF and increased heart size at diagnosis baseline, the presence of left bundle‐branch block and use of β blockers. The Northern Chinese pediatric population may have a high proportion of left ventricular noncompaction.Keywords: cardiomyopathy, echocardiography, heart failure, longitudinal, pediatricsSubject Categories: Cardiomyopathy, Heart Failure

Heart failure (HF) is defined as the inability of the heart to supply adequate blood flow to meet the demands of the systemic or pulmonary circulation or the inability of the heart to receive venous return at appropriate filling pressures. 1 , 2 Cardiomyopathies and congenital heart diseases are the most common causes of HF in children in developed countries. The annual incidence of cardiomyopathies ranges from 0.8 to 1.3 cases per 100 000 children, but it is significantly higher in infants aged <1 year, with reported rates of up to 7.84 to 8.34 cases per 100 000 children. 3 , 4 Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the most prevalent type of cardiomyopathy. 5 In a 10‐year retrospective study of 1823 children with cardiomyopathy in China, 33% of the patients were diagnosed with DCM, followed by endocardial fibroelastosis (EFE; 27.81%) and left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC) (11.03%). 6

DCM is the leading indication for heart transplantation in pediatric patients, with reported death or heart transplant rates of 31% and 46% over 1‐ and 5‐year periods, respectively. 7 , 8 However, large‐scale studies on cardiomyopathy‐associated HF in infants aged <1 year are lacking. The purpose of this study was to assess the outcomes of children diagnosed with cardiomyopathy‐associated chronic HF before the age of 1 year who have survived for >1 year, with a focus on longitudinal echocardiographic data up to 5 years from diagnosis.

Methods

The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

This prospective observational cohort study enrolled all patients diagnosed with HF who received treatment at either the Heart Center, Beijing Anzhen Hospital, or Qingdao Women and Children's Hospital between January 2010 and December 2018. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee, and all patients' parents provided informed consent. This study adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Patients

The inclusion criteria for this study were: any patient who (1) met diagnostic criteria for DCM, LVNC, or EFE; (2) had left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <55% at time of diagnosis; (3) was ≤12 months of age at time of diagnosis; and (4) was followed for at least 1 year from time of diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were: (1) patients whose parents refused medical advice or follow up; (2) patients with HF secondary to tachyarrhythmia‐induced cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, restrictive cardiomyopathy, inborn errors of metabolism (such as primary carnitine deficiency), mitochondrial cardiomyopathy (such as Barth syndrome), or malformation syndrome (such as Noonan syndrome); (3) patients with cardiomyopathy with positive immune‐related markers or detectable perinatally transferred infection such as toxoplasma, rubella virus, or cytomegalovirus; and (4) with HF secondary to other reversible causes such as micronutrient deficiencies. Identification of Pediatric HF and Subtypes of Cardiomyopathy

The diagnosis of HF was made on the basis of the European Society of Cardiology guidelines and Canadian Cardiovascular Society guidelines. 1 , 2 Transthoracic echocardiography was used to assess left ventricular (LV) systolic function and chamber dimensions, and LV systolic dysfunction in children was defined by an LVEF <55%. 1 , 2 The diagnostic criteria of DCM and LVNC complied with the American Heart Association and European Society of Cardiology guidelines. 9 , 10 DCM was diagnosed by the presence of decreased systolic function and increased LV end‐diastolic diameter (LVEDD) on echocardiography. 10 LVEDD Z scores were obtained from the Boston Children's Hospital Z score calculator (http://zscore.chboston.org/) on the basis of body surface area, and cardiac enlargement was defined as a Z score >2. Due to the unavailability of a suitable Z score system for Chinese children, we employed the Z score system to identify cardiac enlargement. However, we did not use it as a standard for normalizing heart size and used it only for trend analysis without conducting a detailed statistical analysis. LVNC was diagnosed by a ratio of bilayer myocardium (noncompacted/compacted layers ratio) >2 at end systole in the parasternal short‐axis view at the level of the ventricular apex, in the absence of other congenital cardiac anomalies. 11 EFE was diagnosed by the presence of a dilated left ventricle; diffuse endocardial thickening; upward displacement of the papillary muscles; thickening of the free edge of papillary muscle; and bright, white‐appearing endocardium with clearly defined margins, in the absence of underlying congenital malformations, vascular abnormalities, or metabolic disorders. 12

Data Collection

Clinical and echocardiographic data were collected for a follow‐up period of up to 5 years. Management of HF was individualized, and medications included diuretics, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers, digoxin, intravenous immunoglobin (IVIG), and glucocorticoids. Echocardiographic images were acquired at baseline, every 3 months in the first year after diagnosis, and every 1 year subsequently. LVEDD was measured between the endocardium of the intraventricular septum and the posterior LV wall at end diastole on the parasternal short‐axis view. 2 The LVEF was calculated using the biplane Simpson's method on apical 4‐chamber and 2‐chamber views.

We used initial echocardiography to classify the patients into 2 groups: HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF group), defined as ejection fraction (EF) <40% and HF with midrange EF (HFmrEF group), defined as EF ≥40% and <55%. We compared baseline characteristics and outcomes between these groups to investigate the relationship between initial EF, LVEF improvement, and time to LVEF normalization. The end point of the study was the normalization of LVEF, defined as LVEF ≥55% by echocardiography.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were presented as mean with SD or median with interquartile range, depending on the data distribution. A generalized linear model was used to analyze the quantitative echocardiographic data through different follow‐up time points. Normal distribution quantitative data comparisons between the HFrEF and HFmrEF groups at each time point in Figure 1A through 1D were analyzed using Student's t test, while nonnormally distributed quantitative data comparisons between the HFrEF and HFmrEF groups in Table 1 were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables in Table 1 were reported as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the χ2 test. Linear mixed models were used to study correlations between LVEF and follow‐up times. Echocardiographic parameters were graphed with regression estimates set at a 95% CI. Significance of the regression models was determined using the likelihood ratio test, and the squared term was removed if its coefficient was not significantly different from 0 by the Wald test.

The time to LVEF normalization was defined as the time between enrollment and the earliest measured normal LVEF. To account for different predictors effect on overall LVEF normalization time, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) with a corresponding 95% CI. The proportional hazards assumption was also analyzed. Kaplan–Meier curves illustrating probability of LVEF normalization were constructed with the log‐rank test.

A backward stepwise procedure was used to select the subset of the most important independent predictors due to the limited sample size in this group. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 21.0 software (IBM, Chicago, IL).

Optional Serum Genetic Testing

Optional genetic testing was performed by third‐party commercial companies at the out‐of‐pocket expense of the patients' families.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 327 patients were enrolled in this study from 716 screened patients (Figure 1), and baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. No family we approached for recruitment refused participation in the study. Pediatric cardiomyopathies can be classified by structural and functional phenotypes on the basis of multimodal imaging and subcategorized by genetic and nongenetic causes. However, due to limited resources, we currently are unable to identify the specific pathogenesis in most of our patients; thus, our focus was on structural and functional phenotypes of cardiomyopathies in this study. Twenty‐eight patients were excluded with a definite pathogenesis at screening, of which 6 cases were diagnosed with inborn errors of metabolism (1 case with methylmalonic acidemia, 4 cases with Pompe disease, and 1 case with mucopolysaccharidosis type I); 10 cases with mitochondrial cardiomyopathy (6 cases with Barth syndrome, 2 cases with acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase 9 deficiency, and 2 cases with deficiencies in the oxidative phosphorylation system); and 6 cases with malformation syndrome (1 case with Noonan syndrome, 4 cases with Alstrom syndrome, and 1 case with α‐protein kinase 3–associated cardiomyopathy).

Among the 327 enrolled patients, 144 (44%) were male infants, and 183 (56%) were female infants. The median age of diagnosis was 4.5 (interquartile range, 2.5–7.0) months. Patients with HFmrEF were generally younger than those with HFrEF (P=0.028). There was no significant difference in total duration of mechanical ventilation and intensive care unit hospitalization between the subgroups. LVNC was the most common type of cardiomyopathy (62.4%) in this study. The overall incidence of abnormal ECG findings was 16.5%, with left bundle‐branch block (LBBB) being the most common (n=26 [8.0%]). All patients were treated with at least 1 medication, including digoxin (98.2%), diuretics (98.5%), ACEIs or angiotensin II receptor blockers (100%), β blockers (59.9%), and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (97.2%). Following a shared protocol between 2 collaborated heart centers written in 2008, amended and revised from 2006 HF diagnosis and treatment guideline in the pediatric population in China, we started β blockers with a small dosage when the acute HF symptoms were well controlled under other medication treatment, while moderate HF clinical symptoms were still presented, following the gradual increase of dosages with a close monitor of vital signs and clinical HF symptoms. When maximal tolerance dose is reached, we usually keep using β blockers for long‐term treatment. One patient was intolerant to an ACEI and switched to angiotensin receptor blocker treatment instead. Additionally, immunotherapy with IVIG and glucocorticoids was used in the majority of patients (66.4% and 84.4%, respectively). Immunotherapy was more often used in patients with HFrEF than those with HFmrEF (P<0.001). Angiotensin receptor–neprolysin inhibitors (ARNIs) were not administered to any patients, as ARNIs were not approved for clinical use in China until 2019, and this cohort ended in 2018. Three deaths occurred at 12, 13, and 32 months after the initial visit due to acute exacerbation of chronic HF. The first death was a 2‐year‐old girl diagnosed with LVNC at the age of 8 months; the second was a 13‐month‐old girl diagnosed with LVNC at the age of 15 days; and the third was a 3‐year‐old girl diagnosed with DCM at the age of 10 months and had an excessively dilated LV (LVEDD was 61 mm at the last visit). Nine patients with cardiomyopathy had Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome, with 4 of them (3 with DCM, 1 with LVNC) undergoing successful ablation. No patients received heart transplantation or ventricular assist device therapy.

Clinical Course: Short and Midterm Follow‐Up Outcomes

Patient follow‐up varied between 1 and 5 years, with 122 patients (37.3%) followed out to 5 years. Repeat LVEF and LVEDD measurements were collected at each follow‐up visit, resulting in a total of 2273 measurements. LVEF and LVEDD Z scores generally improved significantly in the first year after diagnosis and remained stable in years 2 through 5 (Figure 2A through 2F). Among 327 patients in this study, 267 (81.7%) patients had normalization of LVEF and maintained normal LVEF through the end of the follow‐up window, while 37 patients initially exhibited LVEF normalization but then experienced worsening LVEF to <55%. Of these 37 patients, 20 had subsequent renormalization of LVEF by the end of the follow‐up period, while the remaining 17 continued to exhibit decreased LVEF.

The median LVEF for the 236 patients in the HFrEF group improved from 29.7% to 57.75% at 1 year of follow‐up (P<0.001), while that for the 91 patients in the HFmrEF group improved from 45.0% to 61.0% (P<0.001). Among the 122 patients followed out to 5 years, the median LVEF improved from 29.0% to 62.0% in the HFrEF group (P<0.001) and from 43.0% to 61.4% in the HFmrEF group (P<0.001) (Figure 2A through 2B). Patients with HFmrEF had a significantly higher probability of LVEF normalization than those with HFrEF (Figure 3A).

Twenty‐one patients in this study were aged <1 month at the time of enrollment. Among these, 19 patients (90.5%) initially exhibited LVEF normalization, of which 4 experienced subsequent worsening of LVEF. Ultimately, 2 patients had subsequent renormalization of LVEF, while the remaining 2 maintained LVEF values of 50% and 51%, respectively. One of the remaining 2 patients with consistently decreased LVEF died at the age of 13 months. No difference in the final LVEF normalization ratio was found between patients diagnosed at age <1 month (17/21 [81%]) and those aged >1 month (250/306 [81.7%]). A higher mortality rate (4.8%) was found in patients diagnosed at age <1 month in our study when compared with those aged >1 month (0.65%; P<0.01).

Predictors of Echocardiographic Normalization

Univariate Cox regression analyses revealed that baseline LVEF <40%; baseline LVEDD Z score ≥7.8 (the median of LVEDD Z scores); the presence of ECG abnormalities (particularly LBBB); and the use of loop diuretics, β blockers, glucocorticoids, and IVIG were negative predictors of eventual LVEF normalization. Sex, age at diagnosis, type of cardiomyopathy, intensive care unit length of stay, and use of hydrochlorothiazide did not predict LVEF normalization. Multivariate analysis showed that patients in the HFmrEF group had a significantly greater likelihood of LVEF normalization than those in the HFrEF group during the 5‐year follow‐up (adjusted HR, 1.40 [95% CI, 1.07–1.82]; P=0.013). The presence of LBBB, larger LVEDD Z score, and use of β blockers were independent predictors of poor prognosis (adjusted HR, 0.35 [95% CI, 0.20–0.62]; P<0.001; adjusted HR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.52–0.85]; P=0.001; adjusted HR, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.54–0.87] P=0.002, respectively; Table 2, Figure 3B through 3D).

Of all the patients enrolled, 204 (62.4%) were diagnosed with LVNC, 70 (21.4%) with DCM, and 53 (16.2%) with EFE. The Kaplan–Meier curves revealed no significant difference in LVEF normalization among the different types of cardiomyopathy (Figure 4).

Genetic Testing

A total of 83 participants (26.3% of all enrolled patients) underwent optional genetic testing. Genetic changes were detected in 54 patients (65.1% of tested subjects); all were associated with gene mutations except 1 patient with a chromosomal abnormality. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics standards and guidelines, a total of 13 pathogenic variants were found in 12 patients in TNNT2, MYH7, MYBPC3, ACTC1, and CASZ1. Twenty‐three likely pathogenic variants were found in 21 patients in TTN, TNNT2, MYH7, MYBPC3, ACTC1, TPM1, and NOTCH1. There were another 34 variants of uncertain significance found in 20 patients in a multitude of genes. Overall, 39.8% of patients with cardiomyopathies had a positive genetic result including pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, and another 24.1% had a variant of uncertain significance. Diagnostic yield at LVNC, DCM, and EFE were 46.8%, 33.3%, and 25%, respectively (Table 3). One patient with LVNC had a chromosomal abnormality.

Discussion

This is the first large, prospective, multicenter cohort study evaluating the outcomes of infants aged <1 year diagnosed with cardiomyopathy‐associated chronic HF. As the participating heart centers are among the largest in northern China for pediatric HF management, our findings likely reflect the general population in this region. Most patients followed beyond 1 year exhibited significant recovery of LVEF. Independent negative predictors of LVEF normalization included baseline LVEF <40%, LVEDD Z score ≥7.8, presence of LBBB, and the use of β blockers.

Chronic HF is a significant cause of death and morbidity in adults, but its association with outcomes in infants and children is not well understood. Patients with <1 year's follow‐up visits were excluded from this study; therefore, we did not study acute HF in this age group. This might explain why our patients with HFmrEF were generally younger than those with HFrEF, as the younger‐aged patients with HFrEF might have experienced early death or being lost to follow‐up <1 year at our heart centers. Fifty‐six patients with <1 year's follow‐up visits were excluded from this study. It was challenging to reach the family or referral physician to verify the patients' health status. In China, electronic medical records and report system are not available for a lot of family doctors and small or community hospitals to share the clinical notes between providers. As the 2 big pediatric heart centers in China, we had busy and overwhelmed clinic workflow every day. Our study principal investigators and coinvestigators are physicians and fellows who see patients nationwide with an average of 30 daily outpatient cases per provider and annual 1600 (in Qingdao) or 4000 (in Beijing) hospitalized patients. We rely on patients’ families for maintaining their follow‐up visits and updating medical records between providers in different locations. This might also explain our finding that although 72.2% of the infants in our study initially exhibited severely reduced cardiac function (LVEF <40%), only 3 deaths occurred, resulting in a low mortality rate (0.92%) compared with previous pediatric HF studies reporting rates ranging from 3% to 7%. 5 , 13 Our findings suggest that younger children with HF may have a better prognosis than older children, which is consistent with previous literature. 5 , 13 , 14 , 15 Although patients aged <1 year may have a better prognosis, those aged <1 month may be particularly vulnerable. For example, a national population‐based study conducted in Australia reported that patients diagnosed at age <4 weeks had a higher risk of death or requiring heart transplantation. 5 This is consistent with the findings in our study showing a higher mortality rate (4.8%) in patients diagnosed at age <1 month when compared with those aged >1 month (0.65%). In China, heart transplantation and ventricular assist device therapies are not available options for pediatric patients with cardiomyopathy‐associated HF, so we might expect the mortality rate of infants and children with severe cardiomyopathy‐associated acute HFs to be higher. There may be additional risk factors specifically associated with the neonatal period (such as maternal factors, genetic and metabolic diseases, and electrolyte disturbances) that were not delineated in this study.

In our study, 87.2% (285/327) of infants with HF demonstrated improvement in cardiac function during follow‐up, with 90.5% of patients enrolled at <1 month achieving LVEF normalization. These findings are higher than previous reports where only 16% to 63% of children with DCM had cardiac improvement. 15 , 16 , 17 In addition to patient selection bias (our study excluded those who were not followed out to at least 1 year), another possibility for this disparity may be due to differences in the definition of cardiac improvement. Our study defined cardiac improvement on the basis of LVEF normalization by echocardiography, while other studies also included normalization of LV size and resolution of HF symptoms as criteria for cardiac improvement. We used the Z score system from Boston Children's Hospital to evaluate cardiac enlargement and track changes in Z scores over time for Chinese children, as there is no population‐specific Z score system available in China. However, we did not use it as a standard for normalizing heart size. As has been suggested by previous studies, improvement in cardiac function following treatment may reflect remission rather than complete recovery. 18 In our study, 37 patients had a relapse following initial normalization of LVEF, and further investigation is needed to assess these subgroups.

Previous studies have identified several factors associated with improved outcomes in children with HF, including younger age, lower LVEDD Z scores, better LV systolic and diastolic function, higher fractional shortening measurements, and less severe mitral regurgitation at presentation. 5 , 15 , 17 , 19 , 20 , 21 Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that higher LVEF and lower LVEDD Z scores at diagnosis were independent predictors of heart function recovery. Age at diagnosis did not impact the probability of LVEF normalization in our study, likely due to a narrow age range (<1 year) among all enrolled children. Although various echocardiographic parameters have been used as predictors of cardiac recovery, we chose LVEF because it is the most used functional index by cardiologists in China.

Previous studies have also reported that prolonged QRS complex duration in ECG signal at baseline is a strong predictor of sudden cardiac death in children with DCM. 22 In our study, LBBB significantly predicted a poorer prognosis in children with cardiomyopathy‐associated HF. This observation is consistent with previous studies suggesting LBBB as an unfavorable prognostic marker in patients with chronic HF, independent of age, HF severity, and medical management. 23 , 24 , 25 Furthermore, some studies propose LBBB may be the cause of cardiac dilation and dysfunction. 24 , 25

LVNC was classified as a distinct form of cardiomyopathy in 2006 and accounts for up to about 5% to 9% of pediatric cardiomyopathies. 26 , 27 In our study, 62.4% of patients were diagnosed with LVNC, which is significantly higher than previous reports. One possible explanation for this variation is that many patients in our cohort who met diagnostic criteria for LVNC also fulfilled the criteria for DCM. Moreover, some patients who barely met the noncompacted/compacted ratio criterion for LVNC may have been classified as DCM in earlier studies. Alternatively, there could be an inherent higher incidence of LVNC relative to other types of cardiomyopathies in the specific Northern Chinese population we investigated. Our study found no significant difference in prognosis between patients with LVNC and those with other types of cardiomyopathies. However, each criterion used for diagnosis of LVNC has limitations. 28 We used the Jenni criteria in our study because they are the most widely used. A UK study conducted in 2008 used 3 echocardiographic criteria for identifying LVNC and suggested that current diagnostic criteria are overly sensitive, potentially leading to overdiagnosis of LVNC. 28 Furthermore, prior research suggests that infants have a higher proportion of LVNC than other age groups. 27 It has been speculated that the myocardium still has the potential to develop or remodel after birth, and it remains unclear whether LVNC during this period should be definitely considered on pathological changes. 29 The high proportion of patients with LVNC is an interesting new finding from this study, indicating the potential need for further research.

Our study was not designed to assess the impact of treatment strategies. Globally, medical therapy, heart transplant, and ventricular assist device therapy are considered for severe cases of cardiomyopathy; however, due to donor shortages and limited ventricular assist device usage, these therapies are not available in China. In our cohort, IVIG and glucocorticoids were commonly used in addition to conventional treatments (such as diuretics, ACEIs, and β blockers), especially for severe cases. Potential side effects associated with IVIG or glucocorticoid use, including allergic reactions, blood‐borne infections, headache, refractory infections, obesity, muscle atrophy, ecchymosis, skin thinning, acne, and osteoporosis were not identified during long‐term follow‐up. Previous studies have suggested immune mechanisms in cardiomyopathy, especially maternal autoantibody‐mediated cardiomyopathy, which may explain the therapeutic effect of immunotherapy. 30 In our study, IVIG and glucocorticoids were found to be negatively correlated with the prognosis of HF. In addition, loop diuretics were commonly used in patients with more severe presentation, which may explain why their use was a negative predictor of LVEF normalization in our study. Further research is needed to evaluate these drugs' efficacy in treating cardiomyopathy‐associated chronic HF. It is worth noting that we did not withdraw pharmacological treatment completely during the follow‐up, even after patients returned to normal cardiac function. All patients were maintained with a low dosage of β blocker or ACEIs as chronic therapy.

Genetic testing of children with cardiomyopathy has the potential of providing a definitive diagnosis, which may inform prognosis. 31 , 32 In our cohort, a total of 83 participants (26.3% of all enrolled patients) underwent optional genetic testing. Genetic changes were detected in 54 patients (65.1% of tested subjects). Pathogenic variants, likely pathogenic variants, or variants of uncertain significance were detected in 53 patients, and another patient with LVNC had a chromosomal abnormality. The mutations found were consistent with those characterized in previously published studies, including TNNT2, MYH7, and MYBPC3. 31 , 32 Mutations were also found in less reported genes including ACTC1 and CASZ1. The percentage of patients with cardiomyopathies with positive genetic analysis results (39.8%) in our study was much higher than a recent analysis with a reported 18% positive genetic results rate in patients diagnosed with cardiomyopathies as infants. 32 Due to the small subset of patients who underwent genetic testing in our study, this could reflect selection bias, and more comprehensive genetic testing would be needed to determine if this population has a higher‐than‐expected genetic pathogenesis. In our population, genetic testing came at the full out‐of‐pocket expense of patients' families, which both limited the number of patients who underwent genetic testing and may lead to selection bias. Also note that different ancestries had different positive genetic results rates. 32 Further studies to evaluate the clinical diagnoses and genetic causes of childhood cardiomyopathy and outcomes of cascade genetic testing in family members are needed to better understand the pathogenesis of cardiomyopathies and effectively treat the cardiomyopathy‐associated chronic HF.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that may impact data interpretation. The primary limitation was that we included only patients who were followed up for at least 1 year after diagnosis, which prevents us from assessing acute HF outcomes. It was challenging to reach families of patients who were not followed up to 1 year, so we were unable to determine the number of deaths within the first year after HF diagnosis. Socioeconomic factors may have prevented some families from accessing tertiary hospitals, leading to underestimation of total cases and death if affected children died before receiving HF treatment. While echocardiography quality control measures exist, the subjective nature of some measurements, such as LVEF and LVEDD, may introduce bias into the data. Although the 5‐year follow‐up period provides sufficient duration to evaluate trends, this time frame is still relatively short, and further follow‐up studies are needed to assess long‐term trends. Family history of heritable conditions and genetic testing were available for only a few children, so these variables were not included in the analysis.

Conclusions

Our large, prospective, observational cohort study demonstrates that a considerable proportion of infants with cardiomyopathy who survived >1 year respond positively to medical therapies during the first year of diagnosis. Decreased LVEF and increased LVEDD Z scores at presentation, presence of LBBB, and use of β blockers were independently associated with poorer outcomes. Furthermore, we observed a notably high incidence of LVNC relative to other subtypes of cardiomyopathy in the northern Chinese pediatric population.
