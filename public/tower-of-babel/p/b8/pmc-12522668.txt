Rap2B-mediated reprogramming of the PI3K/AKT signaling axis drives resistance to cetuximab-targeted therapy in colorectal carcinoma
Authors: Shanshan Zhang, Ying Wei, Tingting Liu, Chenhao Sun, Xiuzhen Ge, Yang Ni, Xiaonan Tang, Mridul Roy, Tingmin Chang, Xiaohong Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTargeted therapy resistance represents a significant clinical challenge in cancer treatment. Rap2B, a member of the Ras superfamily of small GTPases, is frequently overexpressed in various cancers and has been implicated in promoting tumor progression and therapy resistance. However, the role and underlying mechanisms of Rap2B in cetuximab resistance in colorectal cancer (CRC) remain to be elucidated. This study aims to investigate Rap2B expression patterns in CRC models and explore the mechanisms by which Rap2B mediates cetuximab resistance.ResultsThe expression level of Rap2B in CRC cell lines exhibits a significant negative correlation with sensitivity to cetuximab. In vivo experiments demonstrated that Rap2B downregulation significantly reduced tumor growth and enhanced cetuximab efficacy. Knockdown of Rap2B increases cetuximab sensitivity by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, resulting in reduced cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis. Conversely, overexpression of Rap2B induces cetuximab resistance by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway.ConclusionThis study highlights a close association between Rap2B overexpression and cetuximab resistance in CRC. The enhanced expression of Rap2B plays a pivotal role in conferring resistance, by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Targeting Rap2B or its downstream pathways has the potential to provide novel therapeutic strategies for overcoming cetuximab resistance in colorectal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12575-025-00303-3.Keywords: Rap2B, Colorectal cancer, Cetuximab resistance, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) is a typical malignancy and one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Patients with CRC face a particularly poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate below10% [2]. Clinically, the current standard treatment for CRC primarily involves local resection of the tumor, supplemented by systemic radiotherapy and chemotherapy [3]. In particular, adjuvant chemotherapy plays a critical role in the treatment of locally advanced and metastatic CRC. Nevertheless, the objective response rate of adjuvant chemotherapy is limited and is often accompanied by significant treatment-related toxicity. Moreover, treatment failure in locally advanced and metastatic patients is frequent due to chemoresistance, which has driven the development of targeted therapies against the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) as a precision treatment strategy.

EGFR exerts a crucial role in the growth and progression of CRC by activating downstream pathways such as RAS/RAF/MEK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR [4]. Based on this mechanism, monoclonal antibody therapies targeting EGFR have showed significant survival benefits in treating metastatic CRC (mCRC), improving patients’ overall survival (OS) [5]. Among various targeted drugs, cetuximab (CET) is the first EGFR inhibitor formally approved for treating mCRC and has demonstrated good clinical efficacy [3]. Cetuximab binds specifically to domain II of the EGFR, inhibiting receptor dimerization and phosphorylation of related kinases, thereby blocking downstream signaling cascades [6], and promoting apoptosis. In addition, cetuximab can reduce the expression of vascular EGFR [7] and DNA repair following radio chemotherapy [8], thereby suppressing tumor growth. Multiple studies have shown that the combination of cetuximab and chemotherapy significantly improves OS and PFS in patients with wild-type RAS mCRC, providing superior outcomes than chemotherapy alone [9–11].

However, despite the significant clinical benefits of cetuximab, 30%−40% of cases with wild-type RAS still exhibit primary resistance, and cetuximab only effective in 60–70% of cases with wild-type RAS/KRAS [12]. This suggests that a considerable proportion of patients do not benefit from cetuximab treatment, indicating the presence of intrinsic resistance mechanisms that reduce the efficacy of the drug. Further research has identified downstream RAS/BRAF mutations in the EGFR signaling pathway, ERBB2 (HER2) amplification, and other bypass activations that hinder the therapeutic effect of cetuximab in colorectal cancer patients. Therefore, testing for RAS/BRAF mutations before administering cetuximab is a standard practice to guide treatment decisions. Additionally, primary resistance may be related to genomic alterations during the development of mCRC, including the loss of mismatch repair genes [13], dysregulation of long non-coding RNA expression [14], mutations in the extracellular domain of EGFR [15], changes in miRNA expression [15], and mutations in RAS/RAF and AT-rich Interaction Domain 1 A (ARID1A) [16]. However, some cases of cetuximab resistance remain unexplained by known mechanisms, suggesting the involvement of novel regulatory factors. Recent studies using single-cell sequencing have revealed the molecular mechanisms and potential salvage strategies of acquired resistance to cetuximab in CRC. These studies suggest that the ERK and SMAD signaling pathways may also drive resistance [17]. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the molecular mechanisms of cetuximab resistance in CRC and the identification of potential targets and therapeutic strategies hold significant clinical importance.

As an important member of the small GTPase family of RAS superfamily, Rap2B (Ras-related protein Rap-2b) was initially discovered from a platelet cDNA library [18] and has exerted diverse roles in tumor biology through its unique palmitoylation modification (Cys180/181 sites). Rap2B is involved in the progression of multiple malignancies, including gliomas, lung, breast, prostate, and renal cancers [19–23]. It can induce platelet aggregation, promote cancer cell adhesion to endothelial cells, trigger cell invasion and migration, angiogenesis, and other biological functions, and promote cancer progression by activating signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and FAK. Recently, researchers have focused on investigating the role of Rap2B in modulating drug resistance. For example, inhibiting Rap2B can sensitize colorectal cancer cells to doxorubicin-induced apoptosis, which suggests that Rap2B enhances resistance to doxorubicin in CRC cells [24]. Moreover, the palmitoylation of Rap2B is crucial for its localization on the cell membrane and its role in cell invasion, which can promote the metastasis of CRC cells through the PI3K/AKT pathway [25]. However, the role of Rap2B in cetuximab resistance in colorectal cancer remains unclear, providing a theoretical basis for our study.

This study investigates the key regulatory role of Rap2B in cetuximab resistance in colorectal cancer. Mechanistic analyses have revealed that Rap2B is overexpressed in colorectal cancer cells with primary resistance to cetuximab and promotes resistance to EGFR-targeted therapy by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, as demonstrated in experimental models. These findings not only elucidate a novel mechanism underlying cetuximab resistance but also provide a potential therapeutic strategy for colorectal cancer patients with high Rap2B expression, offering new treatment approaches to overcome resistance to targeted therapy.

Materials and methods

Specimens cell culture

Tissue specimens from colorectal cancer patients with drug resistance and sensitivity were provided by the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University. All specimen collections were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University, and written informed consent was obtained from patients prior to collection. Human colon cancer cell lines RKO and SW48 were used to conduct the cell culture experiments, obtained from the Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Company. Cells were incubated in high-glucose DMEM medium (Zhongqiao Xinzhou, China) containing 10% FBS (Royacel, China), 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). CRC cell lines HT29 and Caco-2 were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Science’s Cell Bank. HT29 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Pricella, China) added 10% FBS, 100 µg/ml penicillin-streptomycin (Procell, China). Caco-2 cells were cultured in MEM medium (Pricella, China) added 20% FBS. All cells were cultured at standard conditions in a humidified incubator.

Antibodies and reagents

Cetuximab was purchased from Merck Millipore, Germany. The PI3K/AKT inhibitor Uprosertib (GSK2141795) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Primary antibodies against Rap2B (ab101369), PI3K (600225-1-Ag), p-PI3K (Y607), AKT (2090 S), p-AKT (4040s), MAPK (4695T), p-MAPK (9101 S), ERK (4695 S), p-ERK (AP0235), Bcl-2 (ab182858), Bax (WL101637), Caspase-3 (ab32351), and GAPDH (AP0063) were bought from Abways (Shanghai, China), Abcam (Cambridge, UK), Cell Signaling Technology (USA), Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA), Abclonal (Woburn, MA), Bioworld (Dublin, OH, USA), and Wanlei (Wuhan, China). All reagents were stored following the instructions.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‑PCR)

Total RNA was extracted via relevant RNAiso reagent (Takara Bio, Japan). The NovoScriptPlus cDNA Synthesis kit (Novoprotein, China) was used to synthesize cDNA following the relevant instructions. qRT-PCR was conducted for three times based on SYBR Green detection kit (Novoprotein, China). The primers used in this study are as follows: GAPDH: Forward: 5′-TCAAGAAGGTGGTGAAGCAGG-3′; Reverse:5′-TCAAAGGTGGAGGAGTGGGT-3′; Rap2B Forward: 5′-CCACCCTCATCCTGTCCAA-3′; Reverse: 5′-CTTCGTCCGCTCCTTCTCTT-3′. Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to the internal reference (GAPDH) using the 2−(ΔΔCt) method. The ΔΔCt was obtained via ΔCt (treated) − ΔCt (control).

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (NCM Biotech, China) Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Epizyme, China). Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto a 0.2 μm PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore, Germany) at a transfer current of 200 mA. The membrane was blocked in TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.5% Tween-20) with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h, the membrane was washed in TBST and preserved for signal detection. And the result was visualized via the Amersham Imager 600 system (Cytiva, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software. GAPDH level was used as baseline.

Lentivirus construction and transfection

A lentiviral vector carrying the PCR product covering the Rap2B open reading frame (Rap2B-Homo, GenePharma, China) was stably transfected into HT29 cells using Polybrene (GenePharma, China). Overexpression of Rap2B was confirmed by the Western blot method. Three shRNA sequences targeting Rap2B (Rap2B-Homo-919, 543, 785) were stably transfected into RKO cells via the Polybrene (GenePharma, China). The shRNA sequence with highest knockdown efficiency (56%) was selected for stable silencing of Rap2B in RKO cells for in vivo studies.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Kermey, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In the detection process, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. After proper treatments, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The microplate reader was applied to measure OD value at 450 nm. Each determination was conducted three times. Dose-response curves were plotted after cetuximab treatment to determine the IC50 values.

The EdU staining

Cell proliferation was evaluated based on the EdU Kit (Beyotime, China) as the instructions. Specifically, colorectal cancer cells were cultured with EdU (10 µM) for 2 h at 37 °C. Next, 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, China) was applied to fix cell for 15 min, and permeabilize for 15 min, and subjected to the Click reaction solution at 25℃ in the dark for 0.5 h. Nuclei were stained with 1× Hoechst solution (Beyotime, China) for 10 min at 25℃ without light. Fluorescence images were captured via the Axio microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) at 400× magnification. Cells undergoing DNA replication exhibited red fluorescence, while nuclei were stained blue.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (Servicebio, China) and cultured for 2 weeks under various treatments until colonies formed. Before counting, cells were fixed with 4% tissue cell fixative (Solarbio, China) for 0.5 h and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images were captured and processed via ImageJ software. Each determination was performed in triplicate.

Annexin V-APC/PI apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was detected using a BD FACS Calibur™ Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Annexin V-APC Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) was used following the relevant instructions. In this experiment, 5 × 10⁵ cells were resuspended in 500 µl of Binding Buffer, followed by adding Annexin V-APC reagent (5 µl) and PI reagent (5 µl). Cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min before analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Xenografts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, then sectioned at 4 μm. After deparaffinization, and blocking of peroxidase activity, sections were cultured with anti-Ki67 antibody (Abcam, UK) for 12 h at 4 °C. After rehydration, sections were stained with secondary antibody and DAB (ZSGB-BIO, China). Briefly, results were evaluated by independent pathologists based on the ratio of positively stained cells and staining intensity using the following method: staining intensity was graded on a scale of 0–3 (0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong), while the ratio of positive cells was graded on a scale of 0–4 (0, negative; 1, 1–25% positive; 2, 26–50%; 3, 51–75%; 4, 76–100%). The scores for ratio and intensity were multiplied, the obtained results were categorized as positive (total score ≥ 4) or negative (total score < 4).

The TUNEL analysis

Apoptosis was assessed via the TUNEL kit (Beyotime, China) method. Cells were fixed for 0.5 h and permeabilized via Triton to allow TdT and dUTP to enter the cells. The reaction mixture was added and incubated for the specified time following the relevant instructions. The reaction was terminated by PBS. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclear morphology. Apoptotic cells were identified by fluorescence microscopy, with positive cells displaying distinct fluorescent signals. Apoptosis rate was determined as the ratio of positive cells.

In vivo xenograft studies

All experiment procedures were approved by the Committee of Henan Medical University. Corresponding cell suspension was injected into the right abdominal cavity of nude mice (4 weeks old, 5.0 × 106 cells per mouse). The tumor size was measured using a precision Vernier caliper. Once the tumor volume reaches approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly divided into two groups: a control group and a treatment group. Each group contained 5–6 mice. The treatment dose of cetuximab was 10 mg/kg, administered via intraperitoneal injection on an every-other-day basis. The dosage of the PI3K/AKT inhibitor GSK2141795 (MCE, USA) was 10 mg/kg. GSK2141795 was formulated in DMSO using 15% SBE-β-CD (Captisol) as a carrier, with a maximum drug concentration of 10%. The administration of the substance was conducted via oral gavage for a period of five days, encompassing a two-week duration. The maximum volume permitted for gavage was set at 200 µl per mouse. Tumor growth was monitored every 2 days, the mice were sacrificed after 2 weeks. Subcutaneous tumor tissues were dissected and measured for final size. Subsequently, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed on tumor tissues to assess cell proliferation markers (Ki-67) and other relevant indicators. Additionally, to detect changes in apoptosis levels in tumor tissues, apoptosis of colorectal cancer cells was evaluated via TUNEL kit (Servicebio, China) following the relevant instructions, and fluorescence images were obtained via a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Statistical analysis

The GraphPad Prism 10.0 software was applied for data analysis. All data were expressed as mean ± SD. Differences in two groups were analyzed using the independent t-test. The ANOVA was applied to check the Differences among multiple groups, or post-hoc tests. The statistical difference level was set as 0.05.

Results

Rap2B expression levels correlate with cetuximab sensitivity in colorectal cancer cells

The present study aimed to investigate the effect of Rap2B expression on the sensitivity of CRC cells to cetuximab in vitro. Four RAS/KRAS wild-type CRC cell Lines were treated with a multi-concentration gradient of cetuximab ranging from 0 to 4 mg/ml for a period of 48 h, and the IC50 values were determined using CCK-8 assay (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, Rap2B expression at protein and mRNA levels was quantified in these CRC cell lines. Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses (Figs. 1B and C) revealed that Rap2B expression was highest in RKO cells and was lowest in HT29 and Caco-2 cells. The findings suggested a correlation between CRC cell sensitivity to cetuximab and Rap2B expression levels. Notably, Caco-2 cells (IC50 = 0.85 mg/ml) and HT29 cells (IC50 = 1.48 mg/ml) with low Rap2B expression showed higher sensitivity to cetuximab, whereas RKO cells (IC50 = 1.90 mg/ml) with high Rap2B expression exhibited pronounced resistance (Fig. 1A). This findings indicated that higher Rap2B expression is closely related to reduced cetuximab sensitivity in CRC cells. To further validate this observation, immunohistochemistry experiments were performed on tissue samples from colorectal cancer patients resistant and sensitive to cetuximab. The results showed that, in comparison with relatively sensitive samples, Rap2B expression was considerably elevated in colorectal cancer samples that were resistant to cetuximab (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table 1).

Based on these findings and considering the growth characteristics and differences in Rap2B expression levels among the cell lines, we selected RKO and HT29 cells for constructing gene regulation models. Lentivirus (LV)-mediated Rap2B overexpression vectors were stably transfected into HT29 cells to enhance Rap2B expression (Fig. 1E, G, I). Meanwhile, three short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated Rap2B constructs were stably transfected into RKO cells to induce Rap2B knockdown (Fig. 1F, H, J). Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses confirmed the transfection efficacy and validated the successful establishment of Rap2B overexpressing and knockdown CRC cell lines. Among the cell lines, sh-Rap2B#3 demonstrated the most significant knockdown efficiency, and this cell line was used for subsequent experiments (Fig. 1E-J).

High Rap2B expression promotes cetuximab resistance in CRC cells in vitro

To clarify the correlation between Rap2B levels and CET resistance in CRC cells, this study compared the viability of HT29 cells and their Rap2B-overexpressing counterparts under different doses of cetuximab (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 mg/ml). The CCK-8 assay results showed that after exposure to different concentrations of cetuximab, the viability of Rap2B-overexpressing CRC cells was significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.001; Fig. 2A, B). In long-term drug exposure experiments, the colony formation ability of Rap2B-overexpressing cells was obviously enhanced to 129.97 ± 13.54% compared to the control group (p < 0.05; Fig. 2C). The EdU assays showed that Rap2B-overexpressing cells exhibited a significantly higher proliferation rate (44.08 ± 1.79%) compared with the control group (25.64 ± 2.76%; p < 0.001; Fig. 2D).To assess cetuximab-induced apoptosis differences, we performed flow cytometry analysis after APC/PI staining. The results showed that, after 24 h of cetuximab treatment, the total apoptosis rate of Rap2B-overexpressing CRC cells (11.69 ± 1.33%) was significantly lower than that of the control group (15.14 ± 1.39%) (p < 0.05; Fig. 2E). Western blot analysis further showed that the expression level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was significantly elevated in Rap2B-overexpressing cells, while the expression levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and Cleaved Caspase-3/Caspase-3 were significantly reduced (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Figure S1A). These results indicate that high Rap2B expression significantly enhances cetuximab resistance in CRC cells in vitro.

Rap2B knockdown enhances sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to cetuximab

To further elucidate the association between Rap2B and cetuximab resistance in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, we used an shRNA-mediated knockdown model in RKO cells. The CCK-8 assay results showed that, under cetuximab treatment, CRC cells with low Rap2B expression exhibited significantly enhanced sensitivity to cetuximab compared to wild-type RKO cells (p<0.01; Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, clonogenic assays revealed that the number of colonies formed by Rap2B-knockdown CRC cells reduced to 90.87% of the control group (p<0.05; Fig. 3C). EdU proliferation assays demonstrated that the proliferation rate of Rap2B-knockdown CRC cells decreased to 15.38%, significantly lower than the control group (22.94 ± 2.17%, p < 0.01; Fig. 3D). Flow cytometry (APC/PI staining) analysis showed that, after 48 h of treatment with 1.90 mg/ml cetuximab, the total apoptosis rate of Rap2B-knockdown CRC cells reached 10.25 ± 1.10%, obviously higher than the control group (8.33 ± 0.27%, p < 0.05; Fig. 3E). Western blot analysis showed that the Bcl-2 protein expression was significantly reduced in Rap2B-knockdown cells, while the protein levels of Bax and Caspase-3 were significantly enhanced (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Figure S1B). These findings demonstrate that Rap2B knockdown significantly enhances the sensitivity of CRC cells to cetuximab, highlighting its important role in the mechanisms of cetuximab resistance in CRC.

Rap2B knockdown enhances the cetuximab sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells in vivo

To explore the biological effect of Rap2B in CRC cells and its impact on sensitivity to cetuximab in vivo, we subcutaneously injected shRNA-mediated Rap2B knockdown RKO cells (sh-Rap2B group) and control cells (sh-Ctrl group) into nude mice. Once the tumor volume reached 100 ± 10 mm³, intraperitoneal injections of cetuximab were initiated and continued for 2 weeks, with tumor growth monitored every 2 days (Fig. 4A). Statistical analysis of tumor index indicated that tumors from the sh-Rap2B group were significantly smaller in both volume and weight compared to the control group, with cetuximab significantly inhibiting tumor growth (Fig. 4B, C, D). Additionally, the volume of xenografts derived from sh-Rap2B cells treated with cetuximab was noticeably smaller compared to that of control cell-derived tumors (0.53 ± 0.18 cm³ vs. 0.93 ± 0.31 cm³, Fig. 4B), indicating that Rap2B knockdown significantly enhances CRC cell sensitivity to cetuximab. TUNEL assays showed apoptosis rates of 0.21 ± 0.19% (sh-Ctrl), 3.41 ± 0.81% (sh-Ctrl + CET), 1.27 ± 0.35% (sh-Rap2B), and 10.60 ± 0.87% (sh-Rap2B + CET) in tumor tissues from the respective groups (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, Immunohistochemistry staining of the proliferation marker Ki-67 and apoptosis-related cleaved Caspase-3 in subcutaneous tumor tissues revealed lower Ki-67 and cleaved Caspase-3 expression levels in Rap2B-knockdown tumors treated with cetuximab, suggesting reduced malignancy (Fig. 4F).

These results indicate that Rap2B knockdown significantly enhances in vivo sensitivity of CRC cells to cetuximab, characterized by reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis. These in vivo findings are consistent with in vitro results, collectively supporting the close correlation between Rap2B expression and cetuximab sensitivity in CRC cells, with Rap2B knockdown enhancing cetuximab sensitivity.

Rap2B promotes cetuximab resistance in colorectal cancer cells through the PI3K/AKT pathway

To clarify the potential molecular mechanisms underlying Rap2B-mediated cetuximab resistance in CRC cells, we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis on cells from the sh-Rap2B and sh-Ctrl group after treatment with cetuximab to identify genes with significantly different expression levels between the two groups. The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway (Fig. 5A). Previous studies indicate that Rap2B promotes oncogenesis through various pathways in different cancers: in renal cell carcinoma, Rap2B activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to promote tumor growth [22]; in breast cancer, it enhances cell proliferation, migration through the MAPK/ERK pathway [21]; in glioma, Rap2B upregulates MMP2 expression via the ERK pathway [26]; and in hepatocellular carcinoma, Rap2B knockdown inhibits the PI3K/AKT pathways, suppressing tumor progression [27]. In light of the aforementioned findings, we hypothesized that Rap2B may serve as a mediator of cetuximab resistance in colorectal cancer cells exhibiting high Rap2B expression through the PI3K/AKT and/or the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway.

To test this hypothesis, Western blot was used to analyze the levels of the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways in CRC cells with varying Rap2B expression under cetuximab treatment (Fig. 5B, C). Results showed that in cetuximab-treated cells overexpressing Rap2B, p-PI3K and p-AKT levels increased by 84.26% and 52.48%, respectively (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Figure S2A). Conversely, in cetuximab-treated cells with low Rap2B expression, p-PI3K and p-AKT levels decreased to 65.67% and 38.30% of the control group respectively, whereas p-ERK remained unchanged (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Figure S2B). This phenomenon yielded consistent results in animal tissue samples (Fig. 5F). These findings suggest that Rap2B promotes CET resistance in CRC cells primarily through the PI3K/AKT pathway, consistent with recent discoveries in understanding cetuximab resistance mechanisms [28].

To further explore the causal relationship between Rap2B-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling and cetuximab resistance, we treated Rap2B-overexpressing HT29 cells with the AKT-specific inhibitor GSK2141795 (20 µM) and assessed its effects on PI3K/AKT pathway markers. GSK2141795 co-treatment augmented the inhibitory effect of cetuximab on PI3K/AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 5G, H). Furthermore, we investigated cetuximab sensitivity in combination with GSK2141795. The results showed that GSK2141795 treatment significantly enhanced cetuximab sensitivity (p < 0.001; Fig. 6A), reduced colony formation by 23.35 ± 9.24% (p < 0.001; Fig. 6B), and decreased EdU-positive proliferating cells from 26.30 ± 0.95% to 7.80 ± 2.01% (p < 0.001; Fig. 6C). Annexin V-APC/PI staining confirmed that GSK2141795 reversed the decrease in apoptosis rate caused by Rap2B overexpression (10.35 ± 0.32% vs. 14.11 ± 1.62%, p < 0.01; Fig. 6D). Western blot results also showed that GSK2141795 downregulated Bcl-2 and upregulated Bax (Fig. 6E, Supplementary Figure S2C). These results indicate that GSK2141795 can restore the decreased cetuximab sensitivity caused by Rap2B overexpression, further confirming that Rap2B promotes cetuximab resistance through the PI3K/AKT pathway.

To further validate the role of the PI3K/AKT signaling axis in promoting colorectal cancer cell resistance to cetuximab in vivo, a mouse xenograft model was established using RKO cells, and the PI3K/AKT pathway was selectively inhibited using GSK2141795. The results demonstrated that, compared to cetuximab monotherapy, the combination of cetuximab with GSK2141795 resulted in a substantial reduction in both tumor volume(0.61 ± 0.04 c m3 vs. 1.66 ± 0.37 c m3; P < 0.001; Fig. 6F, G) and tumor weight (0.39 ± 0.44 g vs. 0.20 ± 1.46 g; P < 0.001; Fig. 6H). Furthermore, the tumor growth inhibition rate (TGI) in the combination therapy group reached 80.00%, compared to 60.03% with cetuximab alone. These findings suggest that suppression of the PI3K/AKT axis significantly enhances the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to cetuximab, resulting in more pronounced antitumor effects in vivo.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that in CRC cells, high Rap2B expression promotes cetuximab resistance by aberrantly activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Wild-type RAS colorectal cancers (CRCs) refers to tumors in which the RAS family (including KRAS and NRAS) is not mutated and maintains a normal genetic sequence. Patients with this type of CRC can benefit from anti-epidermal growth factor receptor monoclonal antibody therapies, such as cetuximab and panitumumab. Cetuximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting EGFR, enhances receptor internalization and degradation and inhibits downstream signaling activation, playing a significant role in the treatment of metastatic, recurrent CRC. Nevertheless, resistance to cetuximab remains a major clinical challenge, with mechanisms including EGFR gene mutations, RAS/RAF/PI3K mutations, and activation of ERBB2/MET/IGF-1R [29]. Additionally, changes in immune cells [30], cancer-related fibroblasts [31], and angiogenesis [32] within the tumor microenvironment can also affect cetuximab efficacy. However, these mechanisms do not fully explain cetuximab resistance in metastatic CRC. Therefore, investigating new resistance mechanisms is a critical research direction for developing novel therapeutic targets to improve patient survival and quality of life.

Rap2B, an important member of the RAS superfamily of GTPases, exhibits oncogenic properties in various cancers and is closely associated with treatment resistance. Previous studies have shown that Rap2B is highly expressed in CRC, particularly in cases with lymph node metastasis and mCRC, and is linked to poor prognosis [25]. Our study found that Rap2B expression levels in CRC cells significantly correlate with cetuximab sensitivity. Rap2B overexpression significantly enhances cetuximab resistance, while Rap2B knockdown increases drug sensitivity. These results suggest that Rap2B may regulate cetuximab efficacy by modulating cell proliferation and apoptosis-related pathways. Notably, this phenomenon was also validated in an in vivo subcutaneous xenograft mouse model. These findings improve our understanding of the molecular basis of cetuximab resistance and provide potential therapeutic strategies for patients with high Rap2B expression. Additionally, the role of Rap2B in cancer cell response to chemotherapeutic drugs suggests it may serve as a biomarker to predict the efficacy of targeted therapies and support the development of new therapeutic targets.

The impaired sensitivity of cancer cells overexpressing Rap2B to targeted therapies is closely associated with the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. This pathway is an important intracellular signaling network involved in regulating cell survival, metabolism, and migration. In CRC, activation of this pathway has been linked to various biological behaviors. For instance, GNG2 (γ-subunit 2) inhibits CRC cell proliferation and metastasis by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway and inducing cell cycle arrest [33]. Krüppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) inhibits the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) capabilities of CRC cells by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway [34]. Additionally, various herbal extracts, such as Patrinia villosa Juss. (P.V) [35], evodiamine [36] and isoliquiritigenin [37], inhibit this pathway and effectively induces apoptosis in CRC cells, thereby suppressing proliferation. Platycodin D enhances the sensitivity of CRC cells to cetuximab by blocking the PI3K/AKT pathway [38]. Our study identified a novel mechanism in which Rap2B overexpression induces CET resistance in CRC cells via PI3K/AKT pathway activation. Furthermore, the AKT-specific inhibitor GSK2141795 reversed the resistance caused by Rap2B overexpression, further confirming that Rap2B-mediated PI3K/AKT activation leads to primary cetuximab resistance in CRC cells. These findings suggest that targeting Rap2B or the downstream PI3K/AKT pathway could provide new therapeutic strategies to overcome cetuximab resistance in CRC. For example, the AKT inhibitor GSK2141795 shows potential in reversing resistance, offering new ideas for combination therapies.

Despite the findings of the present study highlight the critical role of Rap2B in the context of colorectal cancer drug resistance, it is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of the study. First, our study employed in vitro cell experiments and animal models, and has not yet conducted a comprehensive analysis of clinical samples. Despite the execution of preliminary validation through the analysis of supplementary clinical cases, the sample size remains constrained, thereby precluding complete reflection of the association between Rap2B expression and cetuximab resistance within clinical settings. Consequently, subsequent studies should augment the scale of clinical samples, further explore the expression characteristics of Rap2B in clinical samples and its association with resistance, and evaluate the safety and efficacy of therapies targeting Rap2B or the PI3K/AKT pathway in CRC patients through clinical trials. Second, the role of Rap2B within the tumor microenvironment and its interactions with other resistance-related pathways remain to be elucidated. Further investigation in these domains may yield novel strategies and insights for overcoming CRC resistance. Moreover, while the palmitoylation of Rap2B is imperative for its functionality, its precise role in resistance mechanisms remains to be elucidated. Future studies should focus on the post-translational modifications of Rap2B, particularly their contribution in the development of resistance, to provide more precise therapeutic targets for the treatment of CRC resistance.

In summary, our study reveal that Rap2B overexpression regulates cetuximab resistance in CRC cells through PI3K/AKT pathway. Targeting Rap2B or the PI3K/AKT pathway could improve CRC patients’ responses to existing treatments and enhance therapeutic outcomes. These findings offer novel insights into the development of novel therapies targeting Rap2B and further highlight its potential importance in personalized treatment strategies for CRC.
