Systematic review and meta-analysis of information source usage: do medical specialists use the best evidence for clinical decision-making?
Authors: Floris S Weller, Sjoerd Repping, Jaap F Hamming, Leti van Bodegom - Vos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine which information sources medical specialists use to answer clinical questions in daily practice and to describe the relative frequency of use for each source.DesignSystematic review with narrative synthesis and meta-analysis.Data sourcesAcademic Search Premier, APA PsycINFO, CINAHL, Emcare, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Embase and PubMed were searched for relevant studies published from 2000 to 1 June 2025.Eligibility criteriaWe included peer-reviewed English-language studies reporting on the frequency of information source usage by medical specialists when addressing clinical questions. Studies reporting usage on a continuous (0–100%) scale were eligible for meta-analysis.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened studies. Data were extracted by one reviewer and checked by a second. Study quality was assessed using the Quality Assessment tool with Diverse Studies tool. A narrative synthesis was conducted for studies that were not eligible for quantitative pooling to summarise patterns in information-seeking behaviour and reported barriers. A random-effects meta-analysis was performed for studies reporting continuous usage percentages and assessing at least four information sources. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach. Potential publication bias was explored descriptively using funnel plots.Results25 studies were included, of which 6 (with 8641 participants) were eligible for meta-analysis. The narrative synthesis of non-pooled studies showed a consistent reliance on standalone information sources and identified barriers to the use of aggregated sources. In the meta-analysis, digital databases such as PubMed were the most frequently used information source (74%, 95% CI 63% to 85%), followed by textbooks (71%, 95% CI 57% to 85%) and consultation with colleagues (43%, 95% CI 15% to 71%). Systematically aggregated sources, including clinical practice guidelines (38%, 95% CI 27% to 49%) and point-of-care websites (49%, 95% CI 17% to 81%), were used less frequently. Sensitivity analyses indicated that pooled estimates were generally robust, although results should be interpreted cautiously given methodological variability across studies.ConclusionsMedical specialists predominantly rely on standalone information sources when addressing clinical questions, while systematically aggregated and interpreted sources such as clinical practice guidelines and point-of-care tools are used less frequently. These findings highlight the need to better understand and address barriers to the use of aggregated information sources in clinical practice.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42022267431.Keywords: Information source management, clinical information, medical specialists, systematic review, Evidence-Based Medicine, digital resources

Introduction

Given the vast and ever-growing amount of data, medical specialists need up-to-date information to facilitate decision-making in patient care.1 2 Access to accurate, reliable and up-to-date information is crucial to providing high-quality medical services for their patients.

Medical specialists, defined as hospital-based physicians who have completed postgraduate training in a recognised medical specialty, often encounter clinical questions that require timely and accurate answers.3 Despite the availability of numerous sources of information, medical specialists often struggle to find satisfactory answers to their questions. Barriers to finding answers in the literature include information overload, inefficient information management practices and difficulties in selecting appropriate information for a specific question from the retrieved information.4,6 These barriers can result in up to 30% of clinical inquiries being unanswered, potentially impacting decision quality and patient outcomes.4 Moreover, not being able to retrieve the ‘right’ answer to a clinical question could lead to misdiagnosis or mistreatment. Interestingly, 90% of medical specialists indicate that they do not actively use any source of information in more than 90% of their medical decisions.7 This could at least partly be due to the aforementioned barriers in rapidly accessing information of good quality.

Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) and other systematically aggregated information sources have been developed to support medical specialists in answering clinical questions without having to individually search and interpret single-source evidence. CPGs summarise the available evidence and offer practical recommendations, but challenges remain, including slow update cycles and poor accessibility.7 8 Point-of-care websites like UpToDate and Cochrane Library offer a more user-friendly alternative, though questions persist about how well they meet the specific needs and search behaviour of medical specialists.9 10

Previous reviews on the use of information sources by medical specialists have focused on qualitatively analysing the information sources used in medical decision-making or identifying barriers and facilitators in the search for information.110,12 The general conclusion was that a variety of information sources are used in clinical practice, with barriers to accessing the best available evidence grouped into five domains: time, access to resources, personal attitudes and information seeker skills, institutional attitudes, culture and policies and features of the knowledge source.10 However, these reviews did not quantitatively assess the frequency with which medical professionals use different information resources. Insight into the sources they most frequently use is essential to tailor future initiatives that aim to facilitate medical specialists in finding answers to their clinical questions.

Our objective was to systematically review the existing literature and to determine how frequently various information resources are used by medical specialists.

Methods

This systematic review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. This systematic review was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD42022267431). The review initially formed part of a broader academic project with an exploratory scope. While the original protocol included both qualitative and quantitative studies, we later refined the scope to include only quantitative research, due to challenges in synthesising qualitative data.12

Search strategy

To identify all eligible studies reporting the frequency of use of information sources by medical specialists in hospitals, a systematic literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Emcare, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science and Google Scholar. The complete search strategy is provided in online supplemental file 1. The search was limited to literature published from January 2000 to June 2025. We limited our search to studies published from January 2000 onward, as the widespread availability and routine use of digital information tools began around this time, improving the comparability of included studies. An experienced health librarian at the Leiden University Medical Centre guided the search. In addition to the initial search, references lists and citations of all included studies were also reviewed to identify further relevant studies.

Selection of studies

Two researchers (FSW and LvB-V) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all studies identified in the search. In cases of disagreement regarding study inclusion, a third reviewer (JFH) was consulted. Rayyan QCRI software was used for the screening process.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they focused on the frequency of use of information sources by medical specialists in hospitals, were published in English and were available in full text. We excluded studies that focused on the use of information sources by primary healthcare professionals or by nursing and paramedical professionals, as our focus was specifically on the frequency of use of information sources by hospital-based medical specialists. Additionally, we limited the inclusion to studies conducted in first-world countries to avoid bias, as medical specialists in developing countries may have limited access to digital information sources. We also included relevant review articles to identify additional original studies that met our inclusion criteria. Editorials, letters, conference abstracts and study protocols were excluded.

Data extraction and quality appraisal

For each included study, we extracted the following using a standardised data abstraction form: year of publication, study goal, study design and data collection method, study setting and subjects, sampling method, background and number of participants, response rate and frequency of use of information sources. Data were initially extracted by one researcher (FSW) and subsequently checked for accuracy and completeness by a second researcher (LvB-V). Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus.

Primary outcomes were the frequency of information sources used by medical specialists in medical decision-making. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment tool with Diverse Studies (QuADS).13 This validated tool is designed to evaluate multimethodological studies in different disciplines. Each included study was reviewed according to 13 criteria, and each criterion was scored from 0 to 3. A percentage score of the maximum possible points was then calculated for each study. This maximum possible point depended on the study design and methodology (see online supplemental file 2). All assessments were performed by one reviewer (FSW), and subsequently checked for consistency and accuracy by a second reviewer (LvB-V). Any uncertainties or differences in interpretation were discussed and resolved by consensus.

In order to clarify our outcomes, digital databases refer to repositories of original biomedical literature such as PubMed, Embase and Medline. These platforms allow clinicians to search for and access primary research articles, but do not offer pre-appraised summaries or clinical recommendations. Digital point-of-care websites refer to clinical tools that provide synthesised, pre-appraised information to support clinical decision-making at the bedside. Examples include UpToDate, Epocrates, Cochrane Library, Micromedex, eMedicine, WebMD, Medscape, BMJ Learning, Doctors.net.uk and AskMayoExpert. These platforms are considered systematically aggregated and interpreted information sources.

Data analysis

To summarise the use of information sources by medical specialists, we first conducted a descriptive synthesis of the data from all included studies in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365 V.2208), reporting the frequency (%) of each information source used to answer clinical questions.

Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted for studies that reported usage on a continuous 0–100% scale. Studies using categorical or Likert-scale data were excluded due to incompatible response formats, which precluded pooling. Although we considered transforming Likert-scale or categorical data into continuous proportions, this was not feasible due to substantial variation in the number and meaning of response categories across studies, the ordinal nature of the data and the lack of a valid or replicable transformation method. These studies were therefore synthesised narratively rather than pooled quantitatively. To ensure meaningful comparison across studies, we only included studies that assessed at least four different information sources, as studies with fewer options were not considered sufficiently comparable. We applied a random-effects model to calculate weighted mean usage percentages and 95% CIs, weighting for study sample size.

Heterogeneity was explored qualitatively by comparing study designs, populations, measurement approaches and the number of information sources assessed. Given the small number of studies per outcome and known limitations of the I² statistic in this context, we did not interpret I² values as indicators of true heterogeneity.14 15 Instead, methodological variability across studies was described narratively, in line with recent guidance. Because fewer than 10 studies contributed to each outcome, we did not apply formal statistical tests for publication bias such as Egger’s test, consistent with methodological recommendations. Funnel plots were generated to visually explore potential publication bias. Given the limited number of studies and the resulting instability of such plots, they are not suitable for formal interpretation and are presented in online supplemental file 3 for transparency only.

Narrative synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted for studies that were not eligible for quantitative pooling, in order to summarise patterns in information-seeking behaviour, reported barriers and sources of methodological variability. This synthesis focused on non-pooled studies to provide contextual insights that could not be captured numerically.

Integration of narrative synthesis and meta-analysis

To complement the quantitative synthesis, we integrated the findings from the narrative synthesis with the meta-analysis. This allowed us to place the pooled usage estimates in the context of the broader patterns observed across the non-pooled studies, taking into account methodological variability and factors that could not be quantified. The integration focused on identifying convergence, divergence and explanatory factors across the two types of evidence. We assessed the certainty of evidence using an adapted Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach suitable for descriptive (non-effect) outcomes, following Cochrane guidance for prevalence and frequency data. The certainty ratings considered risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias and were summarised as low or very low certainty for all pooled outcomes.

Results

Study selection

The initial search, which included forward and backward citation checking, returned a total of 13 556 records. After removing duplicates (n=8422), 5134 records remained. Screening the titles and abstracts for eligibility resulted in the exclusion of 4614 records, leaving 522 records for full-text review. Finally, 25 studies met our selection criteria and were included in this review. After further analysis, only six studies were included in the meta-analysis. Study characteristics and quality analysis

The study characteristics of the 25 articles included in step one of this systematic review are presented in online supplemental table 1.116,42 Most studies focused on unspecified medical specialists, while one study concentrated solely on neurologists.24 The outcomes reported from these studies can be found in online supplemental table 2.

Meta-analysis

After further examination of methodology, outcomes and study quality of each individual study as described in the Methods section, a final six studies could be pooled in our meta-analysis.18 19 24 28 32 42 Two of these six studies were conducted in the USA, one in the UK, one in Canada, one in Taiwan and one in Italy. Five studies were solely surveys and one study was a mixed methods design with a major survey part. The number of respondents ranged from 50 to 3347. The overall quality of the studies was 81.3% as scored by QuADS. To assess the robustness of the pooled results, we conducted a leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis for each information source. For most sources, excluding any single study had minimal impact on the pooled estimates and CIs, indicating overall stability of the findings. An exception was observed for the information source Colleagues: removing Bennett et al18 increased the pooled mean from 52.4% to 62.0% (95% CI 58.5% to 65.6%), suggesting this study had a downward influence on the overall estimate. However, given the small number of studies included per outcome, the results of the sensitivity analysis should be interpreted with caution. Full LOO results are provided in online supplemental file 3.

Frequency of information source usage

We have pooled and presented the data from these studies in figure 2. The most commonly used information sources were digital databases 74% (95% CI 63% to 85%), textbooks 71% (95% CI 57% to 85%) and journals 62% (95% CI 31% to 93%). In contrast, clinical practice guidelines were among the least used, at 38% (95% CI 27% to 49%).

Heterogeneity across included studies

Substantial methodological variation was observed across the studies included in the meta-analysis. Studies differed in the number of information sources assessed, the way usage was measured and the clinical and geographical context of participating specialists. These differences contributed to the wide CIs observed in the pooled estimates. Consistent with methodological guidance, we did not interpret I² values due to the small number of studies per outcome and instead relied on qualitative assessment of heterogeneity.

Certainty of evidence

Using an adapted GRADE approach suitable for descriptive outcomes, the certainty of evidence for all pooled information sources was rated as low or very low. The main reasons for downgrading were methodological heterogeneity, imprecision due to the small number of studies per outcome and reliance on self-reported survey data. A summary of these ratings is provided in online supplemental file 3.

Narrative synthesis

Across the 19 studies that could not be pooled quantitatively, and were therefore synthesised narratively, several consistent patterns emerged regarding how medical specialists seek information in clinical practice. Despite differences in study design, settings and measurement formats, most studies reported a strong reliance on standalone or interpersonal sources such as colleagues, textbooks, journals and digital databases. These sources were described as familiar, easy to access and practical to use during time-pressured clinical encounters.

In contrast, aggregated and interpreted information sources, including clinical practice guidelines and point-of-care tools, were used less frequently. Several studies described barriers such as limited awareness of available guidelines, uncertainty about whether guidelines were up to date, insufficient integration into clinical workflows and perceptions that guideline recommendations were too generic for specialty-specific needs. Point-of-care tools were also not consistently available across institutions, contributing to variation in use.

Information-seeking behaviour also differed across specialties, hospital settings and levels of digital infrastructure. Variation in measurement scales, the number of information sources assessed and the way outcomes were reported reflects substantial methodological diversity across the non-pooled studies.

Integration of narrative synthesis and meta-analysis

Integrating the findings from the narrative synthesis with the results of the meta-analysis revealed both convergence and divergence across the evidence base. There was clear convergence in the overall pattern of information source usage: both narrative and quantitative findings showed that medical specialists predominantly rely on standalone information sources, while aggregated information sources are used less frequently.

Divergence was observed in the magnitude and consistency of reported usage frequencies across studies. The narrative synthesis highlighted both contextual and methodological factors. Contextual factors included variation across clinical settings, specialties and healthcare systems. Methodological factors included differences in measurement formats and the number of information sources assessed. These methodological differences became particularly apparent during the meta-analysis, where they limited pooling and contributed to wide CIs and substantial heterogeneity in the pooled estimates.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides exploratory insights into the frequency with which medical specialists use various information sources in daily practice. Rather than aiming to produce precise pooled estimates, our goal was to map the landscape of information-seeking behaviour using available evidence. This study demonstrates that medical specialists predominantly look for answers themselves when faced with clinical questions, instead of relying on the systematic approach of aggregated and interpreted information sources.

Although only 6 studies met the strict methodological criteria for quantitative pooling, the results of the 19 non-pooled studies were highly consistent with the patterns observed in the meta-analysis. Across both types of evidence, medical specialists most often relied on standalone and interpersonal information sources such as colleagues, journals and digital databases. Aggregated information sources, including clinical practice guidelines and point-of-care tools, were used less frequently. The narrative synthesis also clarified factors that likely contributed to the substantial methodological variation and the wide CIs in the pooled estimates. Examples include differences in measurement formats, the number of information sources assessed, and contextual variation across countries and specialties. By combining the quantitative estimates with the broader findings from the narrative synthesis, this integrated approach offers a more complete understanding of information-seeking behaviour than either method alone. Given the considerable methodological variation across studies, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Recent methodological guidance indicates that I² is not a reliable indicator of heterogeneity when few studies are available.14 15 Consistent with this, the adapted GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence for all pooled outcomes as low or very low, indicating that the pooled proportions should be interpreted as approximate summaries rather than precise estimates.

This integrated evidence base allows for a more nuanced interpretation of the observed usage patterns. A clear illustration of this is the relatively low use of clinical practice guidelines compared with other information sources. These findings reveal an intriguing paradox: although CPGs are theoretically the most reliable sources of evidence-based recommendations that are based on a systematic aggregation and interpretation of evidence by a group of experts (guideline developers), they are among the least used sources, with a usage frequency of only 38% (95% CI 27% to 49%). In contrast, digital databases (eg, PubMed, Embase) were the most frequently used, with a usage rate of 74% (95% CI 63% to 85%). These databases offer access to a vast number of individual articles but do not themselves offer pre-appraised or synthesised information. Their effective use depends on the clinician’s ability to identify, select and interpret relevant evidence, an approach susceptible to risks of confirmation and anchoring biases.43 44 In contrast, clinical practice guidelines and point-of-care tools synthesise and appraise evidence in a structured and accessible way, reducing the risk of bias and supporting consistent decision-making. Additionally, given the limited time and training available for critically appraising evidence, specialists may explore only a small portion of the available literature and conclude their search once they find an answer that satisfies them. Conducting a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of all evidence for each clinical question is impractical and time-consuming.

Our findings align with previous qualitative research on information-seeking behaviour among healthcare professionals, which has shown that clinicians often prefer quickly accessible and familiar sources, even when more comprehensive or evidence-based resources are available.45 This preference for convenience, speed and familiarity may help explain the relatively low use of clinical practice guidelines and point-of-care tools in our study, despite their methodological strengths. Challenges associated with standalone information sources, such as information overload9 and the difficulty of filtering relevant data,46 47 are documented in existing literature. These challenges likely contribute to the observed variation in reported usage across studies. Indeed, the high heterogeneity in our meta-analysis highlights the diverse ways in which information-seeking behaviour is measured and reported across contexts, specialties and settings. This underscores the complexity of synthesising such data and supports the use of a random-effects model.

The burning question which remains is why clinical practice guidelines (38% (95% CI 27% to 49%) and 49% (95% CI 16% to 82%) point-of-care websites) are so infrequently used despite their potential to deliver concise, systematic and evidence-based answers to clinical questions. Previous studies have suggested that the low frequencies of CPG use by medical specialists can be attributed to factors such as poor accessibility, fear of being outdated and recommendations being too generic.6 7 Therefore, literature indicates that guideline developers and associations of medical specialists should focus on enhancing the usage and implementation of CPGs in clinical settings48 by improving design features like search functionality, update frequency and integration into electronic patient records for easier access. These modifications would help to ensure that aggregated sources become relevant, accessible and easy to use in clinical practice. Finally, ongoing research is necessary to better understand the evolving information needs of medical specialists and adapt aggregated information sources accordingly.

Limitations and future directions

Our review provides valuable insights into the information source preferences and practices of medical specialists. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the aim of this review was to describe broad patterns in information-seeking behaviour rather than to generate precise or unbiased pooled estimates. The substantial methodological variability across studies means that the pooled proportions should be interpreted as approximate summaries rather than exact effect estimates. Second, the methodological heterogeneity and variable quality of the included studies limited the number suitable for meta-analysis. To preserve comparability, we only included studies that assessed at least four different information sources and reported continuous usage percentages. Given the challenges in researching this topic, all studies relied on self-reported surveys, which are prone to recall bias and social desirability bias. Specialists may overestimate their use of certain information sources or give responses they feel are more socially acceptable. Additionally, the overall quality of included studies was modest, as reflected in QUADS scores. Despite extensive screening and selection, the limited number of studies in our meta-analysis led to rather wide CIs, underscoring the diverse nature of research in information source usage.

Conclusions

Medical specialists predominantly autonomously search for answers when faced with clinical questions through standalone information sources, rather than using systematically aggregated and interpreted information sources. The use of these aggregated information sources (CPGs and point-of-care websites) is relatively low, despite their potential to provide more comprehensive evidence and reduce biases. Enhancing the integration and utilisation of these aggregated information sources in clinical practice requires overcoming existing barriers. Future research should focus on identifying and addressing these barriers to optimise aggregated information sources to better meet the needs of medical specialists, ultimately improving clinical decision-making.
