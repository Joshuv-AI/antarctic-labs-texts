Cell phone image analysis as a proxy for laboratory egg quality measurements
Authors: Anna Wolc, Usamah Kabuye, Tricia Veldhuizen, Jaedyn Condon, Ye Bi, Kaylee Rowland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecise individual-level recording of egg quality for genetic improvement requires laboratory instruments often unavailable at field test locations or in developing countries where breeding programs where local breeds are being developed. This study evaluated smartphone camera-derived measurements validating them against laboratory references for shell color (measured with Minolta colorimeter), egg weight, and defect classification in 359 matched eggs (180 brown, 179 white; 180 defective, 179 normal). Eggs were segmented out using SAM3 text-prompted segmentation applied to each raw photograph. A custom Python image pipeline extracted CIE L* (lightness), a*(redness on red-green scale), b*(yellowness on blue-yellow scale), chroma, morphometric axes, projected area, shape index, and surface defect scores from a mean of 2 images per egg acquired with a smartphone stabilized on a tripod, additionally a scale calibration from a physical reference sticker was considered. Combined-group validation statistics for image-derived color components against colorimeter values were high (Pearson r ≥ 0.986, 10-fold cross-validated R2 ≥ 0.971) but inflated by the Brown/White group contrast, which explained ≥ 95% of total variance in every color component. Egg weight was predicted from pixel-based morphometric measurements (egg_area_px + long_axis_px + short_axis_px) with 10-fold cross-validated R2 = 0.922 overall, 0.961 for Brown eggs, and 0.842 for White eggs (cross-validated RMSE = 1.149 g). Shell breaking strength was weakly predicted by image features (cross-validated R2 ≈ 0.11). A soft-voting ensemble (Random Forest + Gradient Boosting + SVM) trained on 30 SAM3-derived image features achieved AUC = 0.810 ± 0.078, accuracy = 0.730 ± 0.052, sensitivity = 0.744, and specificity = 0.715 for binary defect classification; a decision tree and logistic regression on 13 summary features each achieved AUC = 0.673 by the same 10-fold cross-validation. These results demonstrate that smartphone-based image analysis accurately replaces laboratory colorimetry and weighing, shifting the operational challenge from instrument access to data transfer and computational analysis, and thereby extending the reach of genetic improvement programs.Keywords: Image analysis, Egg quality, Shell color, Egg weight, Defect detection

Introduction

Egg quality is commercially important in the table egg market, with pricing determined by egg weight categories and by consumer preference for shell color, color uniformity, and shell stability (Tůmová et al., 2014). Egg shape is associated with resistance to shell damage (Altuntaş and Şekeroğlu, 2008) and with hatchability (Narushin and Romanov, 2002; Cavero et al., 2011). Shell strength is relevant to both table eggs (delivery of an intact product with maximum food safety) and hatching eggs (optimal embryo development) especially in terms of avoiding cracks and breaks. Genetic improvement of these traits requires individual-level phenotyping using a colorimeter, precision scale, acoustic egg tester, and individual identification system. This equipment is typically found only in specialized facilities and is unavailable on most of farms. This limits the ability to collect phenotypic data needed for genetic evaluations, especially from the field conditions, which is a recognized barrier to genetic progress in poultry (Wolc et al., 2012; Klingström et al., 2025).

Computer vision has been applied to egg quality assessment (identification of blood spots) since the 1990s (Patel et al., 1998), progressing to deep learning systems that classify cracks with 95–99% accuracy under controlled industrial conditions (Botta et al., 2022) and predict egg weight from axis measurements with R2 = 0.96 using random forest regression on machine vision data (Yang et al., 2023). Eggshell color in brown eggs is determined primarily by protoporphyrin IX concentration, which is linearly correlated with CIE a* and b* values (Zeng et al., 2022). However, the practical application of image-based color measurement to field settings using cell phones, and rigorous component-level validation against reference colorimetry have not been reported.

The objectives of this study were to evaluate prediction accuracy of egg quality traits (shell color, egg weight, breaking strength, presence of defects) based on cell phone images using computer vision models.

Materials and methods

Eggs and reference measurements

A total of 360 eggs were collected from 37-week-old laying hens (White Leghorn, Plymouth Rock, and Rhode Island Red) housed in an aviary system at a Hy-Line International pure line breeding farm at Dallas Center, Iowa, USA. The collected eggs were identified with individual bird stickers bearing a DataMatrix 2D barcode and a printed five-digit identifier. Egg collection included 179 white and 180 brown eggs, half of which had defects. Defective eggs were classified as such based on visual inspection at the time of collection and included a combination of cracks, dirt, calcium deposits, misshapes etc. After transferring to a field test laboratory, in Minburn, Iowa, USA, shell color was measured with a Minolta colorimeter, a single measurement per egg (L* (lightness, recorded as L), a*(redness on the red-green scale, recorded as A), b*(yellowness on the blue-yellow scale, recorded as B); (CIE, 1976). All eggs were processed within 2 days from collection. Egg weight was recorded on a high-precision scale (±0.1 g). Shell breaking strength was measured with a digital force gauge (Futura-FEST; Broring; Lohne, Germany) applied to the equatorial region (N). After matching to image data by the numeric egg identifier, 359 eggs had both reference and image measurements and were used in all analyses. Shell breaking strength was missing for 17 eggs, due to cracks, which were excluded from that analysis only as the machine is not able to record breaking strength for already broken eggs.

Prior to egg quality evaluation, eggs and their identification stickers were placed in designated positions on an egg flat on a table under natural room light. The analysis was performed within a 3-hour window assuring consistent light conditions; for larger studies taking longer or spread over multiple days a consistent artificial light would be recommended. A mobile phone camera stabilized on a tripod, to ensure consistent distance from the eggs, was used to acquire 2 photographs per egg at approximately 180° rotation to capture both sides of the egg, yielding a mean of 2 images per egg. The reference sticker remained visible in each image.

Image analysis

Images were processed in batch using a custom Python pipeline built on OpenCV (Bradski, 2000) and NumPy (Harris et al., 2020). Prior to analysis, images were automatically rotated in 90° increments to place the egg on the left and sticker on the right, using connected-component blob detection on a 600-pixel thumbnail. Egg foreground segmentation was performed using SAM3 (Carion et al., 2025), a vision-language model queried with the text prompts “egg” and “sticker” to produce a binary egg mask and localize the reference sticker simultaneously. GrabCut (Rother et al., 2004) was applied as a fallback in case SAM3 failed. The sticker pixel width was used to compute a pixels-per-millimeter calibration factor (pixels_per_mm), and a grey-world white balance correction was applied using the sticker surface as the reference patch. Steps of image processing A) raw image B) identified sticker C) identified egg D) segmented egg mask.

Color was quantified from up to 5,000 randomly sampled foreground pixels in both RGB (red, green, blue) and CIE L*a*b* color spaces (CIE, 1976). Mean L*, a*, b*, chroma (C* = √(a*2 + b*2)), and color uniformity were derived. Morphometric measurements—long axis, short axis, projected egg area (egg_area_px in pixels), and shape index (short/long × 100; Preston, 1953)—were extracted from the segmentation mask. The sticker size based pixels-per-millimeter calibration factor was used to convert axis lengths to millimeters (long_axis_mm, short_axis_mm). All geometric predictors used in regression models were retained in pixel units to avoid the quadratic noise introduced when pixels_per_mm varies across images. Pixel-based axis lengths were derived as long_axis_px = long_axis_mm × pixels_per_mm and short_axis_px = short_axis_mm × pixels_per_mm. A Narushin (1997) prolate-spheroid volume proxy was computed as Vpx = (π/6) × long_axis_px × short_axis_px2.

Surface quality was assessed via four defect indicators: Canny edge density (Canny, 1986), color-anomaly spot fraction (proportion of segmented pixels whose Euclidean RGB distance from the per-egg median color exceeded the 97th percentile of that distance distribution), calcium deposit fraction (luminance (calculated as a function of RGB) > 0.92), and stain fraction (luminance < 0.25). Based on these defect indicators a composite defect score (0–100) was computed as edge_density * 200 + spot_franction * 2 + calcium_deposite_fraction * 1.5 + stain_fraction * 3, egg classified as defective when the score exceeded a threshold of 30. The crack_suspected flag was set when the pipeline detected linear discontinuities consistent with hairline cracks. Per-egg mean values were computed across all images. Barcode and OCR reading from identification stickers used pylibdmtx (Townsend, 2016) and Tesseract OCR (Smith, 2007) respectively, with reconciliation by Levenshtein edit distance.

Color Validation

The composite color score CO was defined as CO = −L* + a* + b* + 100. CO and the four individual components (L*, a*, b*, chroma) were each validated by comparing per-egg image means against the respective colorimeter reference values. Agreement was quantified with Pearson r, in-sample ordinary least squares (OLS) R2, 10-fold cross-validated R2 and RMSE (caret package; Kuhn, 2008). Between-group variance partitioning quantified the fraction of total sum of squares attributable to the Brown/White contrast. All analyses were conducted for all eggs jointly and separately for brown and white eggs.

Egg weight prediction

Three OLS regression models were evaluated: M1 (egg_area_px only), M2 (egg_area_px + long_axis_px + short_axis_px), and M3 (vol_px, Narushin approximation). Model accuracy was assessed using in-sample R2, 10-fold cross-validated R2, cross-validated RMSE. All models were fitted for all 359 eggs jointly and separately for brown and white eggs. As a methodological reference, the mm2-based area (area_mm2), computed by dividing the segmentation-mask pixel count (egg_area_px) by pixels_per_mm2 (where pixels_per_mm is derived from the known physical diameter of the co-photographed calibration sticker divided by its measured pixel width in that image), was also evaluated to demonstrate the effect of variable scale calibration.

Defect classification

Three classifiers were evaluated for binary (Normal vs. Defective) classification, all using 10-fold cross-validation. The first two classifiers relied on 13 summary features derived from the image pipeline - including geometric measurements (long and short axis in mm and pixels, egg area), color statistics (L*, a*, b* means, chroma, color uniformity), shape descriptors (shape index), a composite defect score, and a binary crack indicator. These features were fitted in a decision tree and a logistic regression.

The third classifier was a soft-voting ensemble trained on a substantially richer feature set of 30 features extracted by the SAM3 segmentation pipeline (Carion et al., 2025) from the masked egg region. These features spanned four domains: color (RGB, L A B, and HSV channel statistics including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis, plus chroma statistics), texture (local binary patterns and gray-level co-occurrence matrix statistics: contrast, dissimilarity, homogeneity, energy, and correlation), shape (area, perimeter, circularity, ellipse axes and angle, shape index, convexity, and solidity), and surface defect indicators (Canny edge density, Laplacian variance and mean, dirt score, low-luminance pixel fraction, and color uniformity). For eggs with multiple images, defect-sensitive features (edge density, chroma, dirt score, and Laplacian variance) were summarized by taking the worst-case value across views to capture the most severely affected surface, while structural features were averaged.

The ensemble combined three component classifiers using soft voting (Dietterich, 2000): a Random Forest (300 trees, balanced class weights), a Gradient Boosting classifier (200 trees, learning rate 0.05), and a Support Vector Machine (RBF kernel, probability calibration, balanced class weights). All classifiers were implemented in scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011). To prevent data leakage, median imputation and standardization were applied within each cross-validation fold. Feature importances were derived from a final Random Forest fitted on all 359 eggs. Classifier performance was assessed by AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, all estimated by 10-fold cross-validation.

Breaking strength regression

All 13 summary features derived from the image pipeline were included in a linear model to predict breaking strength. Stepwise AIC variable selection (both directions) identified the final model. Multicollinearity was assessed by variance inflation factors (VIF; threshold < 5). Ten-fold cross-validated R2, RMSE, and AIC were reported.

Statistical Software

Regression analyses, repeatability models, and simple classifiers were performed in R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024) using packages lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), caret (Kuhn, 2008), pROC (Robin et al., 2011), rpart (Therneau and Atkinson., 2023), and ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016). The SAM3 ensemble classifier was implemented in Python using scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), OpenCV (Bradski, 2000), and scikit-image (van der Walt et al., 2014). The significance threshold was α = 0.05 throughout.

Results and discussion

Color component validation

Variance partitioning showed that the brown/white group contrast explained 95.8% of total variance in L*, 95.1% in a*, 98.6% in b*, 98.1% in chroma, and 97.3% in CO. As a result, combined-group statistics were high for all components and CO (r = 0.986–0.996, 10-fold cross-validated R2 = 0.971–0.991; Table 1; Fig. 2) but primarily reflected the separation of color groups rather than within-group measurement precision. If a study mixes Brown and White eggs and reports r ≈ 0.99 for L* it is not reporting a finding about measurement accuracy; it is reporting that a camera can distinguish brown from white eggs, which requires no calibration and no image processing. For breeding program applications, where all eggs within a flock typically share the same shell color, the relevant question is whether the camera can rank individual eggs within a color group. The answer is moderate for brown eggs and poor for white eggs.

Within brown eggs agreement was moderate: L* r = 0.875, cross-validated R2 = 0.756; a* r = 0.903, cross-validated R2 = 0.811; b* r = 0.714, cross-validated R2 = 0.450; chroma r = 0.811, cross-validated R2 = 0.630; CO r = 0.909, cross-validated R2 = 0.816 (Table 1). The stronger agreement for a* than for b* or chroma is consistent with the biochemistry of brown eggshell color: protoporphyrin IX, the primary pigment, contributes predominantly to the red-green axis of CIE color space, with darker shells characterized by lower L* and higher a* values (Zeng et al., 2022). Camera a* therefore captures the primary axis of biologically meaningful color variation in brown eggs, making it the most informative single component for genetic evaluation purposes. Heritability of CO colorimeter readings has been reported to range from 0.60 to 0.80 in brown-egg layers (Wolc et al., 2014), sufficient to capture a substantial fraction of the heritable variation in shell color.

Within white eggs agreement was poor for L*, A*, B*, chroma and CO (|r| ≤ 0.356, cross-validated R2 ≤ 0.096), reflecting the narrow biological range of white egg color (L*: SD = 1.28; a*: SD = 0.10; CO: SD = 1.34). This is not a limitation of the image pipeline but of the biological signal available: the White-egg color range in this study spanned L* = 88.1–95.5, a* = −0.62 to −0.05, and chroma = 0.38–5.03 (SD = 0.66). At these ranges, the systematic bias of approximately +3 units in L* is comparable to the entire range of biological variation. Wei and Bitgood (1990) noted similar limitations when measuring White Leghorn eggs with a Minolta colorimeter, finding that yolk shade influenced color readings in white eggs. Camera-based color validation is therefore not meaningful within populations of white eggs using standard CIE colorimetry; hyperspectral or near-infrared imaging would be required to resolve biologically informative within white color variation but while potentially interesting from research perspective current variation level in white eggs shell color is not a major challenge for egg industry.

Systematic camera biases were identified across all groups (Table 1). Camera L* overestimated colorimeter L* by a mean of 3.1 units (brown) and 3.2 units (white), consistent with a fixed calibration offset. Camera a* underestimated colorimeter a* by 3.7 units in brown eggs; b* and chroma were underestimated by 2.0 and 3.6 units, respectively. Because CO accumulates the contributions of all three component biases (−L*, +a*, +b*), the L* overestimation partially counteracts the chromatic underestimation, but the net effect remains a systematic underestimation of image CO relative to colorimeter CO by 8.8 units in brown eggs and 4.0 units in white eggs. The OLS regression slope for CO in brown eggs was 0.697, indicating stronger scale compression than for any individual component, which is consistent with the additive propagation of the individual slope departures from 1.0. OLS regression slopes for individual chromatic components in brown eggs were: a* = 0.880, b* = 0.659, chroma = 0.790. Potential solutions would include using consistent artificial light, rather than natural light, and including a reference color strip within each image.

Egg weight prediction

The scale calibration factor pixels_per_mm ranged from 11.1 to 14.1 (SD = 0.51) across images, causing quadratic amplification of noise in mm2-converted area. The area_mm2 predictor alone yielded 10-fold cross-validated R2 ≈ 0.20. Using pixel-based area (egg_area_px) alone raised cross-validated R2 to 0.745. The three-predictor model (egg_area_px + long_axis_px + short_axis_px; Model M2) achieved in-sample R2 = 0.924 and cross-validated R2 = 0.922 (cross-validated RMSE = 1.149 g, Table 2, Fig. 3). The Narushin volume proxy (Vpx; Model M3) achieved cross-validated R2 = 0.844 as a single predictor (Table 2). Brown eggs were predicted with higher accuracy (cross-validated R2 = 0.961, cross-validated RMSE = 0.836 g) than white eggs (cross-validated R2 = 0.842, cross-validated RMSE = 1.361 g). The cross-validated R2 of 0.922 for Model M2 (egg_area_px + long_axis_px + short_axis_px) compares favorably with the literature. Asadi and Raoufat (2010) achieved r = 0.96 (maximum absolute error = 2.3 g) for egg weight prediction from machine vision using a CCD camera with fixed geometry, while Yang et al. (2023) reported R2 = 0.96 using random forest regression on major and minor axis features from a controlled conveyor-belt system. The present study achieves comparable accuracy in a field-test setting using only a smartphone and tripod, demonstrating that consistent within-session scale calibration is more important than the camera hardware.

The critical methodological finding is the 78% reduction in cross-validated R2 (from 0.922 to approximately 0.20) caused by the variable pixels_per_mm calibration factor (range 11.1–14.1). Because area converts as egg_area_px / pixels_per_mm2, a 27% range in linear scale translates to approximately a 60% potential range in area units, which is larger than the biological size variation among eggs in this dataset. This problem is not specific to eggs: any morphometric measurement derived from images with variable magnification will suffer the same quadratic error amplification on unit conversion. The solution used here—retaining pixel-based measurements throughout—is practical and requires no additional equipment. Investigators who need measurements in physical units should either fix the camera geometry to ensure constant pixels_per_mm, or apply the per-image calibration factor only to convert final predictions rather than to transform the predictors themselves. The Narushin (1997) volume proxy Vpx = (π/6) × long_axis_px × short_axis_px2 provided a single interpretable predictor with cross-validated R2 = 0.844, offering a useful alternative when axis measurements are available but area segmentation is imperfect.

Breaking strength

The stepwise OLS model retained egg_area_px, long_axis_px, shape_index, a_mean, b_mean, chroma, defect_score, var_R, var_G and explained R2 ≈ 0.13 of breaking strength variance (10-fold cross-validated R2 ≈ 0.11, cross-validated RMSE ≈ 1,215 N; Fig. 4). Collinearity between some of the covariates was noted. Color-group sub-models showed similar performance (Brown R2 ≈ 0.15, White R2 ≈ 0.11). Breaking strength in this dataset ranged from 1,040 to 6,560 N (mean 3,850 N, SD 1,250 N), with Defective eggs having lower mean breaking strength (3,370 vs. 4,287 N for Normal eggs) and unrecordable breaking strength for eggs with large cracks.

Shell breaking strength: predicted vs. measured values from stepwise OLS model. Points colored by shell color group. Dashed line = identity.

Low predictive ability for breaking strength is consistent with the established understanding that shell mechanical resistance depends on shell thickness, calcium carbonate crystal organization, and porosity rather than on surface appearance (Hamilton et al., 1979). Moreover, cracked eggs – a feature potentially captured by the camera do not receive a value for breaking strength therefore the low extreme of the distribution which may be more predictable was missing.

Performance metrics for all three classifiers from 10-fold stratified cross-validation are presented in Table 3 (Fig.s 5–7). The decision tree (depth = 3, tuned by 10-fold AUC) achieved mean AUC = 0.673 ± 0.089, mean accuracy = 0.640 ± 0.094, sensitivity = 0.706, specificity = 0.575. The logistic regression achieved mean AUC = 0.673 ± 0.104, mean accuracy = 0.621 ± 0.084, sensitivity = 0.633, specificity = 0.609. The SAM3 soft-voting ensemble (RF + GBT + SVM) achieved substantially higher performance: mean AUC = 0.810 ± 0.078, mean accuracy = 0.730 ± 0.052, sensitivity = 0.744, specificity = 0.715. The top features in the ensemble RF by permutation importance were shape_index (0.028), edge_density_norm (0.025), ellipse_minor (0.024), glcm_contrast (0.021), and edge_density (0.021), with color variation features (hsv_S_std, chroma_std) and texture features (lbp_std, lab_b_kurt) also ranking highly.

The 14-percentage-point AUC improvement reflects the richer pixel-level feature set from SAM3 segmentation: beyond the basic shape and mean color statistics available to the simpler classifiers, the ensemble incorporated texture features (GLCM contrast, LBP statistics), higher-order color moments (skewness, kurtosis per channel), and continuously quantified surface anomaly indicators. Shape distortion was the top discriminating feature, confirming that defective eggs differ primarily in morphology; edge density features ranked next, capturing crack-related surface discontinuities that the simpler pipeline's binary crack_suspected flag cannot quantify continuously. Color texture features (GLCM contrast, chroma variance) captured heterogeneity associated with dirty and rough-shelled eggs. Despite the overall improvement, ensemble AUC = 0.810 remains below the 95–99% accuracy of purpose-built industrial deep learning systems on single, well-defined defect types under controlled conditions (Botta et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023). The principal remaining limitations are the heterogeneity of the Defective label (Wolc et al., 2012), limited two-view surface coverage, and the invisibility of internal defects (thin shell, poor mineralisation) to visible-wavelength photography.

Conclusions

The overall conclusion that smartphone image analysis can accurately replace laboratory colorimetry and weighing in field settings is relevant for the broader challenge of expanding genetic evaluation infrastructure. Klingström et al., 2025 identified data collection cost as one of the primary barriers to high-throughput phenotyping in low- and middle-income countries, where breeding programs for local breeds are being established. The image-based system described here requires only a smartphone, a tripod, and printed reference stickers—equipment that is widely available and low-cost—shifting the bottleneck from instrument access to data transfer and analysis. The open-source pipeline (Python, R) and the per-image scale calibration from the sticker make the system portable and reproducible across locations. Digital phenotyping approaches of this type are expected to become increasingly central to livestock breeding programs as computational infrastructure becomes more accessible (Wolc et al., 2016; Klingström et al., 2025).

The main limitation for color measurement in the current implementation is the systematic camera bias, which prevents direct comparison of camera-derived L*, a*, b* values to colorimeter values from other instruments without recalibration. Future work should include a per-session reference tile of known color to enable automatic bias correction. For weight prediction, the scale calibration variability observed here (CV = 4.2% for pixels_per_mm) could be eliminated by fixing the camera position and using a rigid tripod mount.

These results support the use of smartphone image analysis as a practical replacement for laboratory colorimetry and weighing in field genetic evaluation settings, with the SAM3 ensemble providing meaningful defect detection capability, and data transfer and analysis as the remaining operational challenges.
