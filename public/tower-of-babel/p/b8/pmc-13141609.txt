Seaweed bioactives: A comprehensive review of their nutritional, medicinal, and functional roles
Authors: Anbu Chezhiyan Elango, Mugesh Sankaranarayanan, Nadana Raja Vadivu Ganapathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySeaweeds contain a rich nutritional composition and various bioactive components including polysaccharides, proteins, pigments, fatty acids, polyphenols, and phlorotannins. Recent studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of seaweed bioactives, demonstrating their functional efficacy as anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-microbial, anti-cancer, anti-obesity, and anti-diabetic agents in different models. Owing to those bioactive properties, the integration of seaweeds into food, feed, pharmaceutical and nutraceutical industries is expanding rapidly. However, the lack of robust large-scale processing technologies and limited understanding of biocompatibility and bioavailability remain significant barriers to their wider utilization. This review delivers a comprehensive overview of the nutritional, bioactive, and therapeutic potentials of seaweeds and their major bioactive compounds and further highlights how molecular structure, processing, and food matrix influence their bioactivity and absorption. It also examines downstream processing strategies and evaluates their impact on product quality, safety, and scalability. Finally, the review discusses future research directions, including the development of multiproduct biorefineries, optimization of bioactive delivery in functional foods, and the need for human pharmacokinetic studies. Collectively, these developments position seaweeds as sustainable foundation in health and wellness, with vast potential to address global nutritional and therapeutic needs through innovative, technology-driven approaches.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, nutrition, natural product chemistry, natural product biochemistry

Introduction

Seaweeds or macroalgae have gained global interests due to their high ecological productivity and ability to address major challenges related to human health, nutrition, and environmental sustainability. Seaweeds are a renewable marine resource with low agricultural inputs, which make them a sustainable future candidate for food systems.1 Marine macroalgae or seaweeds are classified into three major groups based their photosynthetic pigments, namely Phaeophyceae (brown seaweeds), Rhodophyta (red seaweeds), and Chlorophyceae (green seaweeds).2 These classifications were based upon their ecological adaptations to light availability in marine environments. Among them, the green algae cover the shallow regions of coastal waters due to the presence of light-harvesting pigments like chlorophyll a and b, whereas red and brown algae possess pigments such as fucoxanthin and phycoerythrin, which make them well adapted to deeper waters and waters with less-light availability.3

Beyond their ecological significance, seaweeds are rich sources of structurally diverse bioactive compounds, including polysaccharides, polyphenols, phlorotannins, peptides, sterols, and pigments, many of which are uncommon or absent in terrestrial plants.3 These compounds have demonstrated a wide range of biological activities, including anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, anti-microbial, anti-viral, anti-coagulant, and immunomodulatory effects in in vitro and in vivo models.4 Subsequently, seaweeds are increasingly being recognized as valuable raw materials for the development of functional foods and nutraceuticals targeting chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, obesity, diabetes, and certain cancers.5

Seaweeds can be processed at different levels, each conferring distinct nutritional and functional benefits. Whole seaweed biomass, consumed directly as food, provides all essential nutrients such as proteins, dietary fibers, minerals, vitamins, and bioactive polysaccharides that contribute to metabolic health and gut function.4,5,6 In contrast, seaweed extracts, obtained using aqueous, enzymatic, chemical, or emerging green extraction technologies are commonly incorporated into nutraceutical and pharmaceutical formulations. These seaweed extracts contain higher concentrations of certain functional molecules than raw biomass. Further downstream processing allows the isolation of purified compounds and peptide fractions, which have gained attention for targeted therapeutic applications.7,8 This distinction between whole biomass, extracts, and isolated products is critical because the biological activity, bioavailability, and efficacy of seaweed-derived compounds are strongly influenced by the extraction methods, chemical structure, and molecular composition.

Among the seaweed-derived bioactives, sulfated polysaccharides such as fucoidans, carrageenans, and ulvans have been most extensively studied. These compounds have exhibited diverse biological activities, including anti-oxidant, anti-tumor, anti-viral, anti-coagulant, and immunomodulatory effects.4,5,6 Polyphenolic compounds, particularly phlorotannins from brown seaweeds, also exhibit strong anti-oxidant properties and have shown promising potential toward anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective activities.7,8 Additionally, proteins and bioactive peptides derived from marine algae have demonstrated anti-oxidant, anti-hypertensive, anti-diabetic, and anti-microbial activities, highlighting seaweeds as emerging sources of alternative protein and functional ingredients.3,9 Marine pigments such as fucoxanthin from brown seaweeds, phycobiliproteins from red seaweeds, and chlorophylls from green seaweeds further contribute to the biofunctional properties of seaweeds in metabolic regulation, anti-obesity effects, and cancer-related pathways.6

Historically, seaweed consumption dates back thousands of years, particularly in Asian countries such as Japan, China, and Korea, where seaweeds were traditionally valued for both nutritional and medicinal purposes.8 Edible seaweeds such as Porphyra, Pyropia, Neopyropia (Rhodophyta), and Saccharina and Undaria (Phaeophyceae) have long been used to address dietary deficiencies, including iodine deficiency and goiter.6 Modern scientific research has substantiated many of these traditional uses, identifying seaweeds as important sources of biologically active compounds.5,6 Epidemiological and dietary studies have further suggested that regular consumption of seaweeds is associated with reduced incidence of lifestyle-related disorders such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and certain cancers.6 For instance, population-based studies in Japan and Korea have reported inverse associations between seaweed intake and diabetes prevalence, while consumption of brown seaweeds such as Undaria has been linked to reduced risks of breast cancer.6 In addition, seaweeds rich in polysaccharide and fiber content have gained attention as prebiotic ingredients, promoting gut microbiota modulation, colonic fermentation, and immune regulation.3,9,10

Despite these promising attributes, the health effects of seaweed-derived compounds are influenced by multiple factors, including species variability, processing methods, chemical structure, bioavailability, and metabolism. Moreover, while numerous in vitro and animal studies support the therapeutic potential of seaweed bioactives, human clinical evidence remains limited for many compounds and species. Therefore, a critical and balanced synthesis of nutritional composition, bioactive compounds, extraction strategies, and therapeutic outcomes is essential.

In this context, the present review systematically summarizes the nutritional profile of seaweeds, key bioactive compounds, extraction and processing strategies, and their pharmacological properties, with particular emphasis on anti-oxidant, anti-diabetic, anti-cancer, and anti-inflammatory activities. Where available, human intervention studies are critically discussed, and areas lacking clinical evidence are clearly identified. This integrated approach aims to highlight the opportunities, limitations, and future prospects of seaweeds as valuable biomasses for functional foods, nutraceuticals, and therapeutic applications. Recent reviews have emphasized the functional value of seaweed-derived compounds, but most of them have focused on biological activities specific to seaweed types. Unlike previous reviews, this review integrates the compositional, mechanistic, regulatory, and translation perspectives to highlight both the potential and current limitations of seaweeds as functional food ingredients.

The literature includes recent review articles, compositional analyses, and preclinical and human intervention studies, where human evidence remains limited or absent, as explicitly stated in this review. Further, this review aims to provide a critical synthesis of current knowledge on edible seaweeds by consolidating and discussing the reported data on their nutritional composition and relevance to human health. It evaluates the therapeutic potential of seaweed-derived compounds by distinguishing the mechanistic evidence from preclinical findings and available human studies. The review further examines the functional applications of edible seaweeds in the development of value-added functional foods and nutraceutical formulations.

In addition, it discusses the therapeutic potential of seaweed proteins and peptides and highlights various analytical approaches used for the quantification of seaweed proteins. It also summarizes the safety, regulatory, and toxicological considerations associated with dietary seaweed consumption. In addition, the sustainability aspects and global supply chains of seaweed production are assessed. Finally, key challenges, research gaps, and future perspectives related to compositional variability, consumer acceptance, and the translation of seaweed-based products into functional health applications are highlighted.

Nutritional composition of seaweeds

Macronutrients

Lipids and carbohydrates

Seaweeds or macroalgae differ significantly in their biochemical composition. Seaweed biomass is composed mainly of water (up to 85%) with the dry matter including a rich mix of carbohydrates (21%–61%), proteins (3%–50%), lipids (0.5%–3.5%) and minerals (12%–46%).6 In general, the lipid levels of red, brown, and green seaweeds are generally low, as summarized in Table 1, ranging from 0.3% to 5.21%. Although, lipid levels are low, seaweeds comprise higher proportions of polyunsaturated fatty acids, particularly omega-3 fatty acids such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), which are essential for cardiovascular and neurological health.8

Carbohydrates are the abundant macromolecules present in seaweeds, and their proportion can even reach up to 81% on dry weight basis. Specifically, the carbohydrate levels of seaweeds summarized in Table 1, vary between 4.80% and 61.44% of their dry weight. Depending upon their complexity and length of molecules, carbohydrates can be classified as monosaccharides, disaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides. Glucose, mannose, galactose, fructose, rhamnose, fucose, xylose, and arabinose are some of the commonly found monosaccharides in seaweeds. Significant polysaccharides found in seaweeds are fucoidans, alginates, and laminarins in brown algae; carrageenans and agars in red algae; and ulvans in green algae,4 as illustrated in Figure 1. Polysaccharides are formed by the linkages of monosaccharides together by glycosidic bonds. Depending on the composition of monosaccharides, they can be classified as homopolymers or heteropolymers. Homopolysaccharides comprise repeated units of a single type of monosaccharide linked together to form the structural component of cell walls of plants and most of the seaweeds such as cellulose derivatives, whereas heteropolysaccharides are composed of repeated units of different types of monosaccharides to form polymers such as carrageenans and ulvans.3 These polysaccharides serve not only as structural components but also as dietary fibers, which exhibit various health-promoting bioactivities including anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-coagulant, and prebiotic functions.4

Classification of seaweeds and their bioactive compounds

Proteins

Proteins are chains of amino acids that are bound together by peptide bonds. The protein content of seaweeds varies considerably among taxonomic groups. Green and brown seaweeds usually contain lower protein proportions, approximately 20% and 10% of dry weight, respectively, whereas red seaweeds such as Palmaria palmata, Porphyra spp., Gracilaria spp. contain higher protein levels ranging from 19 to 44% of dry weight.5 However, the protein content of macroalgae is comparatively high than that of terrestrial plant sources like soybean, pulses, etc.5 Due to their high protein content, edible seaweeds are increasingly being recognized as promising alternative protein sources and novel functional ingredients for human nutrition.3 Recent studies have demonstrated the successful incorporation of seaweed-derived ingredients into meat products, meat analogues, and low-salt formulations, underscoring the technological functionality and nutritional potential of seaweed proteins in reformulated food systems.23,24

Protein quantification in macroalgae is challenging due to the presence of various non-proteinaceous compounds like nitrate, free amino acids, pigments, and other nitrogenous compounds, which may interfere with analytical measurements and lead to an overestimation of protein content. In addition, high salt content, recalcitrant cell wall structures, and the presence of phycocolloids can affect the efficiency of protein extraction and interfere with analytical reactions during protein quantification.25 Among the different methods of protein quantification, calorimetric methods such as Lowry’s and BCA assays still tend to be commonly used due to low costs and ease of use. Both of these assays estimate the concentration of protein by measuring color changes from copper reduction but differ in terms of their detection reagents. In Lowry’s assay, the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent is reduced, producing blue color, which is measured at 650–750 nm, whereas the BCA chelates the reduced Cu+ ion, forming a purple complex measurable at 562 nm.26,27 Although these methods are rapid and easy to use, their efficacy may be limited when applied to macroalgae. Precise quantification of proteins in both the plant and algal samples can be difficult due to the presence of various bioactive substances including polyphenols, polysaccharides, and pigments. These substances may get co-extracted along with proteins and cause interference with the measurements.25,28,29

Spectroscopic techniques such as Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy have recently been explored as rapid and non-destructive approaches for protein quantification in seaweeds. Unlike colorimetric assays, these methods rely on molecular vibrational signatures rather than chemical reactions, thereby reducing interference from phenolic compounds, polysaccharides, pigments, and salts commonly present in macroalgal biomass.25,30 In FTIR spectroscopy, proteins are identified through characteristic peptide absorption bands, particularly the amide I band (∼1,650 cm−1) associated with C=O stretching and the amide II band (∼1,540 cm−1) corresponding to N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations, respectively. Protein quantification is achieved by correlating spectral intensities with reference protein values, using statistical models such as linear regression or partial least-squares regression (PLSR).25,30,31

Similarly, NIR spectroscopy utilizes overtone and combination vibrations of N–H, C–H, and O–H bonds within the 700–2500 nm wavelength range, where protein-related signals are typically detected between 1400 and 2500 nm. Spectral data are commonly analyzed using chemometric approaches such as PLSR models to predict protein content.25,32 However, reliable implementation requires the development of robust calibration datasets, as the biochemical composition of seaweeds can vary significantly with species, environmental conditions, and seasonal fluctuations.25,30 The quantification of protein using colorimetric and spectroscopic methods can be affected by spectral interferences arising from interactions with non-protein compounds present in the extracts. So, protein precipitation or purification steps may also be employed prior to the colorimetric and spectroscopic analyses to improve measurement accuracy by reducing matrix interferences. However, the efficiency of protein precipitation and removal of non-proteinaceous compounds from the extracts can be influenced by several factors including the choice of precipitation method, solvent, and extraction conditions.25

Protein content in seaweeds is most commonly estimated indirectly from total nitrogen determined using elemental analysis through Kjeldahl or Dumas combustion methods, with crude protein calculated using a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor (N× factor). The Kjeldahl method involves digestion of the sample with concentrated H2SO4 in the presence of catalyst CuSO4, which converts organic nitrogen into ammonia that is sequentially quantified by titration.33 This method has been widely applied in food and algal analysis due to its reliability and relatively simple instrumentation. Alternatively, the Dumas method is a combustion-based dry technique in which the sample is burned at high temperatures in the presence of oxygen, leading to the conversion of nitrogen into nitrogen oxides that are subsequently reduced to molecular nitrogen (N2). The resulting gases are separated from combustion by-products quantified using a thermal conductivity detector.34 Compared with the Kjeldahl method, the Dumas technique is faster, safer, and highly automated, enabling analysis of each sample within minutes, which makes it suitable for high-throughput laboratories. In some seaweed studies, Dumas analysis has also been reported to produce slightly higher nitrogen values with improved analytical precision compared with the Kjeldahl method.35 Both methods are, therefore, widely employed as nitrogen-based protein estimation in seaweed biomass.

In nitrogen-based protein estimation, crude protein is typically calculated using the conventional nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor N× 6.25. However, this factor assumes that proteins contain approximately 16% nitrogen and that all nitrogen present in the sample originates from proteins. However, this assumption may not be valid for macroalgae because seaweeds contain significant amounts of non-protein nitrogen (NPN) compounds, including free amino acids, nitrates, pigments, and other nitrogenous metabolites. So, the conversion factor 6.25 likely overestimates the true protein content in seaweeds.29 Recent studies, therefore, recommend lower conversion factors, typically ranging from 4.59 to 5.7.36 In cases where species-specific factors are not available, a generalized seaweed conversion factor of around 5.0 has been proposed as a practical approximation.37 However, there is a need for standardized, macroalgae-specific conversion factors to improve the reliability of protein quantification in seaweed biomass.

Amino acid analysis followed by acid hydrolysis is widely regarded as the gold standard for determining true protein content in seaweeds. Unlike nitrogen-based methods or colorimetric assays, this approach directly quantifies protein-bound amino acids while excluding non-protein nitrogen sources such as nitrates, free amino acids, and other nitrogenous compounds that may lead to overestimation of protein content.34,38 In this method, proteins are hydrolyzed to their constituent amino acids through the cleavage of peptide bonds, and the total protein content is calculated as the sum of the individual amino acid residues.

Typically, protein hydrolysis is performed using 6 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) at 110°C for approximately 24 h. This treatment effectively breaks most peptide bonds and releases free amino acids for subsequent quantification.34 The liberated amino acids are commonly analyzed chromatographically, using techniques such as reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC), or automated amino acid analyzers.38 Because this method quantifies proteins based solely on amino acid residues, it is considered a direct measurement of true protein. During protein hydrolysis, peptide bonds incorporate a molecule of water, resulting in a mass gain of approximately 18 Da per peptide bond. Therefore, protein estimation based on amino acid analysis requires correction for this water gain, as the measured amino acid residues correspond to the molecular fraction of amino acids after the loss of water during peptide bond formation in proteins. As a result, true protein content is calculated as the sum of amino acid residues after subtracting the molecular mass contribution of water, ensuring accurate representation of the protein fraction.29,34

Despite its high accuracy, certain amino acids such as tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine may be partially degraded during acid hydrolysis and may require alternative hydrolysis.39 In addition, hydrolysis procedures can sometimes result in a slight underestimation of the total protein content due to amino acid losses, which may require correction factors during calculation.38 Nevertheless, amino acid analysis remains the most reliable method for determining true protein content, as it directly measures protein-derived amino acids without interference from non-proteinaceous substances. For this reason, it is often recommended as a reference method for validating nitrogen-based protein estimation approaches and for calibrating spectroscopic prediction models in seaweed research.34,40

Micronutrients (vitamins and minerals)

Seaweeds are a rich source of vitamins (A, B-complex, C, D, E, and K) and contain essential minerals including iodine, calcium, magnesium, iron, and zinc (Figure 1), which contribute to their nutritional and functional food value41 (Table 1). Seaweeds have been reported to contain a high mineral content of up to 40% of the whole biomass. These elevated levels of minerals are due to the absorption of metal ions from seawater and accumulation of mineral salts in their thallus as carbonate salts.3

Seaweeds contains both the water-soluble and fat-soluble vitamins, which include vitamin B complex, vitamin C, and vitamins A, D, E, and K.42 The proportion of vitamin A (carotenoids) in seaweeds varies from 2% to 10% of the reference nutrient intake (RNI). Briefly, brown and green seaweeds are rich sources of vitamin A (carotenoids), containing average levels between 500 and 3,000 mg/kg of dry weight, whereas vitamin A concentration in red seaweeds ranges from 100 to 800 mg/kg dry weight.3 Similarly, vitamin C content ranges from 1% to 23% of RNI, whereas vitamin B12 level ranges from 107% to 446% of RNI.43 Seaweeds contain higher levels of vitamin B12, which can be substituted as an alternative natural source of vitamin B12 for vegetarians. Vitamin B12 cannot be synthesized by most of the terrestrial plants and are only synthesized by certain prokaryotes. Prokaryotes share a symbiotic association with seaweeds, and their interconnection promotes the production of vitamin B12 in seaweeds.44 Discovery of the presence of vitamin B12 in seaweeds was considered a breakthrough because previously it was believed that vitamin B12, considered as most essential for the brain development and functions of the human body, is present only in animal-based foods.42

Dietary fibers and bioactive compounds

Seaweeds possess biologically active compounds with various therapeutic potentials. They have also been utilized as ingredients in functional food components, nutraceuticals, and dietary supplements due to their notable dietary fiber content. Dietary fibers are carbohydrate polymers and are non-absorbable in the small intestine, which enables their interaction with gut microbes and synthesis of beneficial metabolites These fibers are believed to have significant health benefits. Specifically, the dietary fiber derived from seaweeds has tremendous potential in human health and could be used as food supplements.45 Seaweeds contain varying concentrations of dietary fibers ranging from 1% in green algae to the highest 76.97 g/100 g in red algae (Gracilaria changii), as summarized in Table 1. Other studies have also revealed that red seaweeds contain varying and higher levels of dietary fiber, from 5.7 g/100 g (in G. cervicornis) to 64.7 g/100 g (in G. changii). These proportions of soluble dietary fiber in seaweeds are higher than those in fruits and vegetables and are considered advantageous for health, as it reduces the blood cholesterol and postprandial glucose levels and increases satiety.

Pharmacological applications of seaweed-derived compounds

Fucoidans

Fucoidans are fucose-containing sulfated polysaccharides found in many brown seaweed species. They are species specific and do not have a universal chemical structure. Yet, fucoidans are the primary cell wall constituent, present along with the alginate and cellulose in brown seaweeds.46 They are mainly composed of L-fucose units linked by α-(1→3) or alternating α-(1→4) and α-(1→3) glycosidic bonds. They also comprise sulfate ester groups at the C-2 and/or C-4 positions of fucose residues, along with other sugar constituents including glucose, galactose, mannose, xylose, acetyl groups, and uronic acids.47 Fucoidan content in brown seaweed varies from 10% to 20%, with the highest content 46.6% reported in Laminaria digitata (Phaeophyceae).4 Fucoidans possess a high molecular weight of around 20–2,000 kDa and are water soluble. These anionic polysaccharides are negatively charged due to their sulfate content. Presence of sulfate groups enhances their interactions with proteins and lipids, contributing to diverse bioactives. Fucoidans exhibit a wide range of biological activities including anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-viral, anti-bacterial, anti-coagulant, anti-thrombotic, anti-tumor, immunoregulatory, anti-obesity, and anti-diabetic (Table 2).47,83 These distinct biological characteristics of fucoidans are due to the degree of sulfation, molecular weight, and sugar composition. Highly sulfated, low-molecular weight fucoidans are associated with several biological properties, whereas highly branched or high-molecular weight fucoidans exhibit other distinct properties.84

Laminarins

Laminarins are the major storage polysaccharides present predominantly in brown seaweed species such as Laminaria, Saccharina, Ecklonia, and Eisenia and at lower levels in Fucus, Undaria, and Ascophyllum. Laminarins are water soluble and constitute up to 35% of the dry weight.4,85 They have a low molecular weight of approximately 5 kDa, with a degree of polymerization between 20 and 25.86 Structurally, they are made up of the main chain of glucose units linked by β-(1,3) linkages with β-(1,6)-linked side chains.87 Based on the nature of their reducing ends, two structural types of laminarins are found: M chain and G chain. M-chain laminarins have D-mannitol at the reducing end, whereas G-chain laminarins terminate with glucose at the reducing end.88 This short-chain, non-sulfated polysaccharide confer various biological activities, summarized in Table 2, which include anti-cancer, anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-coagulant, anti-obesity, anti-diabetic, and anti-viral.85,88 The biological function of laminarins depends on their molecular size, method of extraction, sugar composition, type of linkages, and molecular geometry.56

Carrageenans

Carrageenans are negatively charged sulfated polysaccharides majorly found in red seaweeds. They are anionic polysaccharides, with a molecular weight ranging from 100 to 1,000 kDa. The exact molecular weight can vary based on the type of carrageenan.89 Red seaweeds such as Kappaphycus alvarezii (formerly Euchema cottonii) and Euchema denticulatum contribute to majority of the carrageenan production globally. Carrageenans are long, straight chains of galactose sugars, with sulfate groups attached at various positions. They are built from repeat pairs of galactose units, with one unit of β-D-galactopyranose linked through its third carbon and α-D-galactopyranose or 3,6-anhydro-α-D-galactopyranose linked through its fourth carbon. Based on these structural differences, carrageenans are classified into three types, namely kappa, iota, and lambda. They differ mainly based on the degree and position of sulfation and the presence of 3,6-anhydro bridges.90 They possess a wide range of biological activities such as anti-oxidant, anti-bacterial, anti-viral, anti-hyperlipidemic, anti-coagulant, anti-cancer, and immunomodulatory effects (Table 2), which are attributed to their sulfated nature.91

Polyphenols and phlorotannins

Polyphenols are secondary metabolites derived from seaweeds. They are produced in response to the environmental and ecological stress including UV exposure, herbivore grazing, and other abiotic factors.44 Structurally, polyphenolic compounds consist of one or more hydroxyl groups (–OH), linked directly to an aromatic hydrocarbon (benzene) group.3 The presence of multiple hydroxyl groups makes them highly reactive toward free radicles, which are generated as an integral part of metabolism and can cause cytotoxicity and cellular dysfunction. Each (–OH) group of a polyphenol can donate a hydrogen or electron to neutralize a free radical, after which the radical diffuses its charge across the aromatic ring and becomes non-reactive.92 Seaweed-derived polyphenols include flavonols, catechins, and phlorotannins. Among them, phlorotannins are predominantly present in brown seaweeds, accounting for about 5%–15% of the dry weight. They are polyphenolic compounds formed through the polymerization of phloroglucinol (1, 3, 5-trihydroxybenzene) units via the acetate-malonate pathway.3,92 Marine algal phlorotannins are an extremely heterogeneous group of polyphenolic molecules composed of varying numbers of phloroglucinol units. Depending on their linkage types, phlorotannins are grouped into six structural subclasses, namely fucols, phlorethols and fuhalols, fucophlorethols, eckols and carmalols, and phenoxyphlorotannins. Their molecular weight ranges from 200 to over 650 kDa.10,44 Their multiple phenolic rings and abundant hydroxyl groups contribute to a wide range of potential biological activities,3,92 which are summarized in Table 2.

Marine alkaloids

Alkaloids are secondary metabolites produced in various seaweeds and serve ecological functions like defense and signaling. They are nitrogen-containing organic compounds with a cyclic ring, derived from amino acids or amine precursors such as tryptophan, tyrosine, and lysine. Seaweed alkaloids contain heterocyclic rings with at least one nitrogen atom. They may also incorporate halogen atoms (such as Cl, Br, and I) into their structures, forming halogenated alkaloids. These compounds often possess aromatic or polycyclic skeletal features, which contribute to their structural diversity. Marine alkaloids range from simple amine derivatives to complex halogenated multiring structures. These seaweed-derived alkaloids can be structurally classified into phenylethylamine derivatives, indole and halogenated indole alkaloids, and other alkaloids.93,94 Among them, caulerpin and racemosin are bis-indole alkaloids derived from the green seaweed Caulerpa species. Red seaweeds like Gracilaria species were also reported to contain halogenated indole alkaloids. Their diversity in terms of ring structures and halogenation patterns gives rise to anti-inflammatory, anti-microbial, cytotoxic, anti-oxidant, and other biological activities.94,95

Carotenoids

Carotenoids are naturally occurring pigments found in most fruits, vegetables, plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria. They have a wide range of functions in human health. However, humans cannot produce carotenoids and must ingest them through dietary intake or via supplementation.96 Different species of seaweeds contain distinct types of carotenoids, among which beta carotene, fucoxanthin, and tocopherol are important.92 Among these, fucoxanthin is a tetraterpenoid carotenoid that is predominantly found in different species of brown algae.8 Fucoxanthin functions as a light-harvesting pigment for photosynthesis and acts as a photoprotective agent against excess light energy. It absorbs specific wavelengths of blue and green light and transfers that energy to chlorophyll in seaweeds. Additionally, fucoxanthin serves as a potent anti-oxidant, protecting against oxidative damage.97 Most of the anti-oxidants such as beta carotene or ascorbic acid donate protons, whereas fucoxanthin donates electron to quench reactive oxygen species (ROS).41 Fucoxanthin derived from different seaweeds exhibits diverse biological properties including anti-oxidant, anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, neuroprotective, photoprotective, and osteoporosis preventive effects.8 Seaweed bioactive compounds, their mode of action, and biological activities are illustrated in Figure 2.

Seaweed bioactive compounds and their health benefits

Proteins and bioactive peptides

Seaweed proteins contain nutritionally valuable bioactive components, which play a significant role in their structure and metabolism. Seaweed proteins are made of a diverse range of molecules including enzymes, glycoproteins, cell wall-associated proteins, and phycobiliproteins.98 These compounds collectively contribute to the biological functionality of seaweeds and represent an important class of marine bioactives. Seaweed proteins and their derived peptides are nitrogen-containing compounds composed of amino acids and are often associated with complex cell wall polysaccharides such as ulvans, alginate, and carrageenans, which can influence their extractability and functional properties.99

Bioactive peptides from the proteins are typically released through enzymatic, physical, or chemical hydrolysis processes. These processes disrupt native protein folding, resulting in the production of short amino acid sequences with enhanced biological activity.99 These proteins are rich in essential amino acids and also include bioactive peptides with therapeutic properties.77 Seaweed proteins are rich in essential amino acids such as glycine, arginine, glutamic acid, and alanine, with the highest EAAs /NEAAs ratios generally found in red seaweeds (0.98–1.02), followed by green (0.72–0.97) and brown taxa (0.73).5

Several studies have reported diverse strategies for the extraction of seaweed proteins and preparation of bioactive peptides. In general, proteins are subjected to hydrolysis using proteolytic enzymes or physical and chemical treatments that disrupt their native structure and facilitate peptide release. Protein extraction may be carried out as a standalone process or as a combined approach involving two or more sequential or integrated steps.99 Enzymatic hydrolysis remains one of the most widely employed and selective methods for producing peptide-rich fractions from seaweeds.77 Beyond conventional enzymatic approaches, emerging green technologies such as subcritical water hydrolysis have gained increasing attention due to their efficiency and reduced chemical input.12 At a broader scale, biorefinery-oriented strategies integrate protein extraction with the recovery of other valuable seaweed components as a key valorization step within integrated processing frameworks.1 Notably, the protein hydrolysates and peptide-rich extracts derived from seaweeds have been widely reported to exhibit a broad spectrum of biological activities, including anti-oxidant, anti-microbial, anti-inflammatory, anti-hypertensive, anti-diabetic, and anti-tumor.98,99 Owing to these multifunctional bioactive properties, seaweed proteins and peptides have gained increasing interest for potential applications in the nutraceutical, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical sectors.100 The bioactive properties of proteins and peptides are summarized in Table 2.

Therapeutic properties and mechanisms of action

Anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory activities

Free radicals and Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) are formed in the body endogenously through metabolic processes and exogenously through environmental exposures. These free radicals cause oxidative stress, which is associated with the development of chronic diseases including cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, cancers, and diabetes. If unmanaged, these free radicals damage lipids, proteins, and DNA, leading to cellular dysfunction.48 Similarly, inflammation is caused by several factors, even without any injury. Factors such as autoimmune disorders, chronic stress, poor sleep, and obesity and external factors including pollution, smoking, and chemical exposure cause chronic inflammation.101 Anti-oxidants play a key role in neutralizing free radicals and alleviating oxidative stress, thus mitigating chronic inflammation,48 whereas anti-inflammatory compounds work by inhibiting pro-inflammatory pathways and reducing the production of inflammatory markers.102 Seaweeds exhibit substantial anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Tables 2 and 3) due to the abundance of bioactive compounds such as phlorotannins, flavonoids, carotenoids, sulfated polysaccharides, and vitamins.

Carrageenans are sulfated polysaccharides derived from red seaweeds and exhibit notable anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Native carrageenan isolated from Kappaphycus alvarezii possesses high anti-inflammatory activity up to 84.89% compared with commercial carrageenans.63 Sodium hydroxide-extracted carrageenan from Kappaphycus alvarezii exhibited a high radical-scavenging activity, measured in vitro using DPPH assays, indicating that the extraction chemistry significantly enhances the activity. The NaOH-extracted carrageenan displayed non-Newtonian gel-forming behavior, making it suitable for pharmaceutical use. Mechanistically, carrageenans can suppress nitric oxide and prostaglandin E2 synthesis that contributes to inflammation. They inhibit the activity or expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) enzymes, which make them a potent therapeutic agent against inflammatory conditions.64

Fucoidans, fucose-rich sulfated polysaccharides found in the brown seaweeds Padina pavonica and Saccharina japonica, exhibit strong anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The extracted fucoidan showed the highest ferric-reducing anti-oxidant power (FRAP; IC50 = 113.21 μg/mL).86 Similarly, another fraction of fucoidan extracted from Saccharina japonica showed significant radical-scavenging activity, with ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) assay) and FRAP values of 1.02 and 5.39 mg Trolox equivalent per gram (TE/g), respectively. This enhanced activity was correlated with its high sulfate content (17.85%) and the presence of (1→3)/(1→4)-linked α-L-fucopyranose (α-L-fucp) residues.55 Similar to fucoidans, laminarins, which are β-glucan polysaccharides present in brown algae, possess potent anti-oxidant activities through hydrogen donation and metal ion reduction.

Laminarins, β-glucan polysaccharides found in brown algae such as Laminaria hyperborea and Sargassum mcclurei, also exhibit anti-oxidant activity through hydrogen donation and metal ion reduction. They have demonstrated strong radical-scavenging, ferric-reducing, and lipoxygenase inhibitory activities (Figure 3). However, excessive purification reduces their bioactivity, likely due to the loss of synergistic co-metabolites.59 Molecular weight and sulfate content further influence their functional potency.60

In vitro anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory actions of laminarin

Brown algal phlorotannins, polymeric phenolic compounds composed of phloroglucinol units, show stronger anti-oxidant activity through electron transfer and radical quenching. Extracts of Eisenia bicyclis showed higher total phenolic content and strong radical-scavenging capacity (DPPH), with IC50 values of 47 μg/mL.67 Similarly, phlorotannins from Cystoseira compressa exhibited potent in vitro radical-scavenging activity, with low IC50 values in DPPH and ABTS assays and no cytotoxicity toward normal cells. Their molecular mechanism involves the direct transfer of hydrogen atoms to neutralize ROS and inhibit oxidative stress-induced cytokine formation, which contribute to the anti-inflammatory activity.68

In addition to polysaccharides and phenolics, seaweed proteins and peptides also exhibit pronounced anti-inflammatory potential. Green macroalgae of the genus Caulerpa exhibited notable anti-inflammatory effects mediated by proteinaceous components. Extracts of Caulerpa racemosa and Caulerpa sertularioides significantly inhibited protein denaturation, membrane destabilization, and protease activity in vitro, with Caulerpa sertularioides displaying stronger anti-inflammatory effects at lower concentrations. The activity was attributed to a ∼17 kDa lectin identified as a remorin-like protein, representing the first report of such an anti-inflammatory lectin from Caulerpa sertularioides.56 Similarly, protein- and peptide-rich fractions from Palmaria palmata exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity. Thermolysin-digested water extracts significantly inhibited TNF-α, IL-6, and nitric oxide production in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated macrophages and attenuated carrageenan-induced acute inflammation in vivo.125 Extracts from the green seaweed Ulva lactuca showed strong anti-oxidant potential in various in vitro assays, including ABTS and total anti-oxidant capacity measurements. The extracts exhibited high ABTS radical-scavenging activity (5.45 ± 0.11 mg AAE/g) and total anti-oxidant capacity (8.03 ± 0.06 mg TE/g), accompanied by substantial recovery of bioactive compounds such as phenolics, flavonoids, and saponins.126 Similarly, bioactive peptides derived from the red seaweed Gracilaria gracilis have shown significant anti-oxidant activity in vitro. Short-chain peptides obtained from enzymatic hydrolysis exhibited strong radical-scavenging and reducing capacities, as demonstrated by TEAC, DPPH, FRAP, and ABTS assays. In particular, the peptide fractions displayed Trolox Equivalent AntioxidantCapacity (TEAC) values of up to 60.8% and FRAP values of 4638 μmol TE/g, indicating that lower-molecular weight peptides contribute substantially to the anti-oxidant potential of G. gracilis.127

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) derived from the red seaweed Champia parvula exhibited strong radical-scavenging activity, as evidenced by DPPH, FRAP, and H2O2-scavenging assays, and are likely to exert protective cellular effects by neutralizing reactive radicals and maintaining a redox equilibrium.105 Meanwhile, methanolic and aqueous extracts from Caulerpa racemosa and Caulerpa sertularioides have shown strong radical-scavenging activity, validated by DPPH and ABTS assays.81 Furthermore, UV-treated samples of Sargassum filipendula exhibited enhanced anti-oxidant activity, as evaluated by DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, and metal-chelation assays, where light-induced generation of secondary metabolites improved anti-oxidant capacity across multiple assay systems.107 In addition, subcritical water hydrolysates of Ulva lactuca containing protein/peptides exhibited strong anti-oxidant activity, showing the highest ABTS radical-scavenging and total anti-oxidant capacity. The enhanced activity correlated with elevated levels of phenolics (gallic and vanillic acids) and protein/peptide-rich fractions, with no toxicity to RAW 264.7 cells observed.12

Similarly, acetone extracts of Chaetomorpha aerea showed strong anti-oxidant activity in DPPH and ABTS assays. The anti-inflammatory potential was validated using in vitro protein denaturation assays and in vivo carrageenan-induced paw edema models.106 Meanwhile, Padina pavonia (Phaeophyceae) and Jania rubens (Rhodophyceae) were evaluated in a CCl4-induced liver injury model in mice. Both the pre-treatment and post-treatment with these seaweed extracts significantly reduced hepatic necrosis, fibrosis, and inflammation.108 Both native and commercial carrageenans derived from Kappaphycus alvarezii exhibited enhanced anti-oxidant enzyme levels and displayed dose-dependent anti-inflammatory activity up to 2,000 mg/kg in Wistar albino rats. Brine shrimp lethality assay on both the carrageenans also showed LC50 values of 160.41 and 200 μg/mL, respectively. Meanwhile, hemolytic assays of the same also exhibited 38.31% and 65.75% activity, respectively. Although mechanistic and preclinical studies support the anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory potential of seaweed-derived compounds, clinical studies are still limited.

Anti-cancer activities

Worldwide, cancer has been considered as one of the leading causes of death. It is characterized by abnormal proliferation of cells and invasiveness of abnormal cells that spread to neighboring tissues. Although several anti-cancer drugs have been developed and used widely, they exhibit high toxicity to the tumor as well as normal healthy cells. Additionally, due to the increase in the occurrence of various types of cancers, there is a need for novel anti-cancer drugs. Currently, the search for novel anti-cancer compounds is being carried out using terrestrial plants and in marine environments.128 Seaweeds are a significant source of bioactive compounds with potential therapeutic applications. Bioactive compounds extracted from various seaweeds have shown anti-cancer properties, as summarized in Tables 2 and 3.109

Fucoidans, sulfated polysaccharides found in brown seaweeds, have repeatedly exhibited selective cytotoxicity to cancer cells without affecting normal cells. Fucoidan extracted from Fucus vesiculosus displayed cytotoxic and growth-inhibitory effects toward the cholangiocarcinoma (CL-6) cells. It disrupts the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), which leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in mitochondria-dependent apoptosis. Furthermore, fucoidan was reported to cause G0/G1 phase arrest by downregulating cyclin D1 and CDK4. These effects were accompanied by the upregulation of Bax and downregulation of Bcl-2 proteins, showing both pro-apoptotic and anti-proliferative roles (Figure 4).50 Similar to fucoidan, laminarin extracted from Padina pavonica exhibited cytotoxic activity against human colon adenocarcinoma (HT-29) cells. It triggered both apoptotic and necrotic pathways of cell death without causing any toxicity to normal cells.56 Additionally, aminated laminarin isolated from Saccharina cichorioides has been reported to radiosensitize MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells to radiation treatment. This radiosensitizing activity enhanced DNA fragmentation and triggered mitochondria-mediated apoptosis.57 Red algal sulfated polysaccharides, κ- and λ-carrageenans from Chondrus armatus, selectively inhibited gastrointestinal cancer cell lines including those of colon cancer (HCT-116 and RKO) and esophageal cancer (KYSE-30 and FLO-1). These bioactives induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest, with minimal toxicity to normal retinal pigment epithelial (RPE-1) cells.61

Proposed in vitro anti-cancer mechanism of fucoidan

Fucoidan extracted from Stoechospermum polypodioides (formerly Stoechospermum marginatum), Sargassum aquifolium (formerly Sargassum echinocarpum), and Fucus vesiculosus demonstrated robust anti-cancer effects against liver, breast, and cholangiocarcinoma cell lines. Fucoidan from the brown seaweed Stoechospermum marginatum and Sargassum echinocarpum exhibited substantial inhibition of HepG2 liver cancer cells and MCF-7 breast cancer cells through apoptosis, DNA fragmentation, and oxidative stress. The extracted fucoidan exhibited a selective index of 7.62 against the MCF-7 breast cancer cells. The fucoidan targeted directly only the cancer cells, without causing any toxicity or loss of viability to normal cells.49,129,130 Similarly, laminarin from Saccharina japonica (formerly Laminaria japonica) suppressed the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma through the upregulation of senescence marker protein-30 (SMP-30) in both in vivo and in vitro models.58 Carrageenan extracted from the red seaweed Gigartina pistillata effectively suppressed colorectal cancer stem cell (CSC)-enriched tumor spheres, derived from HT29, SW620, SW480, and HCT116 cell lines. Tetrasporophyte (T) carrageenan extract significantly reduced cellular viability in SW620- and SW480-derived tumor spheres in a dose-independent manner at sub-micromolar levels.44

Caulerpin, a bis-indole alkaloid extracted from Caulerpa species, has been recognized for its selective cytotoxic properties. In particular, caulerpin from Caulerpa cylindracea exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against the colorectal cancer cell lines HCT-116 and HT-29, with IC50 values of 119 and 179 μM, respectively. The compound induced apoptosis and inhibited colony formation in these cancer cell lines.131 Similarly, monomethyl caulerpinate from Caulerpa cylindracea exhibited dual biological activity; it potently inhibited SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and ACE2 receptor interaction, with an IC50 value of 190.2 nM. While exhibiting selective cytotoxicity toward the breast cancer cells MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231, with IC50 values of 13.0–17.3 μM, without affecting normal cells.47 Surprisingly, caulerpin was isolated from a new source, that is, a brown algae Sargassum platycarpum, for the first time. The isolated caulerpin inhibited HepG2 liver cancer cells (IC50 = 24.6 ± 2.1 μg/mL), with low toxicity.72

Fucoxanthin from Sargassum aquifolium (formerly Sargassum echinocarpum) showed a stable in silico binding affinity toward caspase-3, thus inducing apopstosis.74 In vitro studies from Sargassum fusiforme (formerly Hijikia fusiformis), Undaria pinnatifida, and Sargassum fulvellum have shown its inhibitory effect on ovarian cancer (PA-1) cells in a dose-dependent manner. Remarkably, it achieved an IC50 of 5.9 μM within 24 h, outperforming tamoxifen at equivalent concentrations, with fewer or negligible side effects.75

Crude water-soluble sulfated polysaccharides from Sargassum polycystum demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against HeLa cervical cancer cells without affecting the normal cells.112 Similarly, methanolic extracts of the green seaweed Ulva compressa (formerly Enteromorpha compressa) showed promising anti-cancer activity against oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), which was evidenced by clonogenic, scratch, and 3, (4,5- dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays.132 Meanwhile, methanolic extracts from Halimeda tuna were tested for their cytotoxic effects against the A549 lung cancer cells. The extracts exhibited an IC50 value of 2,771 μg/mL, which inhibited their potential to be used as chemopreventive agents.133

Methanolic extracts from the brown algae Padina pavonica and the red algae Jania rubens exhibited notable cytotoxic effects against human liver cancer Hep-G2 cells in both in vitro and in vivo models. In vitro assays including MTT and trypan blue exclusion confirmed a significant reduction in cell viability. In vivo models using Ehrlich ascites carcinoma (EAC) mice demonstrated reduced tumor cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis upon treatment with these extracts.109

Integration of nanotechnology with the seaweed-derived bioactives to produce novel nanoparticle formulations represents an advancement over traditional extracts in terms of anti-cancer potential and bioavailability. A nanoformulation, Fu/NCur/SeNPs, consisting of fucoidan from Undaria pinnatifida along with curcumin and selenium nanoparticles, was synthesized. This formulation exhibited enhanced efficacy against colorectal carcinoma cells through synergistic effects on DNA damage and autophagy in a dose-dependent manner, with efficiency superior to that of the standard chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin.51 Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) biosynthesized from the green seaweed Caulerpa taxifolia exhibited apoptosis-mediated suppression in HeLa cervical cancer cells.110 Similarly, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) synthesized from Ulva rigida exhibited strong anti-proliferative activity (92.62%) against MCF-7 breast cancer cells, with greater selectivity relative to normal cells (IC50 = 13 μg/mL), with a selective index of 3.2.111 AuNPs synthesized from the red seaweed Champia parvula also exhibited notable cytotoxicity against A549 lung carcinoma cells.105 Additionally, selenium-zinc oxide nanoparticles (Se-ZnO NPs) synthesized from Padina boergesenii induced strong cytotoxic responses in both HepG2 breast cancer and MCF-7 hepatoblastoma cell lines.134

Although available evidence for the anti-cancer role of seaweed-derived compounds remains preclinical, limited human studies have begun to validate the therapeutic potential of these compounds, particularly fucoidan. In a randomized, double-blind trial, low-molecular weight fucoidan (LMF) was tested in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer. The participants received 4 g of LMF twice daily for 6 months alongside standard chemotherapy. The treatment improved disease control and overall response without causing additional side effects and blood, kidney, or liver toxicity.135 In another study, cancer survivors with post-treatment fatigue received fucoidan from Fucus vesiculosus and Undaria pinnatifida orally for 8 weeks. The supplementation was safe, well tolerated, and caused improvements in fatigue, frailty, and inflammation, with no major safety concerns reported.136 These human clinical studies are summarized in Table 5.

Anti-microbial and anti-viral activities

In the last two decades, numerous infectious diseases have emerged or re-emerged, presenting greater threats to global public health. The emergence or re-emergence of pathogenic bacteria and infectious viruses has posed huge global health threats that may be fatal for humans.137 Several pathogens are re-emerging as multidrug-resistant strains due to drug resistance mutations caused by the increased utilization of antibiotics.138 Such challenges in public health have necessitated the development of novel therapeutics.137 Seaweeds contribute to a variety of pharmacological activities including anti-microbial and anti-viral, as summarized in Tables 2 and 3. Multiple studies have demonstrated that seaweed-derived compounds can inhibit the growth of a broad spectrum of bacteria, fungi, and biofilm-forming microbes including multidrug-resistant pathogens while exhibiting notable anti-viral activity or immunomodulatory responses.139

Aqueous extracts from the red seaweeds Chondrus crispus and Sarcodiotheca gaudichaudii, in combination with standard antibiotics such as tetracycline and streptomycin, exhibited enhanced anti-bacterial activity against Salmonella enteritidis. This synergistic activity is likely due to the ability of seaweed components to increase bacterial membrane permeability and downregulate efflux pump genes, thereby promoting the intracellular accumulation of antibiotics. Such combinations could also help reduce the antibiotic usage costs and mitigate the development of resistance in pathogens, particularly within the poultry industry.118 The mechanism of anti-microbial action of seaweed-derived compounds is shown in Figure 5.

Proposed anti-microbial and anti-viral mechanisms of seaweed-derived bioactive compounds based on in vitro studies

Fucoidan from Padina pavonica showed moderate anti-viral activity against HSV-1, with a selectivity index (SI = 4.21). It acts primarily by interfering with viral adsorption onto host cells, thereby inhibiting cell entry and early replication stages.52 Fucoidans from Sargassum henslowianum blocked HSV-1 and HSV-2 effectively at ultra-low concentrations (IC50 = 0.89 and 0.48 μg/mL, respectively), delaying the early infection stage by preventing virus binding to the cell surface.53 This action is thought to be mediated by the heavily charged sulfate groups in fucoidan, which can mask or neutralize viral glycoproteins responsible for host cell recognition. Ulvan, a sulfated heteropolysaccharide from Ulva lactuca, inhibited viral propagation in HAV-10 cells. This action is due to the ability of its negatively charged sulfate groups to disrupt microbial membranes and viral envelopes.119

Caulerpin from Caulerpa recemosa suppressed HSV-1 replication in Vero cells. It affects the early phase of the viral replication cycle by blocking viral DNA synthesis and interfering with key viral enzymes.107 Similarly, methanol extracts of Sargassum filipendula were found to inhibit HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (RT), exhibiting a distinct anti-retroviral mechanism by interfering with viral genome replication.107 Water-soluble polysaccharide extracted from Monostroma nitidum displayed significant anti-viral activity against enterovirus 71 (EV71) in both in vitro and in vivo studies by modulating host immune responses and blocking viral replication, while exhibiting minimal toxicity.140

Ulvan, from Ulva lactuca, also showed notable anti-bacterial activity against several pathogenic bacteria.119 Organic extracts of Amphiroa anceps, Corallina officinalis, and Sargassum filipendula demonstrated significant anti-bacterial effects against Salmonella typhimurium, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli. These extracts were found to suppress the infectivity of rotavirus (RV) and coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3), which were passed through cytotoxicity assays.116 Extracts from Kappaphycus alvarezii (red seaweed) exhibited strong anti-microbial effects against a wide range of human pathogens, including Bacillus subtilis, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Vibrio alginolyticus, Vibrio harveyi, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. The Kappaphycus extracts exhibited substantial anti-microbial activities, mainly by disrupting the bacterial membranes, inhibiting the efflux pumps, or by interfering with the protein synthesis.117 Despite extensive in vitro and in vivo evidence supporting anti-microbial and anti-viral activities, clinical studies evaluating seaweed-derived compounds for infectious diseases remain limited.

Anti-obesity and anti-diabetic activities

Rapid global urbanization, modernization, and socio-economic growth have improved our living standards. But, these developments have had a negative impact on lifestyle in aspects such as unhealthy eating habits, lack of exercise, increased stress, and environmental factors. These factors have contributed to the alarming growth of obesity and type 2 diabetes worldwide. Although prevention strategies like changes in lifestyle habits and medications have been recommended, they have not been successful in reducing the incidence of these diseases.141 As the recommended medications are also expensive, the need for novel compounds to address obesity and diabetes incidence has increased in recent years due to their low cost and negligible side effects.142 Seaweeds’ bioactive compounds have emerged as promising therapeutic agents due to their enriched composition, which could modulate the key metabolic pathways involved in obesity and diabetes, as summarized in Tables 2 and 3.

Compounds such as phlorotannins enhanced glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue by activating insulin signaling pathways, upregulating glucose transporter expression (GLUT4), and stimulating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). Similarly, phlorotannins from Eisenia bicyclis significantly enhanced glucose uptake more than 50% in C2C12 myotubes and upregulated the genes associated with insulin signaling and AMPK pathways, reflecting metformin-like insulin-sensitizing actions.67 Phlorotannin-rich fraction from Sargassum tenerrimum demonstrated comparable effects by lowering fasting glucose levels, reducing inflammatory markers (TNF-α), and improving insulin sensitivity.70 Similarly, phlorotannins from Cystoseira compressa reduced serum glucose and digestive enzyme activity, thereby preserving pancreatic β-cell integrity and elevating anti-oxidant defenses in diabetic rats.68 The simplified mechanism of action of phlorotannins involved in glucose metabolism and their anti-diabetic activity are illustrated in Figure 6.

Proposed mechanism of action of phlorotannins on glucose metabolism

Fucoidan, a sulfated polysaccharide, has been extensively reported for its enzyme inhibitory and insulin-sensitizing activities. Fucoidan from the brown seaweed Padina pavonica exhibited substantial α-amylase inhibitory activity (IC50 = 117.56 μg/mL), demonstrating its potential to reduce postprandial hyperglycemia and oxidative stress linked to diabetes. Similarly, fucoidan from Ecklonia maxima exhibited potent α-glucosidase inhibitory effect (IC50 = 0.27–0.31 mg/mL), compared to the standard anti-diabetic drug acarbose, through a mixed-type inhibition mechanism.54 These inhibitory activities of fucoidan against carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes delay glucose absorption, thereby aiding glycemic control.

Solvent extracts from seaweeds have also displayed distinct enzyme inhibitory and glucose-regulatory effects. Methanolic extracts of Ulva intestinalis (formerly Enteromorpha intestinalis) exhibited significant inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase, comparable to acarbose.132 Chaetomorpha aerea showed both in vitro and in vivo glucose-lowering activities through α-amylase inhibition and improved oral glucose tolerance.106 Methanolic extract of Ulva reticulata exhibited strong anti-diabetic activity through multitarget enzyme inhibition and insulin secretagogue effects. Ulva reticulata extracts demonstrated pronounced anti-diabetic activity by inhibiting key carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes and enhancing insulin secretion. The bioactive fraction significantly inhibited α-amylase and α-glucosidase, reducing blood glucose levels in normoglycemic and streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rats. Furthermore, it showed glucose-dependent insulin secretagogue effects comparable to those of standard anti-diabetic drugs.124 Polyphenols from Padina tetrastromatica showed marked inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase (IC50: 47.2 and 28.8 μg/mL) and a strong anti-oxidant potential, as demonstrated through DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays.65 Additionally, Padina arborescens extract showed strong intracellular lipid-reducing activity, implicating its use in functional food formulations targeting obesity.122

In addition to polysaccharides and polyphenols, protein and peptide-rich extracts from the red seaweed Palmaria palmata have demonstrated significant anti-obesity and anti-diabetic potential through enzyme inhibition mechanisms. Alcalase-hydrolyzed extracts with high protein content and degree of hydrolysis yielded low-molecular weight peptide fractions (<3 kDa) that effectively inhibited pancreatic lipase and α-amylase. Liquid chromatography- tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) profiling and in silico screening identified multiple non-toxic peptides containing known inhibitory motifs, while molecular docking confirmed strong interactions with both enzymes.40 In addition to enzyme inhibition, protein hydrolysates extracted from the red seaweed Palmaria palmata have demonstrated significant metabolic regulatory effects in experimental models. Oral administration of these hydrolysates improved glycemic control and reduced body weight in STZ-induced diabetic mouse models. Furthermore, the treatment significantly lowered the blood glucose levels and produced a substantial reduction in HbA1c levels, comparable to the observed effects of the standard anti-diabetic drug metformin.82 Mechanistically, peptides obtained from Palmaria palmata modulate the incretin pathway by inhibiting DPP-IV activity and enhancing the secretion of incretin hormones such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and Glucose-Dependent Insulinotropic Polypeptide (GIP), which subsequently promote insulin release and improve glucose regulation. Bioactive peptides including ILAP, LLAP, and MAGVDHI were identified as key contributors to this activity, as they help prolong the half-life of GLP-1 and support incretin-mediated insulin secretion. Furthermore, acute administration of these hydrolysates improved glucose tolerance and increased satiety in animal models.79,80 Moreover, protein hydrolysates obtained from the green seaweed Ulva rigida have also been reported to play a potential role in the regulation of metabolic disorders. Enzymatic hydrolysis generated peptides such as Ile-Pro and Ala-Phe-Leu, which exhibited significant angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory activity. In addition to enzyme inhibition, the hydrolysates demonstrated considerable ferric-reducing anti-oxidant capacity, suggesting that Ulva rigida-derived peptides may contribute to metabolic health through combined anti-oxidant and enzyme inhibition mechanisms.78

Fucoxanthin, a carotenoid found abundantly in brown seaweeds such as Sargassum angustifolium, exerted significant anti-obesity and anti-diabetic actions in type 2 diabetic mouse models. Fucoxanthin improved blood glucose levels, insulin secretion, and lipid metabolism in STZ-nicotinamide-induced type 2 diabetic mouse models, while enhancing pancreatic β-cell function and preventing weight gain. Fucoxanthin enhances metabolism by upregulating the uncoupling protein-1 (UCP1) in white adipose tissues, thereby promoting thermogenesis and fatty acid oxidation. It also improves insulin sensitivity by modulating adipokines and oxidative balance.143 Compounds derived from red algae have also significantly contributed to the metabolic regulation. Polysaccharide from Bangia fuscopurpurea substantially reduced body weight and lipid accumulation in the adipose tissue of obese C57BL/6 mice. Furthermore, it effectively lowered lipid levels in both blood and liver, with an efficiency comparable to that of inulin.123 Ethanolic extracts from Grateloupia elliptica suppressed lipid accumulation and adipogenesis in both adipocyte cultures and high-fat diet (HFD)-fed mice, indicating the regulation of lipogenic pathways both in vitro and in vivo.120 Similarly, extracts from Gloiopeltis furcata exhibited both anti-obesity and anti-diabetic effects by inhibiting pancreatic lipase and improved glucose tolerance in obese mice.121 Furthermore, oral administration of enzymatically derived Palmaria palmata hydrolysates significantly reduced non-fasting and fasting blood glucose levels, lowered HbA1c, and decreased food intake in STZ-induced diabetic mouse models, with efficacy comparable to that of metformin. These effects are likely linked to the capability of bioactive peptides in inhibiting DPP-4 and modulating glycemic control pathways. Collectively, these findings support Palmaria palmata-derived peptides as promising functional food ingredients for modulating lipid digestion and postprandial glucose regulation.144

In addition to the preclinical findings, a human study also evaluated brown seaweed extract. The polyphenol extract of Ascophyllum nodosum + Fucus vesiculosus (InSea2) was given at 500 mg/day during a moderate calorie-restricted diet. The extract helped in weight loss and improved fasting glucose, insulin, and C-peptide levels, as well as glucose and insulin responses during a 2-h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). The results indicated that the supplement was safe and well tolerated (Table 5).145

Seaweeds in functional foods and nutraceuticals

Edible seaweed varieties

Seaweeds are nutritionally significant and a prolific source of bioactive substances; thus, they can serve as an alternative food source. Seaweeds are conventionally consumed as soups and vegetables; however, they are also used as condiments and incorporated into sauces. Seaweeds are regarded as universally suitable foods due to their low caloric content and the variety of essential nutritional compounds.146 All of the three groups of seaweeds comprise many edible species, which are widely used in various cuisines around the world. On the other hand, edible seaweeds as functional food ingredients have gained recognition due the presence of bioactive compounds.23

Among brown seaweeds, notable edible species include Saccharina japonica (Kombu), Undaria pinnatifida (wakame),147 Himanthalia elongata,148 and Durvillaea antarctica.149 These seaweeds are particularly important because they contain a high proportion of complex polysaccharides like fucoidan, alginate, and laminarin, which have biological properties like anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, prebiotic, etc. They are also rich in calcium, iodine, iron, and other essential trace minerals, which render them nutritionally dense and vital for metabolic and thyroid function. Red seaweed-derived compounds such as phycoerythrin and hydrocolloids, like agar and carrageenans, are widely used as coloring, gelling, stabilizing, and thickening agents in food industries. Apart from their industrial use, several species of red seaweeds such as Chondrus crispus, Porphyra spp. (Nori), Gracillaria spp., and Palmaria palmata (Dulse) are used directly as foods due to their rich protein content.100 These seaweeds are often consumed in dried or roasted forms, especially in sushi wraps, soups, and seaweed snacks.150,151 Edible green seaweeds, including Ulva lactuca (sea lettuce) and Caulerpa lentillifera (sea grapes), are characterized by their vibrant green color due to high chlorophyll content.13 They are abundant in ulvans, unusual sulfated polysaccharides with immunomodulatory and anti-oxidant capabilities. These seaweeds are rich in vitamins (A, C, and B-complex), minerals, and phenolic compounds that contribute to their health-promoting potential.152 All edible seaweeds vary in their taste, texture, and culinary application, but they serve as valuable sources of fiber, trace elements, and bioactive compounds, with potential health-promoting effects. Incorporation of edible seaweeds in daily diets can improve overall health and metabolism.

Bioactive extracts and formulated products

Seaweeds serve as a rich source of bioactive compounds such as polysaccharides, peptides, phlorotannins, and essential nutrients including omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals. This makes them valuable for developing functional foods and nutraceuticals. Polysaccharides like alginate, carrageenans, and agar are widely used as stabilizers and gelling agents in syrups, desserts, sauces, and dairy products. Apart from commercial seaweed-based products, functional seaweed products are now mainstream. For instance, GimMe organic, Tao Kae Noi, and Simple Truth produce organically certified, low-calorie, nutrient-rich roasted seaweed snacks that combine crisp texture with iodine, vitamins, and minerals. Similarly, sea moss gel, seaweed capsules, and sea moss gummies are produced by Myla’s Doctor Seaweed that provides easy-to-consume nutrition boosters packed with iodine and anti-oxidants. In food and meat innovation, seaweed extracts serve as alternatives that reduce sodium and increase mineral levels (Algysalt, PureSea, and Seagreens), without compromising sensory quality.24

Institutions like ICAR-CMFRI have developed patented nutraceuticals (e.g., Cadalmin ADe, ACe, ATe, AHe, and AOe) targeting conditions such as diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and arthritis by using marine extracts from Sargassum spp., Turbinaria spp., Gracilaria spp., and Kappaphycus spp.153 Furthermore, other seaweed-based commercial products and their specifications are summarized in Table 4. The listed marketed and health claims are supported to varying extents by compositional, pre-clinical, or limited human evidence. Collectively, these products highlight the seaweed’s translational potential in functional health products.

Prebiotics and gut health

Whole seaweed and seaweed polysaccharides possess notable prebiotic potential, primarily attributed to their rich nutritional composition, especially the high dietary fiber content.170 These polysaccharides, including carbohydrates, sulfates, and associated proteins, have been shown to favorably modulate the gut microbiota. For instance, the polysaccharide extracted from Bangia fuscopurpurea (BFP) showed anti-obesity activity in HFD-induced C57BL/6 mice. The polysaccharide could more effectively reduce body weight and fat accumulation in obese mice than inulin. BFP modulated the intestinal microbial taxa by inhibiting Clostridium and Aerococcus. Thus, BFP polysaccharide may serve as an effective dietary prebiotic in reducing obesity.123 In a simulated human gut model, Australian seaweeds such as Phyllospora comosa, Ecklonia radiata, and Ulvan ohnoi significantly enhanced the development of butyrate-producing firmicutes and increased the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA). Their effects were stronger that those observed with standard prebiotics like inulin, and they also increased the overall microbial diversity, which highlight their potential as functional foods for supporting gut and immune health.171 Further evidence comes from an in vitro colon fermentation model using Himanthalia elongata, which selectively stimulated the growth of Bacteroides ovatus, in contrast to inulin, which promoted Parabacteroides distasonis growth. Although Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus levels remained largely unchanged, H. elongata yielded a substantial SCFA concentration (56.11 mM at 24 h), suggesting effective fermentability and prebiotic action despite taxonomic differences in the microbiota compositon.120 Likewise, Durvillaea antarctica, a commonly consumed brown seaweed, contains prebiotic polysaccharides such as alginate and fucoidan that enhance beneficial bacteria like Bifidobacteria and stimulate SCFA production. Its bioactive compounds support gut barrier integrity and are linked to metabolic and immune benefits, including anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, and hypoglycemic effects.149 Similarly, in vitro studies have shown that whole seaweeds like Saccharina japonica and Undaria pinnatifida promote the growth of beneficial microbes, such as Bifidobacterium adolescentis and Parabacteroides distasonis, while boosting the levels of SCFAs, especially acetic and butyric acids. Their effects on microbial composition and fermentation activity were comparable to those of inulin, which reinforce their prebiotic potential.172

Furthermore, aquaculture studies indicate that seaweed-based diets enhance gut health by increasing microbial diversity, stimulating digestive enzymes, and improving intestinal structure, such as villus height and goblet cell count. Sulfated polysaccharides like laminarins, ulvans, and fucoidans also contribute to SCFA production and gut barrier maintenance.173,174 Overall, seaweed-derived polysaccharides and bioactive compounds effectively influence gut microbiota and promote SCFA production, thereby maintaining the intestinal integrity. The mechanism of prebiotic action of seaweed polysaccharides and their role in gut microbiota modulation for SCFA production are illustrated in Figure 7. Due to their ability to improve the gut barrier integrity, seaweeds hold promising potential in the development of functional food for gut and metabolic health.

Bioavailability and biocompatibility of seaweed bioactives

Fucoidans

High-molecular weight fucoidans from brown seaweeds exhibit very limited absorption across the intestinal epithelium, and current evidence indicates that their primary site of action is within the gastrointestinal lumen rather than systemic circulation.84,86,87 In contrast, depolymerized or low-molecular weight fractions of fucoidans demonstrate enhanced biological activity. Molecular weight, degree of sulfation, and position are the key determinants of interactions with gut mucosa, microbiota, and host tissues.47,84 Several studies reporting the anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, and immunomodulatory effects have used purified fucoidans in cell cultures or administered them via non-oral routes in animal models.53,54,56 While these studies provide valuable mechanistic insight, they do not necessarily reflect oral bioavailability or clinically relevant systemic exposure in humans.52,55 Therefore, translating in vitro and in vivo efficacy into human outcomes requires careful consideration of absorption, metabolism, and microbiome-mediated effects.

Laminarins and β-glucans

Laminarins, non-sulfated β-glucans predominantly found in brown seaweeds, largely resist digestion in the upper gastrointestinal tract, reach the colon intact, and undergo microbial fermentation to produce SCFAs. Thus, their biological effects are predominantly microbiome mediated, including modulation of gut immunity and metabolic signaling.85,88 Structural features such as molecular size, degree of polymerization, and branching pattern strongly influence fermentability, SCFA production, and immunomodulatory activity. However, human pharmacokinetic and systemic absorption data remain scarce, limiting the ability to predict in vivo efficacy beyond microbiome-dependent mechanisms.2

Ulvans

Ulvans, sulfated polysaccharides from the genus Ulva, exhibit anti-viral, anti-microbial, anti-oxidant, and metabolic activities, and they have shown good tolerability in preclinical studies.140 However, direct evidence for the systemic bioavailability of intact polymers is limited.119 The majority of reported effects are inferred from changes in immune markers, metabolic parameters, or gut fermentation profiles, rather than measured circulating levels. Structural characteristics, such as high charge density and complex branching, favor colonic fermentation and mucosal immune interactions, suggesting that microbiome-mediated mechanisms largely influence the bioavailability. Current in vitro and in vivo studies do not fully characterize digestion, intestinal absorption, or circulating metabolites; so, oral biocompatibility and systemic exposure remain inferred.91

Phlorotannins and seaweed-derived peptides

Phlorotannins, polyphenolic compounds from brown seaweeds, have exhibited potent anti-oxidant, anti-diabetic, and metabolic activities in vitro models.44,68,70 Seaweed-derived peptides, including anti-coagulant peptides from nori hydrolysates and anti-diabetic peptide fractions, are typically assessed using simulated digestion models or in vitro cell systems, which have provided mechanistic insight, but without any data, into bioavailability.77,124,175 Consequently, while these compounds show promising bioactivity, their oral biocompatibility and systemic exposure in humans remain largely inferred, rather than empirically validated.

Matrix, processing, and microbiome-mediated effects

The food matrix and processing methods strongly influence the release and stability of nutrients in seaweeds. For instance, processing-induced changes can significantly affect the content of minerals, including iodine, and modulate polysaccharide structure, protein availability, and anti-oxidant profiles in edible seaweeds.11,45,176 Furthermore, in vitro colonic fermentation and prebiotic studies indicate that the majority of health effects from seaweed polysaccharides are microbiome mediated. Shifts in the gut microbial composition and the production of SCFAs appear to drive the observed metabolic, immunomodulatory, and anti-inflammatory benefits, rather than the absorption of intact macromolecules.9,41 Together, these findings underscore the absence of robust clinical pharmacokinetic studies across all major classes. Standardized extraction methods, molecular characterization, and dose-exposure relationships are required to bridge the gap between promising preclinical findings and clinically validated applications.

Safety, regulatory, and toxicological considerations

Iodine and heavy metal content

Iodine (I), an essential nutrient found abundantly in seaweeds, regulates metabolism and plays a crucial role in overall growth and development. It is a base component for the synthesis of thyroid hormones, triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4).177 Iodine is a key component that regulates various biochemical reactions like protein synthesis and enzymatic activity. Additionally, it aids in the development of brain, pituitary glands, heart, muscle, and kidneys.178 Nearly 30% of the global population is at the risk of iodine deficiency.179 In India, around 23.6% tribal population of Orissa was found to have endemic goiter, which is directly caused by iodine deficiency.180 Other disorders caused by iodine deficiency include cretinism, hypothyroidism, and neurological impairments such as mental retardation, deaf mutism, and neuromotor defects, especially in children.181,182,183

Iodine exists in three forms: iodate (IO3−), iodide (I−), and iodine (I2). Seaweeds contain higher concentrations of iodine than other sources. The concentration of iodine and its available chemical form vary according to the seaweeds. Among them, brown seaweeds, particularly kelp, contain a higher concentration of iodine than other seaweeds.184,185 Brown algae such as Laminaria species have been reported to contain iodine levels ranging from 1,700 to 31,000 μg/g dw, and the species of Saccharina contain iodine in the range 200–7,000 μg/g dw.176 Iodine levels of 5,700 ± 660 mg kg−1 DW were found in Saccharina latissima.186 The highest iodine concentration of 22,911 mg/kg was reported in dried seaweeds by European Union (EU), Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF).187 Excessive intake of iodine also results in several disorders such as goiter, thyroid autoimmunity, and hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, which was found to be prevalent in populations consuming seaweeds.185,188 Thus, both the insufficient and excessive intake of iodine levels may result in lack of thyroid synthesis and other negative effects.177

Daily prescribed levels of iodine as recommended by the National Institutes of Health are as follows: 110–130 μg for new born and infants, 90–120 μg for children, 150 μg for teens and adults, and 220–290 μg of iodine for pregnant and lactating woman.189 Excessive levels of iodine in algae can be reduced by several processing methods. Simple washing of the fresh biomass of Ascophyllum nodosum and F. vesiculosis and in water reduced 34% and 44% of iodine in the seaweeds, respectively.190 Soaking freshly collected Saccharina latissima in tap water at 30°C–32°C for 5 min significantly reduced iodine levels up to 78%–85%. Blanching the dried Saccharina latissima for 2–5 min in brief intervals reduced 93%–94% of iodine.191

Similar to iodine, other heavy metals can also be readily absorbed by seaweeds due to the accumulation of toxic compounds in the marine ecosystems. External factors such as pH, salinity, water temperature, concentration of toxic elements, and species specificity determine the accumulation of heavy metals in seaweeds.192 Due to the higher biosorption capacity, seaweeds can bind to and concentrate heavy metals multi-folds higher than the levels in seawater, which indicate them as substitutes for biological filters and sinks for the pollutants.192 More specifically, the cell wall of seaweeds containing polysaccharides (agar, alginates, carragenans, etc.) contains binding sites for carboxyl, phosphate, and sulfate groups that attract and bind to the surrounding metal ions.193 These heavy metals may pose risks to both animal and human health.194 Some of the common elements reported in seaweeds include arsenic (As), lead (Pb), mercury (Hg), and cadmium (Cd).176,195

Cd contamination occurs due to the leaching of materials such as paints, fossil fuels, fertilizers, and PVC polymers in the environment.196 Exposure to Cd causes osteoporosis, renal failure, and cancer in vital organs.197 Among heavy metals, Cd poses the highest risk of 1.73 for stroke.198 Seaweed such as Codium fragile have been reported to accumulate 32.8 mg/kg dw of Cd.199

Pb contamination in the environment occurs from battery wastes, paints, mining, metal plating, metal smelting, and from agricultural fertilizers and pesticides. Brown algae such as Turbinaria ornata have higher capacity to absorb Pb due to the presence of a higher polysaccharide content with carboxyl and sulfate groups, which have active metal-binding sites.200 Seaweeds in Malaysia have been reported to contain 3.75–10.85 μg/g of Pb. A study in Italy recorded 3.4 ± 1.9 mg kg dw of Pb in various seaweed species.193 Prevalence of respiratory problems such as asthma in workers have been reported to be caused by higher exposure to Pb. It also interrupts physiological functions and triggers various respiratory problems, such as asthma, lung cancer, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and also affects other systems like nervous, kidney, and cardiovascular systems.201 Prevalence of Pb exposure also increases the risk of stroke, with risk factor of 1.63, which is next to Cd.198

Hg persists in both organic and inorganic forms in marine environments. Seaweeds such as Codium fragile and Ulva (formerly Enteromorpha) species from Mediterranean Sea have been found to contain Hg levels of 26.1 ± 0.23 μg/kg dw and 336.5 ± 130.9 μg/kg dw, respectively.199 Both forms of Hg are readily absorbed by seaweeds.202 Among these, the inorganic form of Hg is absorbed by the respiratory tract, whereas the organic form, particularly methylmercury, is absorbed by the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.203 Methylmercury, once ingested, readily crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and is accumulated in the brain. This leads to neurotoxic effects and makes a person more vulnerable to cognitive impairments and developmental delays. In contrast, the organic form of Hg tends to affect vital organs such as liver and kidneys.202

As contamination mainly occurs through industrial process. Seaweeds have the ability to absorb As. In seaweeds, As is assimilated via phosphorous pathways and can be converted into various forms. Total As contents vary according to the seaweed type and typically range between 10.7 and 162 mg/kg of dry biomass in brown seaweeds, 10.3–39.4 mg/kg in red algae, and 0.30–1.22 mg/kg in green algae. A brown seaweed, Sargassum fusiforme (formerly Hizikia fusiformis), consumed as food in Japan and Korea, has been reported to contain higher As levels 67. 5 mg/kg.204 Upon exposure, As can be converted into toxic dimethylarsinic acid (DMA) in mammals. 3.75 mg/kg of inorganic As has been reported in Himanthalia elongata, without detectable DMA. This DMA was considered as a potential human carcinogen, which could induce strand breaks in DNA.205 The list of heavy metals reported in seaweeds and its health effects are illustrated in Figure 8. Overall, reports for the presence for higher concentrations of iodine and heavy metal contaminants in seaweeds necessitate robust monitoring, proper processing, and regulatory oversight to ensure consumer safety.

GRAS status and global regulations

GRAS (generally recognized as safe) designation has been established by the FDA, intended specifically for food ingredients. This recognition exempts certain food ingredients to be marketed without FDA approval. However, GRAS status can be applicable only to the food ingredients and not extended to therapeutic purposes.206 As of now, GRAS listings have not been issued for all individual edible seaweeds. GRAS status has been assigned to numerous seaweed products, particularly phycocolloids such as alginate from brown seaweeds, and carrageenans and agar from red seaweeds. These seaweed polysaccharides are specifically used as emulsifiers, thickeners, stabilizers, and gelling agents in food products.207 Among them, carrageenans have been reported to cause allergic reactions, although such cases are relatively rare. Carrageenans have been associated with rare IgE-mediated allergic reaction. Such allergic reactions are sometimes triggered by dairy products containing carrageenan as a hidden ingredient, and it may cause intestinal inflammation in sensitive individuals, especially those with gut disorders or compromised intestinal barriers.207,208 However, some of the whole seaweed powders, oligosaccharides like ulvans and laminarians, and other seaweed extracts are still under regulatory consideration and can be self-affirmed as GRAS by individual companies with supporting safety data.209 Some of the seaweeds are used as foods in countries like Korea, Japan, and China. These seaweeds are traditionally consumed and are often considered as safe through longer consumption, rather than being recognized as GRAS.210

Currently, there are no technical guidelines or specific international standards established by the Codex Alimentarius Commission to directly address food safety concerns related to seaweeds.211 However, several countries have developed their national regulations, focusing on the control of contaminants like heavy metals such as As and iodine. In Europe, the European Union Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 and regulation (EC) no. 396/2005 have established maximum levels (MLs) for certain contaminants and Hg in algae. But, specific standards for contaminants like As, Cd, and Pb in whole seaweed remain limited.212 Furthermore, iodine in seaweed is not regulated under EU legislation, and it is still considered as a nutrient. France was the first country in the European Union to conduct a safety evaluation on seaweeds for human consumption and has established MLs for heavy metals in edible seaweeds.213,214,215 Similarly, Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR), Germany, has imposed regulatory limits on iodine levels of dried seaweeds.216 The United States offers only regulatory limits to iodine content in seaweed products but has not established safety regulations for toxic contaminants and heavy metals in edible seaweeds.217 For Australia, several edible seaweed species are permitted, but the iodine content of those seaweeds alone has been regulated by the FSANZ (Food Standards Australia New Zealand) due to the health risks associated with overconsumption of iodine.218

In Asia, National Food Safety Standard of China (GB 2762-2022) has specified maximum levels for the presence of As and Pb in seaweed products,219 while the feed hygiene standard sets maximum levels for heavy metals like As, Pb, Cd, and Hg in feeds containing seaweed materials.220 Hong Kong has regulated MLs for As in seaweeds under its Food Adulteration (Metallic Contamination) Regulation,221 and Taiwan has regulated standards to include MLs for heavy metals in seaweeds.222 In India, there is no specific limit for heavy metal contaminants or iodine in whole seaweeds. But, specific limits have been set for iodine content in seaweed-derived food additives like agar, alginate, and carrageenans by FSSAI standards.223 While GRAS status and national regulations for certain seaweed-derived ingredients are available, there is no comprehensive international standards available for whole seaweeds. These variations in contaminant levels across different countries need to be standardized to ensure consumer protection and global compliance.

The global seaweed farming industries reached an impressive yield of 35.2 million tons (live weight) in 2021. From 2000 to 2021, its market value increased 3-folds, growing from $5 billion to $17 billion, and is projected to grow by 7-fold and reach $85 billion by 2026.224 The seaweed industries are geographically leaded by Asia.225 In Asia, China alone contributed 61% of the global production, with 99.1% of its output being cultivated. Followed by China, Indonesia is another major producer, contributing 26% to the global supply, while countries like South Korea, North Korea, the Philippines, Japan, and Malaysia also remain as significant players in seaweed production. Outside Asia, countries like Tanzania, Russia, and Chile contribute to seaweed production marginally, together accounting for 0.34% of global production.226 In Africa, seaweed production is largely driven by Tanzania, which peaked around 178,000 tons in 2015 but declined to 81,000 tons by 2021.227

In terms of global trade, seaweed exports were estimated to be approximately $943 million, whereas imports reached $1.2 billion in 2021.228 Asian countries dominated both the exports ($590 million) and imports ($762 million). Followed by Asia, America and Europe have exported around $192 million and $143 million and imported $165 million and $245 million worth of seaweed products, respectively.228 Interestingly, China remains as the largest producer and consumer of seaweeds. It ranked fourth in seaweed exports, next to the Indonesia, South Korea, and Chile due to its high domestic demand. However, China remains as the largest importer, accounting for about 41% of global seaweed imports in 2021, followed by Japan ($193 million) and the United States ($104 million). Other top importing countries include Russia ($51 million), France ($30 million), and Australia ($29 million).226,228

In the global context, India is an emerging key player, with significant untapped potential in seaweed cultivation. As of 2023, India has reported a total seaweed production of 72,385 tonnes (wet weight).229 While this volume remains modest compared to those of other leading Asian producers like China and Indonesia, the country’s seaweed industry is slowly gaining a momentum. Based on the reports of Central Marine Fisheries Research Institute (CMFRI), India has the capacity to cultivate approximately 9.7 million tonnes of seaweeds annually across 342 potentially identified coastal sites, indicating a greater scope for scale-up and commercialization. The Indian seaweed market was valued at $2.03 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach $2.12 billion by 2030, growing at a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 3.66% during the forecast period.230

Sustainability and ethical harvesting

In general, seaweed production takes places through two primary methods—cultivation and wild harvesting, with majority of global production now being derived from cultivation. Seaweed farming, which is a key constituent of the circular blue bioeconomy, not only contributes to biomass production but also offers important ecological functions such as carbon sequestration and climatic change mitigation.231 Seaweeds absorb the atmospheric carbon-di-oxide and converts it into polysaccharides, thus playing a major role in reduction of greenhouse gases. Seaweed cultivation was considered a sustainable method that requires no freshwater, fertilizers, or arable land and exerts minimal ecological impact.226 Accordingly, seaweed cultivation has been recognized for its potential to alleviate marine pollution, product biodiversity, and support food security (Figure 9). Cultivation of seaweeds has emerged as a promising source of livelihood, especially for coastal and deprived communities in Zanzibar, Tanzania. Seaweed farming began in Zanzibar in 1989 and grew rapidly, which employed around 15,000–20,000 people, majorly women. This initiative increased the seaweed production from 8,080 tonnes to 176,000 tonnes by 2016.232 Moreover, this woman-led sector not only provided income but also promoted social cohesion, knowledge sharing, and dietary improvements through the use of cultivated seaweeds.233

Seaweed sustainability, health, and industrial application nexus

Similarly, in India, Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY) scheme, which was launched in 2020 with a budget of ₹640 crores, has encouraged sustainable seaweed cultivation initiatives by approving 46,095 rafts and 65,330 monoline tube nets for the people of coastal and fishing communities. These efforts have enabled coastal women to cultivate seaweeds, mainly Kappaphycus alvarezii, producing over 36,000 tonnes of wet seaweeds, and the initiative was aimed to scale-up the cultivation to 1.12 million tonnes in five years.229 These incidences highlight how the sustainable seaweed practices have contributed to the empowerment of woman and enhancement of livelihoods, while simultaneously addressing the ecological concerns.

Despite its benefits, seaweed cultivation also raises several environmental concerns when not managed properly. Large-scale monoculture farms, especially those located in ecologically sensitive shallow marine zones, could degrade the seagrass ecosystems, thereby reducing the biodiversity and disrupting habitats for several species including fish, turtles, and dugongs.234 Seaweed monocultures are highly prone to diseases and pest invasions, thus acting as reservoirs for pathogens that may affect native seaweed flora.235 In India, red seaweeds such as Gracilaria sp., and Kappaphycus alvarezii are cultivated widely. Among them, Kappaphycus alvarezii, an exotic species cultivated extensively in the Palk Bay region, smothered and caused mortality of coral reefs such as Acropora species in Gulf of Mannar regions.236,237 Such ecological impacts necessitate stricter controls and biodiversity assessments prior to the introduction of non-native species.

Parallel to the cultivation practices, wild harvesting also poses critical sustainability challenges. Seaweeds play crucial ecological roles in marine food webs and serve as foundational species in many coastal habitats. However, unethical harvesting practices pose significant threats to marine biodiversity. Historically, species like Ascophyllum nodosum have been harvested in the North Atlantic for centuries.238 In Tamil Nadu, India, wild harvesting still remains a traditional livelihood activity, especially for coastal women. However, indiscriminate and excessive harvesting of species such as Sargassum, Gracilaria, Gelidiella, and Turbinaria has led to the loss of biodiversity and decline in their population.239,240 Overharvesting has even caused the extinction of seaweed colonies in several regions.241 In response to those degradations, Tamil Nadu government imposed a temporary ban on harvesting Gracilaria spices and has restricted Sargassum collection to promote its conservation.242

In order to support seaweed conservation and provide alternative livelihoods, the government of Tamil Nadu announced the establishment of a multipurpose seaweed park, which focuses on sustainable seaweed cultivation, marine biodiversity protection, and education. This initiative is targeted to promote the cultivation of seaweeds in 136 coastal villages across six districts, which involves over 8,821 people.242,243 The “seaweed park” will also feature aquaria and educational facilities that showcase the ecological role of seaweeds. Global initiatives including the Kelp Forest Challenge aim to restore 1 million hectares of kelp forest and further protect 3 million hectares of kelp forests by 2040. Such initiatives exemplify the growing emphasis on ethical, sustainable seaweed practices supported by international collaborations.244

Seaweed value chain and processing-quality-safety nexus

Beyond sustainable cultivation and ethical harvesting, the downstream processing of seaweeds critically regulates the feasibility, safety, and quality of seaweed-derived products. The seaweed value chain typically follows a series of stages including cultivation or wild collection, harvesting, primary dewatering, stabilization or drying, extraction, and fractionation of target components, further followed by final formulation into food, nutraceutical, feed, or biostimulant products.245

After harvesting of fresh biomass, water is usually drained and pre-cleaned. Stabilization or drying may then be achieved through simple, low-technology methods, such as sun-drying or higher-throughput techniques including hot-air or tunnel dryers. Although less common, freeze drying is sometimes applied for high-value applications.245,246,247 The dried biomass is further processed for the extraction of hydrocolloids, proteins and peptides, pigments, lipids, and mineral-rich fractions. These extracts can then be formulated into a wide range of end products.247,248

At industrial scale, the production process for certain single products, particularly hydrocolloids such as carrageenans, agar, and alginate, is well established. These processes typically involve alkaline or acid treatments, followed by filtration, salt or alcohol precipitation, and drying, which are implemented in commercial plants worldwide.245,247 In contrast, seaweed biorefineries are being developed to move beyond single-product value chains (e.g., carrageenan or alginate only) toward integrated processes that recover multiple product streams from the same biomass. These integrated multiproduct biorefineries sequentially recover polysaccharides, proteins, pigments, and other bioactives from the same biomass, thereby improving resource efficiency, economic returns, and sustainability.249 Yet, they remain largely at pilot or demonstration scale. These approaches often rely on enzyme-assisted extraction, membrane separations, and large-volume aqueous processing.248

In designing such biorefineries, top-down and bottom-up approaches are applied for complementary reasons. Top-down approach starts with the processing of whole seaweed biomass and sequentially fractionate it to extract high-value compounds first, followed by lower-value fractions.250 This method is attractive because it can be aligned with existing large-scale unit operations and is well suited to high-volume, relatively low-margin products. This approach allows utilization of most of the biomass, minimalizing waste and infrastructure costs across several product streams.251

Meanwhile, the bottom-up approach focuses on selectively isolating or synthesizing target compounds from seaweed fractions. This approach is applied when the primary objective is to obtain high-value, functionally tailored ingredients, rather than maximal bulk recovery.250 It involves milder and slower processes like controlled enzymatic hydrolysis and membrane fractionation for the extraction of ingredients with specific bioactivities (anti-oxidant, anti-diabetic, and anti-obesity) or techno-functional properties (emulsifying capacity, gel strength, improved stability, and sensory profile).248,252 This method allows precise control over product composition and supports compliance with food and nutraceutical regulations.253 But, it may involve higher processing costs and lower overall biomass utilization.249,251 Techno-economic analyses indicate that large working volumes, high raw-material costs, and significant utility requirements for heating, mixing, concentration, and drying currently limit the scalability and competitiveness of multiproduct biorefineries, which occupy major operating costs.254 Thus, it primarily reflects the gap between single-product hydrocolloid value chains and emerging, multiproduct biorefinery concepts.

Processing steps along value chains also strongly influence iodine and heavy metal loads in seaweeds. Processes like washing, soaking, blanching, and rehydration can substantially reduce the content of mobile iodine and certain heavy metals, such as inorganic As and Cd.255,256 However, more intensive or harsh treatments can lead to losses or structural changes in the nutrient composition as well as denaturation or aggregation of proteins. These changes may alter both bioactivity profiles and techno-functional properties, including gelling, emulsification, and texture.255 Overall, this highlights the critical need to design processing pipelines that balance contaminant reduction and sensory acceptance with the preservation of key nutritional and bioactive components, ensuring safety, quality, and functional integrity in seaweed-derived products. The integration of top-down and bottom-up strategies provides a conceptual framework for optimizing biomass utilization, product quality, and process efficiency in future seaweed biorefineries.

Challenges and research gaps

Variability in composition and bioactivity

One of the major challenges in utilizing seaweeds for health and functional applications is the significant variation in their biochemical composition and bioactivity. The nutrient and bioactive compound profiles of macroalgae are strongly influenced by environmental, physiological, and geographical factors. Considerable variations have been reported, even within the same species, due to seasonal shifts and site-specific conditions. For instance, Chaetomorpha antennina collected from the intertidal rocks of the Covelong coast, India, between June and March exhibited significant seasonal variations in anti-oxidants, minerals, and metabolites. Amino acid levels in seaweeds were the highest during the post-monsoon season and lowest in summer, while fatty acid concentrations peaked during the monsoon season.257 Similarly, a study on red seaweeds from different coastal regions of Kalpitiya, Sri Lanka, revealed notable differences in mineral content (K, Ca, Mn, Fe, Cu, and Zn) among samples of the same species collected during the same season, which indicated a strong site-specific influence beyond the seasonal variability.258 These fluctuations in nutrient content might have a direct impact on the yield, potency, and overall quality of extracted seaweed compounds. Abiotic factors including sunlight, water temperature, wave motion, pH, salinity, and turbidity influence the chemical composition of seaweeds. For example, a comparative study reported that carrageenan yield in Calliblepharis jubata was the highest in summer (18.73%), alginate content in Sargassum muticum was high in winter (36.88%), and agar yield from Gracilaria gracilis remained relatively constant throughout the year (27.04%).259

In addition to these environmental variations, species-specific metabolic pathways also contribute to differences in composition. Brown algae are typically rich in fucoidans and phlorotannins, whereas red algae are rich in sulfated galactans like agar and carrageenans, and green algae are enriched in ulvans and rhamnans.260 Post-harvest methods such as drying, extraction, and storage could also influence the stability and concentration of these bioactive compounds.261 Such variations in the compositions lower the reproducibility of experimental studies, which present challenges for regulatory approvals. So, there is a need for the development of standard cultivation, harvesting, and processing protocols, followed by the integration of advanced analytical and omics-based approaches to ensure consistency and reliability in seaweed-based health products.

Consumer awareness and product acceptance

Seaweeds have been consumed traditionally in East Asian cultures. Specifically, in countries like Japan, one-fifth of the daily diet incorporates seaweeds in any form, and packaged seaweed foods have also been a part of everyday consumption. In contrast, Western countries have historically shown limited consumption of seaweeds, except in some coastal regions.262 However, over the last decade, seaweeds have gained popularity in Europe and North America, driven by the growing awareness of its nutritional value and potential applications.8 Consumption of seaweed-based products such as nori, wakame, kombu, kelp, and dulse is rapidly increasing in these western regions.226

One of the significant challenges faced in Indian seaweed market is the lack of consumer awareness and acceptance of seaweed-derived products.263 Unlike other countries such as China, South Korea, and Japan where seaweed is a staple dietary food, in India, seaweed consumption is largely confined to certain coastal communities.264 Indian cuisine does not traditionally include seaweeds in diet, and most of the populations, especially vegetarians, are hesitant to consume seaweed due to its strong oceanic aroma, unfamiliar taste, and slimy texture.265 Although edible species like Ulva and Caulerpa are available in Indian waters, their acceptance is very low. Also, there is a perception that seaweeds are exotic or a foreign ingredient. In order to improve their acceptance, increasing public awareness about the health benefits of seaweeds is essential. Strategies such as incorporation of seaweeds into familiar foods, using them as a condiment, or masking their aroma and taste could help integrate seaweeds into mainstream Indian diets.10,265

Future perspectives

Seaweeds demonstrate substantial variations in their nutritional composition. These variations in composition are greatly influenced by several factors including season, genetic makeup, and geographical location. Presently, seaweed cultivation largely depends on wild-type or less-improved strains, which leads to inconsistencies in their nutritional quality. These strains also have limited adaptability to the varying environmental conditions and increased susceptibility to diseases. Genome editing and synthetic biology strategies offer promising approaches at the molecular level to overcome existing barriers in macroalgal research. One of the key bottlenecks in this field is the lack of reliable protocols for the development of transgenic macroalgal strains. Only a few studies have reported transformation protocols and cloning toolkits for certain macroalgal species.266,267 Genome editing has been successfully demonstrated in a limited number of species, including two brown algae Ectocarpus spp268 and Saccharina japonica269 and one green alga, Ulva prolifera,270 using CRISPR technology. This technology has been used to knock-out conserved genes such as adenine phosphoribosyl transferase (APT), with the long-term goal of developing cultivars exhibiting improved disease resistance, stress tolerance, and yield.267 Meanwhile, synthetic biology approaches enable the expression of novel biosynthetic pathways in seaweeds to improve the synthesis of desirable compounds and achieve higher yield.271 However, most of these studies remain at the initial levels. Therefore, additional research is required for the development of species-specific genetic tools and robust transformation protocols for seaweeds.

Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a revolutionary platform to overcome those technological complications in the field of macroalgal research. The combination of AI with synthetic biology facilitates in-depth analysis of the metabolic pathways and gene regulatory networks, which enables a precise control over the biosynthesis of targeted metabolites.272 This symbiotic combination may accelerate rapid optimization of novel biosynthetic pathways in seaweeds. Furthermore, advanced AI models such as QSAR (quantitative structure-activity relationship) and neural networks are already playing critical role in the prediction of bioactivity profiles of novel drug compounds. The QSAR model predicts compounds by determining their chemical structure. The integration of neural networks with this model enhances this process to find the complex molecular structures.273,274 In future, the QSAR and neural networks may play a critical role in predicting the bioactivity profiles such as anti-oxidant, anti-microbial, anti-cancer, or anti-inflammatory properties of seaweed-derived metabolites with greater accuracy.

Abiotic factors, such as pH, temperature, salinity, wave motion, etc., can have a direct impact on yield and chemical composition of seaweeds. The integration of sendor-based systems and IoT technologies with machine learning algorithms, allows for efficient real-time monitoring of cultivation conditions.275 In addition, drone-based monitoring and remote sensing technologies help in estimation of growth and early disease detection. These technologies help in preventing losses due to climate variations caused by temperature rise and cyclones.276

Another key technological innovation that needs to be established well is the development of offshore seaweed farming techniques. This system utilizes floating cultivation structures in deeper waters, rather than relying solely on nearshore harvesting. Suggestions have been proposed for utilizing the offshore wind systems and decommissioned petroleum platforms for seaweed cultivation.277 This enables higher yield potential, reduces environmental degradation, and ensures a steady supply of seaweed biomass for industrial applications.

While there is a vast coastline and expanding ocean available for cultivation, there is need for a land-based cultivation system for seaweeds. Such systems have been established successfully in some regions in Canada, Denmark, and the Netherlands, with focus on cultivating seaweeds such as Chondrus crispus, Ulva, Palmaria palmata, Gracilaria, and Asparagopsis species.278,279,280 This method offers several advantages, including higher control over environmental factors like light, temperature, and nutrient levels, which can lead to higher productivity and the cultivation of specific species. Specifically, this system allows for the cultivation of edible seaweeds and seaweeds with novel therapeutic properties, without any contamination or diseases. The nutrient requirements can also be fortified, which can significantly alleviate stress and increase the yield of biomass. Research gaps and future directions in seaweeds for food and healthcare

Today, seaweed cultivation comes with several greater risks, which affects the yield negatively. In parallel, the need for seaweeds in various fields is increasing, particularly in nutrition and healthcare. Future research needs to be focused on integration of seaweed-derived bioactives into personalized diet and nutritional frameworks based on an individual’s genetic, metabolic, and microbiome data. As seaweeds play a major role in achieving the UN Sustainable Development Goals, addressing seaweed-associated challenges from the molecular level to scalable cultivation and processing systems is crucial. So, there is a need for coordinated multidisciplinary effort to utilize the full potential of seaweeds for sustainable health, industry, and environmental benefits.

Conclusion

This review details the multifunctional role of bioactive compounds from seaweeds, which include polysaccharides, proteins, pigments, polyphenols, and essential micronutrients. It further discusses their pharmacological effects that enhance metabolic regulation and disease protection. However, a more integrated and deep approach is required to understand the complete health benefits of seaweeds. Successful innovations in genome editing and synthetic biology can enhance the biochemical value of seaweeds by increasing yield and quality of the desired compounds, while lowering their anti-nutritional components. Furthermore, the construction of off-shore and land-based cultivation systems in an environmentally controlled conditions could provide clean and scalable cultivation of seaweeds for nutritional and therapeutic applications. In addition, integrating advanced systems like IoT-based sensors, remote sensing, and drone-based surveillance with these cultivation systems will enable real-time monitoring of such cultivation systems. Meanwhile, the integration of AI and other emerging technologies with these combined systems could further aid in the optimization of environmental conditions. Combination of these technologies could help mitigate the impacts of climatic change and aid in achieving desirable yield and consistency in nutrient composition. Implementation of such regulations, frameworks, and technologies, along with sustainable practices, will ensure the incorporation of seaweeds as foods and supplements into everyone’s diet. Such initiatives may increase the overall consumption of seaweeds worldwide, thereby improving human health and mitigating diseases.
