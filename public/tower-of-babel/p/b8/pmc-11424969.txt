Fluorescence-guided laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair using indocyanine green angiography to prevent iatrogenic vascular injury: A case report and video
Authors: Hernán Todeschini, Fernando Dip, Martin Drago, Kevin P White, Raúl J Rosenthal, Luis Sarotto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLaparoscopic inguinal hernia repair (LIHR) is one of the most common surgical procedures performed worldwide, associated with a roughly 10 % rate of complications, most commonly iatrogenic injury to blood vessels, sometimes necessitating conversion to open surgery. Fluorescence-guided laparoscopic surgery using indocyanine green fluorescence angiography (ICG-FA) facilitates the precise identification of numerous anatomical structures, especially vascular, reducing their risk of iatrogenic injury. After reversing the peritoneum, but before placing the extraperitoneal mesh, another dose of ICG was administered intravenously to aid in safely securing the mesh. The hernia was repaired successfully with no complications.DiscussionICG-FA appears to facilitate inguinal hernia repair by enabling real-time visualization of anatomical structures, theoretically reducing the risk of complications, particularly vascular injuries. Introduction

Inguinal hernias are a common surgical problem that can cause significant morbidity if left untreated. In the United States of America alone, an estimated 800,000 to one million inguinal hernia repairs are performed annually [1]. Laparoscopic repair has been shown to offer several advantages over traditional open surgery, including less pain, faster recovery, and lower recurrence rates. However, the time to complete surgery and the rates of certain complications may increase [2,3]. Among the most common injuries are injuries involving both blood and lymph vessels. Most troublesome of these is injury to the iliac vessels within the so-called “triangle of doom”, which typically requires immediate conversion to open surgery to control bleeding [4]. However, injuries to even small vessels may require conversion to open surgery, in addition to causing increased post-operative pain, enlarging post-operative hematomas and lymphoceles, and an increased risk of wound infections [3]. Avoiding such vascular injuries may be challenging, however, given the altered anatomy caused by the herniation, the complex network of vessels in this relatively small anatomical area, and the small caliber that many of these vessel have.

Intra-operative visualization of the fluorescent dye indocyanine green (ICG) under near-infrared light has expanded exponentially over the past two decades to involve virtually every field of surgery [5,6]. Among its more established uses is in the delineation of small lymphatic vessels to prevent, detect, characterize, treat, and monitor the progress of iatrogenic lymphedema [7]. However, its use preventing injury to small blood vessels is much less well established.

Given the risks of injury to both small-diameter blood and lymphatic vessels during inguinal hernia repair, we elected to perform fluorescence-guided laparoscopic surgery and ICG-fluorescence angiography in a 45-year-old gentleman who required right inguinal hernia repair. This report describes the case and includes a narrated video demonstrating the use of ICG-fluorescence angiography during inguinal hernia repair to prevent vascular injury. The case report has been drafted in full accordance with current consensus-based guidelines for surgical case reports (2023 SCARE Statement) [8].

Case presentation

A 46-year-old, previously-healthy, normal-weight male presented with a right inguinal hernia. Medical history was negative except for hypertension. On physical examination, a slightly tender, right inguinal bulge was identified that was accentuated when the patient coughed. Diagnostic ultrasound confirmed the presence of a right inguinal hernia.

A decision was made for the patient to undergo fluorescence-guided laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair. To minimize the risk of vascular injury, surgery was performed using fluorescent angiography after the injection of ICG dye, as detailed below. The surgical procedure was performed by a fully-accredited, university-affiliated surgeon with extensive experience both performing inguinal hernia repair and using fluorescence imaging during other surgical procedures.

Prior to surgery, the patient provided informed, written consent to undergo surgery using fluorescence imaging and ICG and to have the details of his case reported in writing, all in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. For the current report, all information that might identify the patient's identity has been omitted. The institution's ethics review board had previously granted written approval for our use of ICG-angiography intra-operatively and reporting of this case.

In the operating room, the patient was placed in an anti-Trendelenburg position under general anesthesia, and a pneumoperitoneum established using the Hassan technique. Three trocars were placed: one 10 mm port in the umbilical region and two 5 mm ports, one each in the right and left flank. The hernia was identified as a direct inguinal hernia.

Before dissecting the peritoneum, we administered an initial 2 ml dose of ICG intravenously, followed by 10 ml of a physiological solution. The surgical field was then illuminated using the Stryker fluorescence system (Video 1). After 45 s, an initial arterial phase was observed during which both the iliac artery and spermatic arteries were visible. Once the vascular structures were located, the sac was dissected. After reversing the peritoneum and before placing the extraperitoneal mesh, another dose of ICG was administered intravenously to aid in safely securing the mesh. This again allowed for precise identification of the inferior epigastric vessels, spermatic vessels, and the triangle of doom to prevent injury while four absorbable tacks were placed to secure the mesh in position. The hernia was successfully repaired without any complications, and the patient had an uneventful postoperative course.

Discussion

Laparoscopic surgery is a minimally-invasive surgical technique that has gained popularity in inguinal hernia repair. However, laparoscopic surgery may be associated with an increased risk of complications, relative to open surgery, due to difficulties visualizing certain anatomical structures. Among the most common complications is vascular injury. In a worldwide report published in 2018 on laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair, roughly 0.3 % of patients suffered vascular injuries during laparoscopic inguinal repair [9]. Most vascular injuries were caused by incorrect trocar placement or the use of electrocautery.

Fluorescence-guided laparoscopic surgery with ICG angiography is an emerging technique that enables real-time visualization of anatomical structures using fluorescence imaging.

Indocyanine green is a water-soluble dye that binds to plasma proteins, is excreted by the liver, and fluoresces brightly in NIRL. During laparoscopic procedures, ICG is administered intravenously either before or during surgery, depending on the procedure and anatomical structure being sought, and its fluorescence properties are used to intra-operatively identify predominantly vascular structures, which include blood and lymph vessels; highly-vascular organs like parathyroid and adrenal glands, the liver, and spleen; and both primary and secondary vascular tumors. To date, however, only four single-case reports and one case series of six patients have been published of ICG use during inguinal hernia repairs. Two of these case reports described ICG lymphography being used to identify per-existing lymphoceles [10,11]. In the remaining eight patients, ICG-angiography was performed for the sole purpose of assessing the adequacy of tissue perfusion in patients with strangulated bowel [[12], [13], [14]]. To our knowledge, ours is the very first report of ICG-angiography being performed to prevent iatrogenic vessel injury during any hernia repair, inguinal or otherwise, and the results are indeed promising.

As with any case report, the experience we describe herein must be interpreted with caution, given the single case. On the other hand, as noted above, the intra-operative use of fluorescence imaging with ICG has now been reported extensively, including countless reports of its use assessing tissue perfusion and lymphatic vasculature. What makes our case highly unique is its use preventing vascular injury during inguinal hernia repair.

In conclusion, fluorescence-guided laparoscopic surgery with ICG-angiography appears to be a promising technique that can facilitate inguinal hernia repair by enabling real-time visualization of anatomical structures and reducing the risk of complications, particularly vascular injuries. Further studies are needed to evaluate its long-term outcomes and cost-effectiveness, as well as its potential use in the repair of hernias at other anatomical sites like the abdomen (ventral hernias).
