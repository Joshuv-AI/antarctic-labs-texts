Early experience scaling up a breast cancer early detection initiative integrated with cervical cancer screening in Rwanda
Authors: Lydia E Pace, Jean de Dieu Uwihaye, Vestine Mukandayisenga, Amanda M Fata, Anne F Rositch, Joshua Byiringiro, Jeanine Nyinawabega, Sylvie Gaju, Jean Marie Vianney Dusengimana, Vestine Rugema, Theoneste Maniragaba, Marc Hagenimana, Francois Uwinkindi, Vincent Cubaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLate-stage breast cancer contributes to a growing number of deaths in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) but few studies examine scalable early detection strategies. Following small-scale pilots, in 2020 Rwanda launched an adapted Women’s Cancer Early Detection Programme (WCEDP), integrating clinical breast exam (CBE) for symptomatic patients with cervical cancer screening. A WCEDP-specific electronic health record (EHR) was developed to facilitate patient tracking.MethodsWe used the RE-AIM implementation science framework to retrospectively evaluate implementation of breast cancer early detection within the WCEDP over 12 months in the first three scale-up districts (population: 2 009 888), using routinely-collected electronic and paper data from 15 health centres and 3 hospitals. We examined the WCEDP’s Reach in the target population, Effectiveness linking patients to care, Adoption by facilities and fidelity to the Implementation protocol.ResultsRegarding Reach, average weekly health centre visits for CBE increased from 18 to 33 post-WCEDP launch; of 1688 women receiving CBE through the WCEDP, 12.0% were ≥50 years. Regarding effectiveness, among 383 women referred to district or referral hospitals, 157 (40.9%) had no documented referral facility visit. Of those seen at a referral facility, median days from health centre to district hospital visit and from district to referral hospital visit were 6 (IQR 1.8–14.8) and 8 (IQR 5.0–40.5) respectively. Among the 36 patients receiving biopsy, 72.2% were biopsied within 60 days of initial presentation. In terms of adoption, 79 clinicians were trained in cancer early detection, with 69.6% remaining at WCEDP facilities after 3 years. Regarding implementation fidelity, WCEDP clinics were held 52.6% of weeks. EHR data quality was inconsistent, with half of patients seen at district hospitals for breast care lacking EHR documentation.InterpretationBreast cancer early detection services can be implemented in resource-constrained SSA health facilities. Integration with cervical cancer screening may be a promising strategy. However, investing in data systems is critical to support programme evaluation and high-quality care.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Breast cancer early detection through clinical breast examination in primary healthcare facilities can reduce late-stage diagnoses and may reduce breast cancer mortality in low- and middle-income countries. However, little is known about how to implement such initiatives, including ensuring timely management of abnormal findings, in resource-constrained health systems at scale.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study describes the first year of Rwanda’s real-world implementation of breast cancer early detection integrated with a cervical cancer screening programme in health centres, in three districts serving a population of over 2 million. We identified implementation successes—such as increased provision of clinical breast examination, increased reach of women over 50 and guideline-concordant time intervals from first presentation to biopsy among those patients seen at higher levels of care—and challenges, including high rates of referral non-completion and inconsistent use of the electronic health record.

Background

Breast cancer is the most common cause of cancer death among women worldwide.1 Breast cancer incidence and mortality are increasing rapidly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including in sub-Saharan Africa where breast cancer outcomes are poor, in large part due to late-stage diagnoses.2 In Rwanda, for example, about 75% of cases are diagnosed at stage 3 or 4 when the disease is difficult or impossible to cure.3 4 Several groups, including the WHO’s Global Breast Cancer Initiative (GBCI), advocate for systematic early detection interventions in LMICs to reduce breast cancer deaths.5 The GBCI recommends building health system capacity for symptom recognition and timely diagnosis so that <60 days elapse between a patient’s initial presentation for breast evaluation and breast cancer diagnosis, with a goal of diagnosing at least 60% of breast cancers at Stage 1 or 2.

There is a small body of literature describing initiatives to facilitate early-stage breast cancer diagnoses in LMICs. Since mammography screening is not yet widely available in most LMICs, research and policies have largely focused on provision of clinical breast exam (CBE) screening (to women without breast symptoms) or diagnostic CBE (targeting women with symptoms). Studies suggest that both strategies may decrease late-stage diagnoses.69 However, few countries have incorporated breast cancer early detection into routine primary care services at scale, and there is scarce literature describing real-world scale-up.10 Resource-constrained health systems face numerous barriers to scaling up breast cancer early detection programmes, including limited healthcare worker training in cancer,11 health facility understaffing and turnover, patient-level barriers including stigma and poverty12 and a lack of robust health information systems to facilitate follow-up and monitor quality.13 14 Efforts to scale breast cancer early detection programmes in LMICs must consider these challenges and require rigorous evaluation to allow continuous improvement and contribute to global knowledge.

Rwanda is an East African country of 13 million people whose Ministry of Health has prioritised addressing non-communicable diseases including cancer.15 16 Since 2015, Rwanda has piloted several initiatives to advance breast cancer early detection, starting with a single-district cluster RCT focused on training community health workers and clinicians in timely evaluation of women with breast concerns, and establishing weekly breast clinics at health centres. Financed by dedicated research funding, this initiative increased health worker skills, evaluation of breast concerns and early-stage diagnoses.6 17 In 2018, Rwanda Biomedical Centre (RBC; Rwanda’s chief health implementation agency) launched the government-funded Women’s Cancer Early Detection Programme (WCEDP) in three districts, in which all women aged 30–49 received both screening CBE and cervical cancer screening18; women of any age with breast symptoms were also encouraged to seek diagnostic CBE. Despite high numbers of women reached, early evaluation showed that 30% of women referred from primary health centres to district hospitals for abnormal CBE were not seen at district hospitals. Further, all breast cancers diagnosed occurred in women who initially presented with breast symptoms, suggesting limited additive short-term benefit of systematic screening CBE among women presenting for cervical cancer screening.

In 2020, RBC launched an adapted WCEDP in three districts (total population: 2 009 888); the WCEDP was scaled to a total of 24 districts by 2024. This was a government-led and government-funded initiative implemented in partnership with non-governmental organisations who operated in the participating districts. One key adaptation was the development of an electronic health record (EHR) for women undergoing breast evaluation and/or cervical cancer screening. The EHR permits offline data entry on tablets at rural health centres (most of which did not previously use an EHR) and linkage to an online system at district hospitals19 to permit identification and re-engagement of patients missing referral visits. To enhance efficiency, and consistent with WHO’s Guide to Early Cancer Diagnosis, the adapted WCEDP recommended diagnostic CBE, primarily for women with breast symptoms (whether receiving cervical cancer screening or not).

In this retrospective study, we utilise the RE-AIM framework20 to describe initial implementation of breast cancer early detection within the adapted WCEDP in 2020, including the programme’s effectiveness linking individuals to care, which is a key requirement for early detection to improve outcomes. A primary goal was to identify implementation successes and challenges during the programme’s first year to inform and optimise continued scale-up. In addition, we sought to contribute to global understanding of challenges and opportunities in real-world breast cancer early detection in LMICs,21 22 including potential benefits of integrating breast and cervical cancer screening, a strategy of great interest to the global cancer community.23

Methods

Study setting and design

The WCEDP

Clinicians from participating districts’ health facilities (2–3/health centre) were invited to receive standardised training in diagnostic CBE and cervical cancer screening as well as in use of the new WCEDP-specific EHR tool; community health workers were trained to raise community awareness about the WCEDP, and breast and cervical cancer broadly. Facilities were then asked to assign at least one clinician 1 day/week to staff a WCEDP clinic where women 30–49 received cervical cancer screening and any woman with a breast concern (whether receiving cervical cancer screening or not) could receive diagnostic CBE.

Health facility selection

This study focused on the first three districts to implement the adapted WCEDP in 2020. We sampled five health centres from each district, examining a total of 15 out of the districts’ 43 health centres. To attain a representative sample, we selected health centres based on a combination of factors that might influence performance in the programme: geographic location within the district, population size and scores in the national performance-based financing programme (an aggregate measure of health facility quality)24 (see online supplemental appendix box for details).

Study period

We gathered pre-WCEDP data from the 12 months prior to WCEDP launch in each district, while implementation data and outcomes were gathered from 12 months after intervention launch in each district (District 1 in July, District 2 in August and District 3 in October).

Data sources

All data for this retrospective study were extracted from routine medical records, both paper and electronic. Pre-WCEDP data (from when the WCEDP EHR was not yet in use) were abstracted from outpatient department paper registries at health centres and entered into a REDCap database. Post-intervention data from the 12 months after WCEDP launch were extracted directly from the WCEDP EHR, supplemented with paper records when key variables were missing. For patients referred from health centres to district hospitals after the WCEDP launch, we used data from the WCEDP EHR to see if they were seen at the district hospital. When we did not find patients in the WCEDP EHR, we looked for them in district hospital paper registries or health facility electronic billing systems. For encounters identified in paper records, we characterised whether visits were for breast-related or other care. For women referred from district hospitals, we identified whether they were seen at referral hospitals in several ways: (1) Searching the EHR of Butaro Cancer Centre of Excellence (Rwanda’s first public cancer facility, providing the highest volume of cancer care nationally); (2) electronic billing records of University Teaching Hospital of Kigali, and Rwanda Military Hospital, two other sites of cancer care.

Finally, for patients with documented referrals from a health centre or district hospital to a district hospital or referral hospital, but with no corresponding encounter found in district or referral hospital records, we contacted patients to understand potential medical record discrepancies, calling all patients who had documented phone numbers.

We obtained numbers of staff trained from governmental training reports and health centre staff reports.

Study outcomes and analysis

Selection of outcomes was guided by the RE-AIM framework (figure 1).

Use of the RE-AIM framework to examine breast cancer early detection services in initial scale-up of Rwanda’s WCEDP. CBE, clinical breast exam; CHW, community health worker; HC, health centre; EHR, electronic health record; WCEDP, Women’s Cancer Early Detection Programme.

Reach

We defined Reach as the number of women received by health centres for CBE (screening or diagnostic) per week during the 12 months after the launch of the intervention, compared with the 12 months before the intervention. Additionally, we were interested in understanding characteristics of women reached, and the degree to which the integrated breast and cervical cancer screening programme reached key populations at risk for breast cancer, including women with breast symptoms (the target population for the WCEDP and a key population to reach to achieve early diagnosis). Finally, we were interested in the WCEDP’s engagement of women 50 and over, who probably represent about half of breast cancers in Rwanda3 but who were not eligible for cervical cancer screening during the study period. To capture these variables, we abstracted data from paper registries at health centres over the 12 months pre-WCEDP launch, using registries from women’s cancer programmes if they existed prior to launch (since some districts had cervical cancer screening programmes), and from outpatient departments. Registries contained visit dates, visit reasons and follow-up plans. Data were stored in a REDCap database. To capture post-intervention patient characteristics, we abstracted health centre breast and cervical cancer encounters from the WCEDP EHR over the 12- month period following WCEDP launch in each district.

Effectiveness

We defined effectiveness as the proportion of women successfully linked to indicated follow-up. We examined the proportion of women referred from health centres to district hospitals who were seen at the district hospital within our study period, and the median number of days from their health centre to their district hospital visit. Among women referred from district hospitals to referral facilities, we examined the proportion seen at the referral hospital during the study period. Among those recommended to receive a biopsy, we examined the proportion who received it within 60 days of their initial health centre visit, the diagnostic interval recommended by the GBCI.

Adoption

We defined Adoption as the number of clinicians trained in CBE and the WCEDP EHR and also examined the number who remained employed at their health facility.

Implementation

We examined two dimensions of Implementation fidelity: (1) regular hosting of WCEDP clinics (% of weeks that WCEDP clinics were held); and (2) use of the WCEDP EHR. Although we had planned to assess facilities’ use of EHR missed-visit reports and patient call logs, key features designed to facilitate patient tracking and linkage to care, these functions were not yet in place during the study period. Instead, we documented frequency of missingness of key variables in the EHR and enumerated patients receiving CBE who were documented in paper records or local (district hospital level) EHRs but not in the WCEDP EHR.

Data analysis

Clinical and demographic characteristics were examined using descriptive statistics. To compute intervals between facility visits for referred patients, we considered the last health centre (or district hospital) visit date and first district hospital (or referral hospital) visit date. We used frequencies and percentages to describe categorical variables, and medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Analysis was conducted in Stata (v16).

Role of funding source

The study sponsor played no role in study design, in the collection, analysis or interpretation of data; in the writing of the report; or in the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Patient and public involvement

Patients’ experiences with and priorities for breast cancer early detection in Rwanda were elicited in prior quantitative25 and qualitative12 work that informed the development of the WCEDP and guided the current analysis, including selection of outcomes.

Effectiveness

Figure 3 describes patients’ care pathways and linkage to timely follow-up and diagnosis (see online supplemental appendix table 3 for detailed data sources). Following the WCEDP launch, 211/1,688 (12.5%) of patients seen at health centres were referred to district hospitals; of these, 128 (60.7%) had documented visits at the district hospital in paper or electronic records following their health centre visit. Of note, 11 of these were documented as being seen for non-breast reasons and 10 were seen for unknown reasons; thus, if breast concerns were not addressed at these visits, only 107 (49%) of patients had documented breast follow-up. A total of 403 patients were seen at district hospitals for breast concerns who did not have preceding documented health centre visits; these patients largely reflected patients seen in WCEDP training campaigns held at hospitals.

Of the 510 patients seen at the DH, 172 were referred to referral hospitals for breast reasons, and based on referral hospital EHRs, 98 of these (57.0%) were seen, though 6 were documented as seen for reasons other than breast concerns. Of the 41 patients recommended to receive biopsy, 36 received biopsy and 18 were diagnosed with breast cancer.

On average, of those referred from health centres who were seen at district hospitals, median time between initial health centre visit and district hospital visit was 6 days (IQR 1.8–14.8). Of those referred from district hospitals and seen at referral hospitals, the median number of days was 8 (IQR 5–40.5). Among the 35 patients who had a confirmed biopsy date, median days from first health centre visit to biopsy was 13 (IQR 6–59.5), with 72.2% receiving biopsy within 60 days.

Implementation fidelity

Among the 15 health centres included in this study, on average WCEDP clinics were held during 52.6% of weeks. There was wide variability across health centres, ranging from 13.5%–75% of weeks. WCEDP EHR data were incomplete. For example, of 107 patients who were confirmed to be seen at district hospitals for breast evaluation based on review of paper and electronic records, only 55 of these had visits recorded in the WCEDP EHR (online supplemental appendix table 3). This discrepancy often occurred when patients were seen outside the WCEDP clinic—for example, if they were at the general outpatient department clinic, their encounter was entered in hospital paper records or the hospital’s own electronic records. Data on key variables were often missing, for example, about 20% of records extracted from the WCEDP EHR were missing information about the next step planned for patients’ care. Paper registry review located about 50% of missing data on next steps.

Phone calls to patients who were reported as being referred but who were not documented as seen at the next level of care revealed further potential data discrepancies (online supplemental appendix table 4). 10 of 17 (58.8%) patients documented as referred from district hospitals and 10/24 (41.7%) of those documented as referred to referral hospitals reported that they were not, in fact, referred. Of those who confirmed they were referred, 7/7 (100%) and 9/14 (64.3%) reported that they were seen at the district hospital and referral hospital, respectively, even though their visit was not found in any facility records reviewed.

Discussion

Rwanda’s WCEDP aims to integrate breast cancer early detection and cervical cancer screening into routine care at health centres throughout the country to reduce preventable deaths from these cancers, including through using a dedicated EHR to track patients and facilitate care coordination across different levels of the healthcare system. This study identifies implementation successes and challenges of the programme in its first year and provides lessons to inform ongoing adaptation of the WCEDP. Our findings also illuminate implementation barriers and opportunities for other LMICs undertaking the challenging but critical task of adopting and scaling cancer screening and early detection.

Our study demonstrates successes achieved by Rwanda’s WCEDP. With regard to Reach, the programme was associated with an increase in the number of women receiving CBE at health facilities. Almost half of those receiving CBE did not receive cervical cancer screening, suggesting the programme successfully engaged women explicitly seeking breast care, including those who were <30 years or ≥50 years and not eligible for cervical cancer screening. Integration of breast and cervical cancer screening has been identified as being a potentially patient-centred and accessible strategy to facilitate women’s cancer early detection.23 However, since cervical cancer screening focuses on younger women, some experts have been concerned that integrating breast cancer early detection into cervical cancer screening initiatives might insufficiently engage postmenopausal women, a key target population for breast cancer screening and early detection. The median age of breast cancer diagnosis in Rwanda is around 50 years.3 Although women over 50 were not eligible for cervical cancer screening during our study period (with protocols changing subsequently with updated WHO guidelines),26 the proportion of women receiving CBE who were ≥50 years nonetheless doubled after WCEDP launch. Our prior work has demonstrated challenges reaching postmenopausal women for routine healthcare in Rwanda,27 28 so it was promising that this proportion increased. However, it was still low at 12%, suggesting the continued need for dedicated outreach to this sub-population for breast healthcare.

Although the WCEDP was intended to focus on women with breast symptoms, almost 70% of women receiving CBE at health centres overall were identified in the EHR as asymptomatic; among women aged 30–49 who were eligible for cervical cancer screening, almost 80% were asymptomatic. This finding suggests that health centre nurses’ practice was different from that encouraged in WCEDP trainings and protocols, with many asymptomatic women receiving screening CBE while they received cervical cancer screening. Notably, the ideal target population for breast cancer early detection initiatives in LMICs remains uncertain,29 and there are likely benefits to engaging asymptomatic women in CBE screening (for example, breast health education, identification of asymptomatic cancers and identification of symptomatic cancers in communities with low awareness of breast cancer symptoms). However, there are also downsides (eg, low efficiency in populations or subgroups with low breast cancer incidence including younger women; false positive results with associated burdens for the health system and patients). The most appropriate approach is likely to be dynamic and may depend on a health system’s capacity for linkage to care and the need to triage the highest risk patients.22 30 Clinicians’ and policymakers’ perspectives on this issue have been explored through qualitative work to be reported separately.

An additional important finding about WCEDP Reach was that during our assessment period, most patients were received at health centres during short-term screening campaigns. During campaigns, there was an influx of staff supervision and supplies, as well as additional staff compensation. Screening campaigns may be an important strategy to facilitate access, as demonstrated by a study comparing campaigns vs health centre-based care for cervical cancer screening in Kenya.31 32 Further evaluation is needed to characterise the trade-offs of providing CBE routinely at health centres vs through dedicated community- or facility-based campaigns. Nonetheless, even if most screening occurs during campaigns, prompt health centre evaluation of women with breast or cervical cancer symptoms remains important for timely cancer diagnoses. Our finding that health centres held WCEDP clinics only about 50% of weeks may suggest a need to enhance routine facility-based breast care.

Our findings regarding the WCEDP’s effectiveness in linking women to care were mixed. Among patients who were referred to a higher level of care and seen, time elapsing between referral and being seen was short; further, three-quarters of women receiving biopsies received them within 60 days of first presentation, concordant with WHO recommendations.33 However, about 40% of patients referred from health centres to district hospitals, or from district to referral hospitals, had no documentation of being seen at the higher level of care despite review of multiple data sources. Although this may in part reflect insufficient documentation, this is a higher rate of apparent loss-to-follow-up than seen in the earliest rollout of the WCEDP.18 In that iteration, a full-time patient navigator was employed to identify and contact patients who missed visits, and our findings, combined with our prior work12 and qualitative work on scale-up to be published separately, suggest patient navigators, along with dedicated resources to address financial barriers such as transport costs, may remain important to address obstacles patients face in accessing care. Patient navigation is an evidence-based intervention to optimise cancer screening34 that has been identified as a priority by the WHO35 and in Rwanda’s new National Cancer Control Plan. Prospective research would be valuable to understand the most feasible and effective patient navigation models in the Rwandan context.

Our ability to monitor the WCEDP’s effectiveness in linking women to care was constrained by inadequate uptake of the WCEDP EHR in its first year; in turn, during this period, the EHR did not realise its potential to help clinicians track patients and reconnect them to care. Rwanda’s experience aligns with implementation challenges with digital tools to facilitate cancer care that have been noted in other LMIC settings.36 For most health centre nurses participating in the WCEDP, the WCEDP EHR was the first EHR they had used, and the EHR was a new and relatively untested system. Data entered into the system were often incomplete, and patients who were confirmed through other sources as being seen at higher levels of care were often not documented in the WCEDP EHR. Qualitative interviews (to be published separately) have suggested areas in which the EHR could improve, and since our study period, RBC and partners have incorporated feedback such as improving data entry forms and finalising ‘missed visit reports’ to allow hospital-based nurses to identify and contact patients who have missed visits. Workflows to support clinicians in utilising these forms—ideally in combination with standardised approaches to patient navigation—will be essential to ensure they impact care, and rigorous evaluation of their impact is needed. Finally, the WCEDP EHR was a stand-alone system dedicated to breast and cervical cancer screening patients. Integration of the WCEDP EHR into a future national EHR might improve ease of documentation for clinicians and improve tracking of patients across clinical departments and facilities.

Our study has strengths, including integration of data across multiple paper and electronic sources as well as patient outreach. Our governmental and nongovernmental stakeholders and programme implementers provided contextual expertise to inform study design, facilitate data collection and interpret findings. However, assessment of WCEDP effectiveness was limited by data incompleteness, itself an indicator of early challenges with implementation fidelity. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic likely affected early WCEDP reach and effectiveness, though shutdowns of healthcare facilities and public transportation did not occur within our study districts during the study period. Further evaluation is critical to examine how WCEDP implementation has evolved following the pandemic and as the programme has matured, and to optimise its public health impact.
