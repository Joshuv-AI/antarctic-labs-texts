Novel insights into choline's role in promoting myofiber hypertrophy and mitochondrial function in muscle of sub-adult grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella)
Authors: Qingrui Li, Xiaoqiu Zhou, Lin Feng, Weidan Jiang, Yang Liu, Yaobin Ma, Shengyao Kuang, Shuwei Li, Ling Tang, Chengbo Zhong, Pei Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCholine is an essential nutrient for fish, and its metabolites are components of all membrane systems. Membrane phospholipids play an important role in maintaining the morphology and function of mitochondria, which could affect the growth and development of myofiber. To evaluate the effects of choline on growth performance, muscle fiber development, and mitochondrial morphology and function in grass carp, six diets containing graded choline levels [154.0 (basal diet), 321.8, 672.2, 1024.9, 1373.2, and 1722.8 mg/kg] were fed to 450 sub-adult grass carp (583.44 ± 1.65 g) for 56 d. The fish were divided into six groups with three replicates of 25 fish each. The results showed as follows: 1) compared with the control group, choline increased the growth performance, muscle protein content and choline deposition in sub-adult grass carp (P < 0.05), while decreased adhesive force (P < 0.001) and cooking loss (P = 0.037), thus improving fish flesh quality. 2) Dietary choline levels of 1024.9 to 1373.2 mg/kg significantly increased the frequency of myofibers with diameter >100 μm (P = 0.002) and fusion coefficient (P < 0.001). The mRNA expression of MyoG (P < 0.001), MyoD (P < 0.001), Myf5 (P = 0.005), MRF4 (P = 0.028), MYHC (P = 0.005), and myomaker (P < 0.001) increased quadratically with dietary choline, whereas MSTN expression was non-significantly lower than controls at 1024.9 to 1373.2 mg/kg (P = 0.078). Furthermore, choline increased the mRNA expression of IGF1, PI3K, AKT, and TOR (P < 0.05) and decreased the mRNA expression of FoxO3a and MuRF1 (P < 0.05). 3) Muscle adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content (P < 0.001), protein levels of NDUFV1 (P = 0.004) and ATP5A1 (P = 0.046), and the mRNA levels of NRF1 (P = 0.017), TFAM (P = 0.014), and OPA1 (P < 0.001) were increased quadratically. Conversely, the mRNA levels of DRP1 (P < 0.001), PINK (P < 0.001), and PARKIN (P = 0.002) were decreased quadratically. 4) According to the quadratic regression analysis of percent weight gain (PWG) and muscle ATP content, the choline requirement of sub-adult grass carp was 951.2 and 1084.9 mg/kg diet, respectively. In summary, optimal levels of dietary choline improved the myofiber hypertrophy in grass carp, which was associated with upregulation of myogenesis regulator and the activation of the IGF1/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and maintained mitochondrial stability probably via regulating mitochondrial quality control.Keywords: Choline, Grass carp, Muscle, Myofiber, Protein deposition, Mitochondrial function

Introduction

With its rapid economic growth, aquaculture has contributed substantially to meeting the growing food demand (Garlock et al., 2022). At the same time, as consumers pursuing quality of life, fish flesh quality (hygienic, nutritional, and organoleptic quality) has become increasingly important (Listrat et al., 2016). Numerous studies have confirmed that nutrients can enhance fish flesh quality (Ma et al., 2023). Choline, an essential water-soluble vitamin for fish, can be converted into phosphatidylcholine (PC), acetylcholine (ACH) and betaine to exert biological effects (Onono and Morris, 2020). Choline improved growth performance and muscle shear force in young grass carp (Zhao et al., 2015). Studies have shown that there is a close link between the physicochemical properties of muscle and the growth and development of myofiber (Ismail and Joo, 2017). Up to now, the effect of choline on the growth and development of myofiber has not been reported. In vitro studies have demonstrated that choline deficiency induced apoptosis in C2C12 cells, and inhibited the proliferation of rat fetal progenitor cells and IMR-32 neuroblastoma cells (da Costa et al., 2004; Niculescu et al., 2004; Zeisel, 2011). Therefore, this study speculates that choline may contribute to the growth and development of myofiber.

Muscle growth is a dynamically regulated biological process involving myofiber hyperplasia and hypertrophy. Hyperplasia refers to the proliferation, differentiation and fusion of myoblasts to form new myofiber. Hypertrophy is caused by the fusion of myoblasts with myofiber or protein deposition (Valente et al., 2013). Myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs) play crucial roles in myofiber proliferation, differentiation and fusion (Zammit, 2017). The insulin/insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R)- protein kinase B (AKT)-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) axis has been suggested to be a central pathway that promotes protein synthesis in hypertrophied muscle (Sartori et al., 2021). However, the effect of choline on myogenic regulators has not been reported in any study and requires further investigation. Betaine is one of the metabolites of choline, which has been found to increase MRFs expression in muscle of mice and partridge shank broiler (Chen et al., 2018; Senesi et al., 2013). In addition, choline increased the protein content of grass carp muscle (Zhao et al., 2015). Choline deficiency reduced gene expression of TOR in fish muscle and intestine (Wu et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2015). Therefore, choline may affect the proliferation and hypertrophy of myofiber and needs to be further investigated.

Myofiber growth and development is not only a process of morphological and structural changes but also contains metabolic remodeling (Romanello and Sandri, 2021). During muscle generation, the increase in protein synthesis leads to an increase in energy demand. Mitochondria are the main place for producing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Ferri et al., 2020). Mitochondrial function is reflected by the respiratory chain complexes I–V and is also influenced by mitochondrial morphology (Picard et al., 2013). For example, choline deficiency led to the phenomenon of giant mitochondria and a decrease in respiratory complex I activity in rat liver (Arao et al., 2020; Hensley et al., 2000). This suggests a close link between choline and mitochondrial function. In addition, mitochondrial morphologic functions are dynamically regulated by mitochondrial quality control (MQC) (Gan et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2023a). However, little research has been done on the role of choline in MQC. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number is a general marker of mitochondrial biogenesis (Remels et al., 2010). A study found that methionine/choline deficiency (MCD) caused a reduction in liver mtDNA levels in mice (Arao et al., 2020). Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), PC and cardiolipin are important for maintaining the structural integrity and stability of the mitochondrial membrane. Altered membrane phospholipid composition leads to abnormal function of optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1), dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) and fission protein 1 (FIS1), interfering with the mitochondrial fusion division process (Basu Ball et al., 2018; Mejia and Hatch, 2016). Whereas, choline acts as a precursor substance for PC, its deficiency leads to an increased PE/PC ratio. Therefore, choline may regulate mitochondrial morphology and function through MQC, although the exact mechanism remains to be investigated.

This study systematically investigated the effects of choline on MRFs, protein deposition, and mitochondrial morphology and function in order to reveal the effects of choline on the growth and development of skeletal muscle and its mechanism of action. Partial theoretical basis was provided for the mechanism of choline to improve fish flesh quality. Additionally, this study determined the choline requirement of sub-adult grass carp, providing theoretical guidance for the formulation of grass carp diets.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The experimental protocols involved in this study were approved by the Animal Care Advisory Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (No. LQR2022214008).

Animals and experimental design

The experimental grass carp were purchased from a fishery in Deyang, Sichuan, China. After feeding with a commercial diet for 30 d, a total of 450 healthy grass carp (583.44 ± 1.65 g) were randomly assigned to eighteen 900-L glass-fiber tanks (six treatments, three replicates per treatment, 25 fish per replicate). Subsequently, a choline-deficient diet was conducted for two weeks to reduce the choline content in the fish. The fish were fed six diets supplemented with different levels of choline for 56 d. During this period, normal conditions for fish growth were maintained, with water temperature of 28.4 ± 1.5 °C, pH of 7.5 ± 0.4, and oxygen content consistently greater than 6.0 mg/L. Feed the fish four times a day at fixed times (satiation feeding). A circular disc with a diameter of 80 cm was placed in the barrel to collect the leftover feed. After each feeding for 30 min, lift out the circular disc to collect the leftover, and accurately record the feed intake (FI) during the experiment period. The fish were fasted for 24 h and weighed to calculate their production performance, such as weight gain (WG) and percent weight gain (PWG).

Experimental diet preparation

The experimental diet was designed based on the nutritional requirements of grass carp, and its formulation and proximate composition are shown in Table 1. Protein sources include mainly casein, gelatin and fish meal. Lipid sources were fish oil and soybean oil. This experimental gradient was designed with reference to the choline requirement of young grass carp (Zhao et al., 2015). In general, juvenile fish have higher nutrient requirements. Therefore, test gradient is 0, 350, 700, 1050, 1400 and 1750 mg/kg choline. The actual choline levels in the diets were determined using ion chromatography at 154.0 (basal diet), 321.8, 672.2, 1024.9, 1373.2 and 1722.8 mg/kg. The prepared diets were stored in a dry and cool environment to prevent spoilage.

Sample collection

After the experiment, this study used MS-222 (50 mg/L) to anesthetize the fish and measured their body weight. Subsequently, the fish were rapidly dissected, and the white muscle on both sides of the spine was divided into four parts (referred to as M1, M2, M3, and M4, respectively) according to the experimental requirements. The schematic diagram for muscle sample collection is detailed in Fig. S1. M1 was used to detect pH. Muscle pH0 h and pH24 h were measured using a calibrated pH probe (Testo 205 pH meter, Testo AG Company, Lenzkirch, Germany). During the process, the muscle samples were always kept on ice. The physicochemical properties of M2, including hardness, springiness and adhesion force, were detected by using a texture analyzer (XCTX00U00, AMETEK Brookfield, Middleton, CA, USA). Treated M3 with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses, including proximate composition, tissue enzyme activity assay, qPCR and Western blotting. Finally, M4 was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and 3% glutaraldehyde solutions for histological observation and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations, respectively.

Chemical analysis

The proximate composition of the muscle was analyzed using the methods specified by AOAC (2005). Moisture content was determined by drying the samples at 105 °C, following method No. 925.10. Crude protein content was measured using the Kjeldahl method, as outlined in method No. 990.03. Crude lipid content was assessed using the Soxhlet exhaustive extraction technique, in accordance with method No. 2003.05. The feed gross energy level was assessed by the automatic calorimeter (Parr Oxygen Bomb Calorimeter, 6400, Parr Instrument Company, Moline, IL, USA).

ATP (No. A095-1-1) and ACH (No. A105-1-1) in muscle tissue were analyzed by the phosphomolybdic acid colorimetric method and microplate method, respectively, using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Muscle reactive oxygen species (ROS, No. S0034S) content was determined according to the reagents from Beyotime Biotechnology Inc. (Shanghai, China). Muscle betaine (No. KTB-2350) content was determined according to the reagent of Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Muscle choline (No. AAT-40007) content was determined by a quantitation kit purchased from AAT Bioquest Co., Ltd. (Pleasanton, CA, USA).

Histology observation and TEM observation

The 4% paraformaldehyde-treated muscles were embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, sections were made and stained by hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). Images were acquired using a Nikon light microscope (TS100, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Thereafter, the frequency distribution of myofibers was counted by Image-J software. At the same time, each section was observed under a 200 × microscope, and three fields of view were selected and photographed in the upper, middle and lower positions under the microscope. After recording the total number of intact skeletal muscle cells (denoted as Ncells) and the total number of myonuclei (denoted as Nnuclei), the ratio of Nnuclei to Ncells was calculated, defined as the fusion coefficient. Reverse transcription used PrimeScript RT kit (RR037Q, TaKaRa Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Acquired RNA was stored at −80 °C. Primers were provided by Hzykang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). The primer sequences utilized for RT-qPCR are presented in Table 2. β-Actin was selected as an internal reference based on previous studies. Finally, the gene expression level was detected using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Tissue proteins were obtained from muscle samples using a mixture of phenylmethylsulphonyl fluoride (PMSF) and radio immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (RIPA) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Tissue protein concentrations were assayed using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (P0010S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The target protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate - polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) electrophoresis. Subsequently, the target proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After sealing, they were co-incubated with primary antibodies (NDUFV1, SDHB, UQCRC2, ATP5A1, P-AKT, P-TOR, P-FoxO3, and MuRF1) at 4 °C for 18 h. Antibody information is shown in Table S1. Primary antibodies were removed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with secondary antibodies for 80 min. Finally, the bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kits (P0018S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and quantified by Image J software.

Immunofluorescence assay

The paraffin sections were first placed on a staining rack in an oven for 1 h, and then treated with xylene to deparaffinize the sections. Sections were treated with 3% H2O2 solution and rinsed 2 to 3 times with PBS for antigen repair. After the repair was completed, the sections were rinsed again with PBS, closed for 1 h and incubated for 18 h in a refrigerator at 4 °C with primary antibody added dropwise, and rewarmed for 1 h and then the secondary antibody was incubated for 1 h under the condition of light protection. Sections were rinsed with PBS, stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, and rinsed again with PBS prior to mounting. Antibody information is shown in Table S1. Finally, images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI4000B, Mannheim, Germany) and quantified using Image J software.

Results

Dietary choline promoted the growth and improved fish flesh quality

As exhibited in Table 3, the IBW of fish was essentially the same for all treatments, with no significant differences (P = 0.849). The final body weight (FBW), WG, PWG, specific growth rate (SGR) and FI in the 321.8 to 1373.2 mg/kg choline groups were significantly higher than the choline-deficient group (P < 0.05) and reached the highest in the 1024.9 mg/kg choline group. After a homogeneity of variance test, this experiment generally conformed to the parabolic model. The optimal choline requirement for sub-adult grass carp was 951.2 mg/kg by quadratic regression analysis of PWG.

Discussion

Dietary choline promoted the growth and improved flesh quality of grass carp

Choline is essential for the growth of fish. This study found that moderate amounts of choline improved feed efficiency and accelerated growth in grass carp, a result similar to those found in juvenile and young grass carp (Yuan et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2015). Muscle levels of choline and its metabolites can be assessed as an indicator of choline nutritional status, and lower levels may cause a range of losses to the organism. This study found that choline deficiency reduced choline, betaine and ACH content in grass carp muscle, and the same results were found in cobia (Rachycentron canadum) and yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco) muscle (Lu et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2016; Mai et al., 2009). This may be closely related to the expression of choline transporter proteins. Choline molecules are difficult to diffuse passively because of their charged structure, making the presence of choline transporter proteins essential. In this experiment, choline deficiency reduced the mRNA abundance of only CTL1 and CHT1, while the mRNA abundance of CTL2 was unchanged. This phenomenon may be attributed to the functional and tissue-specific characteristics of choline transporters. The high-affinity choline transporter 1 (CHT1), a biomarker of cholinergic neurons, is specifically localized to the presynaptic membranes of neural synapses and critically supports ACH synthesis (Ribeiro et al., 2006). Its mRNA upregulation positively correlates with increased ACH content in muscle tissue. CTL1 and CTL2 are expressed in multiple tissues of animals, but the expression in intensities vary (Inazu, 2019). For example, in human intestinal epithelial LS180 cells, the expression intensity of choline transporter carriers is CTL1 > CTL4 > CTL3 > CTL2 > CTL5 (Horie et al., 2014). In the skin of grass carp, the expression intensity of choline transporter carriers is CTL5 > CTL2 > CTL1 (Yuan et al., 2021a). In the hepatopancreas of Pacific white shrimp, it has been found that the expression of CTL2 is slightly higher than that of CTL1 (Lu et al., 2023). Therefore, choline uptake in grass carp muscle is likely mediated mainly by CTL1 rather than CTL2.

In addition to growth performance, better meat quality is an important goal in aquaculture production. Physicochemical properties of fish, such as hardness, springiness and cooking loss, are also important factors influencing consumer purchases. Hardness and springiness reflect chewing ability to some extent, while adhesive force tends to be negatively correlated with springiness (Wang et al., 2024). This study found that choline deficiency reduced muscle hardness and springiness in grass carp while increasing cooking loss. This is similar to previous studies on young grass carp (Zhao et al., 2015). Fish flesh quality is closely related to the growth and development of myofibers, which in turn involves hyperplasia and hypertrophy. Therefore, the effect of choline on the growth and development of myofibers in sub-adult grass carp was next discussed in this study.

Effect of dietary choline on the growth and development of skeletal muscle in grass carp

Muscle growth in fish differs from that of mammals in that hyperplasia and hypertrophy are always present throughout the growth cycle. The distribution of the mean diameter of myofibers can be used as a differentiating criterion (Listrat et al., 2016; Zammit, 2017). Here, when 672.2 and 1024.9 mg/kg choline were added to the diets, the proportion of myofibers below 50 μm was reduced while the proportion of myofibers greater than 100 μm was increased, implying that choline may accelerate muscle growth primarily by promoting myofiber hypertrophy. Additionally, the increase in the muscle fusion coefficient of grass carp further validates this point. This study found that optimal choline levels increased the expression of genes associated with proliferation and differentiation, and MyHC protein levels in muscle. However, MSTN, which acts as a negative regulator, was suppressed. There are no reports on the effect of choline on myofiber development. Betaine is a homomeric methyl donor and can be oxidized from choline. Ma et al. (2024) study showed that betaine up-regulated the expression of regulators of muscle production, including the mRNA and protein of MyoG and Pax7, and down-regulated the expression of the mRNA and protein of MSTN, which promoted the growth of breast muscles during embryonic development of goslings. This is similar to our findings. Myomaker and myomixer have received much attention in recent years as key myogenic molecules regulating myofibroblast fusion (Chen et al., 2020). Test data showed that choline upregulated myomaker mRNA abundance in the muscle of grass carp, while the mRNA abundance of myomixer remained unchanged. This may be related to the expression patterns of myomaker and myomixer. Studies on rainbow trout and gilthead sea bream have found that myomixer is continuously expressed during embryonic and post-embryonic development, and the expression level decreases with weight gain (Perelló-Amorós et al., 2022; Perello-Amoros et al., 2021). This suggests that the contribution of myoblast fusion to myofibers gradually decreases during the later stages of fish growth. Based on the above analysis, choline may contribute to myofiber hypertrophy by promoting myoblast proliferation and differentiation, as well as fusion.

Besides myofiber fusion, protein deposition also plays a very important role in myofiber hypertrophy (Valente et al., 2013; Johnston et al., 2011). This research found that choline increased the meat yield of grass carp as well as the protein content of the muscle, which is consistent with previous studies on grass carp (Zhao et al., 2015). Protein deposition is closely related to protein synthesis, which is primarily controlled by the PI3K/AKT/TOR pathway (Hay and Sonenberg, 2004). This experiment found that choline deficiency reduced the levels of IGF1, PI3K, AKT and TOR mRNA, and p-TOR and p-AKT proteins. Furthermore, it boosted the mRNA levels of 4E-BP1 within the muscle of sub-adult grass carp. Oster et al. showed that a choline-rich maternal diet (MET) upregulated IGF signaling, increased IGF2 levels and decreased insulin-like growth factor binding protein (IGFBP) in fetal muscle (Oster et al., 2016). Accordingly, it is reasonable to believe that choline may promote muscle protein synthesis through the TOR pathway.

Protein deposition is a dynamically regulated process, in which catabolism plays an equally important role. MuRF1 and MaFbx play pivotal roles in the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, specifically recognizing and ubiquitinating muscle protein substrates for proteasomal degradation (Bodine et al., 2001). In muscle tissue, FoxO transcription factors bind to specific DNA sequences in the promoter regions of the MuRF1 and MaFbx genes, thereby initiating the expression program of such downstream target genes (Zhou et al., 2023b). In this experiment, dietary choline decreased the mRNA abundance and protein expression of MuRF1 in muscle. Interestingly, choline increased the phosphorylation of FoxO3a and attenuated the gene expression of FoxO3a, whereas the mRNA abundance of FoxO1a and FoxO1b remained unchanged. This suggests that choline may regulate the expression of MuRF1 mainly through FoxO3a. In addition, phosphorylation of FoxO3a is regulated by the activation state of the PI3K/AKT pathway (Ebert et al., 2019). Combined with the previous section, the following idea has been derived: dietary choline inhibits muscle protein degradation through activation of the PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway.

In summary, choline may promote myofiber hypertrophy by enhancing myofiber fusion and protein deposition. Both of these pathways require energy, and mitochondria serve as the primary site of energy production. Therefore, we have further developed our research on the morphology and function of muscle mitochondria.

Dietary choline improved mitochondrial morphology and function in grass carp muscle

In this study, choline increased the ATP content in the muscle of the grass carp. Mitochondria are the main site of ATP production (Ferri et al., 2020). Their function can be reflected by the respiratory complexes I–V. Mitochondrial dysfunction leads to increased ROS levels. The results showed that choline deficiency decreased the protein levels of NDUFV1 and ATP5A1 and increased ROS levels. However, mitochondrial complexes II and III were not altered. Similarly, studies in rat liver found that choline deficiency reduced mitochondrial complex I and ATP synthase activity (Petrosillo et al., 2007; Teodoro et al., 2008). The reason for the differences in the effects of choline on mitochondrial complexes is not clear. A specific mechanism needs to be further investigated. The function of mitochondria is closely related to their morphology and structure. TEM results showed that choline deficiency leads to the vacuolization of mitochondria and loss of mitochondrial cristae. This suggests that choline has an important role in maintaining mitochondrial morphology and structure. To sum up, choline ensures proper mitochondrial function and thus energy production.

The morphology and structure of mitochondria are closely related to MQC. Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles that undergo fusion and fission and balance mitochondrial mass through mitochondrial biogenesis and autophagy (Liu et al., 2023). PGC-1α and NRF1/2 play extremely critical regulatory roles in mitochondrial biogenesis (Scarpulla, 2008). Mitochondrial fusion is mainly regulated by OPA1, MFN1/2, and mitochondrial fission is dependent on DRP1 (Fonseca et al., 2019). The Parkin-dependent pathway (PINK/Parkin)-mediated mitochondrial autophagy protects cells by removing intracellularly damaged, dysfunctional and excess mitochondria (Bingol and Sheng, 2016). In the present study, appropriate levels of dietary choline up-regulated the mRNA abundance of PGC-1α, NRF1, TFAM, OPA1 and MFN1/2, and down-regulated the mRNA abundance of DRP1, PINK and PARKIN. However, no studies have been reported on the effect of choline on MQC. This study hypothesize that this may have something to do with the basic physiological function of choline. Choline is a precursor for the synthesis of PC. Studies on rat liver mitochondria found that choline deficiency resulted in an elevated PE/PC ratio. Alterations in mitochondrial membrane phospholipids affect mitochondrial fusion division (Basu Ball et al., 2018). The above results then suggest that choline may affect MQC through PC, but the exact mechanism needs to be further investigated. Interestingly, studies on mouse liver mitochondria found that concurrent choline methionine deficiency upregulated biogenesis and autophagy-related genes accelerating mitochondrial turnover (Arao et al., 2020). This suggests that choline deficiency alone and simultaneous choline/methionine deficiency have different effects on MQC.

Choline requirements of sub-adult grass carp based on different indicators

In this study, dietary choline requirements for sub-adult grass carp were estimated to be 951.2 or 1084.9 mg/kg based on PWG and muscle ATP content (Fig. 5). Choline requirements determined based on growth performance were slightly lower than for young grass carp (Zhao et al., 2015). This may be related to the different nutrient requirements of fish at different growth stages. In general, juvenile fish have higher nutrient requirements (NRC, 2011). In addition, requirements determined by muscle ATP content were slightly higher than those determined by production performance. Similarly, studies in grass carp found that requirements based on gut health indices and gill barrier function were higher than those based on production performance (Yuan et al., 2021b; Zhao et al., 2016). This suggests that in addition to meeting growth requirements, fish need additional choline to maintain normal muscle energy metabolism.

Conclusions

This study found that dietary choline improved the growth performance and flesh quality of sub-adult grass carp. It promoted myofiber hypertrophy, which was associated with upregulation of myogenesis regulator expression and the activation of the IGF1/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Moreover, choline improved mitochondrial morphology and regulated MQC to maintain mitochondrial stability, thereby ensuring efficient energy supply. Finally, based on PWG and muscle ATP content, the dietary choline requirements for sub-adult grass carp were estimated to be 951.2 or 1084.9 mg/kg. These findings have important implications for optimizing feed formulations for grass carp.
