Dear Editor,

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has emerged as one of the most important tools in the management of critically ill patients inside a critical care setup.1 With the advent of multiorgan bedside POCUS in various specialties, a structured checklist has yet to be developed. A checklist systematically guides clinicians through the evaluation of major organ systems—cardiac, pulmonary, abdominal/trauma, vascular, and neuro—along with fluid status in an extremely simple yet practical way. This comprehensive approach allows for rapid identification of life-threatening conditions such as cardiac tamponade, pneumothorax, hemothorax, pulmonary embolism, hypovolemia, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and raised intracranial pressure, as well as supports in early exclusion of various differentials, thereby helping in decision-making in acute scenarios.2

Due to the inflow of a high number of critical patients in our ICU and the limitation of manpower, we have recently introduced a structured Multiorgan POCUS Checklist (Annexure 1) in our central ICU, with the aim of standardizing bedside POCUS findings and improving outcomes. The utility of this checklist extends beyond diagnosis. It enables real-time monitoring of responses to interventions, especially during hemodynamic instability, fluid resuscitation, and vasoactive drug titration. For instance, serial assessment of inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter, venous excess ultrasound (VEXUS) grading, and lung ultrasound patterns can help in titrating fluid therapy in an individualized approach.3 Similarly, cardiac function assessment and DVT screening contribute significantly to patient safety and help in early diagnosis of impending pulmonary embolism.4 Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) measurement, along with pupillary reflex (PLR) assessment, improves sensitivity in detecting raised intracranial pressure, thereby making the diagnosis more robust.5,6 In ocular trauma with hematoma or hyphema, direct PLR assessment may be difficult, so ultrasonography offers a useful tool in measuring PLR.7

From a practical standpoint, the checklist enhances efficiency by minimizing unnecessary shifting of the patient for imaging, thereby reducing radiation exposure and associated risks of endotracheal tube dislodgment, ventilation failure, etc. Another strength is that it is presented in a very simple, more binary, and practical manner, which is easily applicable as well as reproducible at the bedside for clinicians with varying levels of ultrasound expertise. In essence, the structured Multiorgan POCUS Checklist offers a holistic and dynamic framework that integrates diagnostic accuracy with therapeutic guidance. By streamlining the use of POCUS, it not only augments traditional physical examination but also has the potential to improve patient outcomes in the ICU setting. We believe that this structured checklist should be incorporated into routine critical care practice to ensure reproducibility, safety, and quality of care with further validation.
