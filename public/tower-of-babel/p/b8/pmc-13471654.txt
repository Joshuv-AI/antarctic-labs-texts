Ultrasound-stimulated BTO-BSA-gen inhibits implant-associated infections by suppressing bacterial adhesion and potentiating aminoglycosides
Authors: Jianing Ding, Renke He, Siyue Tao, Xiao-Er Wei, Xiangwei Yuan, Liehu Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiofilms render implant-associated infections (IAIs) particularly challenging to manage. Inhibiting bacterial adhesion, the initial stage of biofilm formation, holds great potential in alleviating IAIs. In this work, we proved that the ultrasound(US)-generated electricity provided by barium titanate nanoparticles (BTO) could inhibit bacterial adhesion. Transcriptomics and metabolomics results validated that the BTO under ultrasound reduces the amount of integral membrane molecules in S. aureus and suppresses the expression of toxin molecules. Moreover, ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO improved the effectiveness of aminoglycosides through “bioelectric effect”. Based on these findings, we further synthesized gentamicin(gen) loaded BTO-BSA (BTO-BSA-Gen), which demonstrated promising bactericidal effects in an ex vivo human skin model. Animal studies show the engineered BTO-BSA-Gen could substantially safeguard implants against bacteremia, consequently diminishing the onset and severity of IAIs. The frequency of hematogenous IAIs decreased significantly from 83.7% to 25% in response to ultrasound following BTO-BSA-Gen administration. This research offers valuable insights for the development of noninvasive, wireless therapies for IAIs treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04597-7.Keywords: Biofilm, Ultrasound, Implant-associated infections, Piezoelectric nanoparticle

Introduction

As the global population grows and life expectancy increases, the prevalence of implants, such as joint prostheses and cardiovascular implantable electronic devices, has notably increased [1, 2]. Nevertheless, the increasing prevalence of implant-associated infections (IAIs) is a matter of great concern [3]. Annually, individuals in the United States suffer more than 70,000 periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs). Bacterial implant-infections differ from other bacterial infections in that the bacteria do not exist as dispersed cells. Instead, biofilms are formed and adhere strongly to the implant [4, 5]. Biofilms are capable of withstanding biocides and antibiotics, leading to the common practice of extracting and replacing implants [6, 7]. Consequently, proactive inhibition of biofilm formation is imperative for preventing IAIs.

As the first step in biofilm formation, bacterial adhesion paves the way for bacterial colonization of implants [8, 9]. Preventing bacterial adhesion is an effective strategy for inhibiting biofilm formation. Additionally, targeting bacterial adhesion facilitates the colonization of implants by host cells, thereby allowing additional time for the host to develop immunity and for antibiotics to effectively combat infections [10]. Bacterial adhesion can be divided into the following two stages: in the first stage, reversible adhesion is mediated by physicochemical forces, and in the second stage, irreversible adhesion is mediated by microbial surface complexes [11]. The first stage of bacterial adhesion is characterized by limited stability and can be disrupted by physical interventions such as electricity [12, 13]. Electric currents could inhibit bacterial adhesion by enhancing the mutual repulsion between bacteria and biomaterials [14]. Moreover, electricity can enhance the efficacy of antibiotics against planktonic bacteria through a phenomenon known as the “bioelectric effect.” [15]. Based on these phenomena, a clinical trial is underway for an FDA-approved electrical wound care dressing. Cathodic-voltage-controlled electrical stimulation has been investigated in the context of external fixators and dental prostheses [16–18]. However, invasive procedures and the presence of wires may exacerbate infections, thereby preventing the use of electricity with implants such as cardiac pacemakers and artificial joints. To solve this problem, a noninvasive, wireless technique for producing electric current is desperately needed.

Piezoelectric materials exhibit considerable potential in the domains of wireless, nonintrusive, electrical stimulation. By external stimulation, piezoelectric materials can transform external stimuli into electrical energy, which can be introduced into the human body [19]. Barium titanate (BT) was selected due to its lead-free composition, favorable biocompatibility, stable piezoelectricity, and ease of surface functionalization [20]. Ultrasound has emerged as an effective external stimulus due to the ease of control, capability of deep tissue penetration, and high precision. Recent research has indicated that the combined use of ultrasound with piezoelectric nanomaterials has widespread applications in electrical stimulation [21].

We hypothesize that under ultrasound stimulation, electricity from BTO nanoparticles may inhibit the bacterial adhesion of pathogens to biofilms and hinder the formation of biofilms. At the same time, BTO under ultrasound stimulation may, like direct current, enhance the effectiveness of antibiotics. Our findings indicate that, under ultrasound stimulation, BTO effectively interferes with bacterial adhesion, thus inhibiting biofilm formation. Additionally, the electricity produced by BTO augments the bactericidal efficiency of antibiotics, particularly aminoglycosides, against Staphylococcus (S.) aureus and Pseudomonas (P.) aeruginosa. The mechanism study shows that the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO reduces the amount of integral membrane molecules in S. aureus, suppresses the expression of toxin molecules, and hinders the ability of the bacterium to adapt to the host environment. Drawing from these results, we synthesized BTO-BSA-Gen. Under ultrasound stimulation, BTO-BSA-Gen can release antibiotics while simultaneously inhibiting the adhesion of bacteria to implants. An ex vivo human skin model demonstrated the biosafety and antibacterial effect of BTO-BSA-Gen. This research holds promise for offering valuable insights into the development of noninvasive therapy for IAIs.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of BTO-BSA-gen

The Gen loaded piezoelectric nanoparticle (BTO-BSA-Gen) was prepared by stepwise processes including the synthesis of BTO nanoparticles, surface coating of the BSA and the loading of Gen onto the surface of BTO-BSA (Scheme 1). BTO nanoparticles with a size of 68.7 ± 8.9 nm were synthesized by a solvothermal process (Figure S1). Bare BTO nanoparticles were coated with BSA to improve their physiological stability. Compared with the aggregational behavior of bare BTO, the prepared BTO-BSA was well dispersed for more than 12 h, indicating the higher colloidal stability. After that, BTO-BSA and Gen were physically mixed to obtain the BTO-BSA-Gen nanoparticle. The TEM image showed that the prepared BTO-BSA-Gen feature a polygon structure with a uniform diameter of approximately 75.3 ± 6.1 nm (Fig. 1A). The corresponding elemental mapping images indicated a uniform distribution of Ti, Ba, O and N in the as-prepared BTO-BSA-Gen nanoparticles, which was further confirmed by the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Figure S2). FT-IR analysis further verified successful BSA modification, as characteristic peaks of amide II (1536 cm⁻¹) and amide III (1464 cm⁻¹) appeared in BTO-BSA and BTO-BSA-Gen(Figure S2D).The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of BTO showed an obvious peak at 2θ = 45° (Fig. 1B), which was attributed to cubic BaTiO3 structure [22]. In particular, this peak was retained in the spectrum of BTO-BSA-Gen, indicating the surface modification had no considerable influence on the crystallinity of BTO. As shown in Fig. 1C, the synthesized BTO-BSA-Gen had a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 114.7 ± 3.6 nm, which is larger than that of the bare BTO (79.8 ± 2.4 nm) and BTO-BSA (91.4 ± 3.3 nm) nanoparticles. The larger hydrodynamic diameter detected by DLS was ascribed to the hydrated shell in aqueous dispersion, whereas TEM was performed under dry conditions. Meanwhile, after surface modifications, the zeta potential changed from − 17.7 ± 0.8 mV (bare BTO) to -27.4 ± 1.6 mV (BTO-BSA) and further increased to 9.8 ± 0.5 mV (BTO-BSA-Gen), indicating the successful surface coating and the Gen loading (Fig. 1D). Despite the mild surface potential, BTO-BSA-Gen remained well dispersed in PBS and serum for over 12 h due to the steric stabilization of BSA. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicated that the mass contents of BSA and Gen were 11.54 wt% and 16.41 wt%, respectively (Fig. 1E). For verification, TGA curves of pure BSA, pure gentamicin, and BTO-BSA-Gen were compared and are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S2E). Under ultrasound waves, the charge separation resulted in generation of electric potential. Under the input power of 2 W, the output voltage of the BTO-BSA-Gen NPs composed device reached 400 mV.

The generated electricity effect of BTO driven by US for suppressed biofilm formation via the inhibition of bacterial adhesion

Electricity was shown to enhance the mutual repulsion between bacteria and biomaterials in the first stage of bacterial adhesion, thereby affecting various forces, such as van der Waals forces, acid–base interactions, and electrostatic forces [23]. According to our research, the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO hindered bacterial adhesion, thus inhibiting biofilm formation (Fig. 2A). First, a bacterial adhesion assay demonstrated that exposing P. aeruginosa (PAO 1) and S. aureus (ATCC43300) to a BTO solution (200 µg/ml) for 4 h with ultrasound resulted in a notable reduction in the number of adherent bacteria on the custom plates. The reduction in adherent bacteria on titanium plates was more significant than that on PEEK plates (Fig. 2B, Figure S3). However, the decrease in bacterial adherence may not be solely attributed to the inhibition of bacterial adhesion. Other factors, such as a decrease in the bacterial suspension concentration or the mortality of adherent bacteria, could also contribute to this phenomenon. To determine the effects of the ultrasound-irradiated BTO solution on bacterial suspensions and the mortality of adherent bacteria, the following trials were carried out. The application of 200 µg/ml of BTO solution under ultrasound (2 W cm− 2) did not obviously inhibit bacterial proliferation (Fig. 2C, Figure S4A). This result excludes the possibility that the reduced bacterial adhesion was caused by planktonic growth inhibition.The anti-adhesion effect reached a plateau at 3 h; thus, 3 h was chosen as the optimal ultrasound duration (Figure S4B). Time-killing assays indicated that the BTO solution (200 µg/ml) under ultrasound (2 W cm− 2) did not effectively eliminate S. aureus (Figure S5). This further verifies that the observed antibacterial effect mainly arises from inhibiting bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation, rather than direct bactericidal activity.The morphology of S. aureus was not altered by the ultrasonic-irradiated BTO solution, according to the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results (Fig. 2D, Figure S6A). PI staining and flow cytometry demonstrated that BTO + US significantly increased the membrane permeability of S. aureus (Figure S6B). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to visualize the quantity and morphology of the adherent bacteria. The results showed that the number of adherent bacteria significantly decreased in comparison to that in the control groups and that there was no discernible bacterial death on the plates (Fig. 2E, Figure S7). The purpose of the following experiments was to determine whether the ultrasound-generated electricity from the BTO could effectively prevent biofilm formation. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed that biofilm formation by S. aureus and P. aeruginosa was significantly suppressed in the BTO + US group compared with the other groups (Fig. 2F, G). In the BTO + US group, continuous ultrasound stimulation significantly inhibited the biofilm formation of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus within the initial 12 h. After the discontinuation of ultrasound stimulation, the biomass of the S. aureus biofilms significantly increased, ultimately reaching levels comparable to those of the control group (Fig. 2H). The aforementioned trial demonstrated that the ultrasound-generated electrical activity of BTO could hinder biofilm formation by inhibiting bacterial adhesion.

Ultrasound-generated electricity from BTO increases the efficacy of aminoglycosides

The “bioelectric effect” caused by electricity is influenced by electrical parameters, the type of antibiotic, and the strain of bacteria. In our study, we found that ultrasound-generated electricity improved the effectiveness of antibiotics, particularly aminoglycosides (Fig. 2J), against S. aureus (ATCC 43300) and P. aeruginosa (PAO 1). Linezolid (oxazolidinone), erythromycin (macrolide), gentamicin (aminoglycoside), daptomycin (lipopeptide), minocycline (tetracycline), vancomycin (glycopeptide) and rifampin (rifamycin) are classical antibiotics used against S. aureus [24]. BTO nanoparticles were successfully incorporated into TSB at a concentration of 200 µg/ml. We examined the potential of the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO to improve the bactericidal effectiveness of antibiotics. The findings of our study indicate that the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO enhanced the efficacy of linezolid, erythromycin, gentamicin, and minocycline in combating S. aureus. In contrast, the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO did not enhance the activity of daptomycin, vancomycin, or rifampin against S. aureus (Fig. 2I). Cefepime (cephalosporin), ciprofloxacin (fluoroquinolone), erythromycin (macrolide), gentamicin (aminoglycoside) and colistin (polymyxins) are classical antibiotics used against P. aeruginosa [25]. The ultrasound-generated electricity afforded by BTO has been shown to augment the bactericidal properties of ciprofloxacin, colistin and gentamicin against P. aeruginosa. These antibiotics were selected as first-line agents for implant-associated infections, covering distinct antibacterial mechanisms and representing typical choices in related studies. Nevertheless, the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO did not enhance the efficacy of cefepime or erythromycin against P. aeruginosa (Figure S8A). These results further confirm that ultrasound-triggered electricity from BTO specifically enhances the bactericidal efficacy of aminoglycosides (gentamicin) in P. aeruginosa, consistent with the trend observed in S. aureus. Based on these findings, the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO significantly enhanced the antibacterial properties of aminoglycosides, specifically gentamicin. Further uptake assays using FITC-gentamicin confirmed that BTO + US remarkably promoted the intracellular influx of gentamicin in S. aureus (Figure S8B).

Mechanistic study of the effect of ultrasound-generated electricity from BTO on S. aureus

Transcriptomics is a technique for the comprehensive analysis of all transcripts in a biological sample, which enables investigation of the entire genome [26]. Thus, transcriptomics was employed to evaluate alterations in gene expression following treatment with a BTO concentration (200 µg/ml) under ultrasound (2 W cm− 2). Volcano plot analysis revealed that 17 genes had upregulated expression and 8 genes had downregulated expression in the BTO + US group (Fig. 3A). A heatmap is a useful graphical tool that can be utilized for visualizing gene expression. Additionally, these findings provide insights into the uniformity of the changes in gene expression between the BTO + US group and the control group (Fig. 3B). GO enrichment analysis revealed the suppression of terms such as “plasma membrane” and “integral component of membrane”, which implies that ultrasound-mediated BTO modifies the bacterial membrane composition and the expression of integral molecules of the bacterial membrane (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed the suppression of the “S. aureus infection”, as well as many other metabolic pathways (Fig. 3D). Changes in bacterial metabolism may impact antibiotic susceptibility and the composition of bacterial membranes [27]. Subsequently, the impact of the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO on the metabolism of S. aureus was examined through metabolomics. Principal component analysis (PCA) is a statistical technique used to analyze and reduce the dimensionality of a dataset. The metabolic changes in the BTO + US group differed from those in the control group. PCA analysis (Figure S9) shows clear separation between groups, indicating remarkable gene expression changes. The statistics of up- and down-regulated genes are shown in Figure S10. Metabolomic PCA analysis (Figure S11) further reveals distinct metabolic profiles, supporting the reliability of transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. The metabolic activity of S. aureus was significantly inhibited by BTO under ultrasound, as shown by the volcano plot (Fig. 3E). Additionally, a heatmap provides a visual representation of metabolite expression (Figure S12). GO enrichment analysis revealed that several metabolic pathways, including the “biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids,” “glutathione metabolism,” and “two-component system”, were suppressed in the BTO + US group (Fig. 2F). The “biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids” pathway is associated with bacterial membrane composition and adaptation to the environment [28]. The inhibition of this pathway and the suppression of the component “plasma membrane” according to transcriptomics indicated that the application of BTO under ultrasound affects the composition of bacterial membranes (Fig. 3C, G). Glutathione is essential for S. aureus defense against reactive oxygen species (ROS), and a decrease in glutathione expression corresponds with a decrease in antibiotic susceptibility [29]. The enhanced bactericidal effect of various antibiotics under ultrasound may be partially attributed to the inhibition of glutathione metabolism (Fig. 3H). The two-component system is a crucial signal transduction mechanism in bacteria that allows bacteria to accurately detect and react to environmental signals. The system includes two proteins, a membrane-bound sensor protein and a cytoplasmic response regulator protein [30]. Downregulation of the expression of the two-component system in S. aureus after BTO + US treatment indicated inhibition of the bacterial response to signals from the host and its environment (Fig. 3I). In summary, transcriptomics and metabolomics results validated that the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO reduces the amount of integral membrane molecules in S. aureus, suppresses the expression of toxin molecules, and hinders the ability of the bacterium to adapt to the host environment.

Ultrasound-induced BTO-BSA-Gen: strong antibiofilm efficiency and excellent biosafety

Previous research has demonstrated that the ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO is effective for inhibiting bacterial adhesion to implants. Moreover, the combination of this approach with aminoglycosides has demonstrated significant synergy. Consequently, the combination of gentamicin with BTO led to the development of a new nanodrug called BTO-BSA-Gen.

A subsequent investigation was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of BTO-BSA-Gen in inhibiting biofilm formation. Crystal violet staining and CLSM, demonstrated that, under ultrasound stimulation, BTO-BSA-Gen effectively inhibited the biofilm formation of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus (Fig. 4A, B). Quantitative biofilm biomass analysis (Figure S13A) further confirms that BTO-BSA-Gen combined with ultrasound significantly suppresses biofilm formation, consistent with CLSM observations. BTO-BSA-Gen displayed a sustained gentamicin release profile, and ultrasound stimulation significantly accelerated the release rate (Figure S13B). Under ultrasound stimulation, BTO-BSA-Gen is highly effective at eliminating mature biofilms of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa. (Fig. 4C, D). To evaluate the antibacterial properties and biocompatibility of BTO-BSA-Gen, an ex vivo human skin model was developed using clinical waste tissue [31] (Fig. 4E). SEM was employed to observe the bacterial load and biofilm matrix. The findings revealed that, compared with the control treatment, the BTO-BSA-Gen + US treatment effectively suppressed biofilm formation, as evidenced by the reduced amount of S. aureus and diminished extracellular matrix (Fig. 4G). Flow cytometry showed that the percentages of viable cells in the BTO-BSA-Gen and BTO-BSA-Gen + US groups were similar to that in the control group, indicating that BTO-BSA-Gen and ultrasound are biocompatible (Fig. 4F). The antibacterial efficacy of the BTO-BSA-Gen+ultrasound treatment was confirmed using the plate counting method, which involves detecting viable bacteria in wound tissue homogenates. The results demonstrated that, compared with the other treatments, the BTO-BSA-Gen+ultrasound treatment significantly reduced the bacterial load (Fig. 4H). The expression of repair factors is negatively associated with infection severity, whereas the expression of inflammatory factors is positively correlated with infection severity.21 Following treatment with BTO-BSA-Gen with ultrasound, the skin tissue expression levels of TNFα decreased, whereas those of TGFβ increased, indicating reduced severity of bacterial infection (Figure S14). To guarantee the biocompatibility of BTO-BSA-Gen for future applications, a thorough evaluation of its toxicity was conducted. The results from the in vitro cytotoxicity tests and in vivo toxicity evaluations of BTO-BSA-Gen did not reveal any notable abnormalities (Figure S15-19).

Ultrasound-stimulated BTO-BSA-gen reduces the risk of IAI

Postoperative antibiotic therapy is commonly used in clinical practice to reduce the occurrence of IAIs. However, its effectiveness as a preventive measure is inadequate [32]. Based on the ability of the ultrasound-generated electricity and BTO to dramatically hinder bacterial colonization of implants and boost antibiotic antimicrobial efficacy, we postulated that BTO-BSA-Gen has the potential to safeguard implants from pathogens and further reduce the incidence of IAIs. A mouse model of hematogenous IAIs was established. Briefly, we injected 100 µl of a 106/ml S. aureus (ATCC43300) solution into the tail vein of mice on the second day after Peek plate implantation. The mice were divided into four groups, each containing eight individuals: the BTO-BSA-Gen + US, BTO-BSA-Gen, US, and control groups. In the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group, mice received a systemic injection of BTO-BSA-Gen at a dose of 50 mg/kg via the tail vein after bacterial injection. Ultrasound stimulation was applied to the dorsal region of the mice at a power intensity of 2 W cm− 2 for 3 h, with a frequency of every 12 h. After a three-day period, 25% of the mice developed IAIs. In the control group, 83.7% of the mice exhibited IAIs (Fig. 4I). The CBC test indicated that the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group exhibited normal neutrophil and lymphocyte counts compared to those of the other groups. These findings suggested that systemic inflammation was effectively managed in this group (Fig. 4J).

Ultrasound-guided BTO-BSA-gen relieves rat PJIs

The in vitro experiments showing the antibiofilm efficacy of BTO-BSA-Gen motivated our investigation of its potential as an antibiofilm agent in rat PJI in vivo. The diagram illustrates the methodology employed for the establishment of the rat PJI model, along with the relevant treatment and observation procedures (Fig. 5A). Ultrasound at a power intensity of 2.4 W cm⁻² was applied in the rat PJI model to ensure sufficient penetration through bone and muscle tissue, which is within the safe upper limit of 3.0 W cm⁻².The depth of implant invasion is a critical indicator for assessing the severity of rat PJI [33]. Our findings indicate that the depth of invasion in the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group was significantly lower than that in the other groups, suggesting effective infection control (Fig. 5B). Rat PJI has been found to cause a reduction in cortical bone volume, resulting in implant loosening [33]. On days 2, 20, and 45, the placement of the titanium rod and the volume of cortical bone were assessed via X-ray. On days 20 and 45, noticeable loosening of the titanium rod was observed, accompanied by a decrease in femur cortical bone mass in both the control and US groups. However, there were no changes in titanium rod location or femur cortical bone volume loss in the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group (Fig. 5C). The severity of a bacterial infection is directly associated with the quantity of immune cells that are recruited to the infection site. In the control and US groups, H&E staining revealed extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and cortical bone resorption around the titanium rods, indicating infection-induced inflammation. However, the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group exhibited no significant inflammatory cell infiltration or cortical bone dissolution around the titanium rods, indicating successful infection control (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, visualization of the bacterial load in peri-implant tissues was achieved through Giemsa staining (Fig. 5E). Tissue immunofluorescence provides a visual depiction of immune cell infiltration in the surrounding soft tissue. In the US + BTO-BSA-Gen group, there was a small quantity of macrophages and neutrophils, indicating controlled infection. In contrast, in the control group and the US group, numerous neutrophils and macrophages were observed, suggesting that intense bacterial inflammation was still ongoing (Fig. 5F). Bacterial infection is a major cause of substantial bone loss. Preserving bone volume is crucial in cases of rat PJI. Using micro-CT, the volume of bone surrounding the implants was evaluated in multiple groups. Both the volume of trabecular bone surrounding the implants and the thickness of the cortical bone were significantly greater in the US + BTO-BSA-Gen group than in the other groups (Fig. 5G). The CBC provides valuable insights into the body’s inflammatory response. Lymphocyte counts were measured at various time intervals, revealing a noteworthy increase in the lymphocyte and neutrophil counts within both the control group and the US group. In contrast, the US + BTO-BSA-Gen group demonstrated effective control of systemic inflammation (Fig. 5H). To verify the bacterial load in each group, the implanted rod, the bone surrounding the rod, and the soft tissues surrounding the femur were collected following the sacrifice of rats. The findings revealed a significant reduction in the bacterial count in the US + BTO-BSA-Gen group compared to that in the control, US, and BTO-BSA-Gen groups, which demonstrated infection control in the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group (Fig. 5I, J, K).

Ultrasound-guided BTO-BSA-gen alleviates implant-associated subcutaneous infection

An implant-associated subcutaneous mouse model was established and used to evaluate the antibiofilm effectiveness of BTO-BSA-Gen. The mice’s backs were subcutaneously punctured with sterile Peek plates, and an in-situ injection of an S. aureus (MRSA, ATCC43300) suspension was given. The establishment and treatment process of the implant-associated soft tissue infection mouse model are shown in Fig. 6A. The following groups of mice were randomly assigned to receive Peek plate implants: the control group (saline), the US group, the BTO-BSA-Gen group, and the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group. The epidermis on the Peek plates demonstrated the control of soft tissue infections. In the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group, the infection was well controlled. In contrast, the control and US groups demonstrated persistent inflammation and significant local necrosis, respectively (Fig. 6B). The mice were euthanized on the seventh day, after which the PEEK implants and surrounding tissues were collected. Bacterial quantification confirmed that the bacterial loads on the plates and in the peri-implant soft tissue were almost completely cleared in the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group in contrast with those in the control group and the US group (Fig. 6C, D). On the third and seventh days, blood samples were collected from the mice in each group, and CBC was conducted. The number of lymphocytes and neutrophils did not significantly increase in the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group compared with the other groups, thus indicating successful control of the infection (Fig. 6E). Two inflammatory cell biomarkers, MPO, which represents neutrophils that are involved in inflammation development, and NAG, which represents macrophages that are involved in inflammatory recovery and tissue repair, were subsequently quantified in the tissues (Fig. 6F, G). Additionally, Giemsa staining of peri-implant soft tissues visually confirmed the presence of a matched bacterial load (Fig. 6I). H&E staining revealed that the histological morphology of the peri-implant soft tissues, which showed undamaged tissue conditions in the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group, was unaffected, whereas the control group and the US group exhibited severe inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue necrosis (Fig. 6H). In the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group, no significant recruitment of immune cells was observed. Immunofluorescence was subsequently used to visualize the inflammatory cell profiles of the various groups. Macrophage infiltration and neutrophil infiltration were not significantly different in the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group. This finding suggested that the BTO-BSA-Gen + US group exhibited no significant immune cell recruitment. In contrast, numerous neutrophils with unevenly distributed macrophages were found in both the control and US groups, demonstrating that severe bacterial inflammation still progressed (Fig. 6J). Taken together, our results confirm that BTO-BSA-Gen injection combined with US irradiation effectively alleviated subcutaneous IAIs and protected the surrounding tissue.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrated that the low-intensity focused ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO could inhibit bacterial adhesion on implant surfaces, thus hindering biofilm formation. Additionally, the low-intensity focused ultrasound-generated electricity provided by BTO demonstrates a “bioelectric effect”, thus sensitizing aminoglycosides. Transcriptomics and metabolomics results validated that the BTO under ultrasound reduces the amount of integral membrane molecules in S. aureus and suppresses the expression of toxin molecules. Based on these findings, we synthesized BTO-BSA-Gen, which is a nanomedicine that is capable of reducing the incidence of IAIs and alleviating IAIs. The investigation of piezoelectric particles for the prevention of IAIs, as well as the importance of utilizing piezoelectric particles in conjunction with antibiotics for infection treatment, is expected to provide valuable insights into the domains of pharmaceutical sciences, material engineering, and nanomedicine.

Synthesis of gentamicin loaded BTO-BSA (BTO-BSA-Gen) nanoparticles

Titanium butoxide (170 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL ethanol, followed by the addition of 1.5 mL ammonia solution. Then, barium hydroxide hydrate (Ba(OH)2·H2O, 140 mg) dissolved in 15 mL deionized water was then added. The mixture was transferred into a 40 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 200 °C for 48 h. The obtained BTO nanoparticles were repeatedly washed with acetic acid and ethanol for several times and then re-dispersed in deionized water. Subsequently, 3 mL of BSA (10 mg mL− 1) solution was added dropwise into the BTO dispersion under ultrasonication for 0.5 h, followed by further stirring for another 24 h. After that, the obtained BTO-BSA nanoparticles were collected and washed with deionized water for three times, and then dried at 80 °C for 24 h in an oven. For gentamicin loading, 5 mg of Gen dissolved in PBS was mixed with BTO-BSA dispersion (10 mL, 1.0 mg/mL), followed by stirring for 24 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the product (BTO-BSA-Gen) was collected by centrifugation and washed with ethanol for several times. The loading capacity of Gen was also measured by TG analysis.

Bacterial adhesion assay

Different customized plates, such as titanium and Peek plates, were inserted into six-well polystyrene plates. For each group, 1 ml of bacterial suspension (PAO 1 and ATCC43300) at a concentration of 108 CFU/ml was added to the well. The BTO group received 1 ml of BTO solution at a concentration of 200 µg/ml, followed by continuous ultrasound treatment. In both the ultrasound group and the control group, 1 ml of TSB broth was added to the respective wells. Various treatments were subsequently administered to the wells for a duration of 4 h. Subsequently, the bacterial suspension was aspirated, and the plates were washed thrice with PBS. The bacterial population on the plates was determined using the plate spread method.

Bacterial growth assay: Bacterial growth curves were generated to evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy of the BTO solution. The S. aureus (ATCC 43300) overnight culture was diluted at a ratio of 1:1000 using TSB broth. The TSB broth consisted of pancreatic digest of casein (17.0 g/L), enzymatic digest of soya bean (3.0 g/L), NaCl (5.0 g/L), K2HPO4 (2.5 g/L), and glucose (2.5 g/L). A 500 ml aliquot of bacterial inoculum was added to the wells of a 24-well polystyrene plate that contained 500 µL of TSB broth in BTO solution (200 µg/ml). In the control group, 500 µL of bacterial inoculum was combined with 500 µL of TSB broth with BTO. The BTO + 2 W group was subjected to ultrasound stimulation at an intensity of 2 W cm− 2. The optical density at a wavelength of 600 nm (OD600 nm) was measured at 4-hour intervals for a duration of 24 h.

Statistical analysis

In all the experiments, at least three replicates were included, and data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). We analyzed the differences between the two groups by independent sample t-test. Differences among multiple groups were examined using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey post-test. Statistical significance was indicated as P < 0.05 and expressed as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.
