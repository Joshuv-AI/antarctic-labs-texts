Evidence of Decreasing Prenatal Sex-Selection Practice in a Context of Liberal Abortion Rights
Authors: Sylvie Dubuc
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSon preference and prenatal sex-selection against females (PSS) among British Asian communities, raised considerable media attention, leading to parliamentary debates on abortion laws in Britain in 2015. PSS among India-born mothers in the 1990s was indirectly evidenced in past UK study by analysing sex-ratios at birth. However, we lack reliable quantitative evidence to document the practice in Britain since 2005 and regulations remained unchanged, offering a unique opportunity to test the need (or not) for abortion regulation to curtail PSS practice. Using annual sex ratio at birth counts from 1969 to 2018 and applying novel indicators, I found that sex-selection prevalence among India-born mothers is reduced in recent years, from its peak at about 4% in the 1990–2005 period. This decline is independent of specific legislation on sex-selective abortion and suggests a weakening of son preference. A reduction in prenatal sex-selection prevalence in Britain, shows that curbing the practice does not request stricter (sex-selection) abortion law. The findings support policy approaches addressing root-causes of gender preference to reduce PSS, while preserving current abortion rights.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11113-025-09974-y.Keywords: Gender, Prenatal sex-selection, Reproductive health policy, Son preference, Sex-ratio at birth

Introduction and Background

A strong preference for having at least one son and the associated practice of prenatal sex-selection against females (PSS) are well documented in South and East Asia, typically evidenced by the masculinization of the sex-ratio at birth (Guilmoto, 2015; UNFPA, 2014). PSS has also been evidenced among Asian diasporas, notably in the United Kingdom (Dubuc & Coleman, 2007), United States (Almond & Edlund, 2008; Howell et al., 2018) and Canada (Almond et al., 2013). Regulation of prenatal sex-determination technology and/or abortion has been a common policy response to PSS. For instance, in India, the 1994 Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PNDT) Act, prohibit prenatal diagnostic techniques for fetal sex-determination to prevent sex-selective abortion. Similar policies to the Indian PNDT Act, and its 2003 amendment,1 have been adopted in countries where PSS is most prevalent and well documented. In addition, from 2012, bans on sex-selective abortion has been proliferating across US states, before the recent removal of national broader abortion rights protection by the supreme court in 2022. Sex-selective abortion ban might be seen as a stepping stone in a process of fragilization of abortion rights, while its effect on curbing PSS remains unclear (Unnithan & Dubuc, 2017). The rigorous enforcement of the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act 2003 in a few Indian districts may explain local concomitant observed reduction in sex-ratios bias (Rahm, 2020). However, the implementation of such ban remains challenging and controversial (WHO, 2011), and, in countries where sex-selection against female is documented, the causal link between sex-selection ban and a reduction of the practice remains very uncertain and unconvincing (Rahm, 2020; UNFPA, 2014).

The impact of policy restricting prenatal sex-selection, and sex-selective abortion in particular, remains unclear since it generally cannot be disentangled from other potential factors influencing PSS. The United Kingdom, where no such PSS ban is in place, offers a unique context and opportunity to clarify this question and inform reproductive policy. PSS in the United Kingdom has gained increasing public attention since 2011, following a series of media investigations and coverage. Prominently in the public and political space, the debate, largely driven by opponents of abortion rights, rapidly shifted to the lawfulness of sex-selective abortion; In November 2014 conservative MP Fiona Bruce tabled an amendment to section 5 of the Serious Crime Bill, to ban sex-selective abortions, raising high controversy (Unnithan & Dubuc, 2017). In particular, opponents to the bill voiced concern about the associated risks of limiting women’s access to safe abortion services and fueling ethnic profiling in practice (Unnithan & Dubuc, 2017). In contrast to the United States where similar concerns have been voiced, the UK Parliament rejected the bill and called for further research on current trend in PSS in the United Kingdom (Serious Crime Act, 2015). This paper aims to answer this call.

In prior research, a significant bias in the sex-ratio at birth (SRB) among children of India born mothers residing in England and Wales, evidenced PSS over the period 1990–2005 (Dubuc & Coleman, 2007). The lack of robust data on PSS in the more recent period led to claims of widespread practice of sex-selection (see Unnithan & Dubuc, 2017 for details). Drawing on exhaustive birth records, the paper aims to clarify the evidence of PSS motivated by son preference in the United Kingdom, providing new quantitative updated evidence to test and inform policy.

Son preference is the valuation of sons over daughters and is often attributed to combined economic and social factors, linked to classical patriarchal cultural norms (UNFPA, 2014). Demographic manifestations of son preference include excess girls’ mortality (Alkema et al., 2014; Sen, 2010), gender-based fertility stopping behavior, affecting the probability of having another child based on the gender composition of born children within families and resulting in more boys at last birth (Basu & De Jong, 2010; Yamaguchi, 1989). In fertility transitioning countries where son preference prevails, parents pursuing childbearing at higher parity in quest for a son would likely slow fertility reduction while not influencing the overall sex ratio at birth (SRB). Since the 1980s and the diffusion of prenatal sex-determination techniques, PSS, typically at last birth, allows to reconcile gender composition and smaller family size ideals. As a result, biased overall SRB, associated with a distortion especially at higher birth orders, provide indirect evidence for the use of PSS to ensure a son in families with only daughters already born (Croll, 2002; Dubuc, 2018). Biased SRB evidence PSS practice in a population (Das Gupta & Bhat, 1997), but provide an inaccurate measure for the prevalence of that practice, because the number of sex selective interventions weighs disproportionately in a smaller child cohort, increasing SRB bias, i.e. the ‘disproportionality effect’ evidenced by Dubuc and Sivia (2018). The SRB is also a poor indicator for the degree of son preference in a population because of the ‘fertility squeeze’ effect (Guilmoto, 2009), whereby the desire for fewer children adds additional pressure to sex select in order to have a son. Noteworthy, potential sex-selection for so-called ‘family balancing’ in gender composition would not result in biased SRB and cannot be evidenced quantitatively, potentially explaining that it has received much less academic and media attention.

Fetal sex-determination methods, and more recently sperm-sorting and pre-implementation techniques enable PSS. As a result, the regulation of prenatal and preconception sex-determination methods have been proposed as a mean to control PSS and is the focus of ongoing bio-ethical and policy debates (Bathia, 2018). Importantly, it remains unclear to which extent regulating reproductive health technology enabling sex-selection is efficient and a necessary condition to curb PSS. Speaking to this debate, the paper details recent trends in PSS practices based on sex-ratios at birth (SRB) from mothers in England and Wales and among the British South Asian groups in the United Kingdom, i.e., mothers born in India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, where son preference is documented and for which data are available.

Data and Methods

Our analysis builds on the methodology employed by Dubuc and Coleman (2007) to calculate SRB at population (and sub-population) level. Analyzing SRB bias allows evidencing PSS when systematically performed against one gender. The dataset used comprises exhaustive counts of birth from the civil registration service, provided by the Office for National Statistics, from the period 1969–2018.2 Births by sex and by parity of the mother for all women in England and Wales and by main country of birth of mothers (i.e. United Kingdom, India, Pakistan and Bangladesh) are used to calculate sex ratios at birth. For each birth record, birth order as well as the country of birth of the mother are available. However the sex of previous siblings is not, limiting the investigation to simple birth order effects. Using the exhaustive annual counts of births for India-born women, overall SRB and SRB by birth order are calculated for successive periods, avoiding inter-annual variations linked to small populations. Although annual SRB for small sub-population are fluctuating (Online supplement Figure S1), they allow to determine meaningful periods for analyzing potential changes in SRB. Births counts by periods and birth order are shown in the Online Supplement Table S1. Average SRB for successive periods are then calculated and analyzed.

Since changes in SRB alone are difficult to interpret in term of trends in sex-selection due to the disproportional effect of fertility change at aggregated population levels, the indicator of PSS prevalence (PSSφ) proposed by Dubuc and Sivia (2018) is calculated to estimate the scale of PSS practice.3 PSSφ estimates the proportion of couples/parents using sex-selection that reconcile SRB results and average fertility levels in a given population. Calculated SRB and published total fertility estimates (TFR) of Indian immigrant women in the United Kingdom4 are then used to calculate PSSφ, the sex-selection prevalence ratio, among India-born mothers.

Following best practice, a population specific historical baseline is used to provide a PSS-free SRB benchmark (Chao et al., 2019), necessary to measure SRB bias and estimate PSSφ. This is an important methodological point for robust evidence. 105 (boys per 100 girls), approximating the world population SRB prior to the development of PSS enabling technology has been commonly used as benchmark. Recently, and acknowledging some variation across regions and populations, experts have used long-term past trends in SRB (i.e. prior to the diffusion of PSS enabling technologies in the 1980s/1990s) to estimate the PSS-free baseline SRB of specific countries and populations instead (Chao et al., 2019). Similarly, Dubuc and Coleman (2007) observed a SRB of around 104 (boys per 100 girls) for England and Wales’s mothers born in India, consistently over 20 years, from 1969 to the late 1980s—and prior to the 4 points rise in the SRB they observed in the 1990s—making 104 the most probable PSS-free SRB for this population. A similar baseline at 104 (or below) was also measured for other British South Asian groups (Dubuc & Coleman, 2007; Online Supplement Figure S1). However, some SRB fluctuations can be observed on long-term trends; they are low amplitude variations, typically ranging from 0.1 to below 1 point of SRB (per 100) (Jacobson et al. 1999).5 For instance, the overall SRB in England and Wales reduced from 106.1 between 1969 and 1979 to 105.2 thereafter (Online Supplement Fig. S1). Therefore, PSSφ is calculated using the PSS-free ‘historical’ baseline of 104 (Scenario A) as well as an alternative baseline of 105 males per 100 female births (Scenario B) allowing for maximum amplitude variation unrelated to PSS. Given the above, there is little rational to test a higher SRB benchmark for India-born mothers in England and Wales, which would likely strongly underestimate PSSφ. However, as a control check and to minimize the risk of over-estimation of potential PSS, scenario B allows for PSS-free temporal variation while assuming that the SRB of specific sub-population could fluctuate in opposite direction to the overall population.

Results

While the SRB trend for all women in England and Wales has remained relatively flat at around 105.4 males per 100 females, in line with previously observed values (Dubuc & Coleman, 2007; Online Supplement Figure S1), the SRB trend for women born in India shows 3 successive periods of striking variations (Fig. 1). In contrast, the results didn’t show any significant distortion of the SRB (overall and at higher orders) for Pakistan-born and Bangladesh-born women6 over the entire period (Online Supplement Figure S1). For India-born women, we observe the already identified (Dubuc & Coleman, 2007) strong pattern of 4 points increase in the SRB, on average over 1990–2005, compared to an annual SRB consistently at around 104 male births per 100 female births over the 1969–1989 period (Fig. 1; Online Supplement Figure S1). Since 2005 however, the SRB bias appears to have plateaued, and decreased (Online Supplement Figure S1), showing the premise of a reversal (Fig. 1).

Average sex-ratios at birth for India-born mothers, in 1969–1989, 1990–2005 and 2006–2018. Note: Average period sex ratio are represented with standard error (SE). Sources: Author’s calculation based on the Office for National Statistics exhaustive annual birth counts (from birth registrar)

Over 2006–2018, some distortion of the SRB among India-born mothers in England and Wales remains, due to a persistent distortion at third and higher births, observed since the early 1990s (Fig. 1), while no significant bias appears at first and second births. SRB reduction at birth order three and over remains modest, from over 113 boys per 100 girls in 1990–20057 to just below 112 on average over 2006–2018. However, the bias in the overall SRB halved in the recent period, from 4 to 2 points departure from pre-1990 PSS-free baseline SRB (Fig. 1; Online Supplement Figure S2) and despite the persistent bias at higher birth orders. This apparent contradiction is largely explained by the sharp reduction of the proportion of births at higher order linked to the fertility reduction of India-born mothers in the recent period (Fig. 2). Indeed, the proportion of births at order 3 and over nearly halved since 1990–2005, from over 23% to 13% of all births in the most recent period.

Proportion of births by birth order for India-born mothers, 1969–2018. Sources: Author’s calculation based on the Office for National Statistics annual birth counts for married women (accounting for 98% of all births)

Finally, using the method described in Dubuc and Sivia (2018), it was possible to estimate sex-selection prevalence, PSSφ. Based on the historical PSS-free SRB of 104 evidenced empirically, PSSφ remained confined to a maximum of just above 4% of India-born mothers at the peak period of the practice (1990–2005). PSSφ reduced in the most recent period (2006–2018) to 2.3% (Scenario A, Fig. 3). With the alternative conservative 105 PSS-free benchmark allowing for hypothetical temporal variation non-related to sex-selection (Scenario B, Fig. 3), PSSφ is, as expected, even lower at around 1%. Discussion and Conclusion

The findings indicate that PSS is a very marginal practice and increasingly so since 2005. Since Indian immigrant families living in the United Kingdom have about two children on average and approximately 1 in 4 families with 2 children would not have a son ‘by chance’ the relatively low proportion of couples using PSS is perhaps expected. However, the results suggest that PSS is becoming increasingly rare in the United Kingdom, with sex-selection mostly confined to a very small minority of British Indian families with 3 children and over, whose proportion is rapidly shrinking.

Son preference remains likely more widespread than PSSφ, since it is quite unlikely that only parents with at least two daughters, but no son, would have a preference for son. Although PSSφ does not express the level of gender preference, which is not readily measurable, the reduction of the practice since 2005 is an important new finding. This reduction speaks to a relative change in son preference, because PSS does not manifest at second/lower birth order in the recent period, despite a continuous fertility reduction among immigrant women from India, associated with the rarefaction of higher birth orders (Fig. 2). This is contrary to the theoretical expectation of the fertility squeeze effect, where preference for smaller family should trigger and/or exacerbate the practice of PSS at lower birth orders (Guilmoto, 2009). Instead, the results suggest a weakening of son preference with more families stopping childbearing after two daughters.

Potential prenatal sex-selection aimed at so-called ‘gender family balancing’ in the United Kingdom are out of the scope of this study since they cannot manifest in distorted sex-ratio; consequently, they cannot be ruled out. However, the focus of this study provides important novel quantitative evidence for reproductive policy. Indeed, the clear decrease in PSSφ, and near disappearance of the practice, despite fertility reduction among British Indian mothers suggests that increasingly more parents with only daughters value a small family above having a son, and sonless families are becoming better socially accepted. This finding, in the British institutional and legislative landscape, shows that a ban on prenatal sex-selection is not necessary to curb PSS practice; Instead, a weakening of its root-cause, gender preference, is likely effective on its own to do so.

The results support the decision taken by the UK Parliament in 2015 to reject the proposed ban on sex selection as an unnecessary regulation to curb PSS. These new findings prompt us to revisit policy responses to PSS beyond the United Kingdom, notably questioning the justification and impact of bans on sex-selective abortion passed in many US States since 2012 while available quantitative evidence of PSS suggested its marginal practice within the US population (i.e. comparable to the UK context). One may argue that such policy recommendation cannot be readily transferred to countries like India or China for instance where son preference rooted in classical patriarchy is more prevalent across the general population. However, there is an increasingly strong consensus among national and international policy makers that addressing the root-causes of son preference is key to curb PSS (Chung & Das Gupta, 2007; WHO, 2011).

The results from the United Kingdom case study provide tangible evidence in favor of alternative policy options to a sex-selection ban, that are encouraging the weakening of son preference, targeting some of the structural factors and root-causes of gender representations and unequal valuations. Supporting community-based women’s organization, sensitive to context-specific social complexities, in challenging the underlying causes of PSS practice, is a promising policy option to help accelerating ongoing trends of PSS reduction while protecting women’s reproductive rights and well-being.
