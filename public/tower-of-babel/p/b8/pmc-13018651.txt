IFFO1 inhibits breast cancer by coordinating mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis via the LaminA/C-PGC1α axis
Authors: Huaxia Cai, Jianjun He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accumulating evidence indicates that mitochondrial dynamics are closely linked to the biological behaviors of tumor cells, with increased mitochondrial fission being recognized as a phenotype that promotes tumor growth. Although intermediate filament family orphan 1 (IFFO1) has been implicated in mitochondrial dynamics, its specific role and molecular mechanisms in regulating mitochondrial fission during breast cancer (BC) progression remain unclear. In this study, analysis of tumor and adjacent normal tissues from 30 BC patients revealed significant downregulation of IFFO1 in tumor tissues, and low IFFO1 expression predicted poor prognosis in patients. In vitro experiments demonstrated that IFFO1 overexpression suppressed the proliferation, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of BC cells by inhibiting mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis. Mechanistically, IFFO1 interacts with LaminA/C to promote its expression, which subsequently upregulates PGC1α, thereby suppressing mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis in BC cells. Consistent with this mechanism, both LaminA/C and PGC1α were downregulated in BC tissues. Silencing LMNA reversed the inhibitory effects of IFFO1 overexpression on mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis, whereas overexpression of PGC1α effectively counteracted the consequences of LMNA knockdown. In vivo studies confirmed that upregulation of IFFO1 inhibited tumor growth in xenograft models and reduced lung metastasis in a lung metastasis mouse model. These findings underscore the significance of the IFFO1/LaminA/C/PGC1α pathway as a key regulator of mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis during BC progression and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for breast cancer.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a highly heterogeneous malignant tumor commonly found in women [1, 2]. It is primarily classified into four molecular subtypes: Luminal A, Luminal B, HER-2 positive, basal like/triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), with Luminal A being the most prevalent [3]. Current treatment strategies mainly rely on surgery, radiotherapy, targeted drugs, and endocrine therapy. However, some targeted agents fail to significantly improve progression-free survival, and their dosing is often limited by toxicity. Long-term use can also lead to drug resistance, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes for BC patients [4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel potential targets for BC treatment and to elucidate their underlying mechanisms of action.

Mitochondria are highly dynamic, maternally inherited cytoplasmic organelles that play essential roles in cellular oxidative phosphorylation, metabolic regulation, and calcium homeostasis [5]. These organelles undergo continuous cycles of fission and fusion to maintain their structural integrity and functional balance. Mitochondrial fission involves the recruitment of dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) from the cytoplasm to the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it interacts with adaptor proteins such as Fis1, MFF, MiD51, or MiD49. DRP1 subsequently oligomerizes and constricts mitochondria, leading to their division [6, 7]. In contrast, mitochondrial fusion is mediated by mitofusin 1 and 2 (Mfn1/2) and optic atrophy 1 (Opa1), serving as a compensatory mechanism to reduce oxidative stress and mitigate mutational burden [8]. Mitochondrial dysregulation plays a crucial role in cancer cell homeostasis [9, 10]. Accumulating evidence suggests that excessive mitochondrial fission is closely associated with tumorigenesis, malignant progression, metastasis, and impaired immune surveillance [11, 12]. Studies report that mitochondrial DNA defects and functional impairments are common in various cancers, with increased mitochondrial fission representing a pro-tumorigenic phenotype [13, 14].

Tumor lipid metabolic reprogramming plays an indispensable role in all aspects of tumorigenesis. Its distinctive feature is the coexistence of lipogenesis and lipolysis, which leads to de novo lipid synthesis accompanied by enhanced fatty acid oxidation [15]. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism promotes malignant transformation and resistance to anticancer drugs in various cancers, including BC [16, 17]. Key metabolic enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis, such as fatty acid synthase (FASN) and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1), have been reported to play critical roles in the proliferation, metastasis, and invasion of BC cells [18]. Importantly, evidence suggests that mitochondrial fission promotes fatty acid synthesis during cancer progression [19]. Targeting the mitochondrial fission‑fatty acid synthesis axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for BC.

Intermediate filament family orphan 1 (IFFO1) is a member of the intermediate filament family, which comprises fundamental structural components of the cytoskeleton and nuclear lamina, primarily localized in the nucleus, cytoskeleton, and cytoplasm [20]. It has been reported that IFFO1 interacts with X-ray repair cross-complementing protein 4 (XRCC4) to stabilize broken DNA ends and suppress chromosomal translocations. Moreover, IFFO1 interacts with laminin A/C to form an internal nuclear skeleton [21]. Growing evidence suggests that the expression level of IFFO1 is associated with tumor progression and immune infiltration [20, 22, 23]. Nevertheless, the specific role and regulatory mechanisms of IFFO1 in BC progression and metastasis remain to be elucidated.

The purpose of this study was to explore the role and regulatory mechanism of IFFO1 in BC. Our findings revealed that IFFO1 is downregulated in both BC patient tumor tissues and cell lines. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that IFFO1 inhibits fatty acid synthesis by suppressing Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission through the LaminA/C/PGC1α pathway, thereby restraining tumor growth and metastasis in BC. In summary, this study elucidates a novel regulatory mechanism of mitochondrial fission-mediated fatty acid synthesis in BC cells, offering a promising perspective for the development of anti-tumor therapies against BC.

Materials and methods

BC patient collection

A total of 30 BC patients, aged 45–70 years, were enrolled from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The cohort included 5 patients with stage I disease (tumor diameter ≤ 2 cm and no lymph node enlargement), 13 with stage II (tumor diameter > 2 cm but ≤5 cm, with breast mass or axillary lymph node enlargement), 8 with stage III (presence of axillary lymph node metastasis or chest wall/skin infiltration), and 4 with stage IV (distant metastasis). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of BC confirmed by clinicopathological examination; (2) availability of complete clinical and follow-up data; (3) no prior anticancer treatment before surgery; and (4) no history of other malignancies. The demographic information, clinical and follow-up data of each patient were collected by trained staff or clinical specialists. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell purchase and culture

ZR-75-1 (TCHu126), MCF-7 (TCHu74), MDA-MB-453 (TCHu233), SK-BR-3 (TCHu225), and MDA-MB-231 (TCHu227) cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The MCF-10A cell line (SNL-225) was purchased from Shangen Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). All cell lines were routinely authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using a MycoAlert Mycoplasma detection set (Lonza, Allendale, NJ). Mycoplasma testing was performed quarterly, and the most recent test confirmed the absence of contamination throughout the course of this study. ZR-75-1 and MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium containing 10% FBS. MCF-7 cells were maintained in MCF7 complete medium (SCSP-669S). MDA-MB-453 cells were cultured in L-15 complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS (SCSP-675). SK-BR-3 cells were grown in McCOY’s 5A complete medium containing 10% FBS (MD10). MCF-10A cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS, 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor, 0.5 μg/mL Hydrocortisone, 10 μg/mL Insulin, 1% non-essential amino acids. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

The pcDNA3.1-IFFO1 and pcDNA3.1-PGC1α plasmids, as well as the pcDNA3.1 empty vector, were purchased from HANBIO (Shanghai, China). Lentivirus-mediated IFFO1 overexpression vector (LV-IFFO1) and LMNA interference vector (LV-sh-IFFO1) were synthesized and packaged by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). BC cells were transfected with pcDNA-IFFO1, pcDNA-PGC1α, and their negative controls using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). For lentiviral infection, lentiviruses at an MOI of 20 were added to the cell culture together with 8 μg/mL Polybrene (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and ViralPlus Transduction Enhancer (ABM, Vancouver, Canada). After 16 h of infection, the cells were washed with 1× PBS and cultured in fresh medium.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Dalian). RT-qPCR was performed using the SYBR® green PCR MasterMix kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) on an ABI 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). GAPDH was used as an internal reference, and the relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are as follows: IFFO1: F-5′-CGG AGG TGG GAA GAG GAG TA-3′, R-5′-TGG GAC CAG CTT CTT CTG GA-3′; LMNA: F-5′-ACT CTG AGG GCT GCA ATA CC-3′, R-5′-GTC AAT CTC CAC CAG TCG GG-3′; PGC1α: F-5′-CGC TCA TTA TCT CGC CCT CT-3′, R-5′-TCA CTG CAC CAC TTG AGT CC-3′.

Western blot assay

Total protein was extracted from BC cell lines and mouse tumor tissues using RIPA Lysis and extraction buffer (Beyotime). Nuclear proteins and mitochondrial membrane proteins were extracted using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher, #78833) and the Mitochondrial Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (Solarbio, #EX2120), respectively. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h. It was then incubated overnight 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody. The primary antibodies used in this experiment were: IFFO1 (1:500, ab242130, Abcam, UK), LaminA/C (1500, ab314500, Abcam), PGC1α (1.5 µg/mL, ab191838, Abcam), Histone H3 (1:500, ab308373, Abcam), GAPDH (1:1000, ab181602, Abcam), Drp1 (1:500, ab184247, Abcam), Fis1 (1:1000, ab156865, Abcam), Mfn1 (1.5 µg/mL, ab191853, Abcam), Mfn2 (1: 1000, ab205236, Abcam), Tom20 (1:500, ab186735, Abcam), SREBP-1 (1:500, ab313881, Abcam), FSAN (1:500, ab228975, Abcam), SCD1 (1:500, ab236868, Abcam), AK1 (1:500, ab138504, Abcam), and ACOLY (1:500, ab156576, Abcam). Finally, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software.

CCK-8 assay

The BC cells (4 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with 10 μl of CCK-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) reagent after 24, 48, and 72 h of cell culture. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, Burlington, VT, USA).

Cell proliferation assay

BC cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well). After culture for 24 h, cells were incubated with medium containing EdU for 2 h. Subsequently, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution at room temperature for 30 min, followed by incubation with glycine solution (2 mg/mL) on a decolorizing shaker for 5 min. Apollo staining reaction solution was then added, and the cells were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Finally, the staining results were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Transwell assay

BC cells were resuspended in serum-free DMEM medium, and a cell suspension containing 5 × 105 cells was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell insert. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, and the cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated to the lower side of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% toluidine blue (Sigma-Aldrich). The stained cells were counted under an inverted light microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) in five randomly selected fields of view.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

After transfection, BC cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The lysate supernatant was then incubated with protein A/G magnetic beads (Novex, Oslo, Norway), anti-IFFO1 antibody, and anti-LaminA/C antibody at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the beads were washed with lysis buffer and resuspended in SDS-PAGE loading buffer. Co-immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and analyzed by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence assay

BC cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies against Vimentin (1:100, ab92547, Abcam) and E-cadherin (1:100, ab231303, Abcam) at 4 °C for 24 h, followed by incubation with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 2 h. For co-localization analysis, BC cells were transfected with the DsRed2-TOM20 expression plasmid, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and blocking as described above. The cells were subsequently incubated with anti-Drp1 antibody (1:100, ab184247, Abcam) and corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody to evaluate co-localization between TOM20 and Drp1. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software.

MitoTracker Red FM fluorescence staining

BC cells grown on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and subsequently stained with MitoTracker Red FM dye to label mitochondria. After staining, the samples were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. Mitochondrial morphology in the cell climbing specimens was visualized using confocal fluorescence microscopy. Acquired images were analyzed with ImageJ software by converting fluorescence images into binary format to measure mitochondrial length.

Measurement of FASN, ATP, TG, FFA, and TC

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, FASN activity and the levels of ATP, Triglyceride (TG), Free Fatty Acid (FFA), and Total Cholesterol (TC) were measured using the following commercial kits: FASN Assay Kit (spectrophotometric method; ml106287, Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), ATP Content Assay Kit (WST-1 method; ml098821), TG Content Assay Kit (micro-method; ml076637), FFA Content Assay Kit (spectrophotometric method; ml092767), and TC Assay Kit (COD-PAP dual-reagent colorimetric method; ml076637).

Mitochondrial DNA quantification

Total DNA and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) were isolated from treated BC cells using the EasyPure® Genomic DNA Kit (EE101-11; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and the Mitochondrial DNA Extraction Kit (ab65321; Abcam), respectively. Subsequently, mtDNA was analyzed with MT-CO2 (mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase II)-specific primers by using RT-qPCR assay.

Nile red staining

BC cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 70% confluence. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min, the cells were stained with Nile red dye (MedChemExpress, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. After staining, the excess dye was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Lipid deposition was observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Oil red O staining

Lipid droplet deposition in BC cells was assessed by Oil Red O staining. The cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min and washed three times with PBS. The samples were then immersed in 60% isopropanol for 5 min and stained with freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at room temperature for 15 min. Excess stain was removed by briefly rinsing with 60% isopropanol until the background became clear. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, and images were acquired under a light microscope.

Xenograft tumor model

The sample size for animal experiments was set at n = 10 per group. This number was chosen based on: (1) the principle of reduction (3Rs) in animal ethics; (2) common practice and sufficient power demonstrated in established models of BC xenograft tumor in prior studies [24, 25].

A total of 40 BALB/c female nude mice (8 weeks old, 20–22 g body weight) were purchased from the Institute of Medical Laboratory Animals, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). The mice were acclimatized for 1 week with free access to food and water before the experiment. Criteria for inclusion in the final analysis were defined before the experiment began. All mice that received tumor cell injections were intended to be included. Exclusion criteria were pre-specified as follows: mice would be excluded only in cases of (1) premature death unrelated to tumor burden or (2) failure of tumor engraftment. Throughout this study, all mice successfully developed tumors and completed the experimental protocol; therefore, no animals were excluded from the analyses presented.

A total of 40 mice were randomly assigned to two cohorts for subcutaneous injection and tail vein injection, respectively. For the subcutaneous injection experiment, the corresponding cohort of 20 mice was further randomly divided into two groups. Stably IFFO1-overexpressing MCF-7 cells or negative control cells (1 × 107 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the right dorsal flank of the respective two groups of mice. After 28 days of tumor growth, the mice were euthanized, and the tumor tissues were excised, weighed, and measured for volume. The personnel responsible for monitoring mouse health, measuring tumor size with digital calipers, and recording weights were blinded to the group assignments. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University.

Histological staining

Tumor tissue was embedded in OCT compound and sectioned into 5–10 μm thick frozen sections, which were then mounted onto glass slides. The tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed three times with PBS. For Nile Red staining, the working solution was applied to cover the sections and incubated at room temperature protected from light for 10 min. Nuclei were then counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. For Oil Red O staining, sections were first immersed in 60% isopropanol for 1 min, followed by incubation in Oil Red O working solution in the dark for 10 min. Differentiation was performed quickly with 60% isopropanol until the background became clear. After rinsing under running water, nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min. All stained sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. Tissue embedding, sectioning, staining, and subsequent image evaluation were all performed by technicians who were blinded to the experimental design.

Lung metastasis model

The corresponding cohort of 20 mice was further randomly divided into two groups. MDA-MB-231 cells infected with LV-NC or LV-IFFO1 (1 × 107 cells per mouse) were injected into the respective two groups of mice via the tail vein. Twenty-eight days after injection, the mice were euthanized, and lung tissues were collected and processed into paraffin sections. Subsequently, Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was then performed on the lung sections to quantify metastatic foci. Tissue embedding, sectioning, staining, and subsequent image evaluation were all performed by technicians who were blinded to the experimental design.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 was used for data analysis. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data distribution normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene test, respectively. Based on the data normal distribution and homogeneity of variances, differences between groups were compared using Student’s t test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey’s post hoc test (for multiple groups). P value less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Xi’an Jiaotong University. All human parts adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. All animal experiments were in accordance with the guide for the care and use of laboratory animals established by United States National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD, USA).

Results

IFFO1 expression is downregulated in both BC tumor tissues and cell lines

The analysis revealed that IFFO1 was significantly downregulated in BC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A). Consistently, in our cohort of 30 BC patient samples, IFFO1 expression was lower in tumor tissues than in matched normal adjacent tissues (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, IFFO1 expression progressively decreased with advancing tumor stage (Fig. 1C). Survival analysis indicated that patients with higher IFFO1 expression had a more favorable prognosis (Fig. 1D). We next examined IFFO1 expression in BC cell lines and human normal breast epithelial cells. IFFO1 inhibits mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis in BC cells

Intermediate filament family proteins have been reported to be closely associated with mitochondrial dynamics and the regulation of fatty acid metabolism [26–29]. Here, we investigated the role of IFFO1 in mitochondrial fission and fusion and fatty acid metabolism. Since Drp1, Fis1, Mfn1, and Mfn2 are key regulators of mitochondrial fission and fusion, we first examined their expression. Our results showed that overexpression of IFFO1 inhibited the expression of Drp1 and Fis1, while promoting the expression of Mfn1 and Mfn2 in BC cells (Fig. 3A–C and Fig. S2A, B). MitoTracker staining revealed that mitochondria in BC cells appeared fragmented, with a higher proportion of short mitochondria and fewer elongated mitochondria. Overexpression of IFFO1 significantly increased the number of elongated mitochondria and reduced the number of fragmented mitochondria (Fig. 3D–F and Fig. S2C, D). Previous studies have indicated that mitochondrial fission can induce mtDNA stress and ATP accumulation [11, 30]. Consistent with this, we found that IFFO1 overexpression led to a reduction in the elevated levels of mtDNA and ATP in BC cells (Fig. 3G, H and Fig. S2E, F). Overexpression of Drp1 increases fatty acid synthesis in BC cells

Previous studies have reported that activation of mitochondrial fission promotes de novo fatty acid synthesis in various cancer cells, including liver cancer, colon cancer, and pancreatic cancer [13, 19, 31]. Here, we modulated mitochondrial fission by silencing or overexpressing Drp1 in BC cells to assess its impact on lipid metabolism. CCK-8 assays revealed that Drp1 silencing suppressed BC cell viability, whereas Drp1 overexpression enhanced it (Fig. 5A, B). Silencing Drp1 downregulated the expression of Drp1 and Fis1 while upregulating Mfn1 and Mfn2; conversely, Drp1 overexpression increased Drp1 and Fis1 levels and reduced Mfn1 and Mfn2 expression (Fig. 5C–E). Drp1 silencing significantly decreased mtDNA content, whereas Drp1 overexpression increased it (Fig. 5F). We further observed that knockdown of Drp1 markedly attenuated fatty acid synthesis, as evidenced by reduced expression of key lipogenic proteins (SREBP‑1, FASN, SCD1, AK1, and ACLY), lower FASN activity, decreased levels of FFA and TG, and diminished neutral lipid content (Fig. 5G–M). Discussion

BC is one of the most common malignant tumors with extremely high incidence rate among women in the world [33]. The primary factors contributing to the low survival rates of BC patients are postoperative recurrence, distant metastasis, and drug resistance [34]. As a novel tumor inhibitor, IFFO1 plays a significant role in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and the development of drug resistance [20, 35]. It was reported that IFFO1 can serve as a potential marker for distinguishing cell-free tumor DNA in the malignant ascites of ovarian cancer [36]. In endometrial cancer, IFFO1 expression is generally low, and its high expression is associated with a more favorable patient prognosis [37]. Furthermore, IFFO1 has been identified as a key biomarker in liver cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and pituitary tumors [23, 38, 39]. However, the specific role and underlying mechanisms of IFFO1 in BC remain unclear and warrant further investigation. In this study, bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA database revealed that IFFO1 expression was significantly lower in BC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues. This finding was further validated in our cohort of 30 patient-derived samples, where tumor tissues consistently exhibited downregulated IFFO1 expression relative to matched paracancerous tissues. Notably, IFFO1 expression showed a progressive decrease with advancing tumor stage. Correspondingly, survival analysis demonstrated that lower IFFO1 expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival, reinforcing its potential role as a tumor suppressor and an independent prognostic marker in BC. Consistently, IFFO1 was downregulated in BC cell lines relative to normal mammary epithelial cells. Overexpression of IFFO1 inhibited BC cell proliferation and migration. Metastasis of BC to vital organs such as bone, lung, liver, and brain is a major cause of cancer‑related mortality [40]. The EMT process plays an important role in BC metastasis [41]. We found that IFFO1 overexpression suppressed the EMT process in BC cells. Importantly, upregulation of IFFO1 inhibited tumor growth in mouse xenograft models and reduced lung metastasis in vivo. Our findings establish IFFO1 as a tumor suppressor in BC. Importantly, the broad tumor-suppressive effects observed across luminal (MCF-7), HER2-positive (SK-BR-3), and triple-negative (MDA-MB-231) subtypes suggest that therapeutically restoring IFFO1 function could benefit a wide spectrum of patients. The clinical relevance is further supported by IFFO1’s central role in regulating EMT, mitochondrial fission, and metabolic reprogramming, key drivers of tumor progression. Thus, targeting IFFO1 represents a promising multi-mechanistic strategy. Future efforts to develop IFFO1-targeted agents, alone or in combination with existing therapies, may offer new avenues for treatment, particularly in aggressive or therapy-resistant BCs.

Mitochondrial dynamics is a crucial regulator of mitochondrial quality control, playing a vital role in maintaining cellular metabolism and homeostasis [10]. Within mitochondrial dynamics, the fission/fusion process represents a finely tuned balance that is essential for proper mitochondrial function, and disruption of this equilibrium is closely associated with various human diseases [42]. Increasing evidence indicates that enhanced mitochondrial fission contributes to metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells, thereby promoting excessive proliferation, migration, and invasion [43, 44]. Drp1, a cytosolic GTPase, initiates mitochondrial fission upon its recruitment to the outer mitochondrial membrane. Intriguingly, the balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion exhibits a dual role in cancer progression. On one hand, mitochondrial fission can facilitate cancer cell proliferation, migration, and drug resistance. On the other hand, excessive fission may induce apoptosis in cancer cells [45]. For instance, in metastatic BC, inhibition of Drp1 can restore sensitivity to cisplatin and prevent the widespread reactivation of cancer cells [46]. Drp1 inhibitors prevent Drp1 localization to mitochondria, suppress mitochondrial fission and cell proliferation in BC cells, and ultimately inhibit BC metastasis [47]. Furthermore, promoting mitochondrial fission through chemical or genetic approaches has been shown to impede the migration, invasion, and metastasis of TNBC cells [48]. Our results indicated that IFFO1 overexpression suppressed mitochondrial fission and promoted mitochondrial fusion in BC cells. Specifically, IFFO1 overexpression downregulated the expression of Drp1 and Fis1 while upregulating the expression of Mfn1 and Mfn2. It also increased the proportion of elongated mitochondria and impairs the recruitment of Drp1 to the mitochondrial membrane. Collectively, these findings suggest that IFFO1 may inhibit the malignant progression of BC cells by suppressing mitochondrial fission.

Fatty acid metabolism is closely associated with cancer progression [49, 50]. Enhanced fatty acid biosynthesis is commonly observed in cancer cells [51], driven by upregulated expression of key metabolic enzymes such as ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), FASN, stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) [52]. For example, in BC, RGCC-mediated PLK1 activity activates AMPKα2 signaling to promote oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid synthesis, thereby driving lung metastasis [53]. Similarly, in TNBC, protein arginine methyltransferase-1 (PRMT1) enhances fatty acid synthesis through methylation of phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), contributing to paclitaxel resistance and tumor growth [54]. Mitochondrial fission also plays a key role in fatty acid metabolism and tumor progression. It promotes de novo fatty acid synthesis in liver cancer by activating FASN and ACC1, thereby supporting proliferation and metastasis [13]. Mitochondrial elongation factor 2 (MIEF2), a regulator of mitochondrial fission, enhances lipid synthesis to facilitate ovarian cancer growth [55]. Furthermore, Drp1-mediated fission is linked to lipid metabolic reprogramming in various cancers [56]. In cervical cancer, forkhead box K2 (FOXK2) interacts with mTOR to upregulate Drp1 and promote lipid metabolism, while in BC, DEAD-box helicase 3 X-linked (DDX3X) cooperates with CDK1 to phosphorylate Drp1 at S616, enhancing fission and fatty acid oxidation [57]. In this study, we demonstrated that IFFO1 overexpression suppressed fatty acid synthesis in BC cells by downregulating key fatty acid synthesis-related proteins, inhibiting FASN activity, and reducing the levels of FFA, TG, and TC. Additionally, IFFO1 overexpression decreased neutral lipid content and lipid droplet formation. Furthermore, by either silencing or overexpressing Drp1 to inhibit or activate mitochondrial fission in BC cells, we found that Drp1 overexpression significantly promoted fatty acid synthesis, whereas Drp1 silencing exerted the opposite effect. These results suggest that IFFO1 may inhibit BC progression by targeting the mitochondrial fission-fatty acid synthesis axis.

Lamin A/C, encoded by the LMNA gene, is a key component of nuclear lamina proteins situated beneath the inner nuclear membrane, where it interacts with chromatin. It plays a vital role in maintaining chromosomal architecture and regulating gene transcription [58]. Altered expression of Lamin A/C has been shown to influence the migration and survival of both human mesenchymal stem cells and cancer cells [59, 60]. Notably, a previous study have reported lower expression levels of Lamin A/C in BC tumor tissues [61]. Consistently, our results demonstrated downregulation of Lamin A/C expression in BC patient tumor tissues and in BC cell lines. A previous study indicated that during tumorigenesis, lamin A/C and IFFO1 interact to form a nuclear scaffold that prevents chromosomal translocation by anchoring broken DNA ends [21]. Using co-IP assays, we demonstrated that IFFO1 interacts with lamin A/C in BC cells, and that IFFO1 overexpression enhanced lamin A/C protein levels. Silencing LMNA reversed the inhibitory effects of IFFO1 on BC cell proliferation, migration, and EMT. Lamin A/C has been reported to regulate mitochondrial homeostasis and lipid metabolism. It modulates mitochondrial function through the PGC1α and nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT)-NAD⁺ pathway, thereby influencing cellular senescence [32]. In hepatocellular carcinoma cells, lamin A/C silencing activates AMPKα, leading to phosphorylation of ACC1, which suppresses lipid synthesis and promotes glycolysis [62]. Our data further showed that LMNA silencing also attenuated the inhibitory effects of IFFO1 on mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis in BC cells.

PGC-1α is a key transcriptional regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis. Its upregulation promotes the transcription of genes involved in mitochondrial generation, thereby enhancing mitochondrial quality and function [63]. A previous work revealed a direct binding of PGC-1α to the Drp1 promoter, leading to the transcriptional repression of Drp1 [64] and implying transcriptional-level regulation by PGC-1α. Additionally, PERM1 (PGC-1α and ESRR-induced regulator, muscle 1) in the heart interacts physically with PPARα and PGC-1α to participate in the transcriptional regulation of fatty acid oxidation-related genes [65], suggesting that PGC-1α is also involved in the regulation of fatty acid oxidation. Notably, a key transcriptional repressor of PGC-1α can bind to lamin A/C, and enhanced interaction between the two inhibits PGC-1α activity and promotes mitochondrial dysfunction [66]. This suggests a plausible regulatory mechanism wherein lamin A/C may sequester or stabilize this repressor, thereby amplifying its suppression of PGC-1α-mediated transcriptional programs. In this study, we found that PGC-1α expression is downregulated in both BC cells and tissues. Importantly, overexpression of PGC-1α counteracted the ability of LMNA silencing to reverse the inhibitory effects of IFFO1 overexpression on BC cell proliferation, migration, EMT progression, mitochondrial fission, and fatty acid synthesis. These results suggest that IFFO1 likely regulates the mitochondrial fission-fatty acid synthesis axis in BC cells via the Lamin A/C/PGC-1α pathway, thereby influencing malignant progression.

In summary, this study demonstrated that IFFO1 inhibited mitochondrial fission and fatty acid synthesis by regulating the LaminA/C/PGC1α pathway, thereby suppressing BC progression and lung metastasis. These findings provide novel insights for developing more effective therapeutic strategies against BC.
