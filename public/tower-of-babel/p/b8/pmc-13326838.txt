Sigma 1 Receptor Activation Coordinates Metabolic Stress Responses to Protect Retinal Vasculature in Ischemic Retinopathy
Authors: Jing Wang, Xiaowen Lu, Zhengyu Lu, Zhimin Xu, Sylvia B. Smith, Steven E. Brooks, Mitchell A. Watsky, Ruth B. Caldwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRetinopathy of prematurity (ROP)-associated vision loss is driven by pathological retinal angiogenesis. Current therapies suppress neovascularization but do not fully restore vascular integrity or prevent long-term visual deficit. Activation of the sigma-1 receptor (Sig1R) has been reported to confer neuroprotection, yet its role in retinal vascular protection remains largely unexplored. Here, we investigated whether Sig1R activation confers vascular protection in experimental ROP.MethodsThe oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse model was induced in wild-type and Sig1R−/− mice with or without systemic (+)-pentazocine ([+]-PTZ) administration to activate Sig1R. Retinal vascular pathology, barrier integrity, and avascular areas were evaluated by fluorescein angiography and retinal flatmount analysis. Molecular changes in metabolic, oxidative, and inflammatory pathways were assessed by immunostaining/blotting and ELISA assay.ResultsSig1R expression was reduced in OIR retinas, accompanied by decreased cullin-3 ubiquitin ligase (Cul3) and phosphorylated AMP-activated protein kinase (pAMPK) and increased pAkt and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), indicative of metabolic and endothelial stress. Activation of Sig1R with (+)-PTZ restored Sig1R, Cul3, and pAMPK levels, suppressed pAkt/eNOS signaling, reduced oxidative stress, and attenuated Müller glial activation. OIR-induced upregulation of phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (p-STAT3) and pro-angiogenic/inflammatory mediators, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), IL-6, TNF-α, macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1), and tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR), were markedly reduced by (+)-PTZ. Functionally, Sig1R activation improved retinal vascular barrier integrity, reduced arterial tortuosity and pathological neovascularization, and promoted revascularization of avascular retina. Importantly, (+)-PTZ treatment failed to confer any vascular benefit in Sig1R−/− mice, confirming that these vascular benefits are Sig1R dependent.ConclusionsSig1R activation preserves vascular integrity and suppresses pathological angiogenesis in OIR. Together with known neuroprotective effects, Sig1R represents a promising dual neurovascular therapeutic target for ROP.

Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is a leading cause of childhood blindness worldwide, characterized by pathological retinal neovascularization and persistent avascular areas due to disrupted angiogenic signaling during retinal development.1 In the United States, ROP affects approximately 14,000 to 16,000 infants each year, with 400 to 600 cases progressing to legal blindness. Current therapies, including anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injections and laser ablation, can reduce neovascularization but are limited by incomplete vascular repair, potential systemic side effects, and long-term impacts on visual function. Anti-VEGF intraocular injections show efficacy in severe, late-stage ROP, yet disease recurrence is common, repeated dosing is required, and prolonged VEGF inhibition carries the risk of retinal neurodegeneration,2 as well as local3 and systemic complications, such as stroke.4 These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel mechanism-based therapeutic targets that can restore vascular homeostasis while minimizing collateral damage.

Aberrant retinal angiogenesis in ROP is driven by metabolic stress, oxidative injury, and activation of Müller glia, which secrete VEGF and other pro-angiogenic and inflammatory mediators.5–9 Oxidative stress and glial activation also converge on the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway, amplifying VEGF expression and promoting pathological neovascularization.10,11 Despite extensive characterization of downstream angiogenic mediators, upstream modulators that integrate metabolic, redox, and inflammatory signals to limit aberrant vascular growth and promote vascular normalization remain poorly defined.

Sigma-1 receptor (Sig1R), is an unique 25.3-kD endoplasmic reticulum-resident chaperone,12 that regulates cellular stress responses, including oxidative and inflammatory pathways,13 and has established neuroprotective effects in retinal and central nervous system models.14–17 Our in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that Sig1R activation preserves retinal ganglion cells (RGCs)18–23 and photoreceptor cells,24–28 mitigates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and oxidative stress, modulates calcium homeostasis, and enhances antioxidant responses.29–33 Sig1R activation improves mitochondrial stability, suppresses neuroinflammation, and protects the blood–brain barrier dysfunction in models of vascular dementia and stroke34,35; however, its role in vascular protection, particularly in ischemic retinal disease, has not been investigated. We hypothesized that Sig1R activation regulates metabolic, oxidative, glial, and angiogenic signaling, thereby suppressing pathological neovascularization and restoring vascular integrity.

Here, we tested the hypothesis in the oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse model, a widely used model of human ROP.36,37 We evaluated the impact of Sig1R activation on cullin-3 ubiquitin ligase (Cul3)/AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-dependent metabolic signaling, oxidative stress, Müller glial activation, STAT3-dependent VEGF and inflammatory cytokines, and functional vascular outcomes, including vascular barrier integrity, arterial tortuosity, pathological neovascularization, and avascular area. To establish specificity, we used Sig1R knockout mice (Sig1R−/−) to confirm that the vascular benefits of the Sig1R agonist (+)-pentazocine ([+]-PTZ) are entirely Sig1R dependent.

Our study provides the first evidence that Sig1R activation confers vascular protection in ischemic retina, complementing its known neuroprotective effects. These findings suggest that Sig1R may serve as a dual neurovascular therapeutic target, offering a mechanism-based approach to prevent or treat ROP and potentially other ischemic retinal disorders.

Methods

Animals

Male and female C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) and Sig1R−/− mice, both on a C57BL/6J background, were housed in our animal facility under standard conditions. Animals were provided a Teklad Irradiated Rodent Diet 2918 (Inotiv, West Lafayette, IN, USA) and maintained on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. All procedures for animal care and treatment adhered to institutional guidelines, the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research, and the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

OIR Mouse Model and (+)-PTZ Treatment

OIR was induced following the method of Smith et al.36,37 with minor modifications. On postnatal day 7 (P7), pups of both sexes were placed along with their dams in a hyperoxia chamber (75% oxygen) for 5 days and then returned to room air (RA; 21% oxygen) on P12. The protocol produces vaso-obliteration of central retinal vessels during hyperoxia (P7–P12), followed by abnormal vascular regrowth and pathological retinal neovascularization (NV) upon return to RA (P12–P17). Controls were maintained in RA. Mice were compared to their littermate controls, so no specific randomization scheme was used. Data were pooled across litters to minimize variability due to differences in litter size and weight.38 Data from males and females were combined, as no sex-related differences were observed. (+)-PTZ, the dextrorotatory chiral enantiomer of pentazocine, was administered intraperitoneally (IP) at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg body weight (P127; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) starting at P7 and continued every other day.

Alkali-Induced Mouse Corneal Angiogenesis Model

C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized with ketamine–xylazine, and a central cornea injury was created by placing a 0.25-N NaOH-soaked filter paper disc (2-mm diameter) on the central cornea for 28 seconds. Corneas were then rinsed with PBS and treated with topical antibiotic ointment and sustained-release buprenorphine as an analgesic. Slit-lamp examination and photography for the corneal angiogenesis were performed using a ZEISS SL 800 slit lamp (ZEISS Microscopy, Jena, Germany). (+)-PTZ was administered daily starting 4 hours after NaOH-induced corneal injury.

Western Blot

Protein was extracted from neural retina isolated from WT-RA, WT-OIR-non, and WT-OIR+PTZ mice per our method.30 Proteins were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by transferring to nitrocellulose membranes. The nitrocellulose membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies (Table) followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse IgG antibody. The membrane was visualized using the SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA). All western blotting images are representative of three or more independent experiments. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ 1.48 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Antibodies Used in This Study

Dihydroethidium Detection of Oxidative Stress

Oxidative stress was assessed in retinal cryosections using dihydroethidium (DHE) staining (D11347; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following our established protocol.24 Upon oxidation, DHE intercalates with DNA, producing bright red nuclear fluorescence. The mean fluorescence intensity was quantified for each section and averaged per mouse.

Immunostaining of Retinal Cryosections

Cryostat sections (10 µm) were prepared from snap-frozen eyeballs. Cryosectioned slides were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; Z0334, 1:500; Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Slides were then washed three times with 0.1% Triton X-100 in 0.1-M PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated with appropriate Invitrogen fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sections were mounted with Fluorshield mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich) and imaged at 20× magnification using a ZEISS Axio Imager D2 fluorescence microscope (ZEISS Microscopy).

ELISA Assay of VEGF, TNF-α, and IL-6

Inflammatory cytokine levels in retinal lysate were determined using a customized mouse Match & Mix ELISA kits (Signosis, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Retinas were homogenized in lysis buffer (Signosis), and total protein concentrations were measured using the Coomassie Plus (Bradford) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of proteins were loaded into wells coated with primary antibodies against VEGF, TNF-α, and IL-6. Plates were processed according to the manufacturer's instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cytokine levels were calculated using provided standards and expressed as ng/mg total protein.

Mouse Cytokine Array

Retinas were homogenized in lysis buffer, and equal amounts of protein were assayed for proinflammatory and angiogenetic cytokines using a C-series mouse cytokine array C1000 platform (RayBiotech, Peachtree Corners, GA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 1× assay diluent followed by incubation with the retinal lysis harvested from WT-RA, WT-OIR-non, and WT-OIR+PTZ mice overnight at 4°C. The membranes were incubated with biotin-labeled anti-cytokines followed by incubation with avidin–HRP (1:1000). The membranes were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The spots on membranes were quantified using ImageJ and normalized against internal standards.

Measurement of Retinal Vascular Permeability

Retinal vascular permeability was evaluated using Evans blue (EB; Sigma-Aldrich) as described previously.39 Mice were deeply anesthetized and injected intraperitoneally with EB solution (30 mg/mL in saline). After 2 hours of circulation, eyes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 1 hour, retinas were dissected and flatmounted, and images were captured with a STELLARIS confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Fluorescein Angiography

Fluorescein angiograph (FA) was used to visualize and assess retinal vasculature. Mice were anesthetized, and a fluorescein solution (10%; Apollo Ophthalmic, Newport Beach, CA, USA) was administered via subcutaneous injection. Retinal images were captured 3 to 5 minutes post-injection using the Micron IV system (Phoenix-Micron, Bend, OR, USA). Retinal artery tortuosity and vein width was quantified using the Quant BV MATLAB program (MathType, Natick, MA, USA).40

Retinal Central Vaso-Obliteration and Neovascularization

To evaluate retinal vascular growth and pathological neovascularization in OIR mice, mice were euthanized at P17, and eyes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 hour. Retinas were dissected and stained with Invitrogen biotinylated isolectin GS-IB4 from Griffonia simplicifolia (0.2 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The retina was then flatmounted on the slide and visualized with a Leica STELLARIS confocal microscope. Image processing and quantification were performed with ImageJ using the SWIFT_NV macro as described by Stahl et al.41 Neovascular tufts were manually outlined by a masked user and quantified semi-automatically based on fluorescence intensity thresholds. Avascular areas were quantified using polygon selection tools in ImageJ, and results were reported as a percentage of the total retina area. Quantitative analyses were performed on six retinas per experimental group, each from a different mouse, with observers blinded to group identity. Data were statistically analyzed and plotted as mean values for comparison across groups.

Data Analysis

Data analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Depending on the experimental design, Student's t-test or one-way or two-way ANOVA tests were applied, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test as the post hoc analysis when significance was detected. All values are presented as mean ± SEM, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Sig1R Activation Restores Disrupted Metabolic Stress Signaling in OIR Retinas

To determine whether Sig1R signaling is altered in ischemic retinopathy, we first examined Sig1R expression in retinas from WT-RA control mice, OIR-non-treated (WT-OIR-non) mice, and OIR mice treated with the Sig1R agonist (+)-PTZ (WT-OIR+PTZ). Sig1R protein levels were significantly reduced in WT-OIR-non retinas compared with WT-RA controls (Figs. 1A, 1B). This decrease was accompanied by marked reductions in Cul3 (Figs. 1A, 1B) and phosphorylated PRKAA1 (Figs. 1C, 1D), and by increased phosphorylation of Akt and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), consistent with metabolic and endothelial stress (Figs. 1C, 1D). Pharmacological activation of Sig1R by (+)-PTZ significantly restored Sig1R, Cul3, and phosphorylated PRKAA1 expression while suppressing phosphorylation of Akt and eNOS in OIR retinas (Figs. 1A–D). These data indicate that Sig1R might act as an upstream regulator of metabolic–proteostatic signaling in ischemic retina and that Sig1R activation can normalize critical stress pathways associated with pathological angiogenesis.

(C) Western blot detection and (D) quantification analysis of phosphorylated PRKAA1, pAkt, and eNOS in retina lysates from WT-RA, WT-OIR-non and WT-OIR+PTZ mice. Sig1R Activation Suppresses Oxidative Stress and Müller Glial Activation in OIR Retina

Because metabolic stress is closely linked to oxidative injury and glial activation in ischemic retinopathy, we then examined retinal reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels at P9, P14, and P17 using DHE staining, which emits red fluorescence upon reacting with superoxide radicals. Minimal red fluorescence was detected in WT-RA retinas at all time points; however, WT-OIR-non retinas exhibited a progressive increase in superoxide production starting at P14 and through P17 compared with WT-RA controls (Figs. 2A–D). In contrast, (+)-PTZ treatment significantly reduced ROS levels at both P14 and P17, indicating potent anti-oxidative effects of Sig1R activation. At P9, ROS levels were slightly increased in OIR-non retinas, but there were no significant differences when compared to retinas from WT-RA or WT-OIR+PTZ (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Sig1R activation suppresses oxidative stress in OIR retina. (A–D) DHE staining and fluorescence intensity for retinal cryosection from WT-RA, WT-OIR-non, and WT-OIR+PTZ mice at P14 (A, B) and P17 (C, D). Scale bar: 50 µm. Values are mean ± SEM (n = 6 retinas per group). *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01, ****P < 0.0001.

We then assessed Müller glial activation by detecting GFAP immunostaining on retinal cryosections. In WT-RA retinas, GFAP staining was minimal and only present in the ganglion cell layer. However, WT-OIR-non retinas showed marked upregulation of GFAP staining throughout the Müller cell processes, extending from the ganglion cell layer to the outer nuclear layer, indicating robust Müller glial reactivity (Fig. 3A). In contrast, GFAP immunoreactivity in WT-OIR+PTZ retinas was markedly reduced, suggesting that Sig1R activation suppresses Müller glial activation (Figs. 3A, 3B). Western blotting (Fig. 3C) and densitometric analysis (Fig. 3D) supported these findings. GFAP expression was significantly increased in WT-OIR-non retinas compared to WT-RA but significantly reduced in WT-OIR+PTZ retinas (Figs. 3C, 3D). Together, these findings link upstream metabolic stress to oxidative injury and glial reactivity, both of which are attenuated by Sig1R activation.

Sig1R Activation Inhibits STAT3-Dependent VEGF and Inflammatory Signaling

Given that Müller glial cells are a major source of pro-angiogenic and inflammatory mediators in ischemic retina, we next examined downstream pro-angiogenic/inflammatory signaling pathways. VEGF overexpression is a major driver of retinal neovascularization, and VEGF-induced STAT3 activation has been linked to exacerbated inflammation in OIR retinas. Compared to WT-RA retinas, phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) levels were significantly increased (∼fourfold) in WT-OIR-non retinas. In contrast, (+)-PTZ treatment markedly reduced the elevation of p-STAT3 (∼twofold) in OIR retinas (Figs. 4A, 4B). Total STAT3 expression was also elevated in WT-OIR-non retinas and attenuated by (+)-PTZ in WT-OIR+PTZ retinas. Importantly, the p-STAT3/STAT3 ratio significantly increased (∼twofold) in WT-OIR-non retinas compared with WT-RA but was reduced by (+)-PTZ to levels comparable to WT-RA (Fig. 4B). VEGF levels were markedly elevated in WT-OIR-non retinas, whereas (+)-PTZ treatment substantially reduced this increase in WT-OIR+PTZ mice (Fig. 4C). In parallel, inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (Fig. 4D) and TNF-α (Fig. 4E) were elevated in WT-OIR-non retinal lysis but were markedly reduced by (+)-PTZ in WT-OIR+PTZ mice. Protein array assay showed that proinflammatory/angiogenic mediators, including macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1), exhibited a similar pattern of OIR-induced upregulation, and the increases of M-CSF, TNFR1, and VCAM-1 were all significantly suppressed by (+)-PTZ treatment in WT-OIR+PTZ retinas (Figs. 4F, 4G). These results demonstrate that Sig1R activation inhibits the STAT3–VEGF–inflammatory signaling axis, a key driver of pathological angiogenesis in ischemic retina.

Sig1R Activation Restores Vascular Barrier Integrity in OIR Retina

We next evaluated whether the molecular changes induced by Sig1R activation translated into functional vascular rescue. We assessed the vascular permeability as a measurement of blood–retinal barrier (BRB) integrity in OIR mice using an EB leakage assay. EB dye (30 mg/mL) was administered intraperitoneally, and 2 hours later eyes were enucleated for retina flatmount imaging to visualize dye extravasation. EB rapidly binds plasma albumin, and in the event of BRB breakdown the EB–albumin complex extravasates into surrounding tissue. Retinal flatmounts from OIR-non mice showed markedly increased EB leakage compared to WT-RA mice, whereas (+)-PTZ treatment significantly reduced EB extravasation, indicating protection against OIR-induced BRB disruption (Figs. 5A–I). Because BRB breakdown is associated with altered intercellular junction proteins, we further examined zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin expression. Western blot and densitometric analyses revealed significant reductions in ZO-1 and occludin in OIR-non retinas compared with WT-RA control. In contrast, both ZO-1 and occludin were markedly preserved in OIR+PTZ retinas, even higher than WT-RA levels (Figs. 5J, 5K).

Sig1R Activation Reduces Retinal Artery Tortuosity in OIR Mice

Fluorescein angiography demonstrated pronounced and persistent vascular tortuosity, a clinical hallmark of severe ROP plus disease after OIR exposure, which remained evident at both P17 and P49 (Figs. 6A–C). In contrast, (+)-PTZ treatment significantly reduced artery tortuosity in OIR retinas at both P17 and P49. At P49, WT-RA mice exhibited a dense, lattice-like capillary network between major vessels, characterized by numerous neovascular buds at the capillary tips (Fig. 6C). In contrast, OIR-non mice displayed a sparse capillary network with clear evidence of vascular pruning and an absence of neovascular buds, despite full vascular coverage at the late phase (P49). In comparison, OIR+PTZ mice demonstrated improved retinal vascular architecture, with healthier vessels and more active neovascular buds at the endothelia tip cells contributing to vascular remodeling (Fig. 6C). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the increased arterial tortuosity observed in OIR-non mice was significantly reduced by (+)-PTZ treatment in OIR+PTZ mice (Figs. 6D, 6E). Tortuosity values were as follows: P17-WT-RA, 1.02 ± 0.03; OIR-non, 1.24 ± 0.09; OIR+PTZ, 1.13 ± 0.08; P49-WT-RA, 1.02 ± 0.01; OIR-non, 1.19 ± 0.03; OIR+PTZ, 1.09 ± 0.04. Vein width also exhibited increases in WT-OIR-non, but was significantly decreased in WT-OIR+PTZ, although this protection was only shown at P17, not P49 (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Sig1R Activation Inhibits Pathological Retinal Angiogenesis

We further evaluated the effect of Sig1R activation by (+)-PTZ on OIR-induced pathological retinal angiogenesis and physiological vascular repair. The retinas collected at P17 (Fig. 7A) exhibited significant hemorrhage on OIR-non retinas, which was substantially reduced in OIR+PTZ retinas. Isolectin B4 (IB4)-labeled retinal flatmounts (Fig. 7B) revealed extensive pathological neovascular tufts and large avascular areas in OIR-non retinas. In contrast, (+)-PTZ treatment significantly reduced pathological NV tuft area (NV% of total retinal area; OIR+PTZ, 1.79 ± 0.94; OIR-non, 7.87 ± 2.57) (Figs. 7B, 7C, 7E). Meanwhile, (+)-PTZ also promoted revascularization for avascular regions demonstrated by reduction in avascular area (AV% of total retinal area; OIR+PTZ, 8.07 ± 5.76; OIR-non, 18.97 ± 9.60) (Figs. 7B, 7D). These data demonstrate that Sig1R activation not only suppresses pathological angiogenesis but also promotes vascular normalization and functional recovery. To determine whether the inhibition of pathological angiogenic effects of Sig1R activation extends beyond the ischemic retina, we further examined an alkali-induced corneal angiogenesis model. NaOH-induced pathological angiogenesis progressively developed from the limbus, extending from post-injury day 8 (P8D) through P22D and ultimately reaching the center cornea by P34D. In contrast, activation of Sig1R with (+)-PTZ significantly suppressed NaOH-induced pathological neovascularization throughout the entire observation period (P1D–P34D) compared with NaOH-treated controls (Fig. 8). These findings indicate that Sig1R activation broadly inhibits pathological angiogenesis in vivo.

(+)-PTZ-Mediated Vascular Protection Is Sig1R Dependent

To confirm that the protective effects of (+)-PTZ are mediated specifically through Sig1R, we induced OIR in Sig1R knockout (Sig1R−/−) mice. At P17, IB4-stained retinal flatmounts from Sig1R−/− mice induced by OIR (S1RKO-OIR-non) exhibited significantly increased NV (Figs. 9B, 9H) and AV (Figs. 9B, 9G) in Sig1R−/− mice compared with Sig1R−/−RA (S1RKO-RA). However, neither AV nor NV was protected by (+)-PTZ treatment (Figs. 9C, 9G, 9H). FA imaging at P49 demonstrated that, compared to S1RKO-RA mice (Fig. 9D), significantly increased retinal artery tortuosity was induced in S1RKO-OIR-non mice (Fig. 9E) but was not protected by (+)-PTZ treatment in S1RKO-OIR+PTZ mice (Figs. 9F, 9I). Together, (+)-PTZ treatment failed to reduce neovascularization or avascular areas and did not improve arterial tortuosity in Sig1R-deficient OIR retinas. These findings indicate that (+)-PTZ does not confer neurovascular protection in OIR mice lacking Sig1R, confirming that (+)-PTZ-mediated vascular protection is entirely Sig1R dependent.

Discussion

In this study, we provide the first direct evidence, to our knowledge, that activation of Sig1R confers retinal vascular protection under ischemic stress. Using the OIR model, we showed that Sig1R expression is markedly reduced in diseased retinas and that this loss is accompanied by disrupted metabolic and endothelial signaling, including decreased Cul3 and pAMPK, increased pAkt and eNOS, and elevated oxidative stress, as well as Müller glial activation, upregulation of STAT3 and pro-angiogenic/inflammatory mediators, and consequent vascular dysfunction (pathological neovascularization, vascular leakage, arterial tortuosity, and impaired revascularization). Pharmacological activation of Sig1R with (+)-PTZ restored this network toward homeostasis, normalizing Cul3 and pAMPK, suppressing pAkt/eNOS, reducing oxidative stress and glial activation, inhibiting STAT3-dependent VEGF and inflammatory cytokines, and ultimately improving retinal vascular structure and function.

Mechanistically, Sig1R appears to act as an upstream regulator of a metabolic-stress-angiogenesis axis that governs pathological retinal angiogenesis. Cul3 is a central scaffold of E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes that regulate proteostasis, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress responses. We found that Cul3 is reduced in OIR retinas and restored by Sig1R activation. This is consistent with our previous observations that (+)-PTZ increases Cul3 mRNA and protein, whereas Cul3 is suppressed in Sig1R-deficient Müller cells and Sig1R-siRNA–treated 661W photoreceptor cells.26 Prior studies also demonstrate that Cul3 regulates endothelial cell function and angiogenic signaling,42,43 supporting its role as a key metabolic–vascular checkpoint. AMPK is critical for vascular stability and redox homeostasis.44–46 In OIR retinas, decreased pAMPK together with increased pAkt and eNOS indicate dysregulated metabolic and endothelial stress signaling. Sig1R activation restored AMPK phosphorylation and normalized downstream Akt/eNOS signaling, consistent with improved vascular stability and redox homeostasis. These findings suggest that Sig1R maintains a metabolic–proteostatic balance through Cul3–AMPK signaling, thereby restraining aberrant angiogenic responses.

Elevated ROS in OIR retinas was accompanied by robust Müller glial activation, as indicated by increased GFAP. Müller glial cells are a major source of VEGF and inflammatory mediators in ischemic retina.47,48 Sig1R activation significantly suppressed ROS accumulation and glial activation, consistent with reduced VEGF and STAT3 phosphorylation. Sig1R-mediated redox control appears to limit glial reactivity, thereby attenuating STAT3–VEGF signaling and pathological angiogenesis. Supporting this mechanism, previous transcriptomic analysis from Sig1R-deficient retinas revealed upregulation of Ctsg and Nrarp,32 both of which have been linked to VEGF induction.49,50 This is also consistent with previous reports that Sig1R activation stabilizes the blood–brain barrier and suppresses neurovascular inflammation in stroke and vascular dementia models.35 Inflammation and oxidative stress are tightly intertwined in the pathogenesis of ROP.51–53 Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α are elevated in ROP patients54–56 and were similarly increased in OIR-non retinas in our study; however, Sig1R activation significantly reduced the level of IL-6 and TNF-α in OIR retinas. Current antioxidant (e.g., vitamin E, D-penicillamine) and anti-inflammatory (e.g., steroid, NSAIDS) therapeutic strategies have shown limited efficacy,57–62 potentially due to the complex and hostile retinal microenvironment.63 In contrast, Sig1R activation, by simultaneously reducing oxidative stress, dampening inflammation, and stabilizing endothelial junctions, offers a novel and multifaceted strategy for restoring retina homeostasis. This provides a mechanistic explanation for the observed decreases in VEGF, IL-6, TNF-α, M-CSF, VCAM-1, TNFR, and p-STAT3 upon Sig1R activation.

Functionally, Sig1R activation improved retinal vascular barrier integrity, reduced arterial tortuosity, suppressed pathological neovascularization, and promoted revascularization of avascular regions, linking upstream molecular events to meaningful vascular outcomes. Clinically, current ROP treatments, including anti-VEGF therapy and laser ablation, are limited by incomplete vascular repair and potential systemic effects.64–66 Sig1R activation represents a mechanism-based therapeutic target that addresses these limitations, with the added potential for dual neurovascular protection given prior neuroprotection.

Importantly, (+)-PTZ failed to rescue neovascularization, avascular area, or arterial tortuosity in Sig1R-deficient OIR mice, demonstrating that its protective effects are entirely Sig1R dependent. This aligns with prior reports demonstrating that Sig1R ligands require the receptor to exert neuroprotective actions in models of retinal degeneration.24 This genetic validation confirms that Sig1R is the critical upstream regulator of the metabolic–oxidative–glial–angiogenic axis in ischemic retinopathy, and its vascular protection should be generalizable to other Sig1R agonists. The (+)-PTZ used in this study is a prototypical high-affinity Sig1R ligand (Ki =16.7 nM) and is widely used as a reference compound for Sig1R binding assays.67 Pentazocine, however, exists as two stereoisomers with distinct pharmacological profiles: the (–)-PTZ isomer acts as a mixed κ-opioid receptor agonist and weak µ-opioid receptor antagonist,68–72 whereas the (+)-PTZ isomer selectively binds Sig1R with negligible opioid affinity. The clinically used racemic mixture exerts its analgesic effects primarily through the (–)-PTZ, thereby limiting its utility as a Sig1R-specific therapeutic. Although Sig1R agonists have not been evaluated in ROP, evidence from ocular and vascular systems supports a role for Sig1R in regulating endothelial function, barrier integrity and vascular inflammation. Other Sig1R agonists, including SA4503 and PRE-084, have been shown to modulate blood–brain and blood–retinal barrier properties.73–79 Clinically relevant Sig1R agonists, including pridopidine (Ki = 7.1 nM) and fluvoxamine (Ki = 17 nM), have demonstrated favorable safety profiles in neurological diseases such as Huntington's disease80 and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis,81,82 as well as Covid-1983,84 and experimental glaucoma.85 Future comparisons of structurally distinct Sig1R agonists will be important to define their relative efficacy and translational potential in ROP.

Sig1R is expressed in multiple retinal cell types, including RGCs, photoreceptors, Müller cells, and retinal pigment epithelium.30,86–89 Our previous studies have shown that Sig1R ligands demonstrate efficacy in models of retinitis pigmentosa, diabetic retinopathy, and glaucoma.19,24,27,28,86,87,90–94 Previous studies have also shown that Sig1R ligands protect isolated RGCs from oxidative stress and reduce cytokine release in cultured Müller cells, suggesting both direct neuronal and indirect glial contributions to protection.30,95 However, the precise cellular targets responsible for the neurovascular benefits of (+)-PTZ in vivo remain to be defined. Future studies employing cell type–specific Sig1R knockout models will be critical for identifying the relevant cellular targets and delineating Sig1R-specific pathways. Our recently developed Sig1R flox/flox mice provide a valuable tool96 for this purpose.

Conclusions

Our study identified Sig1R as a central regulator of pathological retinal angiogenesis through modulation of metabolic, oxidative, and inflammatory pathways. Activation of Sig1R restores vascular homeostasis in ischemic retina and represents a promising novel therapeutic strategy for ROP and other neovascular retinal diseases.
