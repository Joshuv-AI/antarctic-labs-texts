Uveitis of undifferentiated etiology: insights into diagnosis and evolution from a cohort of 338 patients
Authors: Damien Al-Hamoud, Robin Jacquot, Gaëlle Richard-Colmant, Mathieu Gerfaud-Valentin, Yvan Jamilloux, Arthur Bert, Philipe Denis, Laurent Kodjikian, Pascal Sève
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite considerable advances in diagnostic work-up, approximately one-third of uveitis remains of undifferentiated etiology. Few studies have evaluated the proportion of etiological diagnoses made during follow-up in patients initially classified as undifferentiated uveitis. The study aimed to assess the achievement of a diagnosis during the follow-up and to describe how the diagnosis was reached for these patients.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of patients with uveitis referred to our tertiary center between 2002 and 2023. Uveitis was classified as undifferentiated after an exhaustive diagnostic work-up and at least one year of follow-up. An etiological diagnosis can be established through new clinical signs, ophthalmologic examinations, or additional tests.Results338 patients with uveitis were initially classified as undifferentiated. During a median follow-up of 58.4 months, an etiological diagnosis was established in 52 patients (15.4%), mainly by the appearance of new clinical symptoms in twenty-two patients. Specifically, ten patients presented with neurological symptoms, five with mucocutaneous involvement, and five with rheumatological symptoms. In nineteen patients, an etiology was diagnosed by the contribution of complementary investigations, mainly by next-generation sequencing in 7 patients and a PET (Positron Emission Tomography) scan in 3 patients. In eleven patients, new specific ocular signs were identified during ophthalmological examinations, leading to the establishment of a new diagnosis.ConclusionIn patients with initially undifferentiated uveitis, an etiology was identified during follow-up in about one-sixth of the patients, primarily through repeated physical examinations and the performance of new complementary tests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04719-7.Keywords: Uveitis, Idiopathic, Etiology, Undifferentiated, Diagnosis, Etiological work-up

Introduction

Uveitis is characterized by inflammation of the uvea, including the iris, ciliary body, and choroid, with an estimated incidence ranging from 17 to 52 cases per 100,000 individuals per year [1]. It accounts for approximately 5% of cases of legal blindness, defined as a central visual acuity of 2/20 or worse in the better-seeing eye. This condition usually arises from complications like macular edema, elevated intraocular pressure, or retinal ischemia [2]. It predominantly affects working-age populations [3]. Uveitis etiologies could be divided into 3 main groups: infectious diseases (25%), systemic diseases (50%), and pure-related ophthalmological diseases (25%). Iatrogenic and neoplastic causes, while less common, should also be considered. Despite an exhaustive diagnostic work-up, 24.2% to 52.5% of uveitis remains of undifferentiated cause, called undifferentiated uveitis [4, 5].

Identifying an underlying cause can significantly impact patient management by guiding specific treatment strategies. For instance, detecting an infectious etiology necessitates targeted therapy, preventing the inappropriate use of potentially harmful immunosuppressive treatments [6]. Thus, a thorough initial evaluation and regular reassessment are crucial to enhance management and avert complications stemming from inadequate treatment [7]. Richard Colmant et al. [8] highlighted the significance of extended follow-up in patients with undifferentiated uveitis, noting that an etiological diagnosis was determined in one out of five patients after more than a year of monitoring. The emergence of new clinical signs, especially neurological, rheumatologic, or mucocutaneous, can offer critical insights and enhance etiological diagnosis. Ophthalmologic assessments and additional investigations, such as a Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scanner, are also crucial in establishing a delayed diagnosis. Available data is still limited, mainly due to small sample sizes in earlier studies, and few investigations have examined the diagnostic evolution of undifferentiated uveitis in larger cohorts [8, 9].

Our study aimed to assess the achievement of a diagnosis during the follow-up and to describe how the diagnosis was reached for these patients.

Materials and methods

Population

We conducted a monocentric retrospective observational study, including patients with uveitis of undifferentiated cause referred by the department of ophthalmology (Lyon University Hospital, Lyon, France) or by non-hospital ophthalmologists to the department of internal medicine (Lyon University Hospital, Lyon, France) between July 2002 and November 2023. At diagnosis, uveitis was confirmed by ophthalmological examination and anamnesis, combined with physical examination was performed by an internist (PS, AB, MGV, YJ, GRC). While regional guidelines suggest a multidisciplinary evaluation for all uveitis cases, in practice, referral to internal medicine is often reserved for recurrent, chronic, or severe cases. Thus, our study population reflects a selected cohort of patients requiring tertiary specialized expertise. Thus, our study population reflects a selected cohort of patients requiring tertiary specialized expertise. Patients of all ages were eligible for inclusion. However, it should be noted that a significant portion of pediatric patients in our region is managed in a specialized pediatric ophthalmology department, which explains their relatively lower representation compared to adults in our cohort.

All patients underwent a standardized screening protocol based on the ULISSE recommendations, tailored to the anatomical type of uveitis [10]. Although the ULISSE study was published in 2017, its framework was retrospectively applied to all cases for etiological classification; clinical data collected prior to 2017 were re-evaluated based on medical records. This protocol was organized into a systematic first-line work-up followed by oriented second-line investigations. The first-line work-up, performed for all patients, included a complete blood cell count (CBC), C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), Interferon-Gamma Release Assay (IGRA), syphilis serology, and a chest X-ray [11].

Second-line investigations were conducted based on the clinical phenotype or initial findings. Regarding sarcoidosis screening, a chest CT scan was performed, complemented by extra-ophthalmological biopsies if clinically suggestive features were present. According to the ULISSE framework, patients with a suggestive ocular phenotype underwent a minor salivary gland biopsy (MSGB), a transbronchial lung biopsy, and/or a bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). Neurological or neoplastic suspicions led to further imaging; an MRI was conducted for features indicative of multiple sclerosis or primary intraocular lymphoma, while (18 F-FDG PET/CT) was indicated according to the nomogram described by Chauvelot et al. [12], integrating age at diagnosis, presence of synechiae, and elevated ACE levels.

Invasive procedures were reserved for specific indications. Anterior chamber paracentesis was conducted in cases of acute anterior uveitis with negative HLA-B27 and suspicion of herpetic infection, or in severe, corticosteroid-resistant cases to evaluate for herpesvirus, lymphoma, tuberculosis, or chronic endophthalmitis through cytology, interleukin profiling, and multiplex PCR. A vitreous biopsy or diagnostic vitrectomy was performed when intraocular lymphoma was suspected, and lumbar puncture was considered in cases of chronic intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis of unknown origin to rule out specific conditions such as primary CNS lymphoma or neurosarcoidosis. Following the ULISSE framework, it was also indicated in the presence of bilateral papillary edema to investigate intracranial hypertension. Finally, uveitis was classified as undifferentiated after a negative diagnostic work-up and a follow-up period of at least one year. Exclusion criteria included the presence of a previously known etiology.

Definitions

For uveitis cases where a diagnosis was made during follow-up, the following criteria were used:

The SUN criteria [13] (Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature) for Behçet’s disease, Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) syndrome, presumed tubercular uveitis, sarcoidosis, CMV (cytomegalovirus) anterior uveitis, Fuchs uveitis, spondylitis, MS (multiple sclerosis), HSV (herpes simplex virus) anterior uveitis, pars planitis, toxoplasmic retinochoroiditis, and birdshot chorioretinopathy.

For entities without established classification criteria in the literature (including ROSAH syndrome (Retinal dystrophy, Optic nerve edema, Splenomegaly, Anhidrosis, and Headache syndrome), primary intraocular lymphoma, CADASIL (Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy), Best disease, ANCA-associated vasculitis (Anti-Neutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibodies), NEMO-related disease [IKBKG mutation], IRVAN syndrome (Idiopathic Retinal Vasculitis, Aneurysms, and Neuroretinitis), and Blau syndrome), the diagnosis was retained based on expert consensus (PS, YJ, LK) after a comprehensive review of clinical and paraclinical data for each patient.

Data collection

For patients with a confirmed diagnosis, we recorded the methods used to reach this conclusion. This included specialist consultations, the appearance of new symptoms, a reassessment of ophthalmological or paraclinical tests, and any changes in treatment that resulted from the diagnosis. Ophthalmological data collected at diagnosis and during follow-up included the topography and location of uveitis, along with associated ophthalmological signs such as granulomatous changes and synechiae. The SUN was used in this study for the anatomical classification of uveitis [14].

Clinical data, including signs and symptoms from both ophthalmological and extra-ophthalmological examinations, as well as complementary tests conducted at the time of diagnosis or during follow-up, were collected from patient files by three internists (PS, RJ, DAH). This information was gathered based on consultation reports produced by internists (PS, MGV, YJ, GRC, AB) before establishing the etiological diagnosis. They included pertinent features from the physical examination of the skin, joints, neurological system, pulmonary system, abdomen, ears, nose, throat, and lymph nodes. The clinical features and complementary exams were deemed relevant to the etiological diagnosis if the symptoms, signs, or results of these exams were associated with the underlying cause.

Ethics

According to French law (no. 2004 − 806, 9 August 2004), and because the data were collected retrospectively and patient management was not modified, this study did not require research ethics committee approval. However, the cohort study received approval from the local ethics committee (Hospices Civils de Lyon) in February 2019 (No. 19–31) and was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT 03863782).

Statistical analysis

Data on continuous variables were summarized as the median and and the first and third quartiles (Q1–Q3) and were compared using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test. Data on categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test. All tests were two-sided, and a statistical significance was set at p = 0.05. All analyses were performed using RStudio with R-software version 4.2.3 (R foundation for statistical computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Among 1263 patients referred to a tertiary care internal medicine center for the etiological diagnosis of uveitis, 620 patients (49%) were identified with uveitis of undifferentiated etiology. Out of 620 patients, 338 were followed for over one year and included in this study (Table 1). A total of 31 patients (9.2%) were under 18 years of age at the time of diagnosis. The median follow-up was 58.4 months (Q1-Q3 28.4–110.1). The median age at uveitis onset was 46 years. 226 patients (66.9%) were female, and 272 of the patients (80.5%) were Caucasian. Uveitis was bilateral in 64% and mostly chronic (78.1%). Cystoid macular edema (CME) was present in 36.7% of patients, vasculitis in 26%, and multifocal choroiditis in 8.6%. The main anatomical forms of uveitis were panuveitis in 31% and anterior uveitis in 30%.

Identification of an underlying cause

Among the patients with undifferentiated uveitis, a final diagnosis was made in 52 patients (15.4%) (details presented in the Appendix, Table 5). The median time between the first consultation in internal medicine and the establishment of a delayed etiological diagnosis was 3.1 years (Q1-Q3 1.3; 8.3 years). The most frequently identified etiologies are summarized in Fig. 1. The most common etiology of initially undifferentiated uveitis was systemic inflammatory diseases in 30 patients (55.7%), including sarcoidosis (n = 9), multiple sclerosis (n = 6), spondyloarthritis (n = 5), Behçet’s disease (n = 4), IBD (inflammatory bowel diseases) (n = 3), psoriasis (n = 1), VKH disease (n = 1), and granulomatosis with polyangiitis (n = 1). A significant number of patients were diagnosed with monogenic auto-inflammatory uveitis, totaling seven cases (13.5%). These included the following conditions: ROSAH syndrome (n = 4), Blau syndrome (n = 1), CADASIL (n = 1), and NEMO syndrome (n = 1). There were seven patients (13.5%) diagnosed with pure-related ophthalmological uveitis, including Fuchs uveitis (n = 3), Best disease (n = 1), birdshot retinochoroidopathy (n = 1), pars planitis (n = 1), and IRVAN syndrome (n = 1). Infection was detected in five patients (11.5%), specifically tuberculosis (n = 2), toxoplasmosis (n = 1), CMV-associated uveitis (n = 1), and HSV-associated uveitis (n = 1). More rarely, three cases were identified with a neoplastic etiology, diagnosed as vitreoretinal lymphoma (VRL).

Diagnostic tool to determine the underlying cause of uveitis

The delayed diagnosis of the underlying cause occurred in 22 patients (42.3%) due to the emergence of new clinical signs. In 19 patients (36.6%), it was due to the performance of a complementary exam, while 11 patients (21.1%) necessitated a new ophthalmologic examination (Table 2). The primary clinical signs observed during follow-up were neurological in ten patients, resulting in diagnoses of multiple sclerosis (n = 6), sarcoidosis (n = 3), and viral infections (n = 1). Subsequently, mucocutaneous involvement with aphtosis in 3 patients and articular involvement with axial and peripheral joint pains in 5 patients contributed, respectively, to the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease and spondyloarthritis. Among complementary exams, the PET scan and genetic testing were the most valuable. PET scans primarily led to the diagnosis of sarcoidosis in two patients by detecting hypermetabolism in the mediastinal lymph nodes. Genetic testing enables the diagnosis of autoinflammatory monogenic diseases, including ROSAH syndrome, Blau syndrome, NEMO syndrome, and CADASIL. The ophthalmological examination contributed to the etiological diagnosis either through the evolution of specific ophthalmological signs or expert opinion. This was particularly useful for identifying pure-related ophthalmological diseases (n = 7), including Fuchs disease, IRVAN syndrome, Pars planitis, and birdshot retinochoroidopathy.

Factors associated with etiological diagnosis in patients with presumed undifferentiated uveitis

Among the 338 included patients, 52 (15.8%) received a late etiological diagnosis during follow-up. Analysis of the factors associated with this identification revealed a significant difference in patient age, with a lower median age in the diagnosed group compared to undiagnosed patients (38 vs. 46.5 years; p < 0.01) (Table 4). Additionally, the median follow-up duration was significantly longer in the group with a late diagnosis (103.5 vs. 52.8 months; p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant association was found with sex, anatomical forms of uveitis, acute or granulomatous presentation, laterality, intraocular hypertension, ethnic background, or the presence of cystoid macular edema, retinal vasculitis, papillitis, or multifocal choroiditis.

Discussion

This retrospective study analyzed 338 patients with undifferentiated uveitis followed for over one year. An etiological diagnosis was found in about one-sixth of cases after a median follow-up of nearly six years, primarily due to new clinical signs (42.3%), additional complementary exams (36.6%), or repeated eye examinations (21.1%). The most frequent etiologies diagnosed were sarcoidosis, spondyloarthritis, multiple sclerosis, and monogenic autoinflammatory diseases. A younger age and a longer follow-up period were both significantly linked to a higher diagnostic yield. The results highlight the need for long-term reevaluation in patients with undifferentiated uveitis, as one in six ultimately receives a diagnosis that enables treatment adjustment in 83% of cases.

We acknowledge that the terminology between idiopathic and undetermined etiology may lead to confusion. In our classification, idiopathic uveitis refers to defined clinical entities considered idiopathic by consensus (e.g., pars planitis), and these were regarded as solved cases. Conversely, uveitis of undifferentiated etiology was used for cases in which no specific diagnosis could be reached after a comprehensive evaluation, including entities such as multifocal choroiditis, which were therefore categorized as unsolved.

Few studies have investigated the long-term outcomes of undifferentiated uveitis, apart from the work of Richard Colmant et al. [8], which demonstrated that an etiology could be identified in one in five patients after more than one year of follow-up. In our study, 49% of uveitis cases were classified as undifferentiated, which is consistent with findings from other significant studies conducted in tertiary referral centers [15–17]. The epidemiological characteristics of our undifferentiated uveitis cohort match those reported in Western tertiary centers [16, 17]. However, the ophthalmological characteristics were not similar with less anterior uveitis and more panuveitis (56% vs. 25% in other series) [18–20]. These discrepancies can be attributed to the monocentric design of our study, conducted in a tertiary center, which has specialized departments focused on diagnosing and treating complex uveitis cases.

Our findings are consistent with those of Rosenbaum et al. [9], who reclassified 29% of undifferentiated uveitis cases after referral. However, our lower rate of reclassification at 15% likely reflects methodological differences. Rosenbaum’s patients were referred earlier and had incomplete evaluations, while we focused only on cases that remained undiagnosed after one year, representing a more challenging subgroup. Sharma et al. [21] recently demonstrated that more than 50% of initially undifferentiated uveitis cases were later reclassified, primarily as tuberculous uveitis, in an Indian cohort after 10 years of follow-up. This underscores the influence of regional epidemiology and advancing diagnostic tools, in contrast to the lower reclassification rate observed in our cohort.

Circumstances of diagnosis

General clinical examination plays a crucial role in diagnosing uveitis, initially classified as undifferentiated. Jacquot et al. [22] have shown that extra-ophthalmological signs contributed to the etiological diagnosis in 28% of cases, especially in bilateral acute anterior uveitis and panuveitis with synechiae. Our findings, along with the high rate of late-onset systemic symptoms, suggest that systematic and early referral to an internal medicine specialist should be considered mandatory for patients with persistent undifferentiated uveitis. This collaboration is not merely an option but a key step in identifying subtle clinical changes that ophthalmologists might not primarily focus on. Articular and skin examinations were the most informative, while in our study, the neurological exam was the most contributive. Our findings emphasize the need for a focused clinical evaluation that targets inflammatory joint pain, aphthosis, mucocutaneous lesions, and neurological signs, based on the anatomical type of uveitis. In our cohort, the delayed onset of neurological symptoms led to an etiological diagnosis in nine patients, including six cases of MS and three of sarcoidosis. In MS, uveitis may precede neurological manifestations by several years [23–25]. Le Scanff et al. [24] reported that uveitis preceded MS in 46% of cases. Prolonged monitoring is therefore essential, particularly in undifferentiated intermediate uveitis. In the study by Rochepeau et al. on biopsy-proven sarcoidosis, 7.7% of patients developed extraocular disease after 60 months, highlighting potential diagnostic delays [26]. Four diagnoses of spondyloarthritis were made during follow-up, following the delayed onset of suggestive articular symptoms. In most cases, HLA-B27 positivity was already present at initial evaluation, but without musculoskeletal involvement. Uveitis occurs in 25% of patients with spondyloarthritis and may precede joint involvement in up to 18%. Retrospective analysis often uncovers prior joint or back pain in 20–40% of cases [27, 28]. We also acknowledge that HLA-B27 negativity does not formally rule out spondyloarthritis, particularly in our multi-ethnic cohort where 19.5% of patients were non-Caucasian (including 13% of North African and 3% of Sub-Saharan African descent). In these populations, the prevalence of the HLA-B27 allele is known to be lower than in Caucasians [29], which reduces its negative predictive value. Consequently, in our practice, the diagnosis of spondyloarthritis in HLA-B27 negative cases relied on the ASAS (Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society) criteria, integrating suggestive clinical symptoms and sacroiliac imaging during follow-up.

Six patients initially categorized as undifferentiated underwent whole-exome sequencing, revealing four cases of ROSAH syndrome, one case of Blau syndrome, and one case of NEMO syndrome, in the context of a suggestive monogenic autoinflammatory disease. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has transformed the diagnostic approach to unexplained uveitis by identifying rare monogenic autoinflammatory diseases that may mimic sarcoidosis or undifferentiated granulomatous conditions [30, 31].

While high-cost investigations such as 18 F-FDG PET/CT and NGS are not cost-effective for systematic screening, their targeted use—guided by specific clinical nomograms or highly suggestive phenotypes—is justified by their high diagnostic yield in selected refractory cases. By enabling an early definitive diagnosis, these examinations may ultimately reduce the long-term costs associated with repeated inconclusive tests and inappropriate treatments.

Although rare, these diseases are likely underdiagnosed. Their recognition should be considered in the presence of prolonged fever, skin lesions, or family history, regardless of age, and is expected to increase with broader NGS use. In our cohort, PET scanner also contributed to the reclassification of three patients initially considered undifferentiated —two with sarcoidosis and one with Crohn’s disease. The PET scanner was particularly useful in the context of normal initial chest CT and persistent diagnostic uncertainty. Similarly, Chauvelot et al. [12] reported mediastinal hypermetabolism in 28% of patients with unexplained uveitis and normal CT, leading to a confirmed diagnosis in 10%.

Several diagnoses were established through repeated ophthalmologic evaluations. The final diagnosis could be established based on changes in clinical findings, enabling ophthalmologists to refine the diagnosis towards a specific clinical entity. This is especially the case for the emergence of characteristic retinal lesions (e.g., toxoplasmosis, intraocular lymphoma, or Behçet) or typical choroidal involvement (e.g., birdshot chorioretinopathy, VKH disease). Such entities are known to manifest gradually, preventing early diagnosis, and highlighting the importance of regular follow-up of patients without clear diagnosis. Certain diagnoses may be reassessed or changed following a second opinion from a tertiary center specializing in uveitis. This may apply to some challenging, uncommon cases or to rare diseases that can mimic intraocular inflammation. This underscores the need for non-specialist ophthalmologists to refer patients to reference centers, which can decrease the time for diagnosis and limit the number of ancillary tests [11].

Therapeutic implications

The late identification of an etiological cause in patients initially labeled as having undifferentiated uveitis led to a therapeutic modification in 43 cases, highlighting the impact of diagnostic reassessment on patient management. These modifications provided several benefits depending on the underlying pathology. In some cases, discontinuation of the treatment was necessary.

Limitations

Our study presents several limitations. As a monocentric study conducted at a tertiary center with a limited number of patients, our population may not accurately represent all cases of uveitis with unknown etiology. The retrospective nature of the study resulted in some missing data, especially regarding certain ophthalmologic examinations.

Some patients had limited follow-up or discontinued their care at our center, which may have led to missed secondary diagnoses. Patients with acute anterior undifferentiated uveitis had significantly shorter follow-up periods, likely due to single, non-recurring episodes that resulted in early discontinuation of medical follow-up. Furthermore, a selection bias must be acknowledged: our cohort predominantly includes complex or persistent cases referred to internal medicine, potentially underrepresenting simple, self-limiting anterior uveitis managed solely by community ophthalmologists. Finally, we acknowledge that, while the ULISSE algorithm provides a systematic framework for the diagnostic evaluation of uveitis, it is not universally adopted among uveitis specialists. Some etiologies, including recently described or rare conditions (e.g., monogenic autoinflammatory syndromes), are not encompassed by the ULISSE diagnostic pathway. In this study, the ULISSE recommendations were used primarily to standardize and structure the etiological classification, rather than to exhaustively represent all possible causes of uveitis. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts and rigorous follow-up are essential to better characterize uveitis of unknown etiology and to understand its evolution. It is also important to investigate how early diagnosis affects visual prognosis, especially through more targeted treatments. This research could provide valuable insights to enhance management and improve long-term functional outcomes.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the importance of prolonged follow-up for the etiological diagnosis of undifferentiated uveitis. 15% of patients included in this cohort received an accurate diagnosis during the follow-up with therapeutic modifications in 83% of these cases.
