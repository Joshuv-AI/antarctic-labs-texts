Association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and in‐hospital costs in adults with sepsis: A sub analysis of a prospective cohort study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternational statements suggest using the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria in intensive care units (ICUs); however, the economic impact of GLIM‐defined malnutrition in sepsis remains unclear. This study investigated the association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and in‐hospital costs in sepsis.MethodsWe conducted a sub‐analysis of the prospective cohort study, Investing Long‐term Outcomes of Sepsis or Septic shock, among medically managed critically ill patients with sepsis in 15 Japanese ICUs. Associations between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and cost categories (low, moderate, and high) were assessed using multinomial logistic regression (reference: low‐cost group), adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score. Individual GLIM components were also examined. Total in‐hospital costs and secondary outcomes were compared between malnutrition and non‐malnutrition groups using inverse probability of treatment weighting.ResultsAmong 259 patients, 111 had GLIM‐defined malnutrition. GLIM‐defined malnutrition was associated with both the moderate‐cost (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 2.03, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05–3.89) and high‐cost classifications (AOR: 2.27, 95% CI: 1.20–4.29). Among GLIM components, reduced muscle mass (AOR: 1.95, 95% CI: 1.04–3.68) and reduced food intake (AOR: 2.61, 95% CI: 1.39–4.92) were associated with the high‐cost classification. After weighting, the malnutrition group had significantly higher median in‐hospital costs (24,959 vs. 18,651 USD), longer hospital stays, and longer ICU stays than the non‐malnutrition group.ConclusionsGLIM‐defined malnutrition—particularly reduced muscle mass and reduced food intake—was associated with higher in‐hospital costs in sepsis and may be a potential indicator of high‐cost hospitalization.Keywords: economic burden, hospital costs, intensive care unit, malnutrition, sepsis

INTRODUCTION

Malnutrition is a major factor associated with increased mortality and complications among hospitalized patients. 1 Beyond clinical outcomes, malnutrition imposes a substantial economic burden globally: in‐hospital costs increase by 1.3‐ to 1.7‐fold across multiple countries, 2 , 3 based on traditional nutritional screening and assessment tools. To date, however, most of these economic evaluations have focused on general ward populations. Notably, an economic analysis estimated that the additional in‐hospital costs associated with malnutrition were approximately 6.5‐fold higher among ICU patients than among general ward patients. 4

In 2018, the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria were introduced as a standardized diagnostic framework for malnutrition. 5 Numerous studies have demonstrated that GLIM‐defined malnutrition is associated with higher mortality and prolonged hospitalization. 6 International statements have suggested using the GLIM criteria to diagnose malnutrition in ICUs. 7 Moreover, nutrition‐related risk has been associated with adverse outcomes among critically ill patients with sepsis. 8 Sepsis is one of the most costly conditions requiring hospitalization, 9 particularly in the ICU, where sepsis‐related costs are approximately 1.5‐fold higher than those of non‐sepsis admissions. 10 Given the substantial economic burden of sepsis in the ICU, identifying cost drivers is essential, 11 making malnutrition a plausible and clinically important candidate. 4 However, evidence supporting the association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and healthcare costs among critically ill patients with sepsis remains limited.

Therefore, we conducted a sub‐analysis of the multicenter prospective cohort study, Investing Long‐term Outcomes of Sepsis or Septic shock (ILOSS), 12 to assess the association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition at ICU admission and total in‐hospital costs among critically ill patients with sepsis. We hypothesized that GLIM‐defined malnutrition at ICU admission would be associated with higher total in‐hospital costs in these patients. Clarifying this association may help inform future research on nutritional assessment and intervention strategies in critical care.

METHODS

Study design and ethics

This study was a sub‐analysis of the ILOSS study, 12 a multicenter prospective observational study involving 21 ICUs in Japan. 13 For this sub‐analysis, we used data from 15 ICUs where detailed nutrition therapy information had been prospectively collected. The remaining 6 ICUs did not participate in nutritional data collection and were therefore not eligible for inclusion in this sub‐analysis. In the parent ILOSS study, a per‐site enrollment target of up to 20 cases was used to support balanced representation across participating sites and to limit center‐size imbalance. 14 The study protocol was approved by the central institutional review board (Saiseikai Utsunomiya Hospital, No. 2019‐72) and all local review boards. The parent ILOSS study was registered with the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000041433). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorized representatives. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. 15

Participants

In the parent ILOSS study, patients admitted to the ICU between November 2020 and April 2022 were included if they met the Sepsis‐3 criteria for sepsis or septic shock at admission. 13 , 16 The ILOSS protocol excluded patients based on the following criteria: age <18 years; a diagnosis of COVID‐19; discharge from the ICU within 48 h; a central nervous system disorder not caused by sepsis; inability to communicate due to pre‐existing psychiatric symptoms; inability to walk independently before admission; or being in an end‐of‐life or terminal stage.

For our sub‐analysis, we further included only patients with detailed nutritional data. We also applied two additional exclusion criteria for this analysis: (1) patients who underwent surgery before ICU admission—to avoid the confounding influence of surgical costs on the primary outcome (total in‐hospital costs) 17 —and (2) patients with missing in‐hospital cost data, as these lacked the necessary information for our primary outcome analysis.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was total in‐hospital costs derived from administrative claims data, converted to US dollars using the 2022 mean exchange rate (130 JPY/USD). Secondary outcomes included in‐hospital mortality; quality of life (QOL) score at discharge using the EuroQoL 5‐dimension 5‐level instrument, with death assigned a score of 0 18 ; length of hospital stay after ICU admission (LOS); length of ICU stay; and duration of mechanical ventilation.

Covariates

The exposure was GLIM‐defined malnutrition, and the comparator was non‐malnutrition, both assessed at ICU admission. 5 We collected the following covariates at ICU admission: age; sex; employment status before admission; admission year; body mass index (BMI); Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score for assessing comorbidities 19 ; and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score 16 to evaluate illness severity. We also gathered information on treatments administered upon ICU admission, including mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, inotropes, and sedation. Additionally, we recorded whether septic shock was present and identified the primary site of infection.

Definition of malnutrition

Malnutrition was defined at ICU admission using the GLIM criteria, which require the presence of at least one phenotypic and one etiologic criterion for a diagnosis. 5 We evaluated malnutrition at ICU admission because this time point is clinically relevant for early risk stratification and initial nutritional management. 7

(1)Phenotypic criteriaThe three phenotypic criteria were assessed as follows. Weight loss was defined as a reduction of ≥5% in 6 months. Low BMI was defined according to the GLIM criteria for Asian populations: BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 for patients <70 years and <20 kg/m2 for those ≥70 years. Reduced muscle mass was determined by registered dietitians using methods such as bioelectrical impedance analysis and computed tomography, 20 with the final clinical judgment recorded.

(2)Etiologic criteriaThe two etiologic criteria were defined as follows. Given the difficulty of accurately quantifying dietary intake before ICU admission in critically ill patients, reduced food intake was defined as ≤200 kcal/day—a pragmatic threshold approximating a typical basic meal unit in Japan. This threshold is also consistent with the clinical concept of trophic feeding in ICU nutrition therapy, which refers to a minimal level of energy intake intended to maintain gut integrity. 21 This information was prospectively collected by ICU dietitians through interviews with patients or their families. Inflammation was defined as a C‐reactive protein level ≥5 mg/dL upon ICU admission, based on the GLIM guidance for severe acute inflammation. 22

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Differences between groups were assessed using the Welch's t‐test, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, or the Kruskal‐Wallis test, depending on the number of groups and data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and associations were examined using the chi‐square test. Missing values were imputed using a random forest‐based algorithm. 23

(1)Primary analysisWe evaluated whether GLIM‐defined malnutrition was associated with classification into higher‐cost groups. Because healthcare cost data typically exhibit a right‐skewed distribution, standard linear regression is often inappropriate. 24 A common approach in health economics is to categorize costs based on percentiles to analyze the risk factors for high resource utilization. 17 , 25 , 26 We categorized costs into three groups: low (0–50th percentile), moderate (50–75th percentile), and high (75–100th percentile). We performed a multinomial logistic regression analysis to estimate the odds of being in the moderate‐ or high‐cost categories relative to the low‐cost reference category. The model was adjusted for key clinical confounders, including age, 27 sex, CCI, 28 and SOFA score 29 at ICU admission, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For comparison, the Wald test was used.To confirm the robustness of our findings, we performed the following sensitivity analyses: a complete case analysis to assess the potential impact of missing‐data handling; an analysis among patients discharged alive to assess the potential influence of in‐hospital mortality on cost outcomes; an analysis additionally adjusted for early enteral nutrition and adequate energy intake (defined in Figure 2) 30 ; and an analysis restricted to patients with LOS ≥ 7 days to verify that the overall results were consistent with the population receiving these nutrition therapies.Furthermore, to examine the contribution of each GLIM component to the primary outcome, we conducted an additional multinomial logistic regression analysis. In this analysis, each of the five individual components (phenotypic criteria: weight loss, low BMI, reduced muscle mass; etiologic criteria: reduced food intake, inflammation) was separately entered as the main explanatory variable into the model. This model was adjusted for the same clinical confounders: age, sex, CCI, and SOFA score at ICU admission.

After adjustment, GLIM‐defined malnutrition was significantly associated with classification into both the moderate‐cost (AOR: 2.03, 95% CI: 1.05–3.89, p = 0.03) and high‐cost groups (AOR: 2.27, 95% CI: 1.20–4.29, p = 0.01) compared with the low‐cost group (Table S3). We also examined the contribution of individual GLIM components to this association (Table 2). Among the phenotypic criteria, reduced muscle mass was significantly associated with the high‐cost group (AOR: 1.95, 95% CI: 1.04–3.68, p = 0.04). Among the etiologic criteria, reduced food intake was significantly associated with classification into the high‐cost group (AOR: 2.61, 95% CI: 1.39–4.92, p = 0.003). Neither of these components was significantly associated with the moderate‐cost group.

Secondary analysis

Patients were stratified into GLIM‐defined malnutrition (n = 111) and non‐malnutrition (n = 148) groups. Before weighting, several baseline characteristics were imbalanced between the groups; however, all covariates were well balanced after weighting, with SMDs ≤0.1 (Table S4).

The primary outcome, total in‐hospital costs, exhibited a right‐skewed distribution in both groups (Figure S2). The malnutrition group had higher median in‐hospital costs than the non‐malnutrition group (24,959 USD [IQR: 15,269–36,593] vs. 18,651 USD [IQR: 9,580–29,840], p = 0.01). Additionally, the malnutrition group had a longer median LOS (29.1 days [IQR: 16.3–46.8] vs. 21.5 days [IQR: 13.0–42.0], p = 0.04) and a longer median ICU stay (7.0 days [IQR: 4.6–11.0] vs. 5.0 days [IQR: 3.0–8.0], p = 0.01) than the non‐malnutrition group. There were no significant differences between the groups in in‐hospital mortality, QOL score at discharge, or duration of mechanical ventilation (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this sub‐analysis of a multicenter prospective study, GLIM‐defined malnutrition was associated with classification into the moderate‐ and high‐cost groups in the primary analysis. Among the individual GLIM components, reduced muscle mass and reduced food intake before ICU admission were associated with classification into the high‐cost group. In the secondary analysis, after IPTW adjustment, the median in‐hospital cost in the GLIM‐defined malnutrition group was significantly higher by approximately 6000 USD than in the non‐malnutrition group.

Given that sepsis imposes one of the highest healthcare and economic burdens, particularly in the ICU, 9 , 10 there is a growing need to identify cost drivers and evaluate cost‐effective interventions. 11 , 33 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and in‐hospital costs among critically ill patients with sepsis in the ICU. Previous studies, primarily from China and involving non‐sepsis populations, reported relative cost increases of 1.2‐ to 2.0‐fold between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and non‐malnutrition (Table S5). We observed a comparable relative increase (1.3‐fold) in our cohort. However, the absolute economic impact differed: the additional cost in our ICU sepsis cohort was approximately 6000 USD, exceeding the approximately 300 to 3500 USD reported previously (Table S5). These findings suggest that GLIM‐defined malnutrition may have a greater economic impact in more severe or clinically complex populations, including ICU sepsis, than in non‐sepsis cohorts.

GLIM‐defined malnutrition was associated with higher in‐hospital costs and longer hospital and ICU stays, consistent with previous studies suggesting that prolonged LOS is a key cost driver among patients with malnutrition (Table S5). However, GLIM‐defined malnutrition was not associated with in‐hospital mortality or discharge QOL score, suggesting that patients with malnutrition may have required longer hospitalization to reach a functional status sufficient for discharge.

Several mechanisms may contribute to the higher costs associated with malnutrition. Even after the acute inflammatory and catabolic phase has resolved, malnutrition may be accompanied by persistent immune dysfunction, impaired tissue repair, and anabolic resistance, 7 increasing susceptibility to infectious complications and often leading to prolonged antimicrobial therapy and longer hospital stays. 34 , 35 A food intake of ≤200 kcal/day, defined as reduced food intake in this study, 21 may be insufficient to maintain intestinal structural and functional integrity, potentially impairing gut‐associated lymphoid tissue function and promoting persistent systemic inflammation through bacterial and endotoxin translocation. 36 Reduced muscle mass may reduce the secretion of anti‐inflammatory myokines, further contributing to a pro‐inflammatory state. 37 Collectively, these mechanisms may facilitate the transition to chronic critical illness, a condition common among patients with sepsis, and requiring substantial healthcare resources. 38

Future work is needed in larger and more diverse populations to confirm the association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and healthcare expenditure, thereby helping to identify potential intervention targets that could shorten hospitalization among patients with sepsis and malnutrition. The association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and higher cost categories remained consistent after adjustment for early enteral nutrition and adequate energy intake, suggesting that the excess costs may not be fully explained by differences in post‐admission nutritional management. Because pre‐admission nutritional optimization is difficult in emergency sepsis care, future studies should evaluate whether early multidisciplinary strategies, including individualized nutritional support and rehabilitation, can shorten hospitalization and reduce costs in this population. 30 The observed cost difference of approximately 6000 USD may provide a preliminary benchmark for developing cost‐reduction strategies.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was modest, which constrained the number of covariates that could be included in the multivariable adjustment and may have limited statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses. To our knowledge, prior data on the association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and in‐hospital costs were lacking, making it difficult to estimate an appropriate effect size for a priori sample size calculation in critically ill patients with sepsis. However, this modest sample size was a trade‐off for our focus on a specific, homogeneous cohort of medically managed critically ill patients with sepsis. This specificity is a methodological strength, as it reduces the cost variability introduced by different diagnoses and surgery. 9 , 17 Second, although the GLIM criteria distinguish between moderate and severe malnutrition, 5 the present analysis did not evaluate outcomes according to malnutrition severity. Because severity data for reduced muscle mass were unavailable and severity thresholds for low BMI in Asian populations are not well established, malnutrition severity could not be determined consistently across all GLIM phenotypic components, precluding a severity‐based analysis. Third, given the observational nature of this study, the influence of unmeasured confounding factors cannot be fully excluded. Our findings should be interpreted as exploratory and associative rather than causal. Nevertheless, we attempted to mitigate confounding as rigorously as possible by employing multiple analytic approaches, including multinomial logistic regression, IPTW, and sensitivity analyses. The consistency of the association between GLIM‐defined malnutrition and high hospitalization costs across these methods supports the robustness of our findings. Fourth, this sub‐analysis was restricted to 15 of the 21 ICUs in the ILOSS study, where detailed nutritional data were collected, and the number of patients enrolled per site was modest. Furthermore, cost analyses were based on the Japanese healthcare system using a fixed exchange rate for interpretability, and both this study and the majority of prior reports were conducted in Asian populations (Table S5). These factors may limit the generalizability of our findings beyond the ICUs included in this analysis and to other settings with different ethnic backgrounds, healthcare systems, and reimbursement structures. Fifth, only total in‐hospital cost data were collected, and itemized data on individual components such as pharmacy, imaging, and nursing were unavailable. Therefore, specific cost drivers could not be identified. Finally, nutritional assessment was not fully standardized across participating hospitals. Reduced muscle mass was assessed using locally available techniques, and pre‐ICU food intake was estimated from interview‐based nutritional assessments. Furthermore, the 200 kcal/day threshold in reduced food intake differs from the cutoff generally recommended in the GLIM framework, 5 which may limit comparability with prior research. These procedures may have introduced interinstitutional variability and limited precision in GLIM component assessment. Future studies should use standardized tools, such as bioelectrical impedance analysis, computed tomography, or anthropometry, 39 to improve the validity and reproducibility of muscle mass assessment.

CONCLUSIONS

In medically managed patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU, GLIM‐defined malnutrition was associated with higher in‐hospital costs. These exploratory findings may support prioritizing patients with GLIM‐defined malnutrition as a target population for future studies examining cost‐reduction strategies in critical care.
