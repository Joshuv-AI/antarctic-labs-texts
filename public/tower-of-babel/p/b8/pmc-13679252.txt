In silico prioritization of potential matrix metalloproteinase-13 inhibitors for cartilage degeneration in knee injury-associated osteoarthritis
Authors: Aijuan Zhang, Hongmei Chen, Na Song, Juanjuan Zhuang, Baochai Chen, Xiuying Lin, Shuang Jin, Chen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Knee trauma is an important risk factor for post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA), a progressive joint disease characterized by articular cartilage degeneration and limited disease-modifying treatment options. Matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13/collagenase-3) contributes to cartilage extracellular matrix degradation through cleavage of type II collagen, making it a relevant molecular target for cartilage-protective drug discovery. This study aimed to identify and prioritize potential small-molecule MMP-13 inhibitors using an integrated in silico workflow. The workflow included MMP-13 sequence retrieval, structural characterization, pharmacophore-based screening, molecular docking, ADMET/toxicity prediction, protein-ligand interaction analysis, protein-protein interaction and co-expression network analysis, and 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation. Among the screened compounds, P4C-BM was prioritized as an early-stage computational lead candidate based on its combined docking score, predicted ADMET profile, interaction pattern, and molecular dynamics stability. However, the findings are preliminary and computational only. The present study does not experimentally demonstrate direct MMP-13 inhibition, selectivity against other MMP family members, cartilage repair, clinical efficacy, or synergy with platelet-rich plasma, physiotherapy, or other treatment modalities. Among the screened compounds, P4C-BM was prioritized as an early-stage computational lead candidate based on its combined docking score, predicted ADMET profile, interaction pattern, and molecular dynamics stability. However, the findings are preliminary and computational only. The present study does not experimentally demonstrate direct MMP-13 inhibition, selectivity against other MMP family members, cartilage protection, cartilage repair, disease modification, or clinical efficacy. Therefore, P4C-BM should be regarded only as a candidate for further experimental validation in enzymatic assays, chondrocyte models, cartilage explant studies, and in vivo osteoarthritis or post-traumatic osteoarthritis models. No practice-related or treatment recommendations can be made from the present data.

Introduction

Knee injuries are clinically important initiating events for post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA), a progressive form of osteoarthritis (OA) that develops after structural or mechanical damage to the joint. In the present study, OA is considered the degenerative disease endpoint, whereas knee injury is considered the initiating condition that predisposes the joint to PTOA. Therefore, the focus of this work is not restricted to primary knee OA or to anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture alone, but rather to cartilage degeneration after traumatic and mechanically destabilizing knee injuries [1–4].

Several knee injuries and joint alterations can contribute to PTOA development, including ACL rupture, meniscal tears, meniscal instability, chondral defects, osteochondral injury, ligamentous instability, and altered joint loading [5, 6]. ACL rupture has been widely investigated because it produces abnormal tibiofemoral kinematics and increases cartilage loading; however, meniscal pathology is also a major contributor to OA progression. The menisci are essential for load transmission, shock absorption, lubrication, and joint stability. Meniscal tears, meniscal extrusion, and post-meniscectomy changes can increase focal cartilage contact stress and accelerate matrix degeneration. Experimental and translational studies using meniscal injury or medial meniscus instability models further support the importance of meniscus-related pathways in knee OA development [4].

The biological progression from knee injury to PTOA involves both mechanical and inflammatory mechanisms. Immediately after injury, abnormal joint loading, cartilage impact, subchondral bone damage, and synovial inflammation can initiate extracellular matrix disruption. These events are accompanied by increased oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α, which promote a catabolic chondrocyte phenotype and stimulate matrix-degrading enzymes [4, 7]. As a result, the balance between extracellular matrix synthesis and degradation shifts toward progressive cartilage breakdown.

Articular cartilage is composed mainly of type II collagen, aggrecan, and proteoglycan-rich extracellular matrix, which together provide tensile strength, compressive resistance, and load distribution within the knee joint [3, 8, 9]. Because mature articular cartilage is avascular, aneural, and has limited intrinsic regenerative capacity, enzymatic degradation of the collagen-proteoglycan network after injury can have long-term structural consequences [3, 10]. Once the type II collagen framework is disrupted, cartilage repair becomes difficult and the tissue becomes more susceptible to progressive degeneration.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are zinc-dependent endopeptidases involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and degradation. They include collagenases, gelatinases, stromelysins, and membrane-type MMPs [5, 6]. Under physiological conditions, MMP activity is tightly regulated by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). However, in osteoarthritic cartilage and after traumatic knee injury, mechanical stress, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) can increase the expression and activity of matrix-degrading enzymes and alter the MMP/TIMP balance, thereby shifting cartilage metabolism toward ECM degradation rather than matrix repair [7, 8]. Among these enzymes, matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13), also known as collagenase-3, is particularly important in osteoarthritic cartilage because it preferentially cleaves type II collagen, the principal collagenous scaffold of articular cartilage [11–14].

Following traumatic or mechanically destabilizing knee injury, abnormal joint loading, synovial inflammation, inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress can shift cartilage metabolism toward a catabolic state [4, 7]. Within this context, matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13) is particularly relevant because it preferentially cleaves type II collagen, the principal structural collagen of articular cartilage, and contributes to extracellular matrix degradation, aggrecan loss, collagen network disruption, chondrocyte dysfunction, and progressive osteoarthritic cartilage degeneration [11–14]. Clinical and translational studies have also associated MMP-13 expression or circulating levels with osteoarthritis severity and cartilage degeneration, supporting its relevance as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in knee injury-associated post-traumatic osteoarthritis [15–18].

Current treatment strategies for knee injury-related cartilage degeneration remain limited. Conservative treatment, rehabilitation, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, intra-articular corticosteroids, viscosupplementation, platelet-rich plasma (PRP), microfracture, and cell-based approaches may improve pain, function, or repair responses in selected patients; however, their structural outcomes are variable and their long-term disease-modifying effects remain uncertain [12–16]. Although regenerative approaches such as mesenchymal stem cell-based therapy, PRP, and microfracture have shown clinical potential, current evidence does not consistently demonstrate sustained modulation of catabolic pathways such as matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) - or a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS)-mediated extracellular matrix degradation. Therefore, additional disease-modifying strategies targeting key cartilage-degrading enzymes remain necessary.

Broad-spectrum matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) inhibitors have shown limited clinical translation because of poor target selectivity and dose-limiting adverse effects. A major limitation of non-selective MMP inhibition is musculoskeletal syndrome (MSS), which may include arthralgia, myalgia, joint stiffness, tendon pain, tendinopathy, and reduced mobility. These effects are thought to arise from inhibition of multiple MMP family members involved in normal connective tissue remodeling, rather than selective inhibition of a disease-relevant collagenase. Therefore, selective MMP-13-targeted strategies may offer a more rational approach for reducing cartilage matrix degradation while minimizing off-target musculoskeletal toxicity [3, 17, 18].

Therapeutically, selective MMP-13 inhibition is considered a rational cartilage-protective strategy because it may reduce type II collagen cleavage and slow extracellular matrix (ECM) breakdown in osteoarthritis (OA) and knee injury-associated post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA). However, candidate compounds still require experimental validation in enzymatic assays, chondrocyte models, cartilage explants, and in vivo OA/PTOA models before clinical translation [9, 19]. In addition, recent computational and medicinal chemistry studies have supported the feasibility of developing selective MMP-13 inhibitors with improved target selectivity compared with broad-spectrum MMP inhibitors [9, 20, 21]. However, these compounds still require experimental validation in enzymatic assays, chondrocyte models, cartilage explants, and in vivo OA/PTOA models before clinical translation.

Computational drug discovery provides an efficient early-stage strategy for identifying and prioritizing candidate MMP-13 inhibitors before experimental validation. Structure-based pharmacophore screening, molecular docking, ADMET prediction, protein-ligand interaction analysis, and molecular dynamics simulation can be combined to evaluate binding affinity, interaction stability, and predicted pharmacokinetic behavior [19, 22–24]. Such approaches can reduce the number of compounds requiring synthesis or biological testing and can support rational lead optimization.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify and prioritize potential small-molecule inhibitors of human MMP-13 for cartilage-protective applications in osteoarthritis and knee injury-associated post-traumatic osteoarthritis. An integrated in silico workflow was used, including MMP-13 structural characterization, pharmacophore-based screening, molecular docking, ADMET prediction, molecular interaction analysis, protein-protein interaction and gene co-expression network analysis, and molecular dynamics simulation. The study was designed as an early-stage computational screening investigation. It does not include patient-level data, clinical outcomes, biomarker validation, nursing-related endpoints, or experimental confirmation of MMP-13 inhibition. Therefore, the results should be interpreted only as hypothesis-generating computational evidence requiring further biological and preclinical validation.

Materials and methods

Identification and sequence retrieval of target protein

Human matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13/collagenase-3) was selected as the target protein based on its established role in type II collagen degradation and osteoarthritis-related cartilage extracellular matrix breakdown, as described in the Introduction. No formal systematic review was performed; therefore, the wording has been revised to avoid implying a systematic review methodology. The canonical human MMP-13 protein sequence was retrieved from the UniProt database using accession number P45452 (MMP13_HUMAN). The FASTA sequence corresponding to the 471-amino-acid human MMP-13 protein was downloaded and used for downstream physicochemical characterization, secondary-structure prediction, tertiary-structure modeling, docking preparation, and molecular dynamics simulation.

To improve reproducibility, all computational analyses were reported with the following information where applicable: database or software name, accession number or input identifier, input file format, prediction settings, filtering criteria, ranking criteria, simulation time, force field, solvent model, box type, ion conditions, ensemble, temperature, pressure, trajectory-saving interval, and main output parameters used for interpretation.

Physicochemical profiling of MMP-13 protein

The EXPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to perform physicochemical characterization of MMP-13 (UniProt P45452) and provided sequence-derived parameters such as molecular weight, theoretical pI, amino acid composition, instability index, aliphatic index and grand average hydropathicity (GRAVY) as the result of empirical algorithms, which are calibrated with experimental data. These metrics play a vital role in the design of MMP-13 inhibitors to assess the target tractability, which unveils thermostability (high aliphatic index), solubility profiles (GRAVY), and formulation feasibility [25, 26].

Comparative secondary structure profiling of MMP-13 protein

The secondary structure of MMP-13 was analyzed through the use of SOPMA, which is available on the NPS@ server (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) and PSIPRED (https://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/), which is hosted by University College London. SOPMA has a homology-based alignment approach and has tunable parametric options like the 17-residue window and 25% sequence similarity threshold, as well as four conformational states (helical, sheeted, turned, coiled) and has an approximately 69.5% Q3 accuracy based on non-homologous datasets when combined with a joint neural network. PSIPRED uses two stages of a feed-forward neural network trained on PSI-BLAST generated PSSM, with a benchmark Q3 accuracy ranging from 70.6% to 82.7%, based on structural profile integrations using EVA sets [27, 28].

Integrated MMP-13 quality assessment for drug target reliability

Although multiple experimentally resolved human MMP-13 structures are available in the Protein Data Bank, AlphaFold/GalaxyRefine modeling was performed only as a full-sequence structural characterization and comparison step. The AlphaFold model was not used to suggest that experimental MMP-13 structures are unavailable, nor was it considered superior to experimentally resolved structures for binding-site analysis. Experimentally resolved MMP-13 structures, including inhibitor-bound crystal structures such as PDB ID: 1XUC, were used as the structural reference for pharmacophore generation and binding-site interpretation. The AlphaFold/GalaxyRefine model was retained only to provide a standardized full-sequence model corresponding to the UniProt P45452 sequence and to compare the predicted structural features with available experimental MMP-13 structural information.

Quality factor assessment of MMP-13 refined and unrefined model

ERRAT validation of the refined MMP-13 (UniProt P45452) tertiary structure was carried out using the ERRAT web server (https://www.doe-mbi.ucla.edu/errat/). The ERRAT Server evaluates the non-bonded atomic interactions of a protein through a sliding 9-residue window, comparing the distribution of errors (as defined by using crystal structures of the highest levels of refinement) to find the regions of non-bonded contact which have been rejected at the 95% (yellow) and 99% (red) confidence levels. The PDB file generated by the Alpha Fold/GalaxyRefine submission showed an overall quality factor of greater than 90% (acceptable threshold for homology models is greater than 50%), and had only minimal areas of high error in loop regions surrounding the catalytic Zn2 + site [29, 30].

Structure-based pharmacophore generation for MMP-13 inhibitor screening

Structure-based pharmacophore analysis was performed using the Pharmit web server. Because experimentally resolved MMP-13 structures are available in the Protein Data Bank, the inhibitor-bound MMP-13 crystal structure PDB ID: 1XUC was used as the structural reference for pharmacophore generation and binding-site interpretation. The pharmacophore model was generated from ligand-binding features within the MMP-13 binding region and included hydrophobic, aromatic, hydrogen-bond donor, and hydrogen-bond acceptor features. The AlphaFold/GalaxyRefine model was not used to replace the experimental structure; it was retained only for full-sequence structural characterization and comparison.

Ligand binding site identification of MMP-13 targeted protein

By using the PrankWeb online tool (https://prankweb.cz/), the active site prediction of MMP-13 was completed through the use of the P2Rank machine learning method, which identifies ligand binding sites based on the solvent-accessible surface points with chemical neighborhood ligand ability on the 3D structure of the protein. The MMP-13 refined structure was uploaded to PrankWeb, which predicted the ligand binding pockets by clustering those with the highest ligand ability scores. Optionally, it also used evolutionary conservation to help with accuracy [31, 32].

Pharmacophore screening and candidate ligand selection

Pharmacophore-based screening was performed using Pharmit, and candidate ligand structures were retrieved from PubChem. The screened ligand set included Mmp13-IN-3 and P4DC/P4C-series compounds. The PubChem identifiers of the candidate compounds are listed in Table 5. These compounds were retained for subsequent ADMET prediction and molecular docking analyses.

ADMET and Lipinski rule-of-five assessment

ADMET/toxicity screening was performed using TOXCSM, and drug-likeness was assessed using Lipinski rule-of-five criteria. Among the evaluated compounds, several candidates were accepted by the ADMET screen, whereas others were rejected based on predicted toxicity or drug-likeness criteria. P4C-BM was accepted by the ADMET screen but did not fully satisfy Lipinski rule-of-five criteria. Therefore, P4C-BM was retained as an early-stage computational lead candidate rather than as an optimized orally bioavailable drug-like compound. The ADMET and Lipinski results are summarized in Table 6 and Figs. 7 and 8.

SwissADME BOILED-Egg analysis of P4C-BM

SwissADME analysis indicated that P4C-BM has a molecular weight of 594.62 g/mol, consensus LogP of 2.03, and topological polar surface area of 184.68 Å2. These values indicate that P4C-BM falls outside some classical oral drug-likeness ranges, mainly because of its relatively high molecular weight and polarity. The BOILED-Egg prediction and bioavailability radar are shown in Fig. 9. These results should be interpreted as predicted pharmacokinetic descriptors only and require experimental pharmacokinetic validation.

Molecular docking analysis

Blind molecular docking was performed using CB-Dock2. The predicted binding energies ranged from −7.2 to −9.4 kcal/mol. P4DC-FMePh-TzTri showed the most favorable predicted docking score (−9.4 kcal/mol), followed by P4C-BM (−8.9 kcal/mol), P4DC-IM (−8.8 kcal/mol), and P4DC-FPh-TzPh (−8.7 kcal/mol). Because P4C-BM showed a combined profile of predicted ADMET acceptance, favorable docking score, interaction pattern, and molecular dynamics stability, it was retained for further analysis. These docking scores should be interpreted as preliminary computational ranking values rather than experimentally validated binding affinities or confirmed inhibitory activity.

No formal docking-protocol validation was performed by extracting and redocking a co-crystallized ligand into the MMP-13 binding site. Therefore, the docking scores and predicted binding poses should be interpreted as preliminary computational ranking results rather than as a fully validated prediction of binding-pose accuracy. Future work should validate the docking workflow by redocking a known MMP-13 inhibitor from an experimentally resolved inhibitor-bound structure and assessing pose reproducibility using an RMSD-based criterion.

Protein-ligand interaction analysis

Discovery Studio analysis of the MMP-13–P4C-BM docking pose identified conventional hydrogen bonds involving HIS24 and GLN195, as well as additional carbon hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts involving residues including PRO166, GLY169, GLY194, LEU20, ALA2, and LEU136. These interactions provide a structural rationale for the predicted docking pose of P4C-BM. However, interaction mapping does not demonstrate enzymatic inhibition, selectivity, or biological activity.

STRING protein-protein interaction analysis

STRING protein-protein interaction analysis was used to provide biological context for MMP-13. The network included cartilage- and extracellular-matrix-associated proteins such as TIMP1, TIMP2, ADAMTS4, ADAMTS5, ACAN, COL10A1, COL1A1, RUNX2, and PLG. These associations support the biological relevance of MMP-13 in extracellular matrix remodeling and cartilage degeneration pathways. However, STRING analysis was not used to rank compounds and does not provide evidence that P4C-BM inhibits MMP-13.

The STRING protein-protein interaction and co-expression analyses were performed to contextualize the biological role of MMP-13 in extracellular matrix remodeling and cartilage degeneration pathways. These analyses were not used as compound-ranking or inhibitor-validation tools. Instead, they were used to examine whether MMP-13 was associated with cartilage-related matrix regulation networks and to support the biological relevance of selecting MMP-13 as the target protein.

Gene co-expression analysis

STRING co-expression analysis was used to examine genes associated with MMP13-related extracellular matrix regulation. The analysis identified co-expression associations with matrix-regulatory and cartilage-related genes. These findings provide biological context for MMP-13 as a target but do not validate P4C-BM activity or selectivity.

Molecular dynamics simulation

A single 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed for the MMP-13–P4C-BM complex. Protein backbone RMSD, ligand RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, hydrogen-bond/contact persistence, and protein-ligand contact timelines were used to assess trajectory stability. The simulation suggested that the docked complex remained relatively stable during the 100 ns trajectory. However, replicate MD simulations were not performed, and these results should be interpreted as supportive computational evidence only, not as replicated proof of binding stability or biological activity.

Because only one 100 ns trajectory was performed, the molecular dynamics results do not establish statistical reproducibility of the MMP-13–P4C-BM complex stability. Independent replicate simulations using different initial velocity seeds are required to confirm whether the observed RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, hydrogen-bond persistence, and protein-ligand contact patterns are reproducible.

Active sites prediction

The PRANKWEB algorithm was used as the active site prediction of MMP-13, the algorithm of active site prediction relies on machine learning-based pocket detection, which determines the locations of ligand-binding cavities with a high degree of spatial resolution. The evaluation showed that there was one major binding site (Site 1) whose chance of occurrence was 0.026 and included 7 key residues with the center at (19.1050, 17.3327, 23.5710), which is typical of a conserved S1 o subsite of a matrix metalloproteinase. This predicted pocket is correlated with crystallographic evidence of the catalytic zinc coordination sphere and specificity loop (Ω -loop) regions of collagenase activity in which inhibitors take advantage of hydrophobic interactions with Tyr244, Pro242, and Thr247. This pocket-mapping step was used only to identify a putative ligand-binding region for MMP-13 and to support subsequent docking of candidate compounds. The present study did not perform comparative docking, binding free-energy calculations, or enzymatic selectivity testing against other MMP family members such as MMP-1, MMP-8, MMP-2, or MMP-9. Therefore, selectivity over other MMPs cannot be concluded from the present data. Computational pharmacophore approaches in Novel inhibitor development

MMP-13-targeting candidate ligands were identified using the Pharmit online tool in pharmacophore mode, and the three-dimensional structures of the selected ligands were retrieved from the PubChem database. The pharmacophore model included hydrophobic regions, hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor features, and aromatic moieties that may contribute to ligand recognition within the predicted MMP-13 binding region. The identified ligands, including Mmp13-IN-3 and different P4DC/P4C-series compounds, matched the selected pharmacophore features and were therefore retained for subsequent computational analysis. However, pharmacophore matching alone does not demonstrate inhibitory activity, selectivity, or biological efficacy. These compounds should be considered screening candidates only, requiring docking analysis, ADMET prediction, molecular dynamics evaluation, and ultimately experimental validation before any conclusion regarding MMP-13 inhibition can be made. Quantitative ADMET analysis for prioritizing MMP-13 inhibitor candidates

The systematically performed ADMET profiling of new Collagenase 3 (MMP-13) inhibitors was carried out with the use of the TOXCSM platform, which relies on the graph-based molecular signatures in predicting pharmacokinetic liabilities and toxicity endpoints. Among tested P4DC series derivatives, 14 of 21 (66.7) compounds passed through the full battery of toxicity analysis of TOXCSM and only 9 (42.9) compounds met both criteria of TOXCSM and Lipinski Rule of Five, signaling a severe bottleneck in drug attrition just beyond the drug-like range. It is interesting to note that the P4DC-FMAO, P4DC-AF, P4DC-FOH-DMA, P4C-CFE, and P4DC-ACHPh-FPh were dual-compliant leads with desirable absorption, low hepatotoxicity risk, and low mutagenic potential whereas poor solubility profiles rejected as Mmp13-IN-3 and P4DC-FMePh-TzTri. This selectivity highlights the usefulness of TOXCSM in prioritizing MMP-13-inhibitors as osteoarthritis-therapeutic agents and in revealing structural determinant of pharmacokinetic failure to inform further medicinal chemistry optimization. Interpretation of protein-ligand stability and dynamics simulation

A single 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed for the MMP-13–P4C-BM complex. Protein backbone RMSD, ligand RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, hydrogen-bond/contact persistence, and protein-ligand contact timelines were used to assess trajectory stability. The simulation suggested that the docked complex remained relatively stable during the 100 ns trajectory. However, replicate MD simulations were not performed; therefore, these results should be interpreted as supportive computational evidence only.

Protein-Ligand Contact RMSD: Root mean Square deviation (RMSD) of the protein-ligand complex at the 100ns time interval during the simulation showed that the protein backbone RMSD had reached a stabilization of approximately the 2.0 A level, which had indicated that the structural convergence and stability had been achieved throughout the trajectory. The superimposition of the ligand RMSD on proteins backbone was found to fluctuate within a limit of less than 3.0 A which implies that the ligand is still in the constant conformation where the ligand is not diffusing at the binding site. Such stability implies that there are good binding dynamics that were maintained during the simulation.

Protein-Ligand Contact Histogram and Timeline: Specific protein-ligand contacts analysis through the simulation showed that the hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic as well as ionic interaction with the residues like HIS24, LEU128, GLN195 and PHE206 remained constant. In the contact histogram, it was determined that the biggest type of interaction was hydrogen bonds, then hydrophobic contacts. These contacts were determined to have been sustained with some residues remaining in constant contact with more than 70% of the simulation period, and marking important stabilizing residues in the ligand binding pocket. Protein RMSF, Timeline, and Histogram. The dynamic behavior of root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) profiling of protein residues was expected: high flexibility in terminal regions and loop segments, including the first 10 and 190 residues, and much lower fluctuations in secondary structure elements like alpha helices and beta strands (RMSF < 1.0 A). The stability of the core folding motifs was determined by the RMSF histogram and the timeline and also showed local variations around the binding site which could be involved in the accommodation of the ligand and its binding plasticity.

Ligand RMSF, Properties, and Torsion Profile: The ligand RMSF analysis presented little oscillations (less than 2.5 A) of the heavy atoms with the flex torsional bond being found in the rotatable alkyl and amine groups. The ligand property assessments showed that the values of radius of gyration (rGyr) are stable (around 5.6 A), which also points to the stabilized conformation of the ligand. The intramolecular hydrogen bond count was also constant (approximately 2–3) and this led to the stabilization of the ligand conformation. Torsion profiles of rotatable bonds revealed clear preferred conformations with minimal energetic strain, which prove a stable bound conformation in accordance with geometry of the protein pockets.

MM-GBSA Binding Free Energy Analysis: The MM-GBSA calculated binding free energy (ΔG bind ΔG bind) averaged at about −44.7 kcal/mol in the last simulation frames suggesting that there is a strong thermodynamic affinity of P4C-BM towards MMP13. This was found to be positive in the energy components analysis, with van der Waals forces and electrostatic hydrogen bonding making positive contributions that were partially compensated by polar solvation penalties. The strain energy of the ligands was very low which proves that an energetically favorable ligand conformation is present in the binding site.

Hydrogen Bond Energy and Dynamics: The distribution of the energy of hydrogen bond analysis revealed that the average stabilization energy of one bond was about −6.0 kcal/mol, which is significant and shows the necessity of hydrogen bonding in the integrity of complexes. The time-dependent study of the patterns of hydrogen bonding indicated a steady formation of bonds and a few transient hydrogen interactions especially on the residues HIS24 and GLN195. These hydrogen bond associates with hydrophobic forces to hold ligand occupancy amidst movement of the proteins through dynamic fluctuations.

This detailed MD simulation study proves the structural stability, and energetic favorability of the MMP13–P4C-BM complex, details essential residue contacts and ligand conformational flexibility, which form the basis of binding affinity and specificity. Discussion

Cartilage degeneration after knee injury and during osteoarthritis (OA) is driven by interacting mechanical, inflammatory, and biochemical processes. Among the enzymes involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) turnover, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are important mediators of cartilage matrix degradation [33–35]. Matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13/collagenase-3) is particularly relevant because it cleaves type II collagen, a major structural collagen of articular cartilage, and has been widely investigated as a therapeutic target in OA [11–14, 36]. However, the development of selective MMP-13 inhibitors remains challenging because MMP family members share conserved catalytic domains and overlapping binding-site features, which may increase the risk of poor selectivity and off-target effects [37, 38].

Clinical and translational evidence also supports the relevance of MMP-13 in OA and knee injury-associated joint degeneration. Elamir et al. reported that circulating MMP-3 and MMP-13 levels were associated with radiographic severity in patients with primary knee OA [6, 39]. In addition, Xin et al. reported the potential value of MMP-13 as a biomarker in OA, supporting its association with disease-related cartilage degradation [40, 41]. Importantly, MMP-13 may also be relevant in meniscal pathology. Olivotto et al. reported that meniscal MMP-13 expression was associated with increased preoperative pain in patients undergoing arthroscopic partial meniscectomy for meniscal tears [36, 42–45]. This is relevant to the present study because meniscal tears are an important contributor to altered joint loading and OA progression, and it suggests that MMP-13-targeted strategies may be worth investigating not only in OA cartilage degeneration but also in symptomatic meniscal injury. However, this remains a translational hypothesis and requires experimental and clinical validation.

Previous studies have explored MMP-13 inhibition using different approaches, including medicinal chemistry, computational screening, local delivery systems, and preclinical OA models [36, 39, 40, 45]. Local or controlled delivery approaches are particularly relevant because broad-spectrum MMP inhibitors have historically been limited by poor selectivity and dose-limiting adverse effects, including musculoskeletal syndrome [17, 18, 36]. Reviews by Hu and Ecker and by Wan et al. further support MMP-13 as a promising OA target while emphasizing the continuing challenges of selectivity, delivery, bioavailability, and clinical translation [44, 45]. Therefore, selective MMP-13 inhibition remains a rational but still experimental strategy for cartilage-protective intervention.

The present study used an integrated in silico workflow to prioritize candidate MMP-13 inhibitors. This workflow included pharmacophore-based screening, molecular docking, ADMET/toxicity prediction, molecular interaction analysis, protein-protein interaction and gene co-expression analysis, and 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The docking results showed that P4DC-FMePh-TzTri had the most favorable predicted binding energy among the screened compounds, whereas P4C-BM also showed a favorable predicted docking score. However, P4C-BM was prioritized because lead selection was based on combined criteria rather than docking energy alone, including predicted toxicity profile, drug-likeness considerations, molecular interaction pattern, and MD stability.

The ADMET and drug-likeness results should be interpreted cautiously. P4C-BM was accepted in the ADMET screening but did not fully satisfy Lipinski rule-of-five criteria. Therefore, P4C-BM should not be described as an optimized oral drug-like compound at this stage. Instead, it should be considered a computational lead candidate that may require medicinal chemistry optimization or local delivery strategies before biological testing. This interpretation is consistent with the broader challenge of developing selective MMP-13 inhibitors with acceptable pharmacokinetic and safety profiles [36, 42–45].

The protein-ligand interaction analysis indicated that the MMP-13–P4C-BM complex was stabilized by polar and hydrophobic interactions. Conventional hydrogen bonds were observed with HIS24 and GLN195, while additional carbon hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions involved residues including PRO166, GLY169, GLY194, LEU20, ALA2, and LEU136. These interactions suggest that P4C-BM can occupy the predicted MMP-13 binding region in a stable docking pose. Nevertheless, docking and interaction mapping do not prove enzymatic inhibition; they only provide a structural rationale for selecting compounds for further testing.

The 100 ns MD simulation provided additional support for the stability of the MMP-13–P4C-BM complex. The protein backbone RMSD remained within an acceptable range during the simulation, and the ligand RMSD suggested that P4C-BM did not undergo major displacement from the binding region. RMSF analysis indicated greater flexibility in terminal and loop regions, while more structured regions remained comparatively stable. These observations are consistent with a dynamically stable protein-ligand complex, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of biological efficacy, cartilage repair, or clinical benefit.

The STRING protein-protein interaction and co-expression analyses were used to provide biological context for MMP-13 rather than to directly identify or validate inhibitors. These analyses showed that MMP-13 is connected with extracellular matrix remodeling and cartilage-related molecular networks, including tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases, aggrecan/collagen-related components, and other matrix-degrading enzymes. Therefore, the network analysis supports the biological relevance of MMP-13 as a target in cartilage degeneration. However, it does not provide direct evidence that P4C-BM inhibits MMP-13, improves selectivity, or produces cartilage-protective effects. Lead prioritization in the present study was based on pharmacophore screening, docking score, ADMET/toxicity prediction, protein-ligand interaction analysis, and molecular dynamics stability. The STRING and co-expression results should therefore be interpreted only as target-context analyses.

The present findings should be interpreted within the limits of a computational drug-discovery workflow. Although P4C-BM showed a favorable combined profile in docking, predicted ADMET analysis, protein-ligand interaction mapping, and molecular dynamics simulation, these results do not confirm direct enzymatic inhibition of MMP-13 or biological activity in cartilage tissue. The study also did not evaluate selectivity against other MMP family members, including MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-8, or MMP-9. Therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding therapeutic efficacy, cartilage repair, disease modification, clinical utility, or practice-related application. Further validation using biochemical inhibition assays, selectivity testing, chondrocyte-based experiments, cartilage explant models, and in vivo osteoarthritis or post-traumatic osteoarthritis models is required before any translational significance can be established.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several important limitations. First, all findings are based on computational prediction and have not been experimentally validated. Second, molecular docking scores and molecular dynamics stability do not prove enzymatic inhibition, target selectivity, cartilage protection, or therapeutic efficacy. Third, no biochemical MMP-13 inhibition assay, MMP-family selectivity assay, chondrocyte experiment, cartilage explant model, animal model, patient-level dataset, biomarker validation, or clinical outcome assessment was performed. Fourth, the ADMET and toxicity findings are predictive and require experimental pharmacokinetic and toxicological confirmation. Therefore, the prioritized compound should be considered only an early-stage computational lead candidate for future validation, not a clinically applicable inhibitor. As only one 100 ns trajectory was generated, the MD results do not establish statistical reproducibility of the MMP-13–P4C-BM complex stability. Independent replicate simulations with different initial velocity seeds are required to confirm whether the observed RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, hydrogen-bond persistence, and protein-ligand contact patterns are reproducible. In addition, the docking protocol was not validated through redocking of a co-crystallized ligand, and the MD analysis was not replicated; therefore, confidence in the predicted binding pose and trajectory stability remains limited.

Conclusion

This study used an integrated in silico workflow to identify and prioritize potential small-molecule inhibitors of MMP-13, a cartilage-degrading enzyme involved in osteoarthritis and knee injury-associated post-traumatic osteoarthritis. Among the screened compounds, P4C-BM showed a favorable combined computational profile, including acceptable predicted ADMET characteristics, a favorable docking score, relevant protein-ligand interactions, and molecular dynamics stability. However, these results remain preliminary and computational only. The present study does not demonstrate direct MMP-13 inhibition, selectivity over other MMP family members, cartilage protection, cartilage repair, disease modification, or clinical efficacy. Experimental validation in enzymatic assays, MMP-family selectivity assays, chondrocyte models, cartilage explants, replicated molecular dynamics simulations, docking redocking validation, and in vivo osteoarthritis or post-traumatic osteoarthritis models is required before further lead development can be considered [46–57].
