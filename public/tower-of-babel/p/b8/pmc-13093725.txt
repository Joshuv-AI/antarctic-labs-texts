Multivariate patterns of plasma biomarkers predict region‐specific Alzheimer's pathology and cognitive decline across independent cohorts
Authors: Jafar Zamani, Amirali Vahid, Edward N Wilson, S M Hadi Hosseini, the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by the progressive accumulation of amyloid‐beta (Aβ) plaques, tau tangles, and neurodegeneration (ATN framework). While neuroimaging detects these changes, it is costly and not widely accessible. Blood‐based biomarkers offer scalable alternatives for early detection.METHODSWe integrated plasma biomarkers, neuroimaging, and cognitive data from two large cohorts. We examined multivariate patterns of plasma Aβ42/Aβ40, phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) 217, p‐tau181, neurofilament light (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) that predict amyloid, tau, neurodegeneration, and memory decline.RESULTSSpecific plasma biomarker combinations predicted amyloid burden in the precuneus, tau in the entorhinal cortex, hippocampal atrophy, and memory decline. Models trained in one cohort accurately predicted outcomes in the other, confirming cross‐cohort generalizability.DISCUSSIONDistinct plasma signatures reflect underlying AD pathways, supporting the use of blood‐based biomarkers as cost‐effective, noninvasive tools for early detection, disease monitoring, and individualized risk profiling.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease (AD), ATN framework, pathology, plasma biomarkers, predictive models

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the leading cause of dementia, is characterized by progressive cognitive decline and distinct neuropathological changes. 1 The hallmark histopathological features include amyloid‐beta (Aβ) deposition and hyperphosphorylated tau proteins. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Biomarkers of these pathological processes are systematically categorized under the ATN framework, which classifies Aβ deposition (A), tau pathology (T), and neurodegeneration (N). 7 Early diagnosis of AD primarily relies on detecting Aβ pathology through Aβ positron emission tomography (PET), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assays, or blood‐based markers. 8 Tau PET imaging is used to track tau pathology progression, while neurodegeneration is assessed through structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and increasingly via plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL), a sensitive marker of axonal damage. 8 However, while PET, MRI, and CSF assays have been pivotal in understanding ATN pathology, their high cost, limited accessibility, and invasive nature (e.g., lumbar puncture for CSF collection) restrict their widespread clinical use. 9

Plasma‐based biomarkers have emerged as promising, cost‐effective, and minimally invasive alternatives to CSF and imaging markers. 8 Neuropathological processes in AD trigger neuronal damage and glial activation, releasing brain‐derived proteins into the bloodstream even without overt blood–brain barrier disruption. 10 , 11 Recent analytical advancements have enabled the quantification of plasma biomarkers such as Aβ42/Aβ40, 12 , 13 phosphorylated tau isoforms (p‐tau217 and p‐tau181), 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 NfL, 18 and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), 19 which demonstrate strong correlations with their CSF and imaging counterparts. 16 , 18 , 20 Plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 is a robust indicator of cerebral Aβ burden. 20 Plasma p‐tau217 and p‐tau181 are closely associated with Aβ pathology and, to a lesser extent, tau PET positivity, 14 , 15 , 16 , 21 although their specificity for tau tangle pathology remains under investigation. Plasma GFAP reflects astrocytic activation and neuroinflammation, 22 , 23 while plasma NfL captures axonal injury and correlates with hippocampal atrophy and other neurodegenerative changes. 18 , 19 , 20 Together, these biomarkers offer scalable tools for early detection, disease staging, and longitudinal monitoring in both clinical and research settings. 14 , 21 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30

Plasma p‐tau181 distinguishes AD from frontotemporal lobar degeneration, 14 while plasma p‐tau217 differentiates AD from other neurodegenerative diseases. 16 When combined with age and apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, plasma p‐tau217 outperforms the plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 in predicting Aβ PET status. Mattsson‐Carlgren et al. 21 reported that plasma p‐tau217 is linked to both Aβ and tau, with stronger associations with Aβ in early disease and with both pathologies in later stages. Plasma GFAP and NfL provide complementary insights into neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative processes. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30

Despite these advances, critical gaps remain in understanding how plasma biomarkers correspond to specific ATN features across brain regions central to AD progression. 6 To address these gaps, this study leverages multimodal data from two independent cohorts: the Anti‐Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer's (A4) study and the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 While prior studies often analyzed plasma, imaging, or cognitive data separately, our work simultaneously integrates multiple plasma biomarkers with matched multimodal neuroimaging and cognitive measures from large cohorts. Here, we combine plasma biomarkers, neuroimaging, and cognitive data from A4 and ADNI to develop robust, cross‐cohort predictive models of region‐specific brain pathology and cognition.

We applied a multivariate machine learning framework to evaluate how combinations of plasma biomarkers including Aβ42/Aβ40, p‐tau217, p‐tau181, GFAP, and NfL collectively predict region‐specific Aβ and tau pathology, neurodegeneration, and cognitive outcomes. In the first stage, we examined associations between plasma biomarkers and ATN measures across cortical and subcortical regions. Predictive models were trained and cross‐validated using ADNI data, and model performance was then tested on A4 data to assess cross‐cohort generalizability. We focused on four key outcomes: (1) precuneus Aβ PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR), (2) entorhinal tau PET SUVR, (3) hippocampal volume, and (4) Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (PACC) scores. Finally, probabilistic graphical modeling was applied to explore directional associations and hypothesized pathways linking plasma biomarkers to ATN and cognitive outcomes. This study aims to evaluate the biological relevance and predictive value of blood‐based measures for AD.

Methods

Participants

Data were obtained from the A4 Study 31 , 32 , 33 and the ADNI. 34 , 35 , 36 Participants were included in the study if they had ATN, cognition and plasma markers data available.

A4 cohort: The A4 dataset included 590 participants (537 cognitively normal [CN], and 53 mild cognitive impairment [MCI]) who had complete data on plasma biomarkers, cognitive assessments, Aβ PET, and MRI measures, comprising 343 Aβ+ subjects, and 247 Aβ− individuals. Tau PET imaging was available for a subset of 134 participants in the A4 cohort. As part of the screening process, participants underwent plasma biomarker assessment, structural MRI, cognitive testing, and Aβ PET imaging, with tau PET available in a subset, to determine eligibility for randomization. The analytic dataset was derived from the A4 Study screening cohort, during which clinically normal adults aged 65–85 years underwent plasma biomarker collection, MRI, cognitive testing, and Aβ PET to determine eligibility for randomization. Although individuals with cognitive impairment were not randomized into the trial, the shared screening dataset includes a small number of participants who were later characterized as having MCI based on neuropsychological performance at screening. These individuals were not randomized into the solanezumab trial but remain present in the publicly shared observational dataset; they were retained in this analysis to maximize statistical power and because they had complete multimodal data. The A4 Study (NCT02008357) was a 240‐week, Phase 3 double‐blind, placebo‐controlled randomized clinical trial. The study was aimed to evaluate whether solanezumab, an anti‐Aβ antibody treatment could slow cognitive decline. 32 , 33 The screening process and eligibility criteria were previously published. 33 Briefly, individuals with Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global score of 0, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) of 25–30, and Logical Memory Delayed Recall (LMDR IIA) score of 6–18 were eligible to proceed to 18‐F florbetapir PET.

ADNI cohort: The ADNI dataset included 348 participants (172 CN, 161 MCI, and 15 AD) with complete data on plasma biomarkers, cognitive assessments, Aβ PET, and MRI measures, comprising 143 Aβ+ and 205 Aβ− individuals, and 191 participants also had available tau PET imaging. While ADNI includes a larger number of enrolled participants, only those with fully matched plasma, neuroimaging, and cognitive data were included to ensure reliability and consistency for cross‐cohort predictive modeling. Aβ+ status indicates the presence of significant levels of Aβ plaques in the brain, suggesting pathological accumulation associated with AD. The ADNI was launched in 2003 with the goal of testing whether MRI, PET, other biological markers, clinical and neuropsychological assessments could be combined to improve the understanding of AD development and progression. The diagnosis criteria for CN individuals included MMSE score greater than 24, a CDR of 0, and normal memory function with no significant cognitive or daily living impairments. The eligibility criteria for MCI participants included presence of subjective memory concerns, MMSE score greater than 24, a CDR of 0.5, and abnormal memory function with preserved general cognition and functional performance. AD participants were characterized by subjective memory concerns, an MMSE score between 20 and 26, a CDR of 0.5 or 1.0, and meeting the NINCDS/ADRDA criteria for probable AD. Biomarkers and imaging

Plasma biomarkers

A4: The A4 dataset included biomarker assay results obtained from plasma samples collected during screening visits. 32 The dataset contains quantitative measurements of plasma biomarkers, including Aβ, p‐tau, and neuroinflammatory markers. The Lilly Clinical Diagnostics Laboratory processed the samples using an electrochemiluminescent immunoassay (ECLIA), with automated sample preparation via the Tecan Fluent workstation and detection performed on the MSD Sector S Imager 600 MM. Roche Diagnostics conducted testing using their Elecsys Immunoassays ECLIAs, including plasma GFAP, NfL, Aβ42, Aβ40, and p‐tau181.

ADNI: Plasma biomarker measurements were obtained from ADNI as part of the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) biomarkers consortium project. 34 , 35 , 36 This dataset included longitudinal measurements of key AD‐related plasma biomarkers, analyzed using highly validated commercial assays. Specifically, the plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, plasma p‐tau181, plasma GFAP, and plasma NfL were quantified using the Elecsys® platform (Roche Diagnostics), while plasma p‐tau217 was measured using a mass spectrometry–based assay developed by C2N Diagnostics. These biomarkers were selected based on their relevance to Aβ deposition, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration. Plasma samples were collected in 10 mL dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (K2‐EDTA) tubes, processed within one hour of collection, and frozen at −80°C before shipment to analytical laboratories. Standardized quality control procedures were implemented, including the use of endogenous quality control samples to monitor assay performance across different analytical runs. 34 , 35

PET imaging

A4: Participants eligible for PET imaging underwent 18F‐florbetapir scans. Participants were randomized to A4 if determined to have evidence of elevated Aβ accumulation, as determined by an algorithm that combined visual read and mean cortical SUVR. Following A4 protocol definitions, Aβ positivity was defined as SUVR ≥ 1.10, resulting in 343 Aβ+ and 247 Aβ− participants in the analytic dataset. Additional details of the screening, randomization algorithm and trial results are reported elsewhere. 31 , 32 Flortaucipir (tau PET) data were collected from 80 to 110 min post‐injection and were generally reconstructed in 6 × 5‐min frames. There are some exceptions to this (i.e., some sites reconstructed the 80‐ to 110‐minute data into a single frame). Data underwent realignment and mean/sum over the frames into a single 3D image suitable for SUVR analysis.

ADNI: For ADNI Aβ and tau PET data, Florbetapir (AV45) and Flortaucipir (AV‐1451) measures were used, respectively. Aβ PET imaging involved injecting the radiotracer Florbetapir and acquiring a static 3D scan. The total acquisition time was 20 minutes, divided into four 5‐minute frames. Imaging occurred between 50 and 70 minutes after injection. AV‐1451 PET imaging uses the radiotracer AV‐1451 to visualize tau protein aggregates in the brain. The scan protocol involved a dynamic 3D scan, lasting a total of 30 minutes with six 5‐minute frames. Imaging was conducted between 75‐ and 105‐minutes post‐injection to capture the distribution and accumulation of tau pathology. Cortical Aβ SUVRs were computed using a composite region including the precuneus, posterior cingulate, frontal, lateral temporal, and parietal cortices, with the whole cerebellum as the reference. Entorhinal tau SUVRs were extracted based on FreeSurfer‐defined cortical and subcortical regions. Subsequently, each PET dataset was co‐registered to the MRI scan closest in time. PET SUVRs were computed by dividing the cortical summary region by the reference region. Processing involved a native‐space MRI scan for each subject, which was then processed using FreeSurfer v7.1.1. Amyloid PET SUVRs were computed using a cortical summary region composite, including the precuneus, posterior cingulate, frontal, lateral temporal, and parietal cortices, with the whole cerebellum as the reference region. The summary SUVR, based on the whole cerebellum reference region with a threshold of 1.11, was employed to categorize subjects as Aβ+ and Aβ−. 34 Specifically, we extracted 126 Aβ SUVR features and entorhinal tau SUVR levels based on FreeSurfer‐defined cortical and subcortical regions. 34 , 35 , 36

MRI imaging

A4: The 3D T1‐weighted MRI scans were processed using NeuroQuant (NQ), a fully automated segmentation software which has been cleared by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical use. In brief, NQ first performs quality control to check for severe patient motion and other artifacts. Then, a personalized age‐ and gender‐specific atlas is derived for each subject. This personalized atlas is employed to perform automated segmentation and volume calculation from each subject's scan. Regional volumes are reported. For this study, left and right hippocampal volumes were extracted and summed to compute the total hippocampal volume, which served as the MRI‐based neurodegeneration marker. This measure provides a robust indicator of medial temporal lobe atrophy and is consistent with standard AD literature practices.

ADNI: Processing involved a native‐space MRI scan for each subject, which was then processed using FreeSurfer v7.1.1. For MRI, left and right hippocampal volumes were extracted from FreeSurfer‐derived subcortical segmentation outputs. The total hippocampal volume was computed as the sum of left and right hippocampal volumes, consistent with the approach used in the A4 cohort, enabling cross‐cohort comparability for neurodegeneration measures.

Preprocessing and confound regression

Plasma biomarkers in the A4 and ADNI cohorts were quantified in different laboratories. Extensive prior work has demonstrated strong agreement and biological consistency of plasma Aβ and p‐tau biomarkers across assay platforms, laboratories, and cohorts. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 These findings support the cross‐platform validity of plasma biomarkers used in large multicenter studies. To minimize potential assay‐related differences, plasma biomarkers were standardized within each cohort using z‐score normalization prior to predictive modeling. This strategy emphasizes relative multivariate biomarker patterns rather than absolute concentration values. The observation of consistent predictive relationships across A4 and ADNI, despite differences in biomarker acquisition methods, suggests that the proposed multivariate modeling framework is robust to variability in measurement platforms. Prior to modeling, all plasma biomarkers, brain imaging measures, and cognitive outcomes were adjusted for age, sex, and education using linear regression; residuals were used in subsequent analyses. Total Hippocampal Volume was additionally corrected for intracranial volume (ICV) to account for head‐size variability. This ensures that predictive models focus on variance independent of demographic and anatomical confounds.

Statistical analyses

Correlation analysis and brain map visualization

In addition to predictive modeling, Spearman's rank correlations were computed between each plasma biomarker and regional brain measures, including: Aβ PET SUVR, tau PET SUVR, and MRI across all cortical and subcortical regions. The resulting correlation values were then mapped onto brain surface and subcortical structures to visualize the spatial distribution of biomarker associations. Significant associations were visualized on cortical and subcortical surfaces after false discovery rate (FDR) correction (p < 0.05).

Predicting Aβ status

Aβ+ vs. Aβ− classification was performed using the Elastic‐Net method, which combines L1 (lasso) and L2 (ridge) penalties to enhance feature selection while maintaining model stability. Hyperparameters included alpha values from 10− 6 to 106 and L1 ratios of 0.1, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9. Logistic regression with nested 10‐fold cross‐validation (CV) was also implemented. After fitting the model, we extracted the optimal hyperparameters, including the best alpha value and L1 ratio, which were determined based on the highest CV accuracy. Features were standardized using z‐score normalization. The model's hyperparameters, including the regularization strength (C), were optimized using grid search with a nested five‐fold CV. At each fold, the best hyperparameters were selected, and the logistic regression model was trained on the scaled training data. Predictions were made on the test set, and accuracy scores were recorded for each fold. The final model performance was evaluated using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, with the area under the curve (AUC) score computed as a performance metric. To assess feature importance, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were computed for each test set using a linear explainer. The SHAP values were aggregated across folds to identify the most influential features contributing to Aβ status classification.

Robust predictive modeling of ATN and cognitive outcomes

For each participant, we extracted plasma biomarker measures and brain imaging‐derived phenotypes, including precuneus Aβ burden (Aβ PET SUVR), entorhinal tau deposition (tau PET SUVR), hippocampal volume (MRI), and PACC score (cognitive measure). First, these measures were corrected for the effect of confounding factors including age, sex, and education using linear regression and the residuals of the models were utilized in the next steps. In addition, hippocampal volumes were specifically adjusted for ICV. Next, we conducted model training and validation on the ADNI data using 10‐fold CV. Finally, to test the external validity and generalizability of the model, we tested the performance of the trained model on the independent A4 dataset. The predictor variables consisted of five plasma biomarkers, while the target variables were imaging‐derived brain measures and cognition. To predict each brain imaging phenotype and the PACC score from plasma biomarker features, we employed a multivariate machine learning model with robust fitting, which reduces the influence of outliers and violations of standard regression assumptions. Unlike ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, which minimizes the sum of squared residuals and can be sensitive to extreme values, robust regression methods assign lower weights to observations with large residuals, improving model stability and reliability. 41 We used iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) with a Huber loss function, which combines squared loss for small residuals and absolute loss for larger residuals. This approach ensures that influential outliers do not excessively bias the regression coefficients. The robust regression model was trained on the ADNI dataset, where the predictor matrix consisted of five plasma biomarkers, and the response variable was one of the four brain/cognitive measures. Once trained, the model was applied to the independent A4 dataset to generate out‐of‐sample predictions. Model performance was assessed using Spearman's rank correlation between predicted and actual values, ensuring a non‐parametric evaluation of predictive accuracy. To obtain a robust estimate of model performance, we repeated the entire training and testing process 100 times, generating distributions of predicted values, feature weights, and correlation coefficients. Mean regression coefficients were computed across iterations to evaluate the relative contribution of each plasma biomarker to prediction accuracy.

Directional network modeling

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) 42 , 43 was defined to predict statistical relationships between plasma biomarkers, neuroimaging measures, and cognitive outcomes. Importantly, this framework is not intended to represent biological or temporal causality, but rather to evaluate the extent to which plasma biomarker patterns statistically predict imaging‐based ATN measures and PACC scores within a multivariate regression graph. The following directional connections were specified: (1) plasma biomarkers (Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, GFAP, NfL, p‐tau181, p‐tau217) were hypothesized to directly influence regional brain measures (precuneus Aβ PET SUVR, entorhinal cortex tau PET SUVR, and Hippocampus volume T1‐MRI). 44 (2) Brain measures were further assumed to impact cognitive performance, as measured by the PACC score. (3) Additional direct effects from plasma biomarkers to cognitive outcomes were tested. The DAG was implemented using NetworkX to visualize the hypothesized directional structure. To estimate hypothesized directional effects, OLS regression models were applied using the stats model's package. Separate regression models were estimated for each outcome variable (precuneus Aβ PET SUVR, entorhinal cortex tau PET SUVR, hippocampus volume T1‐MRI, and PACC). Results

Association between plasma biomarkers and ATN neuroimaging measures

We first examined the association between individual AD plasma biomarkers (Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, p‐tau217, p‐tau181, NfL, and GFAP) and ATN neuroimaging markers (Aβ PET SUVR, tau PET SUVR, MRI) across brain regions within the ADNI dataset. As shown in Figure 1, statistically significant correlations (FDR‐corrected) followed well‐established biological patterns: p‐tau217 and p‐tau181 correlated most strongly with regional tau PET burden, GFAP with Aβ and tau, and NfL with neurodegeneration indices. These spatial association maps provide the foundation for subsequent predictive modeling.

Cohort characterization and distributional comparisons

Because the ADNI and A4 cohorts differ in age range, biomarker distributions, and cognitive status, 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 we conducted a brief comparison of plasma and clinical distributions to contextualize the cross‐cohort prediction analyses. The full set of distributional analyses and corresponding statistical results are provided in the Supplementary Material. Histograms and kernel density estimates (KDEs) are shown for five plasma biomarkers (Figure 2). Notable differences emerge across cohorts. For plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, the ADNI cohort exhibited a markedly more peaked and left‐skewed distribution compared to A4, indicating a greater proportion of individuals with lower values (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, p < 0.0001). Plasma GFAP and NfL were positively skewed in both cohorts. Plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 also showed higher skewness and kurtosis in ADNI, reflecting a subset with elevated phosphorylated tau levels. However, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests indicated no significant distributional differences between cohorts for plasma p‐tau181 (h = 0, p = 0.1478) or p‐tau217 (h = 0, p = 0.1776). Figure 3 presents Histograms and KDEs for ATN and clinical outcomes in each cohort. Precuneus Aβ SUVR distributions differed significantly between the cohorts (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, p = 0.046), with ADNI showing more skewness (1.10 vs. 0.81), indicating more individuals with higher Aβ levels. Hippocampal volume distributions did not differ significantly (p = 0.11), although A4 showed more negative skewness (−2.57 vs. −0.44) and higher kurtosis (25.96 vs. 3.57), indicating a subgroup with smaller volumes. Cognitive scores (PACC) differed significantly (p < 0.001), with ADNI having more negative skewness (−2.21 vs. 0.52) and higher kurtosis (9.58 vs. 3.27), reflecting more individuals with lower cognitive performance. These distributional differences assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, highlight distinct patterns of pathology and cognition across cohorts.

Prediction of Aβ status

A logistic regression model trained in ADNI and tested in A4 demonstrated robust discrimination of Aβ status, with performance metrics including AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity presented in Supplementary Figure 1–3. Among the plasma biomarkers, plasma p‐tau217 had the highest contribution to predicting Aβ status with SHAP value of (1.29 and 1.28), followed by plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio with (0.34, and 0.47), plasma GFAP with (0.37 and 0.05). These findings identify the relative contribution of each biomarker to the prediction of Aβ status.

Prediction of regional ATN measures and cognition

Cross‐cohort prediction: Train ADNI → test A4

The model trained on the ADNI data successfully predicted ATN and cognitive outcomes with significant prediction accuracy when tested on the independent A4 dataset. The correlation coefficients and p‐values between observed and predicted values were as follows: precuneus Aβ SUVR burden (r = 0.69, p < 0.001), entorhinal tau SUVR deposition (r = 0.38, p < 0.001), hippocampal volume (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), and PACC score (r = 0.23, p = 0.0019). These results were derived using the ADNI dataset for training and the A4 dataset for testing (Figure 4). Feature weights revealed that plasma p‐tau217 (β = 0.498, p < 0.001) followed by plasma GFAP (β = 0.140, p = 0.0070) and plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (β = −0.126, p = 0.0076) contributed most to predicting precuneus Aβ SUVR. Further, plasma p‐tau217 (β = 0.791, p < 0.001) contributed most to predicting entorhinal tau PET SUVR. In contrast, the majority of plasma biomarkers contributed to predicting hippocampal volume. Particularly, Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was positively associated with hippocampal volume (β = 0.210, p < 0.001), while plasma NfL (β = −0.155, p = 0.0034) and plasma p‐tau181 (β = −0.152, p = 0.0077) showed significant negative contribution, indicating that higher levels of these markers predicted smaller hippocampal volumes. Feature weights revealed that plasma NfL (β = −0.214, p < 0.001) followed by plasma p‐tau217 (β = −0.188, p < 0.001) contributed most to predicting PACC scores, with higher levels of these biomarkers associated with poorer cognitive performance (Figure 5). The supplementary material (Supplementary Figure 4–7) provides the results for training and testing a model within ADNI, within A4, as well as training the model on A4 and testing it externally on ADNI data. This cross‐cohort evaluation was performed to assess the generalizability of the model when the training and testing datasets are switched. Additionally, it allows for comparison of SHAP values across different training conditions to evaluate the consistency of feature importance. While the model's performance remains comparable, further analysis is needed to determine whether the SHAP value patterns are truly similar across cohorts.

Reverse generalization: train A4 → test ADNI

Using a model trained on A4 and independently tested on ADNI, predicted values showed strong correlations with observed data for precuneus Aβ SUVR burden (r = 0.54, p < 0.001), entorhinal cortex tau PET SUVR deposition (r = 0.38, p < 0.001), hippocampal volume (r = 0.32, p < 0.001), and PACC score (r = 0.24, p = 0.0036) (Supplementary Figure 4). Feature weights revealed that plasma p‐tau217 (β = 0.67, p < 0.001), plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (β = −0.22, p < 0.001), plasma NfL (β = −0.11, p = 0.0012), and plasma GFAP (β = 0.095, p = 0.013) contributed most to predicting precuneus Aβ SUVR, plasma p‐tau217 (β = 0.27, p = 0.017) contributed most to entorhinal cortex tau PET SUVR prediction, while plasma p‐tau217 (β = −0.21, p = 0.0002) and plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (β = 0.13, p = 0.003) exhibited the strongest influence on hippocampal volume. Additionally, plasma p‐tau217 (β = −0.45, p < 0.001) contributed most to predicting PACC scores (Supplementary Figure 5). The models trained on ADNI and tested on A4, and vice versa, both showed strong prediction accuracy for ATN and cognitive outcomes. Plasma p‐tau217 was the strongest and most consistent predictor of PET Aβ and tau, while GFAP, Aβ42/Aβ40, NfL, and p‐tau181 tracked neurodegeneration and cognitive decline, supporting a complementary plasma marker panel for staging AD pathology. This consistency underscores their key role in reflecting AD pathology across different cohorts.

A4 cohort

In the A4 cohort, plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was significantly negatively associated with precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = −0.059, p < 0.001), but not with other regional neuroimaging measures or cognition. Plasma GFAP was positively associated with precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = 0.128, p < 0.001), while plasma NfL showed significant negative associations with both precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = −0.042, p = 0.035) and hippocampal volume (β = −0.103, p = 0.009). Plasma P‐tau217 was also positively associated with precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = 0.046, p = 0.006). Downstream, hippocampal volume was positively associated with cognitive performance on the PACC (β = 1.590, p = 0.005). Additionally, plasma NfL, p‐tau181, p‐tau217, and GFAP showed significant direct effects on PACC scores, with specific directions and effect sizes.

Combined dataset

In the combined A4 and ADNI datasets, plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was significantly associated with lower precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = −0.0987, p < 0.0001) and hippocampal volume (β = −0.2396, p = 0.0001), but not with entorhinal tau SUVR or PACC scores. Plasma GFAP was positively associated with precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = 0.0383, p = 0.0128) and entorhinal tau SUVR (β = 0.0348, p = 0.0086), and negatively associated with hippocampal volume (β = −0.2361, p = 0.0018) and PACC (β = −0.6164, p = 0.0357). Plasma NfL was positively related to precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = 0.0367, p = 0.0066) and hippocampal atrophy (β = 0.5048, p < 0.0001), and negatively related to entorhinal tau SUVR (β = −0.0820, p < 0.0001). Both plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 were strongly associated with higher entorhinal tau SUVR (Plasma p‐tau181:β = 0.0858; p‐tau217:β = 0.0486; p < 0.0001). Plasma P‐tau181 was also linked to hippocampal atrophy (β = −0.4504, p < 0.0001), and plasma p‐tau217 to higher precuneus Aβ SUVR (β = 0.0616, p < 0.0001) and worse cognition (β = −1.0833, p < 0.0001).

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that plasma biomarkers are valuable and robust predictors of Aβ status, ATN pathology, and cognitive performance across two large, independent cohorts. Importantly, these plasma biomarkers (Aβ42/Aβ40, p‐tau217, p‐tau181, GFAP, and NfL) reliably predicted outcomes in independent test datasets, underscoring both the reliability and generalizability of our multivariate machine learning models. Using data from the A4 and ADNI studies, we identified significant associations between plasma biomarkers and neuroimaging‐derived measures of precuneus Aβ burden, entorhinal tau deposition, hippocampal atrophy, and cognitive performance. These findings underscore the potential of plasma‐based biomarkers as cost‐effective, noninvasive alternatives to PET and MRI for detecting and monitoring AD pathology. Unlike Aβ PET imaging or CSF assays, no plasma biomarker has yet achieved gold‐standard accuracy for tau pathology detection, Nonetheless, our multivariate machine learning approach successfully predicted entorhinal tau PET signal in an independent dataset. This finding highlights the potential of plasma biomarkers to approximate tau pathology in vivo, particularly in early disease stages where direct tau measures may be unavailable. Our results highlight the differential roles of plasma biomarkers in predicting specific aspects of ATN pathology. Plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio and GFAP were significantly associated with Aβ PET SUVR in the precuneus, supporting previous findings that reduced plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio levels indicate increased Aβ burden. 12 , 13 , 16 , 21 Similarly, plasma Aβ42, and p‐tau217 emerged as strong predictors of tau deposition in the entorhinal cortex, aligning with studies demonstrating their sensitivity to AD‐related tau pathology. 15 , 16 Aβ42/Aβ40, NfL, and p‐tau181 contributed most to hippocampal atrophy, while NfL, p‐tau217, and GFAP contributed to cognitive decline, highlighting their role as markers of neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. 18 , 28 , 30

The robust performance of our predictive models across independent datasets further validates the clinical utility of this approach. Our robust predictive multivariate machine learning models trained on ADNI and tested on A4 effectively predicted regional Aβ and tau burden, hippocampal volume, and cognitive scores, with significant accuracy. Models trained on either dataset demonstrated significant predictive accuracy and identified largely consistent key plasma biomarkers driving ATN and cognitive outcomes. However, differences in correlation magnitudes and feature importance likely reflect underlying variations in cohort characteristics, biomarker distributions, and population heterogeneity between the ADNI and A4 datasets. These findings underscore the challenges and importance of cross‐cohort validation in biomarker‐based predictive modeling.

Our findings extend previous work by jointly modeling multivariate plasma biomarker patterns rather than individual markers, providing a more comprehensive characterization of ATN pathology and cognitive performance. These findings highlight the feasibility of using plasma biomarkers to infer neuroimaging and cognitive outcomes with high reliability. Our findings align with prior research emphasizing the diagnostic and prognostic value of plasma biomarkers. 45 , 46 , 47 Previous work has shown that combining biomarkers can improve diagnostic accuracy compared to each alone. 45 Notably, studies by Mattsson‐Carlgren et al. 21 , 30 and Palmqvist et al. 16 demonstrated that plasma p‐tau217 strongly correlates with both Aβ and tau PET measures, which is consistent with our results. Additionally, Ashton et al. 18 , 20 , 27 and Hampel et al. 3 , 24 reported elevated plasma GFAP levels in AD, which we found to be predictive of Aβ burden and hippocampal atrophy. For example, in a large Chinese cohort, plasma Aβ42/Aβ40, p‑tau181, GFAP, and NfL measured by ultrasensitive single‐molecule assays varied across the Alzheimer's continuum from cognitively unimpaired to dementia and correlated with Aβ status, supporting the feasibility of plasma biomarkers to reflect underlying pathology. 46 Similarly, in a large multinational multicenter study (n = 1,298), plasma p‑tau181 demonstrated high diagnostic performance to discriminate AD from non‐AD dementias and controls. 47 Importantly, our multivariate modeling approach extends this literature by jointly capturing the contribution of multiple plasma markers to neuroimaging and cognitive outcomes, thereby offering a more comprehensive characterization of disease‐related processes. We predicted regional brain pathology (e.g., precuneus Aβ, entorhinal tau, hippocampal atrophy), rather than global/summary measures. This spatial specificity is rarely addressed in prior plasma‑imaging studies but may provide finer‐grained insight into pathophysiological heterogeneity and staging. 48 The importance of regional variation is highlighted in recent neuroimaging–pathology work. 48 Recent work found plasma GFAP correlates with astrocyte reactivity, tau tangle load, cortical thinning, and neurodegeneration underscoring its value in a multimodal biomarker panel. 48 Similarly, longitudinal studies have shown baseline p‐tau181 and GFAP to predict future brain volume loss and cognitive decline. 49

Prediction robustness

While predictive performance for Aβ PET was consistently strong across cohorts, predictions for tau PET and hippocampal volume were more modest. Several biological and methodological factors likely contribute: Tau PET has lower signal‐to‐noise ratio and greater inter‐scanner heterogeneity than Aβ PET, especially in early Braak regions such as entorhinal cortex. 6 Hippocampal volume is influenced by multiple disease and non‐disease processes (aging, vascular changes, and individual neuroanatomical variability), diluting biomarker‐specific signal. ADNI and A4 differ in age, enrichment strategies, cognitive status, and plasma assay distributions, all of which introduce domain shifts that complicate external generalization. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 Tau PET subsets are smaller than Aβ PET subsets in both cohorts, contributing to larger confidence intervals and reduced model stability. The fact that correlations remained statistically significant and generalizable across cohorts despite these challenges highlights the robustness of multivariate plasma signatures but also clarifies where further methodological advances are needed.

Novelty of the multivariate approach

A key contribution of this work is the integration of multiple plasma biomarkers within a unified multivariate framework to predict region‐specific neuroimaging measures. Most prior studies use univariate models or global PET composites. By modeling the joint contribution of Aβ42/Aβ40, p‐tau181, p‐tau217, NfL, and GFAP, we capture complementary biological pathways spanning Aβ processing, tau phosphorylation, axonal injury, and astroglial reactivity. This multivariate approach improves predictive accuracy and yields mechanistic insights that are not apparent from analyzing individual biomarkers in isolation. Additionally, our cross‐cohort design (ADNI → A4 and A4 → ADNI) provides strong evidence of biological generalizability. Very few plasma → imaging prediction studies test performance across independent datasets, despite calls for validation in external samples.

Interpretation of directional model

Our study further extends previous findings by incorporating a directional network model to elucidate the directional relationships among biomarkers, neuroimaging measures, and cognitive outcomes. 17 , 20 , 47 We identified key plasma biomarkers linked to brain Aβ and tau pathology, as well as neurodegeneration, in the A4 cohort. Specifically, lower plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio and higher plasma GFAP and p‐tau217 levels correlated with increased Aβ burden in the precuneus. Plasma NfL was linked to both Aβ load and hippocampal atrophy, while plasma p‐tau217 and p‐tau181 were related to tau pathology in the entorhinal cortex. Hippocampal volume was a strong predictor of cognitive performance, and several plasma markers showed direct effects on cognition. The ability to predict ATN pathology and cognitive decline from plasma biomarkers has significant clinical implications. First, plasma‐based assays offer a scalable and accessible alternative to PET and MRI, which are often limited by high costs and logistical constraints. 50 Second, early detection of Aβ and tau pathologies through plasma tests could facilitate timely interventions, improving patient stratification for clinical trials and personalized treatment approaches. 51 , 52 Third, monitoring disease progression through serial plasma biomarker assessments may enhance the effectiveness of therapeutic strategies aimed at modifying AD pathology. 7

Limitations and future directions

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the predictive models demonstrated robust performance, variability in plasma biomarker assay methodologies across studies may affect generalizability. Plasma biomarker assays differed between the ADNI and A4 cohorts and, to some extent, across collection sites within ADNI. While all assays were analytically validated and widely used, the lack of full cross‐cohort assay harmonization may introduce calibration‐related variability. As a result, a portion of the observed prediction performance may reflect cohort‐ or platform‐specific differences rather than purely biological signal. To mitigate this issue, biomarkers were standardized within cohort prior to modeling, and consistent predictive patterns were observed across datasets. Nevertheless, future studies would benefit from harmonized assay pipelines, formal cross‐platform calibration, or reference‐standard–based normalization to further enhance reproducibility and generalizability across cohorts. Second, our analyses capture associations at a single time point; they cannot resolve temporal sequences or infer causal progression. Longitudinal modeling, including mediation and dynamic structural equation modeling, will be required to map biomarker trajectories. Third, our directional network analysis relies on observational data, and while DAGs provide valuable insights, causal inferences should be interpreted with caution. Future studies employing longitudinal mediation analyses and randomized clinical trials are needed to confirm these relationships. 4 Additionally, while our study primarily focused on five key plasma biomarkers, emerging candidates such as plasma MTBR‐tau242, p‐tau231, YKL‐40, and neurogranin may provide further insights into AD pathology. 19 Future research should explore multimodal integration of additional biomarkers with genetic and lifestyle factors to enhance predictive accuracy. Furthermore, extending analyses to diverse populations beyond ADNI and A4 will be crucial for assessing the generalizability of plasma‐based biomarkers across different ethnic and demographic groups. Both cohorts are predominantly White individuals, highly educated, and research‐volunteer populations. Validation in more diverse cohorts is essential to ensure equitable clinical utility.

Our study provides compelling evidence supporting the utility of plasma biomarkers in predicting ATN pathology and cognitive decline in AD. By leveraging machine learning models and directional network analyses, we elucidated distinct biomarker pathways associated with Aβ burden, tau accumulation, neurodegeneration, and cognitive performance. These findings contribute to the growing body of research advocating for plasma‐based diagnostics as a scalable and noninvasive solution for AD detection and monitoring. Future work integrating longitudinal and multimodal approaches will be essential to fully realize the potential of plasma biomarkers in AD research and clinical practice.
