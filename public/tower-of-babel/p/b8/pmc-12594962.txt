Multi-output deep learning for high-frequency prediction of air and surface temperature in Kuwait
Authors: Shehroz S Khan, Rami Al-Hajj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of air and surface temperature is essential for urban planning and climate resilience, especially in arid regions. This study evaluates the performance of multi-output regression models using high-frequency climate data collected every 5 min over four years in Kuwait. Thirty environmental variables (e.g., including humidity, solar radiation, dew point, and wind direction) were used to predict six air and surface temperature-related outcomes simultaneously. Ten models, including deep learning and traditional machine learning approaches, were benchmarked using a leave-1-year-out validation strategy. Results show that contextual embeddings-based Transformer (FTTransformer) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) achieved strong predictive performance with an of 0.998, a mean squared error of 0.13, and a mean absolute error of 0.24 when forecasting six temperature variables at 5-min resolution. These results significantly outperform traditional machine learning models and demonstrate the robustness of deep learning approaches for high-frequency climate prediction. While deep learning models outperformed conventional methods, LSTM’s performance degraded on anomalous data from previous years, whereas FTTransformer maintained stable accuracy across years. Model interpretation using SHAP and permutation importance identified key predictors for this task, underlining the significance of diverse climate features.Keywords: Regression, Multioutput, Climate, Machine learning, Deep learningSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Kuwait is a country in West Asia, situated on the northern edge of the Arabian Peninsula at the head of the Persian Gulf. The country experiences some of the harshest summer temperature in the broader Middle East region; and is undergoing climate and temperature changes since the known records from 1960s1,2. It is estimated that by the mid of current century, the average temperature in Kuwait could increase by 3. At large, these changes can significantly affect daily activities4, human health5, economic prosperity6, heat-related mortality3, degrading biodiversity7, disrupting ecology8, threatening terrestrial ecosystems, including wildlife habitat9 and worsening agricultural outcomes10. These impacts are along the lines of the larger geographical region of the Middle East11,12.

Climate change is a gradual phenomenon and often necessitates decades of data to understand the slow moving changes in the temperature and other elements. However, short-term climate data may also yield insights into current weather patterns. It is known that several key climatic factors, such as wind speed, relative humidity, radiation, etc., can be utilized to predict air and surface temperatures. However, their application for long-term predictions or annual forecasting is inadequately studied. Given multidimensional climate data, one of the key questions can be whether this data from the previous years is useful to predict future year’s air and surface temperatures? If such an accurate predictive model is built, this can be used to measure any major deviations in a given year, and the reasons for climate change could be analyzed. Advancements made in artificial intelligence and machine learning (ML) have enabled to process, analyze and utilize vast amounts of climate data to achieve both short-term and long-term prediction abilities13–15.

Temperature prediction models have been developed for several geographical regions, which can predict average, maximum, or minimum temperatures at different time horizons (1–24 h)16–20. There is also some research for predicting longer horizon temperature, such as one year ahead21 or next season22,23. Predicting long term temperature trends can provide valuable insights into current climate changes; however, it entails specific advantages and limitations. Long-term prediction of temperature may assist in strategic planning for climate sensitive sectors (e.g., agriculture), and disaster preparedness (e.g., extreme temperatures)24. On the other hand, most of the proposed methods are probabilistic may inadequately address climatic uncertainties, and therefore may provide reduced accuracy13,24. These models may suffer from coverage bias, where incomplete training data used to train the models can lead to an underestimation of climatic variables25. The prediction task could become more challenging if more than one type of outcomes is to be predicted at specific times in a longer horizon.

Recent advances in deep learning (DL) have shown promising results in climate and hydrological forecasting tasks, such as rainfall and precipitation prediction26–28. Some studies use DL approaches to predict other environmental variables, e.g. global sea level29 and methane concentrations30 for a longer horizon in future (up to the year 2050). These studies demonstrate the potential of DL approaches in handling high-resolution climate time series data for both shorter- and longer-term time horizon. However, these prior efforts have focused on either tropical or global environments, whereas our study uniquely addresses high-frequency, multi-output temperature prediction in an arid environment (Kuwait) using different types of DL methods. The goal of this paper is to develop DL models to establish the inter-annual robustness of high frequency multidimensional climate data to predict multiple air and surface temperature variables. By adopting a leave-one-year-out evaluation strategy, we explicitly test model generalization across distinct seasonal cycles, which is particularly important for practical applications, such as urban planning, ecotourism and climate resilience in the region.

Numerous studies have been conducted to forecast temperatures at different time horizons in the broader Middle East region31–33. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no Kuwait specific temperature prediction studies published so far. This study presents the development and evaluation of ten advanced ML, deep learning (DL) and sequential DL algorithms to predict six outcomes related to air and surface temperatures at a longer horizon of one-year. We employed explainability methods—SHAP and permutation importance—for temperature modeling, which are rarely used for multivariate weather data. While Transformer-based architectures have been applied in weather and climate prediction, they have primarily focused on temporal sequence modeling34–36. In contrast, this study employs the contextual embeddings-based Transformer (FTTransformer), which is specifically designed for heterogeneous tabular data with multiple correlated features. The architecture introduces contextual embeddings and feature-wise self-attention, enabling the model to capture nonlinear interactions across diverse climate indicators, such as humidity, solar radiation, and air density. This design allows FTTransformer to provide both strong predictive accuracy and interpretability. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of FTTransformer to high-frequency (5-min resolution) climate data for multi-output temperature prediction in arid regions. Related work

Weather forecasting largely involves predicting and describing short-term atmospheric conditions, while climate modeling focuses on long-term trends and changes in atmospheric factors. Although Numerical Weather Prediction methods are the benchmark, this type of method faces challenges such as inherent atmospheric uncertainty and computational cost. The advent of ML and, more recently, the emergence of DL have transformed the field through data-driven models15. In this section, we will discuss climate studies that use ML and DL approaches for long-term prediction of temperature. Short-term prediction and other methods are available in the existing reviews37,38.

Cachay et al.39 presented a dynamic informed diffusion model for multi-step forecasting and long-range probabilistic spatiotemporal forecasting. They tailor diffusion models to support long range forecasting by coupling the diffusion process and the dynamical nature of the data. Their method imposes an inductive bias and improves computational efficiency compared to Gaussian noise-based diffusion models. Their task of is to forecast a longer horizon from a single initial condition. For a sea surface dataset, they train, validate, and test the model for the years 1982–2019, 2020, and 2021, respectively. Fister et al.22 presented several DL, ML and data reduction techniques to predict long-term summer air temperature. They aim to predict the first and second fortnights for the month of August using input data from previous months, at two different locations in France and Spain. Their results show that the data reduction technique of recurrence plots when combined with a convolutional neural network (CNN) performs best for both shorter and longer horizons in comparison to other studies baselines. This technique was able to accurately detect some maximums in the summer temperature better than other techniques. Pérez-Aracil et al.23 presented two autoencoder based models for long-term temperature prediction across various European cities that encountered major heatwaves. Their approach comprises using an autoencoder to detect anomalies between inputs and outputs, which are then input into the second-stage model that may be either another autoencoder or a multilayer neural network. They used the hourly data from 1950 to 2022. Their goal was to predict 2-m temperature based on seven other climate variables up to 4 weeks in advance. Tran et al.40 employed a genetic optimization algorithm along with several neural network architectures to first select the best architecture, and then predict the 15-day-ahead daily maximum temperature in the summer. The LSTM model optimized by the genetic algorithm gave better results compared to other models. The data were collected at the Cheongju station in South Korea, covering the period from 1976 to 2015.

Nandi et al.41 present a transformer based approach to predict temperature up to 150 days ahead with high accuracy. They trained their models with more than 25 days of data from 5 cities around the globe. They added a time encoding vector to learn temporal ordering and a convolution block to learn the seasonal aspect of the data. Their model outperformed other ML and DL baselines. However, this approach is limited to univariate time series datasets. Durhasan et al.42 applied statistical and sequential DL models to predict the monthly mean surface temperatures of future years. They trained the models from January 1880 to December 2008, and tested the models from January 2009 to December 2022. They further develop prediction models until 2050, which show a rise in global temperature of up to 1 C.

Ayad43 presented the use of SARIMA model to predict monthly air temperature from the Sania Ramel station, Morocco. The SARIMA model was estimated and validated using the data over the period from January 1980 to July 2022, and used to predict the average air temperature from August 2022 to January 2024. However, no other model comparisons were shown in this work. Shrivastava et al.44 used multivariate polynomial regression and deep neural network models to predict the next year’s temperature in 6 hour intervals. They trained the model on the past 6 years of data. Park et al.45 first refined missing data in a weather dataset and achieved higher performance with an LSTM model in comparison to a deep network over a time horizon of up to 14 days. Their data came from the Meteorological Office of South Korea comprising hourly measurements for temperature, wind direction, wind speed, relative humidity, cumulative precipitation, vapor pressure, and barometric pressure. They trained the models on the data from November 1981 to December 2016 and tested them on a year’s data from January 2017 to December 2017. Roy46 used the weather data collected at the John F. Kennedy International Airport, USA to predict the temperature for 1 and 10 days in the future. The input to the model was the data records related to the past seven days. Their results showed that a combination of a CNN and LSTM outperformed other models.

The above discussion indicates that sequential DL models, such as LSTM and transformers, can be good at predicting long term air temperature. However, under certain circumstances, CNN, Recurrent Neural Networks (their combination) and other classical conventional ML models may also be effective. However, RNN models encounter constraints in long-term air temperature forecasting due to their challenges in capturing long-range relationships, mainly attributed to vanishing or exploding gradients during the training process47. This problem limits their capacity to retain and employ pertinent knowledge from prior time steps, which is crucial for precise climate forecasting over extended periods. Most of the literature reviewed focused on predicting future air temperature using historical data collected up to a given point in time. None of the methods performed a year-by-year analysis, i.e. whether data from a given year(s) is helpful to predict for future or past years. This type of detailed analysis could provide insights into the stability of fluctuations in weather patterns. Moreover, most of the approaches predicted one outcome, i.e. air or surface temperature. The application of advanced transformer models for tabular data remains unexplored in this field. This study, for the first time, utilizes four years worth of weather data collected in Kuwait. The key objectives are to (i) assess the role of previous year(s) in predicting the weather for a given year (past or future), and (ii) predict multi-output temperature outcomes using ML, sequential and tabular DL approaches. The next section describes the Kuwait climate data, data processing steps, feature extraction, and exploratory analysis conducted on this data

AUMET dataset

AUMET is a Weather Monitoring Station Weather Monitoring System (WMS) deployed at the research and innovation center of the American University of the Middle East (AUM)—Kuwait in 2019. AUMET is located at Latitude (North) 29 9’ 53.28” N, and Longitude (East) 48 5’ 53.61” E. The main component of this WMS is the GMX501 integrated weather sensor developed by GILL instruments. The entire WMS is situated on a 25-m-high elevated rooftop above ground level. The surface temperature sensor is placed ten centimeters above the rooftop floor. At the same time, all the other sensors in the integrated GMX501 instrument are mounted on a three-meter elevated mast above the rooftop surface. The WMS also includes a battery supplied by a 20W solar panel, a data logger, and a cellular antenna for remote access. Each record of the collected dataset includes the measures of 14 solar and weather parameters. Data processing

For all the years, the dataset was first parsed into dependent and independent variables according to the research question. Six parameters related to the temperatures were selected as dependent variables, i.e. Air Temperature Average, Air Temperature Minimum, Air Temperature Maximum, Surface Temperature Average, Surface Temperature Minimum, and Surface Temperature Maximum. The rest of the parameters were considered as independent variables. The timestamp parameter is recorded in UTC+3, DD/MM/YYYY H:M:S format, which contains vital information about the day and time. The non-numeric nature of the timestamp excludes its utility in training regression models. Consequently, an additional feature extraction step is performed to extract hour, minute, day of the week, and month. The day of the week and month are numerically encoded from 1 to 7 and 1 to 12. Thus, the total number of features on which regressors are trained is 30, and they output six temperature related outcomes.

Methods

It is important to distinguish between weather and climate time scales. Weather typically refers to short-term atmospheric conditions (minutes to days), while climate characterizes the statistical distribution of weather patterns over more extended periods (decades). Our dataset, sampled every 5 min over four years, lies between these scales: the fine temporal resolution captures weather-like variations, but the multi-year coverage enables assessment of climate-related stability. In this study, we frame the problem as high-frequency temperature prediction within a climate monitoring context, acknowledging that the models are trained on meteorological data but evaluated for their potential to support long-term climate resilience and planning in arid regions such as Kuwait.

In this study, we adopt a leave-1-year-out cross-validation strategy, where 3 years of data are used for training and the remaining year is held out for testing. The choice of a 1-year horizon ensures that the models are evaluated on complete seasonal cycles, capturing winter, summer, and transitional periods, which would not be possible with shorter hold-out intervals (e.g., weeks or months). From a practical standpoint, this setup reflects how predictive models may be deployed to forecast an upcoming year based on historical climate records, which is directly relevant for policy and planning in regions such as Kuwait that experience sharp seasonal variations.

Regression models

As the task is to predict six temperature variables (see Table 2), we formulate this as a multi-output regression problem. For performing a comprehensive analysis, we benchmark 10 regression models from ML, DL and deep sequential learning, including a naive baseline—as discussed below.

The naive baseline (BL) is basically the average of six temperature variables of a particular date and time from the 3 years, and uses it as a prediction for the fourth year. However, as per exploratory analysis, the prediction appears non-trivial. The other classical regressors used were Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees (ET) and K-nearest neighbors (KNN). Two tabular DL regressors used in this analysis are the TabNet from Google48 and FTTransformer from Yandex49. TabNet employs a sequential attention technique to select features at every decision step, thereby enhancing interpretability and efficient learning. FTTransformer uses the attention mechanism to capture relationships between numerical and categorical tabular data. The attention mechanism dynamically assigns importance to certain features, allowing learning embeddings to better generalize. We employed three sequential DL based multi-output regressors based on LSTM, Temporal Convolutional Neural Networks (TCN) and Transformers, to capture temporal dependencies in the dataset to predict six temperature variables.

A 30-dimensional vector containing various climate variables is used to train various models, except the sequential models. In the sequential models, a 30-dimensional sequence of size 12 was created. This number was chosen to capture the last 60-min of variation (as data is collected every 5 min). Due to sequence formation, the last 12 timestamps could not be tested from each of the test sets. As a result, these last 12 rows (or timestamps) were not tested for each non-sequential regressor to ensure a fair comparison. All the DL models were run for 20 epochs. The data was scaled before being passed to each regressor (except RF and ET) and then re-scaled after the predictions were made to compute performance metrics. The ML models were trained with the default parameters. All the DL models were kept at a similar level of complexity for better comparison. The key parameters of these models are as follows:

Performance metrics Three metrics were used to assess the performance of the multi-output prediction models of the temperature variables, namely the score, mean squared error (MSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). These metrics were averaged across the six output variables to give one value per metric.

Leave-one-year-out cross validation

We trained the examined models in a leave-1-year-out (LOYO) manner, such that 3 years data is used for training and 1 year of data is used for testing. Table 5 shows the average, standard deviation and 95% confidence interval of , MSE and MAE for each regressor across all folds. The bold highlighted cells show the best performing regression models for a given test year. The results show that FTTransformer and LSTM models performed best in twofolds each. Among the traditional ML models, RF regressor performed better, but its performance is worse than FTTransformer and LSTM. LSTM and FTTransformer performed equivalent across four-folds, RF and Transformer performed next. It is worth noting that LSTM is a simpler model than FTTransformer and faster to train, while achieving similar performance. FTTransformer does not model sequences but excels at learning robust inter-feature patterns across various space variables that may be more stable year-to-year than temporal patterns. The BL performed worse as expected, it shows that temperatures at a given time across years do not follow a simple fixed distribution and depends on variations of various climatic parameters. Thus, a naive baseline would be an oversimplification of this complex problem involving multiple climate variables.

One of the reasons for the degraded performance of LSTM when data from Year1 and Year2 were included in training is that the data from those years had more variations. It can be seen from Figs. 3 and 4 that Year1 and Year2 (i.e. 2021 and 2022) had many seasonal spikes and sudden temperature changes, while Year3 (2023) is more similar to Year4 (2024). As LSTM relies heavily on stable sequential patterns, misaligned training years’ data (including missing data in Year1) could have affected its generalization. On the other hand, FTTransformer learns time-independent interactions, making it less susceptible to sequence inconsistencies across years. Therefore, we could conclude that in this dataset, temporal dependencies may not be the dominant predictor as the distribution of climate data may vary across years (thus affecting LSTM’s performance). On the other hand, FTTransformer learned robust feature-wise relationships that are invariant across years.

Model explainability

In order to explain the best performing model, FTTranformer, we conducted two analyses—SHAP50 and permutation importance51. We present the results of testing FTTranformer on Year4, where the model was trained on the preceding 3 years. In this case, SHAP analysis can be used to interpret the prediction of an FTTransformer by explaining the contribution of each feature to the regression task. In contrast, permutation importance measures feature importance by randomly shuffling the values and assessing their impact on the regression performance. SHAP can indicate the impact on performance by changing feature values, whereas permutation importance cannot. SHAP can handle complex interrelationships between features; however, it can be highly computationally demanding.

SHAP analysis We conducted the SHAP analysis on 500 samples from the test set due to time constraints. Figure 7 shows the SHAP summary plot highlighting the top weather features that impact the model outcomes. It can be observed that higher and lower values for DPoint_C_Avg push the model prediction up and down; this feature appears to be the most important. The CorrWindDir_deg_Min has a mixed impact, with some values increasing (red) and others decreasing (blue) predictions. Its impact further depends on other features (e.g. DPoint_C_Avg); thus it has a lower effect than DPoint_C_Avg. AirDensity_Kgpm3 has a lower influence than DPoint_C_Avg and DPoint_C_Max, indicating a negative trend with higher density may weakly reduce prediction. In summary, features related to dew point turn out to be the most important, followed by wind direction (which depends on dew point). At the same time, air density is comparatively less critical for this model’s performance.

SHAP summary plot showing the impact of top weather features on model predictions. Each dot represents a sample (n = 500), with feature values color-coded (red = high, blue = low). Horizontal axis shows SHAP values (higher = positive prediction impact); vertical axis lists features by importance (top to bottom). Dew point (DPoint_C_Avg) dominates predictions, while wind direction (CorrWindDir_deg) exhibits context-dependent effects.

Permutation importance We also employed the permutation importance technique51 to explain features, which is model agnostic and can be used for global explainability. In this approach, for each feature, the values are randomly shuffled to break the relationship with the target and measure the drop in model performance (here, averaged MSE across all six outputs). A significant performance drop indicates that a feature is important. Figure 8 shows the feature importance for the FTTransformer model. It can be observed that relative humidity and dew point features are the most important, meaning that if their values are shuffled, the model’s predictions degrade significantly.

Average permutation importance of input features for the FTTransformer model across six target variables. The bar heights represent the mean decrease in model performance when each feature is randomly shuffled, with error bars indicating standard deviation. Relative Humidity and Dew Point variables exhibit the highest importance, indicating strong global influence on the model’s predictions.

The features identified by permutation importance and SHAP analysis may differ due to the distinct criteria employed by each method to assess feature significance. While SHAP measures the contribution of a feature to a single prediction, permutation importance measures the impact on model performance when a feature is corrupted. The scope of SHAP is local and captures features interaction, whereas permutation importance’s scope is global and treats features independently. From both analyses, it is clear that dew point features are the most important and humidity, wind direction and air density are also useful. This further justifies the necessity of collecting different climate variables to ensure accurate multi-output temperature predictions.

Impact and limitations

Our results showed that high resolution and multi-output temperature parameters (six in our case) in Kuwait from 2021 to 2024 can be predicted with high reliability from other climate variables in a manner that generalizes across 4 years. The FTTransformer and LSTM regressor emerged as the best choice among the ten different regressors including a baseline, ML, DL and sequential models. This result has implications for sensor fault analysis, missing data imputation and understanding drivers for variations in temperatures in microclimate situations. The high fidelity results lead to other critical scenarios, such as predicting temperatures without direct temperature sensors in situations where these sensors may fail or cannot be calibrated. The results are also valuable for reducing sensor redundancy, missing data reconstruction, quality control and sensor validation. The multi-output LOYO CV results are non-trivial and demonstrate the stability of the weather in the Kuwait region during the study period. This approach can be used for deployments where only certain sensors are feasible. The multi-output modeling of six temperature variables at a high frequency (every 5 min) is valuable for urban heat studies, building energy models, agricultural and environmental control systems. Moreover, the LOYO CV approach is a sound experimental design that provides a reproducible framework and benchmark for others using similar environmental/ecological setups.

The overall four years span of the AUMET data is insufficient to infer climate changes due to its slow progression. The 5-min sampling rate of each data point could also overwhelm sequential models with a large amount of training data, thus hindering their generalization capabilities. We used a sequence length of 12 samples, which may be insufficient to capture long-term temporal dependencies, such as diurnal patterns, sudden or anomalous climate change across days and delayed effects of solar radiation and humidity. Therefore, sequence length serves as a hyperparameter in this context and may be adjusted to inform decisions. The AUMET dataset has several missing values; model performance could be improved by adequately imputing these values. The models were trained on 30 climate variables, including features extracted from the time of the day. The regression models could be improved by incorporating additional contextual information, such as geography, air quality, cloud cover, wind dynamics, and elevation. Techniques based on domain adaptation and co-variate shift detection could help in modeling divergent years (e.g., Years 1 and 2 in our case). The DL models were trained for only 20 epochs, increasing the number of training epochs could further improve model performance. The overall study findings are limited by Kuwait specific regional data that may not be generalizable to other geographical regions.

Conclusions and future work

We conducted a comprehensive investigation of predicting multi-output air and surface temperatures in Kuwait for the years 2021 to 2024 based on various climate variables. We introduced a LOYO CV framework to establish relationship between multiple years’ climate data in predicting six temperature outcomes simultaneously. This study demonstrates the efficacy of advanced multi-output deep learning models for high-frequency temperature forecasting in arid environments. Among the ten regression models, FTTransformer and LSTM achieved an average of 0.998, with MSE of 0.13 and MAE of 0.24 across unseen years. Overall, FTTransformer emerged as the most reliable regressor, with LSTM and RF as other simpler options but with lower performance. LSTM struggles to generalize when either past data contained anomalous or missing values. These results indicate that even fine-grained, 5-min interval temperatures can be reliably predicted from other climate variables. Model explainability analysis showed the importance of collecting various climate variables in making accurate multi-output temperature predictions.

While our results demonstrate the ability of multi-output deep learning models to predict high-frequency temperature variations, we emphasize that this work is positioned within a climate monitoring context rather than operational weather forecasting. The 5-min resolution data allow us to capture weather-scale variability, but the multi-year scope highlights inter-annual stability, making the findings most relevant for long-term climate resilience planning in Kuwait. These findings have practical implications for ecotourism planning and related sectors in Kuwait. Accurate temperature forecasts at daily and seasonal horizons can help optimize outdoor recreational activities, inform safe scheduling of desert tours, and guide infrastructure planning for tourist comfort during peak heat months. More broadly, robust short- to medium-term forecasts enhance preparedness for extreme temperature events, which are critical for sustaining ecotourism in arid regions vulnerable to climate extremes.

In the future, we will perform time series anomaly detection analysis to identify abnormal time variations and the reasons leading to them. The robust performance of FTTransformer across several years can be used to impute missing climate data to assess whether it improves the overall regression performance. Imputing missing values could improve the performance of sequential models (e.g., LSTM, TCN, Transformer) because some of the rows were removed due to missing data that could have led to incorrect sequences. A near term objective is to utilize this data for forecasting temperatures over both short- and long-term horizons and at various time resolutions, as well assessing the stability of forecasting the climate in the Kuwait region using relevant metrics, such as Theil’s U2 and the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency. Lastly, the data from different weather stations across Kuwait could be used to cross-validate each other and build reliable climate modeling techniques.
