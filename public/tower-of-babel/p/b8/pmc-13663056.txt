Temporal alignment of UAV hyperspectral and phenotype data via autoencoders for predicting nitrogen and nicotine content in cured tobacco leaves
Authors: Mingzheng Zhang, Yan Kuai, Dong Chen, Weimin Guo, Long Zhao, Shuai Yuan, Ruomei Zhao, Qiang Xu, Xiaohe Gu, Tian’en Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-curing nitrogen content (cNg) and nicotine content (cNt) constitute fundamental chemical attributes that govern the quality of flue-cured tobacco leaves. In this study, we investigate the use of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)-borne hyperspectral imagery in conjunction with advanced sequence-learning models to obtain canopy-level spectral information and to quantitatively predict cNg and cNt at the field scale. Nevertheless, because field experiments conducted in different growing seasons follow heterogeneous observation schedules, the resulting time series differ in both length and temporal sampling density, which poses a substantial obstacle to the direct deployment of recurrent neural-network models. To mitigate this limitation, we devised three autoencoder-based temporal-alignment schemes to reconcile the temporal dimension of multi-year spectral–phenotypic sequences, namely a fully connected autoencoder (AEF), a one-dimensional convolutional autoencoder (AEC), and a long short-term memory (LSTM) autoencoder (AEL). Within this framework, the autoencoders project the original multi-temporal observations into compact latent representations and subsequently reconstruct them on a standardized temporal grid, thereby preserving salient spectral–phenotypic information while enforcing dimensional consistency across years. For comparative analysis, we additionally considered a straightforward time-step removal (TSR) procedure, which discards non-overlapping observation dates among different years and thus serves as a baseline temporal-harmonization strategy. On the temporally aligned sequences, two representative recurrent-neural architectures—long short-term memory (LSTM) networks and gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks—were trained to establish predictive models for cNg and cNt. Overall, models trained on inputs aligned by the autoencoder-based schemes exhibited markedly higher predictive skill than their TSR-based counterparts, with the convolutional and LSTM autoencoders consistently yielding greater improvements than the fully connected variant. Among all evaluated configurations, the combinations of AEL with LSTM and with GRU delivered the best performance, attaining coefficients of determination of 0.73 for cNg and 0.56 for cNt on the independent test set. Taken together, these results indicate that the proposed autoencoder-based temporal-alignment framework can effectively distil informative features from multi-year UAV hyperspectral and phenotypic observations and constitutes a promising tool for the quantitative prediction of post-curing quality indicators in flue-cured tobacco production.

Introduction

Post-curing nitrogen content (cNg) and nicotine content (cNt) are widely recognized as primary chemical indices for evaluating the quality of flue-cured tobacco leaves. Variations in these constituents markedly modulate the sensory attributes of the final product, including smoke smoothness and irritation, and are therefore central to both quality grading and health-risk management [15]. From a production perspective, reliable, spatially explicit prediction of cNg and cNt prior to marketing is crucial for optimizing processing strategies and implementing harm-reduction measures. In parallel, unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)–based hyperspectral remote sensing (HRS) has emerged as an important platform for large-area, non-destructive and fine-resolution monitoring of crop status [9, 22, 23], providing rapid canopy reflectance observations that can be leveraged to construct quantitative models of tobacco quality–related indicators.

In the broader field of crop phenotyping, a variety of quantitative modelling approaches have been established on the basis of UAV-borne HRS data to characterize functional relationships between canopy spectra and agronomic or physiological traits [10, 16, 21]. In such studies, hyperspectral observations and ground measurements are typically acquired concurrently during the growing period. Tobacco production, by contrast, is distinguished from major staple cereals such as wheat, rice and maize by an intensive post-harvest curing stage. During this stage, mature leaves are held in curing barns for about one week, during which substantial moisture loss and pronounced biochemical transformations, in particular chlorophyll degradation, occur [3, 20]. The resulting quality grade of the cured leaves directly governs their market price, so prior knowledge of post-curing cNg and cNt for individual curing batches is of substantial practical relevance for producers. Against this backdrop, the present work aims to infer cNg and cNt of cured tobacco leaves by integrating UAV-based hyperspectral measurements with phenotypic traits recorded during the field growth stages.

Time-series vegetation indices (VIs) derived from optical remote-sensing data have been extensively exploited to link crop developmental dynamics with final yield or quality outcomes. For instance, Chang et al. [2] acquired normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) values at ten-day intervals beginning 30 days after tobacco transplanting and then applied stepwise multiple regression to estimate final yield, whereas Kamir et al. [8] combined multi-year biweekly NDVI series with an ensemble of base learners to predict wheat yield. Other investigations have incorporated phenological stage descriptors and crop-type information into VI-based models to further enhance yield estimation performance [8, 13]. Notwithstanding these advances, such approaches are conceptually tailored to multispectral imagery with a small number of broad spectral bands and may not fully exploit the rich spectral detail and narrow-band information provided by hyperspectral datasets [11, 18].

Moreover, conventional machine-learning algorithms, although effective for static regression problems, are intrinsically limited in their capacity to model temporal dependencies embedded in multi-stage spectral trajectories. When fitted independently to single-date observations, they largely neglect the cumulative influence of sequential growth stages on post-harvest crop quality. In response to this limitation, a growing body of research has employed recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to describe the temporal evolution of spectral and ancillary variables for predicting crop yield and quality. Architectures such as bidirectional RNNs (BRNNs), long short-term memory (LSTM) networks and gated recurrent units (GRUs) have been applied in diverse agricultural contexts. For example, Shen et al.[12] combined an LSTM network with a random-forest model to forecast wheat yield from multi-stage VIs and canopy water-stress indices,Cheng et al. [4] developed a graph-based RNN framework for winter wheat yield prediction,and. Yu et al. [17] extracted features from full-spectrum hyperspectral data using an Inception-style module and subsequently drove several RNN variants to estimate lettuce quality indicators under contrasting irrigation regimes. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that RNN-based architectures are particularly suitable for time-series prediction tasks related to post-harvest crop quality and yield [5, 14]. Accordingly, in the present study we adopt RNN models to capture the relationships between temporally resolved predictors and the post-curing quality indicators cNg and cNt of flue-cured tobacco leaves [7].

In practical field campaigns, UAV hyperspectral images acquired in different years are seldom collected at an identical sequence of observation dates, because flight operations are constrained by agronomic management, weather conditions and logistical considerations. Persistent rainfall, for example, may substantially reduce the number of usable flight days in a given season, thereby producing unequal numbers of acquisitions and irregular temporal spacing across years. Such discrepancies in the temporal dimension markedly complicate the construction of RNN models, which commonly assume input sequences of fixed length and comparable sampling patterns. While extensive efforts have been devoted to engineering spectral features from hyperspectral data—through, for instance, band selection, dimensionality reduction or feature extraction—relatively few studies have systematically addressed the problem of harmonizing the temporal dimension itself [19].

To explicitly resolve the aforementioned temporal inconsistencies, we design three autoencoder-based schemes that transform multi-year input sequences with different numbers of observations into a unified temporal representation: a fully connected autoencoder (AEF), a one-dimensional convolutional autoencoder (AEC) and an LSTM-based autoencoder (AEL). Autoencoders are unsupervised neural-network models that learn to encode high-dimensional data into compact latent features and subsequently reconstruct the original inputs, and have been widely adopted for dimensionality reduction, feature extraction and noise suppression [6]. Within the present framework, the three autoencoders are exploited to harmonize the temporal dimension of the predictors. In addition, to enrich the predictor space and strengthen the physiological interpretability of the models, UAV-derived hyperspectral features are combined with two field-measured phenotypic traits—leaf nitrogen content (LNC) and leaf chlorophyll content (LCC) of fresh leaves during the growing period—to form joint spectral–phenotypic inputs [1]. The temporally aligned versions of these inputs produced by the three autoencoders are then supplied to RNN models for cNg and cNt prediction. Materials and methods

Experiment description

The experiments employed a randomized complete block design, with six fields in 2022 (S1–S6) and five fields in 2023 (S2 was removed). Each field, approximately 1000 m2 in size, was divided into three replicates (R1–R3), with each replicate containing seven plots (N0-N150), receiving varying levels of nitrogen fertilizer in 25% increments. N100 corresponds to the standard farmer practice, N0 to the omission of nitrogen, and N150 to 150% of N100; the intermediate levels were scaled proportionally. Each plot contained at least 80 plants with a row × plant spacing of 1.2 m × 0.5 m. Other agronomic operations were consistent with local management guidelines.

UAV hyperspectral data acquisition and preprocessing

Hyperspectral imagery was collected using a GaiaSky-mini2-VN® line-scanning camera (Dualix Spectral Imaging Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) mounted on a DJI Matrice 600 Pro® hexacopter (DJI, Shenzhen, China). Three missions (T1–T3) were flown in 2022 and two missions (T4–T5) in 2023 at key growth stages listed in Table 2. Because of persistent rainfall and earlier maturity in 2023, no additional flights could be scheduled that year. All images were acquired on cloud-free days with light winds between 10:00 and 15:00 local time (UTC+8), at an altitude of 80 m above ground level, resulting in a spatial resolution of 3.2 cm. Before each mission a grey reference panel was imaged for subsequent radiometric calibration. Radiometric and geometric corrections of the raw hyperspectral cubes were carried out in ENVI 5.6 (Exelis Visual Information Solutions, Boulder, CO, USA). Reflectance spectra at the plot level were derived in three steps. First, image radiance was converted to surface reflectance using the grey reference panel. Second, canopy pixels were isolated by computing the excess green (ExG) index from co-registered RGB images and applying a threshold to remove soil, shadow and weed pixels. Third, the mean reflectance of the remaining tobacco pixels within each plot was calculated. To reduce noise, the resulting spectra were smoothed with a Savitzky–Golay filter implemented in Python 3.12, using a window size of 15 bands and a second-order polynomial. To better represent the spectral behaviour of tobacco canopies and reduce data dimensionality, the smoothed spectra were further transformed using the segmented fitting for integral (SFI) method. In brief, the full hyperspectral curve was divided into several wavelength segments according to stable extrema in the first-order derivative curve, an appropriate fitting function was fitted to each segment, and the definite integral over each segment was used as a compact spectral feature. The resulting SFI features were used as hyperspectral inputs in subsequent modelling.

Phenotypic traits collection

Phenotypic measurements were obtained at two stages, namely for fresh leaves in the field and for cured leaves. For the field measurements, sampling was carried out on the same dates as the UAV missions. In each plot, two fully expanded leaves were clipped from the upper-middle canopy of two representative plants and placed in paper bags labelled with the field, replicate and nitrogen treatment (e.g., S1-R1-N100). The samples were transported immediately to a commercial laboratory (Yunnan Sanbiao Agro-forestry Technology Co., Ltd., China) for analysis of leaf nitrogen concentration (LNC). Leaf chlorophyll content (LCC) was assessed in situ using a SPAD-502 Plus chlorophyll meter (Konica Minolta Sensing Inc., Osaka, Japan). For each plot, five plants were selected, one middle leaf per plant was measured, and five SPAD readings were taken per leaf following a five-point pattern. The mean of the 25 readings was used as the LCC value of that plot.

Quality traits of cured leaves were determined after flue-curing. In each plot, five representative plants were labelled, and leaves harvested from these plants were kept separate and marked throughout the curing process (Fig. 1). When curing was completed, ten representative cured leaves were selected from every plot and analysed in the laboratory to determine the total nitrogen concentration (cNg) and nicotine concentration (cNt) of cured tobacco. The plot-level cNg and cNt values were calculated as the average of the ten measurements.

Modeling methods

Input feature construction

Based on our previous methodology [20], we applied the Segmented Fitting for Integral (SFI) technique. This approach partitions the spectral curve into five distinct regions, fitting each with a specific function to compute definite integrals. Consequently, the 175 original bands were compressed into five feature values, capturing both reflectance intensity and curve morphology. These SFI features were then integrated with LNC and LCC values to form a 7-dimensional input vector.

Alignment of the time dimension

To address the dimensional inconsistency (3 time steps in 2022 vs. 2 in 2023), three autoencoder architectures—AEF, AEC, and AEL—were developed to map the 2022 data from 3 × 7 to 2 × 7. Next, the working principle of the these autoencoders will be described using a given sample as an example. First, the encoder architecture of AEF is depicted in Figure 4 (with a symmetrical decoder). The 3×7 feature vector is flattened into a 21-dimensional vector, processed through a fully connected (FC) layer to produce a 28-dimensional feature map, and finally compressed into a 14-dimensional latent vector. This latent vector (1×14) is then reshaped into the target 2×7 input format.Fig. 4Encoder part of the AEF

Encoder part of the AEF

Second, Figure 5 displays the encoder part of AEC. For a given sample, the input is reshaped to 7×3. A convolution operation (kernel size 1×2, stride 1) is applied along the feature dimension, reducing the sequence to 2 time steps. Subsequent processing mirrors AEF, transforming the 28×2 dimension to a 1×14 latent vector via two FC layers.Fig. 5Encoder part of the AEC

Encoder part of the AEC

Third, the encoder of AEL is shown in Figure 6. AEL processes the sequence using LSTM units. The hidden state (ht) at each time step serves as the output. Similar to previous models, the data dimension is eventually transformed from 1×84 to 1×14 through FC layers.Fig. 6Encoder part of the AEL

Encoder part of the AEL

After autoencoder processing, the 2022 data dimensions (3×7) are aligned with the 2023 data (2×7). For performance benchmarking, a Time Step Removal (TSR) method was selected as the baseline, which simply discards data from the T1 time step to achieve the 2×7 dimension.

The autoencoder-based alignment learns a latent representation that extracts essential temporal features across different growth stages. Unlike simple interpolation, this approach allows the model to prioritize data-driven patterns that are most predictive of the final cured leaf quality, effectively harmonizing the temporal dimension while preserving critical information.

Modeling results of cNg and cNt prediction

A total of 16 models were developed, integrating four data reconstruction methods (TSR, AEF, AEC, and AEL) with two modeling algorithms (LSTM and GRU) for cNg and cNt prediction. Consistent parameters were maintained across all models: two LSTM/GRU layers with 32 neurons each, followed by a LeakyReLU activation and a fully connected layer. The output layer dimension was set to 1. The learning rate and batch size were 0.001 and 12, respectively. To mitigate overfitting, epochs were constrained to the range [50, 100], and models satisfying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R - R_{v}^{2} \le 0.3$$\end{document} were selected.

During the hyperparameter optimization phase, multiple training runs with different initializations were conducted to verify the consistency of the results. It was observed that while absolute performance values exhibited minor variations, the performance ranking of different architectures (i.e., AEL-based models consistently outperforming TSR-based models) remained stable, confirming the reliability of the proposed temporal alignment framework.

Discussion

Performance and universality analysis of autoencoders

The statistical outcomes presented in Table 4 reveal a distinct performance hierarchy among the temporal alignment strategies under identical modeling conditions: TSR < AEF < AEC ≈ AEL. The inferior performance of the TSR approach is largely attributed to the direct exclusion of data from the T1 time step, which represents the period most distant from the curing phase and leads to significant information loss.

Regarding the autoencoder variants, while they all align dimensions and extract features, their capacity to model temporal dynamics varies significantly. Specifically, AEF treats inputs as flat vectors, failing to account for temporal sequences. In contrast, AEC utilizes convolutional kernels to extract local dependencies between adjacent time steps, whereas the architecture of AEL allows for the capture of global temporal dependencies across the entire sequence. Consequently, models based on AEL consistently yielded the optimal results, with the AEL-LSTM model achieving an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{v}^{2}$$\end{document} of 0.73 for cNg and the AEL-GRU model reaching an\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{v}^{2}$$\end{document} of 0.56 for cNt.

Given the vulnerability of open-field agronomic trials to meteorological variability—particularly in Southwest China's mountainous terrain where summer rainfall frequently disrupts UAV operations—incomplete datasets are a common challenge. The autoencoder frameworks proposed herein offer a robust solution for rectifying dimensional inconsistencies in multi-year datasets. By employing convolutional or LSTM layers, these models adjust temporal dimensions while preserving essential sequential information. Furthermore, the flexibility to configure the latent vector size allows for simultaneous feature dimensionality reduction. Thus, this autoencoder-based methodology holds substantial practical value for processing irregular time-series hyperspectral data.

This phenomenon can be explained by the learning dynamics: in the early stages, the networks effectively assimilated feature patterns from the training data, reducing errors and boosting both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{t}^{2}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{v}^{2}$$\end{document}. The LSTM architecture, with its robust memory cells and gating mechanisms, demonstrated greater resilience to overfitting compared to the GRU, which, due to its simplified structure, was more sensitive to parameter updates and prone to earlier performance degradation on unseen data.

Comparison of cNg and cNt prediction results

A marked disparity is evident in the prediction accuracies (Table 4), with cNg models consistently outperforming those for cNt. This performance gap is likely driven by the feature composition: the input variables include LNC measurements from various growth stages, which hold a strong intrinsic correlation with the final cNg. In contrast, no direct precursor for nicotine (such as field nicotine content) was included in the input features, and the correlation between vegetative LNC and cured leaf nicotine is relatively weak.

To validate this assumption, we conducted an additional analysis to isolate the contributions of spectral features (SF) and LNC data. The dataset was split into two groups: SF only, and SF combined with LNC. Data dimension alignment was performed using AEC, followed by modeling via LSTM and GRU.

Figure 12 displays the results for models relying solely on SF. Even in this scenario, cNg prediction (R2v≈ 0.63–0.65) remains superior to that of cNt (R2v≈ 0.47–0.49). This performance gap is rooted in their distinct physiological and optical properties. Nitrogen is intrinsically coupled with chlorophyll, exhibiting robust absorption in the visible and red-edge regions (400–750 nm), which provides a high-fidelity spectral proxy. In contrast, nicotine is a secondary metabolite synthesized in roots and translocated to leaves. It lacks dominant spectral signatures in the 400–1000 nm range, rendering its remote sensing signal indirect and sensitive to environmental noise. Furthermore, nicotine levels are heavily modulated by agronomic practices like topping and desuckering, as well as complex enzymatic activities during the curing process, which further decouple final quality from in-field canopy reflectance.Fig. 12Ng and cNt prediction models based on SF

In summary, due to differences in spectral response mechanisms, cNg shows a stronger correlation with spectral features compared to cNt. As a result, cNg prediction models built using spectral features perform significantly better than cNt prediction models. Moreover, LNC exhibits stronger spatiotemporal coupling with cNg. Within the RNN framework, LNC collected at different growth stages can be regarded as nitrogen content at previous time steps, while cNg represents the nitrogen content at the next time step. Therefore, LNC data substantially enhance the performance of cNg prediction models, supporting the validity of our hypothesis.

Limitations and future work

In this study, a randomized split was used to validate the proposed framework. Future work should involve larger multi-year datasets to perform grouped split evaluations (e.g., leave-one-year-out) and incorporate environmental covariates (e.g., temperature and precipitation) to further test model robustness across extreme climatic variations. Furthermore, exploring multi-task learning architectures to jointly predict nitrogen and nicotine could leverage their physiological correlations to improve prediction stability. Additionally, incorporating interpretability tools such as SHAP or attention mechanisms could provide deeper insights into the specific growth stages that contribute most to post-curing trait prediction.

Conclusions

This study combines UAV-borne HRS data with time-series phenotypic traits from fresh tobacco leaves to develop post-curing nitrogen and nicotine prediction models. Data form two years with different time dimensions were unified using three designed autoencoders. We also used LSTM and GRU to develop prediction models.
