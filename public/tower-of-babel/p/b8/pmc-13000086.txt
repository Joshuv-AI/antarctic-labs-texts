Skin bacteriota ameliorates androgenetic alopecia via harmonizing skin immuno inflammatory balance
Authors: Yichen Cao, Xusheng Wu, Zixuan Huang, Chen Xu, Zengyan Ge, Yixin Xie, Lan Wu, Ying Qian, Bin Ding, Hongbin Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human skin is the largest heterogeneous organ that protects the body from pathogens such as bacteria, fungi, mites, and viruses. The coexistence of skin microorganisms associated with the host helps maintain tissue integrity and immune homeostasis. This study aimed to investigate the effect of the skin microbiome on hair growth in androgenic alopecia (AGA) model mice. The dihydrotestosterone induced AGA model mice were engaged in this study. The ABF (Antibiotic-Free) and ABT (Antibiotic-Treated) mice were topically administrated with ddH2O and antibiotics, respectively. Seven days later, the three of mice in the ABF and ABT group were co-housed. Compared to ABF mice, ABT mice exhibited sparse hair, thinner skin, and fewer hair follicles, all of which has been improved in the Co-housed ABF/ABT mice. The translational expression of β-catenin and Wnt5a, as well as the mRNA levels of Vegfa, Fgfr, Igf1, and Lef1 decreased in ABT mice but recovered in ABF/ABT mice. Increased the expression of TLR4 and NF-κB, IL-6, and the mRNA levels of Il-17 levels in ABT mice compared to ABF mice indicated inflammation in dorsal skin. Cohousing notably affected Il-6, Il-17, Il-23, and Tnfα levels. The 16S rDNA sequencing results and the correlative analysis indicated that the relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Proteus might be the key genus influencing the immuno-inflammatory balance. Our findings suggest that restoring the dorsal skin bacteriota promotes hair growth in AGA model mice. This improvement is likely mediated by the modulation of the skin’s inflammatory microenvironment. Importance. This study highlights the critical role of skin bacteriota in modulating immuno-inflammatory balance in AGA, suggesting that restoration of bacteriota may contribute to the amelioration of DHT-induced hair loss by regulating TLR4/NF-κB pathways, which offers a novel insight for bacterial -based therapies. Introduction

The skin, which has an average surface area of 1.8–2 m2 in adults, is the biggest organ protecting our body, and defending against harmful factors from the outside world [1, 2]. Skin performs multiple functions, such as thermoregulation, sensory signaling, sebum secretion, metabolic waste excretion, and immune-inflammation responses. Hence, keeping skin healthy is critical. The disturbances of the symbiotic microbial population of the skin can impact the health of the skin and frequently result in the onset of illness [3]. The microbial community includes bacteria, viruses, fungi, and mites. In comparison with the others, the bacteria community is highly focused [4]. The research indicated that the phyla Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes were the most popular in skin [5]. The host supplies the skin flora with a survival space and nutrients, simultaneously the skin flora defends the host against pathogen invasion and controls the immune system [6]. If the balance of the skin flora is destroyed, opportunistic infectors or harmful factors would lead to skin illnesses, including hair loss [7]. In decades, many studies have indicated a correlation between changes in the skin microbiota and hair growth. Specifically, skin symbiotic microorganisms stimulate skin and hair follicle regeneration by stimulating the keratinocyte-dependent IL-1β/IL-1R/Myd88 signaling pathway [8]. Skin microorganisms can enhance hair follicle regeneration by altering glutamine metabolism [9]. However, little evidence indicates the role of skin bacteria in the pathogenesis of androgenetic alopecia (AGA).

AGA is the most frequent kind of hair loss [10], which is characterized by progressive hair follicle atrophy driven by dihydrotestosterone (DHT) [11–14]. The research indicated that compared with the healthy group, AGA subjects showed a significant increase of Cutibacterium acnes and Stenotrophomonas geniculate with more grease on their scalp [15]. All these findings suggested that skin bacteria might be a therapeutic target for AGA treatment. Hence, in this study, we demonstrated whether restoration of the microbiome could ameliorate AGA with a DHT-induced murine model. This study provided a novel remedy for AGA treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials

The mixed antibiotics consisted of polymyxin B sulfate (Meilunbio, 1405-20−5) 200 µg/mL, glycopeptide (Solarbio, B8181) 200 µg/mL, streptomycin sulfate (Solarbio, S8290) 100 µg/mL and ciprofloxacin (Solarbio, C9710) 250 µg/mL, ceftazidime (Solarbio, C9730) 200 µg/mL were configured. Anti-Wnt5a (BOSTER, BA2839), anti-β-catenin (BOSTER, BA0426), anti-GAPDH (BOSTER, A00227-1), Anti-TLR4 (BOSTER, A00017-3), and anti-NF-κB (BOSTER, BA0610), RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, P0013K), BeyoBCA Rapid Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0398L), BeyoECL Plus (Beyotime, P0018M), RNase H- kit (Beyotime, D7168M), Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit(Beyotime, C0105S), Alanine aminotransferase Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, C009-2-1), Aspartate aminotransferase Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, C010-2-1) were employed in this study.

Laboratory animal and animal license

The Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University approved all animal experiments [NO. IACUC-20230918-13]. Eighteen, 6-week-old, male C57BL/6 mice, with an average body weight of 21.0 ± 0.5 g were provided by Shanghai Slaughter Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd [Laboratory Animal Production License No. SCXK (Shanghai) 2022-0004]. They were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University [Laboratory Animal Use License No. SYXK(Zhe)2021-0012], under ABF conditions, which are 20 ± 2℃, 40%~70% humidity, 12/12 light/dark cycle, and free access to food and water.

Animal modeling, treatment and grouping

Following a one-week acclimatization period, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (10 mg/mL). The dorsal hair was shaved and completely removed using depilatory cream (OLOEY, Cat. No. 702459). To establish the androgenetic alopecia (AGA) murine model, all animals intraperitoneally received 200 µL 2 mg/mL dihydrotestosterone (DHT) in every three days during the experiment. Simultaneously the mice were randomly separated into two groups: the Antibiotic-Free (ABF) group (n = 12) and the Antibiotic-Treated (ABT) group (n = 12). The mice in the ABT group were topically treated with 200 µL of the mixture of antibiotics (detailly described in 2.1). And the mice in the ABF group received 200 µL sterile double-distilled water (ddH₂O) as control. The antibiotics were administered at 24-hour intervals for 7 consecutive days. On the 8th day, 6 ABT mice were randomly co-housed with 6 ABF mice (ABF/ABT group), facilitating horizontal transmission of cutaneous microbiota via direct contact and cohabitation [8]. All mice were photographed on days 7, 13, and 16 to monitor the hair growth. On day 16, dorsal skin microbiota were collected using sterile swabs, followed by euthanasia for the collection of serum and dorsal skin tissues.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

The in paraffin embedded skin tissue was sliced into 5 μm sections. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene, dehydrated with ethanol, and washed with ddH2O. Then these sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated with aqueous hydrochloric acid for 2 s, and soaked in water after being counterstained with aqueous ammonia for 15 s. After dehydration with 95% ethanol, the sections were stained in eosin solution for 5 s. These stained slices were washed with ddH2O and dehydrated with ethanol with gradually increased concentrations. Finally, the sections were permeabilized, sealed, and photographed with a microscope. The hair follicle density and skin thickness were quantified with ImageJ software.

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) assay tests

The tests were progressed according to the instructions supplied with the ALT and AST determination kits. Detailly, the collected murine sera were incubated with kit supplied 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution for 20 min and glutamate aminotransferase substrate solution for 20 min at 37℃ respectively. After adding 200 µL of sodium hydroxide solution, the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 24℃. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm with an ultraviolet spectrophotometer, and the associated ALT/GPT or AST/GOT units were calculated with the standard curve.

Protein immunoblotting experiment (Western blot, WB)

Skin tissues were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer, and the proteins were collected by centrifugation. Protein concentration of the lysate was determined with a BCA protein assay kit. Subsequently, 40 µg protein was separated by SDS-PAGE (8–12%) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) via electrophoresis. After a 20-minute incubation with rapid blocking buffer, the membranes were treated with the specific primary antibodies overnight at 4℃. The following day, the membranes were washed with TBST and hybridized with the appropriate secondary antibody for 1 h. The specific luminous signal was developed with enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and recorded with a chemiluminescence imaging analysis system (Tanon, China).

Reversed real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated via the TRIzol method (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The RNA was subsequently reverse transcribed with ReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Master Mix (QPX-201, TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). For each qRT‒PCR assay, 50 ng of the reverse transcribed product was used as template and mixed with Fast Mix Quantitative RT‒PCR Master Mix (MQ10201S, Monad, Suzhou, China) as well as the specific primers in a total volume of 20 µL. Amplification and real-time analysis were performed on a Step One Plus System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

16 S rDNA sequencing and analysis

An E.Z.N.A.®soil kit was used to extract the total microbial genomic DNA. Nanodrop 2000 and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis were used to identify the DNA’s concentration and quality, respectively. Illumina’s MiSeq PE300 platform was engaged to create a MiSeq library by sequencing the variable region of V3–V4 by PCR amplification. Bioinformatics analysis was conducted with the MiSeq BioCloud platform. Beta diversity was evaluated via Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and visualized through Venn diagrams to identify shared and unique microbial species among groups. To identify key microbial signatures significantly enriched in specific groups, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was employed with an LDA score threshold. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was utilized to examine the relationships between the dominant bacterial genera and inflammatory factors.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1. Every experiment was repeated three times at least, and the data were shown as mean ± standard error of the mean. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used as a parametric method. Perform inter group comparison of β diversity using PERMANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The co-housing attenuates the effect of the topic application of antibiotics on hair growth in AGA mice

On the 13th and 16th day, the hair growth in mice of ABF and ABF/ABT mice was more evident, in comparison with that of the ABT group (Fig. 1a). Compared with the ABT group, the skin thickness was increased and hair follicle numbers per field were multiplied in ABF and ABF/ABT group mice (Fig. 1b、Fig. 1c). Additionally, no obviously a variation of ALT and AST content has been found in murine serum of different groups, which indicated that antibiotic treatment did not show significant liver toxicity (Fig. 1d). Together, these results demonstrated that the application of antibiotics could impact hair growth in AGA model mice. And mixing house with ABF mice, the hair follicle density of ABT mice has been statistically recovered.

The co-housing reverses the attenuative effect of antibiotics on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in the skin of AGA mice

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is one of the key pathways in hair follicle generation. Hence, we comparatively identified the expression of Wnt5a and β-catenin in different groups. In comparison with the ABF group, the expression of the level of these two proteins was statistically reduced in the ABT, which was significantly recoved in ABF/ABT mice (Fig. 2a). We next comparatively investigated the transcriptional expression of Vegfa, Fgfr, Igf1, and Lfe1. The result demonstrated that the antibiotics significantly affect the level of the aforementioned factors. And the co-housing with ABF mice could reverse the expression of Vegfa and Fgfr. Although the levels of Lef1 and Igf1 were also increased in the ABF/ABT group, no statistical variation was observed (Fig. 2b).

The co-housing influences the application of antibiotics stimulated immune-inflammatory response in the skin of AGA mice

Our previous study demonstrated the dysregulative capability of DHT on the immune-inflammatory homonstates through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway [16]. To our knowledge, the application of antibiotics could also affect the balance of immune-inflammatory responses by destroying the homeostasis of microbiota [17]. To our surprise, the expression of TLR4 and NF-κB were extremely stimulated in the murine skin of the ABT group, which was slightly reversed in the co-housing (ABF/ABT) group (Fig. 3a). In the meanwhile, Compared with the ABF group, mRNA levels of Il-6, Il-17, Il-23 and Tnf-α were significantly elevated in ABT mice. Notably, these markers showed reduced expression in ABF/ABT mice (Fig. 3b). These results indicated that the co-housing attenuated the immune-inflammatory responses in the skin of AGA mice.

The co-housing reverses the relative enrichment of bacteria influencing the inflammatory responses in AGA mice

Alpha diversity analysis through 16 S rDNA sequencing showed that the ABT group significantly reduced the diversity and evenness of the skin microbiota, while the ABF/ABT group helped restore this diversity balance (Fig. 4a). Subsequently, the species sparsity curves, the Chao index, represented the successfully sequencing. The curves of each sample tended to flatten out, indicating that the current sequencing coverage basically covered the vast majority of microbial species (Fig. 4b). The result of LEfSe highly lighted that the phylum Campylobacterota, Enterococcus, and Chryseobacterium, were significantly enriched in the ABT group (Fig. 4c). The PCoA and Venn plot results showed that the microbial community structure of the ABT group exhibited high dispersion and was significantly different, in compared with the control group (Fig. 4d-e). In three groups, the most bacteria were in the phylum Proteobacteria and Firmicutes. The bacteria in Bacteroidota, Firmicutesb, Cyanobacteria, Campilobacterota, Patescibacteria, Desulfobacterota, Bdellovibrionota, Deinococcota, Acidobacteriota, Spirochaetota, Bacteroidota, and Cyanobacteria were increased in the ABT mice, in comparison with those in the ABF mice. On the contrary, the bacteria in Proteobacteria was decreased. Compared with the ABT mice, the relative enrichment of Bacteroidota, Cyanobacteria, Campilobacterota, Patescibacteria, and Desulfobacterota were reversed by co-housing. Although Proteobacteria was continually decreased (Fig. 4f). At the genus level, the relative dominant bacteria in the ABF group were Achromobacter, Aerococcus, and Staphylococcus. The enrichment of the Acinetobacter was increased in the ABT group, on the contrary, that of the Staphylococcus decreased. The relative enrichment of Sporosarcina was increased in the ABF/ABT group. Compared with the ABF group, the relative abundance of Achromobacter and Staphylococcus in the ABT group decreased, while the abundance of Aeromonas, Acinetobacter, Proteus, Lactobacillus and Enterococcus increased (Fig. 4g). According to the result of the spearman correlation analysis between the top 15 dominating species and inflammatory factors, g_Helicobacter was statistically correlated with IL-6, Lactobacillus and Proteus were positively and significantly correlated with IL-6 and TNF-α. On the contrary, Achromobacter and Sporosarcina were oppositely correlated with the changes of that inflammatory factor (Fig. 4h).

Discussion

Various microbiome plays a critical role in the maintenance of human health [18]. Recent studies have shown that the imbalance of the microbiome or bacterial community and the development of AGA were closely related [19]. However, few pricised results have been reported. It has been known that Propionibacterium acnes produced porphyrin and C3 could lead to inflammatory reactions and oxidative stress, resulting hair loss [20]. Another work demonstrated the increased C. acnes, as well as the decreased S. epidermidis in the AGA hair follicles. Moreover, studies found a significant increase in the number of Stenotropomonas geniculate in the scalp of AGA patients, which may lead to root sheath disintegration in hair follicles by promoting keratinolysis [21–23].

In this study, we observed that antibiotic-induced dysbiosis of skin bacteria significantly impaired hair follicle regeneration in AGA mice. This impairment was accompanied by the down-regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These findings suggest a potential association between hair growth and skin bacteria in ABF AGA mice. In this process, we used the co-housing method to identify the effect of microecology on AGA. By co-housing germ-messy mice with germ-fed mice, germ-messy mice can acquire microbial communities from germ-fed mice, thus mimicking the spread of microbial communities in the natural environment. This approach could help to clarify the causal effect of microbial community colonization on host physiological functions, such as hair growth [24].

Meanwhile, the result of 16 S rDNA sequencing showed that the cutaneous microbiota of the ABF/ABT mice was comparable to that of the ABF mice in terms of beta diversity, and that of the ABT mice was markedly differed from the other two groups. This indicated that antibiotic treatment led to more unstable factors in the skin microecology of mice, resulting in greater variations among individuals. Eventhough, no statistical differences of the bacteriota were obtained among the three groups at the phylum and genus levels. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria and Firmicutes were dominant. Detailly, compared with the ABF group, the level of Proteobacteria in the skin microecology of the ABT group decreased, and the co-housing treatment could not be reversed. This may be due to impaired skin barrier function and immune response disorders [25]. Certain bacteria in Proteobacteria, such as Xanthomonadaceae [26], are associated with keratinocytes in the skin, and their reduction may affect the normal metabolism and repair function of the skin. In contrast, there was a relative increase in the proportions of Bacteroidetes, Phylum Firmicutes, Cyanobacteria, Campilobacterota, Desulfobacterota, Aspergillus, Acidobacteriota and Spirochaetota. However, in the ABF/ABT group, the proportions of Anaplasma, Cyanobacteria, Campilobacterota, Actinobacteria and Desulfobacterota were reversed in comparison to ABT mice. There are many biological events inducing the aforementioned phenomenons. Specifically, certain antibiotic-sensitive strains, such as some Gram-positive bacteria, may be more susceptible to antibiotic inhibition. In contrast, certain strains of Bacteroides and Firmicutes exhibit stronger tolerance to antibiotics [27, 28]. As a result, these resistant strains gain a competitive advantage and greater opportunities for survival under antibiotic exposure. The exchange of genetic material between colonies may also change the resource competition pattern of the original colonies, resulting in the ability of strains of certain phyla to proliferate [29]. At the same time, antibiotics may alter the local microenvironment, allowing certain strains [30], such as those of Cyanobacteria phylum [31], to colonize better. Alterations in the host’s immune system may also reduce the immunosurveillance of the skin microecology, thus providing more favorable conditions for some strains to survive. At the genus level, the proportion of Achromobacter and Staphylococcus in the skin of mice in the ABT group decreased, at the same time, the relative abundance of Aeromonas, Lactobacillus, and Proteus increased, but it could rebound in ABF/ABT. Staphylococcus epidermidis, a common skin symbiotic bacteria, can inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria by producing antibacterial substances [32]. The decrease in the proportion of Achromobacter, an opportunistic pathogen, may be related to the particularity of the ABT environment [33]. Lactobacillus, a probiotic, can maintain skin health by regulating immune response and inhibiting pathogen growth [34]. The increase in Aeromonas and Proteus may reflect a change in microbial diversity [35]. In addition, the abundance of the dominant species, Proteus, was positively correlated with the expression of inflammatory factors. According to other research, Proteus and Aeromonas were the primary triggers of conditions such as cutaneous abscesses and vasculitis [36, 37], while Staphylococcus can help maintain the homeostasis of the skin barrier by producing protective ceramides [38]. It could also potentially associated with Proteus promote the Streptococcus defacement in host, reduce the inflammatory response, and accelerate wound healing [39]. Combined with these findings, it is fair to speculate that alterations of the skin microbiome are in the current study, who may influence skin barrier function by suppressing inflammation.

Numerrous studies demonstrated the impact of intestinal or skin microecology on human health. But the human body is a complex ecosystem, in which the microecology collaborate with organs in the host might affect the initiation and development of AGA. Hence, future studies need to consider the effect of holistic human microbiomes on AGA, to explore the microbiomes and their potential effects on AGA. Meanwhile, the effects of changes in specific bacteria or microbial communities on the growth cycle of hair follicles can be precisely investigated. Additionally, the mechanisms of interfering with hair growth through immune, neural, or endocrine pathways can also be explored with precise microbial intervention therapies. This will help to develop targeted therapeutic strategies to prevent or treat AGA by adjusting the gut or skin microecology to restore its balance. In terms of clinical applications, biomarkers for detecting microecological imbalances could be developed in the future. These biomarkers could be used to predict the risk of developing AGA or to assess the efficacy of treatments through simple assays, such as analysis of fecal samples or microbial sampling of the skin. In addition, by integrating knowledge and technologies from multiple disciplines such as microbiology, immunology, skin physiology, and clinical medicine [40, 41], and by conducting joint analyses of microbiomes and metabolomics, a deeper understanding of the effects of microecological imbalances on the host’s metabolism and immune response can be achieved. This approach can help identify more effective intervention targets and provide new ideas for the treatment of AGA.

Despite advancing our founding of the cutaneous microbiome’s role in AGA, this work still had the limitations. A primary constraint of this study is its reliance on an murine model, which may not fully recapitulate human disease pathophysiology. Otherwise the inflammation stimulating effects and the underlying mechanisms of the key genus, such as Lactobacillus and Proteus, would be addressed in subsequent studies. To our knowledge that DHT treatment stimulatied the expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in murine skin [42]. The protein level of other pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-17 and IL-23, were still need to be determined.
