Development and validation of a machine learning-based diagnostic system for 22 pediatric respiratory pathogens: a large-scale multicenter study
Authors: Dubin Su, Qun Chen, Ruizhi Xu, Qihong Chen, Chiyuan Ma, Xi Chen, Yunyun Yang, Feibo Qin, Ziwei Zhou, Sixian Li, Jie Peng, Dandan Ge, Jiajun Fan, Xingyu Li, Yanbo Wang, Yungang Yang, Yaping Guo, Jingjing Yang, Wanshan Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory tract infections (RTIs) are a significant cause of morbidity in children, caused by a wide range of pathogens. As treatment strategies depend on the causative pathogen, early and accurate diagnosis is crucial. We developed and validated an interpretable Pathogen Diagnostic System for Pediatric Respiratory Infections (Pathog-PDx), conducting a multicenter study involving 134,500 hospitalized children across three clinical centers and two databases. The model integrated 42 clinical and laboratory features from electronic health records (EHR) to enable early pathogen identification. Prospective validation was carried out on an independent cohort of 1338 children to assess the real-world applicability of the model. Pathog-PDx accurately distinguished 22 pathogen subtypes and outperformed conventional models in identifying both single and mixed infections. The model achieved high classification performance for key pathogens, such as influenza virus (AUC = 0.95; Sn: 0.88; Sp: 0.86), with mean AUCs of 0.88 for various pathogens of RTIs. The model has been deployed as a web-based decision support system, which is freely accessible at https://pathogpdx.zzu.edu.cn. Altogether, Pathog-PDx represents a potential tool for the early identification of respiratory tract pathogens in pediatric patients, which can provide actionable predictions ahead of conventional test results to guide timely and targeted therapy.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) represent a significant global health burden in children, causing approximately 344 million cases and 572k deaths according to the Global Burden of Disease study1–3. RTIs can be caused by bacterial, viral, and fungal pathogens, either as primary or secondary infections, and are classified into upper and lower respiratory infections based on the anatomical site of involvement. Upper respiratory infections are primarily viral, while lower respiratory infections are more commonly caused by bacterial pathogens, along with other microbial agents, such as Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP)4. The rapid progression of RTIs (typically 48–72 h) requires prompt empiric treatment. During this critical period, clinicians commonly initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics or antiviral therapies. However, such non-targeted approaches carry the risk of inappropriate treatment and contribute to antimicrobial resistance5. Early and accurate pathogen identification is crucial to avoid inappropriate treatment.

Diagnosing RTIs in children presents unique challenges due to their immature immune responses and the difficulty in articulating symptoms. Moreover, mixed infection, where multiple pathogens co-exist, occurs in approximately one-third of RTI cases and complicates both diagnosis and treatment. Mixed infection, such as MP and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), is commonly associated with refractory pneumonia6, which often responds poorly to standard therapies targeting individual pathogens. These factors result in atypical clinical presentations and more severe disease outcomes7.

Given these challenges, rapid and accurate pathogen diagnosis is crucial for guiding appropriate treatment and improving patient outcomes. Current diagnostic methods for RTIs face several limitations. Microbial cultures are slow due to extended incubation times and inaccuracy8, while polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is still limited by its inability to detect a broad range of pathogens9,10. Furthermore, the use of imaging studies in pediatric populations raises concerns about radiation exposure risks, and in community healthcare settings, there are challenges related to equipment accessibility11. Routine laboratory tests, such as complete blood count, can provide preliminary clues to infection but lack the specificity necessary for a definitive diagnosis without expert interpretation. Collectively, these challenges highlight the urgent need for more efficient, accurate, and accessible diagnostic tools for pediatric RTIs.

Recent applications of machine learning (ML) to electronic health records (EHR) have shown promise in RTIs diagnostics12–14. These models typically integrate clinical and laboratory data, but most focus on single pathogens, overlook mixed infection, and lack specificity for pediatric populations15,16. To address these limitations, we developed the Pathogen Diagnostic System for Pediatric Respiratory Infections (Pathog-PDx). This model is based on multi-center EHR data from 134,500 pediatric patients across three clinical centers and two large-scale databases. Pathog-PDx integrates clinical and laboratory features with disease onset site information to enable early identification of 22 RTI pathogens. Prospective validation was conducted, and a web-based clinical decision support platform was established. The Pathog-PDx model demonstrates potential for accurate pathogen identification, assisting physicians in enhancing clinical efficiency and personalizing the management of pediatric RTIs.

Study cohorts

A total of 134,500 hospitalized pediatric patients (<18 years) with comprehensive clinical and laboratory data were collected from five sources across two continents. After applying strict inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. S1), 85,349 patients were included for model development and validation. The derivation cohort, drawn from MIMIC-III (v2.2), PIC, and XMFH, was randomly split into a training set (N = 43,134) and an internal validation set (N = 18,486) in a 7:3 ratio. Meanwhile, data from XMCH and SSPH served as independent external validation cohorts (N = 11,426 and N = 12,303, respectively) to ensure model generalizability (Fig. S1).

Subset 1 (N = 85,349) was used for infection site classification, including upper respiratory infections, bronchitis, pulmonary infections, and non-infectious cases, as detailed in Table S1. Subset 2 (N = 15,220), derived from confirmed infection cases within Subset 1, was used to evaluate the model’s performance in predicting 22 pathogen subtypes (Table S2) and the risk of ICU admission. To further validate real-world performance, a prospective cohort of 1338 pediatric patients from SSPH (January to December 2024) was established. This prospective evaluation aimed to assess the predictive accuracy and clinical utility of Pathog-PDx in routine practice.

Pathog-PDx development and performance comparison

To achieve species-level identification of respiratory pathogens and prognosticate critical outcomes, we developed Pathog-PDx, a multi-task system that classifies 22 infectious pathogens and predicts ICU admission risk, integrating predicted sites of infection as an auxiliary factor in pathogen identification, applicable to both single infection and mixed infection (Fig. 1).

Based on Subset 1, we developed the basic Pathog-PDx using 42 readily accessible basic demographic features and laboratory test variables extracted from EHRs to differentiate infection sites (Fig. S2 and Table S3). The model demonstrated robust performance across both derivation and validation cohorts, achieving superior discriminative capability compared to traditional ML algorithms (Fig. S4A and Table S4). In internal validation, the model achieved AUCs of 0.87 for upper respiratory infections, 0.84 for bronchitis, and 0.90 for pulmonary infections. External validation confirmed its generalizability, with AUCs ranging from 0.73 to 0.84 (Fig. S4B).

We then developed a pathogen subtyping model based on Subset 2 (Table S5). The Pathog-PDx model achieved AUCs of 0.86 (Sn: 0.84, Sp: 0.70) for bacterial, 0.88 (Sn: 0.79, Sp: 0.77) for viral, and 0.90 (Sn: 0.89, Sp: 0.74) for fungal infections (Table S6, Figs. 2A and S4C). External validation confirmed consistent performance, with AUCs of 0.76 to 0.78 for bacterial and viral infections and 0.72 to 0.73 for fungal infections across both cohorts (Fig. 2A and Table S6). Notably, integrating predicted infection sites (derived from Subset 1 classification) significantly enhanced performance (DeLong test, p < 0.01, Fig. 2B-D). The model’s stability was further affirmed by 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. S4D). Additionally, when the model was tested using only the complete blood count panel or the metabolic panel, both subsets still offered clinically valuable and stable discrimination (Fig. S5A). This indicates that the model can maintain robust diagnostic performance even with a reduced set of routinely available clinical variables. To systematically assess model robustness, we evaluated the tolerance under varying degrees of missing data. The results showed that the performance change with 10 missing features was slight relative to the full model (Fig. S5B). And applying SMOTE not only slightly improved the AUC for classification of pathogen (Fig. S6A), but also alleviated the skewed predicted probability distribution (Fig. S6B). Moreover, subgroup analysis further demonstrated stable performance across gender (Fig. S7A) and age groups (Fig. S7B). These results confirm the robustness and clinical relevance of the optimized Pathog-PDx.

Precise identification of 22 pathogen subtypes

Accurate identification of respiratory pathogens at the species level is important for informing targeted antimicrobial therapy and reducing reliance on empirical treatment. The Pathog-PDx system was developed to support this objective by enabling the classification of 22 commonly encountered respiratory pathogens. Notable demographic differences were observed in Subset 2. Male predominance was found in HBoV (male-to-female ratio [MFR] = 1.84:1; p < 0.01), HPIV (MFR = 1.83:1; p < 0.01), and KLP infections (MFR = 1.91:1; p < 0.001). Age distribution revealed that MP had the highest median age (52 months [IQR: 33–84]) and KLP the lowest (3 months [IQR: 1–12]) (Table 1).

The refined Pathog-PDx demonstrated stable performance in identifying 22 subtypes (Fig. S8, S9 and Table S7). Bacterial pathogens like Ab, E.coli, KLP, and PA achieved high accuracy (AUCs > 0.80; Fig. 2E). Early and precise identification of these bacteria is clinically critical as it can guide prompt initiation of appropriate antibiotic therapy, reducing reliance on broad-spectrum empiric regimens and potentially improving patient outcomes.

For viruses, AUCs ranged from 0.80 to 0.95 and IFV demonstrated the highest classification performance (AUC = 0.95; Sn: 0.88; Sp: 0.86; Fig. 2F, Table S7). The ability to distinguish respiratory viruses at presentation can provide suggestions regarding the specific therapies while awaiting confirmatory testing. Fungal categories were reliably distinguished (AUCs 0.74–0.91; Fig. 2G and Table S7). However, it should be noted that diagnosing fungal respiratory infections remains challenging in clinical practice. Given the low prevalence of primary fungal respiratory infections in the pediatric population, a positive result should be viewed as a screening signal that necessitates confirmation through standard diagnostic procedures. The model also showed reliable performance in classifying prokaryotic pathogens, with AUCs between 0.63 and 0.89 (Fig. 2H). By integrating standard clinical biomarkers with predicted infection site patterns, Pathog-PDx supports timely and accurate pathogen differentiation, offering potential utility as a decision-support tool in the etiological diagnosis of pediatric RTIs.

Model explanation for specific pathogen

To improve clinical interpretability, the SHAP method was used to quantify feature contributions to Pathog-PDx predictions. Both global explanations, identifying key predictors across the population, and local explanations, providing case-level insights, were incorporated.

At the global level, features were ranked by their mean SHAP values to assess their relative importance in pathogen identification (Fig. S10). Variables such as CO2-CP, CI, GLB%, and infection site consistently emerged as strong contributors to pathogen classification (Fig. 3A and Table S8).

For MP, the most prevalent pathogen (53.0% of Subset 2), global SHAP analysis identified GLB, infection site, and TP as the top three discriminative features (Fig. 3B). At the individual level, elevated GLB ( > 22.3 g/L) and TP ( > 62.5 g/L) were associated with higher MP probability (Fig. 3C-E). Comparative analysis further validated these findings: MP patients had significantly higher GLB (26.94 ± 6.21 vs 22.55 ± 6.12 mg/L, p < 0.01) and TP levels (66.14 ± 8.41 vs 61.54 ± 8.96 mg/L, p < 0.01) than patients infected with other pathogens (Fig. 3F, G). This interpretability framework links model output to clinically relevant biomarkers, enhancing transparency and enabling data-driven support for differential diagnosis.

Performance in mixed infection and prediction for critical illness

Studies found that children often carried multiple pathogens (Table S9), highlighting the complex nature of pediatric respiratory infections4,6. Pathog-PDx effectively distinguished single from mixed infection (AUC: 0.80; Fig. 4A, B) and double from multiple mixed infections (AUC: 0.77; Fig. 4C, D). Furthermore, the model possesses unique capability to identify individual pathogens within mixed infections. Pathog-PDx demonstrated robust diagnostic superiority over Ridge classifiers in identifying individual pathogens within the twenty most prevalent mixed-infection subtypes (Fig. 4E and Table S8), achieving significantly higher AUC (p < 0.01), along with Sn (88.24% vs. 82.2%) and Sp (86.96% vs. 70.42%).

Significant rates of mixed infection are frequently associated with severe illnesses. PICU admission rates were substantially higher in mixed bacterial infections (80.13%) and bacterial-fungal mixed infection (81.51%) compared to single infections (18.32%) (p < 0.01, Fig. 4F and Table S10). To further support clinical deployment, we developed a PICU risk prediction model incorporating Pathog-PDx outputs of infection site and mixed-infection status. This model showed high performance with AUCs of 0.903 (internal) and 0.818 (external) (Fig. 4G, H). Feature contribution analysis confirmed that infection site and mixed-infection type were key predictors (Fig. 4I).

For individual-level interpretability, one example showed a patient classified as non-PICU (90.0% probability), with normal values for Na, CO2-CP, LDH, RDW, Cl, GGT, and ALP contributing to this prediction. Although elevated LDH slightly increased PICU risk, it was insufficient to alter the overall classification (Fig. 4J). Conversely, another patient exhibited abnormalities across multiple parameters, leading to a 93.1% predicted probability of PICU admission (Fig. 4K). These case-level insights highlight the value of combining clinical variables with infection site characteristics to support precise risk stratification.

Convenient application for clinical utility

The predictive models were integrated into a web-based application (Fig. S11) to enable rapid (typically within 25 seconds) clinical implementation. Upon entering the required feature values, the application automatically generates: (1) pathogen-specific risk predictions and (2) severe case probability estimates for individual pediatric patients. The interface additionally displays force visualization plots highlighting feature contributions to each prediction. The friendly online service of Pathog-PDx is publicly accessible at: https://pathogpdx.zzu.edu.cn.

The application of the model in real clinical scenarios

From January to December 2024, a prospective blinded validation following STARD-AI guidelines17 was conducted as a non-interventional study in SSPH on 1338 pediatric cases with suspected RTIs lacking definitive confirmation at admission. Clinicians made initial diagnoses independently without model access, using uniform patient data. Analysis of diagnostic timelines revealed that routine tests, such as complete blood count and metabolic panel, were completed in 1.28 and 2.93 h, respectively (Fig. 5A). In contrast, microbiological cultures required an average of 3.5 days, and nucleic acid tests took approximately 20 h, delaying timely diagnosis and treatment.

By providing a prioritized list of likely pathogens for clinical reference, it may enable clinicians to adopt a more targeted testing approach. Compared to initial assessments, use of Pathog-PDx recommendations reduced the number of ultimately non-contributory tests and improved the positive predictive value for all pathogen subtypes (Fig. S12 and Table S11). And in the prospective cohort, Pathog-PDx achieved a mean AUC of 0.78 for infection site classification and performed well in identifying 10 common pathogens (e.g., MP: AUC = 0.81; IFV: AUC = 0.87; Fig. 5B–D and Table S12).

Among the 1,338 pediatric cases, confirmatory laboratory results took over 3 days to finalize for 885 patients (66.1%) and over 1 day for 453 patients (33.9%). In contrast, the Pathog-PDx model provided diagnostic predictions within 3–6 h after admission. The following two representative cases illustrate its clinical utility.

Patient 141, a 5-year-old male admitted on April 3 with persistent fever and bilateral neck masses, received ineffective empirical ceftriaxone. Blood tests revealed abnormalities, including low CO2-CP, L%, elevated NE, GGT and WBC. The Pathog-PDx predicted potential infections with MP, IFV, and SP. Subsequent confirmatory testing verified positivity for IFV-A on April 6th and MP positivity on April 8th. The patient was discharged within four days of starting oseltamivir and azithromycin after confirmation. Patient 610 (Fig. 5E), a 7-year-old female, was admitted on May 15 with a 10-day history of fever, headache, and anorexia and received ineffective empirical ceftriaxone. Her laboratory results revealed elevated ALP, LDH, and platelet count, along with decreased creatinine and CO2-CP. Pathog-PDx predicted an MP infection, which was subsequently confirmed on May 18. Following diagnostic confirmation, initiation of azithromycin therapy led to discharge within three days.

These cases demonstrate that Pathog-PDx provided actionable predictions 3–5 days prior to confirmatory diagnostics. The timely and actionable diagnostic insights aid pathogen identification and inform treatment decisions in real-world pediatric settings.

Discussion

This study developed and validated an explainable ML model, Pathog-PDx, for the early diagnosis of RTIs in children. Using EHR data from multicenter cohorts in China and the United States, the model demonstrated robust predictive performance across internal, external, and prospective validation datasets. Its ability to accurately identify 22 pathogen subtypes, including both single and mixed infections, provides a foundation for clinical deployment as a suggestion-support tool18. Notably, while the model uses a clinically accessible complete blood count panel and metabolic panel, its global applicability in settings with limited laboratory capacity requires consideration. The subgroup analysis using only the complete blood count panel or the metabolic panel indicated that the reduced model retains the clinically useful discriminatory ability. Future work should focus on enhancing model robustness when key features are unavailable.

Evidence shows that antibiotics are frequently prescribed inappropriately for some infections such as acute bronchitis, upper RTIs, and undifferentiated fevers, with rates reaching up to 50%19. Such misuse is a key driver of antimicrobial resistance20. By providing early and actionable pathogen predictions, Pathog-PDx can help clinicians narrow antibiotic therapy more rapidly. Notably, mixed infection, often involving viral and bacterial pathogens, is common in pediatric RTIs and is difficult to diagnose and treat effectively21. In this study, Pathog-PDx successfully identified mixed infection cases at early stages, though further quantitative validation is needed.

Previous studies have shown that multi-omics biomarkers, including clinical parameters and microbiome signatures, can serve as prognostic indicators for patients with RTIs22,23. For example, a regression model incorporating age, BMI, CD4 T lymphocytes, and serum interleukin-6 levels has been used to effectively predict the risk of severe viral pneumonia24. This study pinpointed multiple risk factors as potential predictors of severe RTIs. Markers related to electrolyte and acid-base balance, including potassium, sodium, chlorine, and carbon dioxide combining power, emerged as critical factors, elucidating the association between electrolyte balance and critical illness25. Furthermore, liver and renal biochemical markers, including alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, creatine kinase, and urea, contributed significantly to predicting critical illness, emphasizing their importance in assessing disease severity26.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the analysis primarily focused on hospitalized pediatric patients, which may not fully represent the broader population of children with RTIs. Thus, external validation in diverse clinical environments is essential to assess the generalizability of the model. Second, it is important to emphasize that Pathog-PDx is designed as an auxiliary tool for early pathogen identification and not as a replacement for microbial cultures. Cultures remain essential for determining antimicrobial susceptibility profiles to guide definitive therapy, particularly for bacterial pathogens. For viral infections, selecting the appropriate targeted assay relies on prior clinical suspicion, and the use of broad-spectrum assays is prohibitive. Pathog-PDx serves as a comprehensive early-screening tool designed to widen the diagnostic lens at the point of initial assessment by synthesizing this routine data. When facing diagnostic uncertainty or potential mixed infections, early insight can recommend therapy while awaiting results of confirmatory tests and prompt more targeted testing. And in resource-constrained settings where comprehensive testing is unavailable or significantly delayed, the model could offer valuable triage suggestions. Thus, the tool should be positioned as a comprehensive early support aid, aiming to simultaneously assess the possibility of multiple pathogens or mixed infection when the etiology of infection is uncertain. Furthermore, while models demonstrate strong predictive performance, data incompleteness and confounding factors remain unavoidable challenges in real-world research. The composite reference standard used in this study was a composite of clinical presentation, laboratory tests, imaging findings, and multi-source microbiological evidence, with all final diagnoses reviewed by an independent expert panel. The approach was also supported by the recent Expert Consensus on Etiologic Diagnosis and Clinical Management of Acute Respiratory Tract Infections in Children (2025 edition)27 released by the Pediatrics Branch of the Chinese Medical Association, which referenced the Guidelines for the management of community-acquired pneumonia in children (2024 revision). Although this approach reflected the real-world diagnostic workflow, it inherits the limitations of the underlying clinical tests and sampling accessibility, which was a common challenge in large-scale pediatric studies. For instance, obtaining reliable and uncontaminated sputum specimens is especially difficult in young children28. Additionally, the limitation regarding bronchoalveolar lavage sample should be acknowledged. While bronchoalveolar lavage provided identification of respiratory tract pathogens, performing this invasive procedure on every pediatric patient was clinically infeasible. Consequently, some cases may have been classified as false positives or false negatives due to imperfect sensitivity or specificity of the reference tests. Factors, including pre-admission treatment history, patient compliance, and incomplete records, can further affect result accuracy and model performance. Standardizing data collection, utilizing technology-assisted approaches, and enhancing patient engagement can significantly reduce these issues. Ultimately, seamless integration into clinical workflow will be crucial for widespread adoption29, and additional optimization is required to meet practical needs for routine healthcare use.

It should be noted that the model achieved notably high AUCs for fungal pathogens. However, for conditions with extreme class imbalance, such as fungal respiratory infections, even a high-AUC model can yield a significant number of false positives upon deployment. Therefore, the principal clinical value of Pathog-PDx for fungal detection lies in its role as a sensitive screening tool. A positive prediction should function as a safeguard, prompting clinicians to consider the possibility of fungal involvement and to initiate appropriate confirmatory testing. Regarding data processing, the SMOTE algorithm operates via linear interpolation between minority class samples in feature space. This approach, though effective in broadening the synthetic sample set, carries the risk of generating synthetic data with clinically implausible combinations of laboratory parameters. And this linear interpolation regime suffers from a noise propagation issue, potentially blurring decision boundaries30. Employing random direction vectors, or using hybrid methods that integrate centroid displacement, have been proposed to mitigate these issues31. Future work should develop more sophisticated oversampling techniques that better preserve the physiological plausibility and discriminative structure of the clinical feature space. Furthermore, the observed performance drop in external validation, a common challenge in multi-center machine learning studies, highlights the effect of population heterogeneity and local practice variations on model generalizability. Future work should focus on continuous model calibration using local data to maintain performance across different settings.

In conclusion, this study presents Pathog-PDx, an interpretable ML model developed and validated using multicenter EHR data. The model accurately identifies 22 pathogen types, including both single and mixed infections, and enables early prediction of critical illness. By leveraging routinely collected clinical parameters and explainable ML techniques, Pathog-PDx has the potential to support precise diagnosis and individualized treatment of pediatric RTIs, improving diagnostic efficiency and patient outcomes in real-world settings. For clinical implementation, an accessible online platform for Pathog-PDx has been developed and is planned for integration into hospital Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems to provide real-time diagnostic suggestions upon admission, helping clinicians make faster, evidence-based antimicrobial decisions.

Methods

Study population

A retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted in ill children for the derivation and validation of the model. We used the EHR data obtained from two public critical care databases and three proprietary real-world datasets in China. Children aged <18 years were eligible for inclusion. The derivation cohort consisted of (1) Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III/MIMIC-III (N = 8,142, 2001–2012, US)32, (2) Paediatric Intensive Care Database/PIC (N = 11,979, 2010–2018, Zhejiang, China)33, (3) The First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University (N = 60,123, 2015–2023, Xiamen, China). This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with approvals of the Institutional Review Boards of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University (XMYY-2025KY043-01), Xiamen Children’s Hospital (XEKLS-2024-107), and Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (2024-385-01PJ). Written informed consent was obtained from guardians of all participants. Use of public databases (MIMIC-III and PIC) was exempt from IRB review; all data were de-identified.

In addition, two external validation cohorts were used for testing. The first external cohort consisting of ill children admitted to the Xiamen Children’s Hospital (N = 11,426, 2021–2023, Xiamen, China) from January 2021 to December 2023 was employed for the external validation. The second external cohort consisting of ill children admitted to the Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (N = 12,303, 2018–2023, Shenzhen, China) from January 2018 to December 2023 was employed for the external validation. Furthermore, prospective validation was conducted in an independent cohort from Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (N = 1338, 2024, Shenzhen, China), enrolled between January and December 2024. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were identical to those of the derivation cohort.

Patient enrollment and pathogen detection

The final clinical classification included both the sites of respiratory tract infections and the pathogen type. The ARTIs were defined as (i) at least one of the following conditions: fever, abnormal white blood cell (WBC) differentials; (ii) at least one of the following symptoms/signs: cough (productive, non-productive, or blood-tinged), chills, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, sore throat, chest pain, tachypnea, and abnormal pulmonary breath sounds7. A history of close contact with individuals with respiratory disease was also considered. For pneumonia in children, the classification was guided by the Guidelines for the management of community-acquired pneumonia in children (2024 revision)34. For patients with available imaging data, the pneumonia was established based on clinical symptoms accompanied by new radiographic findings not attributable to other causes. Typical radiological features included lobar or patchy consolidation, silhouette sign involving the diaphragm or cardiac borders, interstitial infiltrates, or bilateral perihilar opacities11,35,36. The classification of pathogens was based on a comprehensive evaluation that strictly adhered to the clinical guideline34. In addition to the above, microbiological confirmation was achieved by a positive result from a standardized pathogen detection assay on a high-quality respiratory or sterile site specimen. Pathogen detection followed a standardized and strictly controlled protocol. Specimen collection was performed within 48 hours of hospital admission by trained pediatric nursing teams. All samples, including blood, sputum, and pharyngeal swabs, were transported under temperature-controlled conditions at 4°C and processed within 4 to 6 hours after rigorous quality assessment. Smear microscopy was performed on each sputum specimen for culture. Sputum specimens that satisfy the following conditions are regarded as qualified specimens: (i) A specimen with > 25 white blood cells (WBC) per average low-power field (LPF); (ii) squamous epithelial cells (SEC) < 10 per LPF and the presence of alveolar macrophages and columnar epithelial cells. In addition to these criteria, when SEC is > 10 per LPF, sputum specimens are considered unqualified. Alternative respiratory specimens, such as nasopharyngeal aspirates, were collected when sampling was difficult37. Blood and sputum specimens were subjected to bacterial culture for the identification of common pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Staphylococcus aureus. Urine samples were analyzed for the detection of Gram-negative bacteria. Serum antibody and antigen testing was used for the detection of Chlamydia and Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Pharyngeal swabs were tested using real-time or conventional PCR for detection of various respiratory pathogens, including influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus, human parainfluenza virus, human metapneumovirus, seasonal human coronavirus, and human rhinovirus. All molecular assays employed kits approved by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Shengxiang Biotechnology, Changsha, China). All laboratory results from cultures, antigen tests, and nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) were reviewed and verified by two senior laboratory medicine specialists (YYY and XC) and a physician (JJY) with over 5 years of experience in respiratory tract infections to ensure technical accuracy and result integrity.

The final reference classification criteria were based on the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness guidelines38 and the Guidelines for the management of community-acquired pneumonia in children (2024 revision)34. The daily medical records of cases were manually independently reviewed and confirmed by a panel of pediatricians specializing in infectious diseases (QHC, DDG, board-certified physicians with over 5 years of experience, and YGY, board-certified senior pediatrician with over 20 years of experience). Specifically, this panel reviewed the complete patient profile, including the clinical symptoms, radiologists’ reports, laboratory findings, and all available microbiological evidence such as blood cultures, qualified sputum cultures, PCR assays, and other related results34,37. Each case was independently assessed by two pediatricians. A consensus diagnosis was required for final inclusion. Any discrepancies between the two initial pediatricians were resolved through discussion with a third senior pediatric consultant (YGY), whose decision was final.

Data collection and processing

A total of 134,500 children were included in this study. The derivation cohort included 81,764 ill children. But there were some deviations, errors, or missing values in our cohorts. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Fig. S1, with a final sample size of 61,620 retained, including MIMIC-III (N = 7,426), PIC (N = 11,979) and XMFH (N = 42,215). We utilized demographic characteristics and laboratory data collected at the first time after hospital admission to identify and construct prediction models.

Since multi-collinearity among features may affect prediction accuracy, one feature that was less correlated with outcome was eliminated from the data set when two features were highly correlated (correlation coefficient > 0.9) in Spearman’s correlation analyses, as shown in Fig. S2. In addition, features and samples with over 65% missing values were excluded to minimize the bias resulting from missing data. Finally, 42 variables remained for further analysis, including age, sex, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), AST/ALT, creatine kinase (CK), direct bilirubin (DBil), total bilirubin (TBil), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), Total protein (TP), albumin, globulin, ALB/GLB, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), potassium (K), sodium (Na), chlorine (Cl), phosphorus (P), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr’), uric acid (UA), carbon dioxide combining power (CO2-CP), triglycerides (TG), neutrophil (NE), monocytes, monocyte ratio (M%), eosinophils, eosinophil ratio (EOS%), basophils, basophil ratio (B%), neutrophil ratio (NE%), lymphocyte ratio (L%), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), white blood cell (WBC), platelet (PLT), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), mean platelet volume (MPV) and platelet distribution width (PDW).

To maximize both performance and validity, we employed a dual-strategy approach to feature scaling to ensure an unbiased comparison between different classes of algorithms. For parametric models (Ridge Regression and LR) and distance-based models (SVM) that are sensitive to feature scales, all continuous features were standardized by Z-score normalization. The standardization parameters were computed exclusively from the training set to prevent leakage. Conversely, for tree-based ensemble models (GBDT, RF, LightGBM, XGBoost), feature scaling is unnecessary, as the underlying decision trees partition the feature space based on value rankings, which are invariant to linear transformations39–41. From the derivation cohort, 70% of the data were used for training and 30% for internal validation to avoid overfitting.

The missing data were processed by K-nearest neighbor (KNN) imputation42. Specifically, a single KNN imputation model was fitted on the training set. Subsequently, this fitted imputer was applied to transform the training set itself, the internal validation set, and the external validation cohorts. A sample augmentation method of Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was used to solve the imbalance problem43. The KNN algorithm was used in SMOTE to oversample the minority sample and generate new cases until each class had an equal number of cases. Specifically, it generated new data from sample data to oversample a small number of sample sets. The synthesis strategy is to find the KNN of the sample xi from the interface residue samples, denoted as xi(near), near∈{1,…,k}. Then, a sample xi(nn) and a random number ζ1 between 0 and 1 were generated from the KNN, and a new sample xi1 was synthesized.

Repeat the above procedure N times until we got N new samples: xi(new), new∈1,…, N.

SMOTE was only applied to our training sets and we did not oversample the testing set, thus maintaining the natural outcome frequency.

Model development and comparison

The 42 features mentioned above were used to develop the prediction models by framing the multi-label classification task as several binary relevance tasks for ML algorithms. Seven ML models, namely, Ridge, Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Random Forest (RF), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) were used for the target classification. To optimize the early diagnosis model, using the methods of random search cross-validation (CV) combined with manual fine-tuning were applied to obtain the final hyperparameters. In addition, we used a two-layer model to integrate the 4 ensemble algorithms. The first layer combined four ensemble algorithms: GBDT, RF, LightGBM, and XGBoost. A second layer was subsequently introduced where four ensemble models were randomly combined for soft-voting integration, and the contributions were adjusted to achieve the best performance, as shown in Fig. S3. To enhance stability and prevent overfitting, multiple sub-models were trained on distinct subsets of the original dataset, reducing variance and improving generalization. Additionally, when constructing a pathogen diagnostic model, we incorporated the model’s diagnostic predicted outcomes from the infection site model as a feature. By integrating the diagnostic results from various pathogen models, we also accomplished the diagnosis of mixed-infections. Finally, by embedding all the diagnostic outcomes, we developed an ICU risk prediction model.

Several commonly performance analysis metrics, such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), sensitivity (Sn), specificity (Sp), accuracy (ACC), Youden’s index, and F1-score, were used to evaluate the reliability of these models.

Model interpretation

The SHAP method is an approach that ranks the importance of input features and explain the results of the prediction model, and it is implemented to mitigate the “black-box” issue commonly associated with ML models44. The SHAP method offered global and local explanations for the model. The global explanation provides consistent and accurate attribution values for each feature within a model to show the associations between input features and sites or pathogens of RTIs. The local explanation could demonstrate a specific prediction for an individual child by inputting the specific data.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using Python version 3.11.7 (https://www.python.org). Continuous variables with skewed distributions were presented as median with interquartile range and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers with percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The AUC was used to evaluate the predictive power, and the optimal cutoff value was established by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
