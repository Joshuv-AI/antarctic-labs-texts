The Echo After the Shockwave: The Residual Risk of Intravascular Coronary Lithotripsy
Authors: Akshay Machanahalli Balakrishna, Saraschandra Vallabhajosyula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorAkshay Machanahalli Balakrishna, MD

Editorial

Coronary artery calcification remains the Achilles heel of contemporary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Severe calcium can impair device delivery, limit lesion expansion, and increase the risk of stent underexpansion, restenosis, thrombosis, and repeat revascularization.1 This may be associated with recurrent stable and unstable coronary syndromes and cardiovascular mortality. Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has transformed the treatment of calcified coronary lesions by providing a simple and relatively safe method of modifying deep and circumferential calcium. Its ease of use and favorable safety profile has led to rapid adoption in contemporary practice. Yet successful delivery of lithotripsy pulses does not always yield an optimal procedural or clinical outcome.2

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Oliveri et al. examine the residual risk after IVL-assisted PCI. Using data from the prospective, multicenter Benelux Intravascular Lithotripsy registry, the investigators evaluated factors associated with technical failure and 12-month target lesion failure (TLF) in 571 patients with 595 calcified coronary lesions. The cohort reflected the complexity of contemporary calcium modification: approximately 70% were long lesions, 28% were in-stent lesions, 24% were ostial lesions, and 22% involved bifurcations. High-pressure dilation was used before and after IVL in more than 90% of cases, whereas rotational atherectomy before IVL was used in 12%. Technical failure and TLF occurred in 10.5% and 7.2% at 12 months, respectively. These event rates are relatively low, but they remain clinically meaningful. The study reminds us that IVL is an effective device, but not a stand-alone procedural strategy (Figure 1).3 We commend the authors for selecting an important topic and for their clinically relevant findings. Multiple aspects of this manuscript merit detailed discussion.

In this study, the absence of intravascular imaging (IVI) was independently associated with technical failure. Specific to calcium modification, angiography alone may underestimate calcium burden, vessel dimensions, residual stent underexpansion, and edge complications. IVI can define calcium depth, arc, and distribution; guide balloon sizing; identify calcium fracture; and confirm adequate stent expansion and apposition.4,5 Multiple prior studies have also demonstrated superior outcomes with IVI compared with angiography alone in complex PCI.6 It is important to note that imaging itself is a diagnostic tool; improved outcomes depend on how operators act on the information obtained from imaging. Nevertheless, the finding is biologically plausible and consistent with randomized evidence supporting imaging-guided PCI in complex coronary disease.6 In-stent lesions were associated with both technical failure and TLF. Lesion failure in these patients is often an amalgamation of patient factors, medical therapy, and technical factors. From a technical standpoint, in addition to calcium, fibrotic tissue, neointimal hyperplasia, calcific neoatherosclerosis, multiple stent layers, and fixed mechanical constraint may all contribute to treatment resistance. Although IVL can fracture calcium behind a previously implanted stent, it has limited efficacy on noncalcific fibrous tissue.7 Therefore, in such cases, the value of IVI is further amplified to identify the dominant mechanism of failure and select the appropriate plaque-modification strategy. Ancillary techniques may include high-pressure noncompliant balloons, super high-pressure OPN-type balloons, scoring or cutting balloons, rotational or orbital atherectomy, excimer laser atherectomy, or combined approaches, depending on whether the dominant problem is calcium, fibrotic tissue, stent underexpansion, or multilayer mechanical constraint.7 The authors noted that left circumflex lesions were independently associated with higher TLF. The circumflex artery often presents unique technical challenges, including an angulated origin, vessel tortuosity, limited guide support, bifurcation involvement, and difficult device delivery. Calcified disease involving the ostial circumflex or distal left main bifurcation may be especially difficult to prepare and optimize.8 This finding should remain hypothesis-generating. Vessel location may be a marker of lesion complexity rather than a direct cause of failure. Future studies should determine whether the observed association is explained by ostial involvement, calcified nodules, guide support, incomplete calcium modification, or residual stent underexpansion.8

Perhaps the most important finding is the association between technical failure and subsequent TLF. Residual stenosis, poor expansion, flow-limiting dissection, perforation, and impaired final flow are not isolated procedural events. They may influence long-term outcomes through restenosis, thrombosis, and repeat intervention.3,9

Lastly, the authors noted that diabetes and reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate were associated with TLF. These findings reinforce that lesion modification does not eliminate systemic risk. Diabetes promotes endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, thrombosis, neointimal proliferation, and diffuse atherosclerosis, whereas chronic kidney disease and reduced renal function are closely linked to vascular calcification and adverse PCI outcomes.3,10 IVL may improve vessel compliance, but it does not reverse the underlying biology of disease. Device-based treatment addresses the lesion, whereas long-term outcomes remain influenced by the patient.

This study should not be interpreted as a formal prediction model. Instead, it identifies clinical, anatomical, and procedural factors associated with failure after IVL-assisted PCI. Its message is practical: IVL works well, but it does not guarantee success. In-stent lesions remain difficult and often require a multimodality approach. IVI may be particularly valuable in severely calcified disease. Technical failure may carry long-term consequences, and systemic risk factors continue to influence outcomes despite effective calcium modification. The future of calcified coronary intervention will not be defined by a single device. It will depend on selecting the right tool for the right lesion, combining technologies when necessary, and confirming that the intended mechanical result has been achieved. The echo after the shockwave is the reminder that calcium fracture is not the endpoint.
