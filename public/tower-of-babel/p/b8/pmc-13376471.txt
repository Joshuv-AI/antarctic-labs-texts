Protocol: Community-Based Interventions to Improve the Health of People Experiencing Homelessness in High-Income Countries: An Evidence and Gap Map
Authors: April M Ballard, Bethany Havas, Kylie R DeBoer, Larry Mason, Shannon Self-Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis is the protocol for a Campbell evidence and gap map. The objectives are as follows: to systematically retrieve, categorize, and map evidence on community-based health interventions for PEH in high-income countries, and identify gaps in the available evidence to inform a prioritized research agenda for health and homelessness.Keywords: systematic mapping, housing instability, health equity, causal inference, intervention effectiveness, public health interventions

Background

Introduction

The Problem, Condition or Issue

Homelessness is a significant determinant of health that contributes to disparities in physical, mental, and social well-being (Walton et al., 2024). People experiencing homelessness (PEH) face disproportionately high rates of chronic and infectious diseases, injury, mental health challenges, trauma exposure, and substance use disorders, resulting in substantially elevated morbidity and mortality compared to the general population (Aldrige et al., 2018; McNeill et al., 2022; Richards & Kuhn, 2022). These disparities stem not only from underlying health conditions but also from the environmental and structural conditions associated with homelessness. PEH often face limited access to healthcare and supportive services (McNeill et al., 2022; Siersbaek et al., 2023) and exposure to violence, exploitation, extreme weather, environmental pollution, and inadequate sanitation (Anthonj et al., 2024; Richards & Kuhn, 2022; Romaszko et al., 2017). Together, these factors contribute to higher rates of illness and premature mortality, with PEH dying a decade or more earlier than the general population (Anthonj et al., 2024; Romaszko et al., 2017). These patterns underscore the need to address homelessness as both a public and population health priority.

Viewing homelessness through a public health lens highlights the importance of both preventive strategies and interventions that address complex, intersecting needs. The relationship between health and homelessness is bidirectional: poor health can increase the risk of unemployment, poverty, and homelessness, while homelessness can exacerbate existing health conditions and create new barriers to stability and care (Flatau et al., 2020; Fowler et al., 2019; Richards & Kuhn, 2022). This dynamic points to the need for interventions that both prevent adverse health outcomes associated with homelessness and improve health outcomes among PEH. Community-based approaches are particularly important because they can reduce logistical, financial, and social barriers to care and can be delivered in settings that are more accessible and less stigmatizing than traditional healthcare environments. In such settings, preventive strategies and interventions may include efforts to improve access to primary care, mental health and substance use treatment, harm reduction services, chronic disease management, and public health measures including vaccination and infection prevention (Fitzpatrick-Lewis et al., 2011; Hwang & Burns, 2014).

Despite the growing recognition of homelessness as a public health priority, the evidence base for health-focused interventions remains fragmented and uneven, with substantial variation in intervention types, populations, and outcomes studied (Fowler et al., 2019; Omerov et al., 2020; Sleet & Francescutti, 2021). As a result, the distribution and strength of evidence across intervention approaches remain unclear, particularly for community-based interventions targeting health outcomes among PEH in high-income countries. A systematic mapping of the evidence is therefore needed to identify where research is concentrated, where important gaps persist, and where future research and investment may be most beneficial.

The Intervention

The study will develop an evidence gap map (EGM) to systematically identify the distribution, characteristics, and gaps in effectiveness evidence on community-based interventions designed to improve the health of PEH in high-income countries. For the purposes of this review, community-based interventions will be broadly defined as programs, services, and policies delivered outside of institutional or hospital-based care that aim to influence health outcomes among PEH. These may include interventions designed to improve access to healthcare, deliver preventive services, support chronic disease management, provide mental health or substance use treatment, implement harm reduction approaches, or address environmental and structural conditions that affect health. Our focus on community settings reflects the ways in which health services for PEH are frequently delivered through outreach, community organizations, and non-traditional care settings rather than formal clinical institutions (Hooshyar et al., 2025; Moledina et al., 2021; White et al., 2019).

Throughout this protocol, we use the term homelessness or people experiencing homelessness (PEH) for clarity and alignment with existing research and policy documents. We acknowledge that some communities prefer alternative terms such as houselessness to emphasize dignity and social connection. We adopt homelessness here to maintain consistency with the terminology used in most empirical studies and policy frameworks.

Why it is Important to Develop the EGM

Despite a substantial and growing body of research on health and homelessness, the evidence base remains highly fragmented. Existing syntheses typically focus on single health outcomes, specific risk factors, or narrow intervention types, making it difficult to understand how health interventions for PEH are distributed across the broader public health landscape. Consequently, policymakers, practitioners, and researchers lack a comprehensive view of where evidence is strong, where it is limited, and where major strategic investment is needed.

Several important initiatives have advanced the evidence base in specific areas. The Canadian evidence-based guidelines and systematic review series (Pottie et al., 2020) address clinical care, substance use and healthcare utilization among PEH. The Campbell review on precarious housing examines the benefits and cost-effectiveness of interventions aimed at improving the health and social outcomes (Moledina et al., 2021). In the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (2022) provides guidance on integrating health and social services for PEH. In addition, the Centre for Homelessness Impact (CHI) has produced two EGMs – one focused on intervention effectiveness studies related to the well-being of PEH (White et al., 2019) and another focused on implementation factors related to intervention delivery (Singh et al., 2023) – each offering essential but broad insights.

Beyond these initiatives, numerous systematic reviews examine discrete health issues relevant to PEH, including tobacco use (Vijayaraghavan et al., 2020), hepatitis C treatment (Hashim et al., 2021) substance use disorders (Miler et al., 2021), diabetes prevention (Constance & Lusher, 2020), community integration (Marshall et al., 2020), and associations between homelessness and health outcomes (Fornaro et al., 2022). While collectively representing a mature evidence base, these reviews are constrained to specific outcomes or sub-populations and do not provide a cross-cutting view of health interventions using a consistent framework.

The proposed EGM addresses this gap by mapping a broad range of health-focused interventions for PEH using a public health framework. Unlike existing EGMs, which are either broader in topic (White et al., 2019) or focused on implementation rather than health (Singh et al., 2023), this map organizes evidence across structural, environmental, behavioral, biomedical, and integrated intervention categories and aligns them with defined health outcomes. This is particularly important as the CHI EGM did not use a health-specific framework but found that most studies leveraged interventions focused on health and social services and outreach, and measured health-related outcomes (Singh et al., 2024). To our knowledge, no current synthesis systematically visualizes the distribution, breadth, and concentration of health interventions across multiple health domains (e.g., chronic disease, infectious disease, mental health, and substance use) for PEH in high-income countries. By integrating the evidence within a coherent public health framework, this EGM can support decision-makers in identifying opportunities, prioritizing research, and improving the coordination of health services for PEH.

Objectives

This research aims to identify and map available effectiveness studies on community-based interventions designed to improve health outcomes for PEH. The primary objectives are to:

Methods

Evidence and Gap Map: Definition and Purpose

EGMs are a synthesis and visual display of evidence from impact evaluations and systematic reviews around a select sector or area. EGM findings are organized and displayed using a framework, or matrix, with interventions as the rows and relevant outcomes as the columns. Bubbles or dots are then placed in matrix cells to represent studies, where the size of the bubble or dot indicates the relative number of studies, and the color indicates the confidence in or quality of existing studies. The combination of the matrix with dots of differing size and color provides a visualization platform to present complex information that is digestible and user-friendly (Campbell et al., 2023; Snilstveit et al., 2016; White et al., 2020).

This EGM will use the intervention-outcome framework, a two-dimensional matrix with interventions as row headings and outcomes as column headings. The effectiveness of community-based health interventions for PEH will be the focus, with evidence on the corresponding intervention and outcomes displayed within each cell of the matrix to identify areas of available research and existing gaps. As an EGM, this project aims to describe and map the distribution and characteristics of evidence rather than to synthesize effect sizes or draw definitive conclusions about intervention effectiveness.

Framework Development and Scope

We developed a draft framework that covers the scope of the EGM with mutually exclusive categories by reviewing key literature and policy documents. Prior to extracting data from studies, we will consult with key stakeholders – including researchers, professionals, and practitioners with expertise in health and homelessness – to review and refine the framework. We will invite 6 to 10 stakeholders outside of the core research team (with at least two from each stakeholder group) to review the draft framework and suggest revisions. Their feedback will inform revisions to the framework, which will be finalized before data extraction. Final adjustments to the framework will be made by the core research team based on piloting with select papers.

Stakeholder Engagement

Stakeholders will be engaged at two stages: (1) framework refinement before data extraction and (2) map interpretation after data extraction. Prior to data extraction, stakeholders will be recruited through professional networks of practitioners and researchers working in the fields of health and homelessness. They will be contacted via email and asked to provide feedback on the proposed framework and recommend sentinel articles (see Search methods and sources section). Following data extraction and preliminary coding, a draft version of the EGM will be created and shared with stakeholders to gather feedback on clarity, interpretability, and usefulness of the map. Stakeholder feedback will inform final adjustments to the EGM prior to publication.

Conceptual Framework for Health Interventions

Grounded in public health theory (Brown et al., 2019; Sommer & Parker, 2013), the framework is intentionally broad to capture the diverse and intersecting determinants of health among PEH. Organizing interventions and outcomes using this structure enables the EGM to reflect the complexity of health needs and the wide range of community-based strategies aimed at improving health within this population.

Dimensions

The primary dimensions of the map will be intervention categories and sub-categories (rows) and health outcome domains and sub-domains (columns).

Types of Intervention/Problem

We will include all types of community-based interventions that aim to improve the health of PEH and classify them into five broad categories (structural, environmental, behavioral, biomedical, and integrated), each containing several sub-categories. Studies in which only a subset of intervention types meet eligibility criteria will be included if data are disaggregated by type or study arm. Some interventions may incorporate components that span multiple domains. When integration of these components is a central design feature of the intervention, the study will be coded as an integrated intervention. When multiple interventions are included within a study but are not designed as an integrated approach, the study will be coded under each relevant intervention category. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We will use the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) framework (Moher et al., 2015) to determine study and review eligibility. The intervention and outcome categories defined in the EGM framework will inform the inclusion and exclusion of studies. To ensure feasibility and relevance, the EGM will focus on high-income countries, community-based interventions, studies reporting health outcomes, and effectiveness designs that address selection bias. As described in the Search methods and sources section, a phased approach beginning with the CHI EGM and followed by targeted searches will allow iterative refinement of the framework. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for population, comparison (study design), and setting are outlined below.

Types of Population

We will include studies focused on PEH in high-income countries, defined using the World Bank 2024 income classifications. Homelessness will be operationalized based on reported housing characteristics, not authors’ terminology. Eligible participants include individuals who lack stable housing, such as those residing in emergency shelters, temporary accommodation, or transitional housing; living in unsheltered environments (e.g., streets, encampments, or places not meant for habitation); or experiencing other documented forms of housing instability or inadequate living conditions (e.g., motels or vehicles).

Individuals of all ages and demographic subgroups will be eligible. Studies that include PEH as a sub-population will also be included if outcome data for PEH are disaggregated or can be extracted separately. The following will be excluded: (1) studies focusing solely on people at risk of homelessness (e.g., rent-burdened households); (2) studies on low-income or “vulnerable” populations without explicit indicators of homelessness or housing instability; studies of individuals residing in public housing.

Types of Study Design

Because this EGM focuses on the effectiveness of community-based interventions, we will include all study designs that control for selection bias and permit causal inference. Eligible designs include: randomized controlled trials, natural experiments, regression discontinuity, propensity score matching, difference in difference, instrumental variables, and other matching designs. Before versus after designs with no control group will be excluded. Systematic reviews of effectiveness studies from high-income countries will be included. For reviews including high-, middle-, and low-income countries, only data from high-income countries will be extracted and mapped. On-going studies will be included, with the status of studies being a filter. We will not include qualitative research but will include mixed method studies that include quantitative data on intervention effectiveness.

Types of Settings

We will only include interventions delivered in community settings within high-income countries. Focusing on high-income contexts increases comparability across health systems, service environments, and policy structures. We will exclude studies that do not report the country in which the intervention was conducted. Community settings will be defined as environments outside of institutional or hospital-based clinical care, including but not limited to: community-based health clinics, mobile outreach or mobile health units, shelters or drop-in centers, syringe service programs, community-based harm reduction sites, and street-based or encampment-based service delivery. Excluded settings include inpatient hospital settings, emergency departments, and residential medical or specialized institutional care. Interventions delivered in mixed settings will be included only if the community-based component can be isolated or predominates.

Search Methods and Sources

We will develop the EGM using a three-staged search strategy designed to efficiently build on existing evidence syntheses while ensuring comprehensive identification of relevant health-focused studies. This approach combines the inclusion assessment of studies from the existing CHI EGM, sentinel article validation to assess coverage, and machine-learning-assisted retrieval to identify additional studies. This staged process maximizes coverage while reducing duplication of prior search efforts.

Reviewing CHI Studies for Inclusion

We will first review the 800 quantitative impact evaluations and effectiveness reviews included in the CHI effectiveness EGM to assess their relevance for inclusion. This follows Campbell’s recommended “low hanging fruit” approach (Singh et al., 2024; White et al., 2020) by leveraging existing, high-quality search work. The CHI EGM identified studies through systematic searches of academic, EGM, and systematic review databases and trial registries. Grey literature was identified through searches of organization and foundation websites (e.g., Homeless Hub, National Coalition Homeless). CHI updates the EGM every two years using machine-learning-assisted screening within EPPI Reviewer (Thomas et al., 2020). The most recent version (2023) includes studies from 1982–2023 conducted in high-income countries across health, social services, housing, and economic intervention domains (Singh et al., 2024; White et al., 2019). We will produce a preliminary EGM based on CHI-included studies that meet our criteria and engage stakeholders to refine the framework and map before proceeding to the next stage.

Assessing the Comprehensiveness of CHI’s Approach

To evaluate whether CHI’s search strategy and machine-learning strategy sufficiently captures the studies relevant to this health-focused EGM, we will identify sentinel articles for each intervention category. Specifically, we will aim to identify approximately 10 primary studies and systematic reviews per intervention category through targeted searches and expert consultation. All searches and consultation methods will be documented in the final report.

For each intervention category: (a) if all sentinel articles appear in CHI’s dataset, the category will be considered adequately represented; (b) if ≥ 20% of sentinel articles are missing, we will conduct a full systematic search for that category; (c) if fewer than five sentinel articles can be identified despite targeted searches and expert consultation, we will conduct a full systematic search for to determine whether the limited evidence reflects a true gap or insufficient capture by the search strategy; (d) if no CHI articles fall within an intervention category and no sentinel articles can be identified, the absence will be documented as an evidence gap. This threshold approach provides a pragmatic mechanism for determining when additional searching is warranted.

Machine Learning Finalization

Once all intervention categories are sufficiently represented, we will use EPPI Reviewer’s Microsoft Academic Graph machine-learning classifier (Thomas et al., 2020) to identify any additional studies that may not have been captured. Sentinel articles published after the most recent CHI update (post-October 2023) will be used to evaluate model performance. If new sentinel articles are not detected, we will refine and retrain the model using updated inclusion and exclusion decisions.

Once stages 1 through 3 have been completed, we will integrate the findings to produce the finalized EGM.

Analysis and Presentation

Report Structure

We will follow the Campbell EGM recommended reporting structure (White et al., 2020) with the following standard headings: abstract, plain language summary, background, methods, results, discussion, and conclusion. The report will be published alongside an online interactive EGM. It will summarize the distribution of evidence, key gaps, and implications for research and policy.

The results section will present tabulations and cross tabulations of the primary and secondary dimensions for the intervention-outcome framework. It will also include information about the study flow, the time trend of publications, as well as the distribution of included studies across select filter categories. Figures in the report will include the conceptual framework for this study, a PRISMA diagram (Moher et al., 2015), a time trend of publications, the distribution of included studies and reviews by select filters (e.g., focal population, community setting where intervention takes place), and summary statistics for the risk of bias assessments for systematic reviews. Tables in the report will include the number of studies by intervention and outcomes categories, the number of included studies by study design, the number of studies by outcome categories and sub-categories, and the quality of studies by intervention category.

The online interactive EGM will display interventions as the row dimension and outcomes as the column dimension. Different size bubbles will represent the number of included studies and different colors will represent the quality of systematic reviews. Filters will be incorporated so that users can stratify results by various characteristics.

Dependency

The unit of analysis for the map will be each included paper. If multiple papers present findings from a single impact evaluation, but the focal outcome or population differs, each paper will be included in the map. However, if multiple papers report on the same impact evaluation and present overlapping outcomes and populations, only the most complete or recent version will be included. It is likely that systematic reviews will include randomized controlled trials in the map. However, all relevant trials will be included, whether they are included in a systematic review or not.

Data Collection and Analysis

Screening and Study Selection

All coders will undergo training and pilot coding to ensure consistency. Titles and abstracts and full texts will be double screened. A third reviewer will resolve disagreements. At each stage, we will document reasons for the exclusion of ineligible studies. We will present a PRISMA flow diagram to show the process of study selection (Moher et al., 2015). The screening tool is presented in Appendix 4.

Data Extraction and Management

We will use a structured, pre-piloted extraction form in EPPI Reviewer to capture basic study characteristics, intervention details, outcomes, and data for critical appraisal of systematic reviews (Thomas et al., 2020). Data will be extracted independently by two coders to improve reliability and validity. Coders will first independently extract data for a subsample of studies (20–30), compare data, and discuss differences with a third party to revise and clarify the coding form and standardize coding and extraction processes. Then, the two coders will independently extract data from the remaining studies, with a third-party deciding disagreement. Coding categories will be based on the intervention and outcome framework, as well as additional filters (e.g., population information, study setting). Coding guidance will be developed to ensure consistent categorization of interventions, outcomes, and filters. When classification is ambiguous, predefined rules will be applied and documented. Unresolved cases will be adjudicated by a third reviewer. A draft coding tool is provided in Appendix 5.

Tools for Assessing Risk of Bias/Study Quality of Included Reviews

We will critically appraise systematic reviews using AMSTAR 2 (Shea et al., 2017), as quality appraisal will provide useful contextual information for interpreting the evidence base. The AMSTAR 2 tool is presented in Appendix 6. Consistent with EGM methodology (White et al., 2020), we will not assess risk of bias for primary studies.

Methods for Mapping

We will use EPPI Reviewer to screen papers and extract data. We will use EPPI Mapper to generate the EGM (Thomas et al., 2020).

Plans for Updating the EGM: The EGM will be updated every two years using EPPI Reviewer’s automated machine-learning and bibliographic update functions. Specifically, the initial set of included and excluded studies will be used to train EPPI Reviewer’s classifier, which draws on OpenAlex and other linked bibliographic sources to identify newly published or indexed records that are likely to meet the original inclusion criteria.

For each update cycle, newly identified records will be screened using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria specified in this protocol. Title and abstract screening, as well as full-text screening, will be conducted in duplicate by two independent reviewers, with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer, consistent with the procedures used in the initial EGM.

Data extraction and coding for newly included studies, including intervention and outcome classification and filter variables, will also be conducted in duplicate using the same pre-specified extraction forms. Any refinements to the intervention–outcome framework or coding guidance identified during the update process will be documented transparently and applied consistently across all newly added studies.

Updates will be clearly versioned, with the date of each update and the number of newly added studies reported in the online EGM and accompanying documentation.
