Abnormal glycosylation of MUC20 mediates TAM polarization and promotes immune escape in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yingru Zhi, Yifeng Zhang, Huanhuan Fei, Jie Yuan, Xiaobei Liu, Wanli Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immune evasion is one of the critical factors contributing to the advanced progression of colorectal cancer (CRC). Our research identified that CRC cells exhibit high expression of the zinc finger protein ZNF30, which transcriptionally activates the expression of the glycosyltransferase B4GALT2. B4GALT2 mediates the N-glycosylation of MUC20, which subsequently interacts with sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin 7 (Siglec-7) on the surface of macrophages. This interaction induces M2 polarization of the macrophages, thereby promoting immune evasion in CRC and ultimately accelerating malignant progression. In vivo experiments also verified that the ZNF30/B4GALT2/MUC20 axis promotes the growth and metastasis of CRC. The discovery of this regulatory mechanism highlights the critical role of aberrant glycosylation of MUC20 in CRC immune evasion and offers new predictive markers and therapeutic targets for patients with advanced CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignant tumors in the digestive system, with approximately 1.8 million new cases diagnosed annually, and both the incidence and mortality rates ranking third globally1,2. Statistics indicate that the five-year survival rate for patients with stage IV CRC, who present with distant metastases, is less than 10%, and about 15% to 25% of patients have distant metastases at the time of diagnosis, leading to a generally poor prognosis3–5. Studies have shown a close relationship between the development and progression of CRC and tumor immune evasion. Therefore, an in-depth exploration of the specific mechanisms underlying immune evasion in advanced CRC and the identification of effective targets to remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment in CRC are critical clinical challenges that urgently need to be addressed.

Tumor cells often evade the immune system by creating a unique tumor microenvironment (TME), which is a complex system composed of tumor cells, stromal cells (including fibroblasts, immune cells, and vascular cells), and various cytokines. Among these, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are the most abundant immune cells regulating tumor malignancy6,7. Under the prolonged influence of the TME, TAMs predominantly differentiate into the M2 phenotype. M2 TAMs not only secrete a variety of cytokines that promote tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis but also suppress the functions of CD8+ T cells and NK cells, thereby mediating immune evasion and tumor progression7,8. Studies have shown that the infiltration of M2 TAMs is significantly higher at the invasive front of CRC tissues compared to the central tumor region9. Furthermore, increasing evidence suggests that in CRC, tumor cells and macrophages engage in unique intercellular signaling, mutually influencing each other to reshape the immunosuppressive microenvironment and collaboratively promote CRC malignancy. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this interaction remain to be fully elucidated.

Zinc finger proteins constitute the largest family of transcription factors in the human genome and are closely associated with various stages of cancer development10,11. Studies have shown that CRC cells with low expression of ZNF143 promote the M2 polarization of TAMs by secreting cytokines such as IL-8, indicating a role in communication between cancer cells and immune cells, which helps establish an environment conducive to tumor immune evasion12. Our research is the first to identify that CRC cells highly express ZNF30, which promotes CRC malignancy. However, whether ZNF30 is involved in regulating macrophage polarization and the specific mechanisms through which it operates remain unclear.

Glycosylation is one of the most important forms of post-translational modification of proteins. Previous studies have demonstrated that aberrant glycosylation patterns are closely associated with the malignant progression of CRC. For example, O-GlcNAc glycosyltransferase (OGT) catalyzes the O-GlcNAc modification of serine 415 (S415) on c-MYC, increasing its stability and thereby upregulating the expression of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 2 (PDK2), which regulates the TCA cycle and promotes CRC tumor growth13. Additionally, an article published in Gastroenterology revealed a novel mechanism of glycosylation in CRC immune evasion: aberrant N-glycosylation of IFN-γRα reduces the stability of IFN-γ protein, leading to IFN-γ resistance, while IFN-γ sensitivity can be restored by increasing the expression of N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase III (MGAT3)14. These findings collectively suggest that glycosylation plays a critical role in CRC.

Glycoproteins, which are proteins modified by glycosylation, play crucial roles in cell recognition, intercellular signaling, and cell migration. They also serve as key mediators of communication between tumor cells and stromal cells (including TAMs) within the TME15. Mucins (MUC) are a typical family of glycoproteins. Studies have demonstrated that mucins can drive the interaction between cancer cells and stromal cells in the TME, thereby accelerating malignant tumor progression16,17. Previous research has shown that several mucins are highly expressed in tumor cells and can promote tumor progression and enhance drug resistance by regulating processes such as tumor cell metabolism and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), including MUC1 and MUC1718. MUC20, another membrane-binding mucin, is highly expressed in various cancers such as pancreatic cancer, gastric cancer, and endometrial cancer, where it acts as an oncogenic factor19–21. Additionally, MUC20 has also been identified as an important predictor of tumor recurrence and poor prognosis in CRC patients22. Consistent with these findings, our research also confirmed that MUC20 is highly expressed in CRC and regulates its malignant progression. However, whether MUC20 influences macrophage M2 polarization and mediates tumor immune evasion in CRC remains unknown.

In this study, we investigated the oncogenic roles of ZNF30 and MUC20 in CRC, as well as their regulatory effects on TAMs polarization. Additionally, we explored the specific molecular mechanisms through which ZNF30 mediates the glycosylation of MUC20 and subsequently induces M2 polarization of TAMs. This research unveils a novel mechanism of immune evasion in CRC and contributes to a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms and patterns through which tumor cells regulate macrophage polarization in CRC. It provides scientific evidence for the development of key targets aimed at reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment in CRC.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture and treatment

NCM460, SW480, HT29, HCT116, and THP-1 cells were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank (BFB) and cultured in DMEM (H) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotics. HCT8 cells were purchased from Wuhan Puno Bio-Tech Co., Ltd., and cultured in 1640 medium with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Cultivation conditions were 37 °C in a 5% CO2 and 95% air environment.

shRNA and plasmids

shRNAs: Loaded into the pLVX-shRNA expression vector. Overexpression Plasmids: Loaded into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. Reporter Gene Plasmids: Loaded into the pGL3 basic vector. Flag-B4GALT2: Purchased from MiaoLing Bio, and loaded into the pEMV-3xFLAG expression vector. HA-MUC20 and MUC20-Flag: HA and Flag tags were added, respectively, and loaded into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. For details of the target sequence used, please see Supplementary Table 1.

Cell transfection and stable cell line screening

Transfection: Cells in logarithmic growth phase were plated, and transfection was performed when cells reached 70%-90% confluence. For a 6-well plate, 4 μg of plasmid DNA was used per well to prepare the transfection working solution. 125 μL of Opti-MEM was used to dilute 9 μL of Lipofectamine™ 3000 Reagent (L3000008, Invitrogen, USA). Another 125 μL of Opti-MEM was used to dilute 2.5 μg of plasmid DNA to prepare a DNA premix, followed by adding 5 μL of P3000™ Reagent. The two solutions were mixed to make 250 μL of transfection working solution. After a 15-minute incubation at room temperature, 250 μL of the working solution was added dropwise to cells in 1.75 mL of complete medium (without P/S). Cells were collected for downstream experiments 24–96 hours post-transfection.

Stable Cell Line Screening: 48 hours after transfection, cells were selected using puromycin at a concentration of 3 μg/mL for 10–15 days, with a maintenance concentration of 1.5 μg/mL. Untransfected cells served as controls. Positive cells were identified when all untransfected cells had died.

Cell co-culture

THP-1 cells were plated into a co-culture chamber (3450, Corning) and treated with PMA (HY-18739, MCE) at a concentration of 100 ng/mL for 48 hours to induce M0 macrophage differentiation. During the 24-hour induction of THP-1 cells, HCT116 (or HT29) shCtrl/shZNF30 (or shMUC20 or shB4GALT2) cells in logarithmic growth phase were plated in the lower chamber. After 48 hours of co-culture, samples were collected for subsequent analysis.

Flow cytometry for CD206

Cells were collected and washed with PBS, then resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 10^6 cells/mL. 5 μL of CD206-FITC was added to every 100 μL of cell suspension. Cells were incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes, washed with PBS, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (CytoFlex S, Beckman Coulter).

CCK8 assay

Cells in logarithmic growth phase were plated in a 96-well plate according to experimental groups. Each well was prepared with 100 μL of cell suspension containing 10^5 cells, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 hours to allow attachment. The first data collection was on Day 1. On Days 2, 3, and 4, 10 μL of CCK8 solution (CK04, Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well. After 2 hours of incubation in the incubator, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (800TS, Bio-Tek).

Colony formation assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin and dispersed into single cells. 500 cells per well were seeded into a 6-well plate. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 14–21 days until visible colonies formed. Cultures were then terminated, fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet solution (C0121, Beyotime), washed with water, and dried. Colonies were photographed and counted.

Transwell assay

Invasion Assay: Matrigel was diluted 1:8 with serum-free medium, and 50 μL of Matrigel working solution was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 4 hours. Cells washed with PBS were resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 5 × 10^6 cells/mL. 100–150 μL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, and 250 μL of medium was added to the lower chamber. The chambers were incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours. After incubation, the chambers were removed, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were fixed with 1 mL of methanol at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by staining with crystal violet solution for 5 minutes. The chambers were rinsed with water to remove unbound dye, and the cells in the upper chamber were removed with a cotton swab. The chambers were dried at 37 °C and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope. The number of invaded cells was calculated using ImageJ software.

Migration Assay: No Matrigel was used. Cells were directly seeded into the upper chamber of the Transwell with serum-free medium. After 24 hours, the procedure was similar to the invasion assay. Cells were stained with crystal violet and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Cell migration was quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells in logarithmic growth phase were plated on 14 mm coverslips (WHB-24-CS) with 1 × 10^6 cells per well. Once cells reached confluence, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Coverslips were fixed with cold methanol, washed with PBS, and incubated with primary antibodies E-cadherin (3195, CST, 1:1600, rabbit) and N-cadherin (14215, CST, 1:800, mouse) at 37 °C for 1 hour. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies FITC-goat anti-mouse (F-2761, Invitrogen, 1:2000) and TRITC-goat anti-rabbit (A16101, Invitrogen, 1:250) at room temperature for 1 hour. Cells were then mounted with mounting medium (P36966, Invitrogen) and observed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, DMI8).

qRT-PCR and ChIP-qPCR

RNA extraction was performed using Trizol (ET101, Full Gold). Cells were lysed with Trizol, followed by RNA extraction with RNA Extraction Agent. The aqueous phase was mixed with isopropanol to precipitate RNA, which was then centrifuged and purified. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit. qPCR was performed to measure the expression of target genes relative to the housekeeping gene GAPDH. For details of primers used, please see Supplementary Table 2.

For ChIP-qPCR, ChIP products were analyzed using qPCR to measure enrichment of the B4GALT2 promoter relative to “Input”.

Western Blotting (WB)

Cell lysates were sonicated briefly and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. 30 μg of protein per lane was mixed with SDS-PAGE sample buffer and heated at 95°C for 10 minutes. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE (80 V for 30 minutes in the stacking gel and 120 V for 60 minutes in the separating gel), transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 3% BSA in TBST. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, washed with TBST, and then incubated with secondary antibodies. The primary antibodies were ZNF30 (NBP1-84108, NBP1-80199; Novus Biologicals), E-cadherin (No.3195; Cell Signaling Technology), N-cadherin (14215; Cell Signaling Technology), Ki67 (ab16667; Abcam), PCNA (ab92552; Abcam), ZO-1 (ab190085; Abcam), Vimentin (ab92547; Abcam), IL-12 (ab9992; Abcam), TNF-α (ab1793; Abcam), IL-10 (ab9969; Abcam), TGF-β (ab215715; Abcam), MUC20 (PA5-14973; Thermo Fisher Scientific), B4GALT2 (PA5-103654; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The concentrations of antibodies were applied according to the instructions of the reagents. Detection was performed using ECL reagent, and images were captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system (4600, Tianen).

Co-immunoprecipitation (CoIP)

Cells in logarithmic phase were collected, and cell lysis buffer with PMSF (P6140, Macklin) and protease inhibitors (B14001, biomake) was used for lysis. The lysate was used for WB and IP experiments. For tissue samples, tissues were minced and homogenized in a Dounce homogenizer, then lysed with the same buffer. 20 μL of magnetic beads were added to each IP with 5 μg of antibody and incubated for 4 hours. After washing, equal amounts of protein were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following washing, 100 μL of SDS-PAGE sample buffer was added, heated at 95 °C for 5 minutes, and analyzed by WB or silver staining. Silver staining was performed using a rapid silver staining kit (P0017S, Beyotime), and gel slices were subjected to mass spectrometry to analyze protein species and abundance.

GST pull-down

The CDS sequence of ZNF30 was cloned into the pTac-GST-WELQ vector (D5005, Beyotime). The plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells (B917825, Macklin), plated on LB agar with ampicillin, and incubated at 37°C for 12 hours. Single colonies were selected and grown in LB liquid medium with ampicillin at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 8 hours. IPTG (0.5 mM) was added, and the culture was incubated at 16 °C, 200 rpm for 12 hours. GST-tagged proteins were purified using glutathione agarose beads (78601, Pierce) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The complex was analyzed by WB.

PNGase F treatment of proteins

De-glycosylation of proteins was performed using the PNGase F deglycosylation kit (P2318S, Beyotime). 50 μg of protein was mixed with the reagents from the kit, heated at 100°C for 10 minutes, then cooled on ice. PNGase F enzyme and buffer were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1-4 hours. Samples were mixed with SDS-PAGE sample buffer, heated at 95 °C for 5 minutes, and analyzed by WB.

Promoter activity fluorescent reporter assay

HT29 and HCT116 cells were plated in 24-well plates with 3×10^5 cells per well, with three replicates per group. Once cells adhered, they were transfected with 1 μg of pGL3-promoter plasmid and 100 ng of pRL-TK Renilla control plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 hours, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (DD1205, Novus) and measured with a microplate reader (SpectraMax ABS, MD).

Vicia villosa agglutinin (VVA) Pull-Down Assay

Cells were lysed to obtain cell lysates. 4 μg of Bio-VVA (B-1235-2, Vector Laboratories) was incubated with 600 μg of protein at 4 °C for 3 hours. Streptavidin magnetic beads were added and incubated at 4 °C for 2 hours. After brief centrifugation, VVA-carbohydrate complexes were collected, washed, and mixed with 50 μL of SDS-PAGE sample buffer. After heating at 95 °C for 10 minutes, the samples were analyzed by WB.

ChIP assay

ChIP assay was performed according to the ChIP kit instructions (P2078, Beyotime). Cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, and crosslinking was terminated with 1X glycine. Cells were scraped, lysed with SDS Lysis Buffer, and chromatin was sheared to 200–1000 bp using a sonicator (10 s on, 10 s off, 40 W, 10 cycles). 20 μL of sheared chromatin was reserved as “Input,” and 200 μL was incubated with 1 μg of antibody overnight. After adding Protein A/G beads, the mixture was incubated for 60 minutes, washed, and the DNA was eluted with Elution Buffer. DNA was purified using Tris-phenol (A505193, Shanghai Sangon) and analyzed by qPCR or next-generation sequencing.

DNA Pull-Down

Nuclear extracts were prepared using a nuclear isolation kit (KTP4002, Abbkine). 1 mg of nuclear protein was incubated with 100 nmol of linearized Bio-BAGALT2 promoter or Bio-NC probes. Streptavidin magnetic beads were used for capturing, and non-specific binding was washed out with protein-binding buffer. Bound proteins were eluted with SDS-PAGE sample buffer, analyzed by WB.

Subcutaneous tumor implantation

BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, female) were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of the Nanjing First Hospital, Nanjing Medical University. The room temperature was controlled at 20–26°C, the relative humidity was 50 ± 10%, and the light and dark cycles lasted for 12/12 hours. Sterile feed and drinking water were provided, and the bedding was changed weekly. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use, and the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital, Nanjing Medical University, approved all animal experiments and procedures performed in our study. The protocol was registered already.

For the subcutaneous tumor implantation assay, 20 mice were randomly divided into 4 groups of 5 mice each using a random number table (based on commonly used sample sizes in similar subcutaneous tumorigenesis assays from previous studies in our laboratory and the literature). Cells (Ctrl, OE-ZNF30, OE-MUC20, and OE-B4GALT2) in logarithmic growth phase were collected and washed with PBS, then resuspended to a concentration of 1 × 10^8 cells/mL. 100 μL of the cell suspension was injected into the right hind flank of each mouse in the corresponding group. Tumor growth was monitored by measuring the length and width of the tumors starting from day 7 post-injection, with measurements taken every 3 days, by the same researcher. Once the mouse’s weight dropped by more than 20% or the animal showed necrosis, the experiment was terminated. The expected adverse events were severe limitations in movement and skin ulcers. After 28 days, all the surviving mice without any serious adverse events were euthanized. The subcutaneous tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. The maximal tumor volume permitted by the Committee was 1500mm3 and in none of the experiments were these limits exceeded.

Liver metastasis model

For the liver metastasis model, 6-week-old BALB/c nude mice (4 groups of 3 mice each, using a random number table) were anesthetized with 0.5% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. Mice were fixed on the surgical table with tape for 3-5 minutes. The skin was disinfected, and a longitudinal incision was made just below the left axillary line. The spleen was exposed, and 0.1 mL of CRC single-cell suspension (1 × 10^6 cells) was injected into the spleen. After ensuring no bleeding, the abdominal muscles and skin were intermittently sutured. Post-surgery, mice were housed under SPF conditions in the animal facility. All procedures were performed with the center’s approval for the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital, Nanjing Medical University.

Human tissue samples

The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed, and informed consent was obtained from all human participants prior to sample collection. CRC patients (n = 48) who underwent surgical resection or colonoscopy in Nanjing First Hospital were included. After excision, specimens were fixed in paraformaldehyde or stored at −80 °C for further studies. This study was conducted in no conflict with the contents of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the safety and privacy of patients and the principles of voluntary and informed consent are fully considered.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Subcutaneous tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and subjected to IHC. Antigen retrieval was performed in citric acid buffer under high pressure for 3 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies Ki67 (27309-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:5000), ZO-1 (21773-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:2000), and Vimentin (10366-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:5000). After washing and incubation with secondary antibodies, staining was developed using DAB chromogenic reagent (AR1022, Beyotime), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with nail polish. Images were captured using a light microscope (Motic, BA410).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0. All quantitative data represent a minimum of three independent experimental replicates and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Specific statistical methodologies were employed according to experimental design requirements: unpaired Student’s t-test for comparisons between two independent groups; paired-samples t-test for matched group comparisons; one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc analysis for multiple comparisons against a single control group; two-way ANOVA followed by either Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc testing for datasets involving two independent variables. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 for all analyses, with explicit thresholds denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

ZNF30 is overexpressed in CRC and promotes proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and EMT of CRC cells

Previous studies have demonstrated that various zinc finger proteins act as oncogenic factors in malignant tumors. In our preliminary research, we confirmed through qRT-PCR and WB that zinc finger protein ZNF30 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and cell lines (Fig. 1A, B). Subsequently, we designed ZNF30 interference plasmids and verified the interference efficiency (Fig. 1C). Then, we assessed the impact of ZNF30 knockdown on cell proliferation using CCK8 and colony formation assays. The results confirmed that ZNF30 promotes CRC cell proliferation (Fig. 1D, E). To evaluate the effect of ZNF30 knockdown on cell invasion and metastasis, we conducted Transwell migration and invasion assays. The findings indicated that ZNF30 enhances the invasive and metastatic capabilities of CRC cells (Fig. 1F). Cell morphology following ZNF30 interference was also observed under a microscope (Fig. 1G). Additionally, we used IF and WB techniques to examine the expression of EMT markers, including ZO-1, E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin. The results confirmed that ZNF30 facilitates the EMT phenotype in CRC cells (Fig. 1H, I). High expression of ZNF30 in CRC cells induces M2 polarization of TAMs

To investigate whether ZNF30 in CRC regulates macrophage polarization, we transfected CRC cells with ZNF30 interference plasmids (or control plasmids) and co-cultured them with macrophages (Fig. 2A). Flow cytometry was used to assess the level of the M2 polarization marker CD206 in macrophages, revealing that ZNF30 interference inhibits M2 polarization of macrophages (Fig. 2B). Additionally, qRT-PCR and WB were used to measure the expression levels of classic M1 markers (IL-12, TNF-α) and M2 markers (IL-10, TGF-β) of macrophages after co-culture. The results showed that ZNF30 interference significantly reduced the expression levels of M2 markers and increased the expression levels of M1 markers (Fig. 2C, D), indicating that ZNF30 interference inhibits macrophage M2 polarization. These findings confirmed that ZNF30 plays an oncogenic role in CRC and may mediate CRC immune evasion by promoting M2 polarization of TAMs, thereby accelerating CRC malignant progression. High expression of MUC20 in CRC promotes cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis and EMT

Previous studies have reported that MUC20 is significantly upregulated in CRC with poor prognosis and may serve as a crucial predictor for recurrence and adverse outcomes in CRC patients22. We employed qRT-PCR and WB assays to assess the expression levels of MUC20 in CRC tissues, confirming its high expression in CRC tissues and cell lines (Fig. 3A, B). Subsequently, we designed MUC20 interference plasmids and, after verifying the interference efficiency (Fig. 3C), used CCK8 and colony formation assays to examine the effects of MUC20 on CRC cell proliferation. The results demonstrated that MUC20 promotes CRC cell proliferation (Fig. 3D, E). Transwell migration and invasion assays were conducted to evaluate the impact of MUC20 on CRC cell metastatic and invasive capabilities, revealing that MUC20 enhances CRC cell invasion and metastasis (Fig. 3F). Cell morphology following MUC20 interference was also observed under a microscope (Fig. 3G). Additionally, we assessed the changes in EMT-related protein expression in CRC cells following MUC20 interference through IF and WB. The results confirmed that MUC20 promotes the formation of the EMT phenotype in CRC cells (Fig. 3H, I). These findings indicated that MUC20 is highly expressed in CRC and plays a role in promoting malignant features, including proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and EMT. Discussion

Over the past decade, advancements in endoscopic technology and various treatment strategies, including surgery, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies, have led to an overall decline in the incidence and mortality rates of CRC. However, tumor metastasis remains a major obstacle impacting CRC treatment and prognosis4,5. Although immunotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment for advanced cancer, offering hope to patients and inducing durable anti-tumor responses in certain cancer patient subgroups27, many tumors can evade immunotherapy, limiting its widespread application. This is particularly true for patients with microsatellite stable (MSS) or proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) tumors. CRC is a prime example, characterized by a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that leads to resistance to immunotherapy28,29. Specifically, individuals with CRC liver metastases (CRLM) derive even less clinical benefit from immunotherapy30–32.

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) provides a “fertile ground” for cancer progression, not only promoting tumor malignancy and aiding tumor immune evasion but also mediating resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. In TIME, immune cells, particularly TAMs, are a key component through their exchange of materials and information with adjacent tumor cells. Research indicates that TAMs can exhibit either M1 or M2 polarization states depending on environmental signals6,7. M2 TAMs are typically induced by factors such as IL-4 and IL-10, displaying strong immunosuppressive activity. They not only directly promote tumor cell growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis but also inhibit T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses, leading to immune evasion and ultimately tumor progression7,8 High infiltration of M2 TAMs has been reported to be closely associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients33,34. Additionally, studies have confirmed that antisense oligonucleotides (ASO-STAT6) carrying specific sequences on the exosomal surface can selectively silence STAT6 expression in TAMs, reprogramming M2 TAMs to M1-type and remodeling the TME to exert potent single-agent anti-tumor activity35. Recently, another study reported that the CSF1R inhibitor PXB17 can reprogram M2-type macrophages to M1-type, enhancing the efficacy of anti-CRC PD-1 therapy and improving the CRC immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting cholesterol biosynthesis36. These studies highlight the critical regulatory role of TAMs in CRC immunosuppressive microenvironments and suggest that identifying effective targets to reprogram TAMs to M1-type has significant potential for enhancing CRC immunotherapy.

Our research has found that the transcription factor ZNF30 is highly expressed in CRC and promotes M2 polarization of macrophages, suggesting a crucial role of ZNF30 in CRC tumor immune evasion. However, the specific molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Previous studies have extensively explored the mechanisms by which tumor cells regulate macrophage polarization within the TME. Much of the research has focused on how CRC cells regulate TAM polarization through the secretion of extracellular vesicles carrying key molecules. For example, exosomal miR-934 from CRC cells induces M2 macrophage polarization by downregulating PTEN expression and activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway37; similarly, exosomal miR-1246 from CRC cells promotes TAM polarization, thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion38. In fact, there are also other types of regulatory mechanisms between tumor cells and macrophages.

Protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) are generally considered mechanisms for protein degradation or turnover to maintain physiological homeostasis39. Glycosylation, as one of the important forms of PTMs, occurs on more than 50% of translated proteins. Research has shown that glycosylation patterns influence tumor metastasis, whether global or site-specific40. Abnormal expression level and glycosylation of various mucins have been reported to be closely associated with tumor invasion and poor prognosis, mediating tumor immune evasion by driving interactions between cancer cells and matrix cells in the TME, especially immune cells. MUC1, a type I transmembrane glycoprotein, is the most studied mucin. Abnormal glycosylation of MUC1 on cancer cells directly binds to selectins or sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin (Siglec) family proteins expressed on immune cells, including macrophages, and inhibits their function24,41. MUC20, another membrane-binding mucin, has been identified as an important predictor of tumor recurrence and poor prognosis in CRC patients22. Our research also confirmed that MUC20 is highly expressed in CRC and induces M2 polarization of macrophages, ultimately promoting tumor immune evasion. Significantly, we detected the expression of ZNF30 and MUC20 in tumor tissues from stage I-IV CRC patients using multiplex immunofluorescence. The results showed that the expression levels of ZNF30 and MUC20 in stage IV CRC tissues are significantly higher compared to those in stages I–III (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating ZNF30 and MUC20 could serve as sensitive markers of advanced CRC, particularly.

The B4GalT (β-1,4-galactosyltransferase) gene family encodes a group of type II transmembrane glycoproteins that transfer galactose to receptor sugars via β-1,4 linkages. Among them, B4GALT1 was the first gene to be cloned and characterized, has been identified as a potential biomarker for CRC42. High expression of B4GALT4 has also been associated with CRC metastasis and poor prognosis43. In this study, we found that B4GALT2 is highly expressed in CRC and promotes CRC proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and EMT. Mechanistically, B4GALT2 acts as a bridging molecule between ZNF30 and MUC20, regulating MUC20 glycosylation levels and mediating macrophage polarization. This research suggests that the ZNF30/B4GALT2/MUC20 glycosylation axis collectively functions in CRC, reprogramming macrophages into a tumor-supportive phenotype and reshaping the TIME. Furthermore, our study confirmed the cancer-promoting function of the ZNF30/B4GALT2/MUC20 axis in a nude mouse model. Although it provides a basis for studying the role of this axis in a more complex immune system environment, it needs to be verified in immune-healthy mice in the future.

Sialylation is a modification involving terminal sialic acid residues on mucins. This modification acts as a bridge between cells and between cells and the extracellular matrix. Tumor cell surface sialylated glycoproteins can bind to Siglecs on immune cells, leading to immune suppression. This process inhibits NK cell cytotoxicity and T cell activation, induces a TAM phenotype that promotes continuous tumor growth44. Research has shown that TAMs express high levels of CD33-related inhibitory Siglec molecules. Human Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 not only inhibit the tumor-killing effect of NK cells and tumor-infiltrating T cells but also promote M2 polarization of TAMs, leading to tumor progression45,46. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, sialylation on tumor cells is recognized by Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 on myeloid cells, causing monocytes to polarize into pro-tumor macrophages47. A significant study by the Biomedical Center at Basel University Hospital published in Science Translational Medicine demonstrated that targeting the sialic acid glycan-Siglec axis can alleviate this immune suppressive microenvironment by promoting M1 polarization of TAMs, enhancing CD8+ T cell function, and sensitizing immune checkpoint inhibitors46. Therefore, targeting sialylation regulators or reducing sialylated glycan levels in the tumor microenvironment represents a potential strategy for remodeling macrophage phenotypes and enhancing adaptive anti-tumor immune responses. Our research confirmed that the sialylated glycoprotein MUC20 on CRC cell surfaces interacts with Siglec-7 on macrophages, promoting M2 polarization and mediating tumor immune evasion. These results suggest that targeting MUC20 or Siglec-7 may be crucial for remodeling the CRC tumor immune suppressive microenvironment, warranting further exploration.

In summary, our study reveals that high expression of ZNF30 in CRC cells promotes MUC20 N-glycosylation by transcriptionally activating the glycosyltransferase B4GALT2. This modification induces M2 polarization of TAMs, thereby remodeling the TIME and facilitating CRC immune evasion (Fig. 8G). Ultimately, this process accelerates CRC malignant progression. This regulatory mechanism provides new insights into the role of MUC20 glycosylation in CRC immune escape, enhancing our understanding of cell interactions within the CRC tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, it offers a theoretical foundation for developing novel combination therapies targeting MUC20 and M2 TAMs, potentially leading to breakthroughs in CRC treatment.
