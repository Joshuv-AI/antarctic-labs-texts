Outcomes and Prognostic Factors of Auto-Transplanted Immature Third Molars: A 15-Year Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Dohyun Kim, Young-Eun Jang, Ukseong Kim, Jingwen Li, Jisoo Song, Ryu Seong-yeon, Sun-Jong Kim, Jung-Hyun Park, Jin-Woo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo evaluate long-term survival, complete healing, and replacement root resorption after immature third-molar auto-transplantation and identify prognostic factors.Materials and methodsThis retrospective single-centre cohort included 108 patients (116 immature and open-apex third molars) treated from 2002 to 2018. Fully mature closed-apex donor teeth were excluded. Survival and complete healing were estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis, and prognostic factors were assessed using Cox proportional-hazards models. Replacement root resorption was explored using an exploratory logistic regression model. Mean follow-up duration was 4.9 ± 3.6 years (range, 0.5-15.5 years).ResultsAmong 116 transplants, 13 (11.2%) were lost during follow-up. Estimated survival was 87.6% at both 5 and 10 years and 81.3% at 15 years; the corresponding complete-healing success estimates were 83.7%, 81.2%, and 75.4%. In the adjusted Cox model, age, donor-root length status, and reason for extraction were associated with survival and complete-healing outcomes. For replacement root resorption, the exploratory logistic model showed associations with age and donor-root length status, whereas fixation method and reason for tooth extraction were not significant.ConclusionsImmature third-molar auto-transplantation showed favourable estimated long-term survival, particularly in fresh extraction sockets. Age, recipient-site status, and donor-root length status should inform case selection and follow-up.

Introduction

Tooth auto-transplantation is defined as the surgical transfer of an impacted or erupted tooth from a donor site to an extraction socket or surgically prepared recipient site within the same individual.1 Compared with prosthetic replacements or dental implants, auto-transplantation preserves viable periodontal ligament cells and natural dentine collagen, which can promote alveolar bone healing, proprioception, and physiologic adaptation.2 These advantages are particularly relevant in children and adolescents, in whom implant placement remains controversial because implants do not adapt to dentoalveolar growth and may later become infra-occluded. Auto-transplantation may also reduce the treatment burden associated with implant osseointegration, treatment cost, compromised infected or pathologic recipient sites, and peri-implant soft-tissue esthetic challenges.3, 4, 5

The biological behaviour of immature donor teeth differs from that of mature closed-apex teeth. Immature donor teeth retain a greater potential for pulp revascularization, continued root development, and periodontal healing, supported by experimental replantation and auto-transplantation studies showing vascular and pulpal healing in immature teeth.6, 7, 8 In contrast, mature closed-apex donor teeth have a higher likelihood of pulp necrosis and often require planned endodontic treatment, consistent with clinical outcome literature involving mature donor teeth.9, 10, 11, 12 Recent umbrella reviews and meta-analyses identified root-development stage as a prognostic factor, with closed apices associated with higher risks of ankylosis or resorption in some datasets.5,13, 14 For this reason, mature closed-apex donor teeth should not be regarded as a simple internal control group for immature donor teeth; they represent a biologically and clinically distinct treatment category. However, the available evidence remains limited—most studies report relatively short- to mid-term follow-up, combine heterogeneous donor-tooth types, or emphasize overall retention without separately analysing biological healing. Few cohorts specifically evaluate immature third molars over more than 10 years while also examining determinants of replacement root resorption.

The definitions of survival and success in auto-transplantation vary across studies.5,14, 15 In the present study, survival was defined as the broader clinical endpoint of a transplanted tooth remaining in situ and serviceable. Complete healing was defined as a stricter biological endpoint requiring normal function, physiologic mobility, absence of pathologic periodontal or periapical findings, and no inflammatory or replacement root resorption. Thus, complete healing is nested within survival: a tooth may survive clinically while failing to meet complete-healing criteria because of ankylosis or resorption. Reporting both endpoints allows a clinically pragmatic interpretation of tooth retention and a stricter assessment of biological healing.

Therefore, the aim of this retrospective single-centre cohort study was to investigate long-term survival and complete-healing rates after auto-transplantation of immature third molars and to identify prognostic factors associated with tooth loss, incomplete healing, and replacement root resorption. By focusing on donor teeth with open apices, this analysis was intended to inform donor selection, recipient-site planning, and follow-up after immature third-molar auto-transplantation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent third molar auto-transplantation at Ewha Womans University Medical Center between February 2002 and December 2018. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of Ewha Womans University Mokdong Hospital (EUMC 2018-01-046-003). Patient records were reviewed to collect baseline data including age at surgery, sex, donor tooth root-development status, donor and recipient jaw (maxilla or mandible), whether root canal treatment was performed, and the fixation method used (Essig wire splint, resin-wire splint, or suture stabilization). Third molars with fully formed roots and closed apices were classified as mature donor teeth and were excluded from the present analysis. Donor teeth with incomplete root formation or complete root length with an open apex were included and collectively considered immature donor teeth. Smoking status, dental follicle integrity, extra-alveolar time, number of fitting attempts, total surgical time, and subsequent restorative or orthodontic treatment were incompletely documented in the retrospective charts and therefore were excluded from the statistical analysis. Three-dimensional surgical guides were not used during the study period. All patients were encouraged to attend follow-up examinations, and clinical and radiographic data from these visits were recorded. Postoperative discomfort or complications, including pain on percussion or palpation, swelling, percussion-sound change, increased mobility, periodontal pocketing, periapical radiolucency, inflammatory root resorption, ankylosis or replacement root resorption, and loss of lamina dura, were extracted from follow-up records and radiographs.

Surgical Procedure

All auto-transplantation surgeries were performed under local anaesthesia (2% lidocaine with 1:80,000-1:100,000 epinephrine). When the recipient site still contained a tooth, the tooth was carefully extracted with forceps, and sectioning was performed when necessary to minimize trauma to adjacent tissues. After extraction, the integrity of the socket was evaluated, and any granulation tissue was removed. The recipient socket was then carefully prepared to accommodate the donor tooth using a low-speed surgical straight handpiece under copious sterile saline irrigation to minimize thermal damage to the bone. Round carbide and fissure surgical burs were used to selectively adjust the socket walls. In cases involving edentulous recipient sites where a transplant bed had to be surgically created, a crestal incision was made, and a full-thickness flap was elevated. The artificial socket was prepared via sequential drilling, starting with a round bur to mark the position, followed by progressive widening using larger burs to replicate the three-dimensional morphology of the donor root estimated from preoperative CBCT or radiographs. The preparation continued until the donor tooth could be inserted without excessive pressure while maintaining close adaptation to the recipient bone. Piezosurgical devices were not used as part of the standardized historical protocol; recipient-site osteotomy was performed with low-speed rotary instruments under irrigation to reduce thermal and mechanical trauma.

The donor third molar was extracted after elevation of a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap. Overlying bone that impeded donor tooth removal was trimmed with a low-speed surgical bur under irrigation. Bone adjacent to the root was selectively removed as needed, and extraction forceps rather than elevators were used whenever possible to reduce injury to the root surface and periodontal ligament. The donor tooth was immediately transferred to the recipient socket. When dental follicle or germ tissue interfered with seating or stabilization, only the obstructing portion was carefully removed while preserving the root surface. The tooth was positioned in slight infraocclusion and stabilized with sutures alone when primary stability was adequate or with semi-rigid splinting, including an Essig wire splint or a resin-wire splint, when additional support was required. Patients were prescribed chlorhexidine mouth rinse and antibiotics with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory analgesics for 5 days postoperatively. They were instructed to avoid direct loading of the transplanted tooth during early healing, maintain careful oral hygiene, follow a soft diet for 1 week, and return for scheduled clinical and radiographic follow-up. Splints, when placed, were maintained for approximately 2-4 weeks. For immature donor teeth, prophylactic root canal treatment was not performed. Pulp status was monitored clinically and radiographically, and root canal treatment was performed only when signs of irreversible pulpitis, pulp necrosis, persistent swelling, or periapical infection developed. Three-dimensional printed donor-tooth replicas or surgical guides were not used as part of the standardized protocol during the study period and were not available for analysis. All surgeries were performed by one of three oral and maxillofacial surgeons at the same institution according to a standardized institutional protocol.

Outcome Criteria

Auto-transplantation outcomes were evaluated using two nested endpoint categories: survival vs removal and complete healing vs incomplete healing/failure.16, 17, 18 Survival was defined as the transplanted tooth remaining in situ and serviceable at follow-up, whereas removal indicated that the transplant had been extracted or lost because of complications. Complete healing was defined as a stricter biological endpoint: normal function without discomfort during chewing, physiologic mobility, absence of pathologic pocketing or radiographic lesions, intact lamina dura, and healthy gingival colour and contour. A surviving tooth was classified as incomplete healing/failure if inflammatory root resorption, ankylosis, replacement root resorption, persistent periapical pathology, or other pathologic clinical/radiographic findings developed during follow-up. Criteria for removal included excessive mobility (greater than Grade II), inability to perform normal masticatory function, progressive periodontal breakdown or bone loss exceeding one-third of the root length, severe root resorption, or a hopeless endodontic or periodontal prognosis. All outcome assessments were performed by a single examiner (JWK) to ensure consistency.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate censoring-adjusted cumulative survival and complete-healing success at 5, 10, and 15 years using the survival package. Between-group differences in time-to-event outcomes were evaluated with the log-rank test. Univariate analyses were performed for all candidate variables for survival, complete healing, and replacement root resorption. For survival and complete healing, adjusted Cox proportional-hazards models were constructed using prespecified clinically relevant variables: age, donor-root length status, fixation method, and reason for tooth extraction. For replacement root resorption, an exploratory logistic regression model was fitted using the same variables because only 8 replacement root resorption events were observed. Stepwise variable selection was not used. Hazard ratios (HRs), odds ratios (ORs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

During the observation period, 13 transplanted teeth (11.2%) failed and were removed. Detailed clinical information for all 13 removed transplants is summarized in Supplemental Table 1. The reasons for removal were replacement root resorption or ankylosis (4 cases), endodontic or apical inflammatory lesions (2 cases), inflammatory root resorption (3 cases), combined inflammatory and replacement root resorption/ankylosis (2 cases), combined inflammatory root resorption and periodontal lesion (1 case), and combined inflammatory root resorption and endodontic complications (1 case). Five additional teeth (4.3%) remained in situ but were classified as incomplete healing/failure because they developed replacement root resorption or ankylosis during follow-up despite being asymptomatic. Postoperative discomfort and complication findings were reviewed from clinical charts and radiographs. Because these data were not collected with a prospective patient-reported outcome, the findings were summarized descriptively rather than as standardized severity scores. Clinically consequential postoperative complications were reflected in the 13 removed transplants and the 5 additional retained teeth classified as incomplete healing/failure.

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed estimated survival of 87.6% at both 5 and 10 years, and 81.3% at 15 years after transplantation. The corresponding estimated complete-healing success rates were 83.7%, 81.2%, and 75.4%, respectively. These Kaplan–Meier estimates were lower than the crude event-count proportions because censoring during follow-up was incorporated. Recipient-site condition was associated with transplant outcomes. Transplants placed into edentulous recipient sites, in which the tooth had already been missing and the site had healed before transplantation, had lower survival than transplants placed into fresh extraction sockets. In the Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified by reason for extraction or edentulism (Figure 2B), the estimated mean survival time was 88.4 ± 11.0 months for edentulous recipient sites, 171.6 ± 7.0 months for sites where the original tooth was extracted for endodontic reasons such as caries or fracture, and 127.2 ± 7.4 months for sites where the original tooth was extracted for periodontal reasons.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort showed durable long-term tooth retention after immature third-molar auto-transplantation: Kaplan–Meier-estimated survival remained 87.6% through 10 years and was 81.3% at 15 years after censoring was accounted for. Complete healing was lower than survival because it used a stricter biological definition that classified teeth with ankylosis or replacement root resorption as incomplete healing even when they remained functional. These results support dental auto-transplantation as a biologically relevant tooth-replacement option, especially in young patients for whom implants may be less suitable because of ongoing dentoalveolar growth, cost, osseointegration time, and soft-tissue or esthetic limitations.5,19, 20 These long-term estimates are clinically relevant because dental auto-transplantation remains underused in many practice settings despite its biological advantages. Al-Khanati and Kara Beit reported that clinicians may hesitate to offer auto-transplantation because of limited training exposure, perceived technique sensitivity, and uncertainty about prognosis.20 The present findings do not eliminate the need for surgical experience and careful case selection, but they provide long-term outcome estimates that may support patient counselling and referral decisions when an immature third molar is available.

The survival pattern in this cohort is consistent with recent evidence syntheses and cohort studies on dental auto-transplantation. Sicilia-Pasos et al reported high pooled survival for developing teeth with incomplete root formation,13 and umbrella reviews by Tan et al and Cremona et al found favourable survival and success despite heterogeneity in endpoint definitions and study quality.5,14 Recent studies of mature third molars and premolars also reported long-term retention across different donor types and clinical settings.21, 22 The present study adds to this literature by focusing specifically on immature third molars and by reporting survival separately from complete healing over follow-up extending to 15.5 years.

Patient age and donor-root length status were associated with prognosis. Younger patients may have greater healing potential, and immature donor teeth with open apices have a greater capacity for pulp revascularization and continued root development than mature closed-apex teeth.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10,13, 14 In our cohort, increasing age was associated with incomplete healing and RRR, and was also associated with a higher hazard of transplant removal in the adjusted Cox model. Complete donor-root length development was associated with lower hazards of transplant removal and incomplete healing in the adjusted Cox models, whereas its association with lower odds of RRR was observed only in the restricted exploratory logistic model. These findings suggest that age should be considered together with radiographic root-development stage rather than used as an isolated exclusion criterion.

Recipient-site biology was the strongest determinant of tooth loss. Transplants placed into pre-existing edentulous sites had worse outcomes than those inserted into fresh extraction sockets. A likely explanation is that fresh extraction sockets retain periodontal ligament remnants, native socket architecture, and vascular conditions that may favour healing, whereas edentulous ridges require surgically created beds and may impose greater bony trauma and adaptation demands. This finding should not be interpreted as indicating that edentulous recipient sites are contraindicated. The successful long-term case shown in Figure 3 demonstrates that transplantation into an edentulous site can be feasible when the recipient bed is well prepared, primary stability is achieved, and follow-up is favourable. However, a single successful case does not negate the cohort-level finding that edentulous sites had a higher average risk of removal and incomplete healing than fresh extraction sockets. Thus, edentulous recipient sites should be regarded as a higher-risk clinical condition requiring careful selection, atraumatic recipient-bed preparation, stable fixation, and structured follow-up rather than as uniformly unfavourable sites.

Standardized periodontal and morbidity assessment is also relevant when interpreting recipient-site effects. In a prospective study of lower third-molar surgery, Pang et al showed that periodontal healing around the adjacent second molar can be evaluated using standardized periodontal probing depth, clinical attachment level, radiographic bone-level changes, and postoperative morbidity measures.23 Although that study did not evaluate auto-transplantation, these measures are relevant to the present cohort because recipient-site condition and surgically created beds may influence periodontal healing, but such standardized periodontal and morbidity variables were not prospectively captured. Future auto-transplantation studies should therefore incorporate these measures to better define how recipient-site biology and surgical preparation affect long-term outcomes.

Dental follicle integrity is another biologically relevant donor-tooth handling variable that could not be evaluated in this retrospective cohort. In experimental tooth-germ transplantation, damage to or removal of the dental follicle prevented eruption, whereas teeth replanted with an intact follicle erupted and showed continued root development.24 In a clinical follow-up study of 215 auto-transplanted teeth, the condition of the follicle was recorded as a perioperative variable together with visible periodontal-ligament damage; although follicle condition itself was not an independent predictor, surgical complications and damaged root periodontium were associated with lower success.25 Prospective protocols for developing premolar transplantation also emphasize atraumatic donor removal with preservation of the dental follicle, cementum, and Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath.26 Recent tooth-germ transplantation evidence also supports the possibility of continued development after autologous transplantation.27 Because follicle integrity was not systematically documented in our historical records, its potential contribution to incomplete healing, ankylosis, or replacement root resorption could not be assessed.

The fixation-method findings should be interpreted within this broader procedural context. Although suture-only stabilization appeared less favourable descriptively, only 10 cases (8.6%) were managed with sutures alone, and fixation choice likely reflected primary stability, root length, recipient-site anatomy, and surgeon judgment rather than a randomized comparison of fixation techniques. Suture-only stabilization may remain appropriate in selected mandibular cases with adequate root length, intimate socket fit, and stable primary seating. Other procedural variables from contemporary literature, including extra-alveolar time, number of fitting attempts, total surgical time, and use of 3D printed donor-tooth replicas, were not systematically available in this historical cohort. Modern 3D-assisted protocols have been introduced to reduce donor-tooth manipulation, extra-alveolar time, and surgical duration.28, 29 The absence of these variables limits mechanistic interpretation and reflects data collection before contemporary digital workflows became routine.

The single-centre retrospective design may introduce selection and documentation bias and may limit generalizability. In addition to the surgical variables discussed above, smoking status, systemic conditions, periodontal/endodontic background variables, and subsequent restorative or orthodontic treatment were not sufficiently standardized for modelling. A recent retrospective case-control study in adult periodontitis patients reported that diabetes mellitus was not significantly associated with endodontically treated teeth or tooth loss after matching and multivariable adjustment; although that study did not evaluate auto-transplantation, it illustrates how systemic status, periodontal diagnosis, endodontic history, tooth type, and jaw location can be captured in a structured retrospective design.30 The number of RRR events was small (n = 8); therefore, the logistic regression model was restricted to prespecified clinically relevant variables and should be interpreted as exploratory, especially for covariates with wide or non-estimable confidence intervals caused by sparse-data separation. Prospective cohorts should record standardized systemic, periodontal, morbidity, digital-planning, intraoperative, and follow-up variables, including dental follicle integrity, to clarify which protocol components most strongly influence long-term prognosis.

In conclusion, immature third-molar auto-transplantation showed favourable long-term outcomes, with Kaplan–Meier estimated survival remaining above 80% at 15 years in this single-centre cohort. Younger age, open-apex donor biology, and fresh extraction socket recipient sites were associated with more favourable survival and complete-healing outcomes, whereas edentulous recipient sites represented a higher-risk condition requiring careful case selection and follow-up. Replacement root resorption was uncommon, but its prognostic analysis should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of events. These findings support donor and recipient-site selection based on root-development stage, recipient-site condition, atraumatic surgical handling, stable fixation, and structured follow-up.
