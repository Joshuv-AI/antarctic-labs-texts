Von Economo Neuron Loss in Frontotemporal Dementia: A Meta‐Analysis of Neuropathological Studies
Authors: Daniel Talmasov, Alexander F. Santillo, Patrick R. Hof
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVon Economo neurons (VENs) have been reported to be vulnerable to neurodegeneration in frontotemporal dementia (FTD), particularly the behavioral variant (bvFTD), but these findings have not been systematically assessed across independent brain banks. We conducted a meta‐analysis of neuropathological studies measuring VEN density in the anterior cingulate cortex or frontoinsular cortex in FTD using random‐effects models with cluster‐robust variance estimation. Seven studies (135 FTD, 68 controls) from four international brain banks showed significantly reduced VEN density in FTD with a large effect size (g = −1.45, 95% CI [−1.69, −1.21], p < 0.001) and remarkable consistency (I2 = 0%). VEN loss was greater in FTD than Alzheimer's disease and occurred across TDP‐43 and tau pathological subtypes.

Introduction

Understanding which cellular populations are vulnerable to neurodegeneration is critical to understanding the biology of neurodegenerative diseases, their clinical manifestations, and potential therapeutic targets. Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) has been associated with early degeneration of von Economo neurons (VENs), a population of specialized, giant excitatory neurons sparsely interspersed within layer V of anterior cortical regions [1, 2, 3, 4]. In anthropoid primates, including humans, they are observed in the anterior cingulate, dorsal prefrontal, and frontoinsular cortices [1, 2, 3, 5, 6], and were later found in many other mammalian species with variable distribution [7].

Since the initial report that VENs are targets of selective neurodegeneration in FTD [4], several neuropathological studies have reported reduced VEN density in FTD compared to healthy control brains [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Comparisons between FTD and Alzheimer's disease (AD) patients have also shown greater VEN loss in FTD [4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14], suggesting that VEN vulnerability distinguishes FTD from other neurodegenerative disorders. Despite replication across multiple independent cohorts, these findings have not been meta‐analytically synthesized to quantify the magnitude and consistency of VEN loss in FTD. Additionally, whether VEN vulnerability differs across FTD pathological subtypes (TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 [TDP‐43] or tau) remains unclear. Here, we conducted a meta‐analysis of neuropathological studies quantifying VEN density in FTD to characterize the magnitude and consistency of VEN reduction relative to control and AD comparison groups.

Methods

Study Selection and Data Extraction

We conducted a PRISMA‐guided literature review using PubMed/Web of Science/Cochrane Library databases. The search strategy included the following terms: (“von Economo” OR “spindle”) AND (“frontotemporal dementia” OR “FTD” OR “frontotemporal degeneration” OR “frontotemporal lobar degeneration”). References of key articles were also manually screened for additional eligible studies.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they (1) quantitatively measured and numerically reported VEN cell counts or densities in postmortem human brain tissue using stereological or systematic cell counting methods, (2) included neuropathologically confirmed FTD cases with clinical diagnosis documented prior to death, (3) reported data for both an FTD group and a pathologically unaffected comparison group numerically within the text (or reported values from which mean values and standard deviations could be readily derived), and (4) examined VEN‐rich cortical regions (anterior cingulate cortex or frontoinsular cortex). We excluded conference abstracts, review articles, studies with insufficient sample sizes (n < 3 per group), studies using qualitative or incompatible quantification methods, and studies lacking a pathologically unaffected comparison group.

For each study, we extracted sample sizes, means, and standard deviations for VEN density or counts. We included only numerical values reported in text or tables or provided through author correspondence. Studies presenting VEN quantification data exclusively in graphical form [12] were excluded to ensure extraction precision. Corresponding authors were contacted to request additional data when values were not numerically reported; one author [9, 10] provided unpublished subgroup‐specific statistics that were incorporated. When one study [11] reported numerical data for only a single FTD subgroup (sporadic FTD cases, expressed as a percentage of controls), we calculated absolute values and standard deviations using reported control group statistics and included only that subgroup. When bilateral measurements were reported [8], we averaged values at the patient level. When studies reported multiple FTD pathological subtypes, we extracted both combined and subtype‐specific data for secondary analyses.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.1) using the metafor [15] and clubSandwich [16] packages. Effect sizes were calculated as Hedges' g [17], the standardized mean difference between groups, which applies a small‐sample bias correction to Cohen's d.

Meta‐analyses were conducted using random‐effects models with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. For the primary FTD versus control analysis and the FTD versus AD comparison, we implemented cluster‐robust variance estimation with small‐sample bias correction [16], using brain bank as the clustering variable. For secondary analyses by pathological subtype, which had fewer studies per comparison (two to three studies), we used standard random‐effects models without cluster adjustments, as robust methods require sufficient clusters for reliable estimation.

Based on consistent prior evidence of VEN reduction in FTD, we tested the directional hypothesis that VEN density would be reduced in FTD relative to comparison groups. We report both one‐tailed and two‐tailed p‐values for transparency. Heterogeneity was assessed using I 2 and τ 2. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

We identified seven studies meeting our inclusion criteria [4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14], published between 2006 and 2022 (Figure 1), comprising tissue from four independent brain banks across three continents: the University of California, San Francisco/University of California, Irvine (the United States), Lund University (Sweden), the Netherlands Brain Bank (the Netherlands), and the Sydney Brain Bank (Australia). Six studies examined the anterior cingulate cortex [4, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14] and one examined the frontoinsular cortex [8].

PRISMA study selection flow diagram. Database searches identified 137 records across PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. After removing duplicates and screening, 14 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Seven studies met inclusion criteria requiring quantitative VEN measurements in FTD with comparison groups, using stereological methods in VEN‐rich cortical regions (anterior cingulate cortex or frontoinsular cortex). Six articles were excluded because they lacked numerically reported VEN quantification; one article was excluded because it lacked a pathologically unaffected control group.

FTD vs. Controls

Seven studies (135 FTD, 68 controls) compared VEN density between FTD and unaffected control groups (Figure 2). VEN density was significantly reduced in FTD with a large effect size (Hedges' g = −1.45, 95% CI [−1.69, −1.21], p < 0.001 one‐tailed, p < 0.001 two‐tailed). The effect was remarkably consistent, with all studies showing reduced VEN density in FTD and no heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%, τ 2 = 0.00). Secondary analyses by pathological subtype revealed VEN loss in both major FTD pathologies. FTLD‐TDP (4 studies, 74 patients) showed lower VEN quantities (g = −1.56 [95% CI: −1.98, −1.13], p < 0.001 one‐tailed, I 2 = 0%). FTLD‐tau (2 studies, 19 patients) also demonstrated significant VEN loss (g = −1.28 [95% CI: −2.47, −0.10], p = 0.016 one‐tailed), though with greater heterogeneity (I 2 = 62%) reflecting the small number of studies.

FTD vs. AD

Six studies (127 FTD, 46 AD) compared VEN density between FTD and AD groups (Figure 3). VEN density was significantly reduced in FTD compared to AD (Hedges' g = −1.07, 95% CI [−2.17, 0.02], p = 0.026 one‐tailed, p = 0.051 two‐tailed), with all studies showing effects in the same direction.

Discussion

Our meta‐analysis demonstrates substantial VEN loss in FTD, with a large pooled effect size (g = −1.45) and remarkable consistency across studies (I 2 = 0%, τ 2 = 0.000). Critically, every study showed reduced VEN density in FTD, with effect sizes ranging from −0.96 to −2.13 standard deviations, making VEN depletion among the most pronounced cellular changes in FTD neuropathology. This consistency is notable given methodological variations across laboratories, different tissue processing protocols, and samples spanning nearly 2 decades.

VENs were significantly reduced in quantity in FTD compared to AD (g = −1.07, p = 0.026 one‐tailed). Notably, all six studies comparing FTD to AD found lower VEN density in FTD, supporting our directional hypothesis based on prior evidence of FTD‐specific VEN vulnerability. This pattern demonstrates that VEN loss reflects disease‐specific pathophysiology rather than nonspecific vulnerability to neurodegeneration.

Secondary analyses demonstrate that VEN loss occurs across both major FTD pathological subtypes. The FTLD‐TDP analysis [9, 11, 13, 14] showed effect sizes and consistency comparable to the primary analysis, while FTLD‐tau cases also showed significant VEN depletion, albeit based on only two studies comparing FTLD‐tau to unaffected controls [9, 13]. These findings suggest VEN vulnerability is a shared feature across FTD pathologies rather than being specific to TDP‐43 proteinopathy, yet additional tau cases are needed to establish the magnitude and consistency of this effect, including across FTLD‐tau subtypes [18].

Important questions remain about the biological context of VEN loss. The magnitude of VEN depletion relative to neighboring layer V pyramidal neurons, fork cells, and other cortical populations requires systematic quantification to establish whether VENs are selectively targeted or reflect broader laminar and regional vulnerability. Laterality analyses were not feasible because most included studies examined a single hemisphere (right ACC: four studies; left ACC: two studies; bilateral FI: one study). Notably, Kim et al. found no group‐level hemispheric differences in VEN density but reported stronger correlations between right‐sided VEN/fork cell loss and clinical severity, consistent with the right‐hemisphere predominance of early behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD) atrophy [8]. Similarly, regional comparisons between the anterior cingulate and frontoinsular cortices were not feasible as only one study [8] quantified VENs in the frontoinsular cortex while the remaining six studies [4, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14] examined the anterior cingulate cortex. Additionally, comparing affected regions (anterior cingulate and frontoinsular areas) to relatively spared cortical areas would clarify regional versus cell type‐specific vulnerability factors. Understanding the molecular changes underlying VEN degeneration, including transcriptional profiles and protein signatures preceding cell death, may reveal vulnerability mechanisms and identify potential therapeutic targets in FTD.
