Organoids in Cancer Research: Current Applications, Limitations, and Technological Advances
Authors: Sujeong Kim, Se Eun Park, Chanwoo Kim, Jae Yeon Kim, Sang Hyeon Han, Eunkyung Park, Jung Eun Kim, Dohun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organoids are 3-dimensional cell culture systems derived from stem cells through self-organization. They recapitulate key aspects of tissue architecture and organ-specific functions, providing a powerful preclinical platform for cancer research. In particular, patient-derived tumor organoids preserve the pathological and genetic features of primary tumors. These models support the prediction of patient-specific drug responses and thereby advance precision medicine. Despite these advantages, several challenges remain, including incomplete reconstruction of the tumor microenvironment—particularly immune components—and insufficient vascularization. Recent advances have sought to address these limitations through innovative approaches, including immune cell co-culture systems and organoid-on-a-chip technologies. This mini-review summarizes recent progress in tumor organoid research and highlights emerging strategies aimed at improving physiological relevance. It also discusses future perspectives, emphasizing the expanding roles of organoids in precision oncology and regenerative medicine.

Introduction

The significance of cancer research

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with millions of individuals affected by its life-threatening consequences. In 2022, approximately 20 million new cancer cases were reported globally, and cancer-related deaths reached nearly 9.7 million [1]. Among all cancer types, lung cancer accounts for the largest proportion of both incidence and mortality. Notably, comparisons between 2020 and 2022 indicate increasing trends in both incidence and mortality for lung cancer [1,2].

Despite the substantial global burden of cancer, effective therapies remain limited for many tumor types. Because ethical and safety considerations preclude direct experimentation in humans, preclinical models that approximate human biology are essential for therapeutic development. Historically, standardized high-throughput 2-dimensional (2D) cell line systems and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models—which retain aspects of tumor histology and heterogeneity—have fulfilled this role. However, both 2D cultures and PDX models fail to fully recapitulate the structural and functional complexity of human tumors, thereby limiting their utility in therapeutic discovery [3-5].

Limitations of conventional models: 2D cultures and patient-derived xenografts

Since the pioneering experiments by Harrison [3] demonstrating that cells can survive and proliferate in vitro, based on observations of frog nerve fiber development, 2D cell culture has been widely used in basic cell biology and preclinical cancer research. However, 2D cell lines accumulate genetic alterations during serial passaging, and tumor cells maintained under these conditions often develop genomic and metabolic profiles that diverge substantially from those of the original tumor, resulting in a loss of heterogeneity [6,7]. Consequently, their ability to predict patient-specific drug responses is limited. Another widely used preclinical model is the PDX, established by transplanting patient tumor tissue into immunocompromised mice [4]. Although PDX models retain certain structural and molecular features of the original tumor and have been extensively used in cancer research, they undergo mouse- specific tumor evolution during adaptation to the host environment. This process can lead to genetic divergence from the original tumor. Indeed, studies tracking aneuploidy and copy number aberration (CNA) dynamics across PDX models have shown that, although similarities to the parental tumor are maintained, CNA patterns frequently change substantially [5]. These alterations limit the ability of PDX models to accurately reflect human tumor progression. Furthermore, their complex procedures, long duration, high cost, and low success rates further constrain their applicability in cancer research. To address these limitations, 3-dimensional (3D) cell culture-based organoid systems have emerged as a next-generation platform with considerable potential for translational cancer research and therapeutic development.

Organoids in cancer research

Organoids are 3D in vitro models derived from stem cells or organ progenitors that self-organize into structures recapitulating the architectural and functional features of native organs [8]. Owing to these properties, organoids have emerged as powerful preclinical tools in cancer research. Patient-derived tumor organoids (PDTOs), established from primary tumor tissues, faithfully recapitulate the pathological characteristics of individual tumors and have expanded the scope of cancer modeling [9,10]. Accordingly, PDTOs are increasingly used as platforms for evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic agents, elucidating mechanisms of drug resistance, and investigating tumor biology [11].

Clinically, organoids contribute to precision oncology by enabling the prediction of patient-specific drug responses and the assessment of immunotherapy efficacy [12,13]. As organoid platforms continue to evolve to better model tumor heterogeneity and the tumor microenvironment (TME), their capacity to inform individualized therapeutic strategies is expected to expand further.

Beyond oncology, organoid technologies are also being explored in regenerative medicine. As these systems increasingly reproduce the structural and functional characteristics of human tissues, their applications may extend to partial organ replacement or transplantation aimed at restoring function after injury or surgical resection. For example, transplantation of intestinal organoids in a mouse small-intestinal ischemia–reperfusion model improved survival and promoted tissue regeneration [14]. Similarly, transplantation of cerebral organoids in a stroke model has been reported to reconstruct infarcted tissue and restore sensorimotor function [15]. However, because these studies were conducted in animal models, further technological refinement is required before meaningful clinical translation to humans can be achieved.

Historical background of organoids

The conceptual origin of organoids can be traced to 1907, when Wilson [16] observed that dissociated sponge cells were capable of self-organizing into functional sponge structures without external guidance. This pioneering finding provided the first evidence that adult cells possess an intrinsic capacity for self-assembly, a principle that later became foundational to organoid biology. Fig. 1 outlines the major historical milestones that contributed to the conceptual, technological, and translational progression of organoid research. The field of stem cell research advanced rapidly after the successful isolation of pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) from mouse embryos in 1981 [17]. This was followed by the derivation and culture of human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) from blastocysts in 1998 [18] and, subsequently, by the landmark development of reprogramming techniques in 2007, which enabled human dermal fibroblasts and other somatic cells to be converted into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) [19]. These advances in stem cell biology provided diverse cellular sources for organoid generation and greatly broadened the scope of organoid research. A major breakthrough was achieved in 2009, when Hans Clevers and colleagues successfully cultured intestinal adult stem cells (ASCs) and generated small- intestinal organoids displaying crypt–villus structures [20]. Unlike spheroids, which typically form simple spherical aggregates in the absence of scaffolds, these organoids self-assembled into tissue-specific architectures when embedded in extracellular matrix scaffolds such as Matrigel [21,22]. Since then, organoid culture protocols have been established for multiple organs, including the stomach, retina, brain, pancreas, liver, and lung, establishing organoids as a versatile 3D model system capable of reproducing organ development and multicellular interactions with greater fidelity than conventional 2D culture systems [23-28].

Stem cell-derived organoid types

Organoids can be broadly classified into 2 major categories according to their stem cell origin: PSC-derived organoids and ASC-derived organoids. PSC-derived organoids are generated from ESCs or iPSCs, whereas ASC-derived organoids originate from organ- or tissue-specific resident stem cells [29].

PSC-derived organoids are powerful models for recapitulating the early stages of organogenesis. In particular, iPSC-derived organoids offer the advantage of being readily established from both healthy and diseased donors, thereby avoiding the ethical constraints associated with embryonic sources [30]. However, the culture of PSC-derived organoids is time-consuming, typically requiring several months and the sequential administration of stage-specific growth factors. Accordingly, PSC-derived organoids are especially well suited for studies of human developmental biology and the mechanisms underlying early organogenesis [31].

In contrast, ASC-derived organoids are established from tissue-specific ASCs and therefore preserve the structural and functional features of the original tissue. Importantly, this category includes patient-derived organoids (PDOs), which can be generated from either normal or tumor tissues obtained from patients. By embedding patient-derived ASCs in a 3D extracellular matrix such as Matrigel, PDOs can be expanded into complex tissue-like structures [9]. Because they are established directly from patient cells, PDOs closely reflect the characteristics and functions of the donor tissue. Consequently, unlike traditional models, PDOs provide highly accurate disease modeling and serve as valuable tools for drug development and drug screening [12].

Patient-derived tumor organoids

With advances in molecular diagnostics and the development of novel anticancer agents, cancer treatment has shifted from a tumor type-centered approach toward patient-centered diagnosis and therapy. This paradigm, referred to as precision therapy, has led to substantial improvements in clinical outcomes for patients with cancer. However, challenges such as therapeutic resistance remain, underscoring the need for research tools that can more accurately predict patient-specific clinical responses to treatment [32]. PDTOs, established directly from patients’ tumor tissues, have emerged as a promising model to address this unmet need [9].

These PDTOs recapitulate the histological, genetic, and transcriptomic features of the parental tumors while preserving intratumoral heterogeneity, thereby providing a highly valuable platform for precision medicine. For example, the establishment of biobanks composed of PDTOs not only enables evaluation of drug sensitivity at the individual-patient level but also facilitates systematic identification of associations between drug response and genomic alterations. Indeed, high-throughput drug screening studies using PDTO biobanks have generated reproducible drug sensitivity data. Moreover, these studies have shown that organoid-based drug screening can identify gene–drug associations, reproduce clinically relevant resistance mechanisms, and ultimately provide a more predictive model of patient treatment response [12].

Recent advances integrating air–liquid interface (ALI) culture and CRISPR-based gene editing have enabled more faithful tumor modeling. In ALI culture, primary tumor fragments are embedded in an extracellular matrix to generate organoids, with the apical surface exposed to air and the basal surface supported by culture medium. This air–liquid configuration promotes superior oxygenation and nutrient delivery compared with submerged culture systems. In turn, this architecture helps preserve the native TME, including intratumoral immune and stromal cells. Consequently, ALI-based tumor organoids retain a native immune environment and can be used to evaluate the preclinical efficacy of immunotherapies [33]. CRISPR/Cas9 introduces DNA double-strand breaks at specific genomic loci, enabling efficient gene knockout and knock-in [34]. When applied to PDTOs, this technology permits precise dissection of how individual genes influence tumorigenesis and drug response. For example, CRISPR screens in gastric cancer PDTOs have delineated gene networks governing chemotherapy sensitivity and resistance [35].

In addition, organoids derived from circulating tumor cells obtained from primary or metastatic tumors have been developed in selected cancer types. This platform therefore provides an experimentally tractable system for studying the mechanisms that drive tumor invasion and metastasis [10].

The surgeon’s perspective on PDTO establishment and application

To date, tumor organoids have been successfully established from a variety of cancer types, including colorectal and lung cancers [9,36]. In line with these advances, our laboratory has also successfully established lung tumor organoids from surgical specimens obtained immediately after tumor resection. Limitations of tumor organoid models

Nevertheless, compared with native human organs, tumor organoid models still have several limitations that pose major obstacles to translational application.

First, tumor organoids remain limited in their ability to model the full complexity of the TME. In particular, they incompletely represent key components of the TME, including immune cells, innervation, and lymphatic vessels, and therefore fail to fully capture the interaction networks present in native tumors [37].

Second, the lack of vascularization remains a major limitation. As organoids increase in size, the absence of a vascular network restricts nutrient and oxygen diffusion and impairs metabolic waste removal. Consequently, long-term culture becomes difficult because growth and functional maintenance are hindered, often leading to central necrosis and disrupted development [38,39].

Third, current organoid platforms have limited capacity to reproduce the physical microenvironment. During organ development, not only biological factors but also external stimuli such as mechanical forces, light stimulation, and electrical stimulation play important roles. However, effective incorporation of these biophysical cues into existing organoid systems remains technically challenging [40].

Technological advances to address current challenges

Immune cell co-culture models

Tumor organoids can reproduce certain structural and functional features of tumors; however, they remain insufficient for fully representing the complex immune TME in vivo. To address this limitation, immune cell co-culture models have been developed either by preserving endogenous immune cells within tumor organoids or by introducing exogenous immune cells. A representative study demonstrated that co-culture of patient-derived colorectal and lung tumor organoids with peripheral blood-derived CD8+ T cells enabled antigen-specific activation within the organoid microenvironment, thereby permitting functional screening of autologous tumor-reactive T-cell populations. In this system, activated T cells exhibited robust cytotoxic activity against matched tumor organoids in vitro. These findings indicate that tumor organoids co-cultured with T cells can closely model the immunological interactions between T cells and tumor cells observed in vivo [41].

Another study provided evidence that transplantation of human intestinal organoids into mice with a humanized immune system generated a next-generation intestinal organoid model incorporating human immune components. In this system, immune cells progressively infiltrated and engrafted within the organoids over time, recapitulating features of the developing human gut immune environment. These findings underscore the potential of organoid-based platforms incorporating functional immune systems in vivo [42].

Organoid-on-a-chip

In traditional organoid culture, the absence of vascular structures and the difficulty of reproducing biophysical environments limit long-term survival and functional maintenance. Organoid-on-a-chip technology addresses these limitations by using microfluidic systems that enable precise control of pressure and fluid flow, thereby establishing a vascularized tissue microenvironment on the chip. This platform not only permits observation of tumor progression—including cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and intravasation—but also supports evaluation of anti-angiogenic agents. Assessment of chemotherapeutic agents within this platform further highlights its potential for precision medicine [43].

Moreover, organoid-on-a-chip systems can emulate structural features of the TME through immune cell co-culture. For instance, a study successfully established a hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) organoid-on-a-chip model by co-culturing HCC organoids with peripheral blood mononuclear cells and mesenchymal stem cells. This model enabled simulation of the HCC immune microenvironment and, when used to evaluate immunotherapeutic responses, outperformed conventional PDO models in predicting treatment efficacy [44]. Such findings highlight the role of organoid-on-a-chip systems as precise platforms for evaluating immune cell infiltration and immunotherapeutic responsiveness.

Organoid-on-a-chip platforms also permit co-culture of organoids derived from multiple tissues within microfluidic arrays, thereby simulating intertissue and interorgan interactions that conventional organoids cannot capture. For example, a research group established a multi-organ model using a tri-culture system in which liver organoids were co-cultured with intestinal and gastric organoids. By allowing culture medium to circulate between separate chambers, they successfully reproduced physiological interorgan interactions and confirmed effective crosstalk among organoids [45]. This approach suggests that organoid-on-a-chip technology may evolve beyond single-organ models into next-generation cancer research platforms capable of reflecting complex multi-organ interactions.

By integrating the advantages of both organoid and organ-on-a-chip technologies, this approach is expected to further advance the development of personalized therapies. Although technical limitations remain, continued progress in this field is likely to make substantial contributions to cancer research.

AI and automation

Conventional organoid culture processes rely heavily on manual handling, which introduces variability between batches and among individual organoids. This variability represents a major obstacle to reproducibility and reliability. To address these limitations, recent efforts have focused on integrating artificial intelligence (AI) and automation technologies to improve the precision and consistency of organoid culture [46].

A platform was recently developed to reduce variability in organoid culture while enabling high-throughput automation and structural uniformity [47]. In this approach, a cell–Matrigel suspension is precisely dispensed into culture wells using a microfluidic droplet-printing system, thereby generating uniform cell–Matrigel spheroids. As a result, the organoids exhibit consistent size, morphology, and yield across wells. Furthermore, RNA sequencing analysis demonstrated that these organoids retained transcriptional similarity to the original tissue, particularly with respect to genes associated with organ function, tumor biology, and anticancer drug response. Such automated approaches enable high-throughput organoid culture in a cost- and time-efficient manner. In addition, by improving inter-organoid uniformity while preserving inter-patient heterogeneity, this strategy is expected to enhance the accuracy of patient-specific cancer diagnosis and therapeutic response prediction.

Three-dimensional image analysis remains challenging because of low signal-to-noise ratios, irregular cell morphologies, and substantial cell movement over time in live-cell imaging datasets. Moreover, conventional rule-based segmentation approaches typically depend on experiment-specific parameter tuning and manual correction, which can compromise reproducibility and scalability in large datasets. In this context, recent advances in AI have addressed important analytical limitations in complex 3D tumor organoid systems. For example, a deep learning-based 3D single-cell imaging platform enabled accurate segmentation of individual cells within tumor organoids, which is difficult to achieve reliably using conventional approaches. This platform further enabled automated single-cell phenotype classification and tracking in complex 3D microenvironments [48]. In addition, AI-based analytical approaches have enabled longitudinal evaluation of time-resolved imaging data from tumor organoid–immune cell co-culture systems, facilitating identification of drug- induced phenotypic alterations and dynamic therapeutic responses [49].

Tissueengineered cell scaffolds

Matrigel, currently the most widely used matrix for organoid culture, plays a pivotal role in supporting the growth of a broad range of organoids. However, because Matrigel is derived from animal sources, it is subject to batch-to-batch variability, which limits reproducibility [50]. To address these limitations, research has increasingly focused on tissue-engineered cell scaffolds that more accurately recapitulate the TME, optimize cell proliferation and attachment, and provide a stable framework for organoid formation. For example, a subset of colorectal cancer organoids was cultured in a gelatin-based synthetic hydrogel to assess survival, growth, metabolic activity, and drug sensitivity across multiple compounds. Organoids maintained in these synthetic matrices more closely mimicked the mechanical alterations associated with tumorigenesis than those grown in natural extracellular matrices. These observations suggest that tissue-engineered scaffolds may replicate the TME more effectively than conventional organoid culture systems, thereby enhancing the utility of organoids for drug response testing and disease modeling [51].

Recently, organoid culture strategies using decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) derived from native tissues have also received increasing attention. In a study using kidney dECM hydrogel for kidney organoid culture, the resulting organoids showed greater similarity to human kidney tissue than organoids cultured without dECM. Moreover, this kidney dECM-based culture system enabled the generation of highly vascularized organoids beyond the levels achieved with conventional protocols. These findings suggest that dECM, by recapitulating the composition of native extracellular matrix, serves as a biomaterial capable of providing a physiologically relevant microenvironment and thereby allowing organoids to more closely reflect the in vivo characteristics of human organs [52].

Conclusion

Organoid technology is rapidly evolving beyond a self- organizing 3D culture system into a robust preclinical and translational platform. Through integration with immune cell co-culture systems, organoid-on-a-chip platforms, AI-driven analytical frameworks, and engineered scaffolds, the field is progressively addressing challenges related to microenvironmental fidelity, scalability, and reproducibility. Although further refinement is required to fully capture the complexity of human tumors, ongoing technological innovation and clinical validation are strengthening the translational applicability of organoid platforms. Collectively, these advances position tumor organoids as powerful platforms for precision medicine and personalized cancer research (Fig. 2). By enabling patient-specific evaluation of therapeutic responses within physiologically relevant 3D systems, organoids serve as a critical bridge between experimental oncology and clinical application.
