Doege-Potter syndrome in a patient with solitary fibrous tumor of the retroperitoneum: a case report and literature review
Authors: Qais Baheen, Mingkun Tan, Bo Zhang, Tao Liu, Xueli Liu, Zhiqiang Ma, Bin Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDoege-Potter syndrome in solitary fibrous tumor of the retroperitoneum is rare. Here, we present a 45 years old Chinese male patient who was admitted to our center after discovering an abdominal mass for 5 years and intermittent episodes of hypoglycemia for 3 months. Imaging examinations confirmed a 19.2*12.3*22.9 cm mass in the abdominal cavity. Through multidisciplinary meeting and rapid preparations, the tumor was completely removed and patient’s hypoglycemia symptoms disappeared. Total operation time was 130 min, and total blood loss during surgery was 10 mL. Post operative pathology results confirmed solitary fibrous tumor. This case highlighted the importance of multidisciplinary meetings in quick and successful management of complex cases.Keywords: Doege-Potter syndrome, Solitary fibrous tumor, Retroperitoneum tumor

Introduction

Solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare mesenchymal neoplasms with an incidence of 2.8 per 100 000 individuals [1, 2]. A subset of SFT patients may develop hypoglycemia, a condition known as Doege-Porter syndrome (DPS). DPS is characterized by non-islet cell hypoglycemia resulting from tumor secretion of low molecular weight insulin-like growth factor-II (IGF-II). This was first explored by Doege in 1930, and it occurs in 5–10% of SFT patients [1]. While SFTs most frequently arise in the pleura, their occurrence in the retroperitoneum, especially when associated with DPS, is exceedingly rare [3], and only a limited number of confirmed cases were reported. This report presented a case of DPS caused by a retroperitoneal SFT, with the aim of contributing to the understanding and management of this rare condition, particularly in extra-pleural locations. The case highlighted the critical role of a multidisciplinary team (MDT) in the effective management of rare and complex clinical scenarios.

Patient’s clinical data

A 45-year-old Chinese patient was admitted to our center after discovering an abdominal mass for 5 years and intermittent episodes of hypoglycemia for 3 months. According to her family report, during regular physical examinations in 2019, the patient’s abdominal ultrasound examination displayed a uniform hypoechoic pattern in the upper right abdomen, closely related to the tail of the pancreas, measuring approximately 11.0 * 9.2 cm in size. Due to the absence of symptoms or discomfort, the patient did not receive treatment and follow-up. In June 2024, the patient occasionally experienced symptoms of hypoglycemia in midnight, such as restlessness, fatigue, and sweating. The symptoms were improved after consuming carbohydrate meals. Since mid-August 2024, the patient experienced hypoglycemic symptoms once a day and blurred consciousness in the morning. The self-measured blood glucose level was 1–2 mmol/L. Related laboratory examinations indicated a glycated hemoglobin level of 4.2%, a blood glucose level of 1.03 mmol/L, a potassium ion level of 3.42 mmol/L, and mild liver function abnormalities. No significant abnormalities were identified in tumor markers, thyroid function, cortisol, and infectious disease markers. The patient denied prior history of diabetes militias and has not received any medication. Abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan displayed an abnormality in the tail of the pancreas and a large anterior mass measuring 19.2 × 12.3 × 22.9 cm, suggestive of a benign tumor (Fig. 1c). Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the upper abdomen showed a huge mass in the abdomen, with features consistent with a benign lesion. Differential diagnoses included a gastric stromal tumor (exophytic type), a neuroendocrine tumor originating from the pancreas, or a mesenchymal tumor, including a solitary fibrous tumor or schwannoma (Fig. 1A–B). Given the diagnostic uncertainty, an MDT meeting was arranged.

MDT meeting

The clinical model implemented at our hospital is based on an MDT approach (Fig. 2). It is the first institution in China to fully adopt a comprehensive MDT model, which is structured into three distinct levels. Level 1 MDT is applied to all newly presenting patients and can be conducted in various settings, including physician offices, consultation rooms, or dedicated MDT conference spaces. The core participants are departmental physicians, with the option to include doctors from other specialties as needed. Level 2 MDT was designated for cases with diagnostic uncertainty or high complexity. These discussions require the involvement of at least three senior physicians and may also include external experts. Meetings were held in MDT conference rooms or conducted via ZOOM. Level 3 MDT addresses rare diseases or uncommon complications, requiring input from hospital-level leadership or internationally recognized experts. Participants typically include the Oncology Committee Board, specialists affiliated with the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC), and senior consultants or department heads from relevant fields. For this particular patient, a Level 2 MDT was convened, involving specialists from general surgery, gastroenterology, vascular surgery, radiology, oncology, and pathology. Given the tumor’s large size and its close proximity to the stomach and pancreas, the possibility of a gastric stromal tumor could not be excluded. The MDT recommended an endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy to make a definitive diagnosis. If a gastric stromal tumor was confirmed, neoadjuvant therapy was advised to reduce tumor size and simplify the subsequent surgical procedure.

FNA biopsy of the tumor under endoscopic

Endoscopic ultrasound examination revealed a huge mass located between the pancreatic tail and the gastric wall, which could not be fully visualized. The tumor exhibited ill-defined margins, low echogenicity, heterogeneous internal echoes, and scattered areas of patchy high echogenicity. Color Doppler imaging identified minimal blood flow in the lesion. After confirming the lesion through endoscopic ultrasound, the position of the ultrasound probe was adjusted to select a puncture path devoid of vascular structures. A 22G COOK needle was used to perform two passes, with approximately 30 strokes per pass. Adequate tissue samples were obtained, and no bleeding was found at the puncture site. The endoscope was thereafter withdrawn, and the collected tissue strips were sent for pathological examination.

Preoperative pathological data

Liquid-based cytology and tissue-embedded sections from the gastric body revealed a small amount of fragmented glandular epithelium, eosinophils, and few cells with nuclear atypia. Immunohistochemical analysis indicated CD117 negativity and partial CD34 positivity (Fig. 3), effectively ruling out a diagnosis of gastric stromal tumor. A Level 1 MDT was subsequently convened. The MDT recommended surgical resection of the tumor, along with percutaneous embolization of the tumor-feeding vessels on the day of surgery to minimize intraoperative bleeding.

Percutaneous tumor artery embolization

Abdominal angiography demonstrated normal branching patterns of the common hepatic artery, splenic artery, and left gastric artery. A pancreatic artery originating from the inferior wall of the abdominal aorta descended and curved horizontally to form the transverse pancreatic artery, which gave off multiple branches supplying a large retroperitoneal mass (Fig. 4A). Embolization was initiated with an appropriate amount of gelatin sponge particles until blood flow slowed, followed by deployment of Poco coils (6–6 mm, 7–10 mm). The main trunk of the transverse pancreatic artery (8–12 mm) was thereafter embolized. Post-embolization angiography confirmed complete occlusion, with no visible distal arterial branches (Fig. 4B). The patient’s vital signs remained stable during the embolization, and was transferred directly to the operating room for tumor resection.

Operation procedure

The surgery was performed under general anesthesia. An “L” - shaped incision was made 30 cm below the left rib margin. Exploration revealed a huge mass, measuring 25 * 20 *13 cm below the incision site, with clear boundaries, tough texture, no adhesion to the stomach, and mild activity. No ascites or metastatic nodules were found in the abdominal cavity, and no abnormalities were observed on the surface of the liver, peritoneum, and gastrointestinal tract. We removed most of the tumor out of the abdominal cavity, as the base of the tumor was closely related to the lower edge of the pancreas and the transverse mesentery. Along the boundary of the tumor, and lower margin of the pancreas, embolized blood supply vessels was disconnected. The local relationship between the tumor and the lower edge of the pancreas was relatively close, but the boundary was intact. We carefully dissociate and completely removed the tumor. The capsule at the lower edge of the pancreatic tumor was incomplete and a part of the transverse colon mesentery was missing. The lower edge of the pancreas and the transverse mesentery rupture was continuously sutured and closed. A drainage tube at the lower edge of the pancreas was placed.

Results and pathology

Total operation time was 130 min, and total blood loss during surgery was 10 mL. The resected tumor was 24*19*12 cm in size (Fig. 5). On the first day after operation, the hemoglobin level was 153 g/L. The patient’s blood glucose levels normalized, and he was grateful that he can sleep during the night without having to wake up for food. Postoperative CT scan showed no obvious free effusion in the abdominal cavity. Post operative amylase level of the drainage fluid was slightly high, indicating the presence of a local pancreatic fistula, but the patient did not experience fever or abdominal pain, as such no further measurement were taken. Postoperative pathology results confirmed isolated fibrous tumor, classic type (Fig. 6). Immunohistochemistry showed: CK (-), Vimentin (+), CD34 (+), Bcl-2 (+), CD99 (+), STAT6 (+), SMA (+), S-100 (-), SOX-10 (-), DOG1 (-), CD117 (-), NF (-), Ki67 (MIB1) (+ 2%), CD56 (-), CgA (-), Syn (scattered weak+).

Discussion

Solitary Fibrous Tumor (SFT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm originating from dendritic stromal cells expressing CD34 antigen, accounting for less than 2% of all soft tissue tumors [1]. Although the pleura is the most common site of origin (approximately 30%), SFTs can arise in virtually any anatomical location, including the meninges (27%), abdominal cavity (20%), trunk (10%), extremities (8%), and head and neck (5%) [4]. The peak incidence is in the sixth to seventh decades of life, with no significant sex predilection [5].

In some cases, the patients could be asymptomatic, and the symptoms may become more pronounced once the tumor grows large enough to secrete large amounts of insulin-like IGF-II, like this patient. Although the relationship between tumor diameter and hypoglycemia is not yet clear, some scholars believe that tumor diameter may be a factor in hypoglycemia in SFT patients. According to the study by England DM et al., large SFTs (i.e. those larger than 20 cm in size) or SFTs with high mitotic rates are often accompanied by reactive hypoglycemia [6]. Fukuda et al. found that 70% of the tumors in 78 DPS patients had a diameter greater than 10 cm [7]. In this case, as the tumor grew, the patient’s symptoms gradually manifested. Therefore, we agree with previous suggestions that the occurrence of hypoglycemia should be highly related to tumor diameter [5].

In addition to hypoglycemia, osteoarthropathy is the most common paraneoplastic symptom, which may result from excessive production of hyaluronic acid by tumor cells. Osteoarthropathy is more common in thoracic SFTs, and has occurred in 11 cases from 41 reported cases [5]. In two malignant cases, a decrease in potassium level was reported [8, 9]. This is consistent with the present case’s potassium level, making this the third documented instance of hypokalemia in DPS. This phenomenon may be due to the overproduction of IGF-II, which can lower serum potassium level. Although the exact mechanism remains elusive, it is hypothesized to involve IGF-II–induced cellular uptake of potassium [5]. Therefore, monitoring and correcting hypokalemia before surgery are essential to avoid perioperative complications.

In terms of treatment, complete tumor resection remains the best and most effective treatment option [10], as in our case. Once the tumor is removed, postoperative hypoglycemic symptoms can be completely relieved [11]. In cases where surgical resection of the tumor is not possible, non-surgical treatments such as neoadjuvant therapy, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and selective embolization of tumor blood vessels, have been reported to be effective. However, SFT is considered to have relatively high chemical resistance, and chemotherapy is used for metastatic or symptomatic unresectable SFT. Radiation therapy has some benefits, especially when used as an adjuvant therapy for surgery and chemotherapy.

In the present case, preoperative percutaneous embolization of the tumor’s main feeding artery was conducted to reduce intraoperative bleeding. Total intraoperative blood loss was approximately 10 mL. In addition to the tumor’s lack of adhesion to adjacent structures, such as the stomach and intestines, embolization could play a notable role in limiting blood loss, especially since only a single main feeding vessel was identified. Previous research described four cases, in which preoperative embolization was used successfully [12]. Based on this and previous evidence, embolization of the primary vascular supply is recommended to reduce surgical bleeding. Notably, the procedure should be scheduled on the day of surgery or one day in advance to limit tumor necrosis, prevent embolization-related complications, and facilitate early recognition and management of potential hemorrhage.

The role of MDT is also critical to timely and successful treatment [13]. Beijing Arion Cancer Center is the first hospital in China to adopt a fully integrated MDT model. In this case for instant, the MDT recommendations to preoperative pathological assessment to distinguish the tumor from a gastrointestinal stromal tumor, followed by preoperative embolization had significant contribution to the favorable clinical outcome. The present case also illustrates the importance of MDT involvement in the management of complex and rare clinical scenarios.

Several limitations are noteworthy in the present case report. Firstly, the scope of preoperative evaluation for diagnosing DPS was limited. Secondly, longitudinal data on tumor growth over time were insufficient. Thirdly, the follow-up time was relatively short. Nonetheless, this case is notable in several respects: the tumor demonstrated a five-year growth prior to the onset of hypoglycemic symptoms; this represents the third documented case of DPS-associated hypokalemia; and it is the fifth reported case to undergo preoperative percutaneous embolization. This case report may serve as a reference for the early diagnosis and management of SFT and associated paraneoplastic syndromes.

Conclusion

Doege-Potter syndrome presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. In this case, we reported a patient with retroperitoneal involvement, highlighting the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach for accurate diagnosis and management of complex cases. The successful management of this case underscores the importance of early detection and tailored treatment strategies to mitigate complications. This case also serves as a reminder of the complexities associated with rare syndromes and the importance of continued collaboration among clinicians, radiologists, and researchers to advance our understanding and management of such conditions.
