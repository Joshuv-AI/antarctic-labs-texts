Enhancing biliary protection via HOPE: Beyond bile composition to cholangiocyte resilience
Authors: Zhaorong Wu, Hongye Wang, Bo Zhai
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the randomized controlled trial by Schliephake et al.1 investigating the effects of hypothermic oxygenated machine perfusion (HOPE) on bile composition after liver transplantation. We commend the authors for their meticulous lipidomic and transcriptomic analysis, which provides robust evidence that HOPE significantly improves biliary lipid secretion and mitigates the intrahepatic accumulation of hydrophobic bile acids. While these findings mark a pivotal step in understanding machine perfusion’s benefits, we believe several nuances regarding the "biliary protection" mechanism warrant deeper discussion.

First, we note a striking discrepancy between the biochemical improvements and clinical outcomes; despite the enhanced lipid secretion, the incidence of biliary complications remained statistically similar between the HOPE and static cold storage (SCS) groups. In our view, this clinical observation indicates that the absolute concentration of biliary lipids alone is an insufficient surrogate for long-term biliary health. Indeed, seminal research has established that biliary protection depends more critically on the delicate balance between phospholipids and toxic bile salts than on lipid abundance alone, as evidenced by the aggravation of ischemic bile duct injury in models of phospholipid deficiency.2 While Schliephake et al. demonstrate a reduction in the "biliary toxicity index," the persistence of ischemic-type biliary lesions in clinical trials suggests that the resilience of the peribiliary vascular plexus and the regenerative capacity of cholangiocyte stem cells are equally critical factors. We believe it is important to consider whether a correlation exists between post-reperfusion bile composition and the structural integrity of the deep peribiliary glands.

Second, we note that the study identifies a significant downregulation of key transporters (ABCB4, ABCG8) immediately after reperfusion in SCS-preserved organs. We find this molecular "stunning" to be a critical driver of early graft injury.3 However, as clinicians, we must consider the temporal granularity of this recovery. Does HOPE act primarily by preventing the initial ATP depletion during ischemia, or does it actively "precondition" the hepatocyte’s transcriptional machinery to withstand the massive bolus of bile acids upon reperfusion? We believe that integrating a longitudinal analysis of post-transplant serum bile acid profiles with the biliary data would clarify whether HOPE’s benefit is a transient "jump-start" or a sustained metabolic reprogramming.

Third, the authors’ finding that hydrophobic bile acid levels correlate with AST/ALT peaks provides a compelling bridge between hepatocellular and biliary injury. We note, however, that the study cohort was relatively homogeneous. As we move toward an era of increased utilization of organs from "marginal" donors – such as those with significant steatosis or donation after circulatory death (DCD), which is associated with prolonged warm ischemia – the baseline bile acid pool is fundamentally more toxic.4 We believe that validating these findings in a high-risk DCD cohort is the essential next step. In such cases, the relative protective effect of HOPE might be even more pronounced, potentially serving as a "rescue" therapy rather than just an optimization strategy.

Finally, we commend the authors for their methodological sophistication. Their research moves the field beyond simple survival metrics toward a functional understanding of graft quality. To further advance this work, we propose: (1) investigating the synergy between HOPE and postoperative pharmacological agents like ursodeoxycholic acid, and (2) utilizing organ-on-a-chip models to test if HOPE-perfused bile directly reduces cholangiocyte apoptosis. Such refinements are essential before bile composition can be formally integrated as a predictive quality indicator in clinical liver transplantation.
