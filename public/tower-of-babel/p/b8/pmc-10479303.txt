An intrinsic polymer electrolyte via in situ cross-linked for solid lithium-based batteries with high performance
Authors: Chen Li, Ajuan Hu, Xinan Zhang, Hongbin Ni, Jingmin Fan, Ruming Yuan, Mingsen Zheng, Quanfeng Dong, Cristina Amon
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince the introduction of poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO)-based polymer electrolytes more than 50 years, few other real polymer electrolytes with commercial application have emerged. Due to the low ion conductivity at room temperature, the PEO-based electrolytes cannot meet the application requirements. Most of the polymer electrolytes reported in recent years are in fact colloidal/composite electrolytes with plasticizers and fillers, not genuine electrolytes. Herein, we designed and synthesized a cross-linked polymer with a three-dimensional (3D) mesh structure which can dissolve the Li bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) salt better than PEO due to its unique 3D structure and rich oxygen-containing chain segments, thus forming an intrinsic polymer electrolyte (IPE) with ionic conductivity of 0.49 mS cm−1 at room temperature. And it can hinder the migration of large anions (e.g. TFSI−) in the electrolyte and increase the energy barrier to their migration, achieving Li+ migration numbers (tLi+) of up to 0.85. At the same time, IPE has good compatibility with lithium metal cathode and LiFePO4 (LFP) cathode, with stable cycles of more than 2,000 and 700 h in Li//Li symmetric batteries at 0.2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2 current densities, respectively. In addition, the Li/IPE/LFP batteries show the capacity retention >90% after 300 cycles at 0.5 C current density. This polymer electrolyte will be a pragmatic way to achieve commercializing all-solid-state, lithium-based batteries.Keywords: polymer electrolytes, lithium salt dissociation, cross-linked polymer, solid solvent

Significance Statement

A true polymer electrolyte in which lithium salts can be dissociated by the polymer is an important way to achieve all-solid-state batteries. It has been a type of electrolyte based on poly(ethylene oxide) or its modified systems since 1973, which cannot meet the demands for applications due to the low ion conductivity. The currently widely reported polymer electrolytes are actually colloidal or hybrid electrolytes and should not be considered as true polymer electrolytes. In this work, we synthesized a cross-linked polymer electrolyte in which lithium salts can be dissolved without the addition of any plasticizer or filler, named intrinsic polymer electrolyte, with ionic conductivity up to 0.49 mS cm−1 at room temperature and up to 0.85 Li+ migration number and show excellent battery performance.

Introduction

Since its first discovery by Wright et al. in 1973, the interaction between poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) and alkali metal salts where the PEO can actually dissociate the salts to form solid-state ion-conducting polymers (1) has paved the way for the application of PEO-based polymer electrolytes in solid-state batteries. The excellent safety performance and processability of polymer electrolytes have attracted extensive attention from academia and industry, making them one of the potential materials to meet the requirements of next generation, high safety, high energy density, and low-cost energy storage systems (2–4).

However, because their low ion conductivity at room temperature, the PEO-based electrolytes have not been applied to practical systems. Subsequently, the widely reported so-called polymer electrolytes, such as based on polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) (5–7), polyethylene carbonate (8), polyvinyl carbonate (9, 10), polyethyleneimine (11), polyacrylonitrile (12, 13), polycaprolactone (14–17), etc., are actually colloidal or hybrid electrolytes; in other words, these polymers need to be compounded with other substances to achieve good lithium salt dissociation and application in batteries and therefore do not belong to true polymer electrolytes. In order to achieve fast ion transport at room temperature, many works are reported to add plasticizers (18–20), active fillers (21–25), grafting functional groups (26, 27), or doping with other substances (28–30) to improve electrochemical performance (31). However, this compounding method leads to the electrolyte being out of the “all-solid-state” category. Some other works add branched chains or doping to achieve the performance improvements, but Li+ conductivity at room temperature still cannot meet the current demand for solid-state electrolytes. One of the more widely reported approaches in recent years has been the use of in situ polymerization to achieve Li+ conductivity at room temperature close to that of liquid electrolytes (32–34); however, the polymerization product in most of these articles is actually a gel and not a pure solid-state electrolyte. Therefore, the true polymer electrolyte is still a goal that, despite great efforts, has not yet been achieved.

Here, a flexible polymeric solid-state electrolyte with high Li+ migration is designed for in situ polymerization, the linear poly(1,3-dioxolane) (PDOL) chains are linked together with pentaerythritol glycidyl ether (PGE) as the cross-linking center to create the poly-PGE-1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (PGD). The cross-linked polymer solid-state electrolyte with a three-dimensional (3D) mesh structure in which Li bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) salt can be dissociated better than PEO due to its unique 3D structure and rich oxygen-containing chain segments, thus named intrinsic polymer electrolyte (IPE). After thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) study, there is no liquid electrolyte DOL fully and the Li+ conductivity is up to 0.49 mS cm−1 at room temperature. Meanwhile, benefiting from the complex cross-linked network structure that hinders the migration of TFSI− large anions, whose migration rate in the electrolyte is slowed down, leading to the high Li+ migration number of 0.85. Long cycles of more than 2,000 and 700 h were achieved in lithium symmetric batteries at current densities of 0.2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2, respectively. The capacity retention >90% after 300 cycles at 0.5 C current density (low loading, ∼2 mg cm−2) and discharge capacity of 160 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C current density (commercially available high loading, about 8 mg cm−2) were achieved in Li//LiFePO4 (LFP) batteries. The design of a complex 3D cross-linked network structure has been proved to be an effective strategy to achieve high-performance and long-cycle all-solid-state, lithium-based batteries (ASSLBs).

Results

Synthesis and physical properties characterization of IPE

Figure 1A shows the synthesis route of IPE. To achieve the cross-linked structure, we used LiBF4 as the initiator and its decomposition product BF3 as Lewis acid to initiate the ring-opening polymerization of DOL to form PDOL. The result is a cross-linked polymer electrolyte with PGE as the cross-linked center and PDOL as the primary polymer. In the IPE matrix, the lithium salt is dissociated by the abundant ether–oxygen polar groups in the polymer PGD, where the green spheres are lithium ions and the pink ovals are anions (TFSI−). Benefited from the complex cross-linked structure of the IPE electrolyte, this reduces the free space between the polymer chain segments, which leads to blocked transport of larger radius anions (e.g. TFSI−) during charging and discharging of the battery, raising the transport energy barrier of such anions. In contrast, this change does not affect smaller radius Li+. To obtain the polymerization product, the IPE polymerization precursor was placed in an oven at 50°C for 3 h to get the solidified IPE electrolyte. The aggregation results are shown in Fig. S1. The optical photograph of Fig. 1B shows that IPE has good film formation, and the Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization results show that the surface of IPE film is flat without apparent bumps.

A) IPE polymerization reaction path. B) Optical picture and SEM images of IPE film formation. C) NMR spectral curves of DOL, PDOL, and PGD. D) FTIR spectra of electrolytes.

To analyze the structure of the above polymerization products, the results of NMR spectroscopy are shown in Fig. 1C. The NMR carbon spectra confirm the successful polymerization of PDOL and PGD, with new carbon peak shifts at 94.5 and 64.3 ppm for DOL and 95.2 and 66.9 ppm for PDOL (35). Unlike the above electrolytes, new carbon characteristic peaks at 67.2 and 95.9 ppm can be found in the graph of NMR results for PGD, which indicates the appearance of a new structure. Solid-state NMR of PGD characterization result also demonstrates that the PGD is all solid and contains no liquid or gel. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra provided further evidence of this, and the results from the 700–1,500 cm−1 wave number range tests are presented in Fig. 1D (the complete spectrum is available in Fig. S2). The vibrational peaks of the ternary monooxygen ring in IPE were not prominent around the 1,250 cm−1 wave number (36), indicating a complete reaction of the PGE monomer, while the symmetric and asymmetric vibrational peaks of the C–O bond represented near 1,040 and 1,090 cm−1, respectively, can be found in IPE; the C–C vibrational peaks of the long chain near 855 and 1,055 cm−1 appear as strong vibrational peaks in both PDOL and IPE (34). Combined with NMR and FTIR, we can prove that the polymerization results and structure of IPE are in accordance with the expected results in Fig. 1B.

It is well known that the thermal stability of electrolytes is crucial for lithium metal batteries. Figure 2A shows the TGA of the three electrolytes. From the differential curve of TGA, it can be seen that DOL shows a significant heat loss at 65°C, which is the reason for the transformation of DOL electrolyte from liquid to gas. Moreover, the thermal decomposition peak of PGD is at 118°C, which is a significant improvement compared with liquid electrolytes, and indicates that the thermal stability of IPE electrolytes has been further improved. In addition, IPE did not show mass loss near 65°C, which suggests that the liquid DOL in IPE has been thoroughly consumed in the polymerization reaction. In addition, by analyzing the differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) pattern, it can be seen that the heat absorption peak of DOL appears at 82°C, while there is no heat absorption peak of DOL in IPE. Therefore, we analyzed that the DOL liquid electrolyte in IPE has wholly participated in the copolymerization reaction. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) test results of PGD in Fig. S3 show that a broad peak pattern at 22° appears in PGD, further revealing the typical amorphous characteristics of PGD.

High ionic conductivity, wide electrochemical window, and high lithium–ion mobility number are key factors to improve the applicability of electrolytes. We first performed variable temperature impedance tests, and the Nyquist plots of the three electrolytes at different temperatures are shown in Fig. S4. We found that the lithium–ion conductivity of IPE was as high as 0.49 mS cm−1 at room temperature, while that of PDOL was 0.1 mS cm−1. The temperature dependence curves of lithium–ion conductivity for in situ polymerized electrolytes are shown in Fig. 2C. We found that these curves can match well with the Arrhenius model. The ion-transport activation energy of IPE (∼0.17 eV) is calculated to be lower than that of PDOL (∼0.46 eV) based on the slope of the curve (37–39), which indicates that lithium–ion migration is faster in IPE and can be promoted. The electrochemical window is a necessary factor in determining the stability of the electrolyte inside the battery. The linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) curve shown in Fig. 2D reveals a significant enhancement of IPE (∼4.2 V) over DOL (∼3.9 V) and PDOL (∼4.0 V), which indicates that the electrolyte after cross-linking polymerization occurs can effectively broaden the electrochemical stability window.

t Li+ is a crucial parameter for the electrolyte, which can affect the concentration polarization of Li+ on the electrode surface during charging and discharging. High tLi+ can effectively inhibit the growth of lithium dendrites, providing a long-life lithium metal. By applying a constant 10 mV interference voltage to the DC polarization test of the three electrolytes, it can be found (Fig. 2E) that IPE exhibits a high Li+ migration number of 0.83 (8, 40, 41), which is much higher than DOL (∼0.63) and PDOL (∼0.69) (Fig. S5), which may be due to the fact that IPE promotes Li+ migration while slowing down large anion migration by its complex network structure migration, which is consistent with the results we inferred earlier. We used Raman spectroscopy to analyze the anion and cation interactions in the electrolyte to further analyze the differences in the solvated structure of the three electrolytes mentioned above. According to previous literature, the anion and cation clusters can be divided into free anion (FA), loose ion pair (LIP), and intimate ion pair (IIP) based on the distances of Li+ and TSFI− (42–45). For this reason, we tested the three electrolytes as shown in Fig. 2F, and we can clearly find that the intensities of FA, LIP, and IIP are very different among the PDG, DOL, and PDOL, implying the different dissociated state of Li salts. As a solid polymer solvent, PGD dissociates lithium salts in a chain segment coupling manner in which polar oxygen with lone pair electrons acts as a ligand.

Electrochemical properties of Li//Li symmetric batteries and characterization

To verify the performance of solid-state electrolytes in the electrochemical system, we assembled the above IPE electrolyte for a button battery. First, in the lithium symmetric battery test in Fig. 3A, the IPE maintained stable cycling for more than 700 h at current density and deposition of 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2, respectively. In comparison, battery failure occurred after 300 and 450 h for DOL and PDOL, respectively. Meanwhile, in the low current density 0.2 mA cm−2 test, the IPE could still maintain more than 2,000 h without short circuit (Fig. S6). In addition, in Fig. 3B, the current density was gradually increased from 0.05 to 2 mA cm−2 in the lithium symmetric battery rate performance test, with lithium deposition of 4 mAh cm−2. We can see from the partial magnification of Fig. 3B that the polarization voltage of DOL was the smallest in the initial rate testing period. However, when the rate performance test was completed and the long cycle test was conducted at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 at the end of the rate test, it was found that the overpotential of DOL became dramatically large and the battery failed rapidly after 500 h. PDOL showed an unstable voltage curve at the rate test stage. At the same time, IPE maintained a stable overpotential after the test mentioned above steps without any dramatic change in voltage, which proved the excellent battery compatibility of IPE with the lithium cathode, which induces uniform lithium deposition and stripping.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is a classical characterization method that does not affect the internal composition and structure of the battery in situ, so to explore the relationship between overpotential and impedance, we studied and analyzed the impedance variation of the symmetric battery for different number of cycles, as shown in Fig. 3C and D. This clearly indicated that the interfacial impedance (Rinterface) of these three different electrolytes reaches a relatively stable state after 50 cycles, while the impedance value of PDOL is the largest at about 45 Ω, followed by DOL at 27 Ω, and IPE the smallest at 15 Ω, which indicates that IPE facilitates lithium deposition and stripping at the interfacial transport during the cycling process. To compare the above phenomena more visually, the interface impedance values for three different cycle counts are shown in the stacked histogram in Fig. S7. The fitted circuit for AC impedance analysis is shown in Fig. S8. Subsequently, we disassembled the above three batteries and characterized the surface morphology of the lithium flakes in them, as shown in Fig. 3F–H. Again, the SEM images reveal that the surfaces of the DOL and PDOL lithium batteries are filled with a large number of filamentary lithium dendrites after 10, 20, and 50 cycles, respectively, while the surface lithium deposition of the IPE lithium batteries is denser and no dendrites appeared. As shown in Figs. 3F–H and S9, the same optical photograph in the upper left corner reveals that the lithium sheet of IPE is brighter and flatter than that of DOL and PDOL. In the Li//Cu half-battery test, 2 mAh cm−2 of lithium metal was deposited on the copper foil at a low current density of 0.2 mA cm−2, uneven deposition of DOL and PDOL could still be found. In contrast, IPE deposited more densely on the copper foil without lithium dendrites, as shown in Fig. S10. The nucleation overpotential of IPE was only 12.4 mV, much smaller than that of 17.3 mV for DOL and 37.2 mV for PDOL (Fig. S11), it indicates that IPE electrolytes are more favorable for lithium metal deposition.

The analysis of the composition of the SEI on the lithium surface using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) can help us further understand the differences in the interfacial composition of the three cells mentioned above. Figure 4A–C shows the XPS spectra of the lithium surface of the symmetric Li//Li battery after 20 cycles at 0.2 mA cm−2. In Fig. 4A, C 1 s spectrum contains C–C, C–O, C=O, and CO32− peaks corresponding to LiC, ROCO2Li, RCOOLi, and Li2CO3, respectively. Organic layers consisting of ROCO2Li and RCOOLi have good flexibility, which can provide some flexibility for SEI, which can mitigate the volume changes during lithium deposition. However, for DOL and PDOL, both C–O and CO32− are significantly higher than IPE, which indicates that the instability of DOL and PDOL itself leads to a continuous interfacial side reaction between the electrolyte and the lithium metal cathode, and more Li2CO3 in the SEI layer will continue to react with the electrolyte, resulting in a rough surface and poorer cycling performance (33, 46). Since the F sources in PDOL and IPE electrolytes are derived from TFSI− and BF4−, the F 1 s spectra in Fig. 4B correspond to the B–F bond at 687.6 eV and the C–F bond at 688.8 eV, respectively. The migration of the large anion TFSI− in the IPE is hindered by the cross-linked polymer structure, resulting in the discovery of a slightly weaker spectral peak at 684.3 eV for the LiF of the IPE than for the PDOL (46, 47), while DOL showed the weakest LiF signal intensity, which may be due to the reduction of the F source caused by the non-addition of LiBF4 to DOL and the violent side reaction between DOL and lithium metal, resulting in the weakened TFSI− decomposition, which is consistent with the weakest Li3N peak signal shown by DOL in N 1 s. Although LiF is an important component in SEI to improve the modulus of SEI, excessive LiF leads to high interfacial impedance (because of the low intrinsic ionic conductivity of LiF), while small amounts of LiF affect the modulus of the SEI layer. The amount of LiF in IPE is between DOL and PDOL, and moderate amounts of LiF and organic components can help the SEI interfacial layer of IPE to maintain certain strength and elasticity, and better adapt to the volume change of lithium metal during deposition and exfoliation. In the N 1 s spectrum, the peak at 400.2 eV is attributed to -NSO2CF3 from LiTFSI. In addition, the peaks at 403.8 and 406.8 eV correspond to NO2− and NO3−, and the peak at 399.3 eV verifies the presence of Li3N (Fig. 4C), both from LiNO3 (46, 48). As expected, for the long cycle after IPE, the intensity of Li3N is significantly higher than that of DOL and PDOL. This indicates that the negative SEI after IPE cycling contains a large amount of Li3N, the stable Li//IPE interface promotes the effective decomposition of LiNO3 on the negative surface, and this inorganic component can improve the mechanical strength of SEI. Meanwhile, Li3N is a special superionic conductor with ionic conductivity of 10−3 S cm−2 at room temperature, which can rapidly transport lithium ions for highly reversible lithium plating and stripping. In contrast, DOL and PDOL exhibit slightly weaker signal strength of Li3N due to their own side reactions with lithium metal, resulting in a higher impedance organic-rich interface that forms a passivation layer that hinders the NO3− reduction reaction. Thus, the Li3N-rich SEI of IPE is more favorable for the rapid passage of lithium ions, which is verified by the impedance fitting data in Fig. S7, where IPE has the smallest interfacial impedance.

Electrochemical performance of Li//LFP batteries

The cross-linked polymer–polymer electrolyte IPE has good compatibility with Li metal and LiFePO4, and we have tested the performance of Li//LFP half-cells with the above three electrolytes. First, we tested the long cycle test at normal loading (2 mg cm−2). We found that after about 300 cycles at 0.5 C current density at normal loading (Fig. 5A), the average capacity of the cell with IPE as electrolyte was 135 mAh g−1, and the capacity retention was 92% with average coulombic efficiency of 99.8%. To further compare the differences between IPE and other electrolytes, the voltage curves at different cycle times (Fig. S12A–C), we can find that the charge/discharge overpotential is only 93 mV and remains almost constant during the long cycle, indicating that the battery remains relatively stable internally. However, the capacity decay and charge/discharge overpotential of DOL and PDOL are larger, with overpotentials of 103 and 178 mV, respectively. In addition, even at a current density of 1 C (Fig. S13A), IPE still maintains a fairly stable discharge capacity and a Coulomb efficiency of more than 99%. At the same time, DOL is extremely unstable and has a very low Coulomb efficiency, which indicates a severe overcharge phenomenon, i.e. DOL is unstable in this system. As shown in Fig. 5C, we can visualize the excellent rate performance of IPE, and the corresponding voltage curves of IPE at different multipliers can show its stability (Fig. 5D). To further illustrate the practicality of IPE, we also used a commercial high-loading LFP cathode sheet (average surface loading of 8 mg cm−2, labeled HL-LFP). The maximum discharge capacity of IPE was up to 160 mAh g−1 after several cycles of activation in the initial test at a current density above and below 0.2 C. The capacity retention rate was greater than 80% after 100 cycles (Fig. 5B), which is a significant improvement over DOL and PDOL. The IPE still maintains the best discharge capacity and capacity retention rate at a current density of 0.1 C (Fig. S13B). In addition, we can see from Fig. S14 that the IPE still maintains the best rate performance for high-loading positive electrodes. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were performed to analyze the electrochemical stability of the in situ polymerized electrolyte (i.e. Li//LFP) in natural battery systems, so we performed CV tests for the three systems, as shown in Fig. 5F. Compared with the unstable DOL and PDOL (Fig. S15A–B), IPE has a stable voltage profile during the first five charge/discharge cycles, with a first turn charge/discharge overpotential was only 0.37 V (e.g. Fig. S15C), which indicated that the side reactions continued to occur at the interface of DOL and PDOL, greatly increasing the interface impedance.

EIS is an effective and efficient method for characterizing various interfacial information inside the in situ reaction cell. We performed in situ electrochemical impedance spectra of the first charge and discharge at different potentials during the charge and discharge of the Li//LFP half-cells assembled with the above three electrolytes. The test method is to first charge to 3.8 V from the open circuit potential and finally discharge to 2.5 V, during which the impedance test is performed every 0.5 h. The frequency of the impedance test is 10 mHz to 1 MHz, and the charge/discharge current density is 0.1 C. The voltage versus time curve during the test is shown in Fig. S16, and the in situ AC impedance test results are shown in Fig. 4H (DOL and PDOL test results showed in Fig. S17A and B, respectively). Comparing the EIS profiles of the three electrolytes, it is easy to find that the overall impedance of IPE remains in dynamic equilibrium within a certain interval, while the overall impedance values of DOL and PDOL keep increasing, which may be due to the gradual failure of the positive and negative interfaces. In order to compare the in situ AC impedance data more intuitively, we further analyzed the above impedance data, and the fitting results are shown in Figs. 5G and S18. It is clear from the comparison that the total internal impedance of IPE has the smallest value and the smallest change, from 190 Ω at the beginning to 208 Ω at the end of the first charge/discharge. The total internal impedance of DOL and PDOL, which are used as the comparison, respectively DOL and PDOL as a comparison, their total internal impedance values increased from 263 to 502 Ω and 708.7 to 915.6 Ω, respectively. The specific fitted detailed values are shown in Table S1. The analysis and comparison reveal that the change in DOL has the largest value, which indicates that the internal liquid electrolyte DOL of the battery containing DOL has the most serious side reaction with the positive and negative active substances at the interface, causing the fastest battery capacity loss, which is consistent with the fastest capacity decay of DOL in the full battery in Fig. 5A and B.

Finally, we disassembled the whole batteries after five cycles as described above and observed the surface morphology of the lithium metal anode and LFP cathode by scanning electron microscopy, as shown in Figs. 5H and S19, compared with the uncycled lithium and LFP electrodes. Side, the LFP particles of DOL and PDOL showed obvious agglomeration, which may lead to the poor conductivity of the LFP, and the conductive agent could not contact each other uniformly, resulting in capacity loss. While in IPE, the lithium metal surface is smooth and flat, which indicates the uniform deposition and stripping of Li+ in this system; meanwhile, observing this cathode surface, it can be seen that the surface of the cathode electrode sheet is still well-defined particles and does not show the agglomeration phenomenon in front, which is closest to the surface morphology of the uncycled cathode electrode sheet, which can make the cathode active material have a higher specific surface area and contact with the conductive agent and binder inside the cathode electrode sheet more The capacity decay is suppressed.

The SEM cross-sectional view of the LFP electrode sheet explains the above performance difference, as shown in Fig. S19. We have performed an EDX surface scan elemental analysis of the commercial LFP electrode sheet after cycling, as shown in Fig. S20 (DOL and PDOL correspond to Figs. S21 and S22, respectively). We analyze that this is the precursor of IPE embedded inside the pores of the LFP pole piece, and in situ polymerization occurs in it. Therefore, we believe that the IPE solid electrolyte with certain elasticity and toughness plays a role in alleviating the contact failure between the active material and the conductive agent and binder caused by the volume change of the active material during the charging and discharging process, alleviating the mutual agglomeration between the materials, maintaining the internal structure of the electrode sheet in the same state as the original structure to the greatest extent, and reducing the capacity failure caused by the poorly conductive LFP active material. The solid electrolyte with high room temperature ionic conductivity and elasticity can facilitate fast Li+ transport inside the electrode sheet. These improvements are reflected in the smaller charge/discharge polarization voltage and increased capacity retention of the active material.

Discussion

In conclusion, we report a new cross-linked polymer electrolyte using PGE and DOL as polymerization monomers and in situ polymerization under the action of LiBF4 as an initiator to form IPE, in which lithium salts can be dissociated via polar oxygen with lone pair electrons on polymer segments as a ligand. The unique cross-linked structure of IPE hinders the migration of large anions TFSI− inside the electrolyte and improves the migration energy barrier of TFSI−, obtaining a tLi+ of up to 0.85 and 0.49 mS cm−1 of Li+ conductivity at room temperature, which greatly reduces the concentration polarization of Li ions at high current densities, promotes uniform deposition stripping of Li metal, and inhibits the growth of Li dendrites. The characterization results of scanning electron microscopy in a Li//Li symmetric cell with a current density of 0.5 mAh cm−2 at a stable cycle of 700 h show that the lithium sheet surface always maintains a uniform and dense lithium deposition morphology after different times of cycles. In addition, in the Li//LFP half-cell, the IPE was stably cycled for 300 cycles at a current density of 0.5 C, with an average discharge capacity of 135 mAh g−1 and a capacity retention rate of >90%, and the Li//LFP half-cell showed excellent rate performance by maintaining a discharge capacity of 90 mAh g−1 at 2 C. Further characterization revealed that the surface active material particles were evenly dispersed after cycling and did not show the agglomeration phenomenon in the comparison experiment. It is also found that the IPE infiltrates the positive electrode's pores and “confines” the material inside the electrode to a certain area, thus buffering the volume change of the LFP during charging and discharging and maintaining the shape of the inner electrode. Therefore, IPE as an all-solid polymer electrolyte is expected to be one of the functional materials for next-generation high-energy-density ASSLBs.

Materials and methods

Preparation of materials

LFP (Kejing Co., Ltd) and Super P (Sigma-Aldrich Inc.) were dried in the oven at 120°C for 12 h before use. PVDF (5130) binder was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Inc. Lithium foil was purchased from Tianjin EV Energies Co., Ltd. and transferred to the glove box for further use. PGE was purchased from Macklin Inc. DOL, LiBF4, and LiTFSI were from DoDoChem and stored in the glove box.

Preparation of in situ polymerization electrolyte and battery assembly

First, PGE and DOL were mixed in a molar ratio of 1:50 and homogeneously mixed in a magnetic stirrer. Then, LiTFSI, LiNO3, and LiBF4 were added to the above solution successively and stirred uniformly until a clear and transparent solution was achieved. Finally, after obtaining the above homogeneous polymerization precursors, they were placed in an oven at 50°C for 3 h to obtain poly(pentaerythritol glycidyl ether-1,3-dioxolane), labeled as IPE.

The LFP positive electrode sheet consisted of 80 wt.% LFP, 10% wt.% Super P, and 10 wt.% PVDF, obtained by placing the above three materials in an appropriate amount of N-methylpyrrolidone solvent and placing them in a planetary ball mill for 6 h after ball milling, and scraping them using a scraper, to obtain a positive electrode sheet with an active substance loading of about 2.5 mg cm−2. In addition, we used a commercially available high-loading LFP, specifically labeled HL-LFP, with a surface loading of 8 mg cm−2.

We used CR2032-type button batteries to assemble the batteries and performed electrochemical tests. We injected 50 μL of the polymeric precursor into the buckle battery, and the PP diaphragm was used as a septum to prevent the battery from short-circuiting. The assembled battery was placed in an oven at 50°C for 3 h, and then it was ready for subsequent electrochemical testing.

Characterization method

NMR (Bruker 400 MHz Avance III SS-NMR and Ascend 600 MHz) spectra were used to characterize the polymerization results and structure of the electrolyte. TGA was performed using a NETZSCH synchronous thermal analyzer, model 449F5, to analyze the thermal stability of three electrolytes at temperatures ranging from 25 to 800°C with a temperature control rate of 10°C min−1 and the DSC (NETZSCH's DSC214) was used to analyze the thermal properties of the polymers. FTIR spectrometers (Nicolet iS50) were used to characterize polymer electrolytes’ polymerization results and structure. SEM (HITACHI S-4800) was used to observe the surface morphology. XRD (SmartLab-SE) was used to characterize the crystal structure of the polymer products. Elemental analysis and composition of the surface of the lithium metal after cycling were characterized by XPS (Thermo Scientific; ESCALAB Xi+).
