Transcriptomic analysis of Rnq1 loss and prionization reveals alterations in translation pathways and energy metabolism
Authors: Zhiqiang Du, Milad J. Alasady, Marc L. Mendillo, Liming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prion proteins can undergo self-perpetuating conformational changes that alter their functions. One such protein, Rnq1, whose prion form acts as a master regulator of several other prions’ formation and transmission, is unknown for its physiological functions. Here, we investigated how Rnq1 loss and prionization affect the yeast transcriptomic profile. We found that both the Rnq1 loss and prionization exert similar global effects on the transcriptome, with more transcripts upregulated than downregulated. Notably, the upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were predominantly enriched for translation-related gene ontology (GO) terms, largely due to the overrepresentation of snoRNAs and tRNAs. In contrast, the downregulated DEGs were primarily associated with energy production, especially in mitochondria. Interestingly, the Rnq1 prion mainly exhibited amplified loss-of-function effects. Further analyses suggest that, as a low-complexity protein, Rnq1 has evolved to interact with multiple cellular partners to maintain physiological balance, particularly between protein synthesis and energy production. The loss and prionization of Rnq1 may disrupt these natural interactions, thereby triggering feedback regulations and/or perturbing RNA processing through crosstalk among transcription factors, RNA polymerases, and other cellular machinery. Together, our results provide new insights into Rnq1 functions and prion-mediated cellular behaviors with broader implications of prion-based phenomena across biological systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46386-0.

Introduction

Prion proteins possess unique properties that enable them to switch from normal physiological conformations to altered, self-perpetuating prion conformations1,2. Prions have been identified in diverse organisms, from bacteria to mammals, and are involved in a broad spectrum of biological processes3–8. In the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, multiple prions have been identified and are transmitted as altered protein conformations8–10. Owing to its simplicity and amenability to genetic manipulation, yeast serves as a critical model organism for studying prion biology. Research using yeast has provided key conceptual insights into the structural basis of prions11–14, the molecular mechanisms underlying prion formation and transmission9,10, the emergence of prion variants, and the interactions between prions and cellular machinery15,16. Yeast systems have also been used to identify risk factors for neurodegenerative diseases17–19 and to discover small anti-prion molecules20–22.

Rnq1 (rich in asparagine (N) and glutamine (Q)) is an intrinsically disordered protein containing a transferable prion domain (PrD) of approximately 250 amino acids at its C-terminus23,24. Its prion form ([RNQ+]) promotes the de novo formation of another prion, [PSI+], and is therefore also known as [PIN+] ([PSI+] inducibility)23,25. It has been shown that [RNQ+] can interact with multiple yeast prions and several aggregation-prone proteins from non-yeast organisms, functioning as a general facilitator of protein aggregation and prion formation. Indeed, [RNQ+] has been demonstrated to promote the initiation of other yeast prions, influence their transmission, and enhance the aggregation of human disease-associated proteins25–29. Despite its importance as a model for studying protein aggregation, the biological function of Rnq1 beyond prion biology remains unclear. According to the Saccharomyces Genome Database (SGD), Rnq1 is a highly abundant protein containing at least three potential ubiquitination sites and five possible phosphorylation sites. A ChIP assay has shown that Tfc7, a transcription factor (TF), binds to the RNQ1 promoter30, suggesting that Rnq1 may be regulated at both transcriptional and post-translational levels. Therefore, Rnq1 may possess bona fide physiological functions beyond its role in prion biology. Supporting this notion, one study found that lipid profiles are distinct among strains with different Rnq1 states using HPTLC-densitometry31. Another study reported that deletion of the RNQ1 open reading frame (ORF) results in eight-spore asci, indicative of a defect in cell division during meiosis32. However, this phenotype may not directly reflect Rnq1 function. As the deletion also reduces the expression of Bik133, a protein required for spindle pole body (SPB) separation34, the observed eight-spore asci phenotype is more likely linked to Bik1 deficiency. Furthermore, the physiological role of Rnq1 has also been implicated in proteostasis and other cellular pathways through its interactions with diverse proteins, particularly molecular chaperones35–38. Similar to the PrD of Sup35 ([PSI+] prion determinant), the Rnq1 PrD also exhibits signs of purifying selection across Saccharomyces species39. Collectively, these findings suggest that Rnq1 may play biological roles beyond prion biology, which remains unelucidated.

In this study, we generated a set of isogenic yeast strains – Rnq1-deprived, prionized, and wild-type non-prion, all of which had intact Bik expression but differed in their Rnq1 state. Using RNA-seq, we analyzed how Rnq1 deprivation and prionization modulate the yeast transcriptome. The underlying RNA-modifying mechanisms and the physiological roles of Rnq1 were further discussed.

Results and discussion

Strain construction

Mutant strains with the deletion of RNQ1-ORF have been commonly used in Rnq1 studies32,35,40,41. However, some of the findings may be confounded as the deletion can reduce the expression of BIK1, a neighboring gene of RNQ133. To generate an Rnq1-null mutant (RM) strain without affecting BIK1 expression, we started from an [RNQ+] prion (RP) strain and introduced a point mutation via site-directed mutagenesis. To obtain a wild-type non-prion ([rnq-], hereafter WT) strain, the RP strain was treated with millimolar concentrations of guanidine hydrochloride (GdnHCl), which, at these low levels, can eliminate prions by selectively inhibiting the molecular chaperone Hsp104, an essential factor for propagating many yeast prions, including [RNQ+]23,25 (Figure 1b). Specifically, to create the RM strain, we used a combination of pop-in/pop-out manipulation12,42, PCR and DNA sequencing to convert the RNQ1 start codon from ATG to ATA. This point mutation was expected to prevent translation of the RNQ1 mRNA. In [RNQ+] cells, the loss of Rnq1 would remove the substrate required for prion propagation, thereby abolishing [RNQ+]. Indeed, the confirmed WT pop-out isolates but not the RM pop-out isolates retained the [RNQ+] prion as evidenced by Rnq1-GFP aggregation assays (Figure 1c).

To determine whether this mutation affected BIK1 expression, BIK1 was tagged with 3xFLAG, and analyzed by immunoblotting. As shown in Figure 1d and Figure S1a, Rnq1 was detectable in WT and RP strains but absent in the RM strain, while Bik1 levels were comparable across all three tested strains. These results indicate that Rnq1 was successfully eliminated without reducing Bik1 production. Additionally, all three strains were confirmed to be flocculation-positive (data not shown), consistent with successful FLO8 repair in the parental strain43, which was required to expose flocculin-related gene expression. Altogether, these results demonstrate that the three isogenic strains - WT, RP, and RM were successfully created as an ideal system to study Rnq1 functions.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

With the new strains, we performed transcriptomic analysis using rRNA-depleted RNA-seq to examine how Rnq1 deprivation and prionization influence RNA homeostasis. For DEG calling, we applied a combined cutoff of fold change (FC) > 2 and adjusted P (adj.P) < 0.001 (see sequencing datasets in Tables S1-S3). Under this stringent criterion, approximately one-sixth of yeast transcripts were significantly altered in both RP and RM cells, indicating that they exert similar effects on global transcriptomes.

Interestingly, both RM and RP strains displayed more upregulated than downregulated DEGs relative to WT, with a substantial number of DEGs shared between the two strains (Figures 2a-c). This pattern suggests that Rnq1 may confer an overall gene-repressing activity under normal conditions. The similarity between Rnq1 depletion and prionization was further supported by the small number of DEGs (two upregulated and eleven downregulated) identified when RP and RM were compared directly (Figure 2b, right). Gene ontology (GO) analysis using Metascape44 revealed that the eleven RP-downregulated DEGs were enriched in plasma membrane components. Notably, eight of these genes were altered in the same direction in both RM and RP cells relative to WT. Nevertheless, differences were also observed between the RM and RP strains. Even though 65% of upregulated and 44% of downregulated RP-DEGs were shared with RM, both RM and RP strains also exhibited subsets of unique DEGs, with RP showing more unique DEGs than RM (Figure 2c). Although prionization is generally associated with loss-of-function phenotypes due to inactivation of the prion protein through aggregation45, our results suggest that [RNQ+] also induces transcriptomic changes distinct from those caused by the lack of Rnq1.

To validate the RNA-seq data, several representative DEGs were examined by semi-quantitative RT-PCR. The results were consistent with the regulatory patterns observed in the RNA-seq analysis (Figure 2d and Figure S1b), confirming the reliability of our sequencing data. Importantly, BIK1 transcript levels were unchanged in both RP and RM strains (Figure 2e), consistent with the immunoblotting results shown in Figure 1d. In contrast, the steady-state RNQ1 mRNA level was markedly reduced in the RM strain but not in the RP strain, indicating that the point mutation, rather than Rnq1 prionization, affects RNQ1 mRNA stability. In this regard, the untranslatable RNQ1 transcript in the RM strain is likely degraded through an mRNA turnover pathway46.

Key features and functional impacts of DEGs

We next examined the characteristics of DEGs using YeastMine47. Several significant findings are summarized in Figure 3a-c. DEGs from both RM and RP cells were distributed across all chromosomes (Figure 3a). A general prevalence of upregulated DEGs was observed, with RP exhibiting more DEGs than RM across all chromosomes. Interestingly, unlike the nuclear chromosomes, where upregulated genes predominated, the mitochondrial chromosome (Chrmt) showed mainly downregulated DEGs in both RM and RP cells, suggesting an overall reduction in mitochondrial activity. Analysis of gene features revealed that protein-coding genes were markedly underrepresented among upregulated DEGs but slightly overrepresented among downregulated DEGs in both RM and RP strains, relative to the genomic prevalence of ORFs (Figure 3b). Notably, these ORF DEGs were enriched for uncharacterized ORFs, especially among the upregulated genes (Figure 3b), suggesting that the Rnq1-associated pathways and functions remain largely unexplored. In contrast, transcripts encoding tRNAs and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) were highly overrepresented among upregulated, but not downregulated DEGs in both cell types (Figure 3c). Indeed, approximately 35 – 40% of total tRNA and snoRNA transcripts in RM cells and 60 – 65% in RP cells were identified as DEGs (Figure 3d), indicating that enhanced expression of tRNAs and snoRNAs is a prominent feature of both Rnq1 loss and prionization.

Based on these transcriptomic signatures, we hypothesized that protein synthesis might be elevated in RM and RP cells. Consistent with this prediction, total protein quantification assays revealed that both RP and RM cells exhibited higher protein levels than WT cells do after normalization (Figure 3e). Collectively, these results indicate that Rnq1 loss and prionization enhance RNA and protein production while suppressing mitochondrial gene expression, suggesting a shift in cellular resource allocation.

Pathways influenced by Rnq1 loss and prionization

To identify the major cellular pathways and functions affected by Rnq1 loss and prionization, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis using a cutoff of P < 0.001. We first examined enriched cellular component (CC) terms (Figure 4a). Six DEG subgroups – commonly or uniquely regulated in RM and RP cells (see the Figure 4a legend) were analyzed. DEGs downregulated in both RM and RP (↓both) were significantly enriched in the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV, suggesting suppression of respiratory activities. In contrast, DEGs upregulated in both strains (↑both) were enriched in the box H/ACA snoRNP complex, the box C/D/methylation guide snoRNP complex, and the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex. Notably, the latter two CCs were also enriched among DEGs uniquely upregulated in RP (↑RPu), for which additional enrichment of the preribosome term was identified. These CCs associated with the ↑both subgroup relate to rRNA modification, maturation, ribosome assembly, and protein translation48,49, suggesting enhanced protein production. Beyond these major trends, modest enrichments were detected in other DEG subgroups. For instance, DEGs uniquely downregulated in RP (↓RPu) were enriched in the glucose-induced degradation (GID) complex, which contains ubiquitin ligases that target enzymes such as fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase for proteasomal degradation, thereby modulating carbohydrate metabolism50. Furthermore, because Metascape excludes tRNAs from CC analysis but includes them in biological process (BP) and molecular function (MF) analyses by default, we also performed BP and MF analyses (Figures 4b and c). Overall, the results obtained were broadly consistent with those from the CC analysis. For example, MF analysis revealed enrichment of heme-based cytochrome c oxidase activity in mitochondria, and BP analysis highlighted enrichment in mitochondrial electron transport for the ↓both DEGs. For the ↑both DEGs, enriched terms included box C/D snoRNPs (rRNA methylation), box H/ACA snoRNPs (rRNA pseudouridylation)48, RNP/ribosome binding, and tRNA-mediated codon–amino acid adaptor activity. The ↑RPu DEGs shared several enriched terms with the ↑both DEGs, such as rRNA modification and translation elongation – but also exhibited unique enrichments in RNA polymerase activity, maturation of the ribosomal SSU and large subunit (LSU), and tRNA methylation. Additionally, we identified several less pronounced enrichments, including ethanol and arginine metabolism, nitrogen utilization, and protein histidyl modification among the ↑both DEGs, as well as carbohydrate phosphatase activity associated with trehalose anabolism among the ↓both DEGs. Together, these results indicate that the predominant effects of Rnq1 loss and prionization are the promotion of translation and the repression of respiratory activities in mitochondria.

Next, we asked if protein-coding DEGs also contributed to the identified enrichment profiles. Especially, we would like to know if tRNA and snoRNA DEGs were the mere contributors to the enrichment of translation-related GO terms. In RM cells, 36 tRNA and 26 snoRNA genes were found among 565 upregulated DEGs, while RP cells contained 69 tRNA and 44 snoRNA genes among 714 upregulated DEGs. In contrast, only two snoRNAs were found among the 192 downregulated DEGs in RM cells, and one tRNA and one snoRNA were found among the 339 downregulated DEGs in RP cells. When the identified tRNAs and snoRNAs were excluded from DEGs in GO analysis, we found that the overall enrichment patterns remained largely unchanged (Figure S2). For example, organellar ribosomal proteins, the tRNA methyltransferase complex, and the RNP complex continued to represent major enriched terms among ↑both DEGs. Thus, transcriptional changes in protein-coding genes also contributed to the enrichment of translation-related pathways for commonly upregulated genes and the enrichment of mitochondrial genes for commonly downregulated in Rnq1 prion and mutant cells. Notably, several upregulated ORF-DEGs (e.g., IMG1, IMG2, MRPL11, MRPL28, MRPL8, MRPL31, MRP17, MRPL23, RSM19, RTC6, FYV4) that fall within the enriched translation-associated terms were functionally linked to the mitochondrial ribosome in RM and RP cells, suggesting an increased mitochondrial translation. Simultaneously, ORF-DEGs downregulated in both strains were enriched in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV, the proton-transporting two-sector ATPase complexes involving vacuolar ATPase components (Vma6 and Vph1), and mitochondrial ATP synthase subunits (Atp19, Atp8, and Atp6) (Figure S2 and data not shown), suggesting that ATP synthesis in the cytoplasm may also be reduced.

Collectively, these findings suggest that Rnq1 loss and prionization reprogram the transcriptome toward increased protein synthesis while reducing energy production in both the cytoplasm and mitochondria. These coordinated shifts implicate a reallocation of cellular resources, potentially reprogramming and rebalancing cellular physiology.

Pathway-dependent impacts of [RNQ+] versus Rnq1 loss

Given the greater number of DEGs identified in [RNQ+] cells compared with Rnq1-null cells (Figures 2 and 3), we further investigated whether the prion exhibited greater regulatory stringency for individual DEGs. To address this question, we first focused on pathways commonly enriched in both RM and RP cells. As expected, most DEGs within these shared pathways exhibited stronger regulatory effects in RP than in RM cells (Figure S3 and data not shown). This pattern was particularly evident for DEGs involved in the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV (Figure S3a), the box C/D snoRNP complex (Figure S3b), and the box H/ACA snoRNP complex (Figure S3c). A similar trend was also observed for ORF-DEGs associated with rRNA modification (Figure S3d), translational regulation (Figure S3e), and other translation-related GO terms (data not shown). These findings indicate that the [RNQ+] prion primarily exerts amplified loss-of-function effects relative to the complete absence of Rnq1. To our knowledge, such an amplification phenomenon has not been reported for other yeast prions. Notably, this amplified effect was most prominent in pathways associated with translation and respiratory activities in mitochondria. In contrast, for other enriched GO terms with lower-level enrichment, such as nitrogen utilization (Figure S3f) and carbohydrate metabolism (data not shown), the [RNQ+] prion displayed only partial loss-of-function effects.

To further dissect the influence of the [RNQ+] prion, we examined DEGs uniquely regulated in RM or RP cells. The ↑RPu genes comprised 38 tRNAs, 18 snoRNAs, and 191 ORFs (Tables S1 and S2). Approximately half of these tRNAs also showed elevated expression in RM cells despite not meeting the DEG threshold. Similar patterns were observed for the 18 snoRNAs and 191 ORFs (data not shown). These observations are also consistent with an amplified loss-of-function effect of [RNQ+]. By contrast, only 5.3% of the 189 ↓RPu genes displayed reduced expression in RM without being DEGs, suggesting an overall gain-of-function effect of [RNQ+]. On the other hand, all 42 ↓RMu genes and 86 of 98 ↑RMu genes, which exhibited moderate upregulation despite not being DEGs in RP cells, exhibited partial loss-of-function prion effects. Intriguingly, 12 ↑RMu genes were undetectable in RP cells but expressed in WT, implying the presence of a distinct gain-of-function effect of [RNQ+]. Altogether, these analyses demonstrate that the [RNQ+] prion primarily acts by mimicking Rnq1’s loss-of-function effects on major pathways in which [RNQ+] mainly confers amplified effects of the Rnq1 loss.

Functional implications of the Rnq1 interactome

Rnq1 is best known for its roles in prion biology such as modulating the formation of other prions23,25, enhancing the aggregation of misfolding-prone proteins23,25–27,38, and interacting with molecular chaperones51. According to the Saccharomyces Genome Database (SGD), Rnq1 has 75 physical and 34 genetic interacting partners (IPs). Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that its physical IPs are enriched in the ER membrane insertion complex, perinuclear region, ribonucleoprotein (RNP) granules, and protein-folding processes. The enrichment of the latter two terms is also shared by its genetic IPs (Figure 5a). Notably, RNP granules often contain chaperones that participate in protein folding processes51,52. Interestingly, we observed that 18 Rnq1-IP genes were co-regulated in both RM and RP cells (Figure 5b). Except for VID24 and RNR3, others displayed changes in the transcript level greater in RP than in RM cells. Therefore, we speculated that these interactors are more likely to be relevant to Rnq1 functions, and that Rnq1 may act as a component of protein complexes.

GO analysis of these 18 IPs revealed enrichment for terms related to protein maturation and chaperone binding, vesicle- and endosome-mediated transport, protein localization, and catabolic processes (Figure 5c). Intriguingly, several chaperone-related proteins including Hsp104, Sis1, Tah1, and Btn2 stood out among the hits falling in these enriched terms (Figure 5C). It is known that Hsp104 co-localizes with Rnq1 in insoluble protein deposits (IPODs)54,55 and in other Hsp104-bound inclusions56,57. In fact, Hsp104 is constantly associated with Rnq1 in both prion and non-prion forms, whereas Sis1 only interacts with the prion form57,58. Tah1 is a chaperone interactor that participates in the assembly of large complexes such as box C/D snoRNPs and RNA polymerase II59, and contributes to the formation of the [URE3] prion60. Its upregulation in our study may help explain the increased snoRNA abundance. In addition, Btn2, plays roles in chaperoning61 and prion curing62. Together, these data suggest that Rnq1 likely functions as an Hsp104-associated protein participating in chaperone-mediated processes. Further, several other hits are factors involved in vesicle trafficking. For example, Vid24 is a component of Vid vesicles that regulates the GID complex and targets FBPase for degradation50,63. Cos7 functions in endosomal sorting and plasma membrane protein turnover64 and also localizes to mitochondria65. Muk166 and Bre567 mediate vacuolar transport and endocytic trafficking, with Bre5 additionally contributing to deubiquitination, ribophagy, and cytoplasmic mRNA processing. Btn2 is also involved in Golgi vesicle targeting68. Hrp1 localizes to both the nucleus and cytoplasmic stress granules, where it participates in mRNA polyadenylation surveillance and DNA damage responses69. Notably, multiple identified hits are shared by the enriched terms (Figure 5c), suggesting functional crosstalk between these pathways involving Rnq1. Consistent with these observations, Rnq1 has been predicted to associate with the nuclear pore central transport channel and to participate in nucleocytoplasmic transport70. For instance, Rnq1 has been implicated in the transport of human mutant huntingtin (mHtt) aggregates from the cytoplasm to the nucleus28. Finally, several Rnq1 IPs (Pih1, Pih1, and Tma108) are RNA-binding proteins, suggesting a potential role of Rnq1 in RNA binding. Given that upregulated ORF-DEGs are enriched for terms related to tRNA methylation and endoribonuclease activity, and that several downregulated Rnq1 IP genes (SIS1, COS7, MUK1, BRE5, BTN2, HRP1, HSP104, APS1, SEC27, and PSG1) are implicated in RNA processing, transport, and turnover (Figure 5b and Figure S4), Rnq1 may participate in various RNA-processing pathways.

We also hypothesized that alterations in the Rnq1 interaction might contribute to the transcriptomic changes observed in this study. To test this possibility, we searched for enriched GO terms shared between Rnq1 physical IP genes and DEGs common to RP and RM cells. Two intersecting terms bridging the Rnq1-IP genes and the ↓both DEGs were identified, related to vesicle-mediated protein trafficking and catabolism (Figure 5d, left). In contrast, Rnq1 physical IP genes and the ↑both DEGs converged through two intersecting terms related to translation (Figure 5d, right). We speculate that the loss of Rnq1 likely disrupts its native interactions. In comparison, its prionization may abolish some interactions but establish novel interactions with its natural IPs and others such as chaperones, stress granules, P-bodies, and/or IPODs, where misfolded Rnq1 may accumulate. In these cases, prion-mediated sequestration might act and lead to the mislocalization of heterologous proteins and/or RNAs, thus affecting RNA homeostasis and overall cellular physiology. Nevertheless, due to the incompleteness of Rnq1 interactomic data, which have largely been identified through prion-based studies, the present analysis is limited, and further research is necessary to explore the functions of Rnq1.

Further insights into the mechanism underlying the reshaped transcriptome

Consistent with earlier reports23,26, we found that Rnq1 is not a typical nuclear protein and lacks a nuclear localization peptide (Figure 1c and data not shown). DRNApred analysis71 predicted that Rnq1 is unlikely to be a DNA-binding protein (data not shown). Importantly, the identified DEGs are genome-wide and can be transcribed by RNA polymerases I, II, or III (Tables S1 and S2). Thus, we conclude that Rnq1 is not a TF and is unlikely to directly regulate transcription.

To investigate if transcription was indeed promoted, we collected all DEGs encoding RNA polymerase–associated proteins with an FC > 2 in RM or RP cells. As shown in Figures S4a and S4b, 13 such genes were identified. Remarkably, their transcripts were altered in the same direction in both RM and RP cells, with ten showing greater changes in RP cells, consistent with the stronger transcriptomic impact of [RNQ+]. These genes encode subunits or mediators of RNA polymerases, except for FPT1 (downregulated) that encodes an RNA polymerase III suppressor. Interestingly, the identified genes that are downregulated in both RM and RP cells are all associated with RNA polymerase III (Figure S5a). Indeed, all six subunit genes (TFC1, TFC3, TFC4, TFC6, TFC7, and TFC8) of the RNA polymerase III transcription initiation factor complex (TFIIIC) are downregulated to some extent in RM and RP cells (Figure S5a; Tables S1 and S2). On the other hand, several polymerase III subunit genes (RPC37, RPC19, RPC82, and RPB8) are upregulated in both types of cells. These findings suggest an overall enhancement of RNA polymerase I and II activities, whereas the alteration in RNA polymerase III activity remains uncertain. These observations may partly explain the higher number of upregulated DEGs and snoRNAs identified in this study. Our analysis also suggests that the predominance of tRNAs among the identified upregulated transcripts may not be attributable to an enhanced transcription of tRNAs. We further speculated that specific TFs might contribute to the modified transcriptome, although TF genes were not overrepresented among DEGs (Figure S5c-d and data not shown). YEATRACT analysis (P < 0.001 cutoff) identified 59 TF genes and 41 TF genes with enriched targets among the DEGs in RM and RP cells, respectively. Among these TFs, 37 were shared by RP and RM cells. These results suggest TFs may contribute to the modified transcriptomic profiles. Interestingly, TFs with enriched DEG targets include four TFs (Fkh1, Rgm1, Stb5, and Tog1) unique to RP cells. Considering their mRNA levels were not altered, the effects may be mediated by post-transcriptional changes for these TFs. Indeed, aggregation propensity and prionogenicity analyses revealed that all the four TFs contain intrinsically disordered regions, and Stb5 even possesses a prion-like domain (Figure S6). This suggests that these TFs may aggregate or form prion-like assemblies, potentially explaining the overrepresentation of their DEG-targets in [RNQ+] cells. Furthermore, five TFs with enriched DEG-targets were themselves DEGs and were upregulated, except for Hap4, in both RP and RM cells (Figure S5c and d). Among them, Sok2 and Sut1 were shared between RM and RP cells. Further analysis showed that TF-target associations could explain the regulation of 57.5% (258 genes) of DEGs in RM cells and 61.7% (318) of DEGs in RP cells. In addition to TFs, a recent study reported that histone H3 modifications (H3K4me3, H3K36me2, H3K36me3, and H3K79me3) decreased in [RNQ+] cells72. Together, these results suggest that the reshaped transcriptome is mediated by RNA polymerases, TFs, and epigenetic regulation, either directly or indirectly in RP and RM cells.

Finally, we hypothesized that feedback regulation73 may contribute to the modified RNA homeostasis. For instance, GO term analysis revealed enrichment for SSU among the ↓RMu DEGs, strongly contrasting the enrichment of translation-related terms among the upregulated DEGs (Figure 4a and b). Indeed, six SSU constituent-encoding genes and RPS1A among the hits falling in the term of SSU, were downregulated more in RM than in RP cells (Figure S7a). In response to the downregulation, feedback regulation may be triggered to activate pathways associated with protein synthesis. In fact, transcripts of 21 hit genes falling in the enriched terms of 90S SSU processome and SSU-rRNA maturation are upregulated, with greater alterations in RP than in RM cells (Figure S7b). This suggests enhanced 18S rRNA and pre-ribosome maturation. The greater changes observed in prion cells are consistant with the amplified loss-of-function effects of prion identified in this study.

Feedback regulation is similarly suggested by other observations. For example, the downregulation of snoRNA genes (SNR42 and SNR10) and the mitochondrial tRNA gene YNCQ001W are contrasting with the widespread upregulation of snoRNAs and tRNAs (Figure 3c and 3d). Feedback regulations may also occur in energy-producing pathways. Despite overall defects in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV and ATPases, several genes (COX17, CYC3, NDE2, CCP1, POT1, NCE103, and HMF1) are upregulated in the mitochondrial intermembrane space in both RM and RP cells. Moreover, the regulation of Rnq1 IP genes may involve similar feedback loops (Figure S5a and b). As a possible example of prion-specific feedback regulation, three Rnq1-interacting genes (GAL80, TMA46, and TAE1) are uniquely upregulated in RP cells. One plausible explanation is that these proteins are sequestered and functionally impaired by Rnq1 aggregates, thereby stimulating prion cells to increase their transcription.

Collectively, our data suggest that loss or prionization of Rnq1 alters its protein–protein and protein–RNA interactions, disrupting normal cellular physiology and RNA homeostasis. However, cells may detect these perturbations and respond through adaptive mechanisms, such as feedback regulation, to rebalance RNA and protein homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Yeast strains, media, oligonucleotide primers, and plasmids

A diploid Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain was purchased from ATCC and sporulated to obtain a wild-type haploid BY4741 strain (MATa his3Δ1 leu2Δ0 met15Δ0 ura3Δ0 flo8 [RNQ+]). This strain was repaired for the FLO8 gene using a previously described method43 to restore FLO gene expression and multicellular phenotypes, resulting in a wild-type [RNQ+] strain (RP). The strain was then grown on medium supplemented with 5 mM guanidine hydrochloride to eliminate the prion74, yielding a non-prion wild-type strain (WT).

To generate an Rnq1-deprived mutant strain (RM), a pop-in/pop-out strategy was applied to the RP strain as described previously12. Yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) medium was used as a rich medium, and synthetic complete medium lacking uracil (SC–Ura) was used for pop-in/pop-out experiments. For counter-selection of pop-outs, 1 mg/mL of 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA) was added to SC–Ura medium. All S. cerevisiae strains were grown at 30 °C. Escherichia coli DH5α was used for plasmid propagation and grown in LB medium at 37 °C. Yeast genomic DNA was used as a PCR template to produce a 0.3-kb fragment with the forward primer RNQ668F (CGA CTAGTAAACATTCCAGCTTTCGTGTCC, with a SpeI site at the 5′ end) and reverse primer RNQ1015C (CTTATCCGTATCTATTTCAGATCTTTGCTA, containing an ATG→ATA mutation in the RNQ1 start codon), and a 0.4-kb fragment with the forward primer RNQ986F (TAGCAAAGATCTGAAATAGATACGGATAAG, containing the same mutation) and reverse primer RNQ1381C (ATC TCGAGGCCAGAACCACTTAGCATTG, with an XhoI site at the 3′ end). The two fragments were mixed and used as templates for bridge PCR using primers RNQ787F (CGC ACTAGTATACACAGCCAGCCACAG, with a SpeI site at the 5′ end) and RNQ1381C. The resulting 0.6-kb PCR product was cloned into pRS306 (ATCC) via the SpeI/XhoI sites to generate an integrative plasmid, pRS306RNQ1m, for pop-in. The plasmid was linearized with MscI and used to transform the RP strain carrying plasmid p316RNQ1GFP (a gift from the Lindquist lab). Ura+ pop-in candidates were screened by PCR using primers RNQ668F and p306R (CGCCAAGCTCGGAATTAACC, located in the vector sequence), and the resulting 0.8-kb fragment was sequenced using RNQ668F. Among 11 pop-in clones, one contained the desired mutation and was then spread onto SC–Ura–Leu medium containing 5-FOA for counter-selection of Ura⁻ isolates. PCR products (generated with primers RNQ668F and RNQ1381C) from Ura⁻ pop-outs were sequenced with RNQ668F to confirm the correct mutations. Verified isolates were further tested by PCR using RNQ787F and RNQ1381C, and the products were sequenced with RNQ787F to verify the mutations. Following this procedure, an RNQ1-deprived mutant (RM) strain was obtained. The Rnq1 prion state was verified by aggregation assays as described below, and the plasmid p315CUP1-RNQ1GFP was subsequently lost spontaneously after growth without selection.

To detect Bik1 levels in RP, RM, and WT strains, the BIK1 gene was tagged with 3×FLAG using a one-step gene replacement method. The URA3 marker, carrying its own promoter, was PCR-amplified from plasmid p416TEF (ATCC) using primers BIK1tag-F and BIK1tag-R. BIK1tag-F contained 100 nt of BIK1 5′ flanking sequence, a 66-nt 3×FLAG tag, the stop codon TAG, and a 26-nt URA3 upstream region. BIK1tag-R contained 100 nt of BIK1 3′ flanking sequence and a 26-nt URA3 ORF 3′ region. The PCR product was transformed into yeast to replace the 3′ end of BIK1, with URA3 used as a selection marker. Ura⁺ isolates were screened by PCR using primers BIK1b-F (AACAGCCGTGGAATCGTTGCCAA) and URA3-R (TTAGTTTTGCTGGCCGCATCTTC), and PCR products were sequenced to confirm correct tagging.

Genomic DNA used as a PCR template was extracted using a lithium acetate/SDS-based protocol75. High-fidelity PrimeSTAR HS DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio) was used for all PCR reactions. Routine methods were used for manipulations such as DNA cloning, Sanger DNA sequencing, and yeast transformation76.

RNA extraction, treatment, sequencing, and DEG analysis

RNA extraction and sequencing followed the protocols described previously77. Briefly, fresh log-phase YPD cultures (~10⁶ cells/mL) were harvested. To avoid potential metabolic perturbations from enzymatic lysis, cells were disrupted by bead beating. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Cat. No. 74104). To ensure complete DNA removal, an additional DNase I treatment was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Ribo-reduced RNA-seq experiments were conducted at the University of Chicago Genomics Facility. After quality control, ribosomal RNA was depleted using the Illumina Ribo-Zero Plus rRNA Depletion Kit before cDNA synthesis and library preparation. Libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq mRNA Library Prep kit and quantified by the Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 using paired-end 50bp reads. Triplicate RNA samples were used for each strain.

RNA-seq reads were trimmed to remove low-quality trailing sequences using Trimmomatic v0.3378, and were then aligned to the sacCer3 assembly of the Saccharomyces cerevisiae genome with TopHat v2.1.079. Reads were further assigned to genes using HTSeq v0.6.180. The sequencing depth was roughly estimated from the length of the S. cerevisiae genome (G), the number of reads (N), and the average read length (L) as N x L / G. DEGs were called by performing EdgeR analysis81, with a calling criterion of ab adjusted p-value cutoff < 0.001 and |log2(FC)| > 1. The plotMDS function of edgeR was used to assess the variance between samples, and triplicate samples for each strain were used for the final DEG calling.

Downstream computational analyses

Comprehensive genomic information for yeast was retrieved from the Saccharomyces Genome Database (SGD). YeastMine47 was used to generate gene lists and analyze the distribution and gene features of given gene lists. The Venn diagram was generated using an online tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). TF enrichment was performed using YEASTRACT82,83 with a cutoff of a p-value < 0.001 based on DNA-binding plus expression evidence, and TF/target association data were retrieved for further analysis. To test whether a gene list contained overrepresented TF genes, the ratio of TF genes in a user list was compared to the ratio of total TF genes (183 total TF genes defined by YEASTRACT) in the yeast genome (7185 genes), and Fisher’s exact test was used to calculate the significance. Metascape44 was used for the Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. The enrichment cutoffs used in this study were set to Min Overlap > 3, p-value < 0.001, and Min Enrichment > 1.5. Gene Prioritization by Evidence Counting (GPEC, beta testing) was performed, and selected GO clusters (beta feature) were chosen. The visualization of enriched GO pathways was edited and optimized with Cytoscape v3.7.184. DRNApred (http://biomine.cs.vcu.edu/servers/DRNApred/)71 was used to predict RNA and DNA binding motifs. FoldIndex, PASTA 2.0, and PLAAC were used to predict intrinsically disordered domains and prion-like domains in proteins using default parameters.

Western blotting and semi-quantitative RT-PCR

To assess Rnq1 and Bik1 expression upon Rnq1 deprivation and prionization, Western blotting was performed using mouse monoclonal antibodies against FLAG (Sigma) and Rnq1 (gift from the Lindquist lab) as primary antibodies, and donkey anti-mouse IgG–HRP (Cell Signaling Technology) as the secondary antibody, following standard protocols. A mouse anti-Act1 monoclonal antibody (Sigma) was used as a loading control.

Semi-quantitative RT-PCR was performed to validate selected RNA-seq results in three strains. Using purified RNA as a template, first-strand cDNA was synthesized with the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen) using oligo(dT) primers according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA templates were diluted and amplified under optimized PCR conditions. Gene-specific primer pairs (forward/reverse) included YAL037CB-303 (CGGAAGTGGACAAGACAATG) / YAL037C-B-737C (TTATGGTTGCCAGAGGTGAC), SIP18-9 (CATGATGAATAAGTTCGCTG)/SIP18-225C (TTCGTAAGTCTTCCAATCGT), RGI1-247 (CACCTGCACCAGCATATTGA) / RGI1-481C (TTGCGCCTACAGGATGTGTT), and GRE1-68 (ACGACAATGACGACCAGACT / GRE1-440C (CTACCGCTGCCGATATTAGA) for selected DEGs in Figure 2.

Rnq1 aggregation assay

The prion status of Rnq1 in RP, RM, and WT strains was analyzed by fluorescence microscopy as described previously12,26. Briefly, Rnq1–GFP fusion protein was expressed from a single-copy plasmid p316CUP1RNQ1GFP (a gift from the Lindquist lab) under the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter (25 µM CuSO₄ in SC–Ura medium). A control plasmid (pCUP1sGFP, also from the Lindquist lab) was used as a negative control. Images were captured using a Zeiss Axiovert 200 epifluorescence microscope with Axiovision AC software (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Quantification of proteins in cell lysate

Protein quantification followed previously described methods77,85. Briefly, overnight YPD cultures were diluted and regrown to mid-log phase before being harvested. Cells were washed twice with cold water and counted. Cell lysis was performed by bead beating (6 × 1 min) in lysis buffer containing 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 2 mM PMSF, 2 µg/mL pepstatin A, 2 µg/mL leupeptin, 5 µg/mL aprotinin, and 1 mM benzamidine-HCl. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce 660 Protein Assay (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with BSA used as the standard. Triplicate assays were performed for each strain, and protein levels were normalized to the cell numbers.
