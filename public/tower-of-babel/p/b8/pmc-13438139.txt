Modification of screen-printed carbon electrode with 2D MoS2 nanosheets/1D Bi2S3 nanorods heterostructures as voltammetric sensing platform for 2,4-dichlorophenol analysis in water samples
Authors: Hadi Beitollahi, Somayeh Tajik, Fariba Garkani Nejad, Sayed Zia Mohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, two-dimensional (2D) molybdenum disulfide nanosheets (MoS2 NSs)/1D bismuth sulfide nanorods (Bi2S3 NRs) heterostructures (MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs) were synthesized via the hydrothermal method and characterized with Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) methods. Then, an electrochemical sensing platform based on a screen-printed carbon electrode modified with MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures (MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE) was designed as a useful platform for determining 2,4-dichlorophenol (DPN). The electrochemical determination of DPN was investigated utilizing different approaches such as chronoamperometry, cyclic voltammetry (CV), and differential pulse voltammetry (DPV). The developed MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE sensor exhibited a remarkable enhancement in the peak current compared to unmodified SPCE and other modified SPCEs in CV studies. Under optimal operating conditions, the sensor demonstrated excellent analytical performance (high sensitivity of 0.175 µA/µM, linear concentration range from 0.002 to 280.0 µM, and limit of detection (LOD) of 0.001 µM for DPN detection. The results indicate that this MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE sensor could provide an advantageous platform for sensitive and rapid detection of DPN in water samples.Keywords: Water pollution; MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures; Voltammetric sensor; Disposable modified electrode; 2,4-dichlorophenol.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Chemistry

Introduction

In today’s world, environmental contaminants considered one of the most critical issues. With the expansion of technology and human population, industrial activities are contributing to ever-increasing environmental pollution. Contaminants can infiltrate the food supply via water, soil, or agricultural systems, posing risks to both food security and public health. Thus, the need for effective, rapid, and efficient monitoring of high-risk contaminants in the environment has birthed numerous research efforts1–5. Chloro-phenolic compounds rank among the most persistent environmental pollutants, showing remarkable resistance to both chemical and biological degradation processes. The utilization of chlorophenols in producing pesticides, colors, medications, antiseptics and industrial chemicals raises significant environmental concerns due to their potential impacts on animal populations and aquatic ecosystems. The transport of chlorophenols in soil, water, and sediment depends on the pH of the system and the number of chlorine atoms they contain. Due to their persistence in the receiving aquatic environments, these semi-volatile organochlorine compounds can be found in natural settings, including surface water, groundwater, and bottom sediments6–8. 2,4-Dichlorophenol (DPN), is prone to bioaccumulation, highly toxic, and commonly found in chemical industry wastewater. The improper use of DPN not only endangers aquatic life and water ecosystems but also has long-term detrimental effects on humans and animals9,10. Due to its low biodegradability, high toxicity, and potential carcinogenic and mutagenic effects even at trace concentrations, DPN has been classified as a priority control pollutant by both the European Union and the US Environmental Protection Agency11. Due to DPN’s toxicological profile, there is a critical need to develop analytical techniques that are rapid, simple, and sensitive for its quantification and detection. Various analytical techniques have been employed for DPN detection, including gas chromatography (GC), high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and spectrophotometric12–14 methods. Nevertheless, these methods are constrained by time-intensive procedures, inability for real-time monitoring, high operational costs, and potential generation of secondary toxic byproducts, limiting their applicability for in situ analysis.

The electrochemical method has increasingly emerged as a promising method for detecting various organic and inorganic contaminants in aquatic environments. Electrochemical sensing systems provide several advantages for detecting target substances, including portability, rapid analysis, user-friendly operation, cost-effectiveness, and adaptability for field applications15–25. A key challenge in the electrochemical analysis of phenolic compounds is electrode surface fouling, which often leads to diminished sensor performance and weak output signals26,27.

The electrode surface serves as the fundamental sensing element in electrochemical detection systems. The electrode’s active surface, where it interacts with the analyte molecule, is meticulously designed and prepared to enhance the necessary communication between the electrode surface and the target analyte. To improve detection performance, electrode surfaces are frequently modified to boost both sensitivity and selectivity. Extensive research confirms that modified electrode surfaces demonstrate significantly improved catalytic performance for many redox-active species28–36. Screen-printed electrodes (SPEs) offer favorable advantages over traditional electrodes by combining disposability, cost-effectiveness, and customizable design with efficient electrochemical performance. In contrast to conventional electrodes, which necessitate tedious procedures and large sample volumes, SPEs are ready-to-use, require lower sample volumes, and can be mass-produced cost-effectively. Furthermore, SPEs are particularly suitable for in-situ detection applications owing to the miniaturization of electrochemical systems and portability37–42.

The last years have seen remarkable progress in material science research, particularly in nanomaterial development, driven by their unique characteristics such as high surface area, superior chemical and physical stabilities, low density, etc. These exceptional characteristics position nanomaterials as ideal components for diverse applications spanning biomedical engineering, electronic devices, catalytic systems, and food technology43–51. Notably, electrochemical sensor development has emerged as a particularly transformative application, demonstrating the tremendous potential of nanomaterials in analytical technologies52,53.

Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) have emerged as particularly promising electrode nanomaterials due to their unique combination of favorable characteristics54–56. Recent years have witnessed growing research interest in molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), as a representative 2D TMD. This is due to the MoS2 has gained prominence as a material with enhanced electrocatalytic performance, exceptional biocompatibility, facile surface functionalization capabilities, and high surface area57,58. The structure of MoS2 consists of S-Mo-S layers, where molybdenum atoms are sandwiched between sulfur atomic planes, with adjacent layers. The unique atomic arrangement of MoS2 nanosheets enhances material utilization efficiency while minimizing charge transport distances, resulting in superior electrochemical characteristics. Despite its advantages, MoS2 faces a significant limitation: inherently poor electrical conductivity. A critical challenge with 2D MoS2 nanosheets lies in their tendency to restack and aggregate, substantially compromising their otherwise excellent application potential59–62. The emergence of heterostructure materials has revolutionized multiple disciplines, creating novel research frontiers in condensed matter physics, advanced materials engineering, and functional device development. The strategic design of transition metal sulfide-based heterostructures enables three key performance enhancements: (i) effective spatial charge separation, (ii) optimized electrical transport through precise band alignment, and (iii) synergistic interfacial effects between constituent materials, collectively yielding superior electrochemical functionality63. Bismuth sulfide (Bi2S3) has emerged as an environmentally benign semiconductor with diverse applications, including hydrogen storage systems, (bio)sensing platforms, photocatalytic processes, energy storage devices, and biomedical imaging technologies. Bi2S3 has attracted considerable research attention due to its unique advantages, such as excellent material compatibility, earth-abundant composition, and tunable nanostructures with precise morphological control - all contributing to enhanced physicochemical properties and optimized application performance64–67. Building upon these fundamental insights, we hypothesized that constructing a hierarchical 2D/1D heterostructure combining these materials could yield a multifunctional system with synergistic electrical and catalytic characteristics.

In the present work, we use a facile hydrothermal approach for constructing 2D MoS2 nanosheets/1D Bi2S3 nanorods heterostructures (MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs) through a single-step synthesis protocol. The as-prepared MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures were utilized to modify screen-printed carbon electrode (SPCE) (MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE) for fabricating electrochemical DPN sensor. The MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE sensor demonstrates the characteristics of fast detection, high sensitivity, and broad linear detecting range for DPN detection. The results established that the created MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE is a cost-effective and simple sensor for determine DPN in real specimens with no need for costly and complicated tools.

Experimental

Equipment and materials

All chemicals and reagents including thiourea (99%), (NH4)6Mo7O24.4H2O (99.9%), Bi(NO3)3.5H2O (98%), ethanol (96%), 2,4-dichlorophenol (≥ 98%), phosphoric acid (85%), sodium hydroxide (98%) were analytical grade and used as received from Sigma-Aldrich and Merck without further purification.

The electrochemical workstation PGSTAT 320 N (Autolab Company, Metrohm, the Netherlands) was implemented to achieve the electrochemical measurements, and it is interfaced to a computer for data acquisition. Disposable SPCEs (model DRP-110, Dropsens) with integrated carbon working and counter electrodes alongside a silver pseudo-reference electrode were employed. The electrolyte pH was precisely measured using a Metrohm 713 digital pH meter (Switzerland).

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was acquired utilizing a Panalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer (The Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The analysis of Fourier transfer infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was conducted via a Bruker spectrometer (Tensor II, Germany). Morphological characterization was performed using a Philips EM 208 S transmission electron microscope (TEM, The Netherlands).

Synthesis of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures

The MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures were synthesized via hydrothermal method according to the procedure reported by Kim et al. with some changes68. Initially, 0.4110 g thiourea (5.40 mmol) was completely dissolved into 35 mL deionized water by stirring for 10 min at ambient temperature. Then, 0.2224 g (NH4)6Mo7O24.4H2O (0.18 mmol), and 0.6111 g Bi(NO3)3.5H2O (1.26 mmol), were added to the above solution under stirring. The prepared solution was magnetically stirred for 1 h at ambient temperature. Afterwards, the mixed solution was poured into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, heated an oven at 200 °C for 15 h, and then naturally cooled to ambient temperature. Subsequently, the precipitates prepared at the bottom of the autoclave were collected, washed with ethanol (several times), and then dried completely at 67 °C for 15 h. The synthesis of MoS2 was performed without using Bi(NO3)3.5H2O, and the synthesis of Bi2S3 was carried out without (NH4)6Mo7O24.4H2O, both following a procedure similar to the synthesis method of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures.

Preparation of SPCE modified with MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures

For modified electrode fabrication (MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE), a uniform suspension was achieved by ultrasonically dispersing 1.0 mg of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures in 1.0 mL deionized water (15 min, room temperature). Then, 3.0 µL of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures suspension was drop-casted onto the SPCE surface and dried at room temperature to fabricate MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE.

Electroactive surface area (A) of unmodified (bare) and MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE were evaluated using [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− as a redox probe by cyclic voltammetry (CV). The CV responses of bare SPCE and MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE were recorded in 0.1 M KCl solution in the presence of 5.0 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−. At the surface of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE, a decrease in ∆Ep and an increase in the values of the redox peak currents of the redox probe were observed compared to bare SPCE. These observations can be related to faster electron transfer kinetics and higher electroactive surface area of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs-modified SPCE. Results and discussion

Characterization of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures

The crystalline structure of the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures was identified by using XRD analysis, and the resulting XRD pattern is exhibited in Fig. 1. In the XRD pattern of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures there are diffraction peaks at 15.7, 17.6, 22.4, 23.7, 25.0, 27.4, 28.6, 31.8, 33.0, 33.9, 35.6, 36.5, 39.1, 40.0, 42.7, 43.2, 43.7, 45.6, 46.6, 48.3, 49.2, 51.3, 52.7, 53.8, 54.7, 56.4, 57.6, 59.2, 60.4, 62.7, 65.1, 67.7, 69.4, and 72.2, which belong to the (020), (120), (220), (101), (130), (021), (211), (221), (301), (311), (240), (231), (041), (141), (421), (250), (520), (440), (501), (060), (251), (222), (312), (061), (360), (611), (412), (640), (720), (152), (721), (532), (561), and (811) crystal planes of Bi2S3, respectively. All the diffraction peaks observed can be indexed to the orthorhombic phase of Bi2S3 (JCPDS Card, No. 00-017-0320)69,70, revealing the successful preparation of Bi2S3 material. Nonetheless, in the XRD pattern of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures, the characteristic diffraction peaks related to MoS2 NSs have not been observed. One possible explanation could be the lower MoS2 content, which results in comparatively weaker diffraction intensity than that of Bi2S3 NRs – a similar observation has also been reported in the literature59. Another possible explanation is related to the presence of ultrathin monolayer of MoS2 nanosheet. Previously, it has been reported that monolayer MoS2 exhibits lower intensity compared to stacked nanosheets of MoS259,71.

Morphological features of the as-prepared MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures have been investigated by TEM, and the corresponding TEM images have been presented in the Fig. 3. As exhibited in the TEM images, it is clear that the Bi2S3 material has a well-defined rod-like morphology with a one-dimensional (1D) structure, and these nanorods are arranged together. Also, during the hydrothermal reaction, two-dimensional (2D) nanosheets of MoS2 have been grown. Therefore, the TEM analysis indicates the presence of both MoS2 NSs and Bi2S3 NRs in the as-prepared structure.

Electrochemical behavior of DPN on MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE

The pH of phosphate buffer solution (PBS) must be optimized to ensure ideal voltammetric analysis conditions, as it can directly influence the oxidation potential and peak current of DPN. So, the effect of pH on the DPN oxidation on MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE was investigated from pH 2.0 to pH 9.0 with 0.1 M PBS. The oxidation peaks current reaches its maximum intensity at pH 7.0, followed by a progressive decrease at higher pH values (Fig. 4). So, pH 7 was used as the supporting electrolyte for all subsequent investigations and measurements. CV studies were employed to comparatively evaluate the electrocatalytic performance of bare SPCE, Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE, MoS2 NSs/SPCE, and MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE. Figure 5 exhibits the CVs of bare SPCE, Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE, MoS2 NSs/SPCE, and MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE in the presence of 100.0 µM of DPN in PBS. At bare SPCE, a weak and sluggish oxidation peak is observed at anodic peak potential (Epa) 745 mV (anodic peak current (Ipa) = 2.5 µA), suggesting that a slow electron transfer emerges at bare SPCE. However, at the surface of Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE, the oxidation peak of DPN is shifted to 730 mV and the Ipa = 5.4 µA is 2.16 times higher than the bare SPCE, arise from the good electrochemical properties of Bi2S3 NRs. At the surface of MoS2 NSs/SPCE, the Ipa of DPN is further increased to 10.2 µA with Epa = 690 mV, which can be attributed to the high surface area and good electrocatalytic activity of MoS2 NSs. Furthermore, the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs-modified SPCE demonstrates superior performance towards the oxidation of DPN, with an Ipa = 17.8 µA at Epa = 600 mV compared to other electrodes due to the synergistic effects of the MoS2 NSs and Bi2S3 NRs.

CVs obtained at bare SPCE, Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE, MoS2 NSs/SPCE, and MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE in PBS containing 100.0 µM DPN.

Role of variable scan rates

The influences of the potential scan rate (υ) between 5 and 500 mV/s on DPN oxidation at the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE was systematically investigated. CVs obtained in the presence of 100.0 µM DPN and a curve of Ip.a. versus the v1/2 (inset) is presented in Fig. 6. The CVs reveal a positive correlation between scan rate and Ipa, with Ip.a. increasing proportionally with higher scan rates. Likewise, the anodic peak potential (Ep.a.) exhibits a slight positive shift with increasing scan rate. The Ipa exhibits a linear dependence on the v1/2, as described by the equation: I (µA) = 2.342v1/2 ((mV s− 1)1/2) + 2.6305 (R² = 0.9999). This strong correlation confirms that the oxidation of DPN at the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE is diffusion-controlled.

CVs of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE in PBS containing 100.0 µM DPN at various scan rates. Inset: Linear dependence between Ip.a. of DPN and υ1/2.

Differential pulse voltammetric (DPV) analysis of DPN

A key feature of the sensor is high sensitivity, enabling the detection of trace-level target analyte concentrations. Based on these considerations, the analytical calibration curve was constructed using DPVs recorded for a series of DPN solutions. The measurements were performed in a supporting electrolyte under optimized experimental conditions on the modified electrode surface (MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE). The DPVs for different concentrations of DPN are displayed in Fig. 8. The oxidation peak currents exhibit a linear increase with rising DPN concentration. The calibration curve exhibited excellent linearity (I (µA) = 0.175CDPN (µM) + 0.7139 (R2 = 0.9999)) (inset of Fig. 8). Using the conventional approach, limit of detection (LOD) was calculated as LOD = 3SBlank/m, where SBlank represents the standard deviation of the blank signal and m corresponds to the slope of the calibration plot [77]. Therefore, a LOD of 0.001 µM was obtained. Also, the sensitivity was determined to be 0.175 µA/µM based on the slope of the calibration plot. Reproducibility, stability, and selectivity studies

The reproducibility of the design of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE sensing platform was assessed by fabricating four independent electrodes and measuring DPV responses in PBS containing 25.0 µM DPN. The RSD value of these measurements was calculated to be 4.4%, showing the design of MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE is reproducible. Also, the stability of the as-developed sensing platform was investigated by maintaining the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE under ambient conditions. The responses to DPN were monitored every week by performing DPVs in PBS containing 25.0 µM DPN. It was found that even after three weeks, the current responses of the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE maintained 95.7% of its initial value, which showed its good stability. Moreover, we evaluated potential interference from different substances commonly found in water samples, including ions and phenolic compounds, on the voltammetric determination of DPN. It was found that the presence of 300-fold higher concentrations of NH4+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Na+, NO3−, and Cl−, as well as 10-fold higher concentrations of hydroquinone, 2-nitrophenol, and 4-aminophenol caused slight changes (˂ ±5%) in the DPV responses of the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE sensor toward DPN, confirming its suitability for selective voltammetric determination.

Analysis of real specimens

To evaluate the method’s practical applicability for DPN determination, the developed MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs/SPCE sensor was employed for detecting DPN in real water specimens (tap and well water samples). All assays were conducted using the standard addition method under optimized experimental conditions. The results for the five concentration levels of DPN are presented in Table 2. The obtained recoveries for water samples varied from 97.1% to 103.7%. These results confirm the applicability of the developed sensors for DPN assessment in diverse matrices.

Conclusion

In this study, MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures were successfully prepared via a facile hydrothermal approach and characterized by using FT-IR, XRD, and TEM methods. Then, we indicated the fabrication and design of an electrochemical sensing platform based on MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs heterostructures modified SPCE to increase the electrochemical sensing responses for DPN. The integration of MoS2 NSs and Bi2S3 NRs created synergistic effects that substantially amplified the voltammetric response for DPN detection. Furthermore, the developed technique led to an acceptable performance with a broad linear concentration range of DPN from 0.002 to 280.0 µM and an LOD of 0.001 µM. Also, the developed sensor showed good reproducibility, stability, and selectivity for DPN detection. Further investigations confirmed that the MoS2 NSs/Bi2S3 NRs-based sensing platform demonstrated good practical applications for detecting DPN in the water samples.
