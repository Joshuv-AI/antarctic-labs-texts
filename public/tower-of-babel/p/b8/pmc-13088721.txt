Analysis of immune cytokines and cell surface markers in FFPE biopsy samples from patients with suspected endometrial and lymph Node Tuberculosis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB), caused by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), remains a major global health concern. It manifests as pulmonary TB and extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), with the latter including TB lymphadenitis (TBLN) and endometrial TB (ETB). While TBLN is the most common form, ETB is among the least studied despite its serious clinical consequences, such as infertility. Owing to its asymptomatic nature, ETB is often diagnosed at a late stage after irreversible tissue damage has occurred. The immune response in TB varies by infection site, yet very little is known about the local immune response at the infection site of the ETB. Cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and TGF-β play critical roles in TB immunity, but their expression patterns differ across infection sites. Limited data exist on site-specific cytokine responses in the ETB and TBLN, making it essential to investigate immune markers in these forms of TB. This study examines cytokine expression and T-cell surface markers in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy samples to provide insights into immune dynamics and potential biomarkers for EPTB diagnosis.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy samples from ETB patients, TBLN patients, and controls. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to assess the expression of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and TGF-β, as well as CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell markers. Cytokine expression levels were quantified via QuPath softwares, and statistical comparisons were made via the Mann‒Whitney U test and the Kruskal‒Wallis test.ResultsCompared with controls, both ETB patients and TBLN patients presented significantly elevated IFN-γ and TNF-α expression (p < 0.05). These site-specific immune responses could contribute to variations in disease presentation and progression. The differential expression of TGF-β in the ETB suggests a potential role in immune modulation and fibrosis, which may contribute to tissue remodeling and reproductive dysfunction. Additionally, the increased levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α in both EPTB forms reinforce the involvement of localized immune responses in disease pathogenesis. Identifying these cytokine patterns may help uncover potential biomarkers for early diagnosis, which, upon validation through further studies, could enhance the detection and management of ETB.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), remains one of the most lethal infectious diseases worldwide [1]. While pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is the most common manifestation and its immunopathogenesis is well studied, data on extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), which includes TB lymphadenitis (TBLN) and endometrial TB (ETB), are limited. TBLN is the most common form, whereas ETB remains one of the least studied despite its serious clinical consequences, such as infertility. Owing to its asymptomatic nature, ETB is often diagnosed at a late stage after irreversible damage has occurred. EPTB infections occur in secondary sites such as lymph nodes and the endometrium, where immune responses and cytokine dynamics may differ substantially [2, 3]. Upon infection, the host immune system initiates a coordinated defense mechanism aimed at eliminating the pathogen. Inflammatory cells, primarily macrophages and dendritic cells, act as the first line of defense against M. tuberculosis. The involvement of other cell types, such as lymphocytes and fibroblasts, has also been shown to influence the immune response to MTB [4–9].

These immune cells release various cytokines that recruit additional immune components to the infection site. Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), a proinflammatory cytokine, promotes inflammation and facilitates the recruitment of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, which then execute their effector functions [10]. Conversely, anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), help prevent excessive immune responses, thereby regulating the immune reaction. The overall effectiveness of the immune response and the extent of tissue damage largely depend on maintaining a balance between proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels [11–14]. Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) also plays a critical role in the immune response to M. tuberculosis by mediating macrophage activation, further enhancing the ability of the host to control infection [15].

Cytokine expression patterns vary between PTB patients and EPTB patients, highlighting potential differences in immune regulation based on the anatomical site of infection [16]. Furthermore, significant differences have been observed in cytokine expression levels between plasma samples and biopsy samples from infection sites in EPTB patients [17–19]. However, limited data exist on the cytokine landscape at the actual site of infection and endometrial TB, indicating that investigating local immune responses in these forms of TB is crucial.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate and compare the expression levels of cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, and TGF-β, along with cellular markers such as CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy samples from selected endometrial TB and TBLN patients, as well as from controls free of TB. Understanding localized immune responses in these EPTB manifestations may provide insights into disease pathogenesis, host immunity, and potential diagnostic markers.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the Department of Pathology, Armauer Hansen Research Institute, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. FFPE biopsy samples were collected from patients suspected of having gynecologic and lymph problems who attended St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The hospital is the second largest teaching hospital in the country, providing many different clinical services, teaching activities and training for specialty and subspecialty levels for health professionals.

Study design and period

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on 27 biopsy samples from patients with endometrial tuberculosis and 14 biopsy samples from patients suspected of having tuberculosis, collected between 2018 and 2022 at the Pathology Department of St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. In addition, 10 Xpert negative control samples were included in this study. The FFPE biopsies were proven to be GeneXpert positive. The laboratory work was performed at the Armauer Hansen Research Institute (AHRI) pathology laboratory (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1Flowchart of FFPE biopsy sample collection

Flowchart of FFPE biopsy sample collection

This study is an extension of our previously published article [20], in which the clinical characteristics of the study cohort, including the presenting symptoms, were described. The present work focuses specifically on immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis using the same archived formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples. As reported in the primary study, all ETB cases were female. Control FFPE tissues were obtained from individuals with no histopathological or GeneXpert evidence of tuberculosis and were collected from the same anatomical sites (endometrial tissue and lymph nodes) to ensure appropriate site-matched comparison.

Tissue sectioning and deparaffinization

Four-micron-thick tissue sections were prepared from each FFPE tissue sample after appropriate trimming via a rotary microtome (Leica RM 2255). Before sectioning, the trimmed block was left on a cryo-console (Leica Histo core Arcadia C) for suitable cooling to prevent the tissue sections from shrinking. Five special charged slides were labeled for each of the samples with the corresponding ID on the tissue container for immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining [21].

The tissue sections on the slides were made free of the paraffin wax in which they were embedded. The slides containing the tissue sections were placed in an oven (DHP 9052) for 1 h at 60 °C. Then, the samples were immersed in two changes of xylene for 5 min each and put into a decreasing concentration of alcohol starting from absolute ethanol (95%, 80%, and 70% for 5 min each) for rehydration, after which they were finally put in distilled water.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue Sect. (4 μm thickness) were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded alcohol, and subjected to antigen retrieval using heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked prior to incubation with primary antibodies against CD4, CD8, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and TGF-β. Following incubation with the respective primary antibodies, sections were treated with species-appropriate secondary antibodies. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate was used for chromogenic detection, and slides were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX63). For each case, at least two representative tissue sections were evaluated. From each section, five non-overlapping high-power fields (HPFs, 400× magnification) were randomly selected in areas with preserved tissue architecture and inflammatory infiltrates. Quantification was performed using a semi-quantitative manual counting approach by two independent pathologists who were blinded to the clinical grouping.

For each high-power field, the number of positively stained cells was counted and expressed as a percentage of the total number of nucleated cells within the same field. The mean percentage of positive cells across analyzed fields was calculated for each sample. The frequency of positive staining was therefore defined as:

A predefined cutoff threshold of ≥ 10% positively stained cells was used to categorize samples as exhibiting high expression [22]. This threshold was selected based on commonly applied semi-quantitative scoring approaches in immunohistochemical studies of inflammatory markers and to minimize background staining variability while preserving biologically meaningful differences between groups. Additionally, the cutoff was supported by inspection of the distribution of staining frequencies across samples, which demonstrated clear separation between low-background and elevated expression patterns.

Cutoff values for cytokines and cell markers

For categorizing cytokine and cell marker expression levels, the average value of the expression level of each cytokine and cell marker from healthy tissues was used. The values below the average of the control were categorized as ‘under expressed’, whereas the value above the average of the control was categorized as ‘overexpressed’.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) due to the non-normal distribution of percentage expression values.

Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test was applied where appropriate. Categorical variables derived from expression cutoffs (under expressed vs. overexpressed) were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical issues

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (Protocol number: PM23/797). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national and institutional guidelines. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of archived samples, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB. The confidentiality and rights of the study participants were protected at all stages of the research.

Discussion

T cells and their associated cytokines, such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, are still recognized as key players in tuberculosis immunity, although the importance of innate and other immune responses has been highlighted in recent years [4, 23, 24]. Previous studies have demonstrated that differences in T-cell subset numbers and cytokine expression profiles are evident in various forms of extrapulmonary TB, particularly in TB lymphadenitis [18, 19, 25, 26]. However, there is a paucity of research examining immune responses directly at the infection site. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) can affect any part of the body, including the lymph nodes, genital area, skin, pleura, and brain. Tuberculous lymphadenitis (TBLN) is the most common form of extrapulmonary TB. Endometrial TB (ETB), an extrapulmonary manifestation, is often diagnosed only after significant damage to female reproductive organs due to its asymptomatic nature and the absence of specific diagnostic procedures [3]. This study utilized immunohistochemistry (IHC) to characterize the local immune response in the TBLN and ETB, with a focus on key T-cell markers (CD4 + and CD8+), proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IFN-γ), and an anti-inflammatory cytokine (TGF-β). Understanding cytokine expression patterns and immune cell distributions at infection sites could provide valuable insights into EPTB immunopathogenesis and inform potential biomarker-based screening strategies for improved diagnosis and disease monitoring.

In TBLN, infected lymph nodes become sites of chronic immune activation, characterized by macrophage infiltration, CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, which drive local inflammation and immune modulation [27]. Similarly, a report has shown that in the ETB, MTB elicits a localized immune response in endometrial tissue involving activated T cells, fibroblasts, and cytokine-driven inflammation, often resulting in tissue fibrosis and reproductive dysfunction [28].

Proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ, are critical mediators of host immune defense against TB. These cytokines are produced primarily by macrophages and T cells and play a key role in activating immune responses against intracellular pathogens [29]. TNF-α contributes to immune activation by inducing macrophage apoptosis and recruiting additional immune cells to the infection site [23]. In our study, the expression level of TNF-α between ETB and TBLN patients have shown no statistical significance. This variability may stem from differences in disease progression among individuals or compensatory immune mechanisms that regulate TNF-α production at various stages of infection. Previous research has also reported inconsistencies in TNF-α levels, suggesting that disease severity may not always directly correlate with TNF-α expression and that other immune factors contribute to disease progression [28, 30].

IFN-γ, another critical proinflammatory cytokine, is central to TB immunity, as it activates macrophages to destroy intracellular MTB [29]. IFN-γ facilitates this process by inducing reactive oxygen and nitrogen intermediates, which are essential for bacterial clearance within the phagolysosome [31]. In addition to its bactericidal function, IFN-γ enhances immune cell recruitment to infection sites, sustaining an effective immune response against MTB [32]. In our study, increased IFN-γ expression was observed in the ETB group, which aligns with its well-established role in TB immunity. The increased IFN-γ levels indicate that the immune system actively responds to ETB, helping to control infection. These findings are supported by previous studies, such as those conducted by Maji et al., which reported similar IFN-γ expression patterns in TBLN patients, reinforcing its importance in TB pathogenesis [33]. Furthermore, the high levels of TNF-α (87.5%) and IFN-γ (72.5%) observed in ETB patients reinforce the idea that a strong proinflammatory response is actively engaged in MTB control within endometrial tissue.

In contrast to proinflammatory cytokines, anti-inflammatory cytokines such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) serve as immune regulators, preventing excessive inflammation that could lead to host tissue damage. However, TGF-β also plays a dual role, as excessive expression can suppress effector immune responses, allowing MTB to persist and evade immune clearance [34]. In this study, ETB patients presented higher TGF-β levels than TBLN patients did, suggesting that the immune system may be attempting to counterbalance inflammation and minimize tissue damage in the endometrium. Similar observations have been reported by Mustafa et al., who noted increased TGF-β levels in TBLN patients, highlighting its role in immunoregulation during chronic infections [35]. However, elevated TGF-β may also promote immune evasion by MTB, as it has been implicated in suppressing effective immune responses and enabling bacterial persistence [36]. This mechanism could contribute to chronic or latent TB infection at extrapulmonary sites.

Notably, CD4 + T cells are involved primarily in the production of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, which activate macrophages and increase their bactericidal activity [37]. In contrast, CD8 + T cells exert cytotoxic effects, leading to the lysis of infected macrophages and destruction of the intracellular MTB [37]. In our study, significant numbers of both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells were observed in the ETB, which is consistent with their established roles in TB immunity [38]. The presence of these cells in endometrial TB patients suggests a coordinated cellular immune response at extrapulmonary infection sites.

In ETB patients, a higher proportion of CD8⁺ T-cell staining relative to CD4⁺ T-cell staining was observed; however, no direct statistical comparison between these two markers within the ETB group was performed. CD8 + T cells are critical for targeting MTB-infected cells, especially in chronic or latent TB infections, where cytotoxic activity plays a key role in controlling infection. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that CD8 + T cells contribute significantly to the immune control of MTB, particularly in settings of coinfection, such as HIV, where CD4 + T-cell depletion compromises immune responses [39].

Despite the robust immune responses observed in this study, certain limitations should be acknowledged. This study focused on a select number of cytokines and immune markers, which may not fully capture the complexity of immune regulation in EPTB. Additionally, the relatively small sample size may limit generalizability. Future research should aim to expand the range of biomarkers assessed and include complementary molecular approaches, such as transcriptomic analysis, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of immune regulation in EPTB.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that endometrial tuberculosis (ETB) is characterized by a distinct local immune profile, marked by increased infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells and a balanced proinflammatory and regulatory cytokine response, as reflected by elevated IFN-γ and TGF-β levels. In contrast, tuberculous lymphadenitis (TBLN) exhibits pronounced upregulation of TNF-α and TGF-β, underscoring site-specific immune responses across extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) manifestations. Together, these findings highlight the complex interplay between immune activation and regulation at infection sites and support the potential utility of cytokine profiling as an adjunct tool for understanding TB immunopathology and improving diagnosis in EPTB. Further large-scale studies incorporating broader immune marker panels are warranted to validate these biomarkers and enhance their diagnostic and prognostic value.
