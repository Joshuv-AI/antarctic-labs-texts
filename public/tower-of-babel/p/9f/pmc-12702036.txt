Background

Ventral abdominal hernias can be categorized as acquired or congenital [1]. Incisional ventral hernias, a form of acquired hernia, are a common complication of abdominal surgery, with a documented incidence of up to 20 % [2]. The risk of developing an incisional hernia following elective abdominal surgery is approximately 10 %, and increases up to 33 % following acute abdominal surgery [3]. The incidence of ventral hernias appears to be on the rise, in large part due to the increasing elderly, diabetic, and obese populations, among other factors, such as smoking and surgical-site infections [1,3]. An estimated 348,000 ventral hernia repairs are performed annually in the United States, accumulating approximately 3.2 billion dollars in healthcare costs, with subsequent complications costing anywhere from $30,000 to $210,000 [4,5]. Thus, ventral incisional hernias contribute a significant burden on our healthcare system. The most serious of these complications includes strangulation of abdominal organs, leading to necrosis [3].

Commonly reported contents of ventral hernias include omentum, preperitoneal adipose tissue, and the small intestine or colon [1]. Some case reports have even documented rare instances of protrusion of the spleen and parts of the pancreas and liver into the hernia [1,6]. While the pancreas is considered a retroperitoneal organ, most documented cases of organs protruding into a ventral hernia sac are classically intraperitoneal. Retroperitoneal organs tend to be less associated with ventral hernias and, to our knowledge, there is only one other report of an incisional hernia containing a kidney [7]. In this case report, we document an extremely rare incidence of a male who developed a giant incisional hernia post-exploratory laparotomy, and on repair, was found to have his right kidney present within the hernia sac. This manuscript was completed using the SCARE criteria [8].

Case

A 49-year-old male with a history of a large, complex incisional hernia was referred to general surgery for evaluation and management. Patient had a history of umbilical hernia that was repaired previously with mesh 3 years prior. At the time of initial presentation, his BMI was considered prohibitive for a durable repair, and he was referred to bariatric surgery. He subsequently underwent sleeve gastrectomy and achieved a total weight loss of 275 pounds through combined surgical and lifestyle efforts over the next eight months. After successful weight reduction, the patient returned to the clinic for re-evaluation. He reported intermittent abdominal pain that worsened with activity. Two months prior, he had been hospitalized at an outside facility for small bowel obstruction believed to be secondary to his hernia. On physical exam, the hernia appeared larger than in prior imaging (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), and a preoperative CT scan was obtained to guide operative planning.

Prior CT scan showing kidney in normal position.

The CT scan demonstrated a massive ventral hernia containing nearly the entire colon and all small bowel, measuring 9.7 cm transverse and 10.1 cm cranial/caudally (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). There was a marked reduction in mesenteric fat, correlating with the patient's significant weight loss. The right kidney demonstrated interval clockwise rotation, with the ureter exiting at the 7 o'clock position and associated mild hydronephrosis, which was concerning for a partial obstruction. Given the complex anatomy and new imaging findings, an open repair with possible transversus abdominis release (TAR) as decided on as the surgical approach.

The day of surgery, the patient was brought to the operating room and a midline incision was made over the central hernia bulge and extended from subxiphoid to suprapubic region. Care was taken to avoid underlying bowel as dissection was carried down to the hernia sac, which was opened. There were dense adhesions between prior mesh at the superior aspect of the hernia and loops of small bowel which were taken down sharply and then excised. The right kidney was found herniated within the sac and was also reduced without issue following complete lysis of adhesions (Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

The right-sided hernia sac was dissected free from subcutaneous tissue and preserved for posterior layer reconstruction. The left hernia sac was dissected free and excised. Retrorectus dissection was performed bilaterally. The posterior layer was reconstructed using the preserved hernia sac centrally and native posterior sheath laterally and closed with running 3-0 V-Loc suture to create a continuous barrier over the abdominal contents. The completed retrorectus space measured 40 cm in height by 21 cm in width. A large sheet of soft polypropylene mesh was trimmed to these dimensions and placed within the retrorectus space, extending from the pubis inferiorly to the xiphoid superiorly. The mesh was anchored laterally to the posterior sheath using 3-0 Vicryl suture. The midline fascia was closed primarily without tension using a running permanent 0 V-Loc suture. Additional reinforcement was provided using intermittent #1 PDS retention sutures. Redundant skin was excised (Fig. 6). An abdominal binder with kerlex reinforcement was applied to reduce seroma formation.

Postoperatively, the patient stayed in the hospital for two nights to ensure adequate pain control on a multimodal regimen and that he could manage good incentive spirometry. The patient did well and on follow-up, had minimal discomfort. He wore an abdominal binder and avoided heavy lifting for 2 months. He healed well without any complications at four months postop.

Discussion

Preoperative imaging plays a critical role in the evaluation of complex ventral hernias [9]. In this case, CT imaging identified not only extensive herniation of abdominal contents but also identified an unusual clockwise rotation and partial obstruction of the right kidney—an unexpected finding that directly altered our surgical plan. Originally scheduled for robotic repair, the procedure was converted to an open approach with possible transversus abdominis release (TAR) to allow for safe reduction and appropriate reconstruction. This highlights how cross-sectional imaging can provide anatomic detail essential for preoperative planning, particularly in patients with prior surgery or massive weight loss, where distorted planes and altered physiology are anticipated. In one study, preoperative CT was found to impact surgical decision-making in over half of complex abdominal wall reconstruction cases, underlining its value in operative preparation and outcomes optimization [9].

While intraperitoneal contents such as omentum, bowel, and occasionally solid organs like the liver or pancreas have been described in large ventral hernias, retroperitoneal organ involvement is exceedingly rare. A review of the literature reveals isolated reports of incisional hernias containing pancreatic or hepatic components [6], but there is a lack of data describing retroperitoneal structures—particularly the kidneys—being affected. Given its retroperitoneal position, the kidney would be among one of the last organs to herniate out of a ventral hernia. In this case, it seems likely that the kidney became affected by the hernia due to the weight of the patient's organs being pulled out and downward by the hernia. Without the normal support of the abdominal wall, gravity gradually pulled more tissue to be pulled into the hernia sac. When combined with visceral fat loss, the patient likely had worsening loss of domain over time, leading to his kidney herniating. Use of an abdominal binder might have provided external support to slow this progression.

In cases such as this with large hernia defects, there are a few options for repair that depend on the loss of domain. An open hernia repair with mesh placement in the preperitoneal space or in the retrorectus space is a common approach, as was used here. When fascial closure is challenging due to loss of domain, a retrorectus or transversus abdominis release—unilateral or bilateral—can facilitate closure. Although this was initially planned for this patient, the fascial edges could be approximated without undue tension, so the release was avoided.

Postoperatively, key aspects of management included a multimodal pain regimen and early mobilization. Effective pain control is essential to prevent splinting and shallow breathing, which can lead to atelectasis or pneumonia, prolonging hospitalization or requiring readmission. Early ambulation and pulmonary hygiene remain vital to optimal recovery.

Conclusion

Given the extent of herniation and loss of domain, a retrorectus approach with posterior sheath reconstruction and mesh reinforcement was used to achieve a durable, tension-free closure. If that was not possible, a TAR would have been completed in this case. The posterior flap was successfully created with a combination of preserved sac and native sheath, and a large prosthetic mesh was placed in the retrorectus plane, spanning the full vertical and lateral extent of the defect. This approach remains standard in complex ventral hernia repair due to its ability to restore normal anatomy while minimizing mesh-related complications [10]. This case adds to the evolving understanding of hernia behavior in post-bariatric, post-laparotomy patients and underscores the need for heightened intraoperative awareness when encountering large, chronic defects.
