Effects of low-dose esketamine on early quality of recovery following minimally invasive esophagectomy: a multicenter, randomized controlled study
Authors: Hongwei Zhang, Feng Yin, Lantao Li, Xuefei Li, Weiwei Xu, Yi Zhou, Liang Jin, Hongyan Lin, Xihua Lu, Jianjun Yang, Hai Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients undergoing minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) frequently experience moderate-to-severe postoperative pain and anxiety-depressive symptoms, compromising postoperative quality of recovery (QoR). Esketamine is a promising adjunct for analgesia and anxiolysis; however, the effect of low-dose esketamine on patient-centered recovery outcomes in MIE remains unclear.MethodsIn this double-blinded, multicenter randomized controlled trial, patients scheduled for elective McKeown esophagectomy were allocated to esketamine (0.25 mg/kg loading dose and 0.125 mg/kg/h continuous infusion during surgery) or placebo (equivalent volume and rate of saline). The primary outcome was the QoR-15 score on postoperative day (POD) 2. Secondary outcomes included QoR-15 scores on POD 1, 3, 7 and 30. Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale - Anxiety Subscale (HADS-A) and Depression Subscale (HADS-D) scores, numeric rating scale (NRS) pain scores, and the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) scores on POD 1 to 3, and safety evaluations.ResultsA total of 198 patients were analyzed (esketamine, n = 98; placebo, n = 100). Intraoperative esketamine significantly improved QoR-15 scores on POD 2 (116.9 ± 9.4 vs. 110.2 ± 9.8, p < 0.001) and POD 3 (123.6 ± 7.2 vs. 116.8 ± 8.4, p < 0.001) compared with placebo. HADS-A and HADS-D scores were lower in the esketamine group on POD 1 to 3 (all p < 0.001). Additionally, esketamine recipients reported significantly lower NRS scores at rest and during movement and BPI scores for pain severity and pain interference items (all p < 0.001). There were no significant between-group differences in safety outcomes.ConclusionsIntraoperative low-dose esketamine improved early, patient-reported recovery after MIE by enhancing analgesia and reducing postoperative anxiety and depression without increasing adverse events.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Register (identifier: ChiCTR2400088916).Keywords: Esketamine, minimally invasive esophagectomy, quality of recovery

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is among the most life-threatening malignant tumors worldwide [1]. Patients commonly experience negative psychological conditions, notably anxiety and depression, driven by concerns about prognosis and recurrence [2]. Surgical resection remains the mainstay for resectable esophageal cancer. Endoscopic advances expanded the clinical application of minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE), which provides smaller incisions, less trauma, and faster recovery. Despite these advantages, perioperative factors associated with MIE, including preoperative anxiety and depression, intraoperative pneumoperitoneum and pneumothorax, prolonged postoperative fasting, and persistent pain, may activate the sympathetic stress responses, impair gastrointestinal and immune function, and disrupt neuroendocrine regulation. These perturbations destabilize physiological homeostasis and exacerbate psychological distress, thereby compromising the early quality of recovery (QoR) and prolonging hospitalization [3,4]. Accordingly, targeted strategies have been proposed to alleviate postoperative pain, anxiety, and depression and optimize enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways.

Esketamine effectively inhibits the excessive activation of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors and attenuates central sensitization associated with hyperalgesia, opioid tolerance, and chronic pain [5]. Given its multifaceted pharmacological profile, including anesthetic, analgesic, and antidepressant properties, esketamine has been increasingly utilized in pain management and depression treatment in recent years [5,6]. However, higher doses of esketamine may provide analgesic effects but are also associated with hallucinations, nightmares, and other side effects, which may limit its clinical use [7] in patients undergoing esophagectomy. In contrast, low-dose esketamine (defined as an intravenous bolus of less than 0.35 mg/kg or a continuous infusion at a rate below 1 mg/kg/h) [8] has been demonstrated to effectively alleviate acute postoperative pain [6] and improve QoR in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy [9], video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) [10,11], and laparoscopic bariatric surgery [12]. However, compared with these procedures, esophagectomy involves extensive visceral manipulation, prolonged operative times, and a more pronounced systemic inflammatory response [13], all of which may modulate the therapeutic effects of esketamine.

To date, no studies have specifically examined the effect and safety of low-dose esketamine on postoperative QoR in patients undergoing MIE. Furthermore, research on recovery in patients with esophageal cancer has predominantly focused on traditional outcomes such as recurrence, complications [14,15], and mortality [14]. Few studies have evaluated overall recovery from the patient’s perspective. The 15-item Quality of Recovery (QoR-15) scale is a validated, patient-reported outcome measure that provides a comprehensive assessment of recovery after surgery and anesthesia [16]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of low-dose esketamine on early postoperative QoR in patients undergoing MIE through a multicenter, randomized controlled trial, using the QoR-15 scale as the primary outcome measure.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter, parallel-arm, randomized, placebo-controlled trial was performed at four centers in China: West China Hospital of Sichuan University, the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, Henan Cancer Hospital, and Leshan People’s Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee (Approval No. 2024-1404) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Register on August 29, 2024 (registration number: ChiCTR2400088916, URL: www.chictr.org.cn). Written informed consent was obtained from all trial participants by qualified local study personnel at each participating site. The trial was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the report followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 statement [17].

Participants

Due to the high prevalence of esophageal cancer in Henan and Sichuan provinces [18], the four participating centers in this study were exclusively major comprehensive tertiary hospitals located in China. The substantial volume of esophageal cancer patients available at these centers ensured efficient recruitment, leading to the successful completion of all case collection prior to the anticipated study duration. Between October 2024 and January 2025, adult (≥18 years) patients who were scheduled for elective McKeown esophagectomy under general anesthesia with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I to III were screened for enrollment. The exclusion criteria included: 1) diagnosed psychiatric disorders; 2) a history of chronic pain, opioid misuse, drug abuse within the last 3 months, or alcoholism; 3) known allergy to or contraindication for esketamine; 4) unstable angina or myocardial infarction within the past 6 months; 5) acute lung injury or acute respiratory distress syndrome within the past 3 months; 6) severe liver dysfunction, severe coronary artery disease, chronic kidney failure, or heart failure; and 7) participation in other clinical trials involving drugs or medical devices within the past 3 months.

After obtaining written informed consent from the patients, a trained researcher conducted preoperative education. This education included instructions on understanding and using the QoR-15 questionnaire, the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Additionally, patients received guidance on managing postoperative pain in the ward.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive esketamine or placebo (0.9% normal saline) using computer-generated permuted block randomization, with a block size of 6. Randomization was stratified by the clinical investigation site and implemented via sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. At each site, a nurse who was not involved in patient recruitment, intraoperative management, postoperative follow-up, or outcome assessment opened the sealed envelope 15–30 min before surgical incision to obtain the group assignment and prepare the study medication according to the intervention protocol. To ensure blinding, in both groups the study medication was prepared in identical 5 mL and 50 mL syringes, with identical external appearance and labeling. The syringes were covered with non-transparent stickers containing the patient’s information, and the infusion rate was labeled in the same manner on the 50 mL syringes. Before surgical incision, the attending anesthesiologist administered a 5 mL intravenous bolus using the 5 mL syringe. During surgery, continuous intravenous infusion was maintained using the 50 mL syringe. In both groups, the total infusion volume and infusion rate were identical according to the study protocol. Participants, responsible anesthesiologists, and the investigators who were responsible for patient recruitment, data collection, and follow-up assessments remained blinded to group assignment throughout the study. Unblinding occurred only after the trial was completed and data analysis was finalized.

Anesthesia and intraoperative care

Perioperative care was conducted in accordance with ERAS guidelines in esophagectomy [19]. All patients were monitored continuously using three-lead electrocardiography, invasive blood pressure, oxygen saturation (SpO2), end-tidal concentration of carbon dioxide (PETCO2), bispectral index (BIS), neuromuscular blockade, and temperature monitoring. Anesthesia was induced using intravenous sufentanil (0.3–0.5 μg/kg), propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2–0.4 mg/kg). Parecoxib sodium (40 mg) was administered 10–15 min before the incision. In the esketamine group, esketamine was administered intravenously at a dose of 0.25 mg/kg (diluted to 5 mL) before the incision. During the surgery, esketamine was continuously infused at a rate of 0.125 mg/kg/h (as indicated on the label) until skin closure, using an infusion concentration of 1 mg/mL (total volume: 50 mL). In the placebo group, an equivalent volume of saline was administered intravenously before the incision and continuously infused throughout the surgery until skin closure. Anesthesia maintenance was achieved with sevoflurane inhalation, maintaining BIS values between 40 and 60. Remifentanil (0.1–0.2 μg/kg/min) was continuously infused for analgesia. Mechanical ventilation was performed with pressure-control with volume-guaranteed ventilation (PCV-VG) mode, with tidal volume calculated based on ideal body weight, following a lung-protective ventilation strategy [20]. Recruitment maneuvers were performed post-intubation and prior to extubation.

Fluid management followed a standard protocol [19], and the goals of intraoperative hemodynamics were to maintain a mean arterial pressure between 70 and 100 mmHg and a heart rate between 50 and 100 beats per min. Ondansetron 4 mg was administered 30 min before the completion of surgery for antiemetic prophylaxis. A warming blanket was used to maintain the body temperature between 36 and 37.2 °C. Neuromuscular monitoring was performed every 15 min, maintaining a train-of-four (TOF) < 2; cisatracurium (0.02 mg/kg) was administered if TOF ≥ 2. The endotracheal tube was removed when TOF reached 0.9 and the patient had regained consciousness and reflexes.

Postoperative management

Multimodal analgesia included intercostal nerve blockade, transversal abdominis plane (TAP) block, and patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). At the end of the thoracic procedure, the surgeon performed an intercostal nerve block at T3-T8 levels under thoracoscopy, administering 5 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine into each space. In the postanesthesia care unit (PACU), the anesthesiologist performed a TAP block under ultrasound guidance, injecting 15 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine on each side for a total of 30 mL. Patients were connected to a PCA pump containing a solution of hydromorphone (10 mg) and ondansetron (8 mg) diluted in normal saline to a total volume of 100 mL, with 2 mL doses available as needed. Additionally, intravenous parecoxib sodium (40 mg) was administered twice daily. Pain was assessed using the NRS, aiming to keep the score < 4. If NRS ≥ 4, 5 mg of dezocine were administered for rescue analgesia.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the QoR-15 score on postoperative day (POD) 2. The QoR-15 was a patient-reported outcome measure for evaluating perioperative recovery quality, encompassing five dimensions (physical comfort, emotional status, psychological support, physical independence, and pain) [16]. Secondary outcomes included: 1) the QoR-15 scores on POD 1, 3, 7 and 30; 2) Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale—Anxiety Subscale (HADS-A) and Depression Subscale (HADS-D) scores on POD 1 to 3; 3) NRS pain scores at rest and during movement, as well as BPI scores for pain severity and pain interference items, were assessed on POD 1 to 3. 4) postoperative surgical complications measured by Clavien-Dindo classification; 5) time to first ambulation; 6) postoperative length of hospital stay and hospital mortality; 7) days alive and at home within 30 days (DAH30); 8) drug-related adverse events (including postoperative nausea and vomiting [PONV], headache, nightmares, sleepiness, and constipation) and other safety assessments (such as anastomotic leakage, surgical site infection, and pulmonary complications, definitions of which were provided in Supplemental Table S1) were assessed within 7 days after surgery. To complement the subjective assessment provided by the QoR-15 scale with more objective measures of patients’ postoperative recovery, we subsequently added items 5, 6, and 7 to the predefined study protocol.

Preoperative and intraoperative data were collected from the anesthetic information system and electronic medical records at each participating center, and postoperative data were collected by designated follow-up staff. After patients returned to the ward, trained nurses assessed pain immediately and subsequently every 4 h. Using nursing records, follow-up staff calculated the average NRS pain scores at rest and during movement and BPI scores for pain severity and interference items over the past 24 h. The QoR-15 was administered on POD 1, 2, 3, 7, and 30, with assessments conducted between 17:00 and 19:00 each day. If a patient had been discharged, the research coordinator completed the assessment by telephone.

Sample size

According to Myles et al.’s study, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the QoR-15 score is 6 points [21]. Our preliminary published research findings [22] indicated that the QoR-15 scores on POD 2 effectively reflected early recovery in patients undergoing MIE, and patients achieved a mean QoR-15 score of 108 with a standard deviation (SD) of 12.1 on POD 2. The sample size calculation was performed with α established at 0.05 and statistical power (1-β) at 0.90. Accounting for an anticipated 5% attrition rate, the calculated necessary sample size was 99 patients per group, culminating in a total of 210 patients (105 per group). All calculations were performed with PASS 15 statistics (NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed based on the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) principle with no planned interim analysis. Per-protocol (PP) analysis for the outcomes was performed in a sensitive analysis. Data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD, with between-group comparisons performed using the independent Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median, and comparisons between groups were made using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequency and percentage, with between-group comparisons conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Standardized differences were calculated using Cohen’s d, and a standardized difference greater than 0.272 indicated an imbalance in the baseline variables. Repeated measures of QoR-15, NRS, and BPI scores were analyzed using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE). Prespecified subgroups analyses for the primary outcome were performed according to age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, ASA physical status, and preoperative neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. No imputation would be performed unless > 5% of data were missing. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 210 patients were randomized to the esketamine group (n = 105) or the placebo group (n = 105). Seven patients in the esketamine group were excluded from the final analysis (three due to conversion to laparotomy and four due to postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) stays with prolonged endotracheal intubation exceeding 24 h). Similarly, five patients in the placebo group were excluded due to intraoperative conversion to laparotomy (n = 2) and ICU stays exceeding 24 h (n = 3). Ultimately, 198 patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 98 patients in the esketamine group and 100 patients in the placebo group (Figure 1). Demographic, surgical, and anesthesia characteristics were comparable between the groups (Table 1).

Secondary outcomes

On POD 3, the esketamine group had a clinically higher QoR-15 score than the placebo group (123.6 ± 7.2 vs. 116.8 ± 8.4; MD, 6.8; 95% CI, 4.6 to 9.0; p < 0.001). However, on POD 1, 7 and 30, although the QoR-15 scores differed statistically (p < 0.001), the differences did not meet the MCID (Figure 2, Table 2). Regarding emotional status, the esketamine group showed lower HADS-A and HADS-D scores on POD 1 to 3, indicating improved postoperative anxiety and depression (Figure 3, Table 2). In terms of pain relief (Table 3), the esketamine group showed significantly lower NRS scores for both at resting and during movement and BPI scores for pain severity and pain interference items on POD 1 to 3 compared to the placebo group (p < 0.001). Additionally, there were no significant differences between the groups in terms of time to first ambulation, postoperative length of hospital stay, and DAH30 (p > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

PP analysis of primary and secondary outcomes was consistent with mITT analysis results. In the PP cohort, the esketamine group had significantly higher QoR-15 scores on POD 2 and 3 than the placebo group (Supplemental Table S2). Prespecified subgroup analyses identified a significant treatment-by-BMI interaction, with the greatest benefit observed among participants with a BMI of 24–28 kg/m2, suggesting that weight-adjusted dosing strategies need further investigation (Figure 4).

Discussion

As perioperative care shifts from provider-centered to patient-centered models, patient-reported outcomes such as the QoR-15 have become preferred primary endpoints for evaluating postoperative recovery. In this prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial, low-dose esketamine improved early postoperative QoR in patients undergoing MIE, enhanced early postoperative pain management, and reduced anxiety and depression on POD 1 to 3. These findings support consideration of esketamine as an adjunct within ERAS pathways for patients undergoing MIE.

Patients undergoing MIE may be particularly suitable candidates for esketamine administration because of several procedure- and patient-related characteristics. First, despite being less invasive than open esophagectomy, MIE remained a complex procedure involving cervical, thoracic, and abdominal regions. The resulting tissue injury can produce mixed somatic and visceral pain, predisposing patients to NMDA receptor-mediated central sensitization and hyperalgesia. Second, patients with esophageal cancer commonly experienced perioperative anxiety and depression symptoms related to cancer prognosis, fear of recurrence, and concerns about postoperative functional recovery. This psychological vulnerability may increase the relevance of esketamine’s mood-modulating effects. Finally, many patients undergoing MIE were older and had limited physiological reserve, making opioid-sparing analgesic strategies particularly important. Low-dose esketamine may therefore be well suitable to this context, given its minimal respiratory or hemodynamic depression and rapid clearance. Collectively, these features provided a strong rationale for incorporating esketamine into perioperative management strategies for patients undergoing MIE.

Esketamine possessed distinct pharmacological advantages over racemic ketamine, including a two-fold higher affinity for NMDA receptors, faster onset, higher clearance, and greater controllability. Consequently, it provided more potent analgesia and anesthesia at equal doses with minimal respiratory or hemodynamic depression [23]. Recent meta-analyses [24,25] have demonstrated that perioperative administration of esketamine substantially alleviated acute postoperative pain and mitigated anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients undergoing VATS, breast surgery, cesarean section, gastrointestinal surgery, and thyroid surgery, thereby enhancing early patient-reported QoR. Given that patients with esophageal cancer were predominantly elderly and prone to significant perioperative hemodynamic fluctuations and severe postoperative pain, esketamine represented a superior choice for perioperative multimodal analgesia and enhanced postoperative recovery. In this prospective randomized controlled trial, we found that low-dose esketamine significantly improved QoR-15 scores on POD 2 and 3 compared to placebo, with clinically meaningful differences consistent with prior studies [12,26]. Potential explanations for this finding included the alleviation of postoperative pain, improvement in negative emotions, and reduced opioid consumption, all of which may accelerate patient recovery.

Consistent with prior findings [10,11,27–32], patients receiving esketamine experienced improved postoperative pain control, lower anxiety and depression scores, and better recovery. An important consideration when interpreting these findings was that the relative contribution of esketamine’s analgesic and mood-modulating effects to the observed improvement in QoR-15 cannot be readily distinguished. Pain and emotion were closely interconnected through shared neurobiological pathways, with postoperative pain exacerbating emotional distress and negative emotional states amplifying pain perception [33]. Esketamine may interrupt this cycle by attenuating it through NMDA receptor antagonism [34]. However, esketamine also exerted rapid antidepressant effects through mechanisms distinct from analgesia, including modulation of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor signaling, activation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor and mammalian target of rapamycin signaling activation, and regulation of the monoaminergic system [35,36]. Previous studies have demonstrated antidepressant effects within hours of administration, supporting this concept [37]. From a clinical perspective, however, the relative contribution of these mechanisms cannot be determined from the present study. Nevertheless, it was plausible that the improvement in patient-reported recovery reflected the combined effects of esketamine on both pain and emotional distress.

The recommended dose of esketamine for postoperative analgesia typically ranged from 0.125 to 0.25 mg/kg [38]. Furthermore, the review has indicated that an intravenous bolus of 0.075–0.500 mg/kg followed by a continuous infusion of 0.075–0.600 mg/kg/h effectively managed postoperative pain [6]. A study comparing various doses (saline vs. 0.25 mg/kg bolus + 0.125 mg/kg/h infusion vs. 0.5 mg/kg bolus + 0.25 mg/kg/h infusion) and administration routes (intravenous vs. epidural) of ketamine found that an intravenous regimen of 0.5 mg/kg bolus plus 0.25 mg/kg/h infusion achieved optimal efficacy in reducing wound hyperalgesia [39]. Considering that esketamine shared similar pharmacodynamics with ketamine but requires approximately half the dose to achieve equivalent anesthetic and analgesic effects [40], the equipotent esketamine dose was calculated as a 0.25 mg/kg intravenous bolus followed by a 0.125 mg/kg/h infusion. This specific dosage has been proven to alleviate pain and improve postoperative cognitive outcomes in patients undergoing major abdominal surgery [41], elderly breast cancer surgery [42], and gastrointestinal tumor surgeries [43] with durations similar to esophagectomy. Although the optimal intraoperative dose of esketamine remained inconclusive, patients with esophageal cancer frequently presented as elderly and required prolonged surgery, factors associated with compromised organ function and reduced metabolic clearance. In this context, low-dose esketamine was preferable for maintaining hemodynamic stability and optimizing recovery [44]. Thus, this study employed the previously validated low-dose esketamine regimen (0.25 mg/kg intravenous bolus + 0.125 mg/kg/h continuous infusion).

Although the beneficial effects of low-dose esketamine on QoR-15 scores were most pronounced on POD 2 and 3, with clinically meaningful differences exceeding the MCID, these early improvements were not accompanied by a corresponding reduction in hospital length of stay or postoperative complications. This may reflect the fact that discharge after MIE was beyond patient-reported recovery, including surgical protocols, anastomotic healing, oral intake tolerance, and institutional practices, which may not be directly affected by esketamine. Additionally, although the sample size was adequately powered for the primary outcome, it may have been insufficient to detect modest differences in secondary outcomes such as hospital stay or complication rates.

This study has some limitations. First, although it focused on early postoperative recovery, the long-term effects of esketamine were not assessed. Previous research has shown a correlation between in-hospital QoR and QoR at three months postoperatively [45], suggesting that our findings may have longer-term relevance. Second, the sample size was calculated based on the QoR-15 score and may have been insufficient to robustly evaluate secondary outcomes. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Finally, only a single dose and a relatively short infusion duration were investigated. Further studies are needed to determine the optimal dose and infusion regimen of esketamine in patients undergoing MIE.

Conclusion

In conclusion, intraoperative low-dose esketamine could improve early patient-reported QoR in patients undergoing MIE. It was also associated with lower postoperative pain scores and amelioration of anxiety and depressive symptoms during the early postoperative period. Future studies should explore optimal doses and evaluate longer-term, patient-centered outcomes.
