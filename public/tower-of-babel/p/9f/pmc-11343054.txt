Research Note: Poria cocos polysaccharide alleviates lipopolysaccharide-induced intestinal inflammation and barrier damage in broiler chickens
Authors: Jinzhou Zhang, Yuting Zhang, Yuya Wang, Wenbo Hou, Dongyang Liu, Zhiguo Miao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to explore the impact of dietary supplementation of Poria cocos polysaccharide (PCP) on the lipopolysaccharide(LPS)-induced intestinal inflammation, morphology, and barrier damage in broilers. A total of 240 1-day-old male Arbor Acre broilers were randomly divided into 4 groups in a 2 × 2 factorial design comprising PCP supplementation (0 or 2 g/kg PCP from d 1 to 23) and LPS challenge (intraperitoneal injection of 1.5 mg/kg body weight of LPS or the same volume of sterile saline at d 22). Our results showed that compared to the non-LPS-treated groups, the treated birds showed a decrease in the ADG, VH, V/C, and the expression of ZO-1, occludin, claudin 1, and mucin2 in the duodenum and jejunum (P < 0.05). However, dietary PCP supplementation significantly mitigated these effects (P < 0.05) except for mucin2 in the duodenum. Furthermore, LPS treatment increased the levels of sIgA and upregulated the mRNA abundances of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, TLR-4, and MyD88 both in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa (P < 0.05). Whereas, PCP supplementation significantly reversed the LPS-induced effects on these genes (P < 0.05) except for the TLR-4 and MyD88. However, LPS did not impact the expression of anti-inflammatory IL-10 in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa (P > 0.05). Briefly, this study implied that dietary PCP supplementation could ameliorate intestinal inflammation and mucosal damage of LPS-challenged broilers, improving broiler performance.Key words: broiler, inflammation, intestinal health, lipopolysaccharide, Poria cocos polysaccharide

INTRODUCTION

Broiler farming occupies a key position in poultry industry in China. In recent years, with the continuous expansion of high-density intensive breeding, the problem of broiler intestinal health has become increasingly prominent. The intensive breeding mode of broilers often lead to immune stress and damage to intestinal health, ultimately affecting broiler production (Zhang et al., 2022). The animal gut is a vital site for nutrient digestion and absorption and is the largest immune organ in the animal body. Furthermore, the intestinal mucosal barrier blocks pathogenic microorganisms and various toxins from entering the body via the intestinal tract (Chelakkot et al., 2018). Therefore, intestinal health directly affects broiler performance and the economy of the poultry industry. Thus, maintaining good intestinal health is a major challenge in modern broiler production.

Poria cocos polysaccharide (PCP), isolated from Poria cocos mushroom kernel, is the main active ingredient of Poria cocos. Previous studies have revealed that PCP exhibits several biological activities, such as immune regulatory, anti-inflammatory, etc. (Ríos, 2011; Lu et al., 2024). Xu et al. (2023) demonstrated that PCP ameliorated antibiotic-related diarrhea in mice by exhibiting immunomodulatory effects via regulation of gut microbiota homeostasis and mucosal barrier.

It is well known that lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a key cell wall component of Gram-negative bacteria, induces systemic inflammatory reactions and severe sepsis in the microbe's host. In the present study, we used an LPS-induced intestinal inflammation broiler model to investigate the influence of dietary PCP supplementation on the intestinal barrier of broilers, aiming to identify the potential of PCP for use as an intestinal protective agent in the poultry industry.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical Statement

The study protocol was approved by the Animal Protection and Utilization Committee of the Henan Academy of Science and Technology (2023HIST041, Xinxiang, China).

PCP Preparation

PCP was provided by Hubei Huisheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China) with a polysaccharide content ≥ 90%.

Experimental Design, Animals, Diets, and Management

A 2 × 2 factorial design was used, with the main factors being LPS (intraperitoneal injection of LPS or saline) and PCP (with or without PCP added the diet). A total of 240 1-day-old male Arbor Acre broilers chosen from the same batch were obtained from Henan Fengyuan Poultry Co., Ltd (Xinxiang, China). They were randomly allocated to 4 groups: 1) Normal group fed with a basal diet (CN); 2) Normal group fed with a basal diet supplemented with 2 g/kg PCP (PC); 3) LPS-challenged group fed with a basal diet (LP; from E. coli, 055:B5; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO); 4) LPS-challenged group fed with a basal diet supplemented with 2 g/kg PCP (PC-LP). Each group included 6 replicates with ten birds per replicate (1 cage per replicate).

The feeding period lasted 23 d. On d 22 of the experiment, the LP and PC-LP groups were intraperitoneally injected with 1.5 mg/kg body weight of LPS dissolved in 0.86% sterile saline, while the CN and PC groups with the same volume of sterile saline. During the trial period, broiler daily feeding and management followed the company's procedures. The basal diet was formulated according to the nutritional requirement standards of NRC (1994) for broilers.

Growth Performance Assessment

During the feeding period, all birds were weighed on d 1, at 8:00 a.m. on d 22, and d 24, respectively, and the feed consumption was recorded. Then, the ADG, ADFI, and F/G were calculated from d 1 to 22. ADG was also recorded on d 23.

Sample Collection

On d 24, after weighing all broilers, 2 birds per replicate were randomly chosen and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Subsequently, the abdominal cavity was opened, the duodenum and jejunum were separated, and 3 cm long intestinal samples were collected from the middle portion of each section. The intestinal contents were rinsed out with sterile saline. The washed samples were then put into 4% paraformaldehyde solution and stored at room temperature for morphological analysis.

Intestinal Mucosa Specimens

The remaining portions of the duodenum and jejunum were collected, and their contents were rinsed with sterile saline. The gut mucosa of the washed portions was scraped with a sterilized slide on an ice bath, placed in RNA-free freezing tubes, put into liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80°C for later analysis.

Morphological Analysis of Duodenum and Jejunum

The specimens fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffinized sections were cut into 5-μm thick sections and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin staining. Then, the sections were placed under a microscope, and 6 fields of view with good orientation were selected for each section. The villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured, and the ratio of villus height to crypt depth (V/C) was calculated.

Quantification of Secretory Immunoglobulin A

0.4 g of the intestinal mucosa specimens were homogenized in 0.86% ice-cold sterile saline at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v) using a homogenizer. Then, the homogenized solution was centrifuged at 4500 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected in 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes and immediately stored at −20°C for further analysis.

The concentration of secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa was quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Mlbio, Shanghai, China) as the manufacturer's guideline.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNAs were isolated from the duodenum and jejunum samples using the Trizol reagent (ThermoFisher, Carlsbad, CA). After detecting their concentration and purity, the RNAs were reverse-transcribed into cDNA and stored at −20°C for quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). The mRNA abundances of gene-encoding cytokines, and tight junction proteins in the duodenal and jejunal samples were detected, and the relative expressions of the target genes were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method. β-actin was used as a reference gene.

Statistical Analysis

The experimental data were analyzed by a 2-way analysis of variance using the general linear models in SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY), with each replicate treated as 1 experimental unit. The main effects included PCP and LPS and their interactions. Multiple comparisons were made using Duncan's method to detect significant differences. The data were expressed as mean ± SEM, and P < 0.05 indicated significant differences.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Growth Performances

Emerging evidence suggests that intestinal status is closely related to animal health and performance, affecting the productivity and achievement of breeding farms (Gu et al., 2022). As shown in Table 1, prior to the LPS challenge, PCP supplementation did not impact the ADG, ADFI, and F/G of the broilers from d 1 to 22 compared to the non-supplemented groups (P > 0.05). However, the LPS-treated broiler exhibited significantly lower ADG than the non-LPS-treated broilers (P < 0.001) and PCP supplementation ameliorated the decrease of LPS-induced ADG, as evidenced by significantly higher ADG of the PC-LP group compared to the LP group (P = 0.001). But, no difference of ADG at d 23 was found between the PC and CN group (P = 0.306). Our previous study also showed that dietary PCP supplementation improved the ADG and health of weaned piglets (Zhang et al., 2024). Another study found that dietary PCP supplementation at doses of 1.2 g/kg positively impacted the growth performance of spotted sea bass (Lu et al., 2024). Our results showed that dietary PCP supplementation alleviated LPS-induced reduction in ADG of the broilers.

Duodenal and Jejunal Morphologies

Intestinal morphology is a critical indicator reflecting the nutrient absorption capacity and health status of the gut. As shown in Table 1, compared to the non-LPS-treated group, the duodenum and jejunum samples of the LPS-treated group exhibited significantly lower VH and V/C (P < 0.001) but no significant changes in the CD (P > 0.05). Furthermore, compared to the CN group, the duodenum and jejunum samples of the PC group exhibited markedly higher VH (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, PCP and LPS had a markedly interactive effect on the VH and V/C in the duodenum and jejunum (P < 0.05), except for the CD. Chen et al. (2020) reported significantly reduced VH and V/C in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum and increased CD in the jejunum and ileum in LPS-treated weaned piglets. Similarly, in the current study, we observed a marked reduction in the VH and V/C in the duodenum and jejunum of LPS-treated broilers, suggesting damaged intestinal morphology. Moreover, PCP supplementation was found to mitigate these LPS-induced effects, indicating that PCP can ameliorate the LPS-triggered intestinal damage and help maintain the integrity of intestinal mucosa.

Duodenal and Jejunal Levels of Mucosals IgA and Cytokines

As shown in Table 2, LPS-treated broilers exhibited significantly higher sIgA protein level and mRNA abundance of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, TLR-4, and MyD88 in the duodenum and jejunum compared to the non-LPS-treated groups (P < 0.05). However, the PC-LP group counteracted the increase of the mRNA levels both in the duodenum and jejunum compared to the LP group (P < 0.05). Interestingly, neither LPS nor PCP treatments seemed to impact IL-10 mRNA abundance either in the duodenum or the jejunum (P > 0.05).

The sIgA is a key immunoglobulin secreted by gut mucosal epithelial cells and primarily acts by aggregating pathogens and enabling their elimination via peristalsis and mucociliary movement (Chelakkot et al., 2018). In the present study, we observed a marked upregulation of sIgA protein, and IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, TLR-4, and MyD88 mRNA levels in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa of LPS-treated broilers and PCP supplementation seemed to mitigate these LPS-induced effects. These findings suggested that LPS stimulation activated the immune response of the animal body, leading to a massive sIgA secretion and cytokine expressions as a defense mechanism. A previous study showed that an upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-8, IL-18, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) and a downregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) in the duodenum of LPS-treated broilers (Rodríguez et al., 2023). TLR4 is a pattern recognition receptor that recognizes LPS or bacterial endotoxins and initiates downstream signaling pathways, such as the NF-κB pathway, mediating the expression of inflammatory cytokines and triggering inflammatory responses in the animal body (Dai et al., 2022). Overall, these findings indicated that PCP attenuates LPS-induced damage to the intestinal mucosa by suppressing inflammation via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway.

Expressions of Mucin 2 and the Genes-Encoding Tight Junction Proteins

The gut barrier is primarily composed of gut epithelial cells and junction complexes, which are the structural basis for sustaining the permeability of intestinal epithelial cells and their barrier function (Chelakkot et al., 2018). As shown in Table 2, the LPS-treated broilers showed a significant decrease in the mRNA levels of ZO-1, occludin, claudin 1, and mucin2 compared to the non- LPS-treated broilers (P < 0.05), whereas the PCP-supplemented broilers exhibited higher mRNA abundances of ZO-1, occludin and claudin 1 in the duodenal mucosa and of ZO-1, occludin, claudin 1, and mucin2 in the jejunal mucosa compared to the non-PCP-supplemented birds (P < 0.05). Finally, compared to the LP group, the PC-LP group exhibited significantly higher mRNA levels of ZO-1, occludin and claudin 1 in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa (P < 0.05). A previous study has found that LPS stimulation downregulated claudin 1, occludin and ZO-1 in the intestinal mucosa (Gu et al., 2022). Our results were consistent with the findings of previous studies, indicating that dietary PCP supplementation upregulates the expression of tight junction proteins and MUC2 in the gut of broilers and alleviates LPS-induced destruction of the intestinal barrier.

In summary, this study revealed that PCP can help maintain the integrity of intestinal mucosa and ameliorate LPS-triggered intestinal inflammation and mucosal damage in broilers, suggesting its potential as a potent intestinal protective agent for use in the poultry industry.
