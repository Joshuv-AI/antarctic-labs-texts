Investigation of the solubility and thermodynamics of Ivermectin in aqueous mixtures of polyethylene glycols 200/400
Authors: Soma Khezri, Reza Ghotaslou, Kader Poturcu, Jafar Soleymani, Elaheh Rahimpour, Abolghasem Jouyban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIvermectin, a poorly water-soluble drug, faces challenges like slow dissolution and variable absorption, affecting its efficacy. While some solubility data exist for ivermectin in mono-solvents and a few binary mixtures, research on its solubility in broader solvent combinations remains limited. This investigation aimed to elucidate the solubility and thermodynamic profiles of ivermectin in binary solvent systems comprising (polyethylene glycol 200 + water) and (polyethylene glycol 400 + water) within a temperature range of 293.2 to 313.2 K. The experimental solubility data were analyzed utilizing various linear and nonlinear models, including van’t Hoff, Jouyban-Acree, Jouyban-Acree-van’t Hoff, and mixture response surface methods. The efficacy of these models was assessed by evaluating their mean relative deviations from the experimental values. Furthermore, apparent thermodynamic parameters, including Gibbs energy, enthalpy, and entropy were calculated using the van’t Hoff and Gibbs equations.Keywords: Ivermectin, Solubility, Thermodynamics, Mathematical analysis

Introduction

Ivermectins (Fig. 1), a class of antiparasitic agents derived from Streptomyces bacteria, exhibit potent activity against arthropods and nematodes. As a prominent avermectin derivative, it is a therapeutic agent for parasitic infections in humans and domesticated animals [1]. The mechanisms underlying ivermectin’s efficacy involve binding to ligand-gated ion channel receptors, including glutamate, GABA, and glycine, thereby inducing parasitic paralysis and death [2]. Due to the differential expression of these ion channel receptors in vertebrate species, ivermectin toxicity is rarely reported in mammals. Additionally, ivermectin’s metabolism is influenced by P-glycoprotein, which limits its neurological toxicity in humans. Genetic polymorphisms in P-glycoprotein or co-administration of P-glycoprotein inhibitors may increase the neurotoxic potential of ivermectin [3]. Besides neurological effects, oral administration of ivermectin can cause mild and transient edema, rash, headache, and ocular complaints [4]. Furthermore, in vitro studies have demonstrated ivermectin’s antiviral properties at high concentrations, prompting explorations into its potential as a treatment for SARS-CoV-2 infection [4]. However, concerns about adverse events, particularly neurotoxicity, combined with the lack of conclusive evidence, preclude the recommendation of ivermectin as a routine treatment for COVID-19 infection at this time.

Ivermectin is categorized as a Biopharmaceutics Classification System class II drug, characterized by its high permeability and low water solubility (0.005 mg/mL). The combination of these properties leads to a slow dissolution rate, which may cause inconsistent absorption and affect the drug’s bioavailability and effectiveness [5]. To address the challenge of improving the solubility of hydrophobic drugs, a range of techniques have been explored, including micronization [6], nanonization [7], using supercritical carbon dioxide [8], solid dispersions [9], derivatization [10], salt formation [11], and cosolvency [12]. These approaches have been designed to enhance the solubility of these compounds and improve their bioavailability [13]. Cosolvency has emerged as the most popular approach for enhancing solubility, largely owing to its convenience and significant contributions to synthesis and purification processes. By mixing a non-toxic organic solvent with water or a primary solvent, cosolvency provides a simple effective means for increasing the solubility of medications [14].

Until today, limited data were reported for ivermectin solubility including some mono-solvents such as water, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, 2-pyrrolidone, triacetine and benzyl benzoate [15] and two binary mixtures of ethanol/methanol + water [16] and 1-propanol/2-propanol + water [17]. Despite its practical applications, the solubility of ivermectin in binary solvent mixtures remains a relatively understudied area, indicating a need for further research and investigation.

Herein, we examined its behavior in two binary solvent mixtures consisting of (polyethylene glycol (PEG) 200 + water) and (PEG 400 + water) over a temperature range of 293.2 to 313.2 K. The study employed a hybrid approach involving the shake-flask method and spectrophotometry, followed by numerical analysis using mathematical models. Additionally, the apparent thermodynamic properties for the dissolution of ivermectin in the investigated solvent systems were estimated using the van’t Hoff and Gibbs equations. The main objective of this work is to expand the cosolvency solubility database for this compound, which is widely used in both industrial and research applications. From this perspective, the novelty of this work was from the fact that no prior studies have reported ivermectin’s solubility in (PEG 200 + water) and (PEG 400 + water) mixtures.

Chemicals and methods

Chemicals

The study employed high-quality chemicals, including ivermectin with a purity of at least 99.9% obtained from Bekir Karliga Deva Holding (CAS ID 70288-86-7, Istanbul, Turkey), and PEG 200 (CAS ID 25322-68-3, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and PEG 400 (CAS ID 25322-68-3, Merck, Germany) with a purity of 99.8%. Additionally, ethanol (CAS ID 64-17-5) with a purity of 93.5% from Jahan Alcohol Teb (Iran), and distilled water with conductivity of < 3 µS cm− 1 (CAS ID 7732-18-5, lab-made) were used to prepare and dilute the saturated solutions of ivermectin before spectrophotometric analysis.

Solubility measurements

To investigate the ivermectin solubility in binary mixtures of (PEG 200 + water) and (PEG 400 + water), a commonly used shake-flask method followed by spectrophotometry measurements was employed over a temperature range of 293.2 K to 313.2 K and atmospheric pressure (≈ 85 kPa) [18]. We previously established the feasibility and reliability of this method [19]. The experiment involved preparation of 2 g from solvent 1, solvent 2 and their mixtures with mass fractions ranging from 1.0 to 0.9 with the intervals of 0.1. For example, to achieve a mass fraction of 0.4 for solvent 1, a mixture was prepared by combining 0.8 g of solvent 1 with 1.2 g of solvent 2, yielding a total mass of 2.0 g. Then, an excess amount of ivermectin was added to mono- and mixed solvents in 2 mL tubes, which were then sealed and placed in an incubator (Nabziran Industrial Group, Tabriz, Iran) on a shaker. The incubator’s shaker ensured continuous agitation of the mixture for a duration previously determined through studies on dissolution rates. To determine the time required for the samples to reach equilibrium (dissolution rate), an excess amount of the drug was added to vials containing three solvent mixtures (typically at mass fractions of 0.0, 0.5, and 1.0). The vials were placed in an incubator shaker at a controlled temperature, and the supernatant was sampled at 24-hour intervals over several days. The absorbance of each sample was measured after appropriate dilution until a constant value—indicating saturation—was achieved. Based on this method, the time required to form a saturated solution of ivermectin in the tested solvent was 72 h. After 72 h, the system reached equilibrium, and the saturated mixtures were centrifuged to separate the solids from the liquids. The clear upper solution was then sampled and, if necessary, diluted using a 30:70% (v/v) ethanol + water mixture to facilitate measurement. The solubility of ivermectin was quantified using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800 model, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) by monitoring the absorbance at 250 nm. The concentration of each ivermectin-containing sample was calculated based on the calibration curve, which had already been constructed using standard solutions of ivermectin in concentrations of 12.5–100 µmol.L− 1. A linear calibration curve was established with the equation Y = 0.0188 Civer + 0.0525, showing strong correlation as evidenced by an R² value of 0.9981. To attain valid data, all measurements were analyzed three times.

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystallinity of ivermectin was analyzed using a Philips PW1730 X-ray diffractometer, which collected data within a 2θ range of 10° to 25° under ambient pressure conditions. The instrument was operated at a current of 30 mA and a voltage of 40 kV.

This means ivermectin prefers non-polar or low-polarity environments, while water is a highly polar solvent, making it less suitable for dissolving lipophilic substances. PEGs, with their lower polarity than water, provide a more favorable environment for ivermectin. Additionally, the dipole moments of PEGs are closer to that of ivermectin, suggesting better compatibility between the molecules, which enhances solubility. Therefore, PEG 200 and PEG 400, with their moderate polarity and solubility parameters, offer a more compatible and efficient medium for dissolving ivermectin compared to water.

Table 3 listed the van’t Hoff model parameters (A and B) along with the corresponding MRDs% for ivermectin in binary mixtures of PEG 200/PEG 400 and water systems. The MRD% values indicated the accuracy of the model predictions compared to experimental data, with values ranging from 0.4 to 3.6%, indicating good agreement between the model and data. Table 4 showed the parameters obtained from the Jouyban-Acree and Jouyban-Acree-van’t Hoff models for ivermectin solubility in the same mixtures, with MRD% values ranging from 5.9 to 7.7 indicating a reasonably good fit between the models and data. Table 5 presented the constants (β1-β5) obtained from the MRS model for ivermectin solubility in the investigated binary mixtures at different temperatures, with MRD% values ranging from 3.0 to 8.0%, suggesting satisfactory agreement between the model predictions and experimental data. The van’t Hoff model is a widely used method for correlating solubility with temperature over narrow ranges. The main drawback of the model is its sole dependence on temperature, requiring a separate equation for each solvent composition. Another model employed was the MRS model, which describes the relationship between solubility and sole solvent composition. A key limitation of this model is that it requires a separate equation for each temperature. Finally, the Jouyban-Acree and Jouyban-Acree-van’t Hoff models as more comprehensive models correlate solubility to both temperature and solvent composition. These models can be able to effectively predict and correlate all collected solubility data across varying temperatures and solvent compositions within a single equation which is a major benefit in computational modeling. Although the van’t Hoff and MRS model demonstrate better performance to the Jouyban-Acree and Jouyban-Acree-van’t Hoff models, the factors discussed above justify the preferential use of these models. Overall, the application of all mentioned models, demonstrates promising results (MRD% < 8.0) in accurately describing and predicting the solubility of ivermectin in the investigated binary solvent mixtures. These models offer valuable insights into the dissolution process and provide a high degree of accuracy in reproducing the experimental solubility data, providing a reliable framework for understanding and predicting the behavior of ivermectin in different solvent environments.

In addition to correlation calculations, the Jouyban-Acree-van’t Hoff model was also evaluated as a predictive model for solubility data. For this purpose, a limited number of data points were used to train the model, including single-solvent solubility at low and high temperatures and binary mixtures (0.3, 0.5, 0.7 mass fractions) at 298.2 K. The remaining data points were then predicted using the trained model. The results showed the prediction MRDs% for the (PEG 200 + water) system at various temperatures were 14.3%, 13.3%, 13.2%, 13.1%, and 13.0 at 293.2 K, 298.2 K, 303.2 K, 308.2 K, and 313.2 K, respectively. For the (PEG 400 + water) system, the prediction MRDs% at 293.2 K, 298.2 K, 303.2 K, 308.2 K, and 313.2 K were 12.3%, 13.0%, 13.6%, 13.2% and 13.8%, respectively.

Computation of the apparent thermodynamic parameters

The thermodynamic properties of ivermectin dissolution in the two binary mixtures at 303.0 K were presented in Table 6; Fig. 6 for visual comprehension. The Table included the ΔG°, ΔH°, ΔS°, enthalpy contribution (ζH), and entropy contribution (ζTS) to the dissolution process. Analysis of the data revealed that as the concentration of PEG 200 and PEG 400 increased in the PEG 200 /PEG 400 + water mixtures, the ΔG° values decreased, indicating that the dissolution of ivermectin became more favorable. A positive ΔH° suggested that the dissolution was endothermic, involving heat uptake. The entropy favorability of the dissolution process was also notable, with positive ΔS° values suggesting a favorable role of entropy. In contrast, negative ΔS° values were observed for some water-rich mixtures in both systems, indicating a disfavored entropy effect. The results imply that dissolution decreases molecular disorder in the system, constituting a thermodynamically disfavored state. Typically, dissolution increases entropy as molecules disperse into the solvent, but in this case, the opposite occurs. This phenomenon can be attributed to the hydrophobic nature of ivermectin, which, when dissolved in water-rich mixtures, induces structured ordering of water molecules around the solute—a process known as hydrophobic hydration. Water forms a rigid, cage-like arrangement around the nonpolar ivermectin molecules, reducing overall entropy [32]. For mixtures with high water content, PEG’s capacity to disturb the ordered network is insufficient, leaving the system more structured than in PEG-rich formulations. As the PEG content increases beyond a mass fraction of 0.6, the entropy change becomes positive because PEG molecules more effectively solvate ivermectin, reducing the need for water structuring and allowing greater molecular disorder. Thus, the negative ΔS° in water-rich mixtures highlights the dominance of solvent structuring effects over dissolution-driven randomness, a typical behavior for hydrophobic solutes in aqueous environments.

Furthermore, comparison of ζH and ζTS allows differentiation between heat-driven and disorder-driven aspects of the dissolution process. The ζH values were larger than the ζTS values (except for PEG 200/400 mass fractions of 0.0-0.2), suggesting that enthalpy was dominant in driving the dissolution process. The complete thermodynamic evaluation suggests that ivermectin’s dissolution in the investigated binary mixture proceeded via an endothermic pathway mediated by dual enthalpy and entropy factors.

The enthalpy-entropy compensation analysis provides a useful tool for understanding the relationships between enthalpy and entropy changes during ivermectin dissolution. By plotting the values of ΔH° and ΔG° for different compositions of the binary mixtures, we can analyze the correlation between enthalpy and entropy changes. Analysis of the enthalpy-entropy compensation plot (Fig. 7a) for PEG 200 + water revealed a non-linear relationship with slope variations, signifying that ivermectin solubility arised from both enthalpic and entropic contributions. In the range of 0.3 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.6, and 0.8 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.9, the plot showed a positive slope, suggesting that ivermectin solubility was primarily driven by enthalpy effect. This was consistent with the idea that enthalpic interactions between ivermectin and solvent components dominate the dissolution process. In contrast, for mixtures with 0.0 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.3, 0.6 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.8, and 0.9 ≤ w1 ≤ 1.0, the plot showed a negative slope, indicating that entropy effects were the main driving force behind ivermectin solubility in these mixtures. The increase in free energy was accompanied by an increase in enthalpy, suggesting that increased disorder or solvation effects play a key role in the dissolution process. Conversely, the enthalpy-entropy compensation plot for PEG 400 + water (Fig. 7b) showed a negative slope for mixtures with 0.0 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.3, 0.4 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.5, and 0.6 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.7, indicating that entropy drived ivermectin dissolution in these mixtures. However, for mixtures with 0.3 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.4, 0.5 ≤ w1 ≤ 0.6, and 0.7 ≤ w1 ≤ 1.0, the plot exhibited a positive slope, suggesting that enthalpy effects were the main factor affecting ivermectin solubility in these mixtures. In regions where the process was enthalpy-driven, the positive slope indicated that stronger solute-solvent interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding between ivermectin and PEG or water molecules) dominate, stabilizing the dissolved state. Conversely, in entropy-driven regions, the negative slope indicated solvent reorganization and increased molecular disorder contribute more significantly to solubility. This may occur when the solvent mixture undergoes structural rearrangement to accommodate ivermectin, such as disruption of water-PEG hydrogen bonds or changes in solvent clustering. Overall, the enthalpy-entropy compensation plot demonstrated that ivermectin solubility was influenced by both enthalpic and entropic contributions, with different driving forces depending on the composition of the solvent mixtures.

Limitations of the study

While the shake-flask method is a routine and straightforward technique for solubility measurement, obtaining reproducible and accurate results requires strict adherence to the following conditions: (i) Temperature fluctuations must be carefully controlled. (ii) All steps must be performed uniformly for each sample, including equilibration time before phase separation, centrifugation duration, dilution factor, etc. (iii) Proper calibration of all equipment used in dilution must be ensured. And (iv) Containers must be thoroughly cleaned to prevent interference between analyte peaks and contamination UV-Vis signals in the analysis.

Conclusion

This work investigated the solubility profile and thermodynamic behavior of ivermectin in two distinct systems of PEG 200 + water and PEG 400 + water over the temperature range 293.2–313.2 K. The results showed that both PEG 200 and PEG 400 containing mixtures exhibited enhanced solubility of ivermectin compared to neat water, and that ivermectin solubility increased with higher cosolvent concentrations and temperature in both systems. The mathematical models employed in this study, including van’t Hoff, Jouyban-Acree, Jouyban-Acree-van’t Hoff, and MRS models, accurately predicted the solubility of ivermectin in the binary solvent mixtures with overall MRD% of < 8%. Thermodynamic analysis revealed that the dissolution of ivermectin in the investigated solvent mixtures was an endothermic process, indicating an increased energy requirement for ivermectin to dissolve in these systems.
