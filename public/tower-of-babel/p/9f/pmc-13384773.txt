A Brief Patient‐Reported Symptom Burden Scale for Diabetic Foot Ulcer: Development and Validation
Authors: Kai Li, Wenhui Dong, Min Huang, Bin Jia, Binglei Li, Yanqun Zhang, Xiangrong Luo, Xiaowen Wu, Rongrong Yan, Xia Jiang, Cuihua Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is associated with substantial morbidity and symptom burden that may be insufficiently captured by wound‐centred documentation. This study developed and psychometrically evaluated a brief DFU‐specific patient‐reported symptom burden scale. A two‐phase design was used. Phase I generated and refined items through literature review, semi‐structured interviews, Delphi consultation, and pilot testing. Phase II was a cross‐sectional validation study in a tertiary hospital in China, using a development sample (n = 160) for item analysis and exploratory factor analysis and a validation sample (n = 200) for confirmatory factor analysis, construct validity testing, and reliability assessment. The final scale included 23 items across six clinically interpretable domains and assessed symptom frequency, severity, and distress. The six‐factor structure explained 64.398% of variance and showed acceptable model fit (χ 2/df = 1.987). Content validity was high (I‐CVI = 0.875–1.00; S‐CVI/UA = 0.923; S‐CVI/Ave = 0.990). Total scores correlated moderately with DFS‐SF scores (r = 0.630). Reliability was satisfactory (Cronbach's α = 0.840; split‐half = 0.923; ICC = 0.983). The scale may support systematic symptom monitoring and symptom‐focused DFU care.

Background

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is one of the most disabling chronic complications of diabetes and is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality [1]. As the number of adults living with diabetes is projected to reach 853 million by 2050 [2], the absolute burden of DFU is unlikely to diminish. Among people with diabetes, the lifetime risk of developing a DFU has been estimated at 19%–34%, and long‐term outcomes remain concerning; in some settings, almost half of patients with DFU may die within 5 years [3]. Beyond delayed healing and recurrence, many patients experience persistent and distressing symptoms that compromise daily functioning, psychological well‐being, and quality of life [4].

DFU‐related symptom burden is rarely limited to a single complaint [5]. Symptoms related to infection, impaired perfusion, neuropathy, skin trophic changes, and wound exudation may coexist with sleep disturbance and emotional distress, with cumulative consequences for mobility, self‐care, and treatment adherence [6]. Research on chronic illness has increasingly regarded co‐occurring symptoms as clinically informative rather than incidental, as they may better reflect functional impact than isolated symptoms [7]. However, in DFU care, the availability of disease‐specific patient‐reported measures that systematically capture DFU‐relevant symptom burden across core experiential dimensions remains limited.

Several patient‐reported instruments have been used to assess the impact of DFU or chronic wounds from the patient perspective. For example, the Diabetic Foot Ulcer Scale–Short Form (DFS‐SF) [8] assesses DFU‐related quality‐of‐life impact, whereas wound‐specific instruments such as the Wound‐QoL [9] and WOUND‐Q [10] evaluate the broader effects of chronic wounds on quality of life, functioning, and psychosocial well‐being. Although these instruments are valuable for capturing patient‐centred outcomes, they were not designed primarily to provide a systematic assessment of DFU‐related symptom burden [11]. In particular, they do not specifically organise DFU‐related symptoms into clinically interpretable symptom domains or consistently assess symptom frequency, severity, and distress for each symptom. Therefore, a brief DFU‐specific patient‐reported symptom burden scale may complement existing quality‐of‐life and wound assessment tools by supporting consistent symptom monitoring, identification of priority symptom domains, and evaluation of symptom‐focused interventions.

The Symptom Experience Model (SEM) offers a useful basis for constructing such an instrument. SEM conceptualises symptom experience in terms of dimensions such as frequency, severity, and distress, and considers how symptoms jointly influence functioning and coping responses [12]. Informed by SEM, we developed and psychometrically evaluated a brief patient‐reported symptom burden scale for patients with DFU. The scale was designed to capture DFU‐relevant symptoms across frequency, severity, and distress, with the aim of supporting systematic symptom monitoring and clinically oriented decision‐making.

Methods

Aim

This study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate a DFU‐specific, patient‐reported symptom burden scale to facilitate systematic symptom monitoring and inform symptom‐focused nursing care and research.

Design

We conducted a two‐phase scale development and cross‐sectional validation study (Figure 1). Reporting followed the STROBE statement [13] for the cross‐sectional component of scale evaluation.

The development procedure of the scale.

Initial Scale Development

Research Team Formation

A multidisciplinary research team was convened for this study, comprising a master's supervisor with expertise in DFU nursing, a nurse manager, two physicians specialising in DFU care, two DFU specialist nurses, and two master's students in DFU nursing.

Literature Review

Item generation was guided by a comprehensive literature review to identify symptom experiences relevant to DFU. A systematic search was conducted in multiple databases, including PubMed, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, NICE guidelines, and two major Chinese databases (CNKI and Wanfang), covering literature published up to September 2024. The search strategy used two key concept blocks combined with Boolean operators: one focusing on DFU‐related terms (“diabetic foot”, “diabetic foot ulceration”, “diabetic foot syndrome”) and the other on symptom‐related terms, including symptom occurrence, clustering, and assessment (“symptom”, “symptom cluster”, “symptom group”, “clinical manifestation”, “symptom scale”, “self‐reported outcome”). The final search structure was (DFU terms) AND (symptom‐related terms), implemented as #1 AND (#2 OR #3 OR #4 OR #5 OR #6). Detailed search sources, search terms, time frame, eligibility criteria, and an example of the full electronic search strategy are provided in Table S1. Eligibility criteria included DFU‐related guidelines, consensus statements, evidence summaries, and original research articles, with a focus on patient‐reported outcomes. Both English and Chinese publications were considered, whereas conference abstracts, news articles, commentaries, newspapers, and study protocols were excluded. Two independent researchers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts to extract symptom concepts, and disagreements were resolved through discussion. The literature screening process is shown in Figure S1.

Semi‐Structured Interviews

The literature review and semi‐structured interviews were integrated using a sequential item‐generation approach. The literature review was conducted first and generated preliminary symptom concepts, which served as the initial conceptual framework for the qualitative phase. Interview transcripts were then coded and compared with this literature‐derived framework. Symptom concepts that overlapped with the literature‐derived pool were regarded as convergent evidence, whereas patient‐reported symptoms that were not captured in the literature‐derived pool were coded as newly emergent concepts. One literature‐derived concept that was not reported in the interviews was retained at this stage to preserve the comprehensiveness of the preliminary item pool before expert evaluation. This process allowed the literature review to provide the initial evidence base, while the interviews verified, contextualised, and supplemented the item pool from the patient perspective.

We conducted semi‐structured interviews with purposively sampled patients with DFU between November 2024 and January 2025. The interview guide was informed by the SEM and explored symptom occurrence, perceived severity, symptom‐related distress, and consequences for daily life. Maximum‐variation sampling was used to achieve diversity in symptom experiences by considering DFU duration, ulcer grade, sex, and age. Interviews were conducted face‐to‐face in a private and quiet setting by trained researchers after written informed consent. Interviews continued until data saturation was reached, defined a priori as the point at which successive interviews yielded no new DFU‐relevant symptom concepts. The interview guide and demographic and clinical characteristics of the interview participants are provided in Tables S2 and S3.

After integrating findings from the literature review and interviews, we generated an initial pool of symptom items. Guided by the Symptom Experience Model, each symptom was assessed across three experiential dimensions: frequency, severity, and distress. Each dimension was rated using 5‐point Likert‐type response options ranging from 0 to 4, with “not experienced” scored as 0. For each symptom, a composite score was calculated as the mean of the three dimension scores; higher scores indicate greater symptom burden. These composite symptom scores were used for subsequent item analysis and factor analysis, from which the final clinically interpretable symptom domains were derived. Additional symptom concepts identified from the interviews and exemplar quotations are provided in Table S4. The preliminary item pool was then subjected to Delphi expert consultation for content review and refinement.

Delphi Method

Two rounds of Delphi consultation were conducted via email between February and March 2025. Experts were recruited from DFU‐related disciplines, including clinical medicine, wound care, clinical nursing, and nursing management. Eligibility criteria were: (1) a bachelor's degree or above; (2) at least 10 years of experience in DFU‐related clinical care, education, or research; (3) an intermediate or senior professional title; and (4) willingness to participate. Experts who did not return the questionnaire by the deadline or provided invalid responses were excluded.

The Delphi package included a cover letter describing the study background and instructions, an item‐rating form, and an expert information form. In Round 1, experts evaluated the initial 56‐item pool generated from the literature review and semi‐structured interviews. Each item was rated for importance using a 5‐point Likert scale, and experts were invited to provide written comments on item retention, deletion, merging, addition, and wording revision. After Round 1, the research team revised the item pool based on quantitative ratings and written feedback. In Round 2, experts evaluated the revised 28‐item version and provided further comments on item relevance, redundancy, and wording clarity.

The Delphi quality indicators included response rate, authority coefficient (Cr), Kendall's W, and the mean and coefficient of variation (CV) of item importance ratings [14]. Items with a mean importance score < 4.0 or a CV > 0.25 were considered for removal. Final item decisions also considered expert comments, item redundancy, patient‐reported relevance, conceptual consistency with the Symptom Experience Model, and clinical interpretability. The Delphi consultation overview, decision rules, and quality indicators are provided in Table S5.

Pilot Test

After the Delphi consultation, the 28‐item preliminary scale was pilot tested in 20 patients with DFU who met the same eligibility criteria as those used in the formal validation study. The pilot test assessed the patient‐facing components of the instrument, including item wording, instructions, response options, completion time, and overall acceptability. Participants completed the scale and were asked to report any unclear, ambiguous, repetitive, irrelevant, or uncomfortable items. Data were analysed descriptively based on completion time, missing responses, and participant feedback. Items were revised if wording ambiguity or response difficulty was reported and were considered for removal if they were consistently judged irrelevant, repetitive, or unacceptable.

Cross‐Sectional Validation Study

Participants and Setting

Between April and October 2025, patients with DFU were recruited by convenience sampling from the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism of a tertiary hospital in China. Eligibility criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a diagnosis of diabetes according to the 2024 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes [15]; (3) DFU diagnosed in line with the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot criteria [16]; and (4) ability to provide informed consent and communicate verbally. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe comorbidities, such as advanced cardiac, hepatic, or renal failure, or late‐stage malignancy; (2) concurrent participation in other interventional studies; and (3) reading, visual, or cognitive impairments that would preclude completion of the questionnaire.

Sample Size and Sample Split

For exploratory factor analysis (EFA), we targeted a sample size of 5–10 participants per item. The preliminary scale contained 28 items; therefore, at least 140 valid responses were required, and a minimum of 156 was planned to allow for an estimated 10% invalid response rate. For confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), recommendations for structural equation modelling suggest a sample size of at least 200 to support model stability [17].

In total, 370 questionnaires were distributed, and 360 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding a valid response rate of 97.30%. To develop and test the measurement model using independent samples, participants were split by recruitment period: the earlier cohort (n = 160) was used for item analysis and EFA, and the later cohort (n = 200) was used for CFA, hypothesis testing of construct validity, and reliability testing.

Measures

Participants completed a demographic and clinical information form and the candidate symptom scale. The Diabetic Foot Ulcer Scale–Short Form (DFS‐SF) [8] was used as an external measure for hypothesis testing of construct validity. As no established DFU‐specific patient‐reported symptom burden scale was available as a criterion measure, the DFS‐SF was selected because it assesses DFU‐related quality of life, a construct theoretically related to but distinct from symptom burden. We hypothesised that higher symptom burden scores would be associated with poorer DFU‐related quality of life. The DFS‐SF was scored using raw item ratings without transformation; higher scores indicate poorer DFU‐related quality of life. The Chinese version [18] has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity.

Item Analysis

Items were screened using three complementary approaches: (1) the critical ratio (CR) method, comparing the highest 27% and lowest 27% of total scores with independent‐samples t‐tests, with items retained when CR (t value) was > 3.00 and p < 0.05; (2) item–total correlation, with items considered for removal if r < 0.40 or p ≥ 0.05; and (3) Cronbach's α if item deleted, with items considered for removal when deleting the item increased the overall α.

Psychometric Evaluation

Validity

Validity was evaluated in five aspects. First, content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI), including the item‐level CVI (I‐CVI) and scale‐level CVI (S‐CVI/UA and S‐CVI/Ave) [19]. Second, construct validity was examined using EFA and CFA to determine whether the observed factor structure was consistent with the underlying theoretical framework [20]. Based on the EFA results, items were refined before CFA to achieve a parsimonious and interpretable model. Third, convergent validity was assessed using composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) [21]. Fourth, discriminant validity was evaluated by comparing the square root of each construct's AVE with the inter‐construct correlations [21]. Fifth, external construct validity was examined using Pearson's correlation with the DFS‐SF, with a moderate positive association expected between symptom burden and poorer DFU‐related quality of life [19].

Reliability

Reliability was evaluated in three ways. First, internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's α [22]. Second, split‐half reliability was examined by correlating scores derived from odd‐ and even‐numbered items. Third, test–retest reliability was assessed in a subsample of 30 participants who completed the scale again after a 2‐week interval; test–retest reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [23].

Data Collection and Quality Control

Two trained master's students explained the study purpose and confidentiality safeguards, obtained written informed consent, and administered the electronic questionnaires face‐to‐face. Standardised instructions were provided, and participants' questions were addressed without leading or suggestive guidance. Questionnaires were reviewed immediately after completion, and participants were invited to rectify missing or inconsistent responses. For test–retest reliability, 30 participants were re‐contacted by telephone after a two‐week interval; the researchers read each item verbatim, recorded participants' responses, and entered them into the same electronic questionnaire system.

This study was approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee [Approval No.: NFEC‐2024‐658]. All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Statistical Analysis

Only complete questionnaires were included in the analysis. Data were exported from the online survey platform, organised in Excel 2021, and analysed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 26.0. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. All statistical tests were two‐sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Phase I: Scale Development

Literature Review and Semi‐Structured Interviews

The literature review identified 62 eligible studies and yielded 48 preliminary symptom concepts. Details of the search strategy and literature screening process are provided in Table S1 and Figure S1. Subsequently, semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 21 patients with DFU until data saturation was achieved. The participant characteristics and additional symptom concepts identified from the interviews are provided in Tables S3 and S4. Comparison of the interview‐derived symptom concepts with the literature‐derived symptom pool identified 47 overlapping concepts and eight additional patient‐reported concepts. After team discussion and integration of overlapping and newly identified concepts, 56 symptom items were included in the initial item pool for Delphi consultation.

Delphi Consultation

The overall valid response rate across the Delphi consultation was 88.46%. In Round 1, 26 experts evaluated the initial 56‐item pool. Seventeen experts provided at least one written comment or suggestion, yielding 64 written comments in total; therefore, the proportion of experts providing written suggestions was 65.38% (17/26). These comments mainly concerned item deletion, item merging, wording revision, and the addition of clinically relevant symptoms.

Across the two rounds, expert authority indicators were high. The judgement coefficient (Ca) was 0.981 in Round 1 and 0.979 in Round 2; the familiarity coefficient (Cs) was 0.954 and 0.950, respectively; and the authority coefficient (Cr) was 0.945 and 0.940, respectively. In Round 1, the CV for item importance ranged from 0.07 to 0.29, and Kendall's coefficient of concordance was 0.296 (χ 2 = 423.222, df = 55, p < 0.001). Based on the predefined criteria and expert feedback, 31 items were removed, three new items were added, and 17 items were revised after team discussion. The revised item pool submitted for Round 2 contained 28 items.

In Round 2, 24 experts evaluated the revised 28‐item version. Six experts provided at least one written comment or suggestion, yielding nine written comments in total; therefore, the proportion of experts providing written suggestions was 25.00% (6/24). Kendall's W increased to 0.398 (χ 2 = 257.951, df = 27, p < 0.001), indicating greater agreement among experts. No further items were deleted after Round 2, and only minor wording refinements were made. The preliminary patient‐reported symptom burden scale for DFU therefore comprised 28 items. The Delphi consultation item‐level ratings, and modification decisions from both rounds are provided in Table S6.

Pilot Testing

The 20 participants completed the 28‐item preliminary scale within 5–10 min, with no missing responses. Participants reported that the instructions, item wording, and response options were generally clear and acceptable. Only minor wording refinements were made to improve clarity, and all 28 items were retained for formal validation.

Participant Characteristics

Of the 370 questionnaires distributed, 360 valid responses were obtained, yielding a valid response rate of 97.30%. Participants were recruited consecutively between April and October 2025. The first sample (n = 160) was used for item analysis and EFA. The second, independent sample (n = 200) was used for CFA, hypothesis testing of construct validity, and reliability testing.

There were no statistically significant differences between the two samples in demographic or clinical characteristics (all p > 0.05), supporting baseline comparability. Detailed participant characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Factor Structure

Exploratory Factor Analysis

An initial EFA was conducted on the 26 items retained after item analysis. Sampling adequacy was supported by the KMO value of 0.804, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ 2 = 2737.277, p < 0.001), indicating that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis. Factors were extracted using principal axis factoring and rotated with direct oblimin rotation, reflecting the a priori expectation that DFU symptom domains would be correlated. Based on eigenvalues > 1, the scree plot, and interpretability, a six‐factor solution was retained, explaining 60.933% of the total variance.

Item retention decisions were informed by statistical performance and clinical interpretability to preserve content relevance. “Social loneliness” showed negligible communality and no salient loading and was considered more consistent with an outcome or consequence than a symptom experience. “Foot deformity” and “restricted ankle/toe joint mobility” showed unstable cross‐loadings and were judged to reflect structural signs or functional limitations rather than symptoms. These three items were therefore removed.

A second EFA was then performed on the remaining 23 items. The KMO value was 0.796, and Bartlett's test remained significant (χ 2 = 2434.618, p < 0.001). Using the same extraction and rotation methods, six factors with eigenvalues > 1 were retained, accounting for 64.398% of the total variance (Table 3). The pattern matrix indicated a clear and interpretable structure: all retained items showed salient primary loadings (|loading| ≥ 0.40; range 0.467–0.954) without problematic cross‐loadings (Table 4). Inter‐factor correlations were modest to moderate, supporting the use of oblique rotation (Table 5). Based on item content, DFU pathophysiology, and team consensus, the six factors were labelled as follows: (1) wound exudation‐related symptoms, (2) sensory disturbance symptoms, (3) lower‐limb ischaemia symptoms, (4) psychological distress symptoms, (5) skin trophic disorder symptoms, and (6) acute inflammatory response symptoms.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

The six‐factor structure was evaluated in the second sample (n = 200). Overall model fit was acceptable (χ 2/df = 1.987; RMSEA = 0.070, 90% CI 0.061–0.080; CFI = 0.918; TLI = 0.903; IFI = 0.919). All standardised factor loadings were statistically significant (0.588–0.973; all p < 0.001).

Given the conceptual overlap between Item 9 (“foot numbness”) and Item 10 (“reduced foot sensation”), the residuals of these two items were allowed to correlate. No further post hoc modifications were introduced. The final model is presented in Figure 2.

Validity and Reliability

Content validity indices suggested a high level of expert agreement (I‐CVI = 0.875–1.00; S‐CVI/UA = 0.923; S‐CVI/Ave = 0.990). Convergent validity was supported, with composite reliability ranging from 0.806 to 0.906 and average variance extracted (AVE) ranging from 0.513 to 0.750 across dimensions. Discriminant validity was also supported: the square roots of AVE (0.716–0.866) exceeded the absolute values of inter‐construct correlations (0.047–0.648). Hypothesis testing for external construct validity showed that total symptom burden scores were moderately and positively correlated with DFS‐SF scores (r = 0.630, p < 0.01), supporting the expected association between greater symptom burden and poorer DFU‐related quality of life.

Reliability indices were satisfactory. Cronbach's α was 0.840 for the total scale and ranged from 0.797 to 0.907 across dimensions. Split‐half reliability was 0.923 for the total scale and ranged from 0.817 to 0.939 across dimensions. Test–retest reliability was excellent: the ICC for the total scale was 0.983 (95% CI 0.965–0.992), and all dimension‐level ICCs exceeded 0.700. Discussion

Principal Findings and Contribution

In this study, we developed and psychometrically evaluated a brief DFU‐specific patient‐reported symptom burden scale. The final instrument comprises 23 items across six clinically interpretable domains: wound exudation, sensory disturbance, lower‐limb ischaemia, psychological distress, skin trophic disorder, and acute inflammatory response. By assessing symptom frequency, severity, and distress, the scale provides a structured representation of DFU‐related symptom experience that may not be fully captured by routine wound‐centred documentation [11].

The main contribution of this scale lies in its specific focus on DFU‐related symptom burden. Existing patient‐reported instruments, such as the DFS‐SF [8], Wound‐QoL [9], and WOUND‐Q [10], are valuable for assessing DFU‐ or wound‐related quality of life, functioning, treatment burden, and psychosocial impact. In contrast, the present scale directly quantifies symptom burden and organises DFU‐relevant symptoms into clinically meaningful domains. It should therefore be viewed as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, existing quality‐of‐life instruments.

The development process was designed to balance patient experience, clinical relevance, and psychometric performance. Item generation integrated evidence from the literature and semi‐structured patient interviews, and the item pool was refined through two rounds of Delphi consultation involving experts from multiple DFU‐related disciplines [19]. The resulting domain‐structured format may provide a standardised basis for describing DFU symptom profiles and examining symptom co‐occurrence patterns in future studies [7].

Psychometric Performance

The scale demonstrated satisfactory psychometric performance and a stable six‐domain structure. In the development sample, EFA using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation produced an interpretable solution with salient primary loadings and limited cross‐loadings, consistent with the expectation that DFU symptom domains would be interrelated. This structure was further supported in an independent validation sample, with acceptable CFA model fit (χ 2/df = 1.987; RMSEA = 0.070, 90% CI 0.061–0.080; CFI = 0.918; TLI = 0.903; IFI = 0.919). Convergent validity was supported by adequate composite reliability and average variance extracted values, and discriminant validity was supported using the Fornell–Larcker criterion [21].

Model modification was kept conservative to reduce the risk of capitalising on chance and to preserve interpretability [17]. The residuals of “foot numbness” and “reduced foot sensation” were allowed to correlate because both items belong to the sensory disturbance domain and have close conceptual overlap. No additional residual covariances were specified, as other potential adjustments lacked clear theoretical justification and might have increased the risk of overfitting.

Hypothesis testing of construct validity using an external measure was supported by a moderate positive correlation with the DFS‐SF (r = 0.630). This finding is conceptually reasonable because greater symptom burden is expected to be associated with poorer DFU‐related quality of life, although the DFS‐SF measures a related but distinct construct. Reliability indices were satisfactory, including internal consistency, split‐half reliability, and test–retest reliability, with a total Cronbach's α of 0.840, split‐half reliability of 0.923, and an ICC of 0.983.

Clinical and Research Implications

Because the scale can be completed within 5–10 min, it may be feasible for use at clinically relevant time points, such as admission assessment, dressing changes, discharge planning, and outpatient follow‐up. By generating domain‐level scores across six clinically interpretable symptom areas, the scale can complement wound‐centred assessments and provide a concise patient‐reported symptom profile for clinical review.

In clinical practise, domain‐level scores may serve as prompts for targeted assessment and care planning rather than as diagnostic labels. Clinicians may use the highest‐scoring domains to identify symptoms that require closer review, multidisciplinary communication, or follow‐up monitoring. For example, if a patient shows a marked increase in the acute inflammatory response domain during outpatient follow‐up, this may prompt clinicians to reassess infection‐related symptoms, wound changes, and the need for expedited clinical review. Repeated assessments may also help document whether patients perceive symptom improvement or deterioration after treatment, thereby supporting longitudinal symptom monitoring alongside wound status.

For research, the scale provides a DFU‐specific, domain‐structured patient‐reported measure for quantifying symptom burden beyond quality‐of‐life assessment alone. It may support studies examining symptom trajectories, heterogeneity in symptom profiles, and mechanisms linking symptom burden to clinical outcomes [9]. The scale may also serve as an outcome measure in trials of symptom‐focused or integrated DFU interventions, enabling evaluation of whether interventions improve patients' symptom experiences in addition to wound status [10].

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be noted. First, participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital using convenience sampling, which may limit external validity. Symptom reporting and scale performance may vary across demographic groups, clinical subgroups, regions, and care settings. Future studies should validate the scale in multicentre and more diverse samples and examine measurement invariance across relevant subgroups. Second, although the six‐factor structure was statistically supported and clinically interpretable, this study did not further examine the relationships among the six symptom domains. These domains may be interconnected through neuropathic, ischaemic, inflammatory, wound‐related, and psychosocial pathways. Future studies should use this scale in larger and longitudinal samples and apply methods such as network analysis or related multivariate approaches to explore domain associations, central symptoms, and potential interaction patterns. Finally, this study evaluated content validity, construct validity, hypothesis testing of construct validity using an external measure, internal consistency, split‐half reliability, and test–retest reliability, but did not assess responsiveness, predictive validity, or longitudinal performance. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether the scale can detect clinically meaningful symptom changes over time, predict subsequent clinical outcomes, and support symptom monitoring, risk stratification, and care planning. In addition, the retest assessment was administered by telephone with researcher entry; potential mode effects should therefore be examined in future studies.

Conclusions

Guided by the Symptom Experience Model and a systematic scale development process, this study developed and psychometrically evaluated a brief DFU‐specific patient‐reported symptom burden scale. The final scale contains 23 items across six clinically meaningful domains: wound exudation, sensory disturbance, lower‐limb ischaemia, psychological distress, skin trophic disorder, and acute inflammatory response. Evidence from exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses supported a stable and interpretable six‐factor structure, and reliability testing indicated satisfactory internal consistency and test–retest stability.

The scale provides a structured way to assess symptom frequency, severity, and distress in patients with DFU. It may complement existing wound‐centred assessments and quality‐of‐life instruments by offering a concise domain‐level symptom profile for clinical review, follow‐up monitoring, and symptom‐focused care planning. Future studies should validate the scale in multicentre and more diverse samples, examine measurement invariance, and evaluate responsiveness, predictive validity, and longitudinal clinical utility.
