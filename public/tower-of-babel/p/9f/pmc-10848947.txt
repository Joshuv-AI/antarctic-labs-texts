Fungal transglutaminase domain–containing proteins are involved in hyphal protection at the septal pore against wounding
Authors: Md. Abdulla Al Mamun, Jun-ichi Maruyama
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Transglutaminase (TG) is a ubiquitous enzyme that crosslinks substrates. In humans, TG participates in blood clotting and wound healing. However, the functions related to the cellular protection of microbial TG are unknown. In filamentous fungi, we previously identified SppB, which contains the transglutaminase core (TGc) domain and functions in hyphal protection at the septal pore upon wounding. Here, we further analyzed the cytokinesis-related protein Cyk3 and peptide N-glycanase Png1, as both contain the TGc domain. All three proteins exhibited functional importance in wound-related hyphal protection at the septal pore. Upon wounding, SppB and AoPng1 accumulated at the septal pore, whereas AoCyk3 and AoPng1 normally localized around the septal pore. The putative Cys-His-Asp catalytic triad of SppB is conserved with the human TGc domain–containing kyphoscoliosis peptidase. Catalytic triad disruptive mutants of SppB and AoCyk3 exhibited septal pore plugging defects. Similar to other TGs, SppB underwent wound-induced truncation of the N-terminal region. Notably, TG activity was detected in vivo at the septal pore of wounded hyphae using a fluorescent-labeled substrate; however, the activity was inhibited by the TG inhibitor cystamine. Our study suggests a conserved role for TGc domain–containing proteins in wound-related protection in fungi, similar to that in humans.

INTRODUCTION

Transglutaminase (TG), a multifunctional enzyme, is known to mediate the cross-links between the carboxamide group of the peptide-bound glutamine side chain and the amino group of the lysine side chain via the formation of an isopeptide bond. TG plays an important role in a range of physiological processes, such as cell differentiation (Sun and Kaartinen, 2018), signaling (Maeda et al., 2019), blood clotting (Hur et al., 2019; Alshehri et al., 2021), and wound healing upon injury (Jiang et al., 2019; Alshehri et al., 2021). However, abnormal regulation of TG is often correlated with pathophysiological conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases (Wilhelmus et al., 2014), autoimmune conditions, such as coeliac disease (Paolella et al., 2022), and cancer (Lee et al., 2018). These pathological conditions are all associated with elevated TG activity; thus, cells modulate rate-limiting levels of catalytically active TG by maintaining the full-length inactive zymogen. Upon the organism’s demand or a particular physiological condition, zymogen undergoes proteolytic cleavage for activation (Ahvazi et al., 2002; Zotzel et al., 2003; Siegel et al., 2008). Notably, the Cys-His-Asp (C-H-D) catalytic triad is critical for TG enzymatic activity, which mediates the acyl transfer reaction between the carboxamide group of glutamine and the amino group of the lysine side chain (Aeschlimann and Paulsson, 1994; Gundemir et al., 2012).

Blood coagulation factor XIII is a member of the TG family. Upon activation by proteolytic cleavage by thrombin (Muszbek et al., 2011), factor XIIIa catalyzes the crosslinking between fibrin monomers to form a polymer with a high molecular mass, which increases the mechanical strength of blood clots and minimizes vulnerability to proteolytic cleavage (Muszbek et al., 2011). Furthermore, substantial TG activity has been detected around wounded fibroblasts, and TG can facilitate the assembly of collagen fibrils and subsequently stabilize them to induce wound healing (Siegel et al., 2008; Jiang et al., 2019). Eukaryotic microorganisms, fungi, do not possess orthologues of the classical (human) TG family but contain proteins such as Cyk3 and Png1 with a transglutaminase core (TGc) domain (Onishi et al., 2013; Hosomi et al., 2016). However, the physiological function of microbial TG, particularly its involvement in wound-related cellular protection, remains largely unknown.

Filamentous fungi under the subdivision of Pezizomycotina of Ascomycota possess a primitive morphological structure known as the septal pore, which mediates cell-to-cell connectivity among flanking cells. Owing to such an interconnected array of cells in fungal hyphae, they are highly vulnerable to the risk of excessive protoplasmic bleeding via the septal pore upon hyphal wounding (Mamun et al., 2020). The Woronin body tethered to the septum is a fungal-specific organelle that plugs the septal pore upon hyphal wounding, thereby protecting the flanking cells from excessive protoplasm loss via septal pores (Jedd and Chua, 2020). Septal pores are dynamically regulated by a range of environmental and physiological factors (Bleichrodt et al., 2012; Shen et al., 2014; Mamun et al., 2020). Considering these dynamic regulations, our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms is limited. In a previous study, we identified several septal pore plugging (SPP) proteins using a combination of bioinformatics, large-scale localization, and functional analysis (Mamun et al., 2023). Notably, SppB containing the TGc domain showed wound-induced accumulation to the septal pore and exhibited function in hyphal protection upon wounding (Mamun et al., 2023).

In this study, we aimed to perform a detailed functional characterization of the total TGc domain-containing proteins in Aspergillus oryzae. Using bioinformatics, we identified AoCyk3 and AoPng1 as the TGc domain-containing proteins in A. oryzae. All three proteins, SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1, exhibited septum or septal pore-associated localization and wound-related protection of flanking cells at the septal pore. The putative catalytic triads of SppB and AoCyk3 were involved in the observed septal pore-related functions. In particular, SppB was cleaved in response to wounding to remove the N-terminal region, which is related to its protective hyphal activity. Finally, we identified in vivo TG activity in the plugged septum of the wounded hyphae using a fluorescent-labeled artificial substrate. Our data suggest that the septal pore is not simply plugged by the Woronin body; rather, protein crosslinking-like activity resembling human blood clotting could be involved in stabilizing the septal plugging.

RESULTS

Identification and phylogenetic distribution of TGc domain-containing proteins

The total TGc domain-containing proteins in A. oryzae were retrieved from the Aspergillus genome database AspGD (www.aspgd.org). Three proteins with the ID AO090023000250 (SppB), AO090102000549 (budding yeast Cyk3), and AO090005001528 (budding yeast Png1) were identified. SppB plays a key role in septal pore plugging upon wounding (Mamun et al., 2023). Cyk3 is involved in the formation and progression of cleavage furrows during cytokinesis in yeast (Onishi et al., 2013). Png1 (human orthologue NGLY1) is the conserved peptide N-glycanase, which cleaves N-glycan moieties bound to misfolded glycoproteins before their proteasome-dependent degradation (Suzuki, 2015; Hosomi et al., 2016).

Domain identification and organization of these proteins were further confirmed using Simple Modular Architecture Research Tools (SMART: http://smart.embl-heide lberg.de/; Schultz et al., 1998). All three proteins contained the TGc domain (Pfam identification number PF01841 and InterPro accession number IPR002931) in the C-terminal region (Figure 1). In addition to the TGc domain, AoCyk3 contains an N-terminal Src homology 3 (SH3) domain (SMART ID: SM000326; InterPro: IPR001452). AoPng1 also has the Rad4 domain (Pfam: PF03835; InterPro: IPR018325), which partially overlaps with the C-terminal TGc domain.

Phylogenetic analysis of TG superfamily. The orthologues were searched using the protein BLAST in NCBI database from the representatives of higher organisms including plant and animal (red colored IDs), and fungi (blue colored ID). Bacterial TGs, which have been characterized in actinomycetes, were labeled with green colored IDs. The clade separation was indicated by the respective color of the branch of the phylogenetic tree. The phylogenetic tree was constructed with Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (Mega).

To identify proteins with sequence homology, the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) was performed individually at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database using SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 sequences as the query in major fungal phyla and outgroups (see Materials and Methods). Orthologue relations were confirmed with reciprocal BLAST using the respective best hits as the query in A. oryzae. Finally, the domain organizations of the individual orthologues were checked using SMART.

Eight catalytically active members, including TG1, TG2, TG3, TG4, TG5, TG6, TG7, and factor XIII, comprise the TG family in humans (Eckert et al., 2014). Bacterial TG (BTG) has been extensively characterized in the actinomycete Streptoverticillium mobaraense (Kashiwagi et al., 2002). Phylogenetic analysis of the TG superfamily identified SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 in three distinct clades, which differed from the typical human TG family and BTG (Figure 1). Notably, the SppB clade was phylogenetically close to the Cyk3 clade, and Cyk3 showed a fungal-specific distribution (Figure 1). In contrast, Png1 is a phylogenetically conserved protein having orthologues in fungi, animals, and plants. Notably, SppB has an interesting phylogenetic distribution; orthologues exist in filamentous fungi, including Pezizomycotina, Agaricomycotina, and Mucoromycota, as well as in vertebrates, including humans (Figure 1).

TGc domain-containing proteins are involved in wound-related septal pore plugging

Wound-induced septal pore plugging resembles human blood clotting, but is spatially different; the former is intracellular, and the latter is an extracellular process. To identify septal pore plugging activity, gene deletions of sppB, Aocyk3, and Aopng1 were performed by the replacement with the pryG marker. Deletion of Aocyk3 caused partial defects in growth and conidiation (Figure 2A). However, the deletion of Aopng1 led to severe defects in growth and conidiation (Figure 2A). Reintroduction of the respective genes into the corresponding deletion strains completely restored the defects in growth and conidiation (Figure 2A), presenting relevant functions in fungal growth.

To evaluate septal pore function in ΔsppB and ΔAocyk3, fungal colonies were subjected to hypotonic shock, which caused hyphal tip wounding (Figure 2B, left). Hyphae protected from excessive protoplasmic bleeding from adjacent cells were counted using differential interference contrast microscopy (DIC; Figure 2B, left). Deletion of sppB and Aocyk3 resulted in a significant reduction in hyphal protection at the septal pore upon wounding (Figure 2B, right). The strain lacking both sppB and Aocyk3 revealed a further reduction in hyphal protection compared with the wild-type control (Figure 2B, right).

Because Aopng1 deletion showed severe growth defects, its role in hyphal protection was evaluated by the development of temperature-sensitive (ts) mutants. Four sites, Glu292, Asp338, Ser326, and Ser332, corresponding to A. oryzae AoPng1, have been previously reported in Neurospora crassa (Maerz et al., 2010; Dieterle et al., 2010; Supplemental Figure S1A). The Glu292, Asp338, Ser326, and Ser332 residues were substituted with Asn (N), N, Phe (F), and Leu (L), respectively, and the variants were expressed in ΔAopng1. The expression of E292N and S332L variants showed severe growth defects, similar to Aopng1 deletion. However, D338N and S326F exhibited normal colony growth at the permissive temperature (30°C) but displayed severe growth defects when grown at 37°C (Supplemental Figure S1B). Filamentous hyphae with regular branching observed in the two ts mutants at 30°C started to lose polarity, generating balloon-shaped hyphal tips and swelling polarly upon shifting the culture at 37°C for 3 h (Supplemental Figure S1C). Furthermore, both ts mutants exhibited a reduced ability to prevent excessive protoplasmic bleeding at the septal pore upon hyphal wounding at a restricted temperature (Supplemental Figure S1D). We then investigated functional relations among the TGc domain-containing proteins by overexpressing AoPng1 in the deletions of sppB and Aocyk3 as well as double deletion ΔsppBΔAocyk3. Overexpression of AoPng1 failed to rescue the septal pore defective phenotypes of the single and double deletions (Supplemental Figure S2, A and B), revealing that the reduced activity of septal pore plugging in the absence of SppB or AoCyk3 cannot be functionally compensated by AoPng1.

Taken together, our results suggest that TGc domain-containing proteins are involved in hyphal protection upon wounding.

TGc domain-containing proteins localize and/or accumulate to the septal pore

As SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 played a role in hyphal protection at the septal pore, we then observed their subcellular localization. The three proteins fused with EGFP were expressed under both native promoters and inducible amyB promoter. The EGFP-tagged constructs were introduced into the corresponding deletion backgrounds. When expressed from the native promoters, SppB and AoPng1 were cytoplasmic under normal-growth conditions (Figure 2C). Additionally, SppB and AoPng1 showed enriched localization at the cell periphery and around the septal pore, respectively, when overexpressed under amyB promoter (Supplemental Figure S3, A and B). Both of them accumulated to the septal pore upon hyphal wounding (Figure 2C; Supplemental Figure S3, A and B). The accumulation of SppB to the septal pore did not occur abruptly but progressively within a few minutes after wounding (Supplemental Figure S3C). The N-terminal disordered region of SppB is essential for its accumulation to the septal pore (Mamun et al., 2023). To determine the regions responsible for the hyphal protective function in SppB, truncation analysis was performed. The expression of both the truncated variants was comparable to that of wild-type (Supplemental Figure S4A), and it was then found that both the N-terminal disordered and C-terminal TGc domain-containing regions were necessary for the hyphal protective function (Supplemental Figure S4B). Additionally, the regions responsible for septal pore accumulation in AoPng1 were identified using truncation analysis. The truncated variants were expressed in the wild-type background owing to the severe growth of the deletion strain. Live-cell imaging revealed that the TGc domain of AoPng1 was critical for its wound-induced accumulation to the septal pore (Supplemental Figure S5, A and B).

In contrast to SppB and AoPng1, AoCyk3 produced an insufficient EGFP signal to detect under native expression; however, under induced conditions, it localized around the septal pore, which did not further enhance at the pore upon wounding (Supplemental Figure S3D). We then analyzed the localization dependency between SppB and AoCyk3 by expressing EGFP-fused SppB and AoCyk3 in ΔAocyk3 and ΔsppB, respectively. Wound-induced accumulation of SppB to the septal pore was decreased in ΔAocyk3, but the localization of AoCyk3 around the septal pore was not affected in ΔsppB (Supplemental Figure S6, A and B), indicating that AoCyk3 is involved in the accumulation of SppB to the septal pore.

Cyk3 shows bud neck localization at the contractile actin ring during yeast cytokinesis (Onishi et al., 2013). We then investigated whether the TGc domain-containing SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 localized to the site of septum formation, which is analogous to yeast cytokinesis. As expected, all three proteins accumulated at the cortical site, the cortical localization was constricted toward the center, and septum formation finally occurred (Figure 2D). We then investigated any abnormalities in septum formation frequency in the single and double-deletion strains. However, septal formation frequency was not affected in ΔsppB. In contrast, infrequent septum formation with increased interseptal distance was observed in ΔAocyk3 (Figure 2E), which showed morphologically abnormal septa (Supplemental Figure S7A).

Structural comparison of SppB, factor XIII, and BTG

The crystal structures of human factor XIII and BTG from Streptomyces mobaraense have been previously determined (Yee et al., 1994; Kashiwagi et al., 2002). Here, we predicted the three-dimensional structures of SppB and its human orthologue kyphoscoliosis (KY) peptidase using AlphaFold2 (Supplemental Figure S8, A and B). The three-dimensional structures of factor XIII and BTG were retrieved from Protein Data Bank (PDB; Supplemental Figure S8, C and D). SppB is composed of three parts, the N-terminal “disordered region” with flexible conformation, “core”, and the C-terminal “β-barrel” (Supplemental Figure S8A). The SppB orthologue, human KY peptidase, also has a β-barrel at the C-terminus, but the N-terminal disordered region is notably short and contains antiparallel β-strands (Supplemental Figure S8B). The structure of the core domain belongs to the α + β folding class. On the other hand, factor XIII is composed of four parts, N-terminal “β-sandwich”, “core”, and C-terminal “barrel 1”, and “barrel 2” (Supplemental Figure S8C). The position of “barrel 2” resembles the C-terminal “β-barrel” of SppB and human KY peptidase (Supplemental Figure S8, A–C). However, apart from the short disordered region at the N-terminus, which is cleaved off during activation (Zotzel et al., 2003), BTG forms a central compact domain that also belongs to the α + β folding class (Supplemental Figure S8D).

However, the relationship among the catalytic triad structures of SppB, factor XIII, and BTG is interesting. SppB has been predicted to contain the Cys417-His464-Asp480 catalytic triad (Figure 3, A and B), which is similar to factor XIII (Yee et al., 1994) and some cysteine proteases (Verma et al., 2016). Interestingly, the positions of the catalytic triad of SppB are conserved among orthologues, including KY peptidase of humans (Figure 3A). In contrast, BTG uses the C-H-D catalytic triad for the crosslinking reaction (Kashiwagi et al., 2002). Strikingly, the secondary structures around the catalytic triad of SppB, factor XIII, and BTG are similar. The catalytic cysteine Cys417 resides near the N-terminal α-helix in SppB, and this α-helix was flanked by multiple β-strands (β1, β2, and β3; Supplemental Figure S8A), which is similar to factor XIII (Yee et al., 1994) and BTG (Kashiwagi et al., 2002). This structural feature has also been identified in the organization of chymotrypsin and subtilisin (Verma et al., 2016), indicating convergent molecular evolution.

The putative catalytic triads of SppB and AoCyk3 are important for septal pore plugging

All the TGc domain-containing proteins participate in either septal pore plugging or proper septum formation. Next, we investigated the function of the putative catalytic triad of SppB and AoCyk3 in hyphal protection at the septal pore upon wounding. Putative catalytic residues Cys417, His464, and Asp480 of SppB were substituted with alanine, and the mutational variants were expressed as either tagged (with EGFP) or untagged in the sppB deletion strain. EGFP fusions of these mutational constructs were expressed similarly to the wild-type SppB (native: Figure 3C). Mutations at the putative catalytic triad did not preclude the accumulation of variants to the septal pore upon hyphal wounding (Supplemental Figure S9A). The involvement of the C-H-D catalytic triad in septal pore plugging was evaluated by hyphal wounding induced by hypotonic shock. The substitution of Cys417 and His464 resulted in a reduced ability to protect the flacking cells, similar to that of the deletion strain, while the mutation at the Asp480 position led to a partial reduction in septal pore plugging activity (Figure 3D).

Based on sequence conservation, five candidates, Cys794, Cys850, His881, Asp889, and Asp896, were initially predicted to form a catalytic triad in AoCyk3 (Figure 3E). The conserved cysteine is comparatively distant (based on AlfaFold2 model) from histidine and aspartic acid and is not conserved in ascomycete yeasts (Figure 3F). The selected sites were substituted with alanine, and mutational variants were expressed in ΔAocyk3. Protein expression levels in the mutant variants were confirmed by western blotting, and the amounts of the variants C794A, H881A, and D896A were slightly lower than that of the wild-type (Figure 3G). Variants C850A and D889A localized around the septal pore, similar to the wild-type AoCyk3 (native: Supplemental Figure S9B). However, the variants C794A, H881A, and D896A failed to localize around the septal pore (Supplemental Figure S9B). As deletion of Aocyk3 showed septation and septal-morphological defects (Figure 2E), we then investigated these septal-defective phenotypes in the mutants C794A, H881A, and D896A, which exhibited abnormal septal localization. These mutants mostly restored the septum formation defects as indicated by the nearly regular length of hyphal cells with inter-septal distance (Supplemental Figure S7B), but the septum morphology was still abnormal (Supplemental Figure S7A). As expected, due to the abnormal septal morphology, the C794A, H881A, and D896A variants exhibited a reduced ability to plug the septal pore to a level similar to that of the deletion strain (Figure 3H).

However, AoPng1 showed sequence divergence at the cysteine and histidine positions of the catalytic triad that is conserved in yeast orthologues and human NGLY1 (Supplemental Figure S10A; Katiyar et al., 2002; Hirsch et al., 2003). AlphaFold2 predicted that Cys272, His303, and Asp305 formed a catalytic triad (Supplemental Figure S10B). Therefore, these residues together with His297 were substituted with alanine. The mutants C272A and D305A led to severe growth defects (Supplemental Figure S10A) whereas H297A and H303A produced normal colonies but did not show septal pore plugging defects (Supplemental Figure S10C).

Our results demonstrate that the putative catalytic triads of SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 play an important role in septal pore plugging, morphologically proper septum formation, and normal growth, respectively.

Wounding induces the cleavage of the N-terminal disordered region in SppB

The active form of TG may be toxic to the cell because aberrant TG activity is associated with diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, where TG induces crosslinking-mediated aggregation of α-synuclein (Junn et al., 2003). Therefore, cells normally maintain TG in the zymogen form, which is then activated by proteolytic cleavage with removal of the N-terminal region (Ahvazi et al., 2002; Zotzel et al., 2003). Mechanical wounding induces cleavage of the N-terminal region of tissue TG2 in humans, which is potentially involved in the wound healing process (Siegel et al., 2008). Here, we investigated the cleavage-mediated processing of SppB, because it has a large disordered region at the N-terminus, which may preclude enzymatic activity by limiting the accessibility of substrates to its catalytic site. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), which creates membrane pores, is used in wound-mediated and stress-related studies in budding yeast (Kono et al., 2016). We tested two separate dilutions of SDS and found partial growth inhibition in the treated fungal colonies (Figure 4A). SppB was tagged with hemagglutinin (HA) at the C-terminus to evaluate the truncation of the N-terminal region upon SDS treatment. Flask-cultured fungal mycelia were treated with 0.010% SDS for 20 min to induce wounding before harvesting. Interestingly, SDS-treated samples exhibited bands at lower molecular weight positions in addition to the full-length band (Figure 4B), suggesting wound-induced cleavage of the N-terminus. BTG, TG2, and TG3 exhibited full-length and single-processed bands (Ahvazi et al., 2002; Zotzel et al., 2003), while SppB produced two additional processed bands (Figure 4B).

Proteases, such as caspases, are activated by self-cleavage-mediated intramolecular arrangement (Boucher et al., 2018). TG employed a C-H-D catalytic triad for protein crosslinking (Gundemir et al., 2012). The cysteine protease family, including papain, also uses a similar C-H-D catalytic triad for its proteolytic function (Zhai et al., 2021). Next, we investigated whether SppB undergoes cleavage via its catalytic activity. First, the nucleophile activity of cysteine was rendered with the known TG inhibitor cystamine, which promotes the oxidation of two vicinal cysteine residues on the enzyme to an allosteric disulfide (Raghu et al., 2015). Interestingly, cystamine treatment prevented cleavage of the N-terminus of SppB (Figure 4C). Next, we evaluated the activity of the putative catalytic triad during wound-induced cleavage. The catalytic triad-disruptive variants mostly inhibited the appearance of bands at the lower molecular weight positions (Figure 4C), suggesting the possible self-cleavage activity of SppB.

We then aimed to identify the cleavage site at the N-terminus of SppB. Proteases generally recognize arginine and lysine for cleavage (Olsen et al., 2004; AhYoung et al., 2019). The N-terminal region was enriched with positively charged residues, particularly arginine (Figure 4D). Conserved arginine residues were selected preferentially, and the selected sites were further cross-checked with the ProP-1.0 server, which predicts arginine and lysine propeptide cleavage sites (Duckert et al., 2004). The selected arginine residues were substituted with alanine (Figure 4, D and E), and the mutated variants were expressed in the sppB deletion. The substitution of Arg338 with alanine mostly inhibited the formation of low-molecular-weight bands (Figure 4E). In addition, the R111A variant partially rendered cleavage activity, whereas the mutations at other positions behaved similarly to the wild-type (Figure 4E). Because none of the single mutations completely precluded cleavage, we employed the multiple mutation strategies. These double and triple mutants, including the Arg338 site, inhibited cleavage of the N-terminus (Figure 4E), suggesting that Arg338 is a potential site for wound-induced processing of SppB. As expected, variants showing inhibition of N-terminal cleavage exhibited a reduced ability for septal pore plugging upon hyphal wounding (Figure 4F), suggesting that N-terminal cleavage is crucial for the septal pore plugging function of SppB.

Detection of TG activity at the septal pore of wounded hyphae

Septal pore-related functions of the TGc domain-containing proteins were thought to be owing to the presence of TG activity at the septal pore upon hyphal wounding. In vivo TG activity was detected at the septal pore using the TG substrate 5-(biotinamido)pentylamine, a biotinylated polyamine, wherein the terminal primary amine (-NH2) can form an isopeptide bond (CO-NH) with the γ-carboxyl group of glutamine present in native substrate proteins upon crosslinking (Figure 5A; Zhang et al., 1998; Hiiragi et al., 1999; Siegel et al., 2008). Streptavidin conjugated with Alexa Fluor 594 was used as a fluorescent probe to detect the biotinylated crosslinked product at the septal pore. TG activity, detected as red fluorescent puncta at the septal pore of wounded hyphae, was colocalized with SppB-EGFP (Figure 5A). Furthermore, the single deletion ΔsppB and double deletion ΔsppBΔAocyk3 exhibited reduced TG activity compared with the wild-type control, whereas the activity was unaffected by the single deletion of Aocyk3 (Figure 5A).

We used another widely used TG substrate, cadaverine (Siegel et al., 2008; Steffen et al., 2017; Oteng-Pabi et al., 2018) conjugated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). The application of FITC-cadaverine is simple because it avoids the use of secondary antibodies for detection. TG activity was detected as green fluorescent puncta at the septal pores of the wounded hyphae (Figure 5B). Similarly, the single deletion ΔsppB and double deletion ΔsppBΔAocyk3 exhibited reduced activity compared with the wild-type control (Figure 5B). To further support the TG activity of SppB, mutants of the putative catalytic triad were analyzed using FITC-cadaverine. The variants C417A and H464A demonstrated a decreased activity at the septal pore similar to sppB deletion (Supplemental Figure S11A), which further supports a relation of SppB with TG activity.

To evaluate the functional relations in the TG activity, AoPng1 was overexpressed in the single deletion and sppBAocyk3 double-deletion backgrounds. The overexpression of AoPng1 rescued the defective TG activity of sppB deletion but failed to rescue that of sppBAocyk3 double deletion (Supplemental Figure S11, B and C), suggesting functional redundancy in the TG activity at the septal pore.

Notably, TG activity at the septal pore was reduced following the order of increasing concentration of TG inhibitor cystamine; at 25 mM concentration (Figure 5C), TG activity was reduced by over 30%, suggesting the possible involvement of TG activity at the septal pore of wounded hyphae.

Septal pore accumulation of several SPP proteins depends on SppB and AoCyk3

Several SPP proteins (Mamun et al., 2023) and a septal pore-accumulating (SpaA) protein (gene ID: AO090020000030), which was newly identified in this study, accumulate to the septal pore upon hyphal wounding, similar to SppB. The molecular mechanism of the septal pore accumulation has not been determined. Because TG activity was detected at the septal pore (Figure 5, A and B), the accumulation could be mediated by TG activity. Furthermore, SPP proteins including SppN, SppC, and SppE as well as SpaA colocalized with SppB at the septal pore of wounded hyphae (Supplemental Figure S12, A–D), and AoCyk3 is involved in the septal pore accumulation of SppB (Supplemental Figure S6A). To evaluate the dependence of the septal pore accumulation on TGc domain-containing proteins, EGFP-fused SppC, SppD, SppE, SppK, SppN, and SpaA were expressed in ΔsppB, ΔAocyk3, and ΔsppBΔAocyk3. Additionally, the accumulation of the proteins was analyzed in the presence of TG inhibitor cystamine. The results showed that the septal pore accumulations of SppE and SppK were reduced in the single/double deletions and cystamine treatment (Figure 6, A and B), while the accumulations of SppN and SpaA were not reduced only in ΔAocyk3 (Figure 6, C and D). In contrast, SppC and SppD still accumulated to the septal pore of the wounded hyphae in all the tested conditions (Supplemental Figure S13, A and B). Our results reveal that the TGc domain-containing proteins are involved in the septal pore accumulations of several SPP and SpaA proteins.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified wound-related protective function of TGc domain-containing proteins in filamentous ascomycetes. Here, we showed a lineage-specific septum-associated function of the conserved peptide N-glycanase Png1. Bioinformatics analysis revealed structural similarities between the conserved catalytic triads of SppB and human KY peptidase, possibly suggesting an unknown shared function. Similar to BTG and human TG2, SppB underwent wound-induced cleavage at the N-terminus, which was necessary for its septum-related function. We identified in vivo TG activity at the septal pore of the wounded hyphae using a fluorescent-labeled substrate. In this study, we reveal that TGc domain-containing proteins are involved in wound-related functions in eukaryotic microorganisms for the first time.

In contrast to human TGs, the physiological function of microbial TG is largely unknown. Candida albicans enolase Eno1, which lacks the TGc domain, exhibits TG activity in vitro (Reyna-Beltrán et al., 2018). However, the localization of A. oryzae Eno1 orthologue was independent of septal localization, even under wounded conditions (Supplemental Figure S14). Ascomycete yeasts have two proteins, Cyk3 and Png1, with a TGc domain. Filamentous fungi, not limited to Pezizomycotina, have evolved a TGc domain-containing SppB, which has orthologues in distant vertebrate clades (Figure 1). Delicate hyphal filaments are vulnerable to mechanical wounding, and therefore, filamentous fungi have conferred the function of wound-related protection on SppB.

Here, TGc domain-containing AoPng1, AoCyk3, and SppB all showed septum/septal pore-related localization (Figure 2C; Supplemental Figure S3, A–C). The hyphal protective function of AoPng1 at the septal pore could be lineage-specific in Pezizomycotina. Yeast Png1 shows N-glycanase activity cleaving the N-glycans from glycoproteins (Hosomi et al., 2016), whereas N. crassa NcPng1 does not exhibit deglycosylation (Maerz et al., 2010). In the PNG1 superfamily, Arabidopsis thaliana AtPng1, which preserves the catalytic triad, has been reported to possess TG activity to date (Della Mea et al., 2004). Pezizomycotina Png1 orthologues, including A. oryzae and N. crassa, commonly have amino acid substitutions in the residues required for deglycosylation activity in yeast and humans (Katiyar et al., 2002; Hirsch et al., 2003; Supplemental Figure 10A). These suggest that the residues placed at the catalytic triad in Png1 orthologues are crucial for determining the presence or absence of deglycosylation activity. On the other hand, why was there a severe growth defect in Aopng1 deletion (Figure 2A)? AoPng1 localized in a crescent-like shape at the hyphal tip (Supplemental Figure 5B). Additionally, ts mutants exhibited hyphal tip swelling and a polar hyphal branching at a restricted temperature (Supplemental Figure S1C), suggesting a relationship between AoPng1 and hyphal morphogenesis. More importantly, TGc domain-dependent accumulation of AoPng1 at the septal pore (Supplemental Figure S5A) and septal pore plugging defects found in ts mutants (Supplemental Figure S1D) suggest a clear functional relevance of AoPng1 to septal pore plugging.

Cyk3 is a fungal-specific protein mainly existing in Ascomycota. The name Cyk3 is used because of its crucial involvement in yeast cytokinesis (Onishi et al., 2013), which is analogous to septum formation in Pezizomycotina. AoCyk3 localization to the site of septum formation (Figure 2D) and the septum formation defects in ΔAocyk3 (Figure 2E) suggested the functional conservation of Cyk3 across Ascomycota. Septal pore plugging defects in ΔAocyk3 (Figure 2B) could be secondary effects that arise due to abnormal septum morphology (Supplemental Figure S7A). Yeast Cyk3 interacts with a cytokinesis-related protein Inn1 and chitin synthase Chs2 via the N-terminal SH3 domain and the C-terminal TGc domain, respectively (Foltman et al., 2016). Cyk3 promotes the formation of the primary septum via the TGc domain, which interacts with and subsequently activates Chs2 function (Foltman et al., 2016; Okada et al., 2020). Additionally, a truncated Cyk3 variant lacking TGc domain or mutants of the putative catalytic triad are defective in the formation of the primary septum (Foltman et al., 2016; Okada et al., 2020). Furthermore, the TGc domain of Cyk3 interacts with Rho1-GTPase and makes it temporarily inactive, thereby inhibiting premature formation of the secondary septum during cell division (Onishi et al., 2013). However, Cyk3 is supposed to be catalytically inactive because of a lack of the conserved cysteine, which is known to form the catalytic triad (Pollard et al., 2012; Onishi et al., 2013). In AoCyk3, our AlphaFold2 prediction suggested a putative catalytic site consisting of Cys794, His881, and Asp896 (Figure 3F). Here, abnormal septum morphology and septal pore plugging defects in the Aocyk3 mutations at the putative catalytic triad (Figure 3H; Supplemental Figure S7, A and B, respectively) could be due to interruption of the interaction with the candidate partners involved in cytokinesis, such as Rho1-GTPase and Chs2 in budding yeast (Onishi et al., 2013; Foltman et al., 2016; Okada et al., 2020). Additionally, the abnormal septum morphology in the mutants (Supplemental Figure S7A) could be accounted for by their failure to localize around the septal pore (Supplemental Figure S9B). Although no contributions of AoCyk3 to the TG activity were found (Figure 5, A and B), it is assumed that a similar catalytic machinery regulates septum morphology formation.

Similar to factor XIII, SppB has the predicted C-H-D catalytic triad, which is conserved among orthologues, including KY peptidase (Figure 3, A and B; Supplemental Figure S8, A and B). KY peptidase has been poorly characterized; its TG activity and three-dimensional structure have not yet been determined. Despite the amino acid compositional variation of TGc domains, the similar organization of secondary structures around the catalytic triad of SppB, factor XIII, and BTG (Kashiwagi et al., 2002; Yee et al., 1994; Supplemental Figure S8A), as well as protease chymotrypsin and subtilisin (Verma et al., 2016), represent convergent molecular evolution. However, the reduced septal pore plugging activity by amino acid substitutions at the putative catalytic positions of SppB suggested the possible involvement of TG activity in septal pore plugging (Figure 3D).

The flexible N-terminal region in BTG, human TG, and SppB (Supplemental Figure S8) could hinder substrate accessibility to the active site; therefore, mature TG emerges by cleaving this region. Autoinhibition protects cells and organisms from aberrant protein crosslinking, which probably leads to toxic effects and is associated with diseases. Wound-induced cleavage of the N-terminal region in SppB is reminiscent of wound-related activation, similar to that of human TG2 and TG3 (Ahvazi et al., 2002; Siegel et al., 2008). The sequence around the putative cleavage site (Arg338 in Figure 4D) of SppB has a similarity with that of the cleavage site of BTG (Zotzel et al., 2003; Figure 4D), strengthening the notion of convergent evolution. To identify the cleavage site involved in TG activity, enzymatic analyses using the truncated form will be required. Importantly, however, septal pore plugging defects were observed in mutants that failed to undergo N-terminal processing (Figure 4, E and F). The N-terminal disordered region was required for septal pore accumulation (Mamun et al., 2023; Figure 2C) and the septal pore plugging activity (Supplemental Figure S4B). It is hypothesized that the N-terminal disordered region recruits SppB to the septal pore and then undergoes the cleavage, thereby facilitating the crosslinking reaction. After hyphal wounding, Woronin bodies could be mechanically sensed by the increased cytoplasmic velocity at the septal pore and then plug it. However, Woronin body-mediated plugging would not be stable without chemical linkages to the membrane around the pore. Therefore, TG-based protein crosslinking could support and stabilize Woronin body-mediated plugging, which might involve the activity of septal pore-accumulating SppB.

Here, the artificial TG substrates 5-(Biotinamido)-pentylamine and FITC-cadaverine, containing terminal amines, which act as acyl acceptors, were used to evaluate TG activity. Both of them are less permeable to cells (Waller and Lichstein, 1967; Samartzidou and Delcour, 1999). TG activity in vivo is generally detected in permeabilized section pieces of tissue (Zhang et al., 1998; Siegel et al., 2008). In our study, TG activity was detected only at the septal pore of the wounded hyphal cells (Figure 5, A and B). sppB deletion resulted in a reduced activity (Figure 5, A and B), and a reduced activity in the sppB mutants at the putative catalytic triad (Supplemental Figure S11A) further supports a relation of SppB with TG activity. Taken together with the defect in septal pore plugging in the same mutants (Figure 3D), these results strengthen the notion that TG activity of SppB is functionally involved in the septal pore plugging. Additionally, overexpression of AoPng1 rescued the impaired TG activity in ΔsppB (Supplemental Figure S11B), and two of the predicted catalytic residues in AoPng1 were functionally important in normal growth (Supplemental Figure S10A). These results suggest that the remaining activity might be attributed to that of the septal pore-accumulating AoPng1. As the mutation of the residue His303 at the AlphaFold2-predicted catalytic triad unexpectedly did not exhibit any defects in growth and septal pore plugging (Supplemental Figure S10, A and C), experimental investigation of protein structures will be required to reveal enzymatic characteristics of TG activity in AoPng1. Although deletion of Aocyk3 did not show a defect in TG activity, the activity of the double deletion ΔsppBΔAocyk3 appeared to be lower than that of the single deletion ΔsppB (Figure 5, A and B). This is related with the result that overexpression of AoPng1 rescued the defective TG activity in ΔsppB but not in ΔsppBΔAocyk3 (Supplemental Figure S11, B and C), suggesting a possible contribution of AoCyk3 to TG activity. A reduction of TG activity in the presence of the TG inhibitor cystamine strengthens our results (Figure 5C). As a higher concentration (25 mM) of the TG inhibitor cystamine did not completely suppress this activity (Figure 5C), abnormally higher concentrations may be required for complete inhibition as reported for yeast TG inhibition (Reyna-Beltrán et al., 2018). Despite the identification of TG activity in vivo, this study lacks in vitro-based direct evidence of TG activity related to individual TGc domain-containing proteins. Besides SppB and AoPng1, a number of SPP proteins accumulated to the septal pore upon hyphal wounding (Mamun et al., 2023). Here, we demonstrated that TGc domain-containing SppB and AoCyk3 were involved in the septal pore accumulations of several SPP and SpaA proteins (Figure 6). TG substrate determination will help explain how these proteins accumulate to the septal pore.

Using bioinformatics, genetics, and biochemical approaches, we identified that TGc domain-containing proteins play an important role in hyphal protection against wounding. In this study, we suggest that eukaryotic microorganisms could employ biochemical machinery similar to TG-mediated wound healing in higher animals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains, media, growth conditions, and fungal transformation

The strains used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table S1. The transformation of A. oryzae was performed as previously described (Maruyama and Kitamoto, 2011). A. oryzae strain NSPlD1 (niaD− sC− ΔpyrG ΔligD; Mamun et al., 2020) was used as the parent strain. Using the pyrG selectable marker, transformants were selected using M+Met medium (0.2% NH4Cl, 0.1% (NH4)2SO4, 0.05% KCl, 0.05% NaCl, 0.1% KH2PO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, 0.002% FeSO4·7H2O, 0.15% methionine, and 2% glucose; pH 5.5). Transformants with niaD and sC selectable markers were selected using CD medium (0.3% NaNO3, 0.2% KCl, 0.1% KH2PO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, 0.002% FeSO4·7H2O, and 2% glucose; pH 5.5) and CD+Met medium supplemented with 0.0015% methionine, respectively. A. oryzae strains were maintained on Potato Dextrose (PD) plates (Nissui, Tokyo, Japan) at 30°C. For transformation, the strains were inoculated into dextrin, peptone, and yeast extract (DPY) liquid medium (0.5% yeast extract, 1% hipolypeptone, 2% dextrin, 0.5% KH2PO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O; pH 5.5) and cultured overnight with constant shaking. For microscopy, conidia were inoculated on glass base dishes containing CD medium supplemented with 1% casamino acids.

Plasmid construction and strain generation

Generating sppB, Aocyk3, and Aopng1 deletions and sppBAocyk3 double deletion.

The primers used for the generation of the deletion strains are listed in Supplemental Table S2. Replacement of individual genes with the Orotidine-5′-phosphate decarboxylase (pyrG) marker was performed by amplifying 1.5 kb upstream and 1.5 kb downstream of the gene of interest using the primer sets gene-upstream1_F/gene-upstream2_R and gene-downstream1_F/gene-downstream2_R, respectively with PrimeSTAR HS DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa, Otsu, Japan) for high-fidelity PCR. The pyrG marker was amplified from RIB40 genomic DNA using the primers PyrG_F and PyrG_R. The three amplified DNA fragments (upstream and downstream regions of the targeted genes and pyrG) and linearized-pUC19 vector were fused using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Clontech Laboratories, Mountain View, CA). Deletion constructs were further amplified using the primer set gene-upstream1_F/gene-downstream2_R from the resultant plasmid, and then introduced into the native locus of the A. oryzae strain NSPlD1 (Mamun et al., 2020) by homologous recombination. Transformants were selected using uridine/uracil prototrophy.

To generate sppBAocyk3 double deletion, 1.5 kb upstream and 1.5 kb downstream of Aocyk3 were amplified from RIB40 genomic DNA using the primer sets Aocyk3-upstream1_F/Aocyk3-upstream2_R and Aocyk3-downstream1_F/Aocyk3-downstream2_R, respectively; the sC marker was amplified separately. The three amplified DNA fragments (upstream and downstream regions of the targeted genes and sC) and linearized-pUC19 vector were fused. Deletion constructs were further amplified from the resulting plasmid using the primer set Aocyk3-upstream1_F/Aocyk3-downstream2_R. PCR-amplified deletion constructs of Aocyk3 were introduced into the native loci of ΔsppB.

Expression of SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 with and without EGFP fusion.

For complementation, the DNA fragments from 1.4 kb upstream to the stop codon and 0.6 kb downstream of the respective genes were amplified separately from RIB40 genomic DNA using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S2. EGFP encoding sequence was amplified from pUt-EGFP (Mamun et al., 2020) using the primers EGFP_F and EGFP_R. The amplified fragments (upstream + open reading frames [ORF], downstream, and EGFP/or without EGFP fusion) were fused with the SmaI-linearized pUXN (Mamun et al., 2020) vector, yielding pUSppBN-EGFP, pUAoCyk3N-EGFP, pUAoPng1N-EGFP, pUSppBN, pUAoCyk3N, and pUAoPng1N. The resulting plasmids were digested with NotI and introduced into the niaD locus of the corresponding deletion strains by homologous recombination. For a negative control, NotI-digested pUXN vector was introduced into the niaD locus of the individual deletion strains.

For expression of SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 under the control of the inducible amyB promoter, respective ORFs were amplified from RIB40 genomic DNA using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S2. The amplified fragments were then fused with SmaI-linearized pUt-EGFP (Mamun et al., 2020) using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit to yield pUt-SppBEGFP, pUt-AoCyk3EGFP, and pUt-AoPng1EGFP. The recombinant plasmids were digested with NotI and introduced into the niaD locus of the corresponding deletion strains by homologous recombination.

For the truncation of AoPng1, fragments encoding residues 1–204aa (1–612bp), 1–283aa (1–849 bp), and 284–557aa (852–1671 bp) were amplified from RIB40 genomic DNA using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S2. A start codon was added for the truncated variant lacking the N-terminal region (284–557aa). Individual fragments were fused with SmaI-linearized pUt-EGFP. The recombinant plasmids were digested with NotI and inserted into the niaD locus of the strain NSlD1 by homologous recombination.

Construction of mutational variants of AoPng1, SppB, and AoCyk3.

To generate ts mutants of AoPng1, the sites Glu292 (874–876 bp), Asp338 (1015–1017 bp), Ser326 (976–978 bp), and Ser332 (994–996 bp) were selected based on previous studies (Dieterle et al., 2010; Maerz et al., 2010). To construct ts mutants, customized primers containing 15 bp overlapping nucleic acid sequences bearing the substituted codons of the desired mutations (E292N, D338N, S326F, and S332L) were used to amplify the flanking ORF fragment before and after the targeted sites. The most frequently used codons in A. oryzae were found at www.kazusa.or.jp.

To construct the catalytic triad disruptive mutants of SppB, Cys417 (12489–1251 bp), His464 (1390–1392 bp), and Asp480 (1441–1443 bp) sites were selected. To construct catalytic triad disruptive mutants of AoCyk3, Cys794 (2380–2382 bp), Cys850 (2548–2550 bp), His881 (2641–2643 bp), Asp889 (2665–2667 bp), and Asp896 (2686–2688 bp) were selected. To construct variants with substitutions in the N-terminal region of SppB, Arg90 (274–276 bp), Arg111 (331–333 bp), Arg157 (469–471 bp), Arg172 (514–516 bp), Arg205 (613–615 bp), Arg302 (904–906 bp), Arg305 (913–915 bp), and Arg338 (1015–1017 bp) were selected. Selected sites of SppB and AoCyk3 were substituted with alanine. Similarly, customized primers containing 15 bp overlapping nucleic acid sequences, which bear the substituted codon (Ala: GCC) of desired mutations, were used to amplify the flanking ORF fragment before and after the targeted sites using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S2. To develop double (R111A/R172R, R111A/R338A, R172A/R338A, and R305A/R338A in SppB) or triple mutants (R90A/R111A/R172A, R172A/R111A/R205A, and R205A/R305A/R338A), flanking ORF fragments before and after the target sites were amplified from the corresponding previously developed single or double mutation-bearing plasmids. Three DNA fragments including the upstream/downstream flanking ORF fragments and SmaI-linearized pUt-NAN (Mamun et al., 2020) were fused. In the case of catalytic triad-disruptive mutants, the DNA fragments were also fused with SmaI-linearized pUt-EGFP. Furthermore, sppB fragments related to mutations in the putative catalytic triad and N-terminal regions were inserted into pUt-NAHA. The resultant plasmids were digested with NotI and introduced into the niaD locus of the corresponding deletion strain by homologous recombination.

Expression of EGFP-labeled SPP and SpaA proteins.

The ORF of spaA gene was amplified from the RIB40 genomic DNA using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S2 and then fused with pUt-EGFP with the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit, yielding pUt-SpaA-EGFP. The ORF of sppB gene was amplified from the RIB40 genomic DNA using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S2. Overexpression of AoPng1.

The ORF of Aopng1 was amplified from RIB40 genomic DNA using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S2. The amplified fragments were then fused with SmaI-linearized pUt-NAN (Mamun et al., 2020) using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit to yield pUt-NPng1AN. Fluorescent microscopy and image processing

Living hyphae expressing EGFP and mCherry-tagged proteins or staining with FITC dye and Alexa Fluor 594 were observed using an IX71 inverted frame confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with 100 × Neofluar objective lenses (1.40 numerical aperture); 488- (Furukawa Electric, Tokyo, Japan) (FITC, EGFP) and 561-nm (Melles Griot, Rochester, NY, USA) (Alexa Fluor 594, mCherry) semiconductor lasers, GFP filters (Nippon Roper, Tokyo, Japan), a CSU22 confocal scanning system (Yokogawa Electronics, Tokyo, Japan), and an Andor iXon cooled digital CCD camera (Andor Technology PLC, Belfast, UK). Images were analyzed using Andor iQ 1.9 software (Andor Technology PLC) and exported as TIFF files.

To quantify fluorescence intensity, images from EGFP-fused SPP proteins were captured during a single session under identical microscopy conditions from live hyphae. The fluorescence intensity was measured by drawing a single transverse line covering the septal pore region using the plot-profile tool in ImageJ.

Hyphal staining with Alexa Fluor 594, FITC, and calcofluor white

The hyphae were grown on a thin layer of CD agar medium in glass bottom dishes (Iwaki Glassware Co., Tokyo, Japan) at 30°C for 18 h. Fungal colonies were flooded with 100 µl of water containing 40 μM EZ-Link Pentylamine-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) and incubated at room temperature for 45 min. The solution was removed with tissue paper and washed with water. The colonies were then submerged in Alexa Fluor 594 streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) at a dilution of 1:100 for 45 min at room temperature and subsequently washed with water for microscopic analysis.

In addition, 5-FITC cadaverine was used to detect TG activity because of its simple application. After growing the fungal colony on a thin layer of CD agar medium for 18 h, it was flooded with 100 µl of water containing 20 μM 5-FITC cadaverine (AnaSpec, CA) and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Before observation, the treated colonies were washed with water.

For calcofluor white staining, after growing the fungal colony on a thin layer of DPY agar medium for 18 h, it was pretreated with 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) for 1 min, as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. The KOH solution was removed by blotting with paper, then two drops of 1 g/L calcofluor white staining solution with 0.5 g/L Evans blue (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) were added before being observed with the inverted DIC microscope (IX71, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Inhibitory treatment with cystamine

To inhibit TG activity with cystamine, after growing the fungal colony on CD agar medium for 18 h, it was flooded with water containing 20 μM 5-FITC cadaverine and various concentrations (0, 5, 10, and 25 mM) of cystamine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MA) and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. To inhibit TG activity in flask culture, after growing the fungal colony on DPY liquid medium for 3 h, it was preincubated with 25 mM cystamine and treated with 0.010% SDS.

Western blotting and immunoprecipitation

The strains were cultured in DPY liquid medium for 18 h in the presence or absence of 0.010% SDS. The fungal mycelia were harvested using liquid nitrogen and subsequently ground using a multi-bead shocker. After extraction, cell lysates were incubated with the buffer containing detergent NP-40 [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 1 × Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma, St. Louis, MO: P8215), 1% NP-40] to solubilize the membrane-incorporated proteins. The mycelial extract was solubilized with the buffer and incubated on ice for 20 min. After centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected. The protein sample with 12 µg for each sample was separated using 10% SDS–PAGE and then transferred to Immobilon-P polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA) using a semidry blotting system (Nihon Eido, Tokyo, Japan). To detect EGFP, Living Colors A.V. monoclonal antibody (catalogue# 632380, 1:2,000, Clontech) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (catalogue# 7076S, 1:2,000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) were used as primary and secondary antibodies, respectively.

For immunoprecipitation of HA-tagged proteins, cell lysates were incubated with Anti-HA magnetic agarose (catalogue# M-180-10, MBL, Nagano, Japan) for 3 h at 4°C at axial rotation. Purified proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. For the detection of HA, Anti HA-tag monoclonal antibody (catalogue# M180-3, 1:2000, MBL, Nagano, Japan) and HRP-linked anti-mouse IgG (catalogue# 7076S, 1:2,000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) were used as primary and secondary antibodies, respectively.

Chemiluminescence was detected using a Western Lightning-ECL system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) and LAS-4000 image analyzer (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK).

Bioinformatics analysis

Proteins were analyzed using a simple modular architecture research tool (SMART: http://smart.embl-heide lberg.de/) to detect the predicted domains (Schultz et al., 1998). To analyze primary sequence conservation, multiple sequence alignments were performed using ClustalW (Thompson et al., 2002). The secondary structures of the analyzed proteins were predicted using the protein-fold recognition server Phyre2 (Kelley et al., 2015). Three-dimensional structures of SppB, factor XIII, and BTG were predicted with AlphaFold2 (Mirdita et al., 2022) and Chimera 1.16 to visualize and analysis of molecular structures.

SppB, AoCyk3, and AoPng1 were analyzed as queries using the BLAST in fungal subphyla within Ascomycota (Taphrinomycotina, Saccharomycotina, and Pezizomycotina), Basidiomycota (Agaricomycotina, Pucciniomycotina, and Ustilaginomycotina), and in representatives of early diverging lineages, Mucoromycota, Zoopagomycota, Chytridiomycota, Blastocladiomycota, and Cryptomycota (Rozellomycota). A BLAST search was also performed in outgroups including Vertebrata and Angiospermae. Orthologue relations were additionally confirmed with reciprocal BLAST (Ward and Moreno-Hagelsieb, 2014; Hernández-Salmerón and Moreno-Hagelsieb, 2020) using the respective best hits as the query in A. oryzae. Phylogenetic trees for the analyzed proteins were constructed using Mega X (Kumar et al., 2016).

Statistical analysis

The results of at least three independent experiments are presented as the mean. Error bars represent SDs as indicated in the Figure Legends. Statistical significance was tested with a two-tailed Student’s t test using Microsoft Excel, with significance indicated as ns: not significant p > 0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.
