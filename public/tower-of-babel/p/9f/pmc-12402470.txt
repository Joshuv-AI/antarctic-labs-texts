Rational design of a multi epitope vaccine against Salmonella typhi via subtractive proteomics, reverse vaccinology and molecular modeling
Authors: Aqel Albutti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalmonella enterica subsp. enterica serotype Typhi (Salmonella typhi) is the cause of typhoid fever, a severe public health issue in impoverished countries with inadequate sanitation. Despite the availability of therapies, infection rates remain high, underscoring the critical need for an effective and long-lasting vaccine. In this study, we used an integrated in silico strategy to develop a multi-epitope vaccine for 122 S. Typhi strains. A core proteome study identified 2,637 conserved proteins, while subtractive proteomics discovered three non-homologous, virulent, antigenic, and non-allergenic proteins: major curlin subunit, outer membrane protein A, and a hypothetical protein. Four B-cell and ten T-cell epitopes (four HTL and six CTL) were predicted and chosen for vaccine development using immunoinformatics methods. In order to improve immunogenicity, these epitopes were adjuvanted with human beta-defensin-2 and linked by suitable linkers in the final vaccine design. Molecular docking demonstrated binding energies of -305.76 kcal/mol (TLR4), -254.28 kcal/mol (MHC-I), and − 270.85 kcal/mol (MHC-II), confirming stable interactions of the vaccine with TLR4 and MHC class I and II molecules. Molecular dynamics simulations showed that the vaccine-receptor complexes were structurally stable and compact. A robust and long-lasting immune response was also suggested by an immunological simulation study, which showed increased numbers of memory B and T cells, IL-2, and IFN-γ. Together, these results show how computational pipelines can speed up the development of bacterial vaccines and support the multi-epitope vaccine’s potential as a viable option for typhoid fever prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15254-8.Keywords: Salmonella Typhi, Typhoid fever, Multi-epitope vaccine, Core proteomics, Subtractive proteomics, Molecular modeling, MD simulationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology

Introduction

Typhoid fever poses a significant global health problem, particularly in areas with poor sanitation and limited access to medical care1. Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi (S. Typhi) is estimated to be responsible for over 110,000 deaths annually, with South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa being the most affected regions2,3. Salmonella Typhi is a facultative intracellular pathogen capable of both extracellular and intracellular phases within a host4. This flexibility and adaptability allow S. Typhi to survive immune responses through a variety of immune evasion mechanisms. There are several inherited units of virulence factor traits that are manipulated for effective treatment, including the mechanisms of immune evasion, such as type III secretion systems or capsules that allow evasion of detection from the immune system, posing significant challenges to macrophages5,6. Altering these traits of the pathogen might offer efficient ways to combat it through vaccines or designed therapies7–9.

Previous studies provide evidence that first-line antibiotics like ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole were administered to treat typhoid fever with great success10. Despite the fact that antibiotics remain the backbone of typhoid therapy with ciprofloxacin, azithromycin, and ceftriaxone, the rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) continues to undermine treatment efficacy11. In South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, the appearance of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) and multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains of S. enterica has worsened disease duration, treatment costs, and mortality12. These constraints stress the need for new, long-lasting, and cross-protective vaccine strategies. With two WHO pre-qualified vaccines (Typbar-TCV and Vi-polysaccharide), there is a requirement for new innovative vaccine candidates because of their inability to confer robust, long-lasting immunity, cross-protect NTS serovars, and reduced efficacy in children under two years that demands more strain breadth and durable immune responses13.

Regarding this, immunoinformatics offers a different approach by allowing for the rapid and targeted selection of vaccine candidates without the need to grow the pathogen. This computational pipeline has shown success in vaccine design against Shigella, Chlamydia, Streptococcus suis, and Neisseria meningitidis, demonstrating its versatility and reliability11,12,14. This study intends to use an integrated approach of core proteomic screening, subtractive genomics, epitope mapping, along with structural bioinformatics, to design a multi-epitope subunit vaccine for S. Typhi computationally. The goal is to engineer a vaccine candidate that is immunologically robust and structurally sound to respond to the rising public health concern of antimicrobial resistance and limitations of existing typhoid vaccines15,16.

Methodology

Core proteome retrieval

The complete proteome information of 122 strains of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhi was retrieved from the GenBank database in order to provide a comprehensive genetic coverage of the pathogen17. The Pan-genome Explorer (PGEx), which allows users to examine the core, accessory, and unique genes of various bacterial strains, was used to investigate the proteomes18. This analysis’s main goal was to find core proteins, or those that are conserved and found in all 122 strains, as these are excellent targets for the creation of broad-spectrum vaccines. By concentrating on proteins that are consistently present in all strains, the Pan-genome Explorer made it possible to eliminate highly variable or strain-specific proteins, which are less appropriate for vaccine creation because of their low capacity for cross-protection. The discovery of these conserved proteins was a critical step in evaluating prospective vaccine candidates with the best chance of eliciting a universal immune response against a variety of clinical isolates of S. Typhi.

Vaccine candidate prioritization

The Database of Essential Genes (DEG) was utilized to identify essential genes for bacteria’s survival using BLASTp, with an E-value cutoff of 1e − 4, ensuring the preservation of all species19. In order to find proteins that are not similar to humans, the study used BLASTp with an E-value of 10 − 4, comparing them to the Human Proteome database from the National Center for Biotechnology Information20. Following the exclusion of proteins with notable sequence similarity, only non-homologous proteins remained for additional study. The harmful effects of pathogenic proteins must be considered during the vaccine design process. Pathogenic proteins were identified using the Virulent Factor Database (VFDB)21. The pathogenic protein VFDB homologs had bit scores above 100 and identities exceeding 30%. The VaxiJen and AllerTop servers were used to evaluate the protein’s antigenicity and allergenicity22,23. A higher antigenicity score indicates that the protein has a greater potential to act as a protective antigen. Protparam server was used to analyze the stability of the vaccine24. The TMHMM server was used to determine how many transmembrane helices were present in the chosen proteins25. Proteins with zero or one transmembrane helix were selected for further examination.

Epitopes prediction and selection

The study generated predictions for B-cell and T-cell epitopes during the epitope selection process, identifying CTL and HTL epitopes using MHC-I and MHC-II binding tools from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB)26. IEDB was utilized to analyze the MHC-I binding of selected proteins, including 18 HLA-A, 32 HLA-B, and 20 HLA-C alleles, focusing mainly on 9-mer epitopes for MHC-I. The study used a consensus methodology for MHC-II epitopes, involving 27 HLA alleles and 15-mer peptide lengths. Epitopes with a binding ability score greater than two were selected. The ABCpred tool was utilized for predicting B-cell epitopes using recurrent neural network algorithms, with a 16-amino acid long epitope threshold of 0.51 for prediction27.

The study used VaxiJen v.2.0 for antigenicity evaluation (0.4 threshold), Toxin Predict for toxic activity assessment, AllergenFP v1.0 for allergenicity prediction, and IEDB immunogenicity tools for MHC class I epitope immunogenicity assessment28–31. The IFNepitope web server was used to analyze the potential of selected HTL epitopes to induce cytokines by stimulating IFN-γ production. All tools were applied with default settings32.

Population coverage analysis

Analyzing population coverage aids in estimating the proportion of residents in a given area who will respond immunologically to the vaccine. A vaccine’s effectiveness in a given geographic area depends on the variety of MHC alleles that its epitopes can identify. Therefore, to estimate the worldwide collective coverage of the selected HTL and CTL epitopes, the IEDB population coverage analysis tool was utilized33.

Vaccine construction

The vaccine sequence was constructed by connecting the targeted B and T cell epitopes with GPGPG and AAY linkers. The ability of Human Beta Defensin-2 (HBD-2) (Uniprot ID: O15263) to activate Toll-Like Receptor 4 (TLR 4) led to its conjugation at the start of the sequence. The EAAAK linker was used to conjugate HBD-2, whereas KK linkers were used to connect the B cell epitopes and AAY and GPGPG linkers to the T cell epitopes. According to earlier studies, the linkers were chosen for their length, stiffness, flexibility, and efficacy15,34.

Evaluation of physiochemical properties

The physicochemical properties of the vaccine construct, such as its molecular weight, aliphatic index (AI), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), instability index (II), in vitro and in vivo half-life, grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), and amino acid composition, were evaluated using the ProtParam server24. The vaccine design must be highly antigenic to elicit an immune response and promote memory cell development. The VaxiJen 2.0 server was used to assess antigenicity, while AllerTOP 2.0 and IEDB immunogenicity servers were used to predict immunogenicity and allergenicity22,23,35.

Vaccine structure prediction and validation

The secondary structure was predicted using the PSIPRED program36. The vaccine’s four conformational states—helices, twists, sheets, and coils—were examined. The Robetta server used the RoseTTAFold technique to create the vaccine’s tertiary structure37. The Galaxy refinement method was used to refine the structure38. ERRAT 3.0, Procheck, and ProSAWeb were utilized to validate the structure39–41.

Discontinuous B-cell epitopes prediction

The ElliPro algorithm, developed by IEDB, was used to predict discontinuous B cell epitopes in the vaccine, based on the target protein’s 3D shape, with a maximum distance of 6 Å and a scoring threshold of 0.542.

Disulphide engineering

Disulphide bridges between cysteine residues are crucial in the molecular structure of proteins and peptides, aiding in protein stabilization by increasing free energy in the denatured state and decreasing conformational entropy. Using the online Disulphide by Design 2.0 (DbD2) server, the disulphide bond was found in this investigation43. Three-dimensional vaccine structures can be generated by the server to find any residue pairs that can form disulphide bonds.

Immune simulation

CImmSim server was used to confirm the vaccine’s in silico immune profile44. The server used fundamental algorithms based on the position-specific matrix (PSSM) to assess the generated vaccine construct’s immunogenic potential. In order to increase immunological reactions, the following input parameters were chosen: volume (10), number of injections (1), number of steps (100), random seed (12,345), and HLA (B0702, A0101, DRB1_0101). There are no changes to the other settings.

Molecular docking

The vaccine interacts with immune cells to elicit a strong immune response. Protein-protein docking was used to examine potential interactions with human TLR4 and MHC molecules. TLR4, MHC-I, and MHC-II were docked with the vaccine using HADDOCK v.2.4 server, an advanced docking method45. PyMOL was used to view the docked complexes, and residues involved were identified using the PDBsum46,47.

MD simulation

A 100 ns MD simulation was performed on complexes solvated in a periodic box containing TIP3P water molecules to assess protein-ligand stability48. Counter ions of K + and Cl- were injected to neutralize the system, and the system was minimized using the steepest descent approach with 5000 steps. The systems were prepared for production run by equilibrating for 100,000 and 50,000 steps at 310 K temperature at the NPT and NVT ensembles after minimization49. The Berendson thermostat and the Parrinello-Rahman algorithms were used in the simulation to keep the pressure (1 atm) and temperature (310 K) constant. While Verlet calculated non-bonded interactions, the system was relaxed by varying time and maintaining ideal hydrogen atom bond lengths using the LINCS algorithm50,51. The particle mesh Ewald approach was used to compute electrostatic interactions beyond the short-range limit, with periodic boundary conditions applied in x, y, and z dimensions52. In x, y, and z dimensions, the Periodic boundary conditions were imposed, and a production run was conducted on the system. A production run was conducted, and the trajectory was saved and examined every 10ps using the CHARMM36 forcefield and GROMACS commands53,54.

Codon adaptation and visualization of cloning

The vaccine sequence underwent reverse translation utilizing the JCat (Java Codon Adaptation Tool) to enhance codon optimization for the E. coli K12 strain55 In addition to this optimization, three critical factors were considered to ensure the modified DNA sequence was free from rho-independent transcription termination, restriction enzyme cleavage sites, and prokaryotic ribosome binding sites. Finally, the GenSmart tool was employed to successfully clone the modified nucleotide sequence into the E. coli pET28a (+) plasmid vector56. This process highlights the meticulous approach taken to ensure the efficacy and compatibility of the vaccine sequence within a prokaryotic system.

Results

Core proteome analysis

Core proteins are essential in vaccine formulations, providing immunity against various pathogen species. A vaccine against S.typhi was developed by evaluating 122 strains (Additional File 1), resulting in a total protein count of 2973 following core proteome analysis.

Vaccine candidate prioritization

The initial phase involved identifying 200 essential proteins from the core proteome, which were uploaded to the DEG. Blast P was utilized to identify essential proteins that exhibit differences from their human homologs. Out of a total of 200 essential proteins analyzed, 158 were found to be non-homologous. Further examination of these proteins in the context of virulence factors revealed that 24 of them are pathogenic. This study highlights the significance of non-homologous proteins in understanding pathogenicity and their potential role in disease mechanisms. Each protein’s mode of action was discovered using subcellular localization prediction. Out of the 24 pathogenic proteins, 17 were identified as extracellular and membrane proteins and thus chosen for further analysis. Furthermore, their transmembrane helices, stability, antigenicity, and allergenicity were assessed. Three proteins were selected as the vaccine’s targets because they were stable, non-allergenic, highly antigenic, and had no transmembrane helices. Table 1 presents their details.

Population coverage analysis

The developed vaccine is expected to protect a huge part of the global population. The analysis revealed that the vaccine design has the potential to cover about 94. 80% of the worldwide human population, demonstrating its ability to target a diverse variety of HLA variations found in ethnic and regional groups (Fig. 1). In addition to the worldwide estimate, a region-specific study was performed to determine its applicability in places with a high S. typhi burden, as indicated by the World Health Organization. The vaccine achieved exceptionally high population coverage in South Asia (91.45%) across Sub-Saharan Africa, including West Africa (97.43%), East Africa (85.02%), Central Africa (79.61%), and Southern Africa (74.06%). This broad and geographically appropriate coverage highlights the vaccine’s potential as a broad-spectrum immunogen, particularly in endemic regions.

Vaccine construction

Ten T-cell epitopes (seven CTL & three HTL) and four B-cell epitopes connected by AAY, GPGPG, and KK linkers were combined to create the multi-epitope vaccine. The EAAAK linker connected HBD-2 at the start of the sequence. The arrangement of various epitopes and the linkers that correspond to them is shown in Fig. 2. There are 282 amino acid residues in the final vaccine design.

Schematic representation of the final multi-epitope vaccine construct. The diagram shows the arrangement of CTL epitopes (blue), HTL epitopes (green), B-cell epitopes (orange), and adjuvant (blue). Each region is color-coded to clearly indicate its functional role in the vaccine design.

Evaluation of physiochemical properties

The vaccine candidate has a theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of 9.21 and a molecular weight of 28995.29. It was calculated that the vaccine has a 30-hour in-vitro half-life in mammalian reticulocytes. The vaccine’s instability index (II) of 31.30 indicates that it is stable. There were twelve and twenty-two residues with negative and positive charges, respectively. Furthermore, the GRAVY is 0.088, and its aliphatic index is 74.57, indicating that it has excellent physicochemical characteristics for vaccine effectiveness. According to the VaxiJen 2.0 server, the vaccine design was highly antigenic, scoring 0.9294 at the 0.5 threshold. The vaccine has been categorized as non-allergenic and immunogenic (1.40) by the AllerTOP 2.0 and IEDB immunogenicity servers.

Vaccine structure prediction and validation

The PSIPRED server is used to show secondary structures. Figure 3A shows that out of 282 amino acids examined 102 (36.2%) formed α-helixes, 65 (23.04%) formed β-strands, and 115 (40.8%) formed coils.

The Robetta server was used to design the tertiary structure of the vaccine, producing five structures with a 0.39 confidence level, and Model-1 was chosen for further research. The derived tertiary structure was refined by a Galaxy refinement server (Fig. 3B). The most refined structure had a GDT-HA score of 0.9796, an RMSD score of 0.322, MoLProbity value of 1.756, a clash score of 9.2, and a low rotamers score of 0.0. ProSAweb was used to forecast the quality and flaws of the vaccine’s structure. The Z-score for the model was − 4.72 (Fig. 3D).

Three pairs of residues (95ALA-162 ALA, 140 ALA − 257ALA, and 196PRO-222HIS) were chosen based on their results that satisfied the predetermined criteria, taking into account the X3 parameters and bond energy (Fig. 5).

Immune simulation

The immune response simulation, performed using the C-ImmSim server, confirmed the vaccine’s ability to produce significant humoral and cellular immunity. As demonstrated in Fig. 6A, the primary immunological response was characterized by an early spike in IgM levels, followed by sustained rises in IgG1 and IgG2 levels following booster doses, indicating successful class flipping and long-term immune memory development. Figure 6B shows a steady increase in B-cell populations, particularly memory B cells, indicating strong humoral memory development. In Fig. 6C, T-helper (Th) cells demonstrated increased activation and proliferation, as well as the generation of memory Th cells, confirming the efficient involvement of cellular immune pathways. Figure 6D depicts the cytotoxic T cell (TC) population in several states (naive, active, and memory), exhibiting a robust cellular response. The significant increase in active and memory TC cells following immunization demonstrates the vaccine’s ability to induce long-lasting cytotoxic immunity capable of identifying and destroying infected host cells.

MD Simulation

MHC-I

The RMSD of Cα atoms of MHC-I complex showed that the RMSD of the complex gradually increased to 0.75 nm at 20 ns and then attained stability in the range of 0.75 to 1 nm and remained in this range till the end of simulation (Fig. 8a). The RMSF plot of the complex revealed that the residues of the protein did not show any significant fluctuations except for the C-terminal, where the RMSF values increased to 2 nm, while the RMSF values of MHC-I were higher in the first 100 residues (Fig. 8b). The radius of gyration analysis was conducted to check the compactness of the system during simulation. The Rg values of the complex were around 3 nm at start and then decreased to 2.9 nm at 30 ns and then reached 3 nm at 100 ns. The Rg values showed deviations between 3 and 3.2 nm in the second half of the simulation (Fig. 8c).

MD analysis of MHC-I complex. (a) The RMSD of Cα atoms of complex. (b) The flexibility of protein and MHC-I (red) residues during simulation. (c) the radius of gyration analysis of complex.

To investigate the conformational diversity and stability of the system, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the molecular dynamics (MD) trajectory and constructed the corresponding free energy landscape (FEL). As shown in Fig. 9a, PCA revealed a distribution of conformational states along the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which collectively capture the most significant structural variations. K-means clustering (K = 3) identified three dominant conformational clusters, suggesting the system populates multiple metastable states. The FEL (Fig. 9b), projected onto the same principal components, provides insight into the thermodynamic stability of these conformations. Deep basins in the FEL correspond to low-energy, highly populated states, while shallower regions represent transient conformations. Notably, the lowest energy basin aligns with the densest cluster identified in the PCA plot, indicating its dominant thermodynamic stability throughout the simulation.

Discussion

Typhoid fever is a serious health concern, especially in low- and middle-income nations with inadequate sanitary facilities57,58. Even though WHO-approved vaccines such as Typbar-TCV and Vi-polysaccharide have improved the control of Salmonella typhi infections, their limited cross-protection, declining immunity over time, and decreased effectiveness in children younger than two years highlight the need for more reliable and widely protective alternatives59,60. In this context, the current study used an integrated in silico pathway to create a multi-epitope subunit vaccine that targets conserved proteins across 122 S. typhi strains, with the goal of overcoming the strain-specific limitations of existing vaccines.

Reverse vaccinology and subtractive proteomics were used to identify three important antigens that were non-allergenic, highly antigenic, and non-homologous to human proteins. Out of them, four B-cell and ten T-cell epitopes were chosen to optimize immunogenicity and reduce the possibility of adverse reactions. The final vaccine construct incorporated Human β-defensin-2 as an adjuvant, known for its ability to stimulate TLR4 and enhance both innate and adaptive immunity. Structural validation, docking with immune receptors (MHC I, MHC II, and TLR4), molecular dynamics simulations, and immune simulations collectively suggested that the vaccine is structurally stable and capable of generating strong humoral and cellular responses.

Despite the encouraging computational findings, some significant limitations must be recognized. As with all in silico techniques, the vaccine’s biological behavior in real-world systems remains unknown. Computational predictions of immunogenicity and stability, while useful, cannot reproduce the intricacy of immune responses in vivo. Post-translational changes, appropriate protein folding, and expression efficiency in a living host were not evaluated and could have an impact on the vaccine’s effectiveness. Additionally, the construct becomes longer when many epitopes are included, which may impair structural stability or prevent effective expression. Furthermore, while HLA allele distributions projected that the vaccine would cover 94.80% of the global population, individual and geographical differences in immune responsiveness may still have an impact on its efficacy.

Compared to previous S. typhi vaccine designs, which typically focused on a single reference strain (such as S. typhi CT18) or specific individual antigens such as outer membrane proteins, Vi capsular polysaccharides, or flagellin subunits57,61–63, this study presents a more inclusive strategy. By evaluating the core proteome of 122 S. typhi strains, we ensured that only highly conserved, non-variant proteins were chosen, enhancing the possibility of cross-strain protection. This pan-genomic strategy reduces the risk of immunological escape due to strain-specific variability, which was a major issue in previous epitope-based or subunit vaccine designs. Overall, this study provides a core framework for experimental vaccine development; however, further validation in vitro and in vivo—particularly in relevant animal models and clinical trials—is required before the candidate may proceed to clinical application.

Conclusion

The strategic design of a multi-epitope subunit vaccine targeting Salmonella typhi, employing core proteomics, subtractive genomics, and immunoinformatics, represents a promising approach to address typhoid fever. By incorporating conserved and antigenic proteins from 122 strains, this study presents a broad-spectrum candidate that addresses the limitations of existing vaccines. Structural modeling, molecular docking, and immune simulations indicate positive stability, binding affinity, and immune activation. The high predicted population coverage and desirable physicochemical properties further support its potential. However, this work is a fundamental step, and substantial experimental validation is required to validate immunogenicity, expression efficiency, and safety in biological systems. Future research should focus on expression in laboratory settings, testing in animal models, and integrating clinical trials to translate these computational findings into a vaccine that is both effective and globally applicable.
