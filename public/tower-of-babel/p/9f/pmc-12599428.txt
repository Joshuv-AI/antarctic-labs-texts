Descriptive analysis of initial experience with partial nephrectomy and retroperitoneal organ surgery using supine anterior retroperitoneal approach with the da Vinci SP robotic system: Pilot cases
Authors: Hoyoung Bae, Ji Hyung Yoon, Seong Cheol Kim, Taekmin Kwon, Sungchan Park, Sang Hyeon Cheon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and perioperative outcomes of the supine anterior retroperitoneal approach (SARA) using the da Vinci single-port (SP) robotic system for partial nephrectomy (PN) and retroperitoneal organ surgeries.Materials and MethodsFrom December 2023 to December 2024, 35 retroperitoneal surgeries, including 25 PN and 10 other procedures (e.g., adrenalectomy, pyeloplasty, ureterectomy), were performed via SARA with the da Vinci SP system by a single surgeon. Patient demographics, operative details, renal function, and postoperative recovery parameters were analyzed.ResultsThe mean patient age was 60.3 years, with a body mass index of 24.8. The mean operative time was 203.1 minutes, and mean console time was 141.9 minutes. Estimated blood loss was 330.4 mL, with minimal hemoglobin drop and renal function decline. No conversions to open surgery or major complications were observed. Postoperative pain was mild (mean numeric rating scale 2.5), and bowel function returned within 1.7 days. Tumor size averaged 3.5 cm, with most being clear cell carcinoma. SARA proved effective for upper and lower pole masses, and for adrenal or ureteral surgeries.ConclusionsThe SARA technique using the da Vinci SP robotic system is a safe and feasible approach for PN and various retroperitoneal procedures. It allows for minimal invasiveness, low complication rates, and rapid recovery. Further studies are needed to validate these findings and define the learning curve.Keywords: Nephrectomy, Retroperitoneal space, Robotic surgical procedures, Treatment outcome

INTRODUCTION

In 2021, the incidence of kidney cancer in South Korea was reported as 6,883, accounting for 2.5% of all cancer incidence and ranking tenth overall. The 5-year relative survival rate for kidney cancer has significantly improved from 64.2% during 1993–1995 to 86.4% in 2017–2021. This improvement can be largely attributed to advancements in robotic surgery [1].

In the United States, the proportion of robot-assisted (RA) partial nephrectomies (PN) among all PNs increased from 20% to 49% between 2009 and 2012 [2]. After U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval of robot-assisted laparoscopic PN in 2011, this technique quickly became more prevalent than either laparoscopic PN or laparoscopic radical nephrectomy, surpassing them in 2012 and 2014, respectively [3]. This trend is likely attributable to the advantages of robot-assisted PN over conventional techniques, including shorter operative times and faster postoperative recovery [4].

Introduction of the da Vinci single-port (SP) system in 2018 marked a significant milestone in the development of minimally invasive surgery. This system, featuring a single 2.5-cm cannula for the insertion of a camera and three robotic arms, offers a less invasive approach than conventional multiport robotic platforms. This technological advancement is particularly beneficial in confined surgical spaces such as the pelvic lesion or retroperitoneum, enhancing instrument triangulation and dexterity [5,6]. In particular, robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (RALRP) performed with the SP system for narrow pelvic lesions demonstrated significantly lower postoperative pain scores, reduced opioid consumption, and shorter hospital stays compared with multiport RALRP [7]. The da Vinci SP system also enables targeting of specific anatomy during a procedure without compromising the less relevant surrounding structures, thus realizing the concept of “regionalization”. Following its introduction, there was a significant increase in the adoption of the retroperitoneal approach, with its usage increasing from 7.3% to 24.8% in a consortium of 12 institutions [8].

The supine anterior retroperitoneal approach (SARA) is a minimally invasive surgical approach that enables direct anterior access to retroperitoneal organs, including the kidney and adrenal gland, with the patient in the supine position. Unlike conventional lateral decubitus retroperitoneal approaches, SARA utilizes anterior anatomical landmarks such as the psoas muscle and peritoneal reflection to develop the retroperitoneal space. This positioning improves ergonomics, facilitates simultaneous bilateral access if needed, and minimizes patient repositioning time. Since its first introduction by Pellegrino et al. [9] in 2023, SARA has demonstrated feasibility in various retroperitoneal surgeries, such as PN, adrenalectomy, and lymph node dissection, and continues to expand its clinical indications.

While some studies have referred to similar approaches as the low anterior approach, these were not exclusively performed in the supine position but involved both the decubitus and supine positions [10,11,12]. Therefore, SARA, first introduced and utilized by Pellegrino et al. [9] is considered more appropriate in this context.

In South Korea, the use of da Vinci SP robots for retroperitoneal PNs has become increasingly common. Bang et al. [13] reported 44 cases of PN using a da Vinci SP robot via a retroperitoneal approach; however, these procedures were performed in the lateral decubitus position.

In this paper, we describe our initial experiences and perioperative outcomes using this novel SARA technique with da Vinci SP robots, aiming to provide a safer, more efficient, and consistently reproducible method for 25 cases of PN, but also 10 cases of retroperitoneal organ surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

All surgical procedures were performed at Ulsan University Hospital by an expert surgeon from December 27, 2023 to December 18, 2024, using the da Vinci SP robotic system (Intuitive). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of SMG-SNU Boramae Medical Center (approval number: 40-2025-4). The written informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study.

1. Surgical technique

The patient was placed in the supine position and a 3-cm vertical incision was made approximately two-thirds laterally along the line from the umbilicus to the anterior superior iliac spine (Fig. 1). The subcutaneous tissue was dissected, and the aponeurosis of the external oblique muscle was incised, after which the three muscle layers were bluntly separated (Fig. 2B). By finger dissection, the retroperitoneal space was prepared for the da Vinci SP access port (Intuitive), and a 12-mm AirSeal assistant port (ConMed Corp.) was inserted in the lower part of the incision in a “sidecar” fashion. An AirSeal insufflation system (ConMed Corp.) was used to maintain an insufflation pressure of 12 mmHg.

Gas insufflation secures the retroperitoneal space without a balloon dissector (Fig. 2C). After docking, the dissection began at the psoas muscle and progressed proximally to expose further anatomical landmarks. This approach led to the identification of the ureter, followed by the inferior renal pole and hilum (Fig. 1). The rest of the procedure was typical of a PN. The artery and vessels were identified from the hilum, loops were placed, and the area was dissected to facilitate clamping. The renal mass was exposed, and the resection margin was marked. The artery was clamped using a bulldog clamp, and the mass was treated by enucleation or resection depending on its characteristics. Renorrhaphy was subsequently performed on the resection bed. Once the clamp was released, bleeding was checked and additional renorrhaphy was performed if necessary.

2. Data collection

Data collected included demographics, preoperative, immediately postoperative, and postoperative 1 day hemoglobin (Hb) levels, estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR), operative time, warm ischemic time, estimated blood loss (EBL), length of stay (LOS), pathology score, pain score using the numeric rating scale (NRS) on postoperative day 1, nephrometry score [14], and day of bowel function return. No statistical analysis was performed.

RESULTS

In this retrospective review, we analyzed 35 surgical cases between December 2023 and December 2024, with the majority being PNs (n=25), followed by less frequent procedures such as nephroureterectomy, pyeloplasty, adrenalectomy, cyst excision, ureterectomy, and retroperitoneal mass excision (Table 1). One patient simultaneously underwent PN and adrenalectomy at the same time. The overall patient demographics included 34 individuals with a mean age of 60.3±11.6 years, with a slightly higher proportion of males (n=22) compared to females (n=12) (Table 2).

The mean body mass index (BMI) was 24.8±3.2 kg/m2 across all surgical types, which indicated a generally healthy weight range in the cohort. Preoperative estimated GFR was 87.9±13.7 mL/min, with immediate postoperative and postoperative decreases of -7.7±10.7 and -3.6±12.9 mL/min, respectively, reflecting the renal impact of surgeries. Hb levels remained stable preoperatively at 14.0±1.4 g/dL with a minor reduction of -1.7±0.9 g/dL postoperatively (Table 2).

The mean surgery time was 203.1±90.0 minutes, with the console time for robotic procedures averaging 141.9±88.5 minutes. EBL was moderate at 330.4±333.7 mL, highlighting the controlled surgical environment. Postoperative LOS in the hospital averaged 6.9±1.5 days, demonstrating efficient recovery processes (Table 2).

Pain management was effective, with a postoperative pain score averaging 2.5±0.7, indicating well-controlled pain. Postoperative complications were minimal and no significant events were reported. Gastrointestinal recovery, as indicated by the mean time to first flatus (‘gas out’), was 1.7±0.8 days (Table 2).

Focusing specifically on the 25 PN cases, the surgeries predominantly managed tumors localized to the mid-pole (n=11) of the kidney, with all patients staged between cT1a and cT1b. The mean tumor size was 3.5±1.9 cm. Histopathological analysis showed that the majority of tumors were of the clear cell type (n=18), followed by papillary (n=4), and angiomyolipomas (n=2), with one case of oncocytoma.

There were no conversions to open surgery, no postoperative major complications (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

1. Feasibility and versatility of the SARA

In our study SARA has demonstrated its viability not only for masses located at the lower pole but also for those at the upper pole and for performing adrenalectomies. Among the 25 cases of PN analyzed, seven involved tumors at the upper pole and required a mean of 149.7 minutes to complete, slightly longer than the 137.8 minutes for other locations, yet proving entirely feasible. Additionally, two successful adrenalectomies highlighted the versatility of the approach, with one involving a 4.5-cm adrenal mass on the right side and the other concurrent with a left PN. The incision site can be slightly modified depending on the location of the target area, with upper pole masses or adrenal lesions being higher and lower ureter lesions being lower, thus proving the versatility of this approach. The patient’s body habitus must be taken into account and no single rule can be universally applied; in cases where the surgical target was located higher, we tended to place the port slightly above the McBurney’s point to facilitate access.

2. Technical considerations in SARA

Surgeons new to the SARA technique may face challenges with orientation, with initially longer operative times. Surgeries typically begin with the identification of the psoas muscle as a landmark, followed by tracing the ureter to locate the pedicle, which is beneficial for procedures such as pyeloplasty and ureterectomy due to early identification of the ureter. However, in patients with abundant visceral fat, these landmarks can be obscured, making orientation more difficult. One challenge was accessibility in patients with high body fat, although surgery was still achievable. Among the cases performed, the patient with the highest BMI (32.47 kg/m2) had a 3 cm mid-pole tumor with a RENAL nephrometry score of 7. In this case, the EBL was 200 mL, LOS was 5 days, console time was 91 minutes, and total operative time was 170 minutes. While higher BMI generally tended to increase technical difficulty, the relationship was not strictly linear; some high-BMI patients could be managed without major challenges, whereas in certain low-BMI patients, abundant visceral fat made the procedure more demanding.

3. Comparison with previous studies

To place our findings in context, previously reported operative times varied across studies. In a systematic review including 11 studies, Bourgi et al. [15] reported mean operative times of 149–241 minutes for the transperitoneal approach and 124–217.2 minutes for the retroperitoneal approach, with EBL ranging from 33 to 395.1 mL and RENAL nephrometry scores between 5.5 and 8. In our PN cohort, the mean operative time was 200.6±79.6 minutes and the mean EBL was 366.6±345.2 mL; after excluding an outlier case with 1,500 mL blood loss, these values were adjusted to 197.3±79.5 minutes and 319.4±257.3 mL, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Considering that the mean nephrometry score in our series was 7 and that 16.0% (4 of 25) of the tumors were hilar lesions abutting the main renal vessels, these outcomes appear acceptable despite being relatively higher than some prior reports.

4. Learning curve

Surgeons new to the SARA technique may face challenges with orientation, with initially longer operative times. All procedures in this study were performed by a single surgeon with experience of more than 300 conventional robot-assisted PN cases. Nevertheless, when compared with previously published series of conventional robot-assisted PN, the operative time and EBL in our cohort remained somewhat higher, suggesting that the learning curve has not yet been fully overcome and that further refinement is still possible. Although we were unable to provide a statistical analysis of the learning curve, such as reductions in operative time, the surgeon appeared to feel comfortable with access and port placement after approximately 10 cases. In practice, the difficulty and outcomes of the SARA technique seemed to be determined less by the number of procedures performed and more by patient-specific factors, including tumor location, BMI, and the distribution of visceral fat. Further studies with a larger case volume will be needed to better characterize and validate the learning curve associated with this approach.

5. Outlier case in initial series

An outlier case involved excessive blood loss (1,500 mL) and prolonged operative time (280 minutes) due to equipment issues during clamping. This case involved a 2.1 cm mid-pole tumor with a RENAL nephrometry score of 8, and the excessive blood loss occurred as a result of a failed clamping attempt. The clamp malfunctioned and dislodged during the procedure; however, the patient remained hemodynamically stable under anesthetic management. Given the intraoperative circumstances, it was considered more beneficial to proceed expeditiously with the operation rather than convert to open surgery. Postoperatively, no additional transfusion or intervention was required, and the patient recovered uneventfully, following a normal clinical course until discharge. Excluding this outlier, the results were considered reliable, and a supplementary table without this case is provided (Supplementary Table 1).

6. Advantages of the retroperitoneal approach

The retroperitoneal approach offers several intrinsic advantages compared with the transperitoneal approach. By avoiding entry into the peritoneal cavity, the risk of injury to intra-abdominal organs is minimized, thereby reducing complications such as adhesions, peritonitis, or intestinal injury. Less manipulation of intra-abdominal structures also facilitates faster return of bowel function, reduced postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stay [11,12]. In our series, no major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥3) were observed, which is favorable compared with the 0%–13.3% complication rates reported in the 11 studies included in the systematic review by Bourgi et al. [15]. Moreover, Jin et al. [16] reported a postoperative day 1 pain score (NRS) of 3.5 in 142 patients undergoing conventional robot-assisted PN, whereas our study demonstrated a lower mean score of 2.4±0.7. These findings suggest that the retroperitoneal approach, as utilized in SARA, may contribute to enhanced perioperative safety and recovery.

For patients with a history of prior abdominal surgery or those with a ‘hostile abdomen,’ the retroperitoneal route is particularly advantageous as it avoids intra-abdominal adhesions. It has been successfully applied in various urological procedures and is especially useful for posterior renal lesions [9]. Moreover, because of the confined nature of the retroperitoneal space, complications such as urinary leakage or infections tend to remain locally contained, leading to safer outcomes [17]. Despite these benefits, retroperitoneal access was not widely adopted with earlier robotic systems, largely due to the limitations of multiport platforms, which often caused instrument clashing and compromised triangulation within the narrow retroperitoneal space [9].

7. Advantages of the supine position

In addition to the benefits of the retroperitoneal route, supine positioning itself offers distinct advantages compared with the lateral decubitus position. Lateral decubitus, though widely used for urological procedures to optimize surgical exposure, has been associated with ophthalmological, musculoskeletal, neurovascular, and hemodynamic complications [18,19,20,21,22]. By contrast, the supine position provides greater stability and maintains anatomical structures in a neutral alignment, which facilitates adequate respiratory mechanics and enhances intraoperative safety [23,24]. Supine positioning also improves anesthetic access to the airway and vascular lines, reduces the risk of ocular and pressure-related injuries, and may lower the incidence of ventilation-perfusion mismatch and elevated airway pressures observed in decubitus positioning. Potential drawbacks of the supine position include a more limited working space compared with the decubitus approach, which may present challenges early in the learning curve. However, the design of the da Vinci SP platform, combined with increasing surgical experience, helps mitigate these limitations. Taken together, the supine position provides complementary advantages that, when combined with retroperitoneal access, may synergistically enhance perioperative safety and recovery in the SARA technique.

8. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to a descriptive evaluation of the PN cohort (25 cases), and the additional retroperitoneal organ procedures (10 cases) were too few and heterogeneous to allow meaningful subgroup assessment. Second, because the total number of cases was small and heterogeneous, it was not possible to determine whether the learning curve could be overcome as case numbers increased. Third, we were unable to perform statistical comparisons with conventional retroperitoneal organ surgeries. As additional cases are accumulated, future studies will be able to provide more robust subgroup analyses and direct comparisons with traditional approaches. Fourth, although the da Vinci SP platform and the SARA are not necessary for all surgeries, they may offer advantages in selected cases. Further research is needed to better define the indications for SARA and to guide appropriate patient selection.

CONCLUSIONS

The SARA with a robotic SP for PN and retroperitoneal organ procedures is feasible and promising, with the potential to enhance benefits related to positioning and approach methods.

Future studies are warranted to define clear indications for SARA, compare outcomes with conventional approaches through prospective trials, and determine the learning curve based on cumulative case volume.
