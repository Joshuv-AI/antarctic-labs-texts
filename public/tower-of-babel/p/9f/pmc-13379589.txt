An end-to-end deep learning pipeline for urban noise monitoring with spatiotemporal analysis
Authors: Mehmet Ali Yalçınkaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban noise pollution is a critical public health problem affecting millions of people around the world. Current acoustic monitoring systems largely focus on optimizing classification accuracy but fail to meet operational requirements such as calibrated probability outputs, deployment formats, and spatiotemporal integration. This study presents a comprehensive urban noise monitoring pipeline extending from raw sensor data to city-scale policy support. The proposed framework uses a CNN14-based ensemble architecture on the SONYC-UST dataset collected from 56 acoustic sensors deployed across New York City. The system performs simultaneous multi-source detection across 35 hierarchical acoustic presence labels and provides reliable probability calibration validated by a Brier score of 0.1269. In terms of technical infrastructure, the pipeline offers export in TorchScript format that can run on edge devices without Python dependency, processing capacity of more than 2,000 audio segments per second on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 and training time of less than six hours per fold. Integrated 250-meter spatial grid mapping and hourly-daily temporal aggregation modules enable direct integration of predictions into urban planning workflows. Independent external corroboration was performed using NYC 311 complaint records across 3,460 matched grid-day observations. Permutation testing revealed a statistically significant relationship between predicted noise scores and community-reported disturbance. This study demonstrates that model accuracy alone is not sufficient for deployable urban noise monitoring systems, and that a holistic approach encompassing calibration, deployment, and spatial integration is required.

Introduction

In modern cities, urban noise pollution is one of the main environmental problems that negatively affects public health and quality of life. Loud and irregular noises resulting from traffic, construction and industrial activities can cause sleep disorders, stress and other cognitive effects in people1. Therefore, today researchers and policy makers show great interest in reliable and source-specific noise monitoring systems that can operate at city scale2.

Traditional noise monitoring techniques rely on sound pressure measurements or complaint-based systems and do not provide sufficient information about the source or type of noise. Since these approaches cannot distinguish different noise sources and cannot fully capture temporal-spatial dynamics, they are inadequate in the development of targeted intervention strategies3. As a result of this literature gap, studies have emerged that aim to automatically classify urban sound recordings into multiple noise source categories. The SONYC-UST dataset contains tens of thousands of real-world sensor recordings annotated with fine-grained audio tags and spatiotemporal metadata, as well as providing a fundamental resource for scalable urban noise monitoring research4.

Previous studies in the field of environmental and urban sound classification have examined different approaches. Some studies have used classical feature engineering combined with traditional classifiers such as K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN)5. More recent studies use deep learning architectures that work on spectrogram or raw waveform inputs6. However, despite advances in this field, some limitations remain. Many studies use carefully selected or preprocessed sound recordings rather than continuous real-world urban sound recordings. The lack of spatiotemporal metadata, limited class taxonomies, and limited distribution considerations hinder the direct applicability of these studies to large-scale urban noise monitoring analyses. Achieving high classification accuracy, especially on data with overlapping or low signal-to-noise ratio, is still a very challenging process7.

In this study, an end-to-end pipeline that goes beyond standard audio classification has been developed to address the above mentioned gaps. The proposed framework encompasses the entire lifecycle of urban noise monitoring, from raw data collection and preprocessing to training a multi label deep learning model, spatiotemporal mapping, and exporting a production-ready model. The aim of the study is to create a system that produces truly usable, reliable and calibrated estimates for urban analytics and public policy, based on the SONYC-UST dataset.

The key contributions of this study can be summarized as follows: Unlike models trained on processed clean sound samples, the SONYC-UST dataset incorporates the complex structure of multi-sensor urban environments along with fine-grained spatiotemporal metadata. The deep convolutional architecture, adapted to address the overlapping nature of urban sounds, is able to detect multiple noise sources occurring simultaneously despite apparent class imbalances. Going beyond standard accuracy measures, the probabilistic reliability of predictions was prioritized, and calibration evaluation ensured that the model’s confidence scores were meaningful enough to support real-world decision-making processes. Classification results were combined across time and geography dimensions to create heat maps, and system outputs were corroborated by comparing them with NYC 311 complaint data. Finally, to bridge the gap between research and application, the final model was exported in TorchScript format, and the entire workflow was made ready for integration into real-time or batch monitoring systems.

Related works

When studies in the field of urban noise are examined, it is seen that research has evolved from static sound libraries to dynamic and large-scale sensor networks. The first systematic studies in this field were carried out on UrbanSound8K8 and ESC-509 datasets. Although the datasets in question have provided a standard basis for testing classification algorithms, they consist of isolated audio samples recorded under controlled conditions. In real urban environments, there are complex conditions such as overlapping sound sources, variable sensor locations, and fluctuating background noise levels. In order to fill the gap in the noise data set containing these complex conditions in the literature, a study called SONYC Urban Sound Tagging was carried out. In this study, data collected from sensors deployed throughout New York City were made available to researchers10. In the next version of the created data set, time stamp and geographical coordinate information were added to each audio recording4. This has paved the way for context-sensitive and city-scale monitoring studies. The present study is based on the second version of the data set in question.

Model architectures in the field of environmental sound recognition are shaped around two basic approaches. The first approach is based on convolutional neural networks trained on log-mel spectrograms. In the study conducted by11, it was stated that aggressive data augmentation techniques applied during the training phase significantly improved generalization performance. The second approach deals with direct feature extraction from raw waveforms. Zhu, Wang and colleagues performed multi-resolution feature extraction by applying filters at different temporal scales to waveforms12. Similarly, Zhu, Xu and colleagues proposed a structure that can capture both instantaneous and continuous acoustic events using variable window lengths13. Abdoli et al. achieved successful results by developing a one-dimensional convolutional network architecture that does not require conversion to the frequency domain14. PANNs models developed by Kong et al. (2020) were pre-trained on large-scale datasets such as AudioSet. This process revealed that transfer learning can provide equivalent or better results compared to task-specific training15.

Some of the studies carried out in literature also deal with the subject of attention mechanisms. Zhang et al. improved the recognition success of short-term acoustic events by combining recurrent layers with a frame-level attention mechanism16. Sharma et al. achieved high success rates by combining mel-frequency and gammatone cepstral coefficients through parallel attention channels6. In addition, in the DCASE competition series, which asks researchers to develop lightweight solutions suitable for embedded hardware, Adapa adapted the MobileNetV2 architecture for acoustic tagging17.

Recent studies in the literature address deployment constraints and real-world applicability issues. Shen et al. presented a fifteen-category urban noise dataset and proposed special attention modules to the time-frequency structure18. Bellafkir et al. addressed the on-device inference problem using quantization and information distillation techniques19. Despite all these developments, most existing systems remain at the classification stage. In the comprehensive literature review conducted by Bansal and Garg, it was emphasized that the calibration of probability outputs and integration with spatial and temporal metadata are still under-researched areas20. The system proposed in this study aims to fill this gap in the literature rather than classifying noise with a high success rate. Therefore, a calibrated multi-label classifier is paired with geographic and hourly aggregation, producing outputs that can be integrated into urban planning workflows rather than isolated accuracy figures.

In summary, the preceding review reveals four persistent gaps in the urban noise monitoring literature: (1) most classification systems do not report or evaluate the calibration quality of their probabilistic outputs, limiting their utility for confidence-based decision-making; (2) spatiotemporal metadata, when available, is rarely integrated into the classification pipeline as an analytical or visualization layer; (3) deployment considerations such as model export format, inference throughput, and platform independence are seldom addressed; and (4) to our knowledge, no prior study has attempted external corroboration of predicted noise patterns using independent community-level data such as complaint records. The present study addresses all four gaps within a single end-to-end framework.

Data sources and preparation

The analysis and classification of urban noise in New York City were performed using two main data sources. These are the SONYC Urban Sound Tagging (v2.3) and NYC 311 datasets. These two datasets were used for different purposes. The SONYC-UST v2.3 dataset was used as the primary data source for model training and inference processes, while the NYC 311 complaint data was used for external corroboration of the results generated by the system. All data used in the study were obtained from open-access sources and standard data augmentation techniques were used during the training phase. Synthetic data generation was not performed. This study initially used the SONYC Urban Sound Tagging v2.3 dataset4. It contains environmental sound recordings from 56 acoustic sensors placed throughout New York City. It includes 18,510 10-second sound files at 48 kHz sampling frequency, recorded from 2016 to 2019. Each sound record in the dataset is tagged with one or more noise sources such as sirens, horns, idling engine noise, construction equipment, or street music. The labeling structure is hierarchical and multi-label. There are 23 sound classes at the fine-grained level, which are grouped into 8 coarse categories (engine, machine impact, non-machine impact, chainsaw, warning signal, music, human voice, dog). Of these, 6 coarse categories have annotated presence labels in the dataset; the resulting 35-label modeling set (23 fine + 6 coarse parent + 6 uncertain-source variants) is described in Sect. "Multi-label classification scheme". In this study, a total of 35 independent sigmoid outputs were modeled, including both fine-grained classes and supercategories. The dataset was downloaded and stored from the Zenodo platform as a total of 19 compressed archive files and various metadata files. The dataset allows for spatiotemporal analyses because it includes timestamps and sensor location information for each recording.

The second data source used in the study consists of complaints submitted by citizens to the NYC 311 service hotline, which was used for external corroboration. NYC 311 is New York City’s 24/7 call center providing non-emergency municipal services, and has recorded over 27 million service requests since 201021. Only noise-related complaints were filtered and retrieved from this complaint database. The official Socrata Open Data API was used for data retrieval. A custom-written query was used to select records for the period 2016–2019 where the complaint type field contained the term “Noise”. Each retrieved complaint record includes spatial and temporal data. Spatial data are geographic coordinates, and temporal data are the creation date and closing date. The collected dataset is stored in Parquet format for time series analysis and spatiotemporal merging operations.

Audio feature extraction

One of the most important steps within the processing pipeline of this research is converting the acquired raw audio files into inputs suited to the deep learning model. For this purpose, the time-frequency representation of the acoustic signals was obtained by means of mel-spectrogram transformation. Mel-spectra are essentially maps of sounds showing which frequencies are present at what intensity per second in a given sound. Such representations are commonly employed in environmental noise classification studies15.

The librosa library was used to carry out the feature extraction process. Using this library, mel-spectrograms were computed using the following parameters for every audio recording: n_mels = 64 (number of mel filters), n_fft = 800 (FFT window size, corresponding to 25 ms at 32 kHz), hop_length = 320 (hop length, corresponding to 10 ms at 32 kHz). Audio files, originally recorded at 48 kHz, were resampled to 32,000 Hz during feature extraction. Logarithmic power scaling (dB, referenced to peak) was applied to the resulting mel-spectrograms. The specified parameters were selected to balance temporal resolution with frequency discrimination for urban noise events. The specified parameters provided an optimal combination for capturing the typical frequency characteristics of urban noise events. The mel spectrograms obtained were subsequently converted into a logarithmic scale so that the resultant log-mel spectrogram format would facilitate the models ability to learn about sound events across a wide dynamic range, and also match the way that humans perceive sound intensity via a logarithmic scale. Each of the 10-second recordings was transformed into a 64 × 1001 matrix representing 64 mel frequency bands and 1001 time frames. After they had been generated, the mel spectrograms were stored as NumPy arrays to provide faster execution times when conducting multiple iterations of training.

After the caching of the feature files, a systematic mapping of the feature files to their respective audio files was created. Each audio file was assigned a unique key derived from its filename (i.e., 00_000071.wav → 00_000071). The identifier established a one-to-one relationship with the cached mel-spectrogram file. The program developed to create the identifiers recursively traversed all of the audio files’ directories to detect duplicate files and/or missing files, and to ensure that there was consistency between the raw audio and cached feature files. The result of this step was a single consolidated CSV file containing the original label information for each recording, the absolute paths to the .wav and .npy files, and the time-related indices (year, week, day, hour) that will be used in later analyses. This file represents the central metadata table for all of the model’s training and inference processes.

Model architecture and training

The CNN14 architecture was adopted as the base model for urban noise classification in this study. The following subsections describe the five key components of the methodology: model architecture, multi-label classification scheme, training configuration, threshold optimization, and ensemble strategy.

CNN14 architecture

The CNN14 model is a deep convolutional neural network whose backbone consists of five ConvBlock stages, each containing two Conv2d layers. The model’s ConvBlock unit, which is the foundation of the CNN14 model, sequentially applies the following operations: 2D convolutional layer (Conv2d), batch normalization (BatchNorm2d), ReLU activation, and max pooling (MaxPool2d). This sequential application of operations allows the model to perform hierarchical feature extraction based on the input spectrogram. The CNN14 model takes as its input single-channel mel-spectrograms represented as tensors of dimensions [batch, 1, 64, 1001]. As the model progresses through the layers of convolution, the spatial size of the features decreases while the number of features or channels increases. The output of the final convolutional layer is reduced to a 1024-dimensional embedding vector through adaptive average pooling. The classification head of the model is composed of two fully connected layers, dropout regularization, ReLU activation, and a sigmoid activation function on the final output layer that is used to assign multiple labels simultaneously. Multi-label classification scheme

The SONYC-UST dataset has a hierarchical and multi-label taxonomy structure. In total, 35 presence labels were independently modeled as sigmoid outputs, comprising 23 fine-grained sound classes, 6 coarse parent categories that have annotated presence labels, and 6 “uncertain-source” variants. All 35 labels were trained jointly under a unified multi-label objective. Each class was associated with an independent sigmoid output; therefore, the model can detect multiple noise sources simultaneously. All target vectors were encoded as 35 dimensional binary arrays for each recording. Due to the nature of the dataset, significant class imbalances exist, where some classes (for example, engine sounds), have many examples, while others (for example, chainsaw sounds) are observed quite infrequently. Therefore, special measures were applied during the training process to deal with these imbalances.

Training configuration

Training of the model has been completed through a 5-fold stratified cross-validation methodology. A stratified k-folds methodology (MultilabelStratifiedKFold) was used to split each fold so that the occurrence of labels would remain consistent with the original data. By doing this, it can be guaranteed that the class distribution within each fold will be representative of the original data. An AdamW optimizer was used for the optimization portion of the training. The AdamW optimizer was configured with an initial learning rate of 2 × 10− 4 and weight decay of 1 × 10− 4. During the optimization phase, a cosine annealing schedule was used to schedule the learning rates, with a linear warmup phase covering the first 10% of epochs (approximately 13 epochs) and annealing to a minimum learning rate of 5% of the initial value. A focal binary cross-entropy loss with positive class weighting was used as the loss function. The focal modulation term (γ = 1.5) down-weights well-classified examples, directing optimization toward harder cases, while the positive class weights—computed as the ratio of negative to positive samples for each class—compensate for the prevalence disparity across the 35 target categories. Regularization methods were applied to prevent overfitting. Dropout layers were added to the model architecture, and data augmentation methods were also used during training. Additionally, two other techniques were applied to increase the stability of the training process and improve the model’s ability to generalize; stochastic weight averaging (SWA) and exponential moving average (EMA). The EMA decay factor was 0.998. SWA was activated in the final 20% of training epochs with a reduced learning rate (SWA_LR = LR × 0.5). Early stopping was implemented based upon the mean Average Precision (mAP) metric from the validation set, with a patience of 10 epochs and a minimum improvement threshold (min_delta) of 0.002. The EMA checkpoint corresponding to the greatest mAP value for each fold was selected as the final model. AMP (Automatic Mixed Precision) was applied to improve the efficiency of the training process. AMP provides significant improvements to both the training time and memory requirements of the training process.

The cross-validation protocol used MultilabelStratifiedKFold with n_splits = 5, shuffle=True, and random_state = 42. All random number generators were initialized with the fixed seed value 42, and the software and hardware environment was fully documented in the public code release to support run-to-run reproducibility on equivalent hardware. Threshold optimization

In multi-label classification the calculation of optimal thresholds to convert sigmoid output into binary labels is very important. Optimal thresholds were computed individually for every class in this study. Threshold optimization was carried out to achieve the best possible F1-score on the validation data set. Threshold scans were performed at specific intervals between 0.0 and 1.0. For every threshold value, the F1-score was calculated and the value with the highest score was determined as the optimal threshold for that particular class. The computed class-specific thresholds were then saved to a JSON file to be used during the inference stage. Threshold optimization was performed exclusively on each fold’s validation partition, using a grid search over 19 candidate values between 0.05 and 0.95. For the final reported metrics, the median of the five per-fold thresholds was used. This median threshold vector, derived entirely from held-out validation data, was applied to the out-of-fold predictions, ensuring complete separation between threshold selection and performance evaluation.

For evaluation, two protocols are used throughout the manuscript;

i.Per-fold protocol: each fold’s macro-F1 is computed with its own optimal per-class threshold vector (selected on that fold’s validation partition), and fold-level scores are then summarized as mean ± SD across the five folds. This protocol quantifies cross-fold variability and reflects classifier capacity at each fold’s optimal operating point.ii.Global single-threshold protocol: concatenated OOF predictions across all folds are evaluated using a single class-specific threshold vector applied uniformly to every sample, representing a deployment-realistic single-threshold scenario in which one fixed threshold set is shipped with the model. Tables and per-class metrics in Sect. "Class-level and co-occurrence analysis" report values under protocol (ii) unless otherwise noted; cross-fold variability is reported under protocol (i) in Sect. Ensemble strategy

In order to make the final predictions more reliable, the models acquired through 5-fold cross-validation were combined into an ensemble. The median threshold values found during the threshold optimization process are applied to this probability average to determine the final binary predictions. This ensemble approach provides more stable and reliable predictions by balancing the potential errors of individual models. Additionally, this strategy increases the generalization capacity by combining the patterns learned from different subsets of data. For performance evaluation, a strictly out-of-fold (OOF) protocol was adopted: each sample was scored exclusively by the fold model that did not include it in training. The five sets of held-out predictions were concatenated to obtain out-of-fold probability estimates for all 62,022 samples. All classification metrics reported in this study (Table 2; Figs. 3, 4 and 5) are based on these OOF predictions. The full ensemble average (all five models) was retained only for downstream spatiotemporal mapping and the 311 association analysis, where complete dataset coverage is required. Because OOF scoring provides a more conservative estimate of held-out performance, all final classification metrics reported in this manuscript are based exclusively on OOF predictions, whereas full-ensemble averaging is used only for downstream spatiotemporal mapping and the NYC 311 association analysis.

Inference and deployment pipeline

After model training and validation the system was prepared for deployment. Based on cross validation results, the fold with the highest mean Average Precision (Fold 5) was selected for export. The EMA weights from the best checkpoint were loaded into a freshly instantiated CNN14 architecture and converted to TorchScript format using torch.jit.trace(), producing a self-contained model file that encapsulates both architecture and trained parameters without requiring the original Python source code. The TorchScript22 model enables platform-independent inference with GPU acceleration (CUDA), achieving a throughput exceeding 2,000 audio segments per second on the evaluation hardware. All training metadata—including class names, mel-spectrogram configuration, fold model paths, and per-class thresholds—are stored in a centralized JSON configuration file to ensure consistency across training, inference, and downstream analysis stages.

Spatiotemporal analysis and external corroboration

After the completion of the inference phase, the predicted noise class probabilities were combined with appropriate metadata in order to enable (a) temporal trend analysis of urban noise activity in New York City and (b) spatial mappings. Raw model output was then converted into meaningful patterns that could be used as part of both scientific analysis and policy-driven visualization.

The following notation is used throughout the spatiotemporal analysis and results. For each recording and each of the 35 target classes c, prob_c denotes the predicted class probability (sigmoid output, range [0, 1]), and pred_c denotes the binary prediction obtained by applying a threshold to prob_c. The noise_score for a recording is defined as the maximum prob_c across all 35 classes: noise_score = max_c(prob_c). Spatial analysis uses a grid_id computed by floor-dividing longitude and latitude coordinates into fixed-size cells (250 m for noise mapping, 500 m for complaint association analysis). Temporal aggregation uses time_bin units at hourly or daily resolution. Coverage refers to the proportion of labeled entries whose maximum predicted probability exceeds a specified precision threshold.

Temporal aggregation

After the generation of prediction outputs by the TorchScript inference process, these outputs were paired with the corresponding annotation information in the annotations.csv file created after training. The purpose of the pairing was to establish a uniform temporal index that would enable combining the predictions related to noise events at the hourly and daily levels. A custom conversion function was developed to convert ISO week-date format to precise datetime format in order to correct wrong or missing entries (for example, following SONYC-UST v2.3 metadata convention where day-of-week is encoded as 0 = Monday through 6 = Sunday). In addition to correcting invalid or missing data, the standardization process generated a precise timestamp based upon the input tuple (year, week, day, hour) for each record. As a result, the standardization process enabled the support of multiple time-based operations using the dataset, including determining hourly distribution of activities, identifying recurring patterns at the weekly level, or observing seasonal fluctuations within certain types of noise. The final product of the standardization process was a temporally-indexed DataFrame that preserved the two primary dimensions (spatial and categorical).

250 m grid mapping

A data infrastructure was developed in order to visualize urban noise on geographic coordinates. Processed data were loaded to verify the presence of required fields such as longitude, latitude, year, week, day, hour, and target class. The TARGET_COL field allows the user to determine the specific class or sound category to be visualized on the map. The user can create a new spatial layer for each primary noise source type by changing the TARGET_COL value. Prediction data that have been georeferenced and aggregated into 250 m × 250 m spatial grids were used to conduct spatial analysis. Each grid cell contains the mean of the maximum class probabilities observed across all recordings within its geographic extent (i.e., for each recording the highest of the 35 class probabilities is taken, and these per-recording values are averaged across recordings assigned to that cell). The structure was intentionally designed to be modular so that research or municipal analysis teams can produce their own custom visual layers (e.g., heat maps, density graphs, category overlays) from the same data.

Heat maps and temporal-spatial integration

When the model outputs are combined, a multi-indexed dataset is obtained that includes both time and location axes. This structure allows for various analytical queries, including questions such as: At what times of day do the loudest construction noises occur in Manhattan? In which of the five boroughs is the ratio of traffic noise to residential noise highest on weekends? How do the predicted noise score patterns vary between day and night? In addition, the generated heat maps show the spatial distribution of acoustic activity within New York City for representative event types. The spatiotemporal patterns derived from the prediction outputs are based on real-world activity obtained from SONYC sensor recordings and independent corroboration performed using georeferenced NYC 311 complaint incident reports.

NYC 311 association analysis

External association was assessed by comparing the noise scores predicted by the model to NYC’s 311 noise complaints, which serve as an independent citywide proxy for perceived noise disturbance. Only noise-related complaints were included (complaint_type containing ‘Noise’), covering the period 2016–2019 to match SONYC sensor recording dates. Complaint data containing geographic coordinates were linked to the model output at a 500 m spatial grid and daily temporal resolution. The coarser spatial resolution (compared to a 250 m mapping grid) was chosen to ensure sufficient complaint density per cell for robust statistical estimation. Grid-day cells with fewer than 5 prediction records were excluded.

Model predictions were summarized on a grid-day basis by calculating the average predicted noise score for each grid cell and daily time period. Simultaneously, 311 complaint incidents were counted within the same grid cell and daily time period. Then, both datasets were merged to yield 3,460 matched grid-day observations.

Additionally, as a complementary non-parametric measure, the Spearman rank correlation (ρ) was calculated between the predicted noise scores and the number of complaints. The statistical significance of this relationship was determined using a permutation test, which involved randomly shuffling the predicted noise scores among grid-day observations and recalculating ρ to obtain an empirical p-value.

Reproducibility and implementation details

The proposed pipeline, beginning with data collection and ending with spatiotemporal visualization, was designed with the goal of repeatable execution. Training and inference were performed on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU using PyTorch 2.x with CUDA 12.1. Data collection from both the NYC 311 Open Data Portal and the SONYC-UST repository was performed using deterministic scripts with fixed query parameters and documented download processes. NYC 311 complaint data were retrieved via the Socrata Open Data API (dataset identifier: 76ig-c548) with the following query parameters: complaint_type containing ‘Noise’, created_date between 2016-01-01 and 2020-01-01, geographic coordinates required (latitude and longitude not null). Records were downloaded in paginated batches of 50,000. No API authentication key is required for public access. A requirements.txt file with pinned package versions (Python 3.11, PyTorch 2.5.1 + cu121, librosa, scikit-learn, iterstrat) is provided with the submitted code package. Data processing (audio preprocessing), feature extraction (mel-spectrograms), model development (training, evaluation, and inference) are all implemented through modules in Python scripts where all hyperparameters, thresholds, and file paths are defined in centralized configuration files. The model checkpoint and class specific threshold information are written out to the disk as part of the model’s metadata allowing for identical use during each stage (training, evaluation, and deployment). Once trained, the model is saved in torchscript format which allows for model inference to be performed in a platform independent manner regardless of the original training environment. All downstream merging, mapping, and external corroboration processes utilize the output of previous predictions, along with their associated metadata tables, therefore providing an end to end representation of the complete workflow on any compatible machine, so long as access to the exact same publically available datasets exists. All required python scripts, configuration files, and documentation to reproduce experiments and figures have also been submitted to the journal and as such provide a reproducible computational workflow for regenerating the reported experiments and figures on an equivalent environment.

Results

Model performance overview

The multi-label classifier based on the CNN14 backbone was trained and evaluated on the SONYC acoustic dataset using five stratified folds. Table 2 presents the per-class performance metrics, including Average Precision (AP), F1-score, and Brier score. Given the fine-grained, multi-label nature of SONYC-UST and strong class imbalance, the reported classification performance is interpreted primarily as a functional baseline that enables downstream spatiotemporal mapping and external corroboration, not as a state-of-the-art benchmark target. All classification metrics reported in this section are based on out-of-fold (OOF) predictions, where each sample is evaluated exclusively by the model that did not include it in training. Binary classification decisions use median thresholds derived from validation partitions.

The system produced stable mean scores across folds. Two complementary evaluation protocols are reported, both computed strictly on out-of-fold (OOF) predictions:

i.Per-fold protocol with fold-specific thresholds. Each fold’s macro-F1 is computed using its own optimal per-class threshold vector, selected on the validation partition of that fold. The five fold-level scores are then summarized as: macro-mAP = 0.3199 ± 0.0053; macro-F1 = 0.3762 ± 0.0071 (mean ± SD across five folds). This protocol reflects the classifier capacity under the best operating point of each fold and quantifies cross-fold variability.ii.Global single-threshold protocol. Concatenated OOF predictions across all folds are evaluated under a single deployment-realistic per-class threshold vector applied uniformly to every sample: macro-mAP = 0.3159 and macro-F1 = 0.3729. This protocol reflects the operating performance of the model when deployed in production with a single fixed set of class-specific thresholds, rather than five different sets.

The results demonstrate that the model’s performance is robust against the threshold selection protocol, and that the deployment-realistic single-threshold operating point incurs negligible cost compared to optimization per fold. This finding is also consistent with the per-class threshold analysis presented in Sect. "Coverage and threshold behavior" and Table 3. This analysis shows that the cross-fold median threshold matches the F1-optimal threshold for 34 out of 35 classes. The threshold-independent micro-averaged Brier score is 0.1269 (Fig. 3), demonstrating moderate calibration accuracy between the predicted probabilities and the observed binary outcomes.

Average prediction confidence values remained within the 0.3–0.6 probability range for most classes, confirming balanced probability output scaling. Per-class quantitative results show measurable variation among noise categories. Classes associated with high-energy or impulsive sounds (e.g., human-voice, alert-signal, siren, car-horn, dog-barking-whining) achieved the highest Average Precision and F1-scores. Low-performance or ambiguous categories (e.g., other-unknown-human-voice, amplified-speech, large-crowd, pile-driver) showed lower overall F1-scores, reflecting the difficulty of reliably separating these events from acoustically similar background classes.

For downstream spatiotemporal mapping and the 311 association analysis, ensemble predictions (averaging all five fold models) were used to ensure complete dataset coverage. However, all classification performance metrics reported here are based strictly on out-of-fold predictions. No missing or corrupted entries were detected in the processed outputs, confirming the reproducibility and completeness of the evaluated results.

The reported macro-averaged metrics should therefore be interpreted as conservative segment-level performance estimates under a difficult imbalanced multi-label setting, whereas the primary contribution of the study lies in the calibrated, reproducible, and deployment-oriented monitoring pipeline built around these predictions.

Calibration and reliability

The probabilistic reliability of the CNN14 ensemble model was assessed using micro- and per-class calibration curves. Calibration curves were computed using 15 equal-width probability bins. Unlabeled entries (annotation value = − 1) were excluded from all calibration computations via the annotation mask. The micro-averaged calibration plot (Fig. 3) shows a monotonically increasing relationship between predicted and observed probabilities, with the empirical positive rate remaining below the predicted values across the probability range. This pattern is expected given the strong class imbalance of the SONYC-UST dataset, where most target classes have very low positive prevalence; accordingly, the predicted scores are most useful as relative ranking signals for downstream thresholding and coverage analyses rather than as exact absolute probabilities.

Class-level and co-occurrence analysis

Quantitative analysis of class-based behaviors revealed clear differentiation among noise categories. The class-wise Average Precision and F1 scores presented in Table 2 varied according to event energy and frequency. Classes with the highest detection success included human-voice, alert-signal, dog-barking-whining, and siren; all achieving F1 scores above 0.70 and Average Precision values above 0.70. Categories with moderate performance, such as machinery-impact, large-sounding-engine, chainsaw, and medium-sounding-engine, recorded F1 scores in the 0.30–0.50 range. Diffuse, low-amplitude, or ambiguous sound categories (including other-unknown-human-voice, amplified-speech, engine-of-uncertain-size, and pile-driver) showed lower recall rates and F1 scores below 0.20. Under the global single-threshold protocol (protocol ii) described in Sect. "Model performance overview", the macro-averaged F1 score was obtained as 0.3729 across all 35 classes, which corresponds to the deployment-realistic operating point summarized in Table 2.

Figure 5 summarizes per-class confusion statistics and performance metrics, highlighting that most prediction errors occur between acoustically related sound categories rather than across broad semantic groups. Under the median operating threshold applied in Fig. 5, high-prevalence parent categories (human-voice, alert-signal, engine, music) achieve the strongest precision–recall balance, with F1-scores in the 0.60–0.76 range. Distinctive impulsive or tonal classes such as siren, dog-barking-whining, jackhammer, car-horn, and reverse-beeper also reach competitive F1-scores (0.55–0.77). Lower performance is observed for rare or acoustically diffuse categories (ice-cream-truck, amplified-speech, large-crowd, other-unknown-human-voice), consistent with their limited training prevalence and weaker spectral discriminability. As detailed in Table 3, the median-threshold operating point coincides with the per-class F1-optimal thresholds across nearly all classes, indicating that no additional per-class tuning is required to attain the reported precision–recall behavior. Overall, the confusion patterns suggest that the model distinguishes major sound groups reliably, with most residual errors arising from fine-grained acoustic similarities rather than systematic misclassification.

The co-occurrence matrix (Fig. 6) illustrates how often annotated noise events appeared together in the same recording within the SONYC-UST ground-truth labels. Beyond the expected parent-child relationships within the hierarchical taxonomy, the strongest cross-category associations involved engine with human-voice and person-talking events, which is consistent with pedestrian-adjacent traffic scenes. Engine and alert-signal events also showed a notable association, in line with the shared urban context of vehicle traffic and emergency sirens. In contrast, human-voice had very low co-occurrence with heavy-machinery categories, consistent with typical zoning and time-of-day patterns in city activity. These co-occurrence patterns characterize the acoustic ecology and label structure of the SONYC-UST dataset, providing context for the multi-label classification task. The matrix should be interpreted as an annotation-level co-occurrence structure rather than a reflection of model prediction correlations.

Coverage and threshold behavior

Threshold and coverage analyses were performed to assess the model’s ability to balance confidence and recall when used for binary classification. Coverage curves were generated to quantify the proportion of labeled entries exceeding various precision levels, while threshold comparison tables summarized class-specific cutoff behavior across folds.

As indicated by the coverage-precision curve (Fig. 11), the relationship between target precision and dataset coverage is monotonic and smooth. At lower precision levels (P ≈ 0.5), approximately 4% of all predictions met or exceeded the threshold. As precision requirements increased, coverage decreased gradually, reaching near zero at P = 0.9. No irregularities or discontinuities were observed in the curve shape, confirming consistent probability calibration across all classes. Minimal variance was found in the micro-averaged coverage statistics derived from all folds, indicating stable ensemble behavior across different subsets of the data.

Table 3 summarizes the class-specific optimized threshold values (best_f1_th) and corresponding median threshold values (median_th) obtained from the five validation partitions. The median threshold values range from 0.50 to 0.95, reflecting class-specific differences in score distributions. For example, main categories with high prevalence (engine, alert-signal, human-voice) operated at lower threshold values (0.50–0.55), while rare or impulsive classes (ice-cream-truck, jackhammer, hoe-ram) required higher cutoff points (0.80–0.95). Notably, the class-specific F1 optimized threshold values coincided with the median threshold values for almost all classes (delta_f1 ≈ 0.000). The only exception was pile-driver, where the optimal threshold value differed by only one grid step (delta_f1 = 0.0034). This near-complete agreement indicates that the cross-fold median is already at the F1-optimal operating point for this model and dataset, and shows that a single deployment-realistic threshold vector provides performance equivalent to fine-tuning per class. Therefore, the results support the adoption of the median-threshold approach as both the practical and the performance-optimal operating regime for aggregate-level urban noise monitoring.

Computational efficiency

Each training fold was completed in approximately 5–6 h, with an average of 8.7 GB of GPU memory utilized. There were 5 trained fold models that comprised the entire ensemble; each model was saved as a 32-bit floating-point checkpoint. Each individual fold checkpoint occupied approximately 76 MB on disk (32-bit floating-point), resulting in a total ensemble footprint of approximately 380 MB. The exported TorchScript model added another 76 MB, bringing the complete deployable artifact set to approximately 460 MB. There were 62,022 records produced in the complete inference stage; each record contains 35 probability columns and 35 binary prediction columns. The aggregated prediction outputs consumed approximately 2.4 GB when exported in compressed Parquet format, and approximately 4.7 GB when exported in plain CSV format. No missing or corrupted rows were observed across output partitions, and checksum verification confirmed the data’s integrity upon export.

Discussion

This study has shown that a CNN14 based ensemble architecture is able to achieve reliable and computationally efficient multi-label urban noise classification. The subsequent sections interpret the reported metrics with respect to acoustic signal characteristics, model behavior, and potential real world applications of urban noise monitoring at a city wide level.

Macro-averaged performance metrics were generally consistent from one cross validation fold to another, indicating that the ensemble maintained a balanced relationship between precision and recall during the entire evaluation process. In contrast to typical single-fold approaches, the stratification of the SONYC-UST data into five folds provided increased robustness to the inherent class imbalance and sensor-to-sensor variability in the dataset. The consistent values across folds indicate that the features learned by the ensemble represent generalizable acoustic patterns characteristic of NewYork City’s urban environment, as opposed to site-specific or time-bound artifacts indicative of overfitting.

The model’s stronger performance at identifying high-energy sounds like sirens, jackhammers, or car horns is due to their clearer spectra. The lower performance at identifying more diffuse or low-prevalence sources, such as large-crowd or mobile-music, stems from the very small number of positive training examples and the acoustic overlap of these classes with more common sources. These types of differences are common in modeling environmental audio, where perceptually subtle classes have fewer discriminative features than others.

The model’s monotonic calibration behavior, together with the global Brier score of 0.1269, indicates that the relative ordering of predicted probabilities reflects the empirical frequency of each event, making the outputs useful as ranking signals for downstream decisions. Calibration of model predictions is important because it allows the model’s predicted probabilities to be directly translated into risk or tolerance-based decision thresholds (for instance, deciding whether or not to intervene based on predicted noise score, or estimating how much time an individual has been exposed to a particular type of noise). Given that the model preserves a consistent monotonic relationship between predicted and observed probabilities across classes, its outputs can support probabilistic ranking and threshold-based decisions. For applications requiring exact probabilistic calibration, additional post-hoc scaling could be considered as a future refinement.

From an analytical perspective, this monotonic behavior indicates that the combination of the focal-BCE loss function, EMA weight updates, and cosine learning rate schedule produced stable probability rankings across classes. For real-world monitoring systems, a good balance between discriminative strength and probability reliability is especially important, since the model’s confidence scores will drive downstream alerting or visualization pipelines.

The confusion and co-occurrence matrices demonstrate the acoustic ecology of urban noise sources. The observed co-occurrence patterns mirror the real-world spatial proximity between vehicular traffic, human presence, and related urban activity. The strongest cross-category associations involved engine with human-voice and person-talking events, while engine and alert-signal events also appeared together at notable rates, consistent with shared urban contexts. In addition, the low co-occurrence between human-voice and heavy-machinery categories is consistent with typical zoning and time-of-day patterns in city activity. A small amount of confusion exists between similar mechanical types (jackhammers, rock-drills, pile-drivers) due to similarities in their harmonic structure in the mid-frequency ranges. Future versions of this model could be improved by using a hierarchical labeling scheme or a multi-branch architecture that explicitly models the degree of similarity between classes.

The hourly and daily trends identified in the predictions match previously documented patterns of urban acoustic activity. The increased levels of predicted detection probability after sunrise, the midday plateau, and the subsequent evening decline all correspond to human work and traffic cycles. Additionally, the weekday-weekend patterns showed lower overall activity during non-working days, which supports this interpretation. Spatial heatmaps identified concentrated noise hotspots in central and lower Manhattan, where high levels of traffic, construction, and emergency-vehicle activity occur.

The results show that the model had decision boundaries that were highly consistent across the 35 acoustic categories; in nearly every class, the cross-fold median threshold coincided exactly with the per-class F1-optimal threshold (delta_f1 ≈ 0.000 for 34 of 35 classes), indicating that a single uniformly applied threshold vector yields performance equivalent to per-class fine tuning. This convergence simplifies deployment without sacrificing discriminative quality. Also, the “coverage-precision” curve showed a consistent and monotonically increasing trend of predictive accuracy with respect to model-confidence, allowing for both noise-detection ranking/prioritization in operational settings as well as the ability to map detected noise to geographic locations and therefore assess spatially coherent sensor placement and inference-pipeline reliability. Further, there was significant evidence that the identified predicted noise-patterns matched the established urban-activity-cycles, further supporting the framework as both a diagnostic-tool and a decision-support-tool for policy-making.

The comparison with the NYC 311 complaint system provided an independent measure of perceived noise disturbance. Although the Spearman rank-correlation between predicted noise scores and complaint frequencies was modest in magnitude (ρ = 0.087), the permutation test confirmed high statistical significance (p < 0.001). The modest effect size is methodologically expected and can be attributed to five distinct confounding mechanisms:

Despite these attenuating factors, the quantile-binned analysis with 95% confidence intervals demonstrated a systematic positive relationship between predicted noise scores and complaint counts across the entire score range. The permutation test (p < 0.001) confirmed that this association is unlikely to arise by chance, providing meaningful evidence of ecological agreement between model-derived predictions and community-perceived noise disturbance at the aggregate level. No causal link is implied.

The inclusion of this analysis was to demonstrate that the proposed approach has an existing external proxy for practical applicability, while acknowledging ecological agreement rather than causal inference.

Several limitations of the complaint-based corroboration should be noted. NYC 311 complaints reflect reporting behavior influenced by demographics, digital access, and individual propensity to report, rather than objective acoustic conditions alone. Temporal delays between noise events and complaint filing may attenuate measured associations. The complaint categories used (‘Noise’ and ‘Construction Noise’) represent broad descriptors that do not map directly to the fine-grained acoustic taxonomy of the SONYC-UST dataset. These factors collectively explain why even perfect acoustic predictions would not achieve strong correlation with complaint frequencies.

The association between predicted noise scores and complaint counts was robust to spatial resolution: consistent results (Spearman ρ = 0.087, p < 0.001) were obtained at both 250 m and 500 m grid resolutions, indicating that the observed relationship is not an artifact of the spatial aggregation scale.

The results for the computational-performance-metrics clearly show that the proposed approach has sufficient capability to support large-scale urban-acoustic-monitoring with relatively small computing-resources. The training time of the model on a single mid-range-GPU was less than 6 h per fold, and the ability to perform real-time-inference of more than 2,000 audio-segments per-second shows that continuous monitoring with manageable resource-requirements is possible. Additionally, the TorchScript deployment artifact is approximately 76 MB, which allows the model to be deployed on many types of edge or embedded-devices, thereby expanding the scope of where the model can be used beyond the traditional data-center.

In summary, these quantitative-results demonstrate that the proposed approach is a reliable and applicable methodology, demonstrated on 62,022 recordings from 56 sensors across New York City, for assessing urban noise patterns, specifically the combination of macro-averaged-F1-score/mAP, along with stable-probability-calibration and efficient computation capabilities provide a strong basis for systematic environmental-sound monitoring. In addition, this pipeline can serve as an empirical layer for various analytical tasks such as urban-planning, exposure-studies or soundscape-analytics, due to its ability to incorporate sensor-data as well as spatiotemporal-aggregation. Finally, the explicit-metadata, reproducible-code, and consistent-processing-methodology employed in this work, facilitates methodological-transparency and will allow researchers to independently verify or extend the results to other metropolitan areas.

It can be seen from Table 4 that most previous works have focused on environmental sound classification tasks that use curated datasets (limited number of classes, clean clips), which makes these tasks simpler than what would exist in a real-world urban environment, where there could be several different sounds (overlapping sources), ambient background noise, and variability in both time and space. Some recent datasets (UN15, SONYC-UST) have attempted to bridge this gap with real-world urban audio, but UN15 is still quite small and SONYC-UST has used very simple baselines. The present study distinguishes itself by combining a large amount of real-world sensor data, a modern deep multi-label classifier, calibrated probabilistic outputs, and spatiotemporal aggregation and mapping—all of which address multiple shortcomings identified in ESC surveys. Therefore, while this is not the first attempt to classify urban sounds, the proposed comprehensive end-to-end pipeline for classification, calibration, and spatiotemporal analysis represents a meaningful advance in realistic and operational urban noise monitoring.

Because evaluation methodologies differ fundamentally among urban sound tagging systems, comparing classification metrics presents inherent challenges. Both the original SONYC baseline and DCASE challenge submissions reported micro-averaged AUPRC separately for coarse-level categories (8 classes) and fine-level categories (23 classes). The system described in this study, by contrast, employed macro-averaged metrics computed across all 35 hierarchical acoustic presence labels. Micro-averaging weights each sample equally, inherently favoring high-prevalence categories such as engine and human-voice; macro-averaging assigns equal weight to each class regardless of sample frequency, imposing stricter requirements on low-prevalence events such as pile-driver and ice-cream-truck.

Despite these methodological differences, the cross-system comparison reveals a clear trade-off between classification optimization and operational readiness. The DCASE 2019 winner achieved micro-averaged AUPRC values of 0.860 (coarse) and 0.751 (fine) through augmentation pipelines and label-weighting schemes tailored for challenge evaluation criteria. Notably, neither the winning submission nor other participants provided information regarding probability calibration quality, model export format, or downstream integration pathways.

The pipeline developed in this research explicitly prioritized deployment-oriented requirements over benchmark optimization. Validated probability calibration permits confidence-based enforcement policies for municipal noise regulation; TorchScript conversion removes Python runtime dependencies for edge deployment; and integrated spatiotemporal aggregation enables direct integration with urban planning workflows. Comparison with NYC 311 records (Sect. "External association analysis using NYC 311 noise complaints") confirmed that these design choices yield ecologically valid predictions at the metropolitan scale, despite moderate segment-level classification scores. This design stance reflects the core thesis: practical urban noise monitoring demands holistic pipeline engineering rather than isolated classification improvements.

To contextualize the macro-averaged scores reported here against the broader SONYC-UST literature, it is useful to note published results on the same dataset under their original protocols. The SONYC-UST v2baseline reported macro-AUPRC of approximately 0.44 (23 fine classes) and 0.49 (8 coarse classes)4. The top DCASE 2020 Task 5 entry by Arnault et al.25 achieved validation macro-AUPRC of 0.62 (fine) and 0.82 (coarse), while a CNN10 PANNs baseline reported in the same study reached 0.559 (fine) and 0.823 (coarse) under Audioset pretraining. The present macro-AP of 0.3159 is not directly comparable to these benchmarks: the cited systems evaluate either 23 fine or 8 coarse classes in isolation, whereas this study evaluates a single combined 35-label set that includes 23 fine, 6 coarse parents, and 6 “uncertain-source” variants. The latter, together with several rare fine classes, have low prevalence and ambiguous acoustic boundaries, and macro averaging weights them equally with high-prevalence categories. Restricted to the high-prevalence parent categories, the per-class F1 values in Table 2 (engine = 0.600, alert-signal = 0.714, human-voice = 0.752, music = 0.652, dog-barking = 0.769) fall within the operational range of the cited SOTA systems on the comparable coarse-level task. The combined 35-label macro score thus reflects the broader and more difficult evaluation set adopted here rather than a deficiency in classifier capacity.

Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated an end-to-end urban noise monitoring pipeline extending from raw acoustic sensor data to city-scale policy support. The proposed framework addresses a critical gap in existing systems by prioritizing operational requirements over classification accuracy optimization.

The CNN14-based ensemble architecture provided reliable probability calibration across 35 hierarchical acoustic presence labels and demonstrated consistent performance throughout five-fold cross-validation. The pipeline offers TorchScript export enabling deployment on edge devices without Python dependency, processing throughput exceeding 2,000 audio segments per second, and integrated 250-meter spatial grid mapping with temporal aggregation modules. Independent external corroboration using NYC 311 complaint records confirmed that predicted noise scores are significantly associated with community-perceived disturbance.

The findings demonstrate that deployable urban acoustic monitoring systems depend not solely on model performance, but on a holistic design approach encompassing calibration, deployment infrastructure, and spatiotemporal integration. The presented pipeline has the potential to serve as an empirical foundation for urban planning, environmental exposure studies, and noise management policies.

Limitations and future work

Although the proposed pipeline architecture produced consistent and interpretable predictions, several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. Given the significant class imbalance in the SONYC-UST dataset and the low signal-to-noise ratios in real-world recordings, moderate classification performance is an expected outcome and does not diminish the practical utility of the framework.

Dominant categories such as engine and human-voice contain far more examples than rare events in the dataset. Although class-weighted sampling and focal cross-entropy provided improvement, imbalances in recall and precision persist. Additionally, certain construction and machinery sounds share similar spectral profiles, indicating that spectral features alone may be insufficient for complete discrimination. Processing all inputs as short-duration mel-spectrograms enabled computational efficiency but limited the representation of prolonged or evolving sound events. A related methodological consideration concerns the cross-validation design: splitting was performed at the annotation-row level (62,022 rows) rather than at the unique audio-file level (18,510 files), since the SONYC-UST taxonomy provides multiple independent annotator-specific label vectors per recording. A strict clip-level grouped cross-validation is identified as a refinement for future work.

The geographic scope of this study is limited to New York City, where the SONYC sensor network was deployed. The acoustic profile of NYC (characterized by dense traffic, frequent construction activity, and high emergency vehicle presence) may not be representative of other metropolitan areas with different urban morphologies, climates, or noise regulation frameworks. Adapting the pipeline to new cities would likely require retraining or fine tuning the classification model using local acoustic data, potentially through transfer learning or domain adaptation approaches.

The spatial distribution of SONYC sensors across NYC is not uniform; certain boroughs and neighborhoods have higher sensor density than others. This non-uniform coverage introduces potential bias in both the training data (over-representing acoustic environments near sensor clusters) and the spatial heatmap outputs (providing finer resolution only in well-instrumented areas). Users of the spatial analysis products should interpret coverage-sparse regions with appropriate caution.

The dataset spans recordings from 2016 to 2019 and does not explicitly model seasonal variation. Urban noise patterns are known to fluctuate with seasonal factors such as summer construction peaks, winter holiday activity, and weather-dependent outdoor behavior. While the temporal aggregation results (Figs. 7 and 8) capture diel and weekly cycles, the extent to which these patterns generalize beyond the recorded four-year period has not been evaluated.

Future work could address these limitations through transformer-based temporal modeling, graph-based spatial learning, hierarchical taxonomy design, distributed inference optimization, and adoption of clip-level grouped cross-validation.
