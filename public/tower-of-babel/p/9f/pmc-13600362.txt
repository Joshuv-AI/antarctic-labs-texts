Nesting a Target Study within a Target Trial
Authors: Xinyi Sun, Theodore J. Iwashyna, Emmanuel F. Drabo, Deidra C. Crews, Kadija Ferryman, John W. Jackson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a novel framework (TS+TT) to nest a target study (TS) within a target trial (TT) for evaluating the effects of interventions on disparities. The TS component grounds the measurement of disparity in ethical assumptions, based on the concept of allowability, and anchors it to an explicit population within calendar time. It specifies an enrollment plan of stratified sampling of eligible persons to yield a sample where social groups are distributionally similar on covariates deemed allowable for measuring disparity. Within this enrolled sample, the TT component specifies randomization of intervention strategies within each social group. Because social groups are similarly situated on allowable covariates at baseline, and because assigned intervention arms are exchangeable within social groups, TS+TT reflects a meaningful causal estimand for evaluating how interventions impact disparity. We describe the framework’s key components, its emulation, and demonstrate its application to evaluate how hypothetical interventions on pulse oximeter bias affect disparities in treatment receipt in clinical care. We also extend semiparametric G-computation to accommodate continuous stochastic interventions and estimate counterfactual disparities in time-to-event outcomes. The TS+TT framework offers a versatile and policy-relevant approach for generating ethically informed causal evidence to reduce disparities and avoid exacerbating disparities.

Disparities in healthcare-related outcomes are defined by the Institute of Medicine as “differences in the quality of care that are not due to access-related factors or clinical needs, preferences, and appropriateness of intervention,”1 a definition which has been influential.2 While certain components of this definition (e.g., access, preferences) are debatable, it places ethics at the center of disparity measurement—highlighting the need to distinguish between fair and unfair sources of difference.3 This principle, often referred to as allowability, builds normative and ethical assumptions into the disparity estimand. Allowable covariates that reflect fair sources of difference (if any) should be adjusted for when measuring a disparity, whereas nonallowable covariates that reflect social disadvantage and contribute to the disparities should not4 (See Cook et al.3 and Jackson et al.5 for discussion on defining allowable and nonallowable covariates).

Despite the central importance of allowability, many analytic approaches used to evaluate the impact of interventions on disparities fail to incorporate this principle. Traditional regression estimators typically condition on all covariates required for confounding control or statistical efficiency, implicitly treating them as appropriate for defining disparity.6 When these covariates include nonallowables (e.g., socioeconomic status), such adjustments can remove unfair components of the disparity that we aim to characterize and doing so can distort the magnitude and direction of the intervention effect on disparity.6

Meanwhile, the target trial (TT) framework provides a rigorous approach for estimating intervention effects on health outcomes using observational data.7 By aligning study components to emulate a hypothetical randomized trial, the TT clarifies the research question and estimand, mitigates common biases, and supports causal inference. However, the original TT framework was not designed to evaluate interventions in terms of their effects on outcome disparities. Although the TT balances all potential confounders across intervention arms (to mimic randomization), it does not specify which covariates should be treated as allowable when defining disparities. If applied to the disparity setting, the TT would implicitly treat all group differences as nonallowable, leaving social groups imbalanced on these factors. Such approaches are adequate when disparity can be conceptualized as inequality (see Caniglia et al.8 for an example in epidemiology, Lundberg9 for an example in sociology, and Braveman10 for further discussion of inequality vs. disparity). But they fall short in healthcare settings where differences in clinical need are often considered allowable and should be balanced across social groups, as outcome differences stemming from the differential distribution of allowables are often not considered morally concerning and are thus excluded from measures of disparities in healthcare outcomes (e.g., receipt or timing of treatment).4,11 Because the interpretation of disparity and intervention effect estimates depends on implicit normative choices, existing methods may have limited value for evaluations of fairness and for policy decision-making.

Recent work by Jackson and colleagues5 has begun to close this gap by reviewing allowability-based criteria and developing analytic approaches for estimating intervention effects on disparities in both health outcomes and healthcare decision-making.6 They also introduced the target study (TS) model to measure disparities descriptively while accounting for allowability, using a sampling-based design that can be emulated using observed data.12

In this article, we build on these advances by proposing a unified framework that nests the TS within the TT (henceforth referred to as TS+TT) to evaluate the effects of hypothetical interventions on disparity. Our TS+TT framework introduces two key innovations, extending the TT framework in two ways. First, we prespecify an enrollment window and a stratified sampling design that, together, reflect a meaningful disparity estimand that is grounded in ethical principles and anchored to a well-defined population in calendar time. Second, we conceptualize a stratified randomized trial in which randomization occurs within each social group, to ensure exchangeability across interventions and support causal inference. By embedding a TS within a TT, our framework enables researchers to estimate intervention effects on disparities in a principled and morally interpretable way. In our motivating example, we also adapt semiparametric G-computation to evaluate stochastic intervention effects on disparities in time-to-event outcomes. Together, this work offers a flexible, policy-relevant framework for both identifying and addressing disparities across outcome and intervention types.

MOTIVATING EXAMPLE

Pulse oximeter estimates peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), whereas true saturation is measured by arterial blood gas (SaO2). Historically, SpO2 overestimates SaO2 in persons with dark skin, potentially leading to delayed treatment and worse health outcomes.13 The Food and Drug Administration’s 2025 draft guidance emphasizes the need to improve pulse oximeter performance across diverse skin tones.14 In healthcare data, race and ethnicity are often used as proxies for skin tone,15 as direct measures are typically unavailable in electronic medical records (EMR). Let G=1 denote the disadvantaged group (e.g., non-Hispanic Black), and G=0 the advantaged group (e.g., non-Hispanic White). Suppose a healthcare regulator seeks to evaluate how differential pulse oximeter accuracy by race impacts disparities in clinical care, such as dexamethasone treatment in COVID-19 patients. These considerations are central to determining what levels of bias should be considered as “acceptable” for regulating device development, validation, and clinical use.

The population of interest is adults receiving inpatient care for COVID-19 at a large regional health system in the United States. Enrollment Windows

We define the enrollment window as a narrow span in calendar time, denoted by k. In medical settings, the length of the window (e.g., days, months) can reflect a clinical timeframe wherein treatment decisions of one window are unrelated to those of earlier windows.12,16 Within each window, individuals are assessed for eligibility, and allowable covariates must be defined and measured by the end of the window. Persons may appear in multiple windows over time but may enroll only once per window. This structure conceptualizes a series of trials, each with a clearly defined target population at its corresponding calendar time, and facilitates the stratified sampling procedure (discussed shortly) that balances allowable covariates across groups at time k. It also makes temporal heterogeneity explicit and amenable to interpretable aggregation or modeling. Both disparities and the effects of interventions may vary over time due to evolving clinical protocols, fluctuating resource constraints, or broader contextual changes.17 Indexing each trial to a calendar time k allows investigators to examine, model, or aggregate results across the distribution of enrollment times, rather than implicitly assuming homogeneity over time (see eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371 for more details on the modeling and aggregation of calendar time).

In our motivating example, we used a 12-hour enrollment window under the conjecture that diagnostic work-ups initiated in response to SpO2 measurements would be unrelated if separated by 12 or more hours. Conceptually, our trial specification assumes that treatment decisions are guided by SpO2, with SaO2 masked. In this idealized trial,18 prior diagnostic work-ups would not influence subsequent decisions, even if the same individual were eligible in consecutive windows.

Enrollment Groups

We define the social groups among whom disparities are measured based on characteristics thought to reflect systems of historical and/or contemporary (dis)advantage.19–22 Here, we focus on race and ethnicity, denoted by G, with the two groups of interest being non-Hispanic Black (G=1) and non-Hispanic White (G=0). Our choice is motivated by the long-standing evidence of degraded pulse oximeter accuracy for persons with dark skin tones.23–26 While skin tone is unmeasured in EMR, it is strongly correlated with Black and White race,27,28 and thus our choice leverages a meaningful proxy for differences in skin pigmentation to assess the population impacts of pulse oximeter bias. In our example, we do not examine Hispanic or mixed racial/ethnic groups for two reasons: (1) our data used to emulate the TS+TT design cannot distinguish racial categories within the Hispanic category, where there is substantial heterogeneity in racial composition and thus skin tone, and (2) insufficient sample size.

Allowable Covariates

We compare social groups who are similarly situated on “allowable” covariates, denoted by Ak, those whose differential distribution is not considered to lead to disparity. The moral reasoning underlying allowability depends on the type of outcome being studied—specifically, whether the outcome represents a health care good (e.g., treatment receipt) or a health status (e.g., mortality). For treatment outcomes (e.g., dexamethasone), allowable covariates typically include indicators of clinical need that are considered appropriate for guiding treatment decisions, consistent with established medical guidelines.11,29 For health status outcomes, allowable covariates may include those that, at baseline, have a favorable distribution for the disadvantaged group, so as to avoid masking estimates of disparity5 (although there are arguments against such adjustments for health status outcomes).30,31 All other covariates used in the analysis are considered nonallowable, denoted by Nk, and are included to account for potential biases such as confounding or selection bias but do not define the measure of disparity or the intervention effect. In our example, because the outcome is dexamethasone treatment, age, sex, comorbidities, vitals, laboratory measurements, and indicators of clinical severity (e.g., steroid and vasopressor use in the inpatient encounter but before SpO2 measurement) reflect legitimate differences in clinical need within the current decision window and are therefore considered allowable. In contrast, smoking status and measures of socioeconomic status (e.g., the Area Deprivation Index) are treated as nonallowable because they do not represent clinical need for treatment decisions and may reflect underlying social disadvantage or potentially lead to implicit bias in treatment decision-making. Since these factors may influence both oxygen saturation levels and treatment decisions,13,32 we include them to adjust for potential confounding but not to define the disparity’s measure.

Standard Population

We define a within-study standard population, denoted by T=1, to guide the balancing of allowable covariates across social groups during enrollment.12 At each calendar time k, we sample persons so that the distribution of allowable covariates Ak is the same across social groups and matches that of the standard population. Under intervention effect heterogeneity (across allowable covariates), the choice of T=1 can impact the magnitude and direction of the effect. Selecting the disadvantaged group helps to estimate excess potential benefits and harms for this population in medical and public health planning.12 We choose the non-Hispanic Black group based on evidence of worse pulse oximeter accuracy among Black patients.23 Note that, with multiple social groups, it is possible to specify simultaneous TS+TT designs each with their own standard population.12

Enrollment Process (Stratified Sampling)

At each calendar time k, eligible persons enroll through a stratified sampling process conducted separately within each social group. First, we identify eligible persons from each group based on criteria measured during the enrollment window, mapping to a well-defined population at calendar time k. Second, within each group, we sample persons so that the distribution of allowable covariates Ak matches that of the within-study standard population T=1. This sampling procedure ensures that any disparity in outcomes is not due to differences in allowable covariate distributions across groups. Although conceptual, a schematic example of how such stratified sampling could be implemented is provided in the eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371.

Intervention Strategies

Intervention strategies specify the actions or exposures whose effects the investigator seeks to evaluate. In applied settings, these strategies may compare two treatments or alternative policies. They may be deterministic or stochastic and may assign categorical (e.g., medication type) or continuous (e.g., levels of environmental pollution) exposures. Intervention strategies define the distribution of the exposure Z that would be assigned in each arm of the trial. It is possible to devise strategies that depend only on covariates that are ethically appropriate for guiding the intervention’s allocation of treatment. This raises the concept of “intervention-allowability”5 that we address in the Discussion. In our example, we consider hypothetical scenarios in which pulse oximeter devices exhibit different degrees of bias, producing different distributions of SpO2 for individuals with darker versus lighter skin tones. Accordingly, we conceptualize each intervention strategy as a stochastic intervention that sets the SpO2 for each social group to the distribution that would arise under a device with a specified level of bias.

Under the hypothetical trial, both providers and patients are assumed to be masked to SaO2. For example, SaO2 might be continuously monitored in a way that is not disclosed to clinical staff or patients, such that treatment decisions are guided solely by SpO2.

Stratified (Within-group) Randomization

After enrollment, we immediately randomize persons within each social group G=g to intervention arms. This setup achieves two forms of balance: first, the stratified sampling procedure in the earlier step ensures that allowable covariates are balanced across social groups, reflecting a meaningful disparity estimand. Second, the randomization procedure in this step balances both allowable covariates Ak and nonallowable covariates Nk across intervention arms within each group, ensuring exchangeability of intervention arms.

EMULATION OF THE TARGET STUDY WITHIN THE TARGET TRIAL USING SECONDARY DATA

Overview

Although we could, in theory, implement the TS+TT protocol to evaluate how pulse oximeter bias influences disparities, doing so would be ethically and logistically infeasible. For example, our motivating example would require assigning Black persons to devices with known unequal accuracy. Therefore, we instead emulate the TS+TT using secondary data, mapping each component (Table 1) to information available in EMR. To emulate components that are not directly observed, such as sampling-based enrollment, within-group randomization, and calendar time aggregation, we apply a sequential regression and prediction procedure known as G-computation.6,33 Beyond our example, other approaches, including clone-censor-weight methods, could be used for emulation using weights that emulate the sampling and randomization steps.6,34

Data Structure

We construct a longitudinal dataset for emulating TS+TT. A column of calendar time k defines the enrollment window. At each k, we identify eligible persons and record their social group membership G. Each person i contributes one row per calendar time k when eligibility criteria are met and may appear in multiple windows over time. We include a variable of exposure Z (e.g., SpO2). We select allowable covariates Ak (e.g., clinical need) to similarly situate social groups within each enrollment window and define a standard population 𝑇 = 1 (e.g., Non-Hispanic Black) that determines the distribution of Ak to which groups are standardized. We also include nonallowable covariates Nk (e.g., Area Deprivation Index) to control for confounding. Finally, we attach the outcome Yk+J (e.g., dexamethasone treatment) for each record indexed by k, and the duration (denoted as Jk) from time zero to the time of either the outcome or administrative censoring, whichever occurs first. The hypothesized causal relationships among these variables are depicted in the directed acyclic graph in the eAppendix (eFigure 1; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371).

In summary, each row of the analytic dataset is indexed by the vector (i,k,G,T,Z,Ak,Nk,Yk+J,Jk) for a person i at time k. The data only include calendar times when all social groups of interest are represented, to ensure that aggregated disparity reflects an aggregation of estimates from well-defined populations across calendar time.

Identification and Estimation

The counterfactual disparity (1) and intervention effect on disparity (2) are not directly observed. To identify these quantities from observational data, we rely on a set of identification assumptions: overlap and innocuous sampling12 (TS component), and additionally conditional exchangeability, positivity, and consistency35 (TT component).

We require overlap12 in the distribution of allowable covariates: At each calendar time k, persons of each social group G=g are observed at each level of the allowable covariates Ak observed within the standard population (T=1). Under innocuous sampling, the sampling procedure itself does not change how outcomes are conditionally distributed.12 We assume that the effect of the intervention z~ is unconfounded given the allowables Ak and nonallowable Nk, separately within each social group G, at each time k. We further assume positivity and consistency.35 Formal definitions of these assumptions appear in the eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371.

DATA APPLICATION

Methods

We applied our framework to EMR data from the Johns Hopkins COVID-19 Registry (JH-CROWN; March 2020–June 2024) to evaluate how differential accuracy in pulse oximeter affect disparities in dexamethasone treatment among hospitalized COVID-19 patients. To characterize differential measurement error, we selected observations with paired SpO2 and SaO2 measurements recorded within 10 minutes of one another within each enrollment window (eFigure 3; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371).15 To emulate our TS+TT design, we further restricted to instances where SaO2 was measured after SpO2 (eFigure 4; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371). To focus on clinically relevant decision thresholds in our TS+TT emulation, analyses were restricted to SaO2 values between 88% and 94%, a range in which measurement inaccuracy is most likely to affect treatment initiation and timing.15 Race and ethnicity were self-reported and categorized according to the 2024 U.S. Office of Management and Budget standards,37 using the “most frequent multiple responses” approach (see details in the eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371). The study was approved by the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine Institutional Review Board.

We modeled nine hypothetical intervention arms (Table 2), corresponding to pulse oximeter devices that differed in the degree of differential accuracy across racial groups. Each strategy specified group-specific distributions of measurement error that generated observed SpO2 values from underlying SaO2, with one reference strategy representing nondifferential accuracy. These strategies were designed to reflect empirically plausible ranges of measurement error observed in prior studies.15,32,38–48

To emulate the TS+TT, we followed the approach described in the previous section, with allowable and nonallowable covariates specified as shown in Table 1. Because some individuals contributed multiple observations, we used a nonparametric, balanced, stratified cluster bootstrap that resamples persons with replacement to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs).6,49 We handled missing covariates (eTable 6; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371) using multiple imputation by chained equations, implemented via the mice package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).50 We also evaluated our model specification by comparing observed outcomes with those estimated under a natural course scenario (eTable 5; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371).51 To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. The second analysis excluded observations with SaO2 measurements in the prior enrollment window to probe potential residual influence of prior diagnostic testing. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2. Results

Observed Measurement Error in Pulse Oximetry

Among 15,122 SpO2–SaO2 pairs (7,350 from non-Hispanic Black; 7,772 from non-Hispanic White) used to characterize differential measurement error, non-Hispanic Black patients had a higher mean (2.0% vs. 0.6%) and greater standard deviation (3.6% vs. 3.3%) (eTable 1; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371). Occult hypoxemia (SaO2 < 88% while SpO2 ≥ 92%) was more common among non-Hispanic Black patients. The cohort for the TS+TT emulation included 1883 eligible observations (907 from non-Hispanic White; 976 from non-Hispanic Black) from 460 trials (eTable 7; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371). Detailed cohort characteristics are provided in eTable 2; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371.

Observed Additive Disparities in 24-hour Dexamethasone Treatment Receipt (%) and Time-to-treatment

Before standardizing on allowable covariates, non-Hispanic Black patients were 6.4% (95% CI: −13.4, 0.6) less likely to receive treatment compared with non-Hispanic White patients and experienced longer time to treatment (RMST difference: 54.2 minutes, 95% CI: −5.5, 114.0) (Table 3). After standardization, the estimated additive disparities were attenuated: the treatment receipt disparity was −2.2% (95% CI: −8.2, 3.7), and the RMST disparity was 23.6 minutes (95% CI: −27.0, 74.2). These results illustrate how the TS component clarifies differences that are not attributable to differences in allowable factors when measuring disparity.

Simulated Additive Disparities in Dexamethasone Treatment and Additive Intervention Effects

Under the reference device with equal accuracy, the additive treatment disparity was −2.7% (95% CI: −8.8, 3.3) and the RMST disparity was 30.0 minutes (95% CI: −26.3, 86.3) (Table 4). Across simulated devices introducing varying degrees of differential measurement error, additive disparities were similar in magnitude to those under the reference scenario, with small intervention effects. For example, under the intervention with Δμ = 2 and Δσ = 0, disparities in treatment receipt and RMST changed little from the reference, with estimated effects of 0.9% (95% CI: −0.9, 2.8) and −1.1 minutes (95% CI: −6.9, 4.8), respectively. Overall, disparities persisted across all simulated scenarios and were only slightly altered across forms of differential measurement error.

Sensitivity Analyses

In the sensitivity analysis that swapped SpO2 and SaO2, simulated disparities were generally larger and increased with the severity of differential accuracy (eTable 3; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371 vs. Table 4). For example, the intervention with Δμ = 2 and Δσ = 3—the most biased device—exacerbated the treatment disparity compared with the reference (−10.7% vs. −5.3%), corresponding to an intervention effect of −5.4% (95% CI: −7.9, −2.8). Results were similar to the primary analysis when excluding observations with SaO2 measurements in the prior window (eTable 4; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371 vs. Table 4).

DISCUSSION

We have proposed a novel TS+TT framework for evaluating intervention effects on disparities. The TS component grounds disparity measurement in allowability-based ethical principles and anchors it to an explicit population in calendar time through stratified sampling, while the TT component conceptualizes randomization of interventions within social groups. Nesting the TS within the TT yields well-defined causal estimands for how interventions impact disparities and provides a transparent, general framework to inform policy and clinical decision-making centered on fairness in health and healthcare.

In our application, we defined “outcome-allowable” covariates appropriate for defining the outcome disparity, but our TS+TT framework is flexible enough to accommodate intervention-allowable covariates. These covariates reflect normative judgments about which characteristics are considered morally appropriate for allocating exposure across social groups5 and, in this sense, help define the interventions. For instance, one could define interventions that assign different degrees of permissible bias in SpO2 within levels of SaO2 (e.g., 70%–85%, ≥85%). In fact, recent Food and Drug Administration draft guidance specifies SaO2-stratified performance thresholds that permit larger bias at lower SaO2 levels (e.g., 3.5% for SaO2 70%–85%) and smaller bias at higher SaO2 levels (e.g., 1.5% for SaO2 ≥85%). This implicitly treats SaO2 as an allowable variable that governs how much bias in device performance is considered tolerable, likely reflecting engineering constraints. We do not take a position on specific allowability choices in this application and do not implement this approach due to limited sample size within SaO2 strata. However, the framework is explicitly structured to support such extensions when allowability criteria are clearly specified and data permit. Specifying strategies that assign treatment according to intervention-allowable covariates is motivated for strategies intended to be salutary.

Our framework provides a practical tool for policy and regulatory decision-making. In the context of pulse oximeter bias, it enables evaluation of how varying degrees of bias affect treatment disparities and can inform evidence-based standards for device performance. More broadly, it can be applied to other measurement or decision systems—such as predictive algorithms or clinical rules52–56—to assess how algorithmic design choices shape downstream disparities and to inform interventions that reduce, rather than inadvertently exacerbate, health disparities.

Our data application faced limitations inherent to emulating an idealized trial with real-world data. First, masking clinicians to SaO2 was infeasible in routine clinical care. Although we approximated masking, residual awareness or unmeasured clinical need may still exist. In addition, our application estimated an intention-to-treat effect of baseline biased SpO2 but did not fully capture the cumulative impact of sustained measurement bias over the duration of care (i.e., subsequent SpO2 readings during follow-up). Turning to the magnitude of the disparities, the risk of adverse consequences from estimated delays in treatment (approximately 30 minutes across scenarios) among non-Hispanic Black patients is unclear. We suspect that such risks are low for any individual but may be noticeable at the population level,57 which could be investigated by examining the impacts of bias thresholds on downstream adverse outcomes. Weak intervention effects may also reflect features of this specific clinical context, such as standardized treatment protocols and the use of multiple clinical indicators beyond SpO2.58 In light of that, we conducted analyses restricted to patients near treatment-relevant clinical thresholds (SaO2 88%–94%), where inaccurate SpO2 readings are most likely to influence treatment decisions.15 Our sensitivity analyses that swapped SaO2 and SpO2 yielded larger disparities and intervention effects, providing insight into settings where pulse oximeter bias may be more consequential when treatment decisions rely more heavily on SpO2. Furthermore, estimation relied on standard causal assumptions. While most of these assumptions are either untestable or difficult to fully verify with EMR data, we have discussed their implications and potential challenges in detail in the eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371. Finally, our data pertained to a single regional health system. We note that the TS component can specify sampling strategies that can be emulated to support generalizability or transportability to well-defined populations of interest,12 which can be adapted to TS+TT.

As discussed in the eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C371, TS+TT shares much in common with other approaches involving hypothetical interventions in the disparities setting, such as causal decompositions that account for allowability,5 or approximations of causal mediation estimands that intervene on group membership.59 But the TS+TT framework is more universal. It allows multiple intervention strategies, aligns naturally with trial-based policy questions, and extends naturally to longitudinal settings with dynamic treatment strategies. In this sense, causal decompositions are a special case that TS+TT can subsume, although TS+TT is not itself a decomposition nor a mediation estimand.
