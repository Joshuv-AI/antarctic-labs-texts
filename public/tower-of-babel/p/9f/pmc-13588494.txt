Virologic outcomes among people living with HIV on dolutegravir-based antiretroviral therapy in Kenya, 2018–2024: a comparison retrospective longitudinal analysis
Authors: Helen M Chun, Sherri L Pals, Mary Gikura, Pacific Akinyi, Ashley Odhiambo, Emilio Dirlikov, Appolonia Aoko, Freda Kinoti, Kyle Milligan, Ruzuna Moturi, Jared Mecha, Catherine Njigua, Junghae Muthoni, Frank Basiye, Kenneth Masamaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundLong-term virologic outcome data of tenofovir fumarate disoproxil-lamivudine-dolutegravir (TLD) versus tenofovir fumarate disoproxil-lamivudine-efavirenz (TLE) from resource-constrained settings remain limited. This study evaluates virologic suppression (VS) among people living with HIV (PLWH) ≥15 years in Kenya following TLD introduction in 2019.MethodsWe analyzed Kenya's National AIDS and STIs Control Programme data (2018–2024) including ART-naïve and treatment-experienced PLWH initiating TLD (TLD1), transitioning to TLD (TLD2), or initiating TLE (TLE1). Outcomes included virologic suppression (VS: <1000 copies/mL)–including undetectable (UD: <50 copies/mL), durable undetectable (durable UD: <50 copies/mL for the three most recent results), and low-level viremia (LLV: 50–999 copies/mL), viral load non-suppression (VLNS: ≥1000 copies/mL), and virologic failure (VF: ≥2 consecutive VLNS). We fit logistic regression models to assess associations with virologic outcomes.FindingsAmong 1,072,209 PLWH records, 758,398 (70.7%) had ≥1 VL. VS was highest in TLD2 (96.3–97.1%), followed by TLD1 (94.4–96.2%) and TLE1 (91.1–92.7%). Long-term VS (at 24–36 months) exceeded 95% for TLD1 and TLD2 but remained approximately 92% for TLE1 (p < 0.0001). Durable UD was higher in TLD2 (aOR) 1.95 [95% CI 1.88–2.01] and TLD1 (aOR 1.56 [95% CI 1.51–1.61]; p < 0.0001) versus TLE1, even with initial VLNS: TLD1 (aOR 2.31 [95% CI 1.94–2.76] and TLD2 aOR 2.39 [95% CI 2.00–2.85]). Even with initial VS, adolescents (15–19 years) had higher odds of VF: females aOR 2.07 [95% CI 1.49–2.87] and males aOR 5.39 [95% CI 3.64–7.98]. Pre-transition and initial LLV increased the risk of post-transition VLNS (aOR 1.51 [95% CI 1.42–1.60]; p < 0.0001) and VF, highest at 500–999 copies/mL (aOR 3.88 [95% CI 3.33–4.53]; p < 0.0001).InterpretationKenya's ART optimization with TLD was associated with superior outcomes compared to TLE, with higher early and sustained VS, better re-suppression post-VF, and improved durability of UD. Targeted interventions are needed for adolescents, those with LLV, and higher pre-transition VL to enhance long-term virologic control and end HIV as a public health threat.FundingNo specific funding was received for the analysis. Introduction

Kenya implemented a structured transition from non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)-based to dolutegravir (DTG)-based tenofovir-lamivudine-dolutegravir (TLD) antiretroviral therapy (ART). Prior to 2017, NNRTI-based regimens were the first-line ART. In 2017, DTG was introduced through a pilot program, and by 2018, national guidelines recommended TLD for adults and adolescents aged 15 and older, requiring a documented viral load within the past 12 months for regimen optimization. Initially, individuals with virologic suppression on NNRTI-based regimens were prioritized for TLD transition, while those with virologic non-suppression were switched to protease inhibitor-based ART. The 2018 World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations endorsed an integrase-strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) DTG-based regimen as preferred first-line for people living with HIV (PLWH) initiating ART and second-line for PLWH failing non-DTG-based regimens.1,2 By 2022, DTG-based regimens were established as the preferred first-line therapy for all populations in Kenya, including pregnant and breastfeeding women, adolescents, and children over 20 kg. DTG has superior efficacy with higher rates of virologic suppression (VS), tolerability, durability, and a higher barrier to HIV drug resistance (HIVDR) development as compared to NNRTIs.3–8 Studies from sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) have shown high rates of VS, improved 12-month retention-in-care5 and an association of viremia at transition with higher odds of subsequent viremia.8–10 However, real-world, non-trial comparative data on DTG versus NNRTI-based ART from resource-constrained settings are limited.5,11–14

We conducted a comparison retrospective longitudinal analysis of virologic outcomes among ART-naïve and treatment-experienced PLWH in Kenya who initiated or transitioned to TLD, as well as ART-naïve individuals who initiated NNRTI-based tenofovir, lamivudine, efavirenz (TLE). We examined the association of pre-transition and initial viral load (VL) and regimen after initial VL non-suppression (VLNS) with subsequent outcomes, including durable undetectable VL (durable UD) and virologic failure (VF).

We deduplicated records using unique client identifiers, verified internal regimen histories for logical consistency, filtered to eligible EFV- or DTG-based regimens, and chronologically ordered viral load (VL) results per client. We excluded records lacking unique identifiers or with incomplete regimen documentation preventing longitudinal analysis. Missing VL data were not imputed due to the high proportion of absent longitudinal covariates.

Methods

Study design, participants, and data source

We conducted a retrospective analysis using longitudinal clinical and VL data from Kenya's National AIDS and Sexually Transmitted Infections Control Programme (NASCOP) national data warehouse (NDW). The study included PLWH aged ≥15 years who initiated ART between January 1, 2016, and July 18, 2024, across 2323 sites. The NDW is an electronic medical record database of PLWH who receive HIV care and treatment services in facilities supported by the NASCOP. De-identified data were provided for analyses, without any personal identifiers. We deduplicated records using unique client identifiers, verified internal regimen histories for logical consistency, filtered to eligible EFV- or DTG-based regimens, and chronologically ordered viral load (VL) results per client. We excluded records lacking unique identifiers or with incomplete regimen documentation preventing longitudinal analysis. Missing VL data were not imputed due to the high proportion of absent longitudinal covariates.

Data abstracted included demographics, ART regimen history, enhanced adherence counseling (EAC) dates, pharmacy data, and VL results. We categorized regimens as NNRTI-based (TLE) or INSTI-based (TLD). The analysis focused on ART-naïve individuals initiated on TLD (TLD1 cohort) or TLE (TLE1 cohort), and ART-experienced individuals transitioned to TLD (TLD2 cohort) in 2018 or later with at least one follow-up VL. We excluded PLWH who initiated or transitioned ART before 2018, were under 15 years of age, used non-TLD DTG- or non-TLE EFV-based ART, or lacked unique identifiers. Individuals who switched from TLD to a non-DTG regimen were censored at the switch date in DTG models.

Statistical analysis and outcomes

Among individuals who met the criteria for inclusion, we described their demographic and clinical characteristics, including sex, age (15–19, 20–44, 45–59, 60+ years), pregnancy status, body mass index (BMI) at ART initiation or transition, and year of ART initiation or transition. We used frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. We estimated the prevalence of HIV virologic outcomes as: virologic suppression (VS, <1000 copies/mL), undetectable (UD, <50 copies/mL), durable UD (3 most recent results <50 copies/mL), low-level viremia (LLV, 50–999 copies/mL), VL non-suppression (VLNS, ≥1000 copies/mL), and virologic failure (VF, two or more consecutive VLNS results on the same regimen). We reported outcomes by order of VL result regardless of the time window and for specific time intervals (less than 6, 6–12, 12–24, and 24–36 months) from the date of TLD or TLE initiation, or TLD transition. We analyzed outcomes among PLWH with initial VLNS at first VL after initiation or transition and three follow-up VLs, and among TLD2 by pre-transition VL.

We fit a series of logistic regression models to examine the association of demographic and clinical correlates with virologic outcomes. Age, sex, and the interaction of age and sex were included in all models. If the interaction of age and sex was not significant, we dropped it from the model and examined the main effects of age and sex. ART regimen was included for models that included all three cohorts. Viral load result (either first VL for non-time-varying models or first of multiple VLs for time-varying models) and the interaction of ART regimen and VL were included, when significant.

Kenya expanded its national VL laboratory network during 2018–2023, increasing VL testing coverage and frequency, particularly for children and adolescents; models included the number of VL observations as a covariate to account for greater opportunity for detection. We fit a model using the first VL, ART regimen, and the interaction of VL and ART regimen to determine whether the relationship between initial VL and subsequent VLNS was dependent on the ART regimen. This model also included age, sex, and the interaction of age and sex. We fit a regression model among TLD2 using pre-transition VL as a predictor of UD and VLNS at the first post-transition VL. We used the VL result closest to the transition date for clients with multiple VL tests prior to TLD transition. This model also included age, sex, and the interaction of age and sex.

We fit time-varying generalized estimating equation (GEE) models to predict VLNS after any VLNS episode and VF among individuals with initial VS, by regimen and initial VL result on the specific regimen. For each pair of VLNS and subsequent VL, one observation was contributed to the analysis. Among individuals with initial VS, the second and third VLs were analyzed to assess the odds of subsequent VF. Time-dependent models for VF used each triplet of VLs, with the first VL predicting VF at the second and third VLs. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) were calculated for each variable.

We used pharmacy data for individuals who either initiated or transitioned to DTG-based ART and compared UD outcomes among individuals with VF with at least one follow-up VL who stayed on versus switched off DTG-based ART and assessed documentation of enhanced adherence counseling (EAC) between VLNS and VF. For analytic comparability, we censored follow-up at the documented date of switch off DTG-based ART; Post-switch outcomes were not analyzed within DTG cohorts.

We assessed changes in BMI over time (baseline, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months) by regimen using a random-effects logistic regression model with time, regimen, and the interaction of time and regimen, adjusting for age, sex, and the interaction of age and sex. Height and weight recorded at each visit resulted in multiple BMI observations within each period. We included the observation closest to baseline, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months for each individual.

We estimated descriptive analyses and fit logistic regression models for virologic outcomes using SAS/STAT software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, 2016) and R statistical software (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024).15 We used the lme4 (Bates, Maechler, Bolker & Walker, 2015) package to fit the random effects model for BMI.16

Results

Participant characteristics

Among 1,155,825 PLWH who initiated ART from January 1, 2016, to July 1, 2024, 939,858 (81.3%) had a unique identifier and were initiated on EFV- or DTG-based ART or transitioned to DTG-based ART (Fig. 1). We included 1,072,209 PLWH records: 513,136 (47.2%) who initiated TLD (TLD1); 211,085 (19.2%) initiated on TLE (TLE1); and 347,988 (32.0%) who transitioned to TLD (TLD2). Among TLE1, 147,053 (67.4%) transitioned to TLD2 and contributed VLs to both groups. Median follow-up time was longest for TLD2 (1421 days), followed by TLD1 (829 days) and TLE1 (443 days). TLD1 and TLD2 had higher VL monitoring rates than TLE1 (Table S1). Among TLD1, TLE1, and TLD2 343,022 (66.8%), 109,888 (52.1%), and 305,488 (87.8%) had ≥1VL, respectively (Fig. 1). Among all groups, the majority were female, median age between 32 and 36 years, 3.8–10.1% pregnant, with median BMI between 21.5 and 22.7 kg (Table 1). Most initiated TLD in 2019 or later (96.4%, 494,820), initiated TLE between 2018 and 2020 (99.5%, 210,113), and transitioned to TLD between 2019 and 2020 (78.7%, 273,938).

Virologic outcomes by regimen and time

Over 75% (648,510/861,124) had ≥1 documented VL result on TLD; median time in days from ART initiation to first VL ranged from 184 to 205 days (Table S2). TLD2 exhibited the highest virologic suppression rates, ranging from 96.3 to 97.1%. TLD1 followed with rates between 94.4 and 96.2%, while TLE1 had the lowest rates, ranging from 91.1 to 92.7% (Table S2). TLD1 and TLD2 demonstrated higher rates of VS and UD compared to TLE1 across various time intervals, starting from higher UD rates within 6 months to 24–36 months of initiation or transition (Table 2, Figure S1). LLV rates declined over time, lowest in TLD2 at 24–36 months. Prevalence of VLNS ranged from 3.3 to 8.5%, highest in TLE1 at less than 6 months (8.5%) and lowest in TLD2 at 24–36 months (3.3%).

Virologic outcomes after initial viral load non-suppression and virologic failure

Among TLD1 with three follow-up VLs who experienced initial VLNS, the rates of achieving UD at the second and third follow-up VLs were higher among TLD1 compared with TLE 1 (Table S3 and S4). All pairwise comparisons of rates of VS and UD between TLD1 and TLE1 and between TLD2 and TLE1 were highly significant (p < 0.0001). VS at the second VL was significantly higher for TLD2 compared to TLD1 (p = 0.0118), but VS at the first viral load and UD rates did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table S5). Among those with VF, TLD1 and TLD 2 achieved higher rates of VS and UD compared with TLE1 (p < 0.0001).

Virologic outcomes by pre-transition viral load result

Analyses were stratified by pre-transition VL category to present all outcomes per group. PLWH with pre-transition UD had significantly higher odds of remaining UD post-transition compared to those with pre-transition VL ≥ 1000 copies/mL (aOR 3.63 [95% CI 3.45–3.81]; p < 0.0001; Table S6). Adolescents aged 15–19 years had lower odds of UD as compared to 60+ (females: aOR 0.84 [95% CI 0.77–0.92]; males: aOR 0.86 [95% CI 0.76–0.96]; p < 0.0001).

Among 120,880 TLD2 with pre-transition UD, UD rates remained high across follow up: 87.7% at the first post-transition VL, 65.0% at the second, and 64.6% at the third (Table S5). These individuals had significantly higher odds of remaining UD at first VL post-TLD transition compared to those with pre-transition VL ≥ 1000 copies/mL(aOR3.63 [95% CI 3.45–3.81]; p < 0.0001; Table S6).

For individuals with pre-transition LLV partial re-suppression occurred (UD 65.0% at the first VL, 73.5% at the second, and 78.7% at the third (Table S5), but was lower than compared to those with pre-transition UD.

Among individuals with pre-transition VLNS, VS rates increased from 83.1% at the first VL, 86.5% at the second, and 88.0% at the third, while UD improved from 64.6% at the first VL to 68.4% at the second, and 72.5% at the third (Table S5).

Pre-transition viral load as a predictor of viral load non-suppression at first post-transition viral load

In a regression model among TLD2 with pre- and post-transition VL assessing pre-transition VL as a predictor of VLNS at first post-transition VL, the odds of VLNS were significantly higher for all VL categories compared to pre-transition UD: LLV (aOR 1.51 [95% CI 1.42–1.60]), VL ranging 1000–9999 copies/mL (aOR 4.50 [95% CI 4.07–4.97]), VL ≥ 100,000 copies/mL (aOR 8.32 [95% CI 7.19–9.62] and missing a pre-transition VL (aOR 1.66 [95% CI 1.59–1.74]; p < 0.0001). Additionally, both females and male adolescents (15–19 years) had higher odds of developing VLNS compared with adults ≥60 years (females: aOR 1.87 [95% CI 1.59–2.20], males: aOR 2.11 [95% CI 1.73–2.59]; p = 0.03; Table S7).

Association between first viral load result, ART status, regimen, and virologic outcomes

Compared to those with UD, individuals with initial VLNS had significantly higher odds of VLNS in TLE1 (aOR 11.55 [95% CI 10.67–12.50]), followed by TLD2 (aOR 6.11 [95% CI 5.73–6.51] and TLD1 (aOR 5.83 [95% CI 5.52–6.15]); p < 0.0001; Table S8). Starting from the lowest range of LLV (50–199 copies/mL), the odds of subsequent VLNS were increased for both TLD 1 and TLD2 with further elevation at higher ranges of LLV. Adolescents (15–19 years) had the highest odds of VLNS with males (aOR 2.74 [95% CI 2.33, 3.21] at greater risk than females (aOR 1.97 [95% CI 1.72, 2.25]; p < 0.0001).

Prediction of viral load non-suppression and virologic failure

Viral load non-suppression after any episode of viral load non-suppression

Time-varying regression models showed that after any episode of VLNS, both females and males aged 15–19 years had higher odds of subsequent VLNS at the next VL compared to those aged ≥60 (aOR 1.89 [95% CI 1.52–2.33] for females; aOR 3.50 [95% CI 2.69–4.54] for males, p < 0.0001; Table S9). Males aged 15–19 also had higher odds of subsequent VLNS at the next VL after any episode of VLNS than their female counterparts (OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.15–1.61, p < 0.0001). Within each VL result range, after any VLNS episode, TLE1 had a significantly greater odds of subsequent VLNS than TLD1 and TLD2. Compared to TLD2, the risk associated with TLE1 increased with VL, from 1000–9999 copies/mL (aOR 2.48 [95% CI 2.27–2.71]) to ≥100,000 copies/mL (aOR 3.87 [95% CI 3.33–4.49]; p < 0.0001).

Virologic failure after initial virologic suppression or low-level viremia

Among individuals with initial VS and ≥2 VLs following VS approximately 2.0% of TLE1 with initial VS went on to develop VF compared with 0.5% of TLD1, p < 0.0001; Table 3). The odds of developing VF were significantly higher among TLE1 (aOR 3.83 [95% CI 3.39–4.32], and lower among TLD2 (aOR 0.69 [95% CI 0.63–0.75]; p < 0.0001), as compared with TLD1. Relative to initial UD, initial LLV had an increased odds of subsequent VF beginning at LLV ranging from 50 to 199 copies/mL (aOR 1.43 [95% CI 1.26–1.62]) up to 500–999 copies/mL (aOR 3.88 [95% CI 3.33–4.53]; p < 0.0001. Younger PLWH (15–19 years) had higher odds of VF compared with older age ranges (p < 0.0001), with males at significantly higher odds than females (aOR 5.39 [95% CI 3.64–7.98] versus aOR 2.07 [95% CI 1.49–2.87], respectively p < 0.0001]; OR 1.95 [95% CI 1.52–2.52; p < 0.0001]).

Virologic outcomes after virologic failure among individuals on DTG versus those who switched regimens

Among 5031 PLWH on DTG-based ART with VF and a post-VF VL, 94.4% (3009) who remained on DTG, 52% (1566) achieved UD, compared to 38.4% (68) of the 177 (5.6%) who switched to an alternate regimen (p = 0.0005). Analyses compared outcomes under stable regimen exposure; observations were censored at regimen switches, including DTG-to-PI transitions. Among those with VF after initiating DTG who remained on DTG 1173 (39.0%) received at least one EAC session between their first VLNS and post-VF VL test, and 512 (17.0%) received at least three. Among those who switched to a non-DTG regimen, 75 (42.4%) received at least one EAC session, and 45 (25.4%) received three or more. The median time from VLNS to EAC1 and EAC3 was 63 (IQR 28–117) days and 135 (IQR 92–204) days. The median time from the date of VLNS to repeat VL was 210 days (IQR 147–364) and 137 days (IQR 91–238), respectively.

Virologic suppression durability

Among individuals with at least 3 VLs, TLD2 had the highest odds of achieving durable UD (aOR 1.95 [95% CI 1.88–2.01]; p < 0.0001) compared to TLE1 (Table S10). Females and males aged 15–19 had lower odds of durable UD (aOR 0.84 [95% CI 0.79–0.90] and aOR 0.69 [95% CI 0.63–0.75], respectively; p < 0.0001) compared to individuals aged ≥60. Among those with at least 4 VLs, using the first VL result to predict durable UD, TLD1, and TLD2 had consistently higher odds of achieving durable UD than TLE1 across all initial VL ranges. Even with initial VLNS TLD1 (aOR 2.31 [95% CI 1.94–1.41] and TLD2 aOR 2.39 [95% CI 2.00–2.85] demonstrated superior virologic outcomes (p < 0.0001; Table 4). Although initial VLNS significantly decreased the odds of durable suppression, 43% of individuals in TLD 1 or TLD2 eventually achieved durable UD compared to <26% for TLE1 (Table S11).

Body mass index by regimen and time after initiation or transition

After adjusting for age and sex, all groups experience a small but significant BMI increase over time. TLD2 had the highest BMI at all time points, with significant differences in pairwise comparisons between groups at each time point (p < 0.001; Figure S2). In our cohort, baseline median BMI (≈21.5–22.7 kg/m2 across DTG groups) appears lower than median BMI reported in NAMSAL (Cameroon) and ADVANCE (South Africa) adult cohorts; however, cross-study comparisons are limited by differing inclusion criteria and incomplete fixed-timepoint BMI reporting.3,4

Discussion

This retrospective longitudinal analysis of data from 2323 sites in Kenya found that PLWH who initiated or transitioned to a DTG-based regimen (TLD) had superior virologic outcomes compared to those on EFV-based regimens (TLE). TLD1 showed higher rates of early VS in the initial six months than TLE1, with a lower risk of developing LLV, VLNS, and VF. TLD regimens also had higher rates of viral re-suppression following LLV, VLNS, or VF, compared to TLE, consistent with prior studies showing greater resuppression with DTG than NNRTIs.17–19 Most individuals with pre-transition VLNS achieved VS after TLD transition, consistent with prior studies.8,11,20

A significant interaction between ART regimen and initial VL on the odds of subsequent VLNS reinforces the importance of optimized regimens from the start. PLWH with LLV, even at 50–199 copies/mL, had an increased risk of VF. Unlike a small, single study by Chen et. Al., which found no association between LLV (defined as 50–999 copies/mL) and VLNS post-DTG transition,21 our large dataset demonstrated a link to higher VF risk even at low VL levels (50–199 copies/mL).

Risk factors for poor virologic outcomes included male and female adolescents (15–19 years), higher pre-transition VL (including LLV), and initial viremia. Other studies have previously established LLV's association with adverse outcomes.22–26 Together, these findings highlight the importance of ARV optimization and targeted adherence support for at-risk groups. By incorporating pre-transition VL data, adherence history, and psychosocial factors, programs may identify at-risk individuals and implement adherence interventions such as case management and closer clinical monitoring.

Our analysis has several limitations. First, it spans multiple years during which variability in VL monitoring and the availability of VL and antiretroviral commodities may have resulted in many recommended VL tests not being completed, or other variations in VL testing over the analytic period. Among individuals in the TLD and TLE1 groups with at least six months of follow-up, 20% and 35% had no VL results, respectively. Because missing VL data often coincided with limited or absent longitudinal data, we could not use methods such as multiple imputation, and this level of missingness may bias viral suppression estimates.

The percentage of individuals with at least one VL result improves substantially when restricting to those with at least one year of follow-up (92% for TLD1 and 89% for TLE1). Many in the TLD1 and TLD2 groups did not meet this criterion due to recent initiation or transition to TLD, while many in the TLE1 group did not due to the rapid national scale-up of TLD.

Missing VL results and gaps in other longitudinal information including regimen documentation and adherence may have introduced potential selection bias and limit generalizability, as individuals with multiple VLs may have represented those more engaged in care or prioritized for VL testing, which could have skewed results toward better outcomes, or limited our ability to evaluate their effects on outcomes. As an observational study, unmeasured confounding between regimens and timing of initiation or transition is also possible. Additionally, residual confounding is also possible despite adjustment for several variables in regression analyses. Our analyses did not apply multiple-comparison adjustments; p-values should be interpreted with caution given the number of tests performed.

A major strength of this analysis is the large sample size, representing the majority of ART facilities and patients in Kenya. Our findings are consistent with other studies comparing these regimens, and our very large cohort enabled stratified and subgroup analyses that would not be feasible in a typical prospective study. While differences in viral suppression between groups were small (2–3 percentage points), they may have clinical relevance, as such improvements could translate into several thousand additional individuals achieving viral suppression in a program of this size.

Future research considerations include incorporating risk, adherence biomarkers (e.g., urine TFV/DTG concentrations), HIVDR testing strategies, and tailored interventions to improve treatment continuity and long-term suppression. Understanding the drivers of incomplete adherence remains crucial to minimizing HIVDR and sustaining virologic control.

Despite the potential limitations, our findings confirm excellent virologic outcomes among PLWH on DTG-based (TLD) in routine care settings in Kenya. TLD led to superior and more durable VS and UD, aligning with DTG's known effectiveness and improved tolerability.22 Sustained VS with TLD has important potential implications for HIV programs, including reducing the risk of HIV transmission, limiting the emergence of HIVDR, and supporting the long-term cost-effectiveness of treatment. Until an HIV cure is realized, ART optimization and proactive, client-centered engagement before and during treatment are essential to supporting adherence and achieving durable suppression. Ongoing surveillance of long-term TLD effectiveness in real-world settings remains vital to optimizing care and outcomes and ending HIV as a public health threat.

Supplementary Material

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched MEDLINE for publications between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024, using the terms “HIV”, “dolutegravir”, and “virologic outcomes”. Evidence from real-world, non-trial data of dolutegravir (DTG) versus non-DTG-based antiretroviral therapy (ART)-associated outcomes from resource-constrained settings, particularly Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), is limited but show higher virologic suppression and retention with DTG versus efavirenz (EFV), even among individuals with baseline viremia (≥50 copies/mL). Additionally, baseline viremia, including low-level viremia (LLV) (≥50–999 copies/mL) predict subsequent virologic non-suppression and failure, with DTG often superior to EFV to resuppression.

Added value of this study

This is the largest real-world analysis with longer-term virologic outcomes for ALWH on DTG-based (TLD) versus NNRTI-based (TLE) ART in Kenya. We examined the impact of pre-transition viral load (VL), initial VL, and regimen after initial virologic non-suppression (VLNS) on subsequent virologic outcomes, including durable undetectable VL (UD), VLNS, and virologic failure (VF).

Implications of all the available evidence

With widespread adoption of TLD, ongoing assessment of the effectiveness of ART provided to millions in resource-constrained settings remain critical to monitor progress towards eliminating HIV as a global public health threat. Our findings confirm that TLD leads to faster, more durable VS and UD, even among those with pre-transition VLNS. However, individuals with low-level viremia (LLV), higher pre-transition VL, and those aged 15–19 years are at greater risk of VLNS and VF, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions and strengthened clinical, laboratory, and data systems.
