Transplantation tolerance versus immune evasion: Mechanistic divergences and therapeutic frontiers for islet cell replacement
Authors: Tobias Deuse, Sonja Schrepfer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAllogeneic transplantation is encumbered by the toxicities of chronic immunosuppression, driving innovations for lifelong graft acceptance without ongoing therapy. Transplantation tolerance, a recipient-centric strategy, utilizes central deletional processes through mixed hematopoietic chimerism and thymic repertoire sculpting, complemented by peripheral mechanisms such as T cell anergy, exhaustion, apoptotic deletion, and regulatory T cell dominance. Adjunctive costimulatory blockade enhances the latter pathways, yet tolerance remains precarious, susceptible to destabilization by infections or inflammatory perturbations via heterologous immunity. Conversely, immune evasion adopts a product-centric approach, engineering grafts to intrinsically circumvent adaptive allorejection, innate cytotoxicity, and autoimmune recognition and responses. These mechanistic contrasts—dynamic recipient modulation versus robust graft stealth—underscore immune evasion’s transformative promise and resilience, with empirical validation for cell and organoid transplantation. This review focusses on islet cell replacement therapy, wherein cadaveric allogeneic islet allografts have been clinically available for over four decades, complemented by recent advancements in stem cell-derived islet products.Keywords: iPSC, regenerative medicine, allogeneic, hypoimmune, tolerance

Introduction

Although the terms “tolerance” and “immune evasion” are frequently used interchangeably to describe attenuated alloimmune responses that promote sustained allograft viability, these concepts are mechanistically distinct and are herein exemplified in the context of pancreatic islet transplantation. Cadaveric allogeneic pancreatic islet transplantation involves infusing insulin-producing islets isolated from deceased donor pancreases into patients with type 1 diabetes (T1D), typically via the hepatic portal vein. The current gold-standard includes immunosuppression according to the established Edmonton protocol. This features anti-IL-2R induction therapy and a glucocorticoid-free regimen comprising sirolimus and low-dose tacrolimus as maintenance therapy.1,2 This approach reliably restores C-peptide secretion, averts severe hypoglycemic episodes, and frequently yields insulin independence, with experienced centers reporting 5-year insulin independence rates exceeding 50%.3 Notwithstanding these advancing long-term outcomes, patients exchange exogenous insulin—a relatively benign therapy—for systemic immunosuppression, incurring risks such as nephrotoxicity,4 opportunistic infections,5 and malignancy.6 Owing to this unfavorable risk-benefit profile, eligibility for allogeneic islet transplantation is restricted to individuals with long-standing T1D and recurrent severe hypoglycemia refractory to optimized medical management.2 Suitable candidates exhibit recurrent severe hypoglycemic events, impaired hypoglycemia awareness, and/or pronounced glycemic variability despite intensive insulin therapy.7 These rigorous inclusion criteria render the procedure accessible to fewer than 10% of patients with T1D.8 Nevertheless, individuals with T1D continue to experience diminished life expectancy—typically 7–10 years shorter than the general population, even under optimal care—and substantially abbreviated lifespans in low-resource settings and developing countries.9,10 Scalable β-cell replacement strategies that obviate immunosuppression could profoundly enhance global access and outcomes for T1D patients. Since T1D is an autoimmune disease, allogeneic islet cell therapies must overcome both allorejection and the recurrence of autoimmune attacks to achieve long-term success, rendering T1D an especially challenging field for cell replacement therapy.

Tolerance

Tolerance is the prevention of an immune response against a particular antigen; thus it is selective and specific. It combines unresponsiveness to tolerogenic alloantigens with preserved responsiveness to other third-party antigens. It is also dynamic: new tolerance can be acquired11 and established tolerance can be broken.12 Mechanistically, immune tolerance requires target cells to present antigens on human leukocyte antigen (HLA) because T cells can only recognize and respond to (or be tolerized against) peptide antigens when bound to HLA molecules.13,14 Without HLA presentation, T cells cannot engage the antigen, leading to immunological ignorance, which is a feature of immune evasion, rather than tolerance.13 In allotransplantation, tolerance induction entails the intentional modulation of the recipient’s immune system to bring about long-term, donor-specific immune unresponsiveness—through central and/or peripheral mechanisms—thereby enabling sustained graft viability without chronic immunosuppressive therapy.

Central tolerance

Central transplantation tolerance fundamentally differs from peripheral tolerance in its anatomic location and mechanistic underpinnings. Central tolerance is the natural process by which the developing immune repertoire is shaped in primary lymphoid organs—the thymus for T cells and the bone marrow for B cells—through the elimination or functional inactivation of lymphocytes that strongly recognize self-antigens. This ensures that the mature immune system is tolerant to self, while retaining the ability to respond to non-self (foreign) antigens.15,16 This mechanism prevents autoimmunity by clonally deleting high-affinity self-reactive T cells through negative selection in the thymus or inducing anergy in B cells in the bone marrow, ensuring that only non-self-reactive cells mature and enter the periphery.17 To establish transplantation tolerance to an allograft, thymic positive selection and negative selection have to be amended to include donor-derived “new-self” epitopes inherent to the allograft, thus reconfiguring the immune repertoire. This prevents alloreactivity and promotes long-term graft acceptance without immunosuppression18 (Figure 1). Thymogenesis can be modulated via the induction of mixed hematopoietic chimerism via donor bone marrow or hematopoietic cell transplantation from the same allograft donor after non-myeloablative conditioning.19 Mixed chimerism, characterized by the concurrent persistence of recipient and donor hematopoietic elements within the bone marrow niche, is favored over complete chimerism (wherein donor cells predominate the lymphohematopoietic compartment) owing to the feasibility of milder recipient conditioning, diminished incidence of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and enhanced preservation of host immunocompetence.20,21 Maturing thymocytes undergo selection predicated on their avidity for both recipient (“self”) and new-self epitopes presented on HLA molecules, particularly those expressed on antigen-presenting cells (APCs).22 The engagement of these progenitors with donor HLA is pivotal for their ontogeny and repertoire sculpting, dictating either positive selection or clonal deletion.23 By instituting a self-renewing reservoir of donor-derived APCs in the recipient, perpetual negative selection and elimination of emergent alloreactive conventional T cells during thymopoiesis are sustained, contingent upon persistent thymic seeding by donor APCs and enduring engraftment.24 Persistent chimerism was achieved in fully HLA-matched recipients of combined kidney and hematopoietic cell transplantation,25 while only transient chimerism typically occurred in HLA-mismatched patients.26,27,28 In some instances, even after chimerism was lost, tolerance persisted through regulatory mechanisms, allowing patients to remain immunosuppression free. Thus, durable chimerism does not appear to be a prerequisite for prolonged tolerance.26

Central transplantation tolerance induction

Central transplantation tolerance arises from reshaping the developing immune repertoire in primary lymphoid organs. Mixed hematopoietic chimerism is induced by non-myeloablative conditioning (1) followed by donor hematopoietic cell infusion (2). Developing thymocytes are then shaped by their avidity for both recipient (self) and donor (new-self) HLA molecules presented on antigen-presenting cells ([APCs], 3). This enables positive selection of non-alloreactive T cells and donor-specific regulatory T cells, while mediating negative selection (clonal deletion) of high-avidity alloreactive clones. A sustained reservoir of donor-derived APCs in the recipient supports ongoing negative selection of newly emerging alloreactive T cells during thymopoiesis.

In parallel, donor-specific regulatory activity is reinforced peripherally through the induction and expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs). These donor-specific Tregs suppress alloreactive responses through mechanisms such as cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β), metabolic disruption, and bystander suppression—ultimately preventing graft rejection and promoting long-term allograft survival.29,30 Unlike the non-Treg T cells that undergo central deletion or become non-reactive to donor antigens in the chimeric thymus, these Tregs actively inhibit alloreactive effector responses.16,31,32 Thymic involution—the inexorable, age-dependent glandular atrophy commencing in puberty and intensifying thereafter—impairs thymic lymphopoiesis and reduces the efficiency of deletional mechanisms by limiting thymocyte production and donor APC seeding. Although central deletional tolerance via mixed chimerism can still be achieved in aged recipients with involuted thymi (as demonstrated in preclinical models),33 remedial strategies under investigation include co-transplantation of donor thymic organoids to bypass the atrophic recipient thymus or targeted thymic irradiation to augment engraftment of donor hematopoietic cell-derived precursors, thereby fostering APC ontogeny, mitigating involution effects, and reinvigorating thymocyte reconstitution.34,35 Nonmyeloablative radiation-based conditioning regimens, including low-dose total body irradiation (TBI) and total lymphoid irradiation (TLI), have been employed across multiple clinical trials to facilitate the induction of central transplantation tolerance.27,28 These approaches attenuate peripheral and intrathymic rejection of donor hematopoietic cells, promote marrow engraftment, and provide a sustained pool of thymic-migrating progenitors.36 Importantly, the reduced intensity of these regimens (relative to myeloablative conditioning) is associated with a substantially lower incidence of GVHD.37

Central tolerance is predicated on the establishment of mixed hematopoietic chimerism, which—even when achieved with reduced-intensity conditioning—requires substantial patient preparation (e.g., low-dose irradiation, fludarabine/cyclophosphamide-based chemotherapy, and/or T cell depletion) and, in HLA-mismatched transplantation, often prolonged immunosuppressive maintenance, rendering the approach clinically demanding.26 Most clinical experience has been gained with kidney transplantation. Pre-transplant conditioning regimens vary across centers and commonly include nonmyeloablative combinations such as fludarabine plus low-dose TBI and cyclophosphamide, cyclophosphamide with thymic irradiation, or TLI combined with anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) prior to hematopoietic cell transplantation.25,27,28 Maintenance immunosuppression has encompassed variable doses of calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), or corticosteroids, which were progressively tapered or withdrawn upon attainment of stable allograft function absent rejection. Sustaining tolerance proves increasingly arduous with escalating HLA disparity from HLA-identical to HLA-matched and HLA-mismatched renal allografts, although successful outcomes have been documented for all scenarios.25,38

In tolerant kidney transplant recipients undergoing combined kidney and bone marrow transplantation (NKDO3 and NCT00801632), antigen-driven expansion of preexisting donor-specific Tregs was observed at 6 months post-transplant through T cell receptor (TCR) β sequencing of Tregs expanded with activated donor B cells, correlating with tolerance induction, whereas these Tregs contracted in nontolerant recipients despite global Treg enrichment. This selective expansion of donor-specific Tregs, exceeding nonspecific Treg proliferation, suggests a pivotal role in suppressing anti-donor responses and facilitating deletional tolerance mechanisms.39

A clinical trial (NCT00315614) evaluating concomitant allogeneic islet and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation to establish mixed chimerism and operational graft tolerance has been documented.40 Six individuals with severe and difficult-to-control T1D received single-donor allogeneic islet transplantation (8,611 ± 2,113 islet equivalents [IEQ]/kg recipient body weight), followed by two infusions of 4.3 ± 1.9 × 106 donor hematopoietic stem cells per kg, without myeloablative conditioning. Immunosuppression followed an Edmonton-like regimen, featuring daclizumab induction augmented by infliximab, with maintenance comprising sirolimus and low-dose tacrolimus; tapering commenced after 12 months. Four recipients sustained allograft function for up to 1 year, achieving peak islet potency within the initial 3 months, succeeded by progressive glycemic dysregulation and augmented exogenous insulin requirements. The islet allografts in the remaining two subjects were rejected within 6 months. Peripheral chimerism attained maximum levels during the first 3 months post-transplantation before inexorably waning. Thus, neither durable donor chimerism nor tolerance was attained.

Peripheral tolerance

Peripheral tolerance, by contrast, comprises post-thymic mechanisms that attenuate or eliminate alloreactive lymphocytes within secondary lymphoid organs (e.g., spleen and lymph nodes) and peripheral non-lymphoid tissues to prevent or resolve autoreactive or alloreactive responses. Intrinsic modalities include T cell anergy, wherein TCR engagement with alloantigen proceeds without requisite costimulatory signals.41,42 T cell exhaustion, induced by chronic alloantigen stimulation amid upregulation of inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3, and TIGIT), and deletion, ensued via Fas/FasL- or Bim-mediated apoptosis, are further intrinsic mechanisms facilitating peripheral immune tolerance.43,44 Cell-extrinsic suppression of T cells is predominantly mediated by Foxp3+ Tregs, with contributions from type 1 Tregs ([Tr1], IL-10-producing) and Th3 cells (TGF-β-producing).45 Tregs consume IL-2, thereby starving adjacent effector T cells, and secrete immunomodulatory cytokines such as IL-10, TGF-β, and IL-35, which attenuate effector T cell responses and reprogram APCs.46 Canonical costimulatory pathway inhibitors disrupt the essential Signal 2 machinery requisite for robust T cell activation against the allograft, encompassing the CD28-B7 and CD40-CD40L axes, or ancillary immunologic synapse interactions (e.g., LFA-1/ICAM-1, and CD2/LFA-3) that modulate T cell priming.47 CTLA4-immunoglobulin ([CTLA4-Ig], belatacept) attenuates donor-specific T cell priming by blocking CD28-CD80/CD86 interactions and inducing a tolerogenic phenotype in APCs, collectively diminishing effector T cell proliferation, while promoting Treg dominance.48 Efalizumab inhibits the LFA-1-ICAM-1 adhesion/costimulatory pathway, thereby curtailing T cell infiltration into the graft and impairing integrin-mediated immunologic synapse formation.49 Blocking monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting the costimulatory T cell pathways involving ICOS,50 OX-40L,51 4-1BBL,52 and TIM-4 on APCs,53 as well as agonistic antibodies against GITR on T cells,54 has been employed in preclinical studies to augment tolerogenic regimens and facilitate peripheral tolerance induction41 (Figure 2). Specifically, anti-ICOS mAbs inhibit T cell proliferation and cytokine production (e.g., IL-2), inducing anergy or apoptosis in effector and memory T cells.55 Anti-OX-40L mAbs reduce effector survival and Th cell differentiation, while promoting activation-induced cell death and Th2 shifts.56 Anti-4-1BBL mAbs suppress CD8+ T cell activation and cytotoxicity by blocking the interaction between 4-1BBL on APCs and 4-1BB on T cells, thereby preventing CD28-independent co-stimulatory signaling that would otherwise enhance T cell proliferation, survival, cytokine production (such as IFN-γ), and effector functions including granule-mediated killing through perforin and granzymes.57 Anti-TIM-4 mAbs downregulate proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IFN-γ), enhance TGF-β effects, and balance effector T cells/Tregs to foster immune tolerance.58

Peripheral transplantation tolerance

Peripheral tolerance involves post-thymic strategies that dampen or delete graft-reactive immune cells in secondary lymphoid organs and non-lymphoid peripheral sites. For instance, T cells enter a state of anergy upon T cell receptor (TCR) binding to alloantigens on HLA in the absence of essential costimulatory cues. Established inhibitors of key costimulatory axes—such as those targeting CD80 (CTLA4-Ig, abatacept), CD40 (bleselumab)/CD40L (AT01501, tegoprubart), and LFA-1 (efalizumab)—have advanced to clinical trials, while preclinical efforts have explored blocking antibodies against APC-expressed ICOS, OX-40L, 4-1BBL, and TIM-4, alongside agonistic antibodies to T cell GITR, to enhance tolerogenic protocols and promote durable peripheral tolerance.

In a clinical trial evaluating CNI-sparing immunosuppression through the addition of belatacept or efalizumab (NCT00501709), recipients underwent induction with two doses of ATG and a single dose of methylprednisolone prior to allogeneic islet transplantation. Maintenance immunosuppression comprised sirolimus and mycophenolic acid. In addition to that, five patients received belatacept at 10 mg/kg intravenously on post-transplant days 0, 4, 14, 28, 56, and 75, followed by 5 mg/kg every 4 weeks through 18 months post-transplant and thereafter every 8 weeks. Another 5 patients received efalizumab at 1 mg/kg subcutaneously weekly, commencing 1 day pre-transplant and continuing for 3 months posttransplant, succeeded by 0.5 mg/kg weekly.59 Two patients from each cohort (40% overall) achieved durable insulin independence following a single islet infusion, sustained for a mean duration of 13 years. The remaining six patients (60% overall) exhibited primary islet allograft failure after a mean of 19 months. Although belatacept and efalizumab did not induce tolerance, CNI doses could be reduced and renal function preserved across the study population.

Antibodies targeting the CD40-CD40L axis like AT-1501 function as immunomodulatory agents that attenuate T cell activation and foster a tolerogenic microenvironment.60 CD40L (CD154) is predominantly expressed on activated CD4+ T cells,61 albeit with expression on activated CD8+ T cells62 and select other immune effectors. Blockade of CD40L promotes a tolerogenic phenotype in dendritic cells (DCs), augments donor-specific Foxp3+ Tregs, attenuates Th1/Th17 polarization, and indirectly impairs CD8+ T cell responses.

In a preclinical study of islet allotransplantation in cynomolgus macaques, the combination of AT-1501 with tacrolimus and/or sirolimus exhibited substantial toxicity, precipitating sporadic early fatalities.63 A modified regimen incorporating high-dose ATG (five doses of 5 mg/kg) and serial AT-1501 administrations (seven doses within the initial month, followed by dosing every 14 days) demonstrated improved tolerability. Among four recipients, three attained transient insulin independence, albeit with two of these reverting to exogenous insulin dependence within the 6-month observation period, suggestive of progressive loss of islet mass.63 The identical protocol facilitated renal allograft survival in three of four major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-disparate rhesus macaques, whereas the remaining subjects succumbed to fulminant combined cellular- and antibody-mediated rejection.63

The inaugural phase 1/2 clinical trial in humans evaluating AT-1501 as an adjunct to immunosuppressive therapy following allogeneic islet transplantation in individuals with T1D (NCT06305286) is ongoing. The regimen incorporates standard immunosuppression with ATG or basiliximab induction, MMF maintenance, and etanercept (a tumor necrosis factor inhibitor), alongside AT-1501 administered intravenously every 3 weeks commencing at week 3 post-transplantation. Interim data reveal that one participant attained insulin independence after a single islet infusion, another after two infusions, and a third demonstrated reduced exogenous insulin requirements.64 Concurrently, AT-1501 is being assessed in renal allotransplantation (NCT05027906; NCT05983770), with preliminary results suggesting analogous encouraging prospects for tacrolimus discontinuation within the immunosuppressive regimen.65

Elements of the structural design of mAbs—such as the Fc region, isotype, valency, or overall format—can independently introduce adverse effects unrelated to the specific epitope engaged. For instance, early anti-CD40L mAb like hu5C8 (ruplizumab) featured an intact Fc domain that triggered platelet activation and aggregation via FcγRIIa binding,66 leading to thromboembolic events in clinical trials,67 prompting the development of the Fc-silent variant AT-1501 to mitigate this design-related risk.68 Similarly, the superagonistic anti-CD28 antibody TGN1412 caused severe cytokine release syndrome in a phase 1 trial due to its bivalent IgG4 format enabling unintended cross-linking and massive T cell activation, independent of epitope specificity69; this led to redesigns favoring monovalent Fab' fragments like FR104 to avoid such agonism while retaining blockade.70 Immunogenicity from non-human sequences in chimeric or murine-derived antibodies can elicit anti-drug antibodies (ADAs), resulting in infusion reactions, accelerated clearance, or neutralization, as seen with early chimeric mAbs where ADA rates reached 50%–80% in transplant patients; humanization reduces but does not eliminate this.71 Repetitive dosing can also induce anaphylaxis via host anti-antibody responses, as observed with rat-derived GITR agonists in mice, where IgG isotype triggered IgE-mediated hypersensitivity unrelated to the target’s biology.72 Efalizumab was voluntarily withdrawn from the US market in 2009 due to its association with progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, a rare and often fatal brain infection caused by John Cunningham (JC) virus reactivation in immunosuppressed patients, following reports of three confirmed cases (all fatal) and one suspected case in long-term users.73 Overall, these design flaws can exacerbate infections or inflammatory events in transplant settings by broadly impairing immune surveillance, highlighting the need for engineered formats like pegylated Fabs or Fc-muted IgGs to minimize off-target toxicities.

Polyclonal Tregs are thought to facilitate peripheral tolerance in allotransplantation because they can be readily expanded ex vivo from peripheral blood to achieve sufficient numbers for infusion, enabling broad suppression of alloreactive T cells and promoting graft survival.74 The production of polyclonal Treg products at clinical scale from patients prior to islet transplantation necessitates the isolation of Tregs and their ex vivo expansion while preserving Treg phenotypic and functional identity.75 Tregs are isolated from autologous peripheral blood by enrichment for CD4+CD25+CD127lo/− cells and subsequently activated using beads conjugated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies in the presence of high concentrations of exogenous IL-2.76 Following a 14-day expansion phase, the product is subjected to comprehensive quality control evaluations, including release criteria that mandate <5% CD8+CD4− contamination, among others.75 Preclinical studies indicate that co-infusion of islets and Tregs via the hepatic portal vein attenuates early islet attrition mediated by the instant blood-mediated inflammatory reaction (IBMIR) and augments islet engraftment.77,78 Furthermore, polyclonal Tregs have been demonstrated to suppress systemic alloimmune responses and thereby facilitate sustained graft viability.79

An ongoing clinical trial (NCT03444064) evaluating intravenous administration of ex vivo-expanded autologous polyclonal Tregs (PolyTreg) in recipients of allogeneic islet transplantation has enrolled 11 participants.80 Subjects receive standard immunosuppression per the Edmonton protocol; the intervention arm undergoes PolyTreg infusion at 6 weeks post-transplantation, with 1-year follow-up to assess safety and preliminary efficacy, including modulation of pathogenic autoimmune and alloimmune responses. No results have been reported to date, and it remains unknown whether PolyTreg therapy facilitates minimization of maintenance immunosuppression following islet transplantation. In a pilot study of early post-transplant Treg infusion after allogeneic liver transplantation (UMIN-000015789), seven of ten recipients achieved operational tolerance, enabling complete immunosuppression withdrawal after structured tapering; the remaining three subjects, with underlying autoimmune liver disorders, developed mild rejection during weaning and recommenced conventional immunosuppressive therapy.81 In a separate study of stable liver allograft recipients receiving Treg infusions 2–6 years post-living donor liver transplantation (NCT02474199), donor alloantigen-specific Tregs exhibited functional impairment, with only two participants fulfilling the primary endpoint of ≥75% CNI dose reduction sustained beyond 12 weeks.82 This study further underscored manufacturing challenges for alloantigen-reactive Tregs, as four of ten subjects failed to achieve target doses and could not be treated. Studies employing Tregs in renal allograft recipients have not consistently enabled immunosuppressant discontinuation, although investigators have posited potential for mitigating the cumulative burden of chronic immunosuppression.83 Consequently, Treg-based therapies have yet to demonstrate reliable efficacy in inducing operational tolerance across solid organ transplantation settings.

Approaches to peripheral tolerance induction are generally well tolerated, mitigate β-cell toxicity,84 and obviate the rigorous conditioning regimens required for central tolerance via mixed hematopoietic chimerism. There have been semantic and definitional debates around the classification of the above mAb strategies as peripheral tolerance-inducing agents versus immunosuppressants. While the mAbs support mechanisms leading to T cell anergy, deletion, and exhaustion, which tie to the definition of peripheral tolerance, they dampen the immune response like immunosuppressants and often miss the goal of operational tolerance (graft acceptance without long-term immunosuppression). In practice, these strategies rarely achieve complete, antigen-specific, drug-free tolerance in humans, often requiring ongoing therapy or combinations with other immunosuppressants, thus functioning more as long-term suppression than durable reprogramming. This highlights the gap between preclinical tolerance induction—where costimulatory blockade like CTLA4-Ig or anti-CD40L can lead to sustained graft acceptance in rodent models85—and clinical outcomes, where barriers like memory T cells, humoral immunity, and incomplete specificity result in persistent rejection risks and the need for adjunctive therapies. Some of these agents are clinically approved and used primarily as targeted immunosuppressants rather than tolerance inducers and merely reduce the requirement for CNIs, which are often still needed. Despite the controversy, current definitions and mechanisms of function support their connection to peripheral tolerance.14,86

The vulnerability of tolerance

Transplantation tolerance manifests as an intrinsically labile equilibrium profoundly susceptible to perturbations within the immunological milieu. Although tolerance to novel antigens may be induced via strategies such as Treg augmentation or costimulatory blockade, established tolerance remains susceptible to disruption by infections or alterations in the gut microbiota.87 This fragility stems fundamentally from the plasticity of the T cell repertoire. When viruses trigger a broad T cell response, they cause widespread clonal expansion that not only targets the immunogen but also diversifies the memory T cell pool.88,89 By chance, certain emergent clones exhibit cross-reactivity with disparate epitopes that can include donor HLA or minor histocompatibility antigens of the allograft. This heterologous immunity emerges as an immunological determinant that can weaken tolerogenic safeguards and induce rejection in grafts that were previously stable.90 Prevalent pathogens such as Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus (CMV), and influenza—or lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus in preclinical mouse models—prime CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cells against viral epitopes that frequently exhibit molecular mimicry with donor alloantigens.91 Empirical data indicate that up to 50% of these virus-specific T cells may recognize and attack allogeneic HLA, facilitating graft infiltration and effector-mediated destruction.92 Compounding this, infections activate the innate immune system through pathogen signals (PAMPs) that bind toll-like receptors (TLRs), such as TLR3, TLR7/8, and TLR9, on DCs and macrophages.93 Such ligation inaugurates proinflammatory cascades via nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and interferon regulatory factor (IRF) transcription factors, culminating in upregulation of costimulatory ligands (e.g., CD80/CD86) and attenuation of Treg suppressive capacity.94 The resultant milieu undermines T cell anergy and deletional mechanisms, while favoring effector polarization. This leads to the release of cytokines, encompassing type I interferons (like IFN-α/β), IL-6, and TNF-α, which collectively destabilize tolerance.88 IFN-α, amplified during viral dissemination, enhances HLA class I expression on allograft endothelium, rendering it more susceptible to cytolytic T cells.88 Concurrently, IL-6 and TNF-α promote Th17 differentiation, B cell hyperactivation, and Treg apoptosis, thereby exacerbating the pro-rejection milieu.95 Clinically, this manifests as accelerated allograft rejection following CMV or BK polyomavirus reactivation, frequently eventuating in chronic rejection phenomena such as allograft vasculopathy.96,97

Immune evasion

Unlike transplantation tolerance, which is negotiated through dynamic engagement with nascent or mature recipient T cells to establish selective, alloantigen-specific immunological unresponsiveness toward HLA-replete allografts, immune evasion is attained through deception.98 It fundamentally reorients the approach from attenuation of recipient donor-specific immune reactivity to a preemptive, cell-intrinsic engineering of donor cells, rendering their immunological persistence independent of the recipient’s immune competence. A multitude of different strategies have been reported, but to avoid confusion, we will focus on the hypoimmune (HIP) concept of complete HLA depletion and utilization of the SIRPα innate immune checkpoint99 and will discuss additional edits where pertinent. CD47, a natural ligand for SIRPα, affords protection against macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells,100 despite their vast heterogeneity.101 Agonistic engagement of SIRPα by a synthetic molecule achieves comparable therapeutic efficacy while conferring enhanced specificity.102 Distinctions to other hypoimmune approaches are discussed below, and methods and technologies for gene editing have been summarized recently.103

Complete HLA depletion is the foundation that obviates T cell recognition and thereby averts downstream escalatory cascades including cytokine release, DC maturation, innate immune cell activation, and clonal expansion of alloantigen-specific plasma cells. Even when donor and recipient are HLA matched, polymorphic regions of donor proteins (including HLA itself and many non-HLA proteins) generate allopeptides. These “minor histocompatibility antigens” can still activate alloreactive T cells and lead to immune responses despite full HLA matching.104,105 Even autologous induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can develop mutations and neoantigens during ex vivo manufacturing strong enough to elicit rejection from autologous T cells.106 Only HLA depletion abrogates both alloantigenic and neoantigenic recognition by the adaptive immune system, thereby potentially rendering allogeneic immune-evasive cell therapies less immunogenic than their autologous counterparts. Preclinical studies in murine models,107 as well as rhesus108 and cynomolgus macaque,109 have demonstrated that allogeneic, immune-evasive HIP islet cell transplants elude recipient T cell surveillance, with no elicitation of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs). Moreover, no T cell memory is developed, permitting undetected persistence of subsequently administered HIP cells. Several alternative strategies for hypoimmune editing that avoid HLA class I depletion have been proposed,110,111,112,113 although their progression toward clinical products remains unclear, and any potential efficacy in such settings would still need to be evaluated.

In transplantation immunology, alloantigen presentation occurs via two distinct pathways: the direct pathway, wherein recipient T cells recognize intact donor HLA molecules bound to peptides on donor APCs, typically eliciting robust but short-lived polyclonal responses that drive acute rejection through rapid activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and the indirect pathway, where donor-derived alloantigens are processed by recipient APCs and presented as self-restricted peptides on recipient HLA molecules, fostering longer-lasting oligoclonal or monoclonal T cell responses that contribute to chronic rejection and DSA production.114 While the indirect pathway predominantly involves HLA class II-mediated CD4+ T cell recognition of processed donor alloantigens, it can also include HLA class I through cross-presentation, enabling CD8+ T cell activation against donor-derived peptides internalized by recipient APCs.115 CD8+ T cells exert direct cytotoxicity in allograft rejection by recognizing donor HLA class I-peptide complexes on graft cells and releasing perforin, granzymes, and cytokines like IFN-γ to induce target cell apoptosis, while CD4+ T cells contribute indirectly by providing cytokine help (e.g., IL-2) to activate CD8+ effectors and B cells for antibody production, or directly through FasL-mediated killing in some contexts, amplifying both acute and chronic rejection responses.116 Islet allografts are particularly effective at inducing a strong CD4 response through the indirect pathway,117 and islets can be rejected by CD4 T cells.118 Although low-level β2m-free MHC class I heavy-chain expression with functional CD8+ T cell recognition has been described in β2-microglobulin-deficient murine cells, comparable evidence in human cells is lacking.119,120 HIP engineering with complete HLA depletion and CD47 overexpression has repeatedly shown not to induce alloreactive antibodies via the indirect pathway. In humanized mice, neither allogeneic primary107 nor iPSC-derived islets108 induced DSAs, and primary rhesus macaque islets similarly failed to do so in allogeneic recipients.108,109 Similarly, no patient who received an HIP cell product showed any DSAs in their follow-up.121,122 The absence of DSAs correlated with a lack of target cell killing in complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity assays. Thus, activation of the indirect allopeptide presentation pathway has never been observed with HIP cells across these studies.

Strategies encompassing partial downregulation of HLA expression,123 selective ablation of HLA class I loci alone,124 or the heterologous re-expression of non-classical HLA molecules following complete HLA knockout125,126 fall outside of this definition of immune evasion. To attenuate residual T cell-mediated recognition, these modalities frequently integrate adjunctive tolerogenic effectors, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) or Fas ligand (FasL). Both PD-L1 and FasL have demonstrated tolerogenic efficacy in HLA-replete allogeneic transplants, correlating with prolonged survival of human islet grafts or in preclinical murine models.127,128 Immune evasion does not rely on tolerogenic factors as it maintains engineered immune ignorance.

Although allogeneic HIP grafts do not elicit de novo alloantibodies, many autoimmune diseases have an autoantibody component that contributes to the destruction of the target cells and similarly imperils the cell replacement therapeutic. In T1D, antibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), insulinoma-associated-2 (IA-2), zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8), and tetraspanin-7 (TSPAN7) are commonly detected.129 Notably, anti-ZnT8 autoantibodies are detectable in 60%–80% of patients with new-onset T1D130 and anti-TSPAN7 autoantibodies in 19%–38% of patients,131,132 and both ZnT8 and TSPAN7 are expressed on the β-cell plasma membrane.133,134 While B cells contribute to β-cell destruction by acting as autoantigen-presenting cells that efficiently drive autoreactive T cell responses,135 emerging evidence also demonstrates that islet autoantibodies themselves can exert direct pathogenic effects on β-cells.136 Ectopic expression of an engineered Fc receptor has been shown to shield pancreatic β-cells from cytolytic IgG-mediated cytotoxicity.137 Collectively, hypoimmune islet cells incorporating these modifications could be protected from both allogeneic rejection and recurrent autoimmunity (Figure 3). Autoimmunity, however, cannot be tested adequately in preclinical models, and clinical trials will have to provide clarity.

Immune evasion

Immune evasion in allogeneic cell therapeutics is achieved by genetic engineering to render grafts resistant to immune recognition and attack. The hypoimmune platform combines complete HLA class I and II deletion to prevent T cell recognition, innate immune cell suppression via SIRPα engagement, and expression of an engineered Fc receptor to neutralize cytolytic IgG antibodies.

The experience with the nascent paradigm of immune evasion99 remains substantially more limited than with tolerance, but preclinical mechanistic evidence demonstrating that allogeneic HIP cells,138,139 tissues,108 and organs140 evade host immunosurveillance continues to accumulate. This year, the first interim readouts from clinical studies have been reported. Allogeneic cadaveric donor islets, genetically engineered to confer an HIP phenotype, were transplanted into a recipient with long-standing T1D without concomitant immunosuppression.121 Over a 3-month period, the allograft maintained stable C-peptide secretion in the absence of any detectable immune response against the HIP islets. By contrast, unedited or partially modified cells were rejected. These findings illustrate that HIP cells can survive in an environment where concomitantly transplanted cells undergo active rejection. After 12 months, the graft remained viable and functional.141 The reliability of HIP engineering was further validated in patients with malignancy or autoimmune disorders who received HIP CD19 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells; intensive monitoring of 15 participants revealed no elicitation of adaptive or innate immune responses attributable to the HIP CAR T cells in any subject.122 The HIP islets were injected into the patient’s left brachioradialis muscle via 17 injections as described earlier.142,143 This location enables minimally invasive cell transplantation in an outpatient setting, facilitates graft imaging,144 and permits easy surgical access in the event of unintended side effects. The liver, accessed via portal vein injection, represents a more established transplant site that has been utilized for decades in cadaveric islet transplantation.3 However, its drawbacks include a more invasive implantation procedure and substantial early islet loss due to IBMIR.145 It remains to be seen which location will become the standard for hypoimmune islet transplantation.

While the end result—the survival of the allograft in the absence of immunosuppression—may be the same with successful tolerance induction and immune evasion, the mechanisms of achieving that and ramifications are vastly different. Even in successful transplantation tolerance for an HLA-replete allograft, the host immune system undergoes education through mechanisms like Treg dominance or alloreactive T cell suppression.12,146 But this state is dynamic and reversible, and memory T cells can lead to rejection of the graft or even accelerated rejection of subsequent re-transplants.12,147 In contrast, preclinical studies have demonstrated that re-injections of HIP grafts do not elicit any heightened immune response.108 The comparative efficacy of alternative hypoimmune strategies versus HIP will become clearer as they advance to clinical trials.

Outlook

The recent ascent of hypoimmune cell products from preclinical data to empirical validation in humans shows that immunological tolerance is not requisite for the survival of allogeneic transplants in the absence of immunosuppression. In contrary, emerging data suggest that the avoidance of interactions with the adaptive immune compartment renders immune evasion a more robust paradigm, impervious to disruption amid systemic immune activation.107,108 Allogeneic HIP cells were selectively spared from immune attacks within environments of active, ongoing rejection in both non-human primates108 and human recipients.121,122 Immune evasion further obviates any manipulations to recipient’s immune cells, thereby precluding the administration of immunomodulatory drugs, whether oral or parenteral, and mitigating the sequelae of excessive immunosuppression, as well as the exigency for stringent preconditioning regimens. The immune evasion strategy is predicated exclusively on the genetic modification of the allogeneic therapeutic cell product. No cases of malignant transformation of HLA-depleted cells have been reported in any preclinical or clinical study to date, rendering oncogenicity a purely hypothetical concern. Nevertheless, the HIP platform incorporates a built-in safety mechanism. In multiple independent studies, systemic administration of an anti-CD47 antibody has been shown to rapidly and selectively eliminate HIP cells in vivo while sparing endogenous CD47-expressing host cells.107,109,139 This antibody-based safety switch provides a reliable and controllable means to clear HIP cells should uncontrolled proliferation or malignant transformation occur. Similarly, while complete HLA class I and II deletion removes the primary pathway for CD8+ T cell recognition of infected cells, it does not abolish viral clearance. Classic studies in MHC class I-deficient mice have demonstrated that these animals can still clear respiratory influenza virus infection, albeit with modestly delayed kinetics, through intact innate mechanisms—including NK-cell “missing-self” recognition and type I interferon responses—as well as compensatory CD4+ T cell activity.148,149 Comparable results were observed with lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, where MHC class I-deficient hosts ultimately achieve viral control via alternative adaptive pathways.150 Collectively, these data indicate that innate and residual adaptive antiviral immunity remain largely functional even in the absence of classical HLA class I presentation. Based on currently available evidence, hypoimmune cell therapy that requires no immunosuppression appears to offer a substantially more favorable safety profile than conventional approaches using allogeneic, HLA-replete cells that rely on either chronic immunosuppression or complex tolerance induction protocols. This enhanced safety profile of hypoimmune islets is anticipated to boost patient demand and broaden inclusion criteria, extending from the most severely affected individuals currently eligible to those with milder disease seeking to minimize long-term complications. Scaling production of these therapeutics will be essential to address the needs of the millions worldwide living with T1D. Even if such products do not confer lifelong insulin independence, the capacity for repeated dosing could represent a practical and effective management option. Although the preclinical and early clinical data on hypoimmune cells are very consistent, this technology still needs to be tested in controlled clinical trials before definitive statements can be made.
