Overweight and Obesity Are Associated with Lower Renal Blood Flow in Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease
Authors: Carissa L. Birznieks, Wei Wang, Cortney Steele, Zhiying You, Berenice Gitomer, Michel Chonchol, Kristen L. Nowak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe have previously reported that overweight and obesity are independently associated with more rapid disease progression in adults with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). In this study, we perform a cross-sectional analysis to evaluate whether overweight and obesity are also associated with renal blood flow (RBF) in this patient population.MethodsA total of 134 non-diabetic adults with ADPKD and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 who participated in a randomized controlled trial of pravastatin therapy were categorized as normal weight, overweight, or obese, based on baseline adjusted body mass index (BMI), calculated after subtracting magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-derived kidney and liver mass from body weight. RBF was measured by phase contrast MRI and adjusted for body surface area (BSA) prior to analysis. The association of baseline BMI with baseline RBF was assessed using multivariable linear regression models.ResultsParticipants were 40 ± 10 years (mean ± SD), 67% female, with a baseline RBF of 635 ± 214 mL/min/1.73 m2, and a baseline Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology (CKD-EPID) eGFR of 90 ± 21 mL/min/1.73 m2. BSA-adjusted RBF was lower in a stepwise manner with increasing BMI category. After adjustment for demographics, systolic blood pressure, blood glucose, high-density lipoprotein, blood pressure medication usage, eGFR, and height-adjusted total kidney volume, both overweight (β-estimate: −78.4; 95% CI: −155.3, −1.6) and obesity (β-estimate: −190.6; 95% CI: −307.4, −73.7) remained significantly associated with lower RBF as compared to normal weight participants. For every one unit increase in BMI, RBF was 10.4 mL/min/1.73 m2 lower (95% CI: −16.9, −3.9) in the fully adjusted model.ConclusionOverweight and obesity are cross-sectionally associated with lower RBF in patients with early-stage ADPKD; however, these findings reflect associations only and do not provide insight into causality. Further research and longitudinal studies are needed to establish the temporal and causal nature of these relationships and to evaluate whether reduced RBF may be a mechanism by which obesity is associated with faster progression in patients with ADPKD.

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is the most common inherited renal disorder and is characterized by growth of numerous fluid-filled cysts that result in progressive kidney enlargement, destruction of the functional renal tissue, and eventual decline in kidney function [1]. Progression of ADPKD is measured by tracking the increase in total kidney volume (TKV) over time [2], though factors that drive faster kidney growth remain incompletely understood. Recent studies have shown that overweight and obesity are associated with greater annual increase in TKV [3, 4], faster declines in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and increased risk of end-stage kidney disease among early-stage patients with ADPKD [5].

Renal blood flow (RBF) is a critical factor that drives glomerular filtration and supports the metabolic needs of the kidneys by regulating the delivery of oxygenated blood. Disruptions to RBF can play a key role in worsening kidney pathologies [6], and early changes in renal microcirculation have been observed in ADPKD prior to any detectable decline in eGFR [7]. Despite the known associations between obesity and kidney disease progression, the relationship between body mass index (BMI), a measure of overweight and obesity, and RBF has not been thoroughly investigated.

Utilizing baseline data from a recent randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial assessing the efficacy of pravastatin therapy in adult patients with ADPKD and early-stage kidney disease (eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2), we explored the relationship between BMI and RBF. This analysis examined baseline BMI both as a categorical variable (overweight, obese) and as a continuous variable, and its association with baseline RBF.

Methods

We examined the association of overweight and obesity and RBF in individuals with ADPKD who participated in the statin therapy in patients with early-stage ADPKD trial (NCT03273413). The Statin trial was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to evaluate the benefits of pravastatin therapy in adults with early-stage ADPKD. Detailed methods of the Statin trial have been previously described [8, 9]. Blood samples were collected following an overnight fast. eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology (CKD-EPI) equation. All participants in the Statin trial underwent T1 and T2 coronal and phase-contrast abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) using a 3.0 T Siemens Skyra MR system (Siemens, Malvern, PA, USA) to measure TKV and RBF at baseline and after 2 years of treatment. MRI-measured RBF was evaluated utilizing Segment for Research (Medviso) and was adjusted for body surface area prior to statistical analysis. This analysis focuses on cross-sectional associations at baseline only, as longitudinal follow-up data collection and processing were still ongoing at the time this manuscript was prepared.

The association of BMI with RBF was assessed using linear regression models. This relationship was evaluated with BMI both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable. As patients with ADPKD often have enlarged kidneys and livers, participant BMI was first adjusted by subtracting the MRI-derived weight of these organs from total body weight, assuming a tissue density equal to that of water (1 g/cm3), as previously reported [4] and based on the 2025 KDIGO ADPKD clinical guidelines [10]. After adjusting BMI to exclude the contributions of liver and kidney weight, four participants were re-classified from overweight to normal weight, and one participant was re-classified from obese to overweight. Analyses were first performed using unadjusted BMI but were similar to those using adjusted BMI values. For categorial analyses, participants were categorized by baseline-adjusted BMI as normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2; n = 74), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2; n = 43), or obese (≥30 kg/m2; n = 17), with the normal weight category serving as the reference group for all analyses. Five participants from the Statin trial were categorized as underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2) and excluded from this analysis. In linear regression models, the dependent variable was RBF (mL/min/1.73 m2).

There was one potential statistical outlier for RBF identified. The analyses were re-run excluding this outlier. Otherwise, linearity, residual normality, and variance inflation factors (all <2.0) supported model validity. The initial model was unadjusted, then multivariable adjusted models were performed to include age and sex (model 1), model 1 plus systolic blood pressure, blood glucose, high-density lipoprotein, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker usage, calcium channel blocker usage, and diuretic usage (model 2), and model 2 plus eGFR, and height-adjusted TKV. The relationship between continuous BMI and demographic characteristics, including age and sex, was also evaluated utilizing a linear regression model. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 and SAS version 9.4. Two-tailed p values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 134 participants with early ADPKD who participated in the Statin study and had complete MRI data collected were included in the analysis of the association of overweight and obesity with RBF. Among these, 33% of participants were male, 96% were white, and the mean ± SD age was 40 ± 10 years. There was no significant difference in age, sex, race, eGFR, or systolic blood pressure between participants characterized as normal weight, overweight, or obese. Participants categorized as obese had significantly higher fasting blood glucose (p < 0.05) compared to normal weight participants. Both overweight and obese participants had significantly lower high-density lipoprotein (p < 0.0001) compared to normal weight participants (Table 1).

Relationship between BMI and RBF

In both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, overweight and obesity were both associated with lower RBF as compared to the normal weight group (Table 2). In a fully adjusted model, participants categorized as overweight had significantly lower RBF compared to normal weight individuals (model 3: β = −78.4 [95% CI: −155.3, −1.6]). This difference was even more pronounced in participants categorized as obese (model 3: β = −190.6 [95% CI: −307.4, −73.7]) (Fig. 1). When BMI was treated as a continuous variable, a 1-unit higher BMI was associated with a −10.4 [95% CI: −16.9, −3.9] unit lower RBF in the fully adjusted model. Results were similar when RBF was not adjusted for body surface area; in this model, each 1-unit increase in BMI was associated with a −17.3 [95% CI: −25.6.5, −9.0] unit lower RBF. Additionally, results remained similar when excluding the statistical outlier for RBF (−10.0 [−4.0, −16.0] unit lower RBF per unit increase in BMI).

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate a strong association between higher BMI and lower RBF in early-stage ADPKD. In the Statin trial, both overweight and obese patients exhibited significantly lower RBF compared to normal-weight individuals, and this association was significantly stronger in women than in men. This observation is consistent with recent data indicating that greater visceral adiposity was more strongly associated with kidney growth in women than in men [11]. While previous studies [3, 4] support that higher BMI correlates with faster ADPKD progression and greater annual percent change in TKV, the mechanisms underlying this accelerated progression remain unclear. Our results add to this body of work by identifying an association between elevated BMI and altered renal hemodynamics.

Because this analysis is cross-sectional in nature, causality cannot be inferred. The observed relationship between elevated BMI and lower RBF may arise through several possible pathways relevant to ADPKD progression. Impaired RBF could plausibly contribute to renal hypoxia and activate hypoxia-inducible transcription factor-1α signaling – an established promoter of cyst growth in ADPKD models [12, 13]. Beyond this signaling pathway, other obesity-related mechanisms may also reduce renal microcirculation, including increased inflammatory adipokine signaling, endothelial dysfunction, or obesity-associated fibrosis [14]. However, it is equally plausible that reduced RBF is a consequence, rather than a mechanistic driver, of more rapid disease progression. Processes associated with advancing ADPKD, such as systemic inflammation, impaired renal autoregulation, or progressive vascular remodeling, could independently lead to diminished RBF.

Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are therefore necessary to clarify the temporal sequence and causal relevance of these associations. Such work will be essential to determine whether reduced RBF plays an active role in accelerating ADPKD progression, reflects downstream consequences of obesity, or results from shared systemic processes. These findings also raise important translational questions regarding whether weight management interventions could improve renal hemodynamics in ADPKD. Future longitudinal and interventional trials – such as structured weight-loss programs, pharmacologic weight-management strategies, or lifestyle interventions paired with serial MRI-based RBF measurements – will be critical for determining whether improving metabolic health can enhance RBF and potentially slow disease progression.

The strengths of this study include the rigorous structure of a clinical trial with a substantial number of participants in the early stages of ADPKD. However, the results were limited to participants from the Statin trial, and extending this research to additional patient cohorts would improve the generalizability of these findings. The study population in this trial was predominantly white (96%), which further limits the applicability of these findings to more diverse racial and ethnic groups. Additionally, a limitation of the current analysis is its cross-sectional design, as only baseline data were available at the time of manuscript preparation. Future analyses will evaluate longitudinal changes in RBF over the 2-year duration of the Statin trial as follow-up data become available. As is common among patients with ADPKD, most participants in the Statin trial were taking antihypertensive medications. Use of these medications was not an exclusion criterion; however, all participants were required to be on a stable, unchanging dose for at least 3 months prior to enrollment. Because certain antihypertensives – such as diuretics and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors – can influence RBF, the inability to control for or adjust for specific medication types represents an additional limitation of this study.

This study also did not assess or had control for participants’ physical activity levels at baseline or during the trial, limiting our ability to account for lifestyle factors that may influence renal hemodynamics. The parent trial excluded individuals who smoked, reducing potential confounding from smoking-related vascular effects, but limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader ADPKD population. Finally, the obese subgroup was relatively small (n = 18) compared with the normal-weight (n = 70) and overweight (n = 56) groups, which reduces statistical power – particularly for sex-stratified analyses – and may limit the precision of the estimates in this category.

In conclusion, BMI is independently associated with lower RBF in patients with early-stage ADPKD. These findings highlight the need for further longitudinal and mechanistic studies to clarify the temporal and causal relationships between BMI, altered renal hemodynamics, and ADPKD progression. They also raise important translational questions regarding whether interventions aimed at improving metabolic health, such as weight management or lifestyle modification, could enhance RBF and potentially slow disease progression.
