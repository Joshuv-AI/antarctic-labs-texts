Application of electron diffraction to rapid structural elucidation of crude reaction products
Authors: Durga Prasad Karothu, Md. Ataur Rahman, Bhagavathula S. Diwakar, Torsten Cellnik, Brij Bhushan Ahuja, Christian R. Göb, Robert Bücker, Sanjit Manohar Majhi, Ejaz Ahmed, Alan R. Healy, Panče Naumov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The explicit confirmation of the identity of desired or side products of a chemical reaction often requires lengthy or iterative purification protocols, combined with multiple analytical procedures that could be restricted by the available sample quantity and purity, or might provide only qualitative information on the chemical composition. Product mixtures with overlapping spectroscopic signals, low solubility, reactivity in solution, poor crystallinity, and multiple sample preparation steps could pose further challenges and currently stand in the way of full automation of product characterization. Being capable of rapidly providing structural details down to atomic scale resolution from nanosized crystalline samples, three-dimensional electron diffraction (3D ED) (microcrystal electron diffraction) effectively overcomes several of these obstacles. Here, we demonstrate the potential for the application of 3D ED in the efficient and rapid characterization of reaction products that are difficult to characterize. Furthermore, the technology utilized in this paper comes with the benefits of reducing the carbon footprint of a synthesis procedure, eliminating redundant steps needed for common characterization methods, and providing a shortcut for characterization without increased costs. We suggest that, based on the speed and minimal sample requirements demonstrated here, coupling 3D ED/MicroED with automated synthetic workflows could help accelerate future drug discovery efforts.

Introduction

Many, if not the majority of the traditional synthetic approaches have relied on ex-situ product identification, following prior isolation procedures that typically generate sizeable amounts of contaminated solvents and that inevitably reduce the overall reaction yield. The environmental footprint of these procedures, however, is particularly compromised in the case of preparation of structurally complex compounds, such as natural products, or asymmetric and total synthetic protocols, where the complexity of characterization adds to the already demanding time- and cost-profiles of the respective preparation routines. Among the available characterization methods, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) has retained its position as the most commonly and routinely used technique to analyze organic and metal–ligand complex substances. However, while quantitative NMR is generally highly precise, its applicability can be limited for analytes that are poorly soluble or contain nuclei not detectable by the chosen probe, which may hinder accurate analysis in these specific cases. Furthermore, and specific to the characterization of metal–ligand complex species, the pronounced reactivity and intrinsic instability of some analytes in solution prevent the use of many common deuterated solvents1–6. The utilization of poorly solubilizing solvents inevitably comes at the expense of increased NMR acquisition times and/or the requirement for sensitive NMR probes. Although solid-state NMR can be used for poorly soluble compounds, its practical application is often limited by the requirement for larger sample quantities, more complex sample preparation, reduced sensitivity, and broader resonance lines that complicate spectral interpretation. Moreover, not all elements in a compound are NMR-active, which can further restrict the structural information obtained. As a result, solid-state NMR is not always a practical substitute for solution-state NMR characterization.

In a traditional synthetic sequence, conclusive evidence for the atomic-scale resolution is provided by analysis using single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). While the benefits of this method remain undisputed, many compounds are not directly amenable to the demanding—and to a large extent—serendipitous process of size- and quality-controlled crystallization, in addition to the requirement for special training in crystallography of the analyst. Synchrotron facilities and state-of-the-art in-house SCXRD can be employed for samples with sizes that can go down to micron sizes7. On the sample size scale, the state-of-the-art SCXRD methods cover a narrow range of suitable crystal sizes between approximately 5 and 300 µm, with larger samples requiring special correction procedures to account for their significant X-ray absorption or larger X-ray beams, while smaller, weakly diffracting crystals can only be analyzed with time- and cost-prohibitive X-ray sources, such as those available at synchrotrons. Alternatively, the samples can be analyzed as powders or by neutron diffraction. Powder diffraction (PXRD) is commonly available in materials characterization laboratories, but comes with challenges related to phase changes during sample preparation, difficulties with analyzing multiple phases, and the need for large amounts of sample. Moreover, determining novel structures can be particularly challenging due to peak overlap and ambiguities in indexing, which may limit its utility for unknown or complex compounds. Single crystal neutron diffraction (SCND) is rarely used8–11 due to the demanding deuteration procedures and unavailability of large-size crystals.

Three-dimensional electron diffraction (3D ED), alternatively referred to as microcrystal electron diffraction (MicroED), has recently emerged as an alternative or complementary approach to SCXRD for structure determination of both small and macromolecules. Electrons are scattered by both electrons and nuclei12,13, and owing to the stronger interaction of electrons with matter relative to X-rays, 3D ED is compliant with submicron size crystals14–16. A groundbreaking determination of the structure of the hen-egg white lysozyme demonstrated that the method can also be applied to determine structures of proteins17–29. More recently, the technical advancement and automation of 3D ED have expanded its application to very small crystals and even polycrystalline materials that are not amenable to SCXRD/PXRD/SCND17,30–52. Structural determination of small molecules using 3D ED/MicroED directly from their commercial formulations or crystalline powders has been demonstrated33. Rapid switching between imaging and diffraction allows for rapid assessment of morphologically distinct crystals without the need for physical separation53,thereby expanding the range of its applications to access powder formulations, suspensions, ointments, and mixtures33,54–63. The minimal sample preparation and fast data acquisition are central to the exclusive potential that this method carries for characterization of beam-sensitive and metastable samples41–49,51. Inspired by some recent efforts that highlight the potential of 3D ED for product identification64–66, here we demonstrate its advantages in the analysis of metal–ligand complex synthetic reaction products. In contrast to previous studies on isolated, pre-purified organometallic compounds, this work demonstrates the application of 3D ED for the direct structural characterization of crude reaction mixtures67. These reactions are often challenged by the necessity for tedious reaction work-ups, multiple purification iterations, poor stability of products, and the tendency to yield powders1–5. The approach presented here overcomes some or all of these challenges by its ability to provide the structure directly from the product mixture, thereby avoiding additional purification and crystallization steps to confirm the desired and/or unanticipated products of a reaction.

Results

In this work, we focused on the salen ligand 6,6′-((1E,1′E)-(((1S,2S)-cyclohexane-1,2-diyl)bis(azaneylylidene))bis(methaneylylidene))bis(2-methoxyphenol) (hereafter, labeled 1) which was synthesized by condensation of chiral (1R,2R)- or (1S,2S)-cyclohexyl diamine with salicylaldehyde derivatives that are crystalline and readily soluble in most organic solvents (Scheme 1). As shown in Scheme 1, when 1 is treated with a transition metal, it forms metal-salen complexes. Specifically, the titanium salen (Ti-salen) complexes (2 and 3) were obtained by treating 1 with [Ti(OiPr)4] in CH2Cl2 (for details, see the Methods section and Supplementary Fig. 1–6). The complex formation can be observed as it separates as an orange powder (Fig. 1a, b). Most similar products are obtained as powders that are moderately soluble in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and dimethyl formamide (DMF)68,69, and are notoriously difficult to crystallize in a size amenable to SCXRD. Due to the poor solubility and lack of propensity for crystallization, structural analysis by NMR and SCXRD is a daunting task; we estimate that the combined procedures for purification, NMR characterization, crystallization, X-ray diffraction and the successful structural analysis of the product would require approximately 150–500 hours of time at a considerable cost (Fig. 1b–d). In an attempt to skip this lengthy procedure, the solvent was removed, and the obtained crude reaction product mixture was analyzed using powder X-ray diffraction and inspected by scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 1e, f; Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). For complex 2, only a few reflections in the experimental PXRD pattern match the simulated pattern. This discrepancy partly arises from differences in data collection temperature (170 K ED data vs. RT PXRD data), which can affect unit cell parameters and thus peak positions. Minor differences in peak intensities are attributed to crystallinity, particle size, and preferred orientation. In addition, the limited agreement suggests that the obtained crystal structure may not be fully representative of the bulk material and could correspond to a minor phase. In contrast, complex 3 shows much better agreement, while coordinated solvent molecules are present in both structures. The byproducts and impurities are expected to include unreacted starting materials and minor side products. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on complexes 2 and 3 to evaluate their thermal stability and decomposition behavior (Supplementary Fig. 9). For complex 2, an initial weight loss observed below approximately 393 K is attributed to the loss of coordinated water molecules, followed by a gradual mass loss at higher temperatures corresponding to ligand pyrolysis. In contrast, complex 3 exhibits an earlier and more continuous weight loss, consistent with the release of benzyl alcohol, followed by thermal decomposition of the ligand framework. In both cases, the residual mass at elevated temperatures is consistent with the formation of TiO₂ under the applied conditions. Based on their thermal stability and decomposition profiles, these metal-ligand complexes were further evaluated by electron diffraction (ED) experiments. Prior to ED measurements, the powder was finely ground to break up agglomerates and reduce particle size for uniform sample distribution. The resulting powder was analyzed by 3D ED/MicroED at selected temperatures by dispersing the sample on holey or lacey carbon TEM grids. The electron diffraction data were collected using a dedicated 3D ED/MicroED instrument. The rotation-range of each tested sample varied depending on the sample position on the TEM grid due to the obstruction by neighboring crystals and grid walls (additional details about individual measurements are provided in Supplementary Table 1).

Before the 3D ED/MicroED experiments, the real-space images of crystallites of complexes 2 and 3 (Scheme 1) were obtained using the Synergy-ED dedicated electron diffractometer in defocused diffraction mode, which provides “real-space” visualization rather than conventional TEM imaging (Fig. 2a–g). Once the suitable crystal was selected, the electron diffraction frames for 2 and 3 were collected, indexed, and integrated separately by using the CrysAlisPro software package70. The crystal structures of both 2 and 3 were solved with SHELXT71, and refined using SHELXL72 in Olex273. Due to the data collection in small wedges, it is often required to merge the individual data sets after the indexing to improve the completeness, as well as the I/σ(I) value for structure solution, and the final refinements. The individual experimental parameters for both 2 and 3 before merging are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The datasets of 2 and 3 were cut to the resolution limits of 0.80 Å and 0.90 Å, respectively, and the positions of the hydrogen atoms were refined by using riding models using bond distances obtained by neutron diffraction74. The total diffraction measurement and the analysis of each complex were completed in less than 30 min, and the entire characterization, starting from the reaction workup (including drying of the sample) to the structural characterization by 3D ED/MicroED, took less than 3 hours for each complex. The dataset of complex 2 contained 14525 total and 7045 independent reflections at 81% completeness and Rint = 0.1600. The crystal is monoclinic, space group C2, with unit cell parameters a = 18.987(5) Å, b = 15.6195(11) Å, c = 15.338(2) Å, and β = 110.78(3)o (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Data 1). Diffraction data collected from four individual crystals of complex 3 were merged, and the resulting dataset consisted of 30604 total and 7379 independent reflections, with a combined completeness of up to 91% and Rint = 0.2030. The completeness of individual datasets of complex 3 was between 59 to 76% for a triclinic solution, which highlights the power of ED in collecting high-quality data over a limited number of frames for weakly diffracting crystals (Fig. 2h). The crystals of complex 3 are triclinic, space group P1, with unit cell parameters a = 9.5841(9) Å, b = 11.3905(11) Å, c = 13.9122(16) Å, α = 98.199(10)o, β = 107.953(11)o, and γ = 95.888(8)o. Additional crystallographic parameters can be found in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Data 2. As shown in Fig. 2b, c, g, and Supplementary Figs. 10, 11, the diffraction and the reciprocal lattices, reconstructed by using Ewald 3D70 from all datasets of 2 and 3, show well-resolved reflections.

The refined crystal structures (Fig. 2d, i) revealed that the Ti-salen complexes are oxo-bridged di-substituted (di-μ-oxotitanium) complexes with a layered structure75,76. Fig. 2d, e, i illustrate the molecular structure and coordination environment of the complex. As shown in Fig. 2f, the molecules of complex 2 are stacked on top of each other along the crystallographic b direction, forming a columnar packing of the ligand-containing complexes. The asymmetric unit and crystal packing analysis reveals that the center of symmetry falls together with the centroid of one 5-membered and two six-membered rings membered rings formed by oxygen, nitrogen and titanium in both 2 and 3 (Fig. 2e–i). Packing analysis of complex 2 reveals a continuous arrangement of water molecules along the crystallographic b axis (Fig. 2f). Several recent reports successfully demonstrated the ability of 3D ED/MicroED to determine crystal structures of active pharmaceutical ingredients directly from commercial samples33,58. Within that context, the analysis performed here directly on the reaction product crude mixture demonstrates further the immense potential that 3D ED/MicroED holds for rapid structural characterization of anticipated or unexpected products of metal–ligand complex reactions by circumventing the need for purification and growth of large-size single crystals.

Conclusions

The identification of the products is at the core of understanding reactivity and structural characterization. The ability to identify these products in a manner that can reduce reliance on certain lengthy procedures, while still employing complementary purification, spectroscopic, and diffraction methods, has long been a desired goal in synthetic chemistry. Electron diffraction is poised to respond to the demand for methods for rapid characterization and identification. The work presented expands the application of 3D ED/MicroED to metal–ligand complex reactions, without the need for purifications or crystallizations. We analyzed the product reaction mixture directly after the solvent removal and fine grinding, and the structures of the two products were determined. The ease of use and rapid data collection set the stage for wider application with the determination of complex and challenging structures, showcasing the potential of 3D ED/MicroED to become an integral part of the drug discovery process. 3D ED/MicroED is a powerful technique that not only reduces the human force needed, time invested and instrumentation utilized for the characterization of synthesis products, but it also holds a potential with reducing the carbon footprint. The minimalistic sample size utilized by 3D ED/MicroED directly participates in lowering the amount of waste produced. Also, the technology helps alleviate the need for other methods, which can ultimately decrease the energy consumption and financial costs of characterization.

Methods

Materials

(1R,2R)/(1S,2S)-cyclohexyldiamine and ortho-vanillin were commercially sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. Solvents were used as received and without further purification.

Synthesis of the salen ligand 1

The ligand 1 was prepared according to the reported procedure77. To a solution of (1R,2R)/(1S,2S)-cyclohexane-1,2-diamine (1 equiv.) in ethanol was added ortho-vanillin (2.05 equiv) in one portion. The resulting solution was stirred for 12 h at 353 K. After completion of the reaction, the formed salen ligand (∼90% yield) was obtained as a precipitate and purified by column chromatography.

Synthesis of the complex 2

In a 10 mL round-bottom flask, 1 (191.2 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) was taken. The ligand was dissolved in dichloromethane (2.0 mL). To this, Ti(OiPr)4 (0.149 mL, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv) was added, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 30 min at room temperature. After 30 min, H2O (0.020 mL, 1.1 mmol, 2.2 equiv) was added to the reaction mixture and stirred for 1 h. After 1 h, the product formed as a yellow solid, which was isolated by solvent removal to obtain the crude Ti-salen dimer 2 (∼70% yield).

Synthesis of the complex 3

3 was prepared similarly to complex 2. To a 10 mL round-bottom flask, 1 (191.2 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv) was added. The ligand was dissolved in dichloromethane (2.0 mL). Ti(OiPr)4 (0.149 mL, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv) was added to the reaction mixture and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. Benzyl alcohol (0.114 mL, 1.1 mmol, 2.2 equiv) was added to the reaction mixture and stirred for 1 h. The product formed as a yellow solid, which was isolated by solvent removal to obtain the crude Ti-salen complex dimer 3 (∼65% yield).

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)

The proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectra (1H NMR) were recorded at 500 MHz, and carbon nuclear magnetic resonance spectra (13C NMR) were recorded at 125 MHz using Bruker Avance FT-NMR spectrometer.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal profiles of the crude products obtained after the removal of the solvent from the reaction product mixture of 2 and 3 were performed on TA Instruments TGA-Q600. About 10–14 mg of sample was weighed on an aluminum pan and heated from room temperature to 773 K at a heating rate of 10 K min–1 and found to be stable at ambient conditions.

Scanning electron microscopy

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was carried out on a SEM stub using carbon tape with a QUANTA FEG 450 electron microscope with an electron energy of 30 kV, a spot size of 3.0 and using a secondary electron (SE) detector.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD)

The synthesized complexes (2 and 3) were used without purification after the removal of the solvent from the product mixture. Crude mixtures were finely ground and the PXRD patterns were collected on a Panalytical Empyrean diffractometer with a PIXcel3D detector in Bragg–Brentano geometry, using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5418740 Å) in the 2θ range 2–50° (continuous scan mode, step size 0.01313°; time/step 48 s; Soller slit 0.04 rad; antiscatter slit 1/16°; divergence slit 1/4; 40 mA × 45 kV). The measurements were performed on both 2 and 3 at ambient temperature.

3D ED/MicroED sample preparation and data collection

The detailed sample preparation and data collection strategies were reported earlier58. 2 and 3 were tested for their vacuum and electron beam stability by SEM. During sample preparation, a pair of microscopic slides was used to reduce the thickness of the crystallites. Holey or lacey carbon TEM grids were used for 3D ED/MicroED measurements. The diffraction experiments of complex 2 were performed at 170 K (cryo-transfer) to stabilize cocrystallized water molecules in the crystal lattice and increase the scattering intensity of the sample. Complex 3 was measured at room temperature, as the utilized tomography holder offered a wider scan range, which is beneficial for data completeness in highly unsymmetric space groups. 3D ED/MicroED measurements were performed on a dedicated and fully-integrated electron diffractometer (XtaLAB Synergy-ED electron diffractometer, the HyPix-ED as a direct electron detector, and CrysAlisPro for 3D ED/MicroED as software controlling these components)70. After loading the samples in the electron diffractometer, suitable crystals were located and centered at the eucentric point of the goniometer using the imaging mode of the device. Consequently, diffraction data were collected, using a selected-area aperture, by the continuous rotation method while the accelerating voltage was set to 200 kV (corresponding to a wavelength of 0.0251 Å). The rotation range can be changed based on the sample position on the grid and the sample holder (ca. −80° to +80° for standard sample holder and ca. −60° to +60° for cryotransfer holder). The individual diffraction measurements of complexes took approximately 3 to 5 min each with an exposure time of 2.0 s. The individual measurements yielded data completeness between 58 and 80% up to a resolution of 0.90 Å. By using CrysAlisPro as a 3D ED/MicroED software package (a combined instrument control and data processing package)70, the obtained electron diffraction frames were indexed, integrated and merged. The structure determination of both complexes was performed with the program SHELXT71 using the intrinsic phasing solution method and refined kinematically using Olex-refine in Olex273 as a graphical interface. Dynamical refinement in Jana2020 was conducted against the unscaled data to confirm the correct chirality assignment of the crystal structures. For this purpose, the scale and ED layer thickness parameters were refined in Jana2020 (version 1.3.61) for each enantiomorph, after which a Z-score was calculated34,78.
