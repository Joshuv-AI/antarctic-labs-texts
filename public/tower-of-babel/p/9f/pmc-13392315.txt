Effectiveness and safety of water laser-assisted endodontic–periodontal therapy: A retrospective controlled study
Authors: Jing Li, Chunyan Chen, Donghui Wu, Fengqing Tan, Juan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCombined endodontic–periodontal lesions are challenging to manage because complete debridement is often difficult to achieve with conventional root canal therapy and scaling and root planing alone. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness and safety of adjunctive water laser therapy in improving pulp–periodontal outcomes.MethodsThis single-center retrospective controlled study included 634 teeth with 12 ± 3 months of follow-up, including 295 treated with adjunctive water laser treatment and 339 treated according to a conventional nonlaser regimen. Propensity score overlap weighting was used to balance baseline characteristics, with all standardized differences <0.10 after weighting. The primary endpoint was pulp–periodontal composite success. Secondary endpoints included changes in probing depth and clinical attachment level, bleeding on probing negativity, and acute exacerbation within 7 postoperative days. Safety was assessed using a Miettinen–Nurminen noninferiority test with a 5% risk-difference margin.ResultsThe water laser group showed a higher composite success rate than the control group (risk ratio = 1.22, 95% confidence interval: 1.10 to 1.36; risk difference = 13.88 percentage points, 95% confidence interval: 6.97 to 20.34; p = 0.003). Radiographic healing was also more favorable in the water laser group. Additionally, the water laser group demonstrated greater improvement in probing depth (β = −0.39 mm, 95% confidence interval: −0.56 to −0.22; p = 0.001) and clinical attachment level (β = −0.28 mm, 95% confidence interval: −0.44 to −0.12; p = 0.001), a higher bleeding on probing–negative rate (risk ratio = 1.20, 95% confidence interval: 1.07 to 1.34; p = 0.003), and fewer acute exacerbations (risk ratio = 0.52, 95% confidence interval: 0.30 to 0.88; p = 0.016). Overall adverse events met the noninferiority criterion. Greater benefit was observed in teeth with baseline probing depth ≥6 mm.ConclusionsAdjunctive water laser therapy was associated with improved short- to mid-term pulp–periodontal outcomes, enhanced periodontal status, and a lower risk of early postoperative exacerbations without compromising safety. The benefits appeared particularly pronounced in teeth presenting with deep periodontal pockets.

Introduction

Apical periodontitis accompanied with periodontitis is common in dental practice and is usually classified as a combined endodontic–periodontal lesion. Its development involves interactions among the root canal system, periapical tissues, and periodontal supporting structures.1,2 The conventional treatment strategy consists of root canal treatment combined with supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing. This approach controls local infection and inflammation through mechanical preparation, chemical irrigation, and root surface debridement. However, complete biofilm removal and bacterial reduction remain challenging in teeth with complex canal anatomy and deep periodontal pockets.3,4

Water laser technology uses water as the medium for energy transmission and can generate hydrodynamic and cavitation effects within the root canal and periodontal environment, thereby improving irrigation and debridement. 5 In vitro and clinical studies have suggested that this technique can reduce bacterial load and support tissue healing, and it has gradually been adopted as an adjunctive tool in endodontic and periodontal treatment. 6 Nevertheless, most studies on combined endodontic–periodontal lesions have focused on either endodontic or periodontal outcomes alone. Composite endpoints have often been based on radiographic or probing indices, with limited attention to simultaneous healing of both periapical and periodontal tissues. 7 Similarly, studies on the use of water laser therapy in endodontics have mainly addressed smear layer removal, bacterial elimination within the canal, or symptom-related outcomes such as pain scores and analgesic use. Real-world cohort evidence that systematically evaluates pulp-side outcomes, periodontal-side outcomes, postoperative acute exacerbation, and detailed adverse event profiles remains scarce. 8 Given the relatively high equipment and operating costs of water laser systems, it remains clinically important to determine whether this approach provides meaningful benefit over conventional root canal treatment combined with basic periodontal therapy, and whether its safety is at least not inferior to that of traditional regimens in general hospital populations. 9 Therefore, this retrospective controlled study compared water laser-assisted root canal treatment combined with supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing with a conventional nonlaser regimen combined with scaling and root planing. Outcomes included composite success, periodontal inflammation control, postoperative acute exacerbation, and adverse events. Propensity score overlap weighting was used to reduce baseline confounding. The study aimed to provide real-world evidence for the use of water laser-assisted therapy in root canal treatment among patients with concomitant periodontal involvement.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, retrospective controlled study was conducted using data from the electronic medical record system and imaging archive system of the Departments of Oral Medicine and Cariology and Endodontics at our hospital. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 10 Consecutive teeth that underwent root canal treatment and supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing within the same treatment period as part of routine clinical care were eligible. The study period extended from 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2023. Ethical approval was obtained before study implementation from the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Haizhu District Stomatological Hospital on 17 August 2025, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024. All patient data were deidentified before analysis to ensure that individual participants could not be identified.

Source of cases, time window, and follow-up

Data sources and case retrieval strategy

Eligible teeth that underwent root canal treatment and scaling/root planing within the same treatment period were identified and retrieved from the electronic medical record system; baseline and follow-up digital periapical radiographs were obtained through the imaging system. A standardized case record form was established for each affected tooth to record the index date, treatment process, baseline covariates, outcome indices, and adverse events. The index date was defined as the later date at which root canal filling and subgingival root planing were completed for that tooth in order to ensure that follow-up uniformly started after completion of the exposure and periodontal infection removal. Follow-up radiographs were obtained within 9–15 months after the index date; if there were multiple follow-up radiographs, the one dating closest to 12 months was selected.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria and sample size

Inclusion criteria. Single-rooted or multirooted affected teeth that were clinically diagnosed with pulp infection or periapical lesions and had formation of periodontal pockets or an increase in probing depth (PD) were eligible. According to the inclusion criteria, the affected teeth should have completed root canal treatment and supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing within the study time window; the index date should be clearly identifiable; baseline and follow-up clinical and radiographic data should be available; patient age should be ≥18 years; and complete medical records should be available for extraction.Exclusion criteria. Affected teeth with vertical root fracture, irreversible periodontal support destruction leading to grade III mobility, grade III furcation involvement, and history of apical surgery or root-end filling were excluded. Cases in which radiographic evaluation was affected due to nonodontogenic reasons were excluded; cases with <9 months of follow-up after the index date or with missing key outcome data were excluded; cases with systemic antibiotic use within 14 days before the index date for reasons unrelated to odontogenic infection were excluded.

Based on the composite success rate of the primary outcome, the sample size for the comparison of two proportions was estimated; assuming that the composite success rate in the water laser group was 80% and that in the control group was 60%, with a two-sided α of 0.05 and power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated by the normal approximation formula as 82 affected teeth per group, resulting in a total of 164 affected teeth. Owing to the retrospective study design, in actual enrollment, a strategy of including all consecutive eligible cases was adopted, and after reaching the above minimum sample size, all cases meeting the eligibility criteria continued to be included. If the actual allocation ratio between groups was unequal, overlap weighting was used at the analysis stage to reduce the impact of imbalance on the variance.

Definition and standardization of interventions and control

Exposure group

During the study period, adjunctive water laser treatment was available in routine practice as an optional addition to conventional root canal treatment and periodontal debridement. Treatment allocation was not randomized. The decision to use water laser therapy was made before treatment initiation by the treating clinician in consultation with the patient, based on clinical feasibility, device availability, appointment scheduling, and patient preference. No institutional policy restricted the use of water laser treatment according to disease severity levels, tooth types, or retreatment cases. However, given the retrospective design, unrecorded factors such as patient preference, willingness to pay, operator preference, and scheduling constraints could not be fully excluded. In the exposure group, water laser-assisted root canal treatment combined with supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing was used. The water laser treatment employed an erbium, chromium:yttrium-scandium-gallium-garnet laser (wavelength 2780 nm) to perform debridement and activation of the root canal system under continuous water spray cooling. The standardized parameters were an output power of 1.25 W (25 mJ × 50 Hz), with a fixed pulse mode, in combination with a radially emitting fiber tip placed at the canal orifice to perform four 30-s cycles with 10-s intervals between cycles, aiming to produce hydrodynamic effects and reduce direct energy transmission toward the apex. During the study period, all water laser procedures were performed using the same institutional laser system, following to a standardized clinical protocol. Review of the available records identified no protocol modifications that would have influenced key treatment settings, including energy output, pulse frequency, fiber tip type, and activation cycle. Adherence to the laser protocol was verified retrospectively by studying treatment notes, operative records, and laser parameters documented in the electronic medical record system. Cases lacking sufficient documentation to confirm compliance with the predefined protocol were excluded from the analyses. The root canal irrigant consisted of 3% sodium hypochlorite and 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, and the laser was used only for irrigant activation and biofilm disruption, without direct irradiation of tissues beyond the apical foramen. Supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing were completed within the same treatment period, scheduled within 14 days before or after root canal filling, with a focus on completing them during the same session.

Control group

The control group underwent conventional nonlaser root canal treatment combined with supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing. The root canal treatment included standardized mechanical preparation and chemical irrigation, uniformly using 3% sodium hypochlorite and 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid as well as two rounds of passive ultrasonic-activated irrigation (30 s per round). Root canal filling was performed using the warm gutta-percha vertical compaction technique, and the sealing method was the same as that used for the exposure group. The specific procedures, instrument types, and timing of supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing were the same as those in the exposure group; however, no laser device was used.

Intraoperative key parameters and operator consistency control

All procedures were completed under rubber dam isolation and local infiltration anesthesia. Mechanical preparation followed a crown-down concept; the final file size reached at least International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 30 with a 0.04 taper and reached ISO size 35 with a 0.04 taper for large roots in multirooted teeth. 11 The total volume of irrigation per affected tooth was ≥15 mL of sodium hypochlorite, and the final irrigation with 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid was ≥5 mL and was left in place for 60 s. 12 Root canal filling was performed using warm gutta-percha vertical compaction in combination with a resin-based sealer. Supragingival scaling was performed using an ultrasonic scaler, and subgingival root planing was performed using a standard curette system with six-point coverage. To limit operator- and procedure-related variability, all treatments were performed by attending dentists or senior clinicians trained in the institutional endodontic–periodontal protocol. Key procedural elements, including irrigant concentration and minimum volume, irrigation activation, root canal obturation, and periodontal instrumentation, were standardized across both groups. Postoperatively, ibuprofen was routinely administered for analgesia as needed. Prophylactic antibiotics were not routinely used; they were prescribed only for systemic symptoms or local acute suppurative manifestations according to institutional guidelines.

Outcome indices and criteria for determination

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the pulp–periodontal composite success rate at 12 ± 3 months. Composite success was defined as the concurrent achievement of both pulp-related and periodontal-related treatment goals. Pulpal success was defined based on whether the follow-up periapical index (PAI) score was ≤2 or had reduced by at least 1 grade compared with that at baseline, with no evidence of spontaneous pain, tenderness to percussion, sinus tract formation, or localized swelling during follow-up. 13

Periodontal success was assessed according to the baseline deepest PD. For teeth with a baseline deepest PD <6 mm, success required a deepest PD ≤4 mm at follow-up together with the absence of bleeding on probing (BOP) at all six sites of the study tooth. For teeth with a baseline deepest PD ≥6 mm, success was defined based on whether there was a reduction of ≥2 mm in the deepest PD compared with baseline and whether BOP was absent at all 6 sites. This approach was adopted to account for differences in baseline periodontal severity to identify periodontal stability in teeth with shallower pockets and meaningful clinical improvement in those presenting with deeper periodontal defects. 14

Secondary outcome

Secondary outcomes comprised periodontal and postoperative clinical outcomes. Periodontal outcomes included the change in deepest probing depth (ΔPD), BOP negativity rate, and the change in clinical attachment level (ΔCAL) from baseline to follow-up. ΔPD was defined as the deepest PD at follow-up minus the deepest PD at baseline. ΔCAL was defined as the CAL at follow-up minus the CAL at baseline. For both measures, negative values indicated clinical improvement. BOP negativity was defined as the absence of bleeding on probing at all six sites of the target tooth. Additionally, postoperative acute exacerbation was defined based on whether there was an unplanned visit within 7 days after the index date because of pain or swelling, requiring oral analgesics, antibiotic prescription, or incision and drainage. The incidence proportion was calculated using the affected tooth as the unit of analysis.

Safety outcome

Adverse events included soft tissue burning pain, bleeding, exacerbation of thermal sensitivity, iatrogenic perforation, instrument separation, root fracture and other events requiring medical management during the treatment period and follow-up period that were related to treatment. Safety was assessed using a noninferiority test on risk difference (RD), with a prespecified noninferiority margin of 5 percentage points. 15 Taking the adverse event incidence in the water laser group minus that in the control group as the index, if the upper bound of the one-sided 97.5% confidence interval (CI) for the RD was less than 5 percentage points, water laser treatment was judged to be noninferior in terms of safety.

Assessment and measurement methods

Radiographic assessment

Periapical radiographs were acquired using the institutional digital imaging system with a standardized parallel technique and positioning holder. Review of the imaging records revealed no documented modifications to the radiographic acquisition protocol during the study period that could have influenced exposure parameters or the assessment of the PAI. Two trained evaluators independently interpreted PAI under conditions in which treatment group information was masked, and the consistent conclusion was adopted; in case of disagreement, a third senior evaluator made the decision. For a randomly selected 15% of the images, repeated scoring was performed to calculate weighted κ values and evaluate agreement, with a target weighted κ of not less than 0.75.

Periodontal clinical assessment

Periodontal examination was performed within 7 days before or after the index date for baseline measurement and within the follow-up window for remeasurement, using a UNC-15 periodontal probe. Measurements were taken at 6 sites on the target tooth with a standard probing force of 20–25 g, and the deepest PD, clinical attachment level and BOP at six sites were recorded. Calibration training was completed and an assessment was passed before measurement, and a 10% sample with repeated measurements was used to calculate intraclass consistency, with a target intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of not less than 0.80.

Assessment of evaluator agreement and implementation of blinding

Radiographic assessment and periodontal assessment were both performed by evaluators who did not participate in treatment; group codes were locked before data extraction, and exposure information was hidden in the assessment interface. Training used a standard case library, and passing the consistency threshold was required before entering formal assessment. All assessment records were stored in audit-traceable electronic spreadsheets.

Definition of variables, data extraction, and quality control

Baseline covariates included age, sex, smoking status, diabetes status, whether root canal retreatment was performed, tooth position category, baseline PAI grade, deepest PD of the target tooth, baseline pain score, and presence or absence of a sinus tract. Research nurses and research physicians independently extracted data according to the data dictionary, and conflicts were judged by a third party. A 10% random sample was checked a second time to evaluate extraction accuracy, and if the error rate was higher than 2%, a full recheck was initiated. Missing values were recorded at the extraction stage with their sources and reasons, and a missingness matrix was created for statistical processing. All data underwent consistency checks and logical verification before database lock.

Statistical analyses

The primary analysis unit was the affected tooth. Considering the correlation among multiple affected teeth within the same patient, all models were handled using cluster-robust standard errors at the patient level or a multilevel structure. First, standardized differences of the original baseline characteristics were reported. Then, a propensity score model was constructed with receipt of water laser treatment as the dependent variable, including prespecified confounders. Overlap weighting was used to calculate weights, with the weight for the water laser group being 1−p and that for the control group being p, with the goal of maximizing the common support region and reducing model instability. After weighting, balance was reassessed, with the threshold for standardized differences set at 0.10. If key variables could not reach the threshold, doubly robust estimation with simultaneous covariate adjustment was performed in the main analysis.

Main analysis and presentation of effect size

For the primary outcome, weighted Poisson regression with robust variance estimation was used to determine the risk ratio (RR), and the corresponding RDs and 95% CIs were reported. The same model structure was used for secondary outcomes. Continuous periodontal outcomes, including ΔPD and ΔCAL, were analyzed using linear mixed models, with patient-level random effects to account for clustering of multiple teeth within the same patient. All analyses were performed using two-sided tests, and the significance level was set at α of 0.05.

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis plan

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to baseline deepest PD ≥6 mm and whether root canal retreatment was performed. Subgroup analyses incorporated interaction terms within the overlap weighting framework to evaluate effect modification. Sensitivity analyses included a complete case analysis which only included cases that completed follow-up; replacement of the main model with a generalized estimating equation binary Logit model; replacement of overlap weighting with 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching using a caliper of 0.2, followed by repetition of the main analysis; and assessment of the robustness of the primary conclusion using different PAI thresholds (a strict threshold of PAI ≤2 and a decrease of ≥1 grade from that at baseline).

Noninferiority determination and multiplicity

For the noninferiority assessment of safety adverse events, the Miettinen–Nurminen method was used to calculate the one-sided 97.5% CI for the RD, and noninferiority was determined if the upper bound was <5 percentage points. The primary outcome was the only target for formal significance assessment, whereas secondary outcomes focused on estimates and CIs; no formal multiplicity correction was performed.

Results

Enrollment and follow-up

In total, 1137 treated teeth were identified from the records. After applying the study period and eligibility criteria, 831 teeth were deemed eligible for follow-up evaluation. Among these, 634 teeth underwent both radiographic and periodontal reassessments within 12 ± 3 months and were included in the final analyses, representing 76.3% of the eligible follow-up cohort and 55.8% of all retrieved teeth. Among the 197 teeth not included in the final analysis, 109 lacked eligible follow-up radiographs, and 88 had no follow-up periodontal records. Because assessment of the pulp–periodontal composite outcome required both radiographic and periodontal data, only teeth with complete reassessment were included in the analyses (Figure 1).

Baseline characteristics and measurement consistency

The analysis set comprised 634 treated teeth, including 295 in the water laser group and 339 in the control group. Baseline balance between the groups was assessed using descriptive statistics and absolute standardized differences, without significance testing. After overlap weighting, all prespecified covariates had absolute standardized differences <0.10, indicating good comparability in the demographic characteristics and disease severity between the water laser and control groups (Table 1). Weighted κ and ICC values were used to assess agreement for radiographic interpretation and periodontal measurements. Agreement for PAI scoring as well as PD and clinical attachment level measurements was good, with all κ/ICC values ≥0.80 (Table 2).

Primary outcome

After overlap weighting, weighted Poisson regression with a log link and patient-level cluster-robust standard errors was used to evaluate the prespecified primary endpoint. At 12 ± 3 months, the pulp–periodontal composite success rate was higher in the water laser group than in the control group (231/295, weighted: 78.26% vs. 219/339, weighted: 64.38%; RR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.10 to 1.36; RD = 13.88 percentage points, 95% CI: 6.97 to 20.34; p = 0.003) (Table 3). To further characterize the distribution of outcomes, teeth were classified into four mutually exclusive categories based on whether they met both pulp-side and periodontal-side success criteria, only the pulp-side criterion, only the periodontal-side criterion, or neither criterion. The proportions of teeth meeting only the pulp-side criterion and neither criterion were lower in the water laser group (weighted: 12.83% vs. 21.89% and 3.67% vs. 8.94%, respectively). The proportion of teeth meeting only the periodontal-side criterion was comparable between the groups (weighted: 5.24% vs. 4.79%). Overall, the higher composite success rate observed in the water laser group was primarily driven by a greater proportion of teeth achieving simultaneous pulp-side and periodontal-side success. Changes in the PAI from baseline to follow-up are shown in Figure 2. Compared with the control group, the water laser group had a higher proportion of teeth showing an improvement of ≥1 PAI grade (57.43% vs. 43.68%), a lower proportion showing worsening by ≥1 PAI grade (6.45% vs. 16.58%), and a similar proportion with no change (36.12% vs. 39.74%), indicating a more favorable radiographic healing pattern.

Periodontal and secondary outcomes

After overlap weighting, periodontal outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models and weighted Poisson regression with a log link and patient-level cluster-robust standard errors. Compared with the control group, the water laser group showed greater reductions in the ΔPD (weighted: −1.68 ± 0.09 vs. −1.29 ± 0.08 mm; β = −0.39 mm, 95% CI: −0.56 to −0.22; p = 0.001) and ΔCAL (weighted: −0.96 ± 0.07 vs. −0.68 ± 0.06 mm; β = −0.28 mm, 95% CI: −0.44 to −0.12; p = 0.001). The water laser group also demonstrated a higher BOP negativity rate than the control group (259/295, weighted: 81.92% vs. 239/339, weighted: 68.45%; RR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.07 to 1.34; p = 0.003). Acute exacerbation within 7 postoperative days was less common in the water laser group (13/295, weighted: 4.62% vs. 31/339, weighted: 8.96%; RR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.30 to 0.88; p = 0.016) (Table 4).

Safety and adverse events

The Miettinen–Nurminen method was used to calculate the one-sided 97.5% CI for RD. For overall adverse events, the RD (water laser group minus control group) was −1.88 percentage points, and the upper bound of the one-sided 97.5% CI was 2.94 percentage points, which was below the noninferiority margin of 5 percentage points. Therefore, the safety of the two groups was judged to be comparable (Figure 3). Incidence rates were estimated using overlap weighting, and RDs with 95% CIs were calculated using weighted binomial distribution. For each adverse event type, the RD was negative, and the 95% CI crossed 0, indicating no observed increase in the risk of any specific adverse event. The distribution of adverse event types was consistent with the noninferiority finding for overall adverse events (Table 5).

Prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses

After overlap weighting, weighted Poisson regression with a log link was used, and treatment-by-subgroup interaction terms were added to the model. Teeth with baseline deepest PD ≥6 mm showed greater relative benefit (p for interaction = 0.048). No significant interaction was observed across strata defined using the baseline PAI, retreatment status, or tooth position (all p for interaction > 0.05), and the direction of effect was consistent and stable (Figure 4). Sensitivity analyses produced effect estimates in the same direction as the main analysis, with all RRs >1 and limited differences in magnitude (change in the risk ratio (ΔRR) = 0.01–0.04), indicating the robustness of the main conclusion (Table 6).

Data completeness

Among the 831 teeth eligible for follow-up, 634 (76.3%) had both radiographic and periodontal reassessments and were included in the primary analysis. No PAI scores or adverse event records were missing within the analysis set. Missing radiographic and periodontal follow-up data were comparable between the groups, with all absolute standardized differences <0.10 (Table 7). To evaluate potential attrition bias, baseline characteristics were compared between teeth with complete and incomplete follow-up. Multivariable logistic regression analyses revealed that there was no significant association of incomplete follow-up with the treatment group, age, sex, diabetes, smoking status, tooth position, root canal retreatment, baseline PAI, or baseline deepest PD (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Table 1). In sensitivity analyses assuming missing-not-at-random mechanisms, the treatment effect remained in favor of the water laser group when all incomplete follow-up outcomes were classified as composite failures (RR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.09 to 1.40; RD = 11.70 percentage points, 95% CI: 4.96 to 18.44). Under the extreme worst-case scenario for the water laser group, with all missing outcomes classified as failures in the water laser group and successes in the control group, the effect estimate was reversed (RR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.75 to 0.91; RD = −12.80 percentage points, 95% CI: −19.19 to −6.42) (Supplementary Table 2). These results indicate that the primary findings were not driven by measured predictors of follow-up completion and remained robust under a conservative equal-failure assumption, although bias from extreme differential missingness cannot be excluded.

Discussion

Water laser-assisted root canal treatment combined with supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing showed a higher pulp–periodontal composite success rate at approximately 12 months of follow-up. Additionally, radiographic findings revealed a higher proportion of improvement in the PAI scores and less worsening in the water laser group, suggesting more favorable periapical healing. Clinically, improvement in the composite endpoint indicates that the same affected tooth achieved both control of periapical lesions and stabilization of periodontal conditions, which is important for long-term tooth preservation and for reducing the likelihood of needing retreatment. 16 Under water laser activation, the root canal irrigant may generate stronger hydrodynamic and cavitation effects, thereby improving smear layer removal, biofilm disruption, and debridement in complex root canal systems. 17 When this approach is combined with supragingival scaling and subgingival root planing, infection control may be achieved from both pulp side and periodontal side, reducing bacterial exchange between periodontal pockets and the apical region. 18 This dual infection control provides a plausible explanation for the consistency between the higher composite success rate and the more favorable radiographic healing pattern. Previous in vitro studies on water laser treatment have mainly focused on intracanal bacterial reduction and smear layer removal, whereas smaller clinical studies have reported potential benefits for apical healing but have often evaluated only single endodontic outcomes. 19 Similarly, studies on conventional root canal treatment combined with periodontal therapy have commonly used PD or radiographic changes as separate endpoints, with limited use of strictly defined pulp–periodontal composite indices. 20 In this context, the present study adds real-world evidence by evaluating pulp-side and periodontal-side healing simultaneously after adjustment for measured baseline differences.

The periodontal and short-term postoperative findings further support the potential clinical value of this combined approach. After overlap weighting, the water laser group demonstrated greater improvement in deepest PD and clinical attachment level, higher BOP-negative rate, and lower incidence of acute exacerbation within 7 days postoperatively. Bacteria and their by-products in the root canal system may continue to stimulate periodontal tissues through the apical region and lateral canals. 21 Therefore, improving intracanal debridement and reducing plaque, calculus, and anaerobic biofilm within periodontal pockets may lower the local inflammatory burden more effectively than periodontal debridement alone.22–24 The reduction in acute exacerbation may also reflect better early control of residual irritation and inflammatory exudation after treatment. Previous studies of combined endodontic–periodontal treatment have mainly emphasized changes in PD and clinical attachment level, whereas studies on laser-assisted irrigation have focused more on postoperative pain or analgesic use rather than clearly defined acute exacerbation events.25–28 Overall, these findings suggest that water laser-assisted root canal treatment, when combined with conventional periodontal therapy, contributes not only to improved periodontal healing but also to a more favorable early postoperative course, supporting its potential role as a comprehensive adjunctive strategy for managing combined endodontic–periodontal lesions.

Certain study limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, treatment allocation was not randomized; although overlap weighting balanced measured baseline characteristics, residual confounding related to treatment selection cannot be ruled out. Unmeasured factors such as operator preference, subtle differences in clinical judgment, patient willingness to accept or pay for adjunctive laser treatment, adherence to home oral hygiene, maintenance attendance, and systemic medications not captured by the exclusion criteria may have influenced the outcomes. Future prospective studies should use randomized allocation or at least prospectively collect detailed information on patient preference, operator factors, adherence, maintenance care, and concomitant medications. Second, the follow-up window of 12 ± 3 months reflects early to mid-term healing rather than definitive long-term resolution. Complete radiographic healing of endodontic–periodontal lesions, particularly in teeth with baseline PAI scores of 4–5 or extensive periapical bone defects, may require 2–4 years; therefore, the present PAI-based composite success rate may overestimate durable healing in some severe cases. Longer follow-up studies with serial radiographic assessments are needed to determine the stability of healing, risk of retreatment requirement, and long-term tooth retention. Third, although laser treatment followed a standardized institutional protocol and protocol adherence was checked through retrospective records, the retrospective design did not allow real-time monitoring of each activation cycle. Future trials should prospectively record device settings, activation time, fiber tip use, and procedural deviations for each case. Fourth, radiographs were acquired using a standardized parallel technique and assessed by masked evaluators; however, minor changes in image acquisition or display conditions over the 4-year study period cannot be completely ruled out. Future multicenter studies should standardize radiographic equipment, calibration procedures, and image review protocols. Finally, because this was a single-center study conducted within a specific clinical workflow and equipment setting, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Multicenter prospective studies involving different operators, devices, and patient populations are needed to confirm the clinical value and safety of water laser-assisted therapy in broader clinical settings.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective controlled study, adjunctive water laser therapy was associated with a higher short- to mid-term pulp–periodontal composite success rate in teeth with concomitant periodontal involvement. The treatment was also associated with more favorable radiographic healing, improved periodontal parameters, and fewer early postoperative acute exacerbations, without an observed increase in adverse events under standardized clinical conditions. These findings suggest that water laser therapy serves as a useful adjunct to conventional root canal treatment combined with periodontal debridement, particularly in teeth with deep periodontal pockets. Prospective multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed for confirmation of these findings and accurate identification of patients and lesions most likely to benefit.
