IL-2: a promising topical treatment for Sjögren’s disease-related dry eye disease
Authors: Bo Huang, Shiyu He, Gong Cheng, Kai Zhang, Junru He, Jing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDry eye disease (DED) is one of the most common features of Sjögren’s Disease (SjD) and lacks effective disease-modifying topical treatment. Previous studies have demonstrated that low-dose interleukin-2 (Ld-IL-2) exerts therapeutic effects in several autoimmune diseases by expanding regulatory T cells (Tregs) and restoring immune homeostasis. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of Ld-IL-2 in SjD-related DED.MethodsWe administered recombinant murine IL-2 (rMuIL-2) to the ocular surface of NOD/ShiLtJGpt mice. The function of the lacrimal gland was estimated using the tear volume. Histological analysis was performed to assess corneal integrity and detect lymphocyte infiltration in the lacrimal gland. The levels of cytokines were estimated by RT-qPCR in the lacrimal functional unit (LFU) and by ELISA in serum.ResultsLd-IL-2 treatment increased tear secretion by more than 50% compared with PBS controls (P = 0.0151), showing efficacy comparable to cyclosporine. Lacrimal gland inflammation was reduced, accompanied by a more than 5-fold increase in interleukin (IL)-10 (P = 0.0147) and significant suppression of interleukin (IL)-1β (P = 0.0011) and TNF(tumor necrosis factor)-α (P = 0.0416). Corneal epithelial thickness decreased significantly (P = 0.0136), with concomitant increases in IL-10 and approximately 30% reductions in IL-1β and TNF-α expression (all P < 0.05). Serum IL-17 levels showed a decreasing trend.ConclusionLd-IL-2 alleviated inflammation within the lacrimal functional unit and improved both lacrimal gland function and corneal epithelial integrity. These findings suggest that topical Ld-IL-2 may represent a potential immunomodulatory therapeutic strategy for SjD-related dry eye disease.Keywords: Sjögren’s Disease, Dry eye disease, Low-dose IL-2, Lacrimal functional unit, Inflammation

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED), also known as Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca (KCS), is a hallmark manifestation of Sjögren’s Disease (SjD), a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by chronic lymphocytic infiltration and destruction of exocrine glands. Notably, approximately 95% of SjD patients experience dry eye, which significantly impairs quality of life [1]. Lacrimal gland dysfunction leads not only to ocular discomfort but also to epithelial injury, visual impairment, and, in severe cases, even sight-threatening corneal complications [2].

The mechanism underlying dry eye involves a complex disruption of the immune-nerve-gland-tear network [1, 3]. As the principal exocrine target in SjD, the lacrimal functional unit (LFU)--comprising the lacrimal glands, ocular surface, eyelids, and their interconnecting sensory and motor nerves–undergoes progressive immune-mediated damage. This process involves the activation and infiltration of autoreactive T and B cells, leading to sustained local inflammation [4, 5]. Given that regulatory T cells (Tregs) are indispensable for the maintenance of immune homeostasis, Treg dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis of SjD [6, 7]. In addition, inflammatory cytokine dysregulation plays a central role in both lacrimal gland hypofunction and ocular surface damage [3, 8]. Consistently, abnormal expression of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines has been observed in tears and conjunctival cells of patients with DED [9–13].

Current treatments primarily focus on symptomatic management by behavioral changes, artificial tears, and conventional anti-inflammatory agents such as cyclosporine [14]. However, these treatments often provide incomplete relief and fail to address the underlying immune dysregulation. Furthermore, long-term use of anti-inflammatory agents can lead to discomfort and an increased risk of eye infection [15]. Therefore, there is an unmet need for novel therapies that strike an improved balance between efficacy and safety.

Low-dose IL-2 (Ld-IL-2) has emerged as an effective and safe therapy for many autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and SjD [16–18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Ld-IL2 preferentially expands regulatory T cells and promotes immune tolerance without broadly activating effector T cells. Mechanistically, Ld-IL-2 has been shown to restore the balance of T follicular helper cell (Tfh)/Treg, T helper cell 17 (Th17)/Treg, and T follicular regulatory cell(Tfr)/Tfh, and ameliorates the impairment of Treg function [19–22]. Despite these systemic insights, its potential to reshape the local immune microenvironment of the ocular surface remains unclear.

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of Ld-IL-2 in the topical treatment of Sjögren’s Disease-Related Dry Eye Disease and illustrate the immune regulation of Ld-IL-2 in the LFU.

Method

Experimental animals

Eight-week-old non-obese diabetic/ShiLtJGpt (NOD/ShiLtJGpt) female mice were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). NOD/Ltj mice are a well-established model for SjD that recapitulates key exocrinopathy and immunological features of the human disease. 10 mice were allocated to each experimental group. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Protection and Use Committee and the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital.

Low-dose recombinant murine interleukin-2 (rMuIL-2) treatment

After one week of acclimatization, NOD/ShiLtJGpt mice received topical ocular administration of recombinant murine IL-2 (rMuIL-2) at a dose of 20 ng/g, 0.05% cyclosporine (100 µl), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). rMuIL-2 was purchased from Shanghai PrimeMagene BioTech Co., Ltd. During the experiment, purified IL-2 was dissolved in sterile water at a concentration of 40 ng/µL and the solution was freshly prepared before use. 10 µL of IL-2 solution was instilled to both eyes and overlaid on the ocular surface [23–26]. The mice were held by the operator for 15 s following drop administration, in order to allow for better absorption [23]. The treatment was administered 3 times daily for a total of 14 days. Twelve hours after the last administration, tear secretion was quantified using a phenol red thread test with the mice euthanized. Subsequently, serum, bilateral lacrimal glands, and corneas were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Histological analysis

After euthanasia, the lacrimal glands and corneas were harvested and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding. All mice were euthanized via orbital blood collection under anesthesia with 5% isoflurane. Paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned into 2 μm thick slices and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for 3 min. Sections were observed, and images were captured using an Olympus BX53 microscope (Tokyo, Japan). ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to calculate the focal areas of lymphocyte infiltration (defined as ≥ 50 lymphocytes within a 4 mm² tissue area) in each lacrimal gland section. The ratio of the lymphocyte infiltration area to the total area in the lacrimal glands, as well as corneal thickness, was ultimately calculated for evaluation.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from freshly explanted lacrimal glands and cornea tissue using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells/tissues were homogenized in Trizol, followed by chloroform-induced phase separation. RNA was precipitated from the aqueous phase with isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol, and dissolved in nuclease-free water. cDNA was synthesized using 1 µg of total RNA, RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen Life Technologies). The cDNA was quantified by spectrophotometry. PCR products were size separated by electrophoresis using a 0.9% agarose gel and visualized with ethidium bromide staining. qPCR was performed in triplicate using a SYBR Green-based master mix on a quantitative PCR system. The thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 60 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 2 min. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene β-actin and analyzed using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The primer sequences used are as follows:

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum was isolated from peripheral blood samples of NOD/ShiLtJGpt mice after centrifugation at 2,400 rpm for 20 min using a centrifuge 5810R (Eppendorf, Germany). Concentrations of interleukin-17 (IL-17) in serum were determined using a commercial mouse IL-17 ELISA kit (MultiSciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.00 software (California, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between the two treatment groups and the PBS control group were analyzed using independent samples t-test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Low-dose IL-2 improves lacrimal gland function and reduces glandular inflammation

Topical administration of low-dose IL-2 significantly improved lacrimal gland function in NOD mice. Tear secretion increased by more than 50% in the IL-2-treated group compared with PBS controls (P = 0.0151), demonstrating a therapeutic effect comparable to that of cyclosporine. Histological examination demonstrated reduced lymphocytic infiltration in the lacrimal glands, accompanied by a trend toward lower pathological scores (P = 0.1497). (Fig. 1A, B, C).

IL-2 treatment increased IL-10 expression (P = 0.0147) by more than fivefold while significantly reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (P = 0.0147), including IL-1β (P = 0.0011) and TNF-α (P = 0.0416). The anti-inflammatory effect of IL-2 was more pronounced than that of cyclosporine, suggesting that IL-2 may be more effective in restoring local glandular immune balance. (Fig. 1D, E, F).

Low-dose IL-2 restores corneal epithelial integrity

IL-2 treatment exerted protective effects on corneal structure. Corneal epithelial thickness was significantly reduced in the IL-2–treated group compared with PBS(P = 0.0136), indicating attenuation of epithelial edema and structural injury. This effect was comparable to that observed with cyclosporine treatment. (Fig. 2A).

Consistently, IL-2 significantly increased IL-10 expression in the cornea (P = 0.014) accompanied by a reduction of approximately 30% in the levels of IL-1β (P = 0.0356) and TNF-α (P = 0.046). These findings suggest that IL-2 is associated with reduced ocular surface inflammation and improved epithelial integrity. (Fig. 2B, C, D).

Low-dose IL-2 re-establishes immune homeostasis at the ocular surface

In both lacrimal gland and corneal tissues, IL-2 treatment consistently shifted the local cytokine profile toward an anti-inflammatory pattern, characterized by increased IL-10 and decreased IL-1β and TNF-α levels.

The cytokine profile suggests enhanced regulatory T-cell–associated pathways and reduced pro-inflammatory signaling. This coordinated modulation of inflammatory mediators supports the role of IL-2 in re-establishing ocular immune tolerance in Sjögren’s disease-related dry eye.

Topical IL-2 exhibits mild systemic immunomodulatory activity

Although administered locally, IL-2 was associated with modest systemic immunological changes. Serum cytokine analysis demonstrated a reduction in circulating IL-17 levels (P = 0.2182) following IL-2 treatment, indicating partial suppression of systemic Th17-mediated inflammation. (Fig. 3A) These findings suggest that topical IL-2 may not only modulate the local ocular microenvironment but also exert limited systemic immunoregulatory effects, however, the biological significance of this observation remains to be clarified.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that topical Ld-IL-2 ameliorates sicca manifestations and modulates local immune responses in a murine model of Sjögren’s disease-related DED. We observed improvements in lacrimal gland function, corneal epithelial integrity, and local inflammatory profiles, suggesting that Ld-IL-2 is associated with restoration of immune homeostasis within the LFU. These findings support the potential of Ld-IL-2 as a local immunomodulatory strategy; however, further studies are required to determine its translational relevance in human disease.

The immunomodulatory effect of Ld-IL-2 has been evaluated in some autoimmune diseases [27]. Treg cells exhibit heightened sensitivity to low concentrations of IL-2 due to their high expression of the α-chain (CD25) of the IL-2 receptor. This characteristic enables low-dose IL-2 to preferentially activate and expand Treg cells, thereby enhancing their immunosuppressive function and restoring immune homeostasis [28]. In the context of Sjögren’s disease (SjD), a reduction in Treg number or function is implicated in the loss of self-tolerance and the hyperactivation of autoreactive lymphocytes, driving disease progression [4].

The pathogenesis of DED has recently been described as a vicious cycle of tear film instability and hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities [29]. Furthermore, the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-II molecules, including human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR, on conjunctival epithelial cells enables these cells to present self-antigens directly to CD4⁺ T cells, potentially initiating local autoimmune inflammation and contributing to the pathogenesis of Sjögren’s disease [30, 31].

Topical administration of Ld-IL-2 tended to directly target the ocular immune imbalance in SjD-related eye disease. Upon local IL-2 application, Tregs in the cornea and lacrimal gland are activated and proliferate. Previous studies have demonstrated that local increase in Treg population resulted in the reduction of ocular inflammation and tissue injury [32, 33]. Mechanistically, IL-2 reverses the defect of Treg function by elevating the expression of Foxp3, CD25 and Bcl-2 in Treg and promoting production of anti-inflammatory factors, including IL-10 [34]. The subsequent activation of Tregs may inhibit pro-inflammatory T-helper 1 (Th1) and Th17 cell responses, leading to measurable improvement in corneal epithelial disease [35–37].

The therapeutic effect is further amplified through cytokine modulation. Tregs may enhance the immunosuppressive role through the increased secretion of IL-10 and decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine production of IL-1β and TNF-α [13]. IL-10 induces the secretion and activation of TGF-β, suppresses Tfh function, and regulates corneal lymphangiogenesis [38–40]. Conversely, pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β stimulates the production of multiple inflammatory factors and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), activates signal transduction pathways, and inhibits neurotransmitter release, ultimately resulting in the impairment of the corneal barrier function and lacrimal secretion [41, 42]. TNF-α is a crucial driver of ocular surface inflammation, and TNF-α blockage potentially reduces inflammation in DED [43, 44]. The down-regulation of inflammation by Ld-IL-2 seemed to block the vicious cycle of tear film instability–hyperosmolarity–inflammation [45].

Beyond adaptive immunity, Ld-IL-2 may benefit other aspects of ocular surface health. IL-2 signaling is essential for corneal epithelial barrier function, and CD25 on ocular surface epithelium decreases in DED. IL-2 possibly reverses this impact and enhances the expression of FasL on epithelium, maintaining the immune tolerance of the ocular surface [46]. Moreover, corneal neuropathy contributes to the development of DED [29, 47]. IL-2 potentially reverses neural abnormality in DED by sustaining neuron survival, promoting neuronal growth and synaptic remodeling, suggesting a potential additional pathway for ameliorating neurosensory abnormalities [48–51].

Compared with cyclosporine, a trend toward greater tear production was observed in the cyclosporine group, although the difference was not statistically significant. This observation may reflect differences in mechanisms of action, as cyclosporine exerts broad immunosuppressive effects by inhibiting calcineurin signaling, thereby reducing cytokine production [52, 53]. In contrast, Ld-IL-2 primarily acts by restoring immune regulation rather than broadly suppressing immune activation, which may result in a more balanced but potentially less rapid anti-inflammatory effect. In additionally, the stability and ocular bioavailability of IL-2 following topical administration may be limited, potentially affecting its therapeutic efficacy [54, 55]. Optimization of delivery systems may therefore enhance the clinical potential of this approach.

Compared with traditional immunomodulators such as corticosteroids and cyclosporine, topical Ld-IL-2 may be associated with a lower risk of ocular infection, elevated intraocular pressure, and cataract, thus potentially offering a favorable safety profile [56]. By directly restoring immune regulation, low-dose IL-2 could probably lead to a more rapid onset of therapeutic effects and sustained treatment responses.

There are several limitations in this study. First, this study was conducted in a murine model, and the findings may not fully reflect human disease. Second, direct assessment of immune cell subsets, including Treg populations within ocular tissues, was not performed. Third, the long-term efficacy and safety of topical Ld-IL-2 remain to be determined. In addition, heterogeneity across DED subtypes may influence treatment response, and further studies are needed to identify optimal patient populations.

In summary, topical Ld-IL-2 represents immunomodulatory effects in the lacrimal functional unit and is associated with improvement in glandular function and ocular surface integrity in SjD-related DED. By targeting local immune dysregulation, Ld-IL-2 represents a promising therapeutic approach; however, its clinical applicability requires further validation in well-designed translational and clinical studies.
