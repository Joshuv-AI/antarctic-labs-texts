Investigating synergistic effects of key medium components and pectin elicitor on taxane fermentation in Neopestalotiopsis vitis: A Box–Behnken response surface methodology approach
Authors: Hamzeh Rezazadeh, Sepideh Mirzaei Rad, Mojdeh Amandadi, Faezeh Ghanati, Saman Hosseinkhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaxanes are high-value diterpenoid compounds with potent anticancer activity, notably used in the treatment of breast and ovarian cancers. The newly isolated Iranian endophytic fungus Neopestalotiopsis vitis (N. vitis), recovered from native hazelnut (Corylus avellana) tissues, has demonstrated the ability to biosynthesize paclitaxel. However, maximizing its biotechnological potential requires a well-optimized culture system that accounts for synergistic interactions between nutritional factors and elicitor treatments. In this study, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) employing a Box–Behnken Design (BBD) was used to systematically investigate the individual and synergistic effects of four parameters—pH (4.0–8.0), malt extract (1.0–5.0 g L⁻¹), (NH₄)₂SO₄ (1.25–5.0 mM), and hazelnut-derived pectin (50–150 mg flask− 1)—on fungal biomass accumulation and total taxane fermentation. Model analysis confirmed pH 6.0 as the major factor with a significant linear effect on both responses, enhancing biomass and total taxane yield by approximately ⁓1.4-fold and 80%, compared to pH 4.0 and pH 8.0, respectively. Among binary interactions, malt extract and (NH₄)₂SO₄ exerted the strongest synergistic impact on the biomass rate (F = 44.39), resulting in an increase of ⁓27.0 g compared to other combinations. In contrast, the synergistic interplay between (NH₄)₂SO₄ and pectin most profoundly enhanced the total taxane yield (F = 67.98), with an improvement exceeding 1200 µg gFW⁻¹ (fresh weight). Collectively, these findings not only establish an optimized framework for scalable taxane fermentation from N. vitis, but also reveal how precisely coordinated synergies among carbon, nitrogen, and a host-derived elicitor orchestrate fungal secondary metabolism.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10123-026-00844-x.Keywords: Biomass, Fermentation optimization, Neopestalotiopsis vitis, Response surface methodology, Synergistic interactions, Taxane

Introduction

Taxanes constitute a highly valued class of diterpenoid natural compounds, best known for their unique ability to stabilize microtubules and arrest cell division, a mechanism that has cemented their role as frontline chemotherapeutic agents (Xu et al. 2023). The journey of taxanes in modern medicine began with paclitaxel, originally isolated from the bark of the Pacific yew (Taxus brevifolia). Its introduction marked a turning point in oncology, and it remains indispensable in managing several major cancers, e.g., the breast (Lai et al. 2022), ovary (Wu et al. 2022), and lung (Zhang et al. 2023b). Despite their therapeutic value, taxanes are extremely scarce in the wild. Their native source, yew tree, grows slowly and is frequently ecologically vulnerable, leading to mounting environmental and logistical challenges in meeting clinical demand. This scarcity has driven sustained efforts to develop sustainable alternative sources, including plant cell culture (Tomilova et al. 2023), semi-synthesis method (Zhang et al. 2023a) from more abundant precursors, and, more recently, microbial biosynthesis—particularly by endophytic fungi capable of taxane production (Walls et al. 2022). Given their clinically indispensable therapeutic value and growing global demand, the development of efficient, environmentally sustainable bioproduction platforms for taxanes represents not only a scientific imperative but also a pressing public health necessity.

In this context, microbial systems, particularly endophytic fungi (Zhong et al. 2024) and selected bacteria (Zhang et al. 2025), have emerged as promising alternative platforms for the taxane biosynthesis. These microorganisms, first isolated from Taxus tissues (Stierle et al. 1993), can biosynthesize taxanes or their key derivatives under fermentative conditions, offering a renewable, scalable, and environmentally benign production route that circumvents the ecological and logistical constraints of other methods. Among microbial producers, high-yielding strains are especially valuable, as they not only enhance titers and reduce production costs but also serve as a genetic and metabolic framework for further optimization through strain engineering or pathway refactoring. Discovering and developing such elite microbial strains is therefore a strategic priority. It may facilitate future transition from laboratory-scale studies to industrial applications, while also potentially unlocking new biochemical diversity within the taxane family, which could lead to novel analogs with improved pharmacological properties.

To harness the full potential of these microbial cell factories, various strategies have been explored to enhance taxane yields. Genetic and metabolic engineering approaches—including overexpression of rate-limiting biosynthetic genes, knockout of competing pathways, or heterologous expression of entire taxane gene clusters in sustainable hosts, offer powerful tools (Perez-Matas et al. 2023b). However, engineering these microbes often faces significant obstacles, including genetic instability, complex regulatory networks, limited knowledge of pathway enzymes in non-model organisms, and the high cost and technical demands of genetic manipulation—particularly in slow-growing or genetically recalcitrant endophytic fungi (Yang et al. 2024). Consequently, non-genetic approaches have gained considerable attention. Nutritional and elicitation-based strategies, including the addition of biotic or abiotic elicitors (e.g., methyl jasmonate, salicylic acid, or heavy metal ions)(Chen et al. 2021; Demidova et al. 2023), precursor feeding (e.g., phenylalanine)(Jamali et al. 2025), and tailored culture media (Obiedallah et al. 2024), can effectively trigger or amplify secondary metabolism. These methods not only bypass the technical and regulatory complexities of genetic engineering but also leverage the organism’s innate metabolic plasticity, often yielding rapid, reproducible, and economically viable improvements in taxane production, making them highly attractive for industrial-scale bioprocess development.

Among these, culture medium optimization stands out as a pivotal lever for maximizing taxane biosynthesis, as nutrient availability directly governs microbial growth, metabolic flux, and expression of biosynthetic gene clusters (Obiedallah et al. 2024). Historically, the one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach, wherein a single variable (e.g., temperature, pH, carbon source, and nutrient elements) is altered while other parameters remain fixed, has been widely used due to its simplicity (Polley and Ghosh 2021; Karamchandani et al. 2024). Nevertheless, OFAT suffers from critical limitations such as ignoring interactions between parameters, often missing the true global optimum, and becoming inefficient when applied to multicomponent systems. In complex processes, including secondary metabolite biosynthesis, where the balance of parameters, their synergy, and redox conditions are tightly interlinked, OFAT may deliver suboptimal or even misleading outcomes, thereby constraining the biotechnological potential of microbial strains.

To overcome these drawbacks, multivariate optimization strategies have been increasingly adopted. In particular, RSM has emerged as a powerful statistical framework for the systematic refinement of bioprocesses (Riswanto et al. 2019). By simultaneously evaluating multiple variables and their interactions, RSM constructs models that map the response surface and pinpoint optimal culture conditions with minimal experimental effort (Myers et al. 2004). This approach not only drastically reduces the number of required trials but also reveals synergistic or antagonistic effects that univariate methods cannot detect. Moreover, RSM provides a quantitative basis for assessing process robustness and enables fine-tuning around the optimum—attributes that are especially valuable when scaling microbial taxane production from the laboratory to industrial settings. Its efficiency, reliability, and balance between yield and resource use have firmly established RSM as a cornerstone of modern bioprocess optimization.

Given the urgent demand for a sustainable and high-yielding platform for taxane production, the endophytic fungus N. vitis has emerged as a promising candidate due to its notable capacity to biosynthesize taxanes. Because secondary metabolite fermentation in fungi is not governed by individual nutrients in isolation, but rather by a complex, synergistic interplay between key nutritional and elicitor factors, a multivariate optimization strategy is essential. To this end, RSM was employed as an optimization framework, enabling systematic identification and quantification of critical synergistic interactions that drive taxane biosynthesis. This approach allows researchers to navigate the multivariate response landscape and pinpoint the optimal combination of culture conditions, thereby unlocking the full biosynthetic potential of this Iranian strain. Ultimately, this work aims to provide a framework for developing sustainable and rationally designed taxane fermentation processes, potentially bridging ecological discovery with advanced bioprocess engineering, and laying the groundwork for future studies.

Materials & methods

Culture maintenance and stabilization of N. vitis MH243066.1

The fungal strain N. vitis MH243066.1 (Accession no. MW296847), originally isolated from Corylus avellana native to Iran, was initially cultivated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at pH 5.5 under dark conditions at 25 ± 2 °C (Mohammadi Ballakuti et al. 2022). To ensure culture viability and genetic stability, subculturing was carried out every 10 days. After several months of continuous propagation on the solid medium, the mycelial inoculum was transferred to potato dextrose broth (PDB) at the same pH to initiate liquid-phase cultivation. Cultures were maintained on a reciprocating shaker (110 rpm) at 25 ± 2 °C and subcultured weekly. Following 40 consecutive transfers, a stable fungal line exhibiting consistent growth and morphological traits was established for subsequent experiments.

Box–behnken design and response surface modeling of culture conditions

In the present study, the influence of key physicochemical parameters at three levels, namely pH (4.0, 6.0, and 8.0), malt extract (1.0, 3.0, and 5.0 g L⁻¹), (NH₄)₂SO₄ (1.25, 2.5, and 5.0 mM), and pectin concentration (50, 100, and 150 mg flask− 1), on the fungal biomass rate, and total taxane yield, was systematically investigated. The pectin concentration range was selected based on preliminary experiments that demonstrated measurable effects on both fungal growth and taxane biosynthesis within this interval.

A BBD was implemented using Design-Expert® software (v13.0.5.0; Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) to evaluate both the individual and interactive effects of these variables on fungal growth and taxane biosynthesis. The experimental design consisted of 29 randomized experimental runs generated based on a quadratic model, including 5 center point replicates. The center points were incorporated to allow estimation of pure experimental error and to assess the stability and adequacy of the fitted response surface model. Each experimental run corresponded to an independent culture prepared using separately adjusted media and inoculated independently. For each biological replicate, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analyses were performed in technical triplicate to ensure analytical precision and reproducibility (Table 1).

Assessment of mycelial biomass rate

At the end of the incubation period under the specified culture conditions, the fungal biomass was recovered by vacuum filtration through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The harvested mycelium was gently rinsed with distilled water to remove residual culture medium. Fresh biomass was immediately weighed to determine the mycelial fresh weight, and aliquots were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. These samples were subsequently used for taxane extraction and quantification by HPLC.

Isolation and assessment of taxanes from N. vitis mycelial cultures

The mycelial biomass was homogenized in CH₃OH (10:1, v/w) and incubated at 25 °C overnight (Jamshidi and Ghanati 2017). The mixture was then centrifuged (8000 × g, 20 min), and the supernatant was collected and evaporated to dryness under ambient conditions. The residue was partitioned with a dichloromethane–water mixture (1:2, v/v), and the organic layer was separated, dried, and reconstituted in 100 µL of methanol. The reconstituted extract was filtered through a 0.2-µm syringe filter and analyzed by HPLC.

Chromatographic separation was carried out on a C18 reversed-phase column (Perfectsil Target ODS-3, 5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm; MZ-Analysentechnik, Germany) using a Waters e2695 HPLC system equipped with a 2489 UV/Vis detector (Waters Corporation, USA). The mobile phase consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) methanol, delivered at 0.8 mL min⁻¹. A gradient program was applied as follows: 40% B increased linearly to 78% over 30 min, held at 78% for 10 min, and returned to 40% over 5 min (total run time: 45 min) (Bemani et al. 2013).

10-deacetylbaccatin III, baccatin III, 10-deacetyl paclitaxel, cephalomannine, 7-epi-10-deacetyl paclitaxel, paclitaxel, and 7-epi-paclitaxel were identified and quantified based on their retention times and peak areas relative to authentic external standards (ChromaDex, USA), analyzed under identical chromatographic conditions. Given the identical carbon skeleton common to all taxanes, and in order to avoid computational errors in the RSM software, the total taxane content, expressed as µg gFW⁻¹, was employed as a representative indicator of total taxane production. In addition, volumetric taxane productivity (mg L⁻¹) was calculated to enhance the bioprocess relevance of the study and to facilitate comparison with other reported taxane-producing systems (Table S1).

Isolation of pectin from C. avellana cells via sequential alkaline and chelator-based extraction

Fresh Corylus avellana cells were homogenized in C₂H₆O (95%, v/v) and filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper to remove soluble sugars and pigments. The resulting cell residue was defatted and delipidated by overnight incubation in a CH₃OH-CHCl₃ mixture (2:1, v/v), followed by filtration and air-drying to yield a crude cell wall preparation. The powder was then treated with acetone to remove residual lipids and pigments, filtered, and dried under ambient conditions.

Pectin was subsequently extracted from the purified cell wall material using sequential alkaline and chelating treatments: first with 20 mM ammonium oxalate at 70 °C for 2 h, followed by 0.1 M NaOH at room temperature for 1 h. The combined extracts were neutralized and dialyzed against distilled water for 48 h (using a 12–14 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane) to obtain purified pectin.

Determination of weight molecular weight of pectin by multi-detector size-exclusion chromatography

A pectin solution (2 mg mL⁻¹ in distilled water) was briefly heated in a microwave (30.0 s) to ensure complete dissolution and then filtered through a 0.22-µm cellulose membrane (Whatman International) to remove particulates. The molecular weight distribution was analyzed using high-performance size-exclusion chromatography (HELEOS; Wyatt Technology Corp., Santa Barbara, CA, USA) coupled with multi-angle laser light scattering and refractive index detection (HPSEC-MALS-RI).

Separation was performed on a TSK G5000PW column (7.5 × 600 mm; Tosoh Bioscience, Montgomeryville, PA, USA) using 0.15 M NaNO₃ (containing 0.02% (w/v) sodium azide) as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.4 mL min⁻¹. The weight-average molecular weight was determined from the MALS signal using manufacturer-provided data analysis software (ASTRA, Wyatt Technology).

Model validation and residual analysis of RSM for biomass rate and taxane yield

To evaluate the predictive accuracy and reliability of the quadratic models under controlled fermentation conditions for biomass accumulation and total taxane content by N. vitis, residual analysis was performed (Table 5).

The residuals, calculated as the difference between observed and predicted values, were generally low, indicating high predictive accuracy and a good fit of the models to the experimental data. For biomass rate, residuals ranged from − 0.40 to + 0.61, with most values distributed symmetrically around zero. Similarly, for total taxane content, residuals remained within a narrow range (− 0.107 to + 0.128), confirming the consistency of model predictions (Table 5).

Runs at the center point conditions (runs 1, 7, 8, 17, and 28) exhibited particularly small residuals and low leverage values (0.20), highlighting the models’ reliability in predicting responses near the design centroid. Across the dataset, leverage values for biomass ranged from 0.20 to 0.58, reflecting a well-balanced distribution of experimental information typical of a BBD. For total taxane content, leverage values were consistent, with no extreme or influential points detected (Table 5).

No outliers or influential observations were identified from residual and leverage diagnostics. Moreover, a strong correlation between observed and predicted values for both biomass and taxane content across all experimental runs further confirms the robustness of the models. Overall, the residual analysis demonstrates that the models are both statistically sound and biologically relevant, reliably predicting the effects of culture parameters on fungal biomass and taxane production (Table 5).

Single-factor effects of medium components on fungal biomass production and total taxane yield: a univariate analysis using boxplots

Single-factor boxplots were generated to provide an initial visual assessment of data distribution, illustrating the variability in both biomass accumulation rate and total taxane yield across distinct culture conditions (Fig. 1). Statistical analysis revealed that medium pH was the most influential factor governing fungal growth, underscoring its pivotal role in modulating the physiological response of N. vitis. The highest mean biomass manufacturing was observed at pH 8.0 up to > 20.0 g, whereas deviations toward lower pH values were associated with a marked decline in the growth rate (Fig. 1a).

Boxplot illustration of the univariate effects of culture parameters on the growth rate and total taxane content in N. vitis. (a–b) pH; (c–d) malt extract; (e–f) (NH₄)₂SO₄; and (g–h) pectin concentration

Figure 1b shows the total taxane content of N. vitis at three acidity levels of 4.0, 6.0, and 8.0. pH 6.0 significantly increased the taxane yield of the strain (approximately 650 µg gFW⁻¹), and deviation from this value towards led to a decrease in the total taxane yield (Fig. 1b).

Figure 1c illustrates the fungal growth rate in response to varying concentrations of malt extract (1.0, 3.0, and 5.0 g L⁻¹). The data indicated that the highest median biomass accumulation (an increase of more than 57% compared to other tested malt extract concentrations) was attained at malt extract 3.0 g L⁻¹. Similarly, the highest taxane yield (⁓750 µg gFW⁻¹) was observed under the same condition, malt extract 3.0% (w/v), as shown in Fig. 1d.

Figure 1e–f depicts the growth response and total taxane yield of N. vitis in supplemented media with different concentrations of (NH₄)₂SO₄ (1.25, 2.5, and 5.0 mM). The results indicated that the highest median biomass accumulation and the maximum taxane yield were achieved at (NH₄)₂SO₄ 2.5 mM, representing increases of more than 76% and 20% compared to other tested concentrations, respectively. Any deviation from this optimum, particularly an increase to 5.0 mM, led to a marked decline in the fungal growth and total taxane yield (Fig. 1e–f).

The effect of pectin on the growth rate and total taxane yield is illustrated in Fig. 1g–h. According to the results, increasing the amount of pectin up to 100 mg flask− 1 had a significant effect on the growth and taxane yield, with an increased biomass yield of ~ 24.0 g and a total taxane content of ~ 1000 µg gFW⁻¹ (Fig. 1g–h).

Two- and three-dimensional response surface modeling of synergistic interactions affecting on biomass accumulation

The resulting 2D and 3D response surface plots depict the synergistic effects of the independent variables on the fungal biomass accumulation under the applied fermentation conditions. These synergistic interactions highlight how combinations of medium components jointly influence the biomass rate, offering deeper insight into the strain’s metabolic response under varying culture conditions (Fig. 2).

(a–b) pH and malt extract; (c–d) pH and (NH₄)₂SO₄; (e–f) malt extract and (NH₄)₂SO₄; (g–h) malt extract and pectin; (i–j) (NH₄)₂SO₄ and pectin. All pairwise interactions were statistically significant (p < 0.05) based on ANOVA of the quadratic response surface model

As shown in Fig. 2a–b, the interaction between medium pH and malt extract exerted a significant influence on the biomass rate, with the highest response observed in the vicinity of pH 7.5 and a malt extract concentration of 4.0 g L⁻¹. Conversely, the lowest biomass rate (⁓ 5.0 g) was observed under highly acidic conditions (pH 4.0) in combination with 5.0 g L⁻¹ malt extract (Fig. 2a–b).

As depicted in Fig. 2c–d, the fungal growth maximized up to 27.0 g when the medium pH was adjusted to ⁓7.5 and (NH₄)₂SO₄ concentration was set to 3.75 mM. In contrast, the lowest growth production was observed under highly acidic conditions (pH 4.0, the lowest tested level) combined with the highest (NH₄)₂SO₄ concentration (5.0 mM) (Fig. 2c–d).

The combined effect of malt extract (3.0 g L⁻¹) and (NH₄)₂SO₄ (2.5 mM) significantly enhanced the growth rate of the fungus, yielding values exceeding 25.0 g. In contrast, suboptimal biomass accumulation was observed under imbalanced nutritional conditions—specifically, when the culture medium contained either high malt extract with low (NH₄)₂SO₄ (5.0 g L⁻¹ and 1.25 mM, respectively) or low malt extract with high (NH₄)₂SO₄ (1.0 g L⁻¹ and 5.0 mM, respectively)—indicating that synergistic interaction between the carbon source (malt extract) and nitrogen source ((NH₄)₂SO₄) is critical for optimal fungal growth (Fig. 2e–f).

Figure 2g–h demonstrates that a narrow optimal range of malt extract and pectin concentrations (malt extract 3.0 g L⁻¹ and pectin 100 mg flask− 1) significantly enhanced the growth of the studied N. vitis strain. Deviations from this range—toward both lower and higher concentrations of either component—resulted in a marked reduction in biomass accumulation (Fig. 2g–h).

The synergistic interaction between (NH₄)₂SO₄ and pectin also exerted a significant influence on the fungal growth rate, with a maximal biomass accumulation (~ 25.0 g) observed within a narrow optimal window—specifically at (NH₄)₂SO₄ 1.75 mM and pectin 100 mg flask− 1 (Fig. 2i–g).

Figure 3a–b illustrates the interactive effects of pH and malt extract concentration on the total taxane biosynthesis across the tested experimental range. As shown, increasing malt extract concentration up to 5.0 g L− 1 in combination with low pH markedly enhanced the total taxane yield, reaching values exceeding 1200 µg g FW⁻¹. In contrast, when acidity was raised to 8.0 (alkaline conditions), even with malt extract maintained at 5.0 g L− 1, a substantial decline in the total taxane yield was observed (Fig. 3a–b).

As shown in Fig. 3c–d, the combined effects of pH and (NH₄)₂SO₄ on the total taxane yield were evident within a relatively narrow optimal window. A distinct peak was observed at approximately pH 5.0 and (NH₄)₂SO₄ 1.75 mM, where the total taxane fermentation increased (Fig. 3c–d).

The culture medium containing the combined parameters of pH and pectin also showed a pronounced influence on the total taxane yield within a narrow optimal range, specifically at pH 5.5 and pectin 100 mg flask− 1. At higher pH levels, the total taxane content decreased sharply, reaching a minimum of approximately 200 µg gFW⁻¹ (Fig. 3e–f).

Malt extract and (NH₄)₂SO₄ produced a broad optimal region for the taxane accumulation, with the combination of malt extract 2.0 g L− 1 and (NH₄)₂SO₄ 1.75 mM yielding the highest total taxane production (Fig. 3g–h).

Figure 3i–j illustrates the significant effect of the malt extract and pectin on the total taxane yield of N. vitis. As shown, the synergistic combination of malt extract at approximately 3.0 g L− 1 and pectin 100 mg flask− 1 enhanced the taxane accumulation. Interestingly, low concentrations of either independent variable had little to no impact on total taxane biosynthesis; however, as the concentrations increased, their positive effects on the taxane yield became more evident (Fig. 3i–j).

As demonstrated in Fig. 3k–l, the combined application of (NH₄)₂SO₄ and pectin significantly influenced the taxane biosynthesis in N. vitis. Notably, the taxane accumulation was markedly enhanced over a broad yet experimentally accessible concentration range of both nitrogen source and elicitor. However, when the culture conditions extended beyond this optimal window—particularly at (NH₄)₂SO₄ 5.0 mM combined with pectin 150 mg flask⁻¹—a sharp decline in the total taxane yield was observed, with values decreasing to approximately 200 µg gFW⁻¹ (Fig. 3k–l).

Discussion

Endophytic fungi have long been heralded as promising microbial alternatives to the slow-growing and ecologically vulnerable yew trees (Taxus spp.), the native producers of taxanes (Sharma et al. 2024). Initial enthusiasm surrounding their potential for scalable and sustainable taxane biosynthesis was soon tempered by challenges, including genetic instability, low and variable yields, and the lack of optimized culture protocols (Stadler and Kolarik 2024). These limitations prompted researchers to explore other bioresources, such as taxane-producing bacteria (Li et al. 2023), non-Taxus plant species (e.g., Podocarpus elongatus) (Mohamed et al. 2024), and plant cell/tissue cultures (Adabi and Rezaei 2024). However, each alternative introduced its own constraints: bacterial systems typically exhibited minimal taxane titers and required stringent aseptic conditions; non-Taxus plants often suffered from slow growth and poor metabolite accumulation; and plant cell cultures remained economically unfeasible due to high operational costs and technical complexity.

In light of these drawbacks, attention has recently refocused on endophytic fungi, owing to their rapid growth, low cultivation costs, and relatively simple nutritional and environmental requirements. Central to unlocking their biotechnological potential is the rational design of culture media, not merely through the independent adjustment of individual components, but through the strategic orchestration of synergistic interactions between carbon, nitrogen, pH, and elicitor signals. As secondary metabolism of organisms is inherently governed by complex, interdependent networks, optimizing isolated factors in a one-factor-at-a-time manner is unlikely to yield maximal productivity. Instead, a multivariate approach that explicitly captures synergistic interactions is essential to simultaneously enhance both biomass accumulation and taxane biosynthesis. Indeed, this laboratory-scale optimization establishes a foundational framework that may inform future scale-up efforts and potentially contribute to the development of industrially viable taxane production processes using endophytic fungi.

Acidity is a fundamental physicochemical parameter that profoundly influences intracellular processes, including enzyme activity, membrane integrity, nutrient uptake, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Even minor deviations from the optimal pH range can disrupt cellular homeostasis and significantly alter metabolic flux, underscoring the critical need for precise pH control in microbial cultivation systems. Based on prior research, Azotobacter vinelandii cultivated under submerged fermentation conditions yielded the highest alginate production when incubated at pH 7.5 and 30 °C (Saeed et al. 2023). RSM optimization by Ismail et al. identified pH 6.0 as the optimal condition for maximizing the growth rate of a batch culture system comprising Sphingobacterium spiritivorum and Aspergillus brasiliensis (Ismail et al. 2023). Under optimized culture conditions (pH 9.0), Pantoea agglomerans CPHN2 achieved its highest yield of indole-3-acetic acid, demonstrating the strain’s ability to thrive and produce target metabolites under alkaline conditions (Kumar et al. 2022). Optimization of cultivation conditions for Anabaena sp. using RSM with a central composite design (CCD) revealed that the highest yield of strain was achieved at pH 7.0 and 45 °C (Doraj et al. 2023). Univariate optimization revealed that the yield of Lactobacillus spp. in soy milk was significantly enhanced when supplemented with 1% (w/v) glucose and adjusted to pH 5.0 (Rayavarapu et al. 2021). In a study by Sobhy et al., Aspergillus flavus cultivated on a modified culture medium (pH 6.0) yielded the highest reported paclitaxel yield under their experimental conditions (Abdel-Fatah et al. 2022). The highest paclitaxel titer, 195.4 µg L⁻¹, was achieved by Alternaria alternata F3 after 8 days of cultivation in a supplemented medium at pH 6.0, 28 °C, and 150 rpm. This represents a 2.12-fold enhancement over the baseline paclitaxel yield obtained under unoptimized conditions (Fu et al. 2024). Optimization of culture conditions using a full factorial design followed by RSM enhanced paclitaxel accumulation in Aspergillus fumigatus to 110.23 µg gDW− 1. The highest yield was achieved after 18 days of fermentation in PDB at an initial pH of 8.0 (Obiedallah et al. 2024). This findings highlights the critical influence of medium composition and pH on taxane biosynthesis in endophytic fungi. Our findings reveal a distinct pH-dependent dichotomy in the physiology of N. vitis: while optimal mycelial growth occurs under alkaline conditions, maximal taxane biosynthesis is achieved in a mildly acidic environment. This apparent decoupling of growth and secondary metabolism may be explained, at least in part, by a pH-mediated regulation of biosynthetic gene expression, whereby mildly acidic conditions enhance the transcription of key taxane-pathway genes (Walker et al. 2002a). In addition, it has been observed that taxane compounds, including paclitaxel, 10-deacetylbaccatin III, and baccatin III, exhibit greater chemical stability under acidic conditions, further favoring their accumulation (Tian and Stella 2010a). This observation is supported by Tian and Stella, who demonstrated that core taxane derivatives, including paclitaxel, 10-deacetylbaccatin III, and baccatin III, exhibit significantly enhanced stability under acidic conditions (Tian and Stella 2010b). Together, these observations position pH not merely as a cultivation parameter, but as a strategic switch that can be tuned to prioritize either biomass accumulation or taxane production in endophytic fungal systems.

Carbon source is a fundamental determinant of microbial metabolism, serving not only as the primary energy reservoir but also as the biochemical backbone for the biosynthesis of cellular macromolecules and secondary metabolites. The selection of an appropriate carbon source and its optimal concentration can profoundly influence both biomass accumulation and metabolic flux toward high-value metabolites, such as taxanes (Wang et al. 2021). In this context, optimizing carbon availability is not merely a matter of sustaining growth, but of strategically channeling cellular resources toward desired biosynthetic pathways. Previous studies demonstrated that an optimized medium formulation, comprising soluble starch (26.26 g L⁻¹), KNO₃ (1.0 g L⁻¹), and soybean cake powder (23.54 g L⁻¹), significantly enhanced the mycelial dry weight of Streptomyces alfalfae XN-04 compared to basal, non-optimized conditions. Based on the quadratic model derived from CCD, the optimal conditions for glutamate production by Corynebacterium glutamicum were identified as 30 °C, glucose 9 g dL⁻¹, and biotin 9 µg L⁻¹, yielding a predicted glutamate titer of 19.84 mg mL⁻¹ (Fahimitabar et al. 2021). In a study by Kumar et al., the highest yield of target metabolites was achieved using mannitol at a concentration of 10.23 g L⁻¹, as determined through OFAT optimization (Kumar et al. 2022). Streptomyces alfalfae XN-04 achieved a maximum dry biomass rate of 6.61 g L⁻¹ in an optimized medium containing 26.26 g L⁻¹ soluble starch as the primary carbon source (Chen et al. 2022). Yap et al. reported that Fusarium sp. achieved maximum metabolite production in an optimized medium containing glucose 0.20% (w/v), with titers increasing from 16.75 ± 0.76 to 22.42 ± 0.20 IU mL⁻¹—a 33.8% enhancement over baseline conditions (Yap et al. 2021). The results of this investigation indicate that although malt extract played a dominant role in shaping taxane production, the overall response emerged from a network of interacting nutritional and physicochemical factors. This pronounced nonlinearity underscores that taxane biosynthesis in N. vitis cannot be attributed to a single nutritional driver but rather arises from the coordinated balance among carbon availability, nitrogen supply, and elicitor signals. From a metabolic standpoint, moderate carbon availability likely supports sufficient energy generation and precursor supply without triggering catabolite repression, thereby enabling efficient resource allocation towards taxane biosynthesis. Conversely, carbon limitation may constrain biosynthetic flux, while excess carbon could impose osmotic or regulatory stress that suppresses the taxane pathway. Critically, the context-dependent nature of these responses suggests that N. vitis employs a growth-decoupled regulatory strategy, redirecting metabolic flux toward secondary metabolism under specific physicochemical constraints. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms governing this metabolic switch—including potential transcriptional regulators of the taxane pathway in fungi—remain to be elucidated in future studies.

Beyond fulfilling basic nutritional requirements, nitrogen feeding plays a pivotal role in modulating the metabolic equilibrium between primary and secondary metabolism in microbial systems. This regulatory influence is highly dependent on both the chemical form and concentration of the nitrogen source supplied. Studies reported that Rhodopseudomonas sp. achieved maximal biomass (1.28 g L⁻¹) and protein yield (0.42 g L⁻¹) when cultivated in a ternary nitrogen system combining urea (0.60%), (NH₄)₂SO₄ (0.68%), and sodium glutamate (0.81%), a formulation identified through CCD optimization (Liu et al. 2024). Similarly, in the microalga Chlorella sp., optimal productivity was attained not with a single nitrogen source, but through a balanced combination of NaNO₃ (101.5 mg L⁻¹), diluted seawater (23.8%), and glycerol (0.25 g L⁻¹), underscoring the importance of integrated nutrient design (Abomohra et al. 2022). The sensitivity of biomass production and metabolite output to nitrogen availability is further illustrated in Nannochloropsis oculata UTEX 2164, where a modest nitrate concentration (NO₃ 21.08 mg L⁻¹) boosted biomass production by ~ 25% compared to the control (576 vs. 460 mg L⁻¹) (Mehra and Jutur 2022). In fungal systems, nitrogen optimization follows a similar logic: Penicillium citrinum PKB20 exhibited peak performance with (NH₄)₂SO₄ 0.38 g L⁻¹ (Bardhan et al. 2021), while Arcopilus aureus maximized secondary metabolite manufacturing (112 µg mL⁻¹) at 0.5% peptone, a complex organic nitrogen source that likely supplies both amino acids and signaling peptides (Dwibedi et al. 2021). Remarkably, in Fusarium maire K178, a Plackett–Burman-guided redesign of the medium, featuring NH₄NO₃ 7.84 g L⁻¹, NaOAc 2.02 g L⁻¹, and MgSO₄ 0.68 g L⁻¹, elevated paclitaxel yield more than tenfold, from 20 to 225.2 µg L⁻¹ (Xu et al. 2006). Our findings suggest that the role of (NH₄)₂SO₄ in modulating biomass production and total taxane yield is inherently context-dependent, with its impact shaped by interactions with other medium components rather than by its absolute concentration alone. Such behavior reflects the integrated nature of microbial metabolic regulation, where nitrogen availability influences secondary metabolism through its interplay with carbon supply, pH, and elicitor signals. Within this dynamic network, shifts in nutrient balance can redirect metabolic flux between growth-associated processes and taxane biosynthetic pathways—a principle widely recognized in fungal secondary metabolism (Chen et al. 2023).

Elicitors are recognized as effective tools to modulate cellular metabolism and enhance the production of secondary metabolites in microbial and plant systems (Bhaskar et al. 2022). However, given the environmental and regulatory concerns associated with synthetic chemical elicitors, there is growing interest in natural elicitors—particularly those derived from plant or microbial biomass—that offer a sustainable, biocompatible, and equally potent alternative for triggering biosynthetic pathways without compromising ecological safety. In Plumbago europaea L. cell suspension cultures, chitosan elicitation at 150.0 mg L⁻¹ triggered a rapid and substantial enhancement in plumbagin biosynthesis, with the maximum yield (6.9-fold higher than the unelicited control) observed just 24 h post-treatment (Beigmohamadi et al. 2025). In cell suspension cultures of Celosia argentea, chitosan (5.0 mg L⁻¹), among the tested elicitors—namely, salicylic acid and methyl jasmonate emerged as the most effective biotic elicitor, significantly enhancing betalain production to 4.65 mg g⁻¹ dry weight—a 1.87-fold increase over the control (Mueangnak et al. 2025). Dehghan et al. (2025) demonstrated that autoclaved culture filtrates of Fusarium graminearum mycelia, an endophytic fungus originally isolated from Taxus baccata bark, acted as a potent elicitor, inducing the highest paclitaxel yield (9.44 µg g⁻¹ dry weight) in in vitro fungal cultures (Dehghan et al. 2025). In Taxus baccata cell suspensions, Pérez-Matas et al. (2023) applied salicylic acid (150 µM) on day 8 of culture and observed a significant but moderate increase in total taxane production, with peak accumulation occurring on day 16. Notably, salicylic acid was less effective than the bacterial elicitor coronatine, underscoring the superior potency of certain biotic signals in activating the taxane biosynthetic pathway (Perez-Matas et al. 2023a). In Corylus avellana L. cell suspension cultures, methyl jasmonate elicitation significantly enhanced taxane biosynthesis by activating the paclitaxel pathway, with the most pronounced effect observed at 200 µmol L⁻¹, a concentration that outperformed both lower (100 µmol L⁻¹) and higher doses (300 µmol L⁻¹) (Goktepe-Atilgan et al. 2023). Zhou et al. (2021) demonstrated that polysaccharides derived from Chrysanthemum indicum L. at a concentration of 2 mg mL⁻¹ acted as effective biotic elicitors, significantly enhancing biomass production, atractylenolides, and endogenous polysaccharides in Atractylodes macrocephala (Zhou et al. 2021). In Pueraria tuberosa cell cultures, the application of natural elicitors—such as yeast extract, pectin, and alginate—has been shown to significantly enhance biomass accumulation, antioxidant activity, and the production of bioactive secondary metabolites (Kanthaliya et al. 2023). Our findings suggest that pectin, a natural polysaccharide derived from the cell walls of Corylus avellana, may act as a potent biological elicitor influencing both fungal growth and taxane biosynthesis in the studied endophytic strain. As pectin and its oligosaccharide derivatives are recognized plant-derived damage-associated molecular patterns, their presence may mimic aspects of host–microbe signaling within this system (Harris et al. 2024). In plant systems, pectin fragments released during cell wall remodeling are known to activate intracellular signaling cascades that promote secondary metabolite production through transcriptional regulation of biosynthetic pathways (Claverie et al. 2018). By analogy, it is plausible that in an endophytic fungal context, pectin may be perceived via conserved recognition mechanisms, thereby triggering defense-like or stress-responsive pathways that indirectly enhance taxane biosynthesis. This host-mimetic elicitation strategy aligns with the ecological role of endophytes and represents a sustainable, biologically informed approach to stimulating the production of high-value secondary metabolites.

Conclusion

This study establishes N. vitis as a promising sustainable microbial platform for taxane biosynthesis, contingent upon the precise optimization of its culture conditions. Through a systematic application of RSM, we revealed that fungal growth and taxane biosynthesis in N. vitis are not driven by individual factors in isolation, but by.

significant interaction effects between pH, carbon source (malt extract), nitrogen supply ((NH₄)₂SO₄), and pectin. The interaction among carbon source, nitrogen source, and pectin proved critical for achieving the maximal biomass and taxane accumulation rate. The highest biomass and taxane titers were not attained at extreme concentrations, but within narrow, balanced windows, highlighting the sensitivity of N. vitis to C/N balance. In addition, most notably, the stimulatory effect of pectin likely stems from the activation of conserved defense-like pathways that thereby taxane biosynthesis—a finding that bridges ecological insight with bioprocess innovation.

Together, these results affirm that rational medium design, grounded in statistical optimization of interaction effects and informed by biological context, can overcome longstanding limitations in taxane production. This strategy, which aligns culture conditions with the organism’s intrinsic metabolic framework rather than imposing inflexible fermentation constraints, establishes a laboratory-scale foundation with potential for scalable taxane production.
