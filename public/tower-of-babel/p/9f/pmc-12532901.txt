Promising anti-cervical cancer efficacy and phytochemical analysis of Vitex doniana Sweet (Verbenaceae) leaf extracts: an in vitro investigation with molecular mechanisms of action
Authors: Gervason Moriasi, Mathew Ngugi, Peter Mwitari, George Omwenga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe exploration of natural sources for novel anticancer agents has garnered significant attention in recent years, driven by the need for effective and safe anticancer medications, considering the escalating global burden of cervical cancer. Vitex doniana Sweet (Verbenaceae), a plant with diverse traditional medicinal uses, especially in Africa, has shown promise in this regard due to its rich phytochemical composition.MethodsThis study investigated the cytotoxic and antiproliferative effects of V. doniana extracts on normal mammalian (Vero-CCL-81) and cervical cancer (HeLa) cells, including cancer-associated gene expression profiles, according to standard procedures. Phytochemical analysis was performed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS).ResultsThe findings revealed significant (P < 0.0001) concentration-dependent increases in cytotoxicity and inhibition of cancer cell proliferation, by the aqueous, methanolic, ethyl acetate, and dichloromethane leaf extracts of V. doniana. Notably, differential cytotoxicity profiles were observed among different extracts. The median cytotoxic concentrations (CC50) of the studied extracts were: 1025.12 µg/ml (Aqueous leaf extract), 10.67 µg/ml (methanolic leaf extract), 964.81 µg/ml (ethyl acetate extract), and 1238.85 µg/ml (dichloromethane leaf extract. Furthermore, high selectivity indices of the ethyl acetate (26.55) and dichloromethane (103.67) leaf extracts of V. doniana against HeLa cells were observed, underscoring their potential as targeted chemotherapeutic agents against cervical cancer. Mechanistic insights into the observed effects revealed significant modulation of key genes involved in cancer progression, including the androgen receptor (AR), B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2), caspase-3 (CASP3), cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1), and tumour protein 53 (TP53/P53). GC–MS analysis revealed 10 bioactive compounds in the dichloromethane leaf extract of V. doniana, with γ-sitosterol and stigmasta-3,5-dien-7-one being the most abundant. Besides, the ethyl acetate leaf extract showed presence of 27 phytochemicals, whereby pentatriacontane and tetratetracontane were more common while α-calacorene and spiro (2.5) octane 5,5-dimethyl-4-(3-oxobutyl)- were less abundant.ConclusionThese findings provide valuable insights into the potential and molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-cervical cancer effects of V. doniana extracts, their attributable phytocompounds, and highlight their putative value as sources of lead compounds for the development of novel anticancer drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-04923-w.Keywords: MTT assay, Median Cytotoxic Concentrations (CC50), HeLa cell line, Ethnopharmacology

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most prevalent cancer in women, with approximately 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths recorded each year [1], and a formidable challenge to public health systems worldwide [2]. Despite advancements in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic interventions, cervical cancer continues to exert a heavy toll, particularly in situations where financial and healthcare resources are scarce, such as in Sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for approximately 85% of global cervical cancer cases [3]. In Kenya alone, recent statistics paint a concerning picture, with cervical cancer ranking as the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, underscoring the urgent need for effective interventions [4].

Current treatment modalities for cervical cancer primarily revolve around surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, often used in combination depending on the stage and extent of the disease (National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), 2022). While these approaches have demonstrated efficacy in controlling tumour progression and improving survival outcomes, they are not without limitations. Surgical interventions can be invasive and may result in significant morbidity, while chemotherapy and radiotherapy are associated with various adverse effects, including systemic toxicity and damage to healthy tissues [5]. Moreover, access to these treatments is often constrained by factors such as cost, infrastructure, and geographical barriers, particularly in low-resource settings [6, 7]. The shortcomings of conventional therapies highlight the imperative need for alternative therapies which are not only effective but also safe, accessible, and affordable, particularly in regions with limited healthcare resources.

Plants have long been revered for their diverse pharmacological properties, with many traditional healing practices relying heavily on botanical remedies [8]. Notably, medicinal plants offer a rich source of bioactive substances including alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and polyphenols, which demonstrate many benefits, including anti-cancer effects [9]. Medicinal herbs and their compounds exert their anticancer effects via intricate pathways, including activation of apoptosis, reduction of cell proliferation, modification of cell cycle progression, and suppression of angiogenesis and metastasis [10]. Moreover, plant-derived compounds have been shown to possess synergistic effects when tandemly used with chemotherapy drugs, potentially enhancing their efficacy while mitigating their adverse effects [11, 12].

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting the anticancer properties of plant extracts, research in this field remains limited, particularly concerning the therapeutic potential of plant-derived products against cervical cancer. Furthermore, the translation of preclinical findings into clinically viable therapies necessitates rigorous scientific investigation, encompassing preclinical studies, clinical trials, and mechanistic studies to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms [13]. Addressing these knowledge gaps is crucial for advancing the development of plant-based therapies for cervical cancer and improving patient outcomes, particularly in regions where access to conventional treatments is limited.

Vitex doniana Sweet (Verbenaceae) holds significant ethnomedicinal value in Africa, where it is traditionally used to treat or manage various ailments including allergies, cervical uteri cancer, microbial infections such as chickenpox and gonorrhoea, inflammation, rheumatic pain, asthenia, dysentery, diarrhoea, abdominal complaints, wound management, nausea and vomiting, leprosy, blood glucose level modulation, anaemia, hemiplegia, jaundice, fever, and headache, among others [14, 15]. Previous scientific investigations have indicated that the aerial parts ofV. donianaexhibit notable antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and hepatocurative properties, lending credence to its traditional uses [14]. Chemical analysis has revealed the presence of various phytoconstituents inV. doniana, including alkaloids, terpenes, saponins, tannins, flavonoids, sterols, essential vitamins (A, B1, and C), and minerals such as potassium, iron, magnesium, calcium, and zinc [14, 16]. These bioactive compounds are believed to underpin the plant's pharmacological activities and contribute to its therapeutic efficacy.

However, despite the wealth of anecdotal evidence supporting the medicinal use of V. doniana, there is a dearth of empirical research to substantiate these claims and provide a foundation for further investigation. Furthermore, while V. donianais commonly employed in Kenyan traditional medicine for managing cervical cancer [17–19], its safety profile and specific anticancer properties were unexplored. The scarcity of scientific inquiry into its efficacy against cervical cancer necessitates a focused investigation to evaluate its potential as an adjunctive or alternative therapy. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the safety and efficacy of V. doniana against cervical cell line (HeLa), to pave way for further research aimed at deciphering leads for anticancer drug development.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The study plant was preliminarily identified in situ in an indigenous community forest, in Mbeere North Sub-County (0°35′24.0"S 37°36′50.9"E), Kenya, with the assistance of a renowned local herb doctor after the approval of Kenyatta University Ethics Review Committee (KUERC) and the National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation (NACOSTI) of Kenya. Fresh leaves were harvested sparingly during the dry season in the month of January 2022, packaged in woven sisal bags, and taken to our research laboratory at Kenyatta University for onward processing. Besides, voucher specimens were prepared as described by Carter et al. [20], and authenticated taxonomically by a competent botanist at the East Africa Herbarium, hosted at the National Museums of Kenya (NMK/BOT/CTX/1/2), where duplicate voucher specimens were deposited for future reference. The gathered leaves were spread out on a hardwood laboratory bench and allowed to naturally dry for 14 days away from sunlight, after which they were triturated using an electrical plant grinder and stored in labelled khaki envelopes.

Chemicals and reagents

Analytical grade reagents and chemicals were used in this study and were purchased from a local supplier. They included methanol (MeOH) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), Ethyl acetate (EA) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), dichloromethane (DCM) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), dimethyl sulphoxide (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), Foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), 2 mM L-glutamine and an antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl) −2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), total RNA miniprep extraction and purification kit (Solis BioDyne, Inqaba Biotec, South Africa), and Doxorubicin hydrochloride (Sigma Aldrich, Germany).

Extraction procedures

Aqueous extraction of the plant materials was done using the approach outlined by Harborne [21] and adapted by Moriasi et al. [22]. Briefly, 100 g of the pulverised material was macerated in distilled water (1 L) and heated indirectly at 60 °C in a water bath (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 2 h. The solution was cooled, filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter papers, and lyophilised in vacuo for 48 h using a Modulyo Edwards freeze-dryer (UK, Britain). The lyophilised extract was weighed, and its percentage yield was calculated according to equation 1 (Eq. 1) [23].

Besides, the previously described cold maceration procedure adopted by Moriasi et al. [22] was used to obtain the methanolic, ethyl acetate, and dichloromethane extracts. Briefly, 250 g the of powdered plant material was infused in 1000 ml of either methanol, ethyl acetate, or dichloromethane in well labelled 2500 ml conical flasks. The merc-menstruum mixtures were shaken occasionally and extracted for 48 h. Subsequently, the individual mixtures were filtered through Whatman filter paper No. 1 (Schleicher and Schuell Microscience, Germany) before being concentrated in vacuo using a rotary evaporator (Buchi, Switzerland), whose water bath temperature was set at 50 °C for the methanolic and ethyl acetate extracts and 30 °C for the dichloromethane extract, respectively. The resultant extracts were further dried completely at 30 °C in a hot-air oven (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) for five days, and their percentage yields computed according to equation 1 (Eq. 1) [23]. Every extract was kept at −4 °C and was only recovered when necessary for experiments.

Preparation of plant extracts

Each studied plant extract was carefully weighed (100 mg), dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide solution (10 ml), and vortexed. After that, 100 μl of each solubilised extract was aspirated, and its volume was made up to 1000 μl with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to achieve a stock concentration of 1000 μg/ml for this study. Afterwards, successful concentrations were prepared through serial dilution technique using a three-fold dilution factor with PBS as diluent. This gave rise to seven concentrations: namely, 1.37 μg/ml, 4.12 μg/ml, 12.35 μg/ml, 37.04 μg/ml, 111.11 μg/ml, 333.33 μg/ml, and 1000.00 μg/ml, for each studied plant extract. The reference drug (Doxorubicin) was prepared using the serial dilution technique as for the plant extracts to obtain seven concentrations: 0.04 μg/ml, 0.12 μg/ml, 0.37 μg/ml, 1.11 μg/ml, 3.33 μg/ml, 10 μg/ml, and 30 μg/ml, respectively.

Bioassay procedures

Cell culture and maintenance

The purity of experimental cell lines was assessed using a MycoStrip™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Invitrogen, USA), after which they were cultured and maintained according to the procedure outlined by Markossian et al. [24]. Vero CCL-81 cells were cultured under aseptic conditions in a 10% foetal bovine albumin-enriched Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine and an antibiotic–antimycotic solution (100 units/ml penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin). High glucose-Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) containing 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (100 units/ml penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin) was used to cultivate and maintain the cervical cancer cell line (HeLa-229). All cell lines were incubated under a humified atmosphere with 5% CO2at 37 °C for 48 h in a tissue culture incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The growth of cells was controlled thrice a week, after which they were trypsinised, passaged, and resuspended in their respective media following previously described protocols [24]. Cells attaining a confluence of ≥ 90% were carefully isolated, resuspended in fresh media, and used for subsequent assays.

The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl) −2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay

In vitro, cytotoxicity of the study plant extracts on the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) Vero CCL-81 (ATCC; CCL81) and cervical cancer HeLa-229 cells (ATCC CCL-2.1) was determined using the MTT assay method described previously [25] with some modifications [24]. In brief, 100 µl of the medium was aseptically transferred into 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), after which the corresponding cell lines were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. The seeded plates were incubated overnight at conditions described in Sect."Cell culture and maintenance"for the cells to attach. After attachment, various serial concentrations (0–1000 µg/ml) of the plant extracts (prepared in 0.1% Dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) and doxorubicin were added to respective wells in triplicate and incubated for 48 h in conditions described in Sect."Cell culture and maintenance". After that, 10 µl of freshly prepared MTT reagent (5 mg/ml) in phosphate-buffered saline was added, and the plates were kept in conditions highlighted in Sect."Cell culture and maintenance"for 4 h. The supernatant from each well was subsequently aspirated, replaced with 100 µl of freshly prepared DMSO (0.1%), and agitated. Absorbances of the formazan complex in each seeded well were measured spectrophotometrically at 570 nm with 720 nm as a reference wavelength to capture non-specific absorbance using a multiwell-microtitre reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The obtained absorbance values were used to calculate the percentage cytotoxicity in Vero CCL-81 cells and percentage inhibition of cancer cells (HeLa-229) according to the following equation (Eq. 2).

Determination of the expression profiles of cancer-associated genes in the HeLa-229 cells treated with the plant extracts

The expression profiles of various genes linked to the initiation and progression of cervical cancer were determined using the quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) technique. In brief, the HeLa-229 cells (1 × 106) were seeded in 96-multiwell plates and treated with the study plant extracts at concentrations corresponding to their IC50 and SI values (as described in Sects. "Cell culture and maintenance" and "The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl) −2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay") and incubated for 48 h. The cells' total RNA was extracted using the total RNA miniprep extraction and purification kit (Solis BioDyne). Then, the concentration and purity of the RNA samples were determined using a nanodrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). After that, the RNA samples (2 µg) were reverse transcribed to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) with a cDNA synthesis kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Solis BioDyne) (Ahmad et al. [27]). Afterwards, the cDNA samples were added SYBR Green dye, and the target genes (ar, Bcl2, cdk1, p53, and caspase 3) were amplified by qPCR in a QuantStudio™ 5 real-time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) using gene specific primers, whose sequences are provided in Table 1, in the following sequence: one cycle of thermal activation (95 °C for 12 min) followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C for 15 s), primer annealing (62 °C for 30 s), and strand elongation (72 °C for 30 s). The target genes'expression levels were normalised using the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene (gapdh) and β-actin (actb). The comparative/cycle threshold method (CT method) was used to analyse the gene expression data, and fold changes were determined using the relative quantification (2−ΔΔCt) approach [28].

Phytochemical analysis

Phytochemical analysis was done by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) [29], using analytical grade dichloromethane and ethyl acetate (Sigma-Aldrich). The calibration and comparison pure compounds for GC–MS, (regular standard reference compounds) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich. The leaf extracts of V. doniana from dichloromethane and ethyl acetate were dissolved using their own solvents to get a concentration of 1 mg/ml. The sample solutions were filtered with a 0.45 µm PTFE syringe filter, then injected into the GC–MS equipment. For this study, analysis was done using Shimadzu QP 2010-SE GC–MS (Shimadzu, Japan) with auto sampler connection. Ultrapure Helium (He) was used as the carrier gas at a linear velocity of 35 cm/s. A BPX5 nonpolar column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID; 0.25 μm film thickness), was used for separation. The equipment was programmed as follows: 60 ˚C; 10˚C/min to 200˚C (hold time1 min); 10 oC/min to 280 0C (10 min). The total runtime was 33 min. Injection temperature was set to 250 0C. The interface temperature was set at 250 0C. The EI ion source was set at 200 0C, working in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV. Mass analysis was done in Scan mode, with a scan range of m/z values of 35–550 a.m.u. Sample was injected in split mode with the split ratio set to 10:0. The steps to identify compounds included matching mass spectra against the NIST library and calculating retention indices with homologous series of n-alkanes (C8-C20) under same GC–MS conditions. For verification of the identified compounds, we compared the mass spectra and retention indices with standards from the National Library of Medicine’s PubChem (nih.gov), NIST Chemistry WebBook, and scholarly literature. The percentage of each compound (abundance) was determined using peak area in total ion chromatogram. Agilent ChemStation software was used for data analysis.

Data management, statistical analysis, and reporting

In vitro cytotoxicity and antiproliferative data were entered and organised in Microsoft Excel spreadsheets (Microsoft 365), cleaned, and then transferred to Graph Pad Prism version 10 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were done, and the findings were reported as x¯±SD of the duplicate experiments. Statistical comparisons between means were done using either one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test at α0.001. Gene expression data were analysed according to 2−ΔΔCt criteria using the QuantStudio™ 5 System's software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The percentage of each compound (abundance), in GC–MS analysis was determined using peak area in total ion chromatogram by the Agilent ChemStation software (Agilent, USA).

Results

Cytotoxicity of the V. doniana extracts against Vero CCL-81

The current study investigated the cytotoxicity of V. doniana leaf extracts on Vero CCL-81 cells. The findings indicated significant concentration-dependent increases in percentage cytotoxicity in Vero CCL-81 cells treated with the aqueous, methanolic, ethyl acetate, and dichloromethane leaf extracts of V. doniana (P < 0.001; Fig. 1). Comparatively, the ethyl acetate leaf extract of V. doniana exhibited significantly higher percentage cytotoxicity than all the other extracts at 1.37 µg/ml (P < 0.001; Fig. 1). At 4.12 µg/ml, the percentage cytotoxicity observed in cells treated with the ethyl acetate and dichloromethane extracts was considerably greater than those of the other extracts (P < 0.001; Fig. 1). However, from 12.35 µg/ml to 1000 µg/ml, the methanolic leaf extract of V. doniana showed significantly higher cytotoxicity to Vero CCL-81 than all the other extracts (P < 0.001; Fig. 1). Notably, Doxorubicin imparted significant cytotoxicity (P < 0.001) to Vero CCL-81 cells and assumed a concentration-dependent pattern, with an exceptionally low CC50 (0.41 µg/ml) as shown in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1, the dichloromethane leaf extract of V. doniana had a higher CC50 (1238.85 µg/ml), followed by the aqueous extract (CC50 = 1025.12 µg/ml), ethyl acetate extract (CC50 = 964.81 µg/ml) and the methanolic extract had the least cytotoxic effect (CC50 = 10.67 µg/ml).

Antiproliferative effects of the V. doniana extracts against cervical cancer (HeLa) cells

This study also investigated the antiproliferative effects of the leaf extracts of V. doniana to unearth their anti-cervical cancer efficacy. At concentrations of 1.37 µg/ml, 4.12 µg/ml, 12.35 µg/ml, and 1000 µg/ml, respectively, the methanolic and dichloromethane leaf extracts did not significantly differ from one another in terms of percentage inhibitions of HeLa cell growth (P > 0.001); however, these inhibitions were significantly greater than those observed by the other extracts at the same concentrations (P < 0.001; Fig. 2). The percentage inhibition of HeLa cells caused by the aqueous and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of V. doniana did not differ significantly at a concentration of 1.37 µg/ml (P > 0.001; Fig. 2). Additionally, it was found that, in comparison to all other extracts, the aqueous leaf extract of V. doniana significantly reduced the growth inhibitions of HeLa cells at all tested concentrations (P < 0.001; Fig. 2). A significant increase in percentage inhibition of HeLa cells’ growth by the leaf extracts of V. doniana was observed in this study and mirrored the observations made on cells treated with the reference drug Doxorubicin (P < 0.001; Fig. 2).

Moreover, the IC50 values for each leaf extract of V. doniana were determined to appraise their anti-proliferative efficacy against cervical cancer cells. In this study, the dichloromethane and methanolic leaf extracts of V. doniana showed lower IC50 values than the other extracts, as shown in Fig. 2. Conversely, the aqueous leaf extract of this plant recorded a higher IC50 than all the other extracts. Overall, the antiproliferative efficacy of the leaf extracts of V. doniana was ordered from the highest to the lowest as follows: dichloromethane extract > methanolic extract > ethyl acetate extract > aqueous extract (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, the reference drug, Doxorubicin, recorded the lowest IC50 value (1.04 µg/ml) compared with the studied plant extracts (Fig. 2).

Selectivity indices

The selectivity indices (SI) of each studied plant extract were computed to determine their ability to exert cytotoxic effects on cancer cells while sparing normal cells. The dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of V. doniana exhibited higher selectivity indices (103.67 and 26.55, respectively) against the cervical cancer cell line (HeLa-229) (Table 2).

Phytochemical composition of the plant extracts

The analysis of the dichloromethane leaf extract of V. doniana revealed the presence of 10 bioactive compounds as shown in Fig. 3. The identified compounds, their respective classes, and chemical characteristics are summarised in Table 4. The results showed that the dichloromethane leaf extract of V. doniana contained diterpenoids, steroids, phytosterols, fatty acid esters, and triterpenoids, with phytosterols being the most dominant class (Table 4). The most abundant compound identified was γ-Sitosterol (41.75%), followed by Stigmasta-3,5-dien-7-one (21.19%), and Stigmast-4-en-3-one (10.91%). In contrast, Neophytadiene (1.18%) and Phytol, acetate (0.51%), both diterpenoids, were the least abundant compounds detected in the dichloromethane leaf extract of V. doniana in this study. Discussion

Cervical cancer remains a significant global health challenge, particularly in low-resource settings where access to early screening and effective treatment is limited [30]. Current therapeutic options, including surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy, are often associated with severe side effects, resistance, and high treatment costs [6, 31], underscoring the need for alternative strategies. Natural products have emerged as promising sources of novel anticancer agents due to their bioactive compounds with selective cytotoxicity against cancer cells [9]. DespiteV. doniana’s ethnomedicinal properties, its anti-cervical cancer potential has not been explored significantly. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the cytotoxic, antiproliferative, and gene-modulatory effects of V. doniana extracts on cervical cancer cells, assess their selectivity, and identify key bioactive compounds, to provide insights into their potential as alternative therapeutic agents against cervical cancer.

The cytotoxic and antiproliferative properties of V. doniana extracts on cervical cancer cells were examined using the MTT technique, initially described by Mosmann [25] and recently modified by Markossian et al. [24]. This experimental method is reputed for its high sensitivity, reliability, and reproducibility of the findings, hence its extensive usage in the assessment of cell viability in drug toxicity and anticancer efficacy studies [32]. This experiment is based on the ability of the mitochondrial NAD(P)H-dependent cellular oxidoreductases, especially the succinate dehydrogenase, to reduce the MTT dye into a water-insoluble formazan complex, whose absorbance can be determined spectrophotometrically at 570 nm, upon dissolution using dimethyl sulphoxide [33]. The determination of absorbance is used to evaluate the cells’ metabolic activity, by which their viability, or inhibition of proliferation due to cytostatic or cytotoxic effects, can be inferred and discerned with greater profundity.

This study demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in cytotoxicity and inhibition of cervical cancer cell proliferation following treatment with V. donianaextracts, suggesting their potential as anticancer agents. The observed effects can be attributed to the presence of bioactive phytochemicals such as certain phenolics, including curcuminoids, flavonoids such as apigenin and fisetin, alkaloids like camptothecin, and terpenoids like lycopene, among others [34]. These compounds have been widely reported to exert cytotoxic and antiproliferative activities through multiple mechanisms, including the disruption of mitochondrial function, generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), modulation of apoptosis-related pathways, and interference with cell cycle progression [35, 36]. The presence of these bioactive constituents inV. doniana extracts ultimately contributed to the dose-dependent cytotoxic effects observed in this study.

The cytotoxic and antiproliferative efficacy of V. doniana leaf extracts was assessed by determining CC50 and IC50values against normal (Vero CCL-81) and cervical cancer (HeLa) cell lines, respectively, based on the American National Cancer Institute (NCI) criteria [37]. Notably, the methanolic leaf extract exhibited significant toxicity towards normal Vero CCL-81 cells (CC50 < 30 µg/ml), whereas other extracts showed negligible toxicity (CC50> 1000 µg/ml). These findings suggest that different solvent extraction methods influence the phytochemical composition and, consequently, the cytotoxic potential of the extracts [38]. Methanol is known to extract a broad range of polar and moderately non-polar compounds, including flavonoids and alkaloids, which have been implicated in cytotoxic activity [39]. The high toxicity of the methanolic extract towards normal cells raises concerns about its potential therapeutic index, emphasising the need for further fractionation and purification to isolate selective cytotoxic compounds.

Besides, the dichloromethane leaf extract of V. donianademonstrated remarkable antiproliferative efficacy against HeLa cells in this study, while, the ethyl acetate, and aqueous leaf extracts of the same plant were moderately antiproliferative and non-inhibitive of HeLa cells’ proliferation, respectively. The variations in antiproliferative efficacy of the studied plant extracts against the Hela cells are attributable to the presence and variations in the type and concentration of crucial phytochemicals responsible for antiproliferative efficacy [40]. Interestingly, the variations in cytotoxicity among differentV. donianaextracts align with previous studies that report differential bioactivity depending on the type of solvent used for extraction [41, 42]. The lower toxicity observed in other extracts suggests a variation in the concentration of bioactive phytochemicals, possibly due to differences in solubility and extraction efficiency, as demonstrated previously [38]. Thus, the observed variations in anticancer efficacy of the studied plant extracts underscore the need for careful consideration of the extraction method to isolate the most bioactive compounds [43].

Selectivity index (SI) is a widely recognised parameter for evaluating the therapeutic potential of plant extracts and bioactive compounds, by determining their ability to selectively inhibit cancer cell proliferation while sparing normal cells [44]. Extracts or compounds with high SI values are considered promising candidates for the development of safe and effective chemotherapeutic agents for treating cancer [45]. According to established criteria, extracts with SI ≤ 1 are classified as non-selective, 1 < SI < 3 as moderately selective, and SI ≥ 3 as highly selective [37].

In this study, the aqueous and methanolic leaf extracts of V. doniana exhibited low SI values (< 1), indicating a lack of selectivity despite their observed antiproliferative effects against cervical cancer (HeLa-229) cells. This suggests that these extracts may contain cytotoxic compounds that affect both normal and cancerous cells indiscriminately, limiting their therapeutic potential and usefulness. Conversely, the dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts displayed a high SI value (> 4) against cervical cancer cells, highlighting their strong selectivity and potential as targeted anticancer agents. The differences in SI values among extracts can be attributed to variations in phytochemical composition, influenced by the solvent used for extraction [38]. Additionally, the high selective toxicity of the dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of the studied plant towards cervical cancer could be attributed to the presence of compounds that specifically target cancer-specific pathways, such as differential redox balance, apoptosis induction, or inhibition of cancer servival-related signaling pathways, while sparing normal cells, as described in previous similar studies [46, 47].

Notably, the reference drug (doxorubicin) was not selective against HeLa cells, denoting its adverse effects when used to treat this cervical cancer, and other cancers. The primary mechanism of action attributed to doxorubicin involves its inherent capability to intercalate within DNA pairs, thereby instigating the fragmentation of DNA strands and impeding DNA and RNA synthesis [44]. This multifaceted impact is further manifested through the inhibition the enzyme topoisomerase II, resulting in discernible DNA damage and the concomitant initiation of apoptotic pathways. Additionally, doxorubicin precipitates free radical-mediated oxidative damage to DNA in conjunction with iron, imposing supplementary restrictions on DNA synthesis [48]. It is worth noting that DNA and RNA synthesis are crucial processes in the body and are tightly regulated to maintain health and life in general. Therefore, the indiscriminate inhibition of proliferation of both the normal and cancerous cells by doxorubicin observed in the present study is hinged on the non-selective inhibition of topoisomerase II and DNA and RNA synthesis [44].

Despite its use in treating various cancers, Doxorubicin causes adverse effects, such as fatigue, alopecia, nausea, vomiting, and oral sores, along with notable instances of bone marrow suppression and elevated susceptibility to secondary malignancies [48]. Existing medical evidence indicates that intravenous administration of doxorubicin may lead to severe tissue ulceration and necrosis due to extravasation [49]. The drug's considerable cardiac toxicity restricts its prolonged usage, as it causes reversible myopericarditis, left ventricular dysfunction, and arrhythmias [50]. Chronic cardiac toxicity, which is the most severe adverse effect, results in irreversible cardiomyopathy and congestive heart failure, accompanied by a mortality rate surpassing 50% within a year [51]. Thus, considering these drawbacks, the utilization of the promising plant extracts and their valorisation, based on empirical evidence, such as this study, is a viable alternative to mitigating the adverse effects. Besides, these plant extracts hold higher promise due to their marked selectivity and the diverse array of anticancer-associated phytocompounds, which, upon identification and characterization, may yield potent anticancer agents [13].

It is noteworthy that the integration of iron chelators, typified by dexrazoxane, presents a prospective avenue for mitigating the formation of free radicals by circumscribing the interaction between doxorubicin and iron [52]. The selectivity of the promising extracts (the dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of V. doniana) is encouraging as they exert their toxicity to cancer cells while sparing the normal cells. This suggests that inhibition of DNA and RNA synthesis in cancer cells is probably one of the mechanisms through which the promising plant extracts exert their anticancer efficacy. Additionally, these extracts may contain various antioxidant compounds that thwart free radical-mediated oxidative damage to essential biological molecules, including DNA, RNA, and proteins in healthy mammalian cells, while perpetuating damage to cancerous cells [13].

Cancer affects somatic cells and is characterised by a complex interplay of multiple mutations rather than a singular genetic anomaly [53]. Each mutation induces uncontrolled cell proliferation, resulting in tumour growth, disorganisation, and malignancy, due to the dysregulation of critical genes governing the cell cycle, proto-oncogenes, and tumour-suppressing genes [54]. Within this framework, molecular biology serves as a vital tool offering both qualitative and quantitative analyses of cellular nucleic acids and aids in identifying tumoral cell alterations associated with malignancy [55]. Accordingly, the expression profile of cancer-associated genes was determined to discern the efficacy of the promising plant extracts (those with the highest selectivity indices) against cervical cancer cells (HeLa-229) and their probable molecular mechanism of activity.

The development of cervical cancer and other gynaecological cancers is significantly influenced by the androgen receptor (AR) [56]. Pharmacological compounds inhibiting AR action target either the ligand binding domain (LBD) of the androgen receptor gene (ar) or interactions with co-regulatory proteins, impeding disease progression [57]. Despite these treatments, resistance challenges affect the clinical management of gynaecological cancers, especially those of the cervix uteri and prostate [58]. Resistance strategies entail the androgen/AR axis reactivating, which restores the AR transcriptional activity [59]. This can arise by AR amplification, gain-of-function mutations, androgen receptor variants (ARVs), intracrine androgen production [60], and overexpression of the argene and AR coactivators [61]. The cervical cancer cells (HeLa-229) treated with the ethyl acetate and dichloromethane leaf extract of V. doniana, had a significantly lower expression of the ar gene than the control cells. These findings suggest these plant extracts exert their antiproliferation effects through various mechanisms, including the inhibition of argene overexpression as described previously [54].

There are two main pathways through which apoptosis can be induced: the intrinsic pathway, predominantly caused by cellular stress such as oxidative stress, activation of oncogenes, DNA damage, and nutritional deprivation, and the extrinsic pathway, which is mostly activated by death receptors, such as Tumour Necrosis Factors (TNF), Trail, and Fas) [62]. Activating these two sophisticated but interrelated pathways induce caspase activity—a type of cysteine proteases that cleave key cellular components – eventually leading to cell death [63]. In the intrinsic mechanism of apoptosis, the activation of caspases requires mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), resulting in cytochrome c leakage from the mitochondrial intermembrane gap into the cytosol [64].

Research has shown that bcl-2 suppresses apoptosis by inhibiting cytochrome c release, thereby impeding the activation of caspases responsible for cell death, in the apoptotic cascade [63]. Therefore,bcl−2 overexpression characterises various cancers and actively drives their progression by initiating angiogenesis and sustaining cell survival [65]. Consequently, chemotherapeutic agent-induced apoptosis by targeting thebcl-2gene and its product (BCL-2 protein) has been recognised as an indispensable strategy for treating various cancers [66]. Thus, in the search for effective chemotherapeutic agents, plant extracts that can inhibit thebcl-2gene overexpression have a higher potential of being efficacious anticancer therapies or lead compounds for drug development [67].

In this study, treatment of HeLa cells with dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of V. doniana resulted in a significant downregulation of the bcl-2 gene compared to the control. Given that bcl-2overexpression is associated with cancer cell survival by inhibiting apoptosis [63], this downregulation suggests that these extracts may promote apoptotic pathways, thereby enhancing cancer cell death. These findings align with previous research demonstrating the ability of plant-derived compounds to suppressbcl-2expression, leading to increased apoptotic sensitivity in cancer cells [68]. The variations inbcl-2 expression between different extracts highlight the influence of solvent polarity in extracting bioactive compounds with distinct pro-apoptotic activities.

The caspase family, particularly caspase-3, plays a crucial role in apoptosis, by acting as an executioner caspase which mediates the cleavage of intracellular substrates leading to programmed cell death [69]. In this study,caspase-3 expression in HeLa cells treated with dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of V. doniana remained within a normal range, suggesting that these extracts contain bioactive phytochemicals capable of modulating caspase-3 activity. This modulation likely enhances apoptosis, as previous research indicate that plant extracts can influence caspase-3activation through both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways [65]. The intrinsic pathway involves mitochondrial damage, resulting in cytochrome c release and apoptosome formation, leading to caspase-9 activation and subsequent cleavage of pro-caspase-3/7 [70]. Conversely, the extrinsic pathway is triggered by death receptor signaling, leading to caspase-8 activation and direct cleavage of pro-caspase-3 [71]. The involvement ofbcl-2 suppression alongside caspase-3 modulation strongly suggests that these extracts may promote apoptosis through both mitochondrial and death receptor-mediated mechanisms. However, given the paradoxical role of caspase-3in both tumor suppression and progression [72], further studies are required to elucidate the precise regulatory interactions governing its expression and activity in cervical cancer cells.

Cell cycle regulation is another crucial factor in cancer progression, with cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) playing a central role in cell cycle transitions [73]. This study revealed thatcdk1 expression remained normal in HeLa cells treated with dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of V. doniana, suggesting that these extracts may help restore proper cell cycle regulation. However, the ethyl acetate extract exhibited a significantly higher cdk1 expression, implying a potential failure to fully inhibit uncontrolled proliferation. Elevated cdk1activity is often linked to malignant transformation due to its role in G2/M transition and mitotic progression [74]. The differential effects of the extracts oncdk1 expression indicate that while some bioactive compounds in V. doniana may help regulate normal cell cycle progression, others might not sufficiently suppress oncogenic cdk1 activity. Thus, there is need for further investigation into the specific phytochemicals responsible for these effects and their potential interactions with other cell cycle regulators such as CDK inhibitors, as altered cdk1activity often fuels unrestricted cell proliferation, which is a hallmark of malignancy [75]

The TP53 gene commonly referred to as p53gene, encodes the tumor suppressor protein P53, and is widely recognised as the"Guardian of the Genome"due to its critical role in maintaining genomic stability, inducing apoptosis, and regulating cell cycle arrest in response to DNA damage [76]. In this study, HeLa cells treated with dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts ofV. doniana exhibited normal p53 expression, suggesting that these extracts may contain bioactive metabolites capable of restoring or maintaining p53 functionality. Considering that many cancers, including cervical cancer, often exhibit p53mutations or dysregulation leading to unchecked proliferation [77], the observed normalisation of p53 expression is a promising finding from this study, valorising the studied plant extracts' potential. The ability of these extracts to reactivate p53expression aligns with previous research demonstrating that plant-derived compounds can influence p53-mediated pathways, thereby reinstating apoptosis and inhibiting tumorigenesis [78]. Further studies are needed to determine whether the modulation ofp53 expression by these extracts translates into enhanced p53-dependent apoptotic activity or improved DNA damage responses.

Recent studies indicate antioxidant phytochemicals, especially flavonoids and phenols, terpenoids, and alkaloids, among others, possess anticancer activity [13, 79]. In the present study, the leaf extract ofV. donianacontained various phytochemicals, among them neophytadiene, a diterpene hydrocarbon. Previous research shows that neophytadiene has anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, antirheumatic, antioxidant, antidiabetic, and antimicrobial properties [80]. These pharmacological effects suggest that neophytadiene may contribute to the inhibition of tumorigenesis by creating an environment that is less conducive to cancer progression while exhibiting minimal toxicity to normal cells at therapeutic doses [80, 81]. Furthermore, Phytol acetate, an esterified form of phytol, is a diterpene known to exert metabolism-modulating, cytotoxic, antioxidant, apoptosis-inducing, and immunomodulatory effects [82]. These biological activities may represent key mechanisms through which the dichloromethane extract ofV. doniana exerted its observed anticancer efficacy against cervical cancer cells in this study.

Another critical bioactive compound identified in the dichloromethane leaf extract was 9,19-cyclocholestan-3-ol, 14-methyl-, (3β,5α), a steroidal compound which has been linked to enhanced wound healing and the suppression of inflammation and cancer development [83]. Steroidal compounds interact with key cellular pathways regulating proliferation and apoptosis, and their anti-inflammatory properties may mitigate chronic inflammation, a well-established risk factor for cancer progression [84]. Additionally, phytosterols such as stigmasterol and γ-sitosterol have demonstrated cholesterol-lowering, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer properties [84]. Besides, preliminary studies further suggest that cyclocholestanes exhibit selective cytotoxicity against cancer cells, positioning them as promising candidates for anticancer therapy [85]. Collectively, these phytochemicals likely contribute to the antiproliferative effects observed in the dichloromethane extract ofV. doniana, reinforcing its potential as a source of novel chemotherapeutic agents for treating cancer.

The ethyl acetate leaf extract of V. donianaexhibited a broad spectrum of bioactive phytochemicals with medicinal relevance. Notably, sesquiterpenes, including (3R,3aR,3bR,4S,7R,7aR)−4-Isopropyl-3,7-dimethyloctahydro-1H-cyclopenta [1,3] cyclopropa [1,2] benzen-3-ol, α-copaene, and Ylangene, have been recognised for their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer activities [86]. Furthermore, τ-muurolol, α-calacorene, and isoaromadendrene epoxide exhibit antimicrobial effects with potential efficacy against cervical cancer cells [86]. These sesquiterpenes are generally well tolerated with low toxicity, making them attractive candidates for pharmaceutical applications [85]. Additionally, compounds such as (-)-spathulenol and (3S,6S)−6Isopropyl-3-methyl-2-(propan-2-ylidene)−3-vinylcyclohexanone possess antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities, further contributing to the observed anticancer potential of the ethyl acetate leaf extract of V. doniana in this study [86, 87].

Other significant phytochemicals detected in the ethyl acetate leaf extract included diterpenoids such as neophytadiene, triterpenoids like 3-O-acetyl-6-methoxy-cycloartenol, and long-chain alkanes including hentriacontane and pentatriacontane. These compounds have been shown to enhance plant resilience to oxidative stress, dehydration, and microbial invasion, primarily through their antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory properties [88, 89]. Additionally, flavonoid derivatives such as 2-(3,4-dimethoxyphenyl)−7-hydroxy-3-methoxy-4H-chromen-4-one exhibit strong antioxidant capacity, mitigating oxidative damage at the cellular level, which is a key driver of carcinogenesis [84]. Their anti-inflammatory effects further contribute to cancer prevention by suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling pathways that promote tumor development properties [88]. Taken together, the diverse array of bioactive phytochemicals identified in the dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts ofV. doniana underscore their pharmacological potential and partially validates this plant’s traditional use in managing various ailments, especially cervical cancer. Further characterisation of these secondary metabolites and determining the intricate mechanisms through which they influence the interplay of key cancer-associated genes, and unravelling the appropriate targets in the molecular dynamics of cancer is imperative in developing efficacious and safe anticancer drugs.

Limitations of this study

Despite the reported promising findings, this study has some limitations. The experiments were conducted in vitro, and the in vivo effects of the studied V. doniana extracts were not explored. Additionally, the potential toxicity of these extracts in normal tissues requires further investigation to fully appraise their toxicity profile and safety. The study also did not assess the pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and synergistic effects of the extracts, and did not isolate and characterise their bioactive compounds, which are critical for therapeutic applications.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study demonstrated the potent antiproliferative and cytotoxic activities of V. doniana leaf extracts against cervical cancer (HeLa-229) cells, for the first time. The cytotoxic efficacy varied among extracts, highlighting the influence of extraction solvents and methods on phytochemical composition. Selectivity analysis reveals that the dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of the studied plant preferentially targeted cancer cells while sparing normal cells, suggesting their potential as safe sources of novel efficacious chemotherapeutic agents. This study further demonstrated that the dichloromethane and ethyl acetate leaf extracts of V. doniana significantly downregulated bcl-2 expression while maintaining normal caspase-3, p53 and cdk1 expression, suggesting their role in apoptosis induction, cell cycle regulation, and cancer suppression. Phytochemical analysis revealed the presence of various bioactive compounds in the dichloromethane leaf extract including diterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and phytosterols, and flavonoids, triterpenoids, and sesquiterpenes the ethyl acetate extract of the studied plant, with anticancer, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects. These findings validate, in part, the traditional use of V. doniana in herbal medicine and highlight its potential as a natural source of efficacious and safe chemotherapeutic agents.

Future research should focus on in vivo studies to confirm the anticancer efficacy and safety of V. doniana leaf extracts. Comprehensive molecular studies are needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms of action, and investigations into potential synergistic effects among the extracts and their phytochemicals may enhance therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, phytochemistry using other techniques such as LC–MS for polar extracts, pharmacokinetic, and toxicity assessments should be conducted to determine the feasibility of developing V. doniana-derived compounds into clinically viable anticancer agents.
