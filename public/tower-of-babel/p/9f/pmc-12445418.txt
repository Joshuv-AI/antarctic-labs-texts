Trends in Volume of Wrist Arthroscopy Among American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery Part II Candidates
Authors: Cole M. Patrick, Josiah Snowden, Alexis B. Sandler, John P. Scanaliato, Benjamin R. Childs, Ali Boolani, Nata Parnes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Advancements in technology have spurred research interest in wrist arthroscopy. This study serves to identify trends in wrist arthroscopy utilization among American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery (ABOS) Part II candidates, with a focus on fellowship training status and the volume of wrist arthroscopy in early practice.Methods:A search of the ABOS Part II Examination Database was conducted, including all cases performed from 2009 to 2020. All cases involving wrist arthroscopy as identified by current procedural terminology code were eligible for inclusion. Trends in wrist arthroscopy utilization rates, surgical volume, fellowship training, and complications were collected and analyzed.Results:Annual wrist arthroscopy case volume remained stable between 2009 and 2020 (P = 0.11), despite an increasing number of ABOS candidates each year (P = 0.005). Most candidates (n = 844, 90%) performed one or fewer wrist arthroscopy cases per month. Hand and upper extremity fellowship-trained surgeons performed 95.7% (n = 2653/2772) of cases. The case volume remained stable among hand fellowship-trained surgeons (P = 0.15); however, case volumes decreased among non–hand-trained surgeons (P = 0.016). Surgical complications occurred in 13.2% of cases and were higher among diagnostic arthroscopy cases (17.9%, P = 0.03).Discussion:Over the past decade, wrist arthroscopy case volumes have remained stable, despite an increasing number of ABOS candidates. Although nearly all procedures were performed by surgeons with hand and upper extremity fellowship training, there was a decrease wrist arthroscopy rates among surgeons without hand and upper extremity fellowship training. Complication rates in this study were high and mirrored complication rates of inexperienced or low-volume surgeons as reported in existing literature.

Wrist arthroscopy is an important surgical technique that has the potential for both diagnostic and therapeutic application, with similar capabilities to what is achievable with arthroscopy of the knee, shoulder, or elbow joints.1-3 The origins of wrist arthroscopy trace back to 1932, with the first organized workshop held in 1986.4,5 Initially, the procedure was challenging because of a small joint space with the use of larger instruments and difficulties with fluid extravasation.3,6 However, there have been advancements with smaller wrist-specific instruments and traction devices to aid in joint distraction, making wrist arthroscopy applicable for many uses such as the evaluation of wrist instability, intrinsic ligament and soft-tissue injury, masses, and fractures.7 Wrist arthroscopy is now a well-established technique, and there has been an increase in the number of scientific papers on the subject, reflecting the continued growth in popularity of this relatively new technique.6

For orthopaedic surgical residents, familiarizing themselves with arthroscopy is routine, given the high prevalence of knee and shoulder arthroscopies during residency. This familiarity may facilitate a smoother transition to wrist arthroscopy, leveraging the fine-tuned hand-eye coordination and triangulation skills honed during training.6 Several studies have explored wrist arthroscopy volume for both residents and fellows. Between 2007 and 2013, analysis of Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education orthopaedic surgery resident case logs demonstrated an increase in the mean number of wrist arthroscopies (5.8 to 6.3, 8.6% overall increase) over an average 5-year residency period, according to two separate studies.8,9

Currently, over 90% of orthopaedic residents now seek fellowship training following residency.10,11 Hand and upper extremity fellowship training continues to be one of the most popular subspecialty training among orthopaedic residency graduates, only second behind sports medicine.12 A recent study using Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education case logs for orthopaedic hand surgery fellowship graduates showed an annual average of 16 wrist arthroscopies in a 1-year fellowship period between 2012 and 2015.13 Regarding trends, however, a separate study demonstrated a decrease in annual wrist arthroscopies performed among orthopaedic hand surgery fellows from 20.8 to 15.7 (P = 0.002) from 2011 to 2019.14

This study aims to examine the clinical applications of wrist arthroscopy among American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery (ABOS) Part II candidates with an emphasis on investigating the relationship between fellowship training status and wrist arthroscopy volume. We hypothesized that (1) there is an increase in the utilization of wrist arthroscopy in the study period (2010 to 2020) (2) and hand and upper extremity fellowship-trained surgeons perform wrist arthroscopy more frequently than non–hand fellowship-trained surgeons.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

Data were sourced from the deidentified ABOS part II database. The ABOS Examination for board certification in orthopaedic surgery consists of two components: a written examination (part I) that must be passed before an oral examination (part II) after 17 months of clinical practice. In part II, candidates present cases from a 6-month long window (or 9 months during the 2021 examination year because of the COVID-19 pandemic) in which they submit case logs documenting patient demographics, ICD-10 and current procedural terminology (CPT) codes, follow-up periods, and complications for all cases performed with revision surgery and readmission data added after 2013. The information obtained in the ABOS part II database is self-reported by each candidate, with information validated by each hospital and audited by the ABOS. Data in the ABOS part II database are often extrapolated to assess trends among orthopaedic surgeons in general.15-18

Database Query

The ABOS part II database was queried for all wrist arthroscopies (CPT 29840, 29843, 29844, 29845, 29846, 29847) performed between 2009 and 2020 by part II examinees between 2010 and 2021. Patient age, sex, follow-up period, medical and surgical complications, and revision surgery and readmission data were collected and assessed as well.

Data Collection

The total numbers of ABOS part II candidates and the unique number of candidates performing wrist arthroscopy were collected and stratified by year as well as performing candidate to calculate the total number of wrist arthroscopies performed annually and case volume per candidate. Candidates were further stratified by completion and type of fellowship training. The overall number of surgical cases was calculated and compared by the type of procedure performed. Overall and monthly surgeon case volume was also analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using linear regression to assess trends over the study period, and student t-tests were used to compare continuous data series with a normal distribution of variance. Regression coefficients (β values), which reflect the rate of change for the variable of interest per year on average, were reported for all linear regressions. Chi squared or Fisher exact tests were used to compare categorical variables between cohorts, with chi squared tests used for cohorts divided into more than five groups and Fisher exact tests for cohorts divided into five or fewer groups. Statistical significance was determined by an alpha value set to 0.05.

Results

Annual Rates

During the 12-year study period, the total number of ABOS candidates increased (n = 8788, β = 7.97, P = 0.005), but the number of those performing wrist arthroscopy remained stable (n = 938, β = 0.40, P = 0.56). In addition, rates of wrist arthroscopies and associated indications for wrist arthroscopy annually remained stable (n = 2772, β = −4.95, P = 0.11; Figure 1). The greatest number of wrist arthroscopies performed were for excision/repair of the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) (n = 1960, 70.7%), followed by synovectomy (n = 407, 14.7%) and diagnostic arthroscopy (n = 285, 10.3%; Table 1).

Candidate Volumes

During the 6 (or 9)-month collection period for each candidate, the greatest number of surgeons performed only one wrist arthroscopy (n = 361/938, 38.5%) with an average of three performed overall (SD = 2.68). In total, 90% of candidates performed one or fewer wrist arthroscopies per month (n = 844/938 candidates, n = 1890/2772 [68%] procedures), and only 10% performed more than one wrist arthroscopies per month (n = 94/938 candidates, n = 882/2772 [32%] procedures; Figure 2).

Fellowship Training

Hand and upper extremity fellowship-trained surgeons performed 95.7% (n = 2653/2772) of total wrist arthroscopies. There was no observed increase in the rates of wrist arthroscopy among surgeons with hand and upper extremity fellowship training (β = −4.15, P = 0.15) as well as among surgeons with a second or third fellowship in addition to a hand and upper extremity fellowship (β = 0.29, P = 0.66; Table 2). However, among non–fellowship-trained surgeons and surgeons with fellowship training in specialties outside of hand and upper extremity, rates of wrist arthroscopy decreased significantly throughout the study period (β = −1.09, P = 0.016; Figure 3).

Complications

A total of 13.2% of patients experienced surgical complications (n = 367/2772). Postoperative pain emerged as the most prevalent complication (n = 83, 3.0%), followed by nerve palsy/injury (n = 57, 2.1%) and stiffness/arthrofibrosis (n = 49, 1.8%; Table 3). Rates of complications following wrist arthroscopy performed for diagnostic arthroscopy were significantly higher at 17.9% compared with complication rates with other surgical indications (P = 0.03). Multiplicity was observed among complications, with patients experiencing complications demonstrating 1.13 (SD = 0.43) complications on average.

Discussion

Wrist arthroscopy is a relatively common procedure performed by young hand fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons, with over 10% of ABOS part II candidates performing the procedure within the first 2 years of their practice during their board collection period, according to our study. Between 2009 and 2020, the rates of wrist arthroscopy performed among ABOS part II candidates remained stable. Hand and upper extremity fellowship-trained surgeons performed 95.7% of all wrist arthroscopies. Most wrist arthroscopies were performed to address TFCC pathologies. Complication rates were higher than previously reported at 13.2%, with significantly higher rates of complications observed after diagnostic wrist arthroscopy versus wrist arthroscopy to address a known pathology.

Although there was an overall increase in ABOS part II candidates over the study period, the average number of candidates performing wrist arthroscopy and the total number of annual wrist arthroscopies performed remained stable between 2009 and 2020. In addition, there was no notable change in any arthroscopic procedure performed—diagnostic arthroscopy, lavage and drainage, synovectomy, excision or repair of the TFCC, or internal fixation. This is in contrast to studies that have demonstrated an increasing trend in publication numbers and awareness of wrist arthroscopy.6 Furthermore, there are mixed data regarding the overall trend of wrist arthroscopy usage among orthopaedic trainees. Studies analyzing orthopaedic surgery case volume over 5 years increased from 5.8 to 6.3 between 2007 and 2013; however, the total number of cases in 1-year orthopaedic hand fellowship decreased from 20.8 to 15.7 between 2011 and 2019.8,9,14 In this study, however, there was a stable utilization of wrist arthroscopy over a decade. Furthermore, these other studies highlight a potential source of improvement in resident education, as, on average, residents perform a small number of wrist arthroscopies during their entire residency training when compared with the volume of a fellow during 1 year of training.

We also found that wrist arthroscopies are almost exclusively performed by surgeons who have completed a fellowship in hand and upper extremity. In total, 95.7% of wrist arthroscopies were performed by fellowship-trained hand surgeons, either with one or multiple fellowships completed. There was no change in trends regarding wrist arthroscopy usage among surgeons with hand fellowship training; however, there was a notable decrease in rates of wrist arthroscopy performed among surgeons without hand training. This observation underscores the significance of specialized training in ensuring the proficiency and widespread application of specific surgical techniques, particularly in this case, wrist arthroscopy. This information comes as no surprise as hand fellows perform approximately three times the number of wrist arthroscopies in 1 year of training compared with residents that complete an entire 5-year residency program and have a broader exposure to the surgical technique.8,9,14

Nevertheless, the stagnant rates of wrist arthroscopy in this study are perplexing considering an increasing field of hand and upper extremity fellowships and graduates of fellowship programs. In a study assessing orthopaedic surgery fellowship match data, the overall number of positions offered (average 160.1) and applicants (average 193.6) for hand surgery fellowships increased markedly between 2011 and 2017 among an average of 78.6 programs annually.12 However, the findings of this study demonstrate no similar increase among rates of wrist arthroscopy, by the very subspecialists that performed 95.7% of wrist arthroscopies in this study. Rather, as previously stated, the overall number of candidates performing wrist arthroscopy and the total number of cases performed remained relatively the same over the decade study period.

It is important to assess treatment indications for wrist arthroscopy to better analyze trends. The primary indications for this procedure are assistance in the treatment of a fracture, TFCC pathology, resection of ganglia, treatment of intrinsic ligament tears, synovectomy, and lavage for infection, among others.2,6 Moreover, among polled members of the International Wrist Arthroscopy Society, there was a consensus that wrist arthroscopy can serve as a diagnostic tool when MRI findings are inconclusive.19 The same group also agreed that wrist arthroscopy was superior to MRI for diagnosing TFCC and scapholunate injuries.19 In this study, we found that the primary indication for surgery was excision or repair of the TFCC (70.7%), followed by synovectomy (14.7%) and diagnostic arthroscopy (10.3%). These findings support the notion that wrist arthroscopy serves an important role in diagnostic and therapeutic indications to address intraarticular wrist pathology, particularly associated with TFCC pathology.

Individual surgeon volume is another important factor to assess because numerous studies in other fields, such as hip arthroscopy and shoulder arthroplasty, have demonstrated decreased complications after reaching and maintaining a certain case volume.20-22 We found that 90% of candidates who performed wrist arthroscopies performed one or fewer wrist arthroscopies per month on average during the 6 (or 9)-month collection period, which accounted for 68% of the total wrist arthroscopy case volume in this study. These findings illustrate a disparity among the subset of candidates who performed wrist arthroscopy as part of their ABOS collection period. This difference may be secondary to ABOS candidates having limited opportunities to perform the procedure as a result of their specific practice setup not being conducive to seeing many patients who are good candidates for this procedure, and who have adequately failed nonsurgical measures before surgery. A 2016 European study found that the learning curve associated with wrist arthroscopy is 25 arthroscopies per year and/or 5 years of experience.23 According to their study, surgeons performing less than 25 wrist arthroscopies per year had a complication rate of 12.06%, compared with 3.95% among surgeons who performed more than 75 per year. Likewise, the complication rate was 13.6% for surgeons with less than 5 years of practice, compared with 2.3% with more than 15 years of practice.23 Although the data in our study only reflect 6 or 9 months of case collection, even if we extrapolate our data to encompass a full year, less than 1% of ABOS II candidates would meet the number of wrist arthroscopies needed to achieve “proficiency.” Nevertheless, among members of the European Wrist Arthroscopy Society, more than half of respondents claimed to perform fewer than five wrist arthroscopies per month and only 10.8% performed more than 10 per month, demonstrating a discrepancy in case volume among well-established surgeons out of their board collection period.6

We found that the complication rates of wrist arthroscopy were relatively high among ABOS candidates. In 2012, among 895 wrist arthroscopies, Ahsan et al observed a complication of 4.7%, which the authors noted was markedly higher than the previously published complication rate of around 2%.1,24 A later study published in 2016 by Leclerq et al23 described a complication rate of 5.98% with most of those complications being listed as “serious,” such as tendon laceration, nerve, artery, iatrogenic cartilage lesion, hematoma, compartment syndrome, failure to achieve the procedure, and complex regional pain syndrome. In our current study, the complication rate was found to be 13.2% among ABOS II candidates. There are several possible explanations for the discrepancies observed among outcomes after wrist arthroscopy. Although inconsistencies may be explained by the variety of indications, procedures, follow-up periods, and definitions of complications that characterize existing literature and render direct comparison difficult, there is also a known threshold for wrist arthroscopy experience and volume. In reference to the previously cited study by Leclerq et al, they found that surgeons who performed less than 25 wrist arthroscopies per year had a complication rate of 12.06% and those with less than 5 years of practice had a complication rate of 13.6%, which is remarkably similar to our reported complication rate of 13.2%.23 The overall complication rates noted in this study are comparable with complication rates noted among other groups of surgeons with low wrist arthroscopy-volume practices or less time in practice. Additional research may elucidate rates of expected complications among different specific wrist arthroscopy procedures as well as patient and surgeon demographics.

This study is not without limitations, most of which are consistent with the constraints of data reported in the sourced database. There may be an inherent bias in cases reported during ABOS collection periods, as examinees may artificially alter their expected case volume during a period of increased scrutiny. The challenge of elucidating surgical indications is a notable limitation, attributable to the inherent nature of a database study and the reliance on CPT codes as the major treatment measure. The reliance on preexisting data may hinder the detailed exploration of specific surgical indications, limiting the comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing surgical decisions. Similarly, inconsistencies in reporting surgical complications can affect data collection. For example, reports of implant failure observed during diagnostic arthroscopy or synovectomy are more likely attributable to a secondary pathology or procedure addressed at the time of surgery, such as arthroscopic-assisted fracture fixation, which may falsely elevate reported complication rates not directly from wrist arthroscopy. Similarly, examinees are under more scrutiny during ABOS part II examination; subsequently, examinees could cautiously over-report complications such as pain that may be normal for postoperative recovery, increasing our reported rates of postoperative pain. In addition, pain following a negative diagnostic procedure is difficult to classify as a complication versus an expected outcome, and the presence of this occurrence in the present data set remains similarly difficult to ascertain. These limitations underscore the need for caution in generalizing the study's results and emphasize the importance of prospective research designs for more accurate and nuanced insights into the examined surgical procedures and outcomes.

Conclusion

Over the past decade, wrist arthroscopy case volumes have remained stable, despite an increasing number of ABOS candidates. Although nearly all procedures were performed by surgeons who had completed a hand and upper extremity fellowship, there was a decrease in the utilization of wrist arthroscopy among surgeons without hand and upper extremity fellowship training. The most common procedure performed was to address excision or repair of the TFCC, followed by synovectomy, and diagnostic arthroscopy. Complication rates in this study were high and mirrored complication rates of inexperienced or low-volume surgeons as reported in existing literature.
