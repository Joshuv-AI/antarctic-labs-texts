Modelling the climate impact of inhalers and mitigation strategies: a population-based study in British Columbia, Canada (2015–2032)
Authors: Solmaz Setayeshgar, Kevin E Liang, Valeria Stoynova, Gillian Frosst, Kate Smolina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanada has one of the highest per capita greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, with healthcare contributing ~5% of the total. Pressurised metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs) are significant contributors due to their use of hydrofluorocarbon propellants. While propellant-free dry powder inhalers (DPIs) and soft mist inhalers (SMIs) are available, their adoption remains limited. This population-based study evaluates inhaler dispensation trends in British Columbia (BC), Canada, projects future dispensation and emissions over the next decade, and explores mitigation strategies through pMDI substitution.MethodsHistorical inhaler dispensation data (2015–2022) from BC were analysed using negative binomial models to assess trends, project future usage and emissions (2023–2032) and evaluate four substitution scenarios replacing pMDIs with low-GHG alternatives or DPIs/SMIs. Emissions were estimated by inhaler type, sex, age and health region, with uncertainties addressed through Monte Carlo simulation for the projected values.ResultsAn average of 2.1 million inhalers are dispensed annually in BC, with pMDIs comprising 64% of total inhaler use but contributing 98% of the ~30 000 tonnes of GHG emissions. There was regional variation and older populations contributed disproportionately, reflecting burden of disease. From 2015 to 2022 (excluding 2020 and 2021, the COVID-19 years), pMDI dispensations decreased by 1% annually while DPI/SMI dispensations increased by 5%. Projections show that, without intervention, emissions could rise to ~37 000 tonnes by 2032, varying by age group. All substitution scenarios, by replacing pMDIs with DPIs/SMIs, could reduce emissions by up to 42%.ConclusionHigh quality, guideline-directed diagnosis and management of respiratory disease is known to improve health and reduce emissions. Building on these benefits, our analysis shows that substituting pMDIs with lower-emission inhalers, when guided by policy and clinical decisions that prioritise patient safety and preference, can significantly reduce healthcare-related GHG emissions.Keywords: Inhaler devices, Clinical Epidemiology, COPD Pharmacology, Asthma Pharmacology, Asthma

Introduction

Climate change is a leading cause of adverse health outcomes globally through critical climate events, increased vector- and water-borne diseases and heat-related illness leading to increased health system utilisation.1 Paradoxically, this increased demand generates outsized greenhouse gas emissions (GHGe) that further contribute to global warming in a vicious cycle.1 Globally, healthcare contributes around 5% of total GHGe.

In Canada, the healthcare system accounts for 4.6% of the national GHGe2 and ranks fifth overall in healthcare carbon expenditure per capita.3 A quarter of these emissions is attributed to medications.2 Pressurised metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs), used to treat lung diseases such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), are outsized contributors because their carrier gas, hydrofluorocarbon (HFC), is a potent GHG.4 5

Inhalers have recently emerged as an attractive target to decrease healthcare-related carbon footprint through multipronged interventions focused on accurate diagnosis, exacerbation prevention, proper inhaler technique and safe inhaler disposal pathways.6

The transition to lower-carbon footprint inhalers has also garnered interest as part of this multimodal approach.6 Lower-carbon alternatives such as lower-volume propellant pMDIs and propellant-free devices including dry powder inhalers (DPIs) and soft mist inhalers (SMIs) have substantially lower GHGs.

In Canada, up to 95% of short-acting β2-agonist (SABA) inhalers dispensed are pMDIs.7 Internationally, prescription patterns vary; for instance, pMDIs represent only 13% of inhaler prescriptions in Sweden, where DPIs and SMIs are more commonly used, compared with 70% in England.8 These prescribing variations highlight the role of established practices, system policies, clinician behaviours, and patient preferences in shaping environmental and health outcomes.

Previous studies have estimated the impact of a large-scale transition to lower-carbon inhalers in several European countries,58,11 Canada7 and the USA.12 These have all been static point-in-time data, without accounting for population growth and ageing and associated increasing prevalence of lung disease and inhaler use over time.

The published estimates also assume an immediate, large-scale transition between 10%5 and 100%11 of pMDI to lower-carbon inhalers. While these studies illustrate the potential impact, they overlook patient values, characteristics and preferences that may limit feasible uptake.11 12 Many patients, including young children, those with limited inspiratory capacity or those who prefer pMDIs, may be unable to switch to lower-carbon inhalers. A safe systemic-level transition that preserves patient-tailored treatment through shared decision-making requires time, which is not realistically assessed in current models.8

Pernignotti et al modelled a slower, clinically feasible shift to lower-carbon inhalers in five European countries, showing substantial GHGe reductions.13 However, the model did not account for population growth and its relevance and applicability to the North American context remains uncertain.

This study addresses these gaps by analysing population-based data from the province of British Columbia (BC) in Canada to describe historical trends in inhaler use, project GHG emissions and model the impact of transitioning to lower-emission inhalers under four clinically relevant scenarios. These scenarios aligned with current best practices and guideline-directed care may also be applicable in other jurisdictions. The findings aim to inform policy and practice changes that reduce emissions.

Methods

Study design and setting

We analysed historical trends in inhaler dispensation and the associated GHG emissions in BC (population 5.3 million in 2022, publicly funded healthcare) from 2015 to 2022, with projections for 2023 to 2032. We examined inhaler counts and emissions, stratified by sex, age group and the five regional health authorities (RHAs): Fraser, Vancouver Coastal, Island, Interior and Northern Health (ordered by the population sizes, 2 024 968; 1 281 504; 894 013; 861 013; and 294,786, respectively).14

We modelled scenarios in which high-emission inhalers were substituted with lower-emission alternatives within the same medication class and with comparable expected clinical benefits. This model used historical dispensation data, inhaler-specific GHG emissions and BC statistics projections of population growth and ageing.14

Data source and ethics

We used population-based administrative health data from Population Data BC, including PharmaNet and the Consolidation File. PharmaNet is a real-time system that records community and outpatient hospital prescriptions in BC, with each drug identified by a Drug Identification Number (DIN), including dose and dispensation date. The Consolidation File provides demographic details (sex, age, geographic location). Further information regarding these data sets is available at: https://my.popdata.bc.ca/project_listings/16-218/collection_approval_dates. All inferences, opinions and conclusions drawn in this publication are those of the author(s) and do not reflect the opinions or policies of the Data Steward(s).

We extracted inhaler dispensation records from PharmaNet between 1 January 2015 (when SMIs were introduced in BC) and 31 December 2022 (the most recent data available), using DINs for each inhaler type (online supplemental table 1), resulting in 17 219 117 records across 72 DINs from 1 218 338 individuals. Inhalers were categorised as either pMDIs (high GHG) or DPIs/SMIs (low GHG). We merged these records with the Consolidation File using unique identifiers to assign demographic information. Records with missing age (n=7) and sex (n=17) were excluded. For 0.6% of records missing RHA, pharmacy location was used as a proxy, given 95% concordance with home RHA. As this work falls under BC Center for Disease Control’s mandate to inform public health policy, ethics board approval was not required.

Statistical analysis

Historical analysis

We calculated the number of inhalers dispensed per person per date by dividing total doses by dose-per-inhaler per DIN, a slight modification of a prior Fraser Health study,15 where the number of unique DINs was reported on each service date instead of the total number of inhalers dispensed. Decimal values (0.5% of records) were rounded to one; extreme outliers (>3 SD, 1% of records) were also adjusted to one inhaler.

We calculated average annual pMDI and DPI/SMI dispensation rates (2015–2022). Trends were analysed using generalised linear models with a negative binomial distribution and log link, accounting for overdispersion. The dependent variable was inhaler count (by pMDI vs DPI/SMI), with calendar year, age group and RHA as predictors and the logarithm of population as an offset. Sex was excluded due to non-significance. The coefficient for calendar year captured the trend over time. Data from 2020 to 2021 were excluded due to COVID-related anomalies.

Total emissions per inhaler type were calculated by multiplying GHG per inhaler by dispensed quantity, converted to tonnes CO₂ equivalent (tCO₂e) and expressed as equivalent kilometres driven. Projected GHG emissions were estimated by applying historical proportions of inhaler DINs by age, sex and RHA to future inhaler counts, assuming these proportions remain constant.

Projection of population growth and ageing in BC

Population estimates and projections for each RHA, stratified by age and sex from 2015 to 2032, were obtained from BC Statistics.14 BC Statistics periodically revises estimates for historical years due to updates in data, methods and census information. For future projections, BC Statistics employs the component/cohort-Survival method, which forecasts population based on trends in births, deaths and migration by age.

To account for uncertainty in population projections, we used Statistics Canada’s provincial population growth estimates, which include ten different scenarios for each year.17 We calculated the annual coefficient of variation (CV) as the ratio of the SD to the mean across these scenarios and applied these CVs to recalculate our study population. This method allowed us to generate probabilistic population estimates for the projected years (further details are provided in the ‘simulation’ section).

Projection scenarios

In addition to the status quo scenario (no change in inhaler dispensation and type), four scenarios were modelled (details are provided in online supplemental table 2) . The status quo scenario extrapolated historical trends in BC and within each RHA into the future.

The first scenario involves replacing high-volume-propellant pMDIs with lower-volume-propellant HFA134a alternatives that deliver the same molecule at equivalent doses but have lower GHG emissions. Specifically, five pMDIs were replaced with three lower-propellant pMDIs. For example, Airomir Salbutamol delivers the same active molecule as other salbutamol pMDIs (eg, Ventolin) but contains less propellant, resulting in a lower carbon footprint. Substitutions in this scenario were made within the same molecule and therapeutic class to ensure comparable clinical effects.

The second scenario involves replacing 17 pMDIs with nine equivalent DPI formulations for patients aged six and older. Here, ‘equivalent DPI formulations’ primarily refer to inhalers within the same therapeutic class, though not always the same molecule due to available DPI products on the market.

The third scenario followed current BC asthma guidelines18 by replacing short-acting ß2-agonist (SABA) for all patients aged 12 to 39 years with budesonide-formoterol DPIs. This age group was selected as a proxy for individuals with mild to very mild asthma,18 while excluding those with COPD.

The fourth scenario applies to patients aged 12 to 39 years who are on a combination of controller pMDI and SABA reliever pMDI (if dispensed within 60 days of each other). Both inhalers were replaced with budesonide-formoterol DPI, a single inhaler that acts as both reliever and controller. This reflects the Single Maintenance and Reliever Therapy approach, which combines maintenance and reliever therapy in one inhaler to improve adherence and enhance asthma control.19 Dose matching between pMDIs and DPIs was approached using dose-to-dose equivalence informed by clinical guidelines and product monographs. When an exact dose match was not possible, the closest clinically appropriate DPI dose was selected to approximate the MDI dose, ensuring comparable therapeutic effect. We also adjusted doses accordingly when modelling these substitutions, which was reflected in the calculated carbon footprint to capture the environmental impact associated with dose changes.

In all scenarios, substitutions were applied uniformly to projected dispensations for new and existing users, without separately modelling initiation rates. This approach reflects our focus on estimating the overall potential environmental impact at the population level.

We evaluated four mitigation strategies in all scenarios similar to those previously investigated:13

Simulations

We developed a model that integrates projected population growth for each RHA with inhaler dispensation projections. Dispensation rates were then converted to total GHG emissions as described in the ‘Estimating greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions of dispensed inhalers’ section. Historical trends were analysed deterministically, using point estimates.

To address uncertainty in projections, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation to capture variability in two main areas: trends in dispensation rates and population growth projections. Non-parametric bootstrapping was used to model variability in historical rates. In each iteration, historical data were resampled, and the negative binomial model was refitted to capture trend variability. For population growth uncertainty, we applied Monte Carlo methods using the CV described in the section ‘Projection of population growth and ageing in British Columbia (BC)’. Population for each year was treated as the mean, and the SD was derived accordingly. Using the mean and SD, probabilistic population sizes were generated by drawing from a normal distribution. They were then resampled in each simulation iteration. We performed 1000 iterations using Monte-Carlo methods and reported uncertainty as 95% CIs using the percentile method.

Data cleaning was conducted with SAS 9.4, while all analyses were carried out using R V.1.3.1093.

Results

Historical inhaler dispensation and GHG emissions

The total average annual number of inhaler dispensations among the residents of BC between 2015 and 2022 was 2.1 million, with 64% from pMDIs (table 1). The resulting estimated GHG emissions were 29 887 tonnes per year, equivalent to 145 million kilometres driven, with pMDIs accounting for 98% of these emissions. Females had a slightly higher annual inhaler dispensation rate (44 371 per 100 000) than males (40 730 per 100 000), despite similar overall counts (1.1M vs 1.0M). The highest average annual emissions were in the 60 to 69 years age group (6022 tonnes) due to the higher total volume of dispensations, reflecting the size of this subpopulation. Although the proportion of pMDI dispensation was lower in those aged 60 years and older (54%) compared with younger groups (68%–100%), emissions remained significant (table 1).

Inhaler dispensation rates and estimated emissions varied by geographic region. For example, total estimated annual emissions were highest in Fraser Health and lowest in the Northern Health region (largely reflecting relative population sizes), whereas per capita inhaler dispensation rates were highest in the Interior Health region and lowest in the Vancouver Coastal Health region. Proportions of pMDI dispensation varied, with northern at 72% and interior at 61% (table 1).

Results from the negative binomial models highlighted the effect of time, age and regional factors on inhaler dispensation patterns (table 2). Examining dispensation rates from 2015 to 2022 (excluding 2020 and 2021), we observed an annual average reduction of 1% in pMDI dispensations (estimate=0.99, 95% Cl 0.99 to 1.00) and a 5% increase in DPI/SMI dispensations (estimate=1.05, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.05). Projected inhaler dispensation and GHG emissions

Projection estimates show that by 2032, total inhaler dispensations in BC will likely increase, leading to a projected 24% rise in the overall emissions compared with 2022, with a more significant increase observed in males (29%) compared with females (21%) (table 3, figure 1). Growth in inhaler dispensation is driven primarily by a projected 110% rise in DPI/SMI dispensation, while the projected growth in pMDI dispensation is limited to 18%, contributing to a more moderate overall increase in emissions (table 3). The largest increases in inhaler dispensation are projected to be observed in the 70 to 79 and 80 years and older age groups (figure 1), driven by historically higher dispensation rates and BC’s rate of growth in these age groups (online supplemental figure 1). The notable rise across several age groups in 2023 in figure 1 is driven by a sharp increase in BC’s population due to higher net immigration growth.20 Projected decreases in pMDI dispensation are expected among the 50 to 59 and 60 to 69 years age groups (−5% and −9%, respectively), leading to stable or slightly reduced emissions (0% and −4%, respectively) (table 3). For children under 6 and those aged 6 to 11 years, declining population sizes are expected to reduce pMDI dispensation, resulting in a 23% and 16% reduction in emissions compared with 2022 levels. Emissions are projected to increase in all health regions, with higher estimated increases in the Fraser (32%) and Vancouver Coastal Health (28%) and a lower projected increase in Northern Health (3%) (table 3).

Discussion

This study is the first provincial-level assessment of inhaler-related GHGe in Canada and the first to model realistic substitution scenarios considering population growth and ageing. Our analysis illustrates that environmentally motivated inhaler substitution policies, particularly those aligned with current clinical guidelines, can reduce inhaler-related GHG emissions.

The pMDI prescribing rate in BC (64%) is similar to Italy (63%), lower than the UK (70%), and higher than Germany (57%), Spain (55%) and the Netherlands (50%).9 13 Sweden has the lowest rate of pMDI prescribing (13%), which supports the idea that the transition to lower-carbon inhalers can be done at a systemic level.13 Regional and demographic variations in inhaler usage identified in this study further underscore the importance of tailored interventions.46 18 21,26 In COPD, appropriate use of maintenance inhalers can also improve health-related quality of life and reduce mortality.6 21 Furthermore, strategies that improve diagnostic accuracy, promote adherence and ensure therapeutic decisions align with evidence-based guidelines are essential for optimising both patient outcomes and environmental impact.6 18 21 While the potential for GHG reduction is important, the primary focus must remain on achieving the best clinical outcomes.2 4 6

The carbon impact of large-scale transition to lower-carbon inhaler devices has been previously investigated57,12 with the assumption that an immediate large-scale transition of up to 100% of pMDI devices11 to lower-carbon alternatives. However, such an approach is neither realistic nor advisable; a small but significant percentage of patients are unable to use DPI/SMIs6 (eg, paediatric patients, patients with very low inspiratory capacity, patients with neuromuscular weakness). Some patients may also prefer pMDIs.27 Unilaterally changing a patient’s inhalers without their input or education is associated with worsening outcomes, which increases the carbon footprint of care to a greater extent than inhaler selection.11 22 A recent naturalistic experiment studied the impact of a forced non-consensual large-scale transition to DPI and demonstrated increased healthcare utilisation.12

Changing to a DPI device can also be associated with improved clinical outcomes and symptom control.23 Such complex decisions require a flexible, patient-centred approach and time to assess clinical factors. A strength of our study is the realistic ‘clinically feasible’ timeframe, reflecting culture change and patient care; even a 5% annual switch from pMDI to DPI showed considerable carbon reduction.

However, the transition to lower-carbon inhalers modelled in this study is only one step in the larger discussion on the sustainable management of respiratory disease. Diagnostic accuracy and high-quality disease management are central tenets of patient care and can decrease the carbon footprint.7 These strategies are not mutually exclusive. Indeed, a multipronged approach to decrease healthcare-related carbon footprint, with a focus on guideline-directed patient-centred care, is most likely to be successful. Our study highlighted only one of the steps in this pathway.

Projections for 2023 to 2032 suggest that while increased adoption of DPIs and SMIs can moderate emissions growth, additional targeted strategies will be required to offset the rising burden of chronic respiratory diseases in ageing populations. Integrating climate-conscious prescribing practices into chronic disease prevention and management frameworks can amplify their impact, reducing both healthcare emissions and disease burden. Achieving meaningful reductions in inhaler-related GHG emissions will require coordinated efforts across policy, clinical practice and public health to create a more sustainable and equitable healthcare system.

These system-level interventions are essential to facilitate the transition to lower-carbon inhalers where clinically appropriate. National-level shifts, such as NHS England’s optimisation programmes that reduced pMDI prescribing;5 broader NHS decarbonisation strategies under the Greener NHS initiative;28 and UK-wide increases in low-Global Warming Potential (GWP) inhaler prescribing, with a noticeable uptake in Wales between 2018 and 2024.29 Reimbursement-linked incentives in the Netherlands9 and Canadian modelling showing emission reductions through care gap closure6 demonstrate that coordinated efforts can modify prescribing behaviour, particularly among primary care physicians. International variation in prescribing practices, such as Sweden’s13 low pMDI use, underscores the need for implementation research to understand behavioural, policy and system-level factors influencing inhaler choice. In Canada, national and provincial guidelines increasingly recommend lower-emission alternatives when clinically appropriate.6 18 21 These considerations should also inform public drug coverage and policies. In addition, enhancing patient awareness and supporting shared decision-making may further accelerate the adoption of low-GWP alternatives.

We applied a practical and clinically appropriate approach to assess the impact of inhaler switching on emissions over time. Under the forced accelerated strategy (50% substitution by 2027, 80% by 2032), lower-propellant pMDIs (scenario 1) reduced emissions by ~20%, while transitioning to DPIs among patients aged 6+ (scenario 2) resulted in a 42% reduction over 10 years. Similar trends were observed in the Fraser Health region15 and in Pernigotti et al,13 though variations existed due to regional and methodological differences. Targeting asthma patients aged 12–39 (scenarios 3 and 4) with budesonide-formoterol DPIs resulted in significant emission reductions. These strategies can overlap, for example, switching to lower-GHG pMDIs (scenario 1) for those preferring pMDIs can coexist with transitioning to budesonide-formoterol DPIs (scenarios 3 and 4) for willing patients. These findings highlight the potential for inhaler switching to contribute meaningfully to environmental sustainability, emphasising the need for integrated strategies in chronic disease management. Our findings align with a recent national analysis by Gagné et al,7 which estimated annual avoidable GHG emissions through improved diagnosis, disease control and inhaler switching using Canadian prescription data. While their study used national prescribing estimates, ours draws on provincial dispensing data and includes future projections. Together, they show that clinical optimisation and device switching are both key to reducing respiratory care’s environmental impact.

Emerging low carbon propellant pMDIs may substantially reduce emissions and reshape prescribing practices if broadly available and used. However, further testing for safety and suitability is planned, and scaling up manufacturing is likely to take time.30 Together, these findings emphasise inhaler switching and innovation as essential components of sustainable, integrated chronic disease management.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its whole-of-population scope in a universal healthcare setting, stratification by age and sex, and use of historical dispensing data for projecting future emissions, accounting for population growth. However, this study has limitations. Scenarios three and four targeted asthma patients aged 12–39 years, using this age group as a proxy to exclude COPD and capture mild asthma, in line with BC guidelines. Due to data limitations, a validated case definition was not used, and the proxy may introduce misclassification and reduce clinical specificity. Additionally, scenario three involves budesonide/formoterol DPI strategies aligned with BC guidelines but may not fully align with Canadian Thoracic Society (CTS) recommendations, which limit such approaches to select patients due to limited evidence and cost. Furthermore, dispensing data may not fully capture medication use, but since all dispensed inhalers are expected to be used over time, emissions estimates remain valid. Although this analysis focused on GHG emissions, pharmaceuticals affect multiple environmental spheres beyond GHG impacts. Inhalers present a practical starting point for reducing healthcare-related emissions, potentially guiding similar efforts for other high-emission medications worldwide. Future studies should gather data to enable more precise comparisons between different inhaler types across broader environmental dimensions. While this study did not assess the cost-effectiveness of inhaler switching, future analyses should consider the relative costs of such interventions vs other carbon reduction strategies, to better inform policy decisions.

Conclusion

This study offers a provincial-level assessment of inhaler-related emissions in BC, leveraging population-based data to model trends and project future emissions. Similar analyses could be replicated for other Canadian jurisdictions. Our findings emphasise the role of pMDIs in healthcare-related GHG emissions and highlight the potential for substantial reductions through strategic shifts to lower-emission alternatives like DPIs and SMIs, when feasible and appropriate. By evaluating four emission reduction scenarios, we demonstrate targeted interventions, particularly among high-use groups, can significantly mitigate the environmental impact of inhaler therapy. Achieving meaningful reductions in emissions will require coordinated efforts, including policy reforms, clinician and respiratory therapist education and adjustments to pharmacy supply chains. These shifts must also be grounded in patient-centred care. Expanding the use of low-emission inhalers should be done with careful attention to patient safety, inhaler technique and clinical appropriateness while incorporating patient preferences through shared decision-making.26
