Taxonomic and functional responses of stream macroinvertebrates across different land use types
Authors: Mi-Jung Bae, Eui-Jin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrbanization and agricultural expansion are major drivers of freshwater biodiversity change, yet their effects on functional diversity remain unclear. We investigated the taxonomic and functional diversity of benthic macroinvertebrates across 121 forest, agricultural, and urban stream sites in South Korea. Macroinvertebrates were classified into 28 categories based on nine functional traits. Taxonomic richness was highest in forest streams and lowest in urban streams. Functional richness declined in disturbed streams, while functional divergence increased in urban and agricultural sites, indicating trait specialization under stress. Species such as Asellus sp. and Limnodrilus gotoi dominated in urban and agricultural sites, reflecting tolerance to pollution and sedimentation. In contrast, forest streams supported sensitive taxa with diverse life strategies. Our findings reveal how trait-based approaches complement taxonomic assessments by capturing adaptive responses to land-use changes, emphasizing the importance of incorporating functional diversity into freshwater biodiversity monitoring and conservation strategies.Subject terms: Biodiversity, Ecosystem ecology

Introduction

The biodiversity of freshwater ecosystems is continuously declining at an unprecedented rate, posing a significant challenge to ecological sustainability and conservation efforts. The European Union’s 2030 Biodiversity Strategy has identified five principal drivers of biodiversity loss: changes in land and maritime use, overexploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive species1,2. These factors collectively undermine ecological balance and accelerate the depletion of biodiversity. Even worse, the decline in freshwater biodiversity surpasses that of marine and terrestrial ecosystems3.

Among these five drivers of biodiversity loss—land-use changes, overexploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive species—land-use changes (e.g., urbanization and agricultural expansion) are significant contributors to freshwater habitat degradation, thereby impacting the biodiversity of freshwater organisms4,5. Development and land transformation into urban and agricultural areas have led to habitat fragmentation and changing the physicochemical conditions of rivers. This results in water quality degradation6, habitat quality decline7, and increased nutrient and pollutant runoff8,9, which directly affects the taxonomic and functional diversity of freshwater biodiversity, particularly that of benthic macroinvertebrates10,11.

Benthic macroinvertebrates are crucial biological indicators within freshwater ecosystems due to their abundance, diversity, and essential positions in food webs, nutrient cycling, and energy flow6,12. Their presence or absence provides valuable insight into the status of freshwater ecosystems and reflects the impacts of land-use changes13, water quality14, and habitat degradation15. Therefore, understanding the influence of land use on benthic macroinvertebrate diversity is necessary to develop effective conservation strategies and inform policy decisions.

While the taxonomic diversity of benthic macroinvertebrates has been extensively studied, functional diversity has recently gained increasing attention for understanding land-use impacts in freshwater ecosystems. In addition, research integrating taxonomic and functional diversity remains less common16,17. Functional diversity, which refers to the range of functional traits within a community that influence ecosystem processes, offers insights into ecosystem responses to human pressures, enabling a more nuanced understanding beyond that provided by taxonomic diversity alone18–20. Recent studies21–23 have highlighted the complex relationships between land-use changes and the diversity of benthic macroinvertebrate communities, providing extensive insights for ecological assessments and conservation strategies. The shift from natural forested areas to urban landscapes triggers habitat simplification owing to typical urban land-use practices such as dredging and canalization, which significantly affect the functional diversity of aquatic communities24. Additionally, the expansion of agricultural areas and the intensification of urbanization are associated with the prevalence of certain macroinvertebrate traits, such as small body size and rapid developmental cycles, underscoring adaptive strategies to survive in altered environments25. Urban and industrial pollution is correlated with the dominance of traits such as aerial respiration, resistance forms, and cylindrical body shapes, indicating the selection of species capable of withstanding polluted conditions26. These findings suggest that land-use changes significantly affect taxonomic diversity by altering species composition and trait distributions. Under such circumstances, functional diversity may remain stable or even increase in certain cases due to trait redistribution and functional redundancy that allow communities to reorganize trait space in response to altered environmental pressures. However, other studies have shown that functional diversity can be sensitive to urbanization and habitat simplification, leading to a reduction in functional diversification26,27. This complexity highlights that the response of functional diversity to anthropogenic disturbances is context-dependent, varying with environmental pressures and the ecological roles of the affected traits. Therefore, further research is essential to unravel how functional diversity responds to varying land-use pressures across different ecological contexts, providing a deeper understanding of its role in maintaining ecosystem functions amidst anthropogenic disturbances.

Therefore, we explored how different land-use types—forest, agriculture, and urban areas—affect the taxonomic and functional diversity of benthic macroinvertebrates, providing insights into their broader implications for freshwater ecosystem health and functionality. This study explores how different land-use types—forest, agriculture, and urban areas—affect the taxonomic and functional diversity of benthic macroinvertebrates, highlighting their ecological consequences and providing a foundation for effective conservation and management strategies to preserve freshwater biodiversity and sustain ecosystem functionality28,29.

Results

Comparisons of taxonomic and functional diversity among land use types

We collected data on 84,343 macroinvertebrates belonging to 22 orders, 91 families, and 211 taxa (Supplementary Table S1). Of the 211 taxa identified, 27 taxa (12.8%) were present in forests, agricultural areas, and urban areas. The forest streams had 73 taxa that were not found in either agricultural or urban streams, 36 taxa were found in agricultural areas but not in forest or urban streams, and 14 taxa were found only in urban streams (Fig. 1a). Chironomidae was the most abundant taxa, with abundances of 816.1 ± 577.5 individuals (ind)/m2 in forest streams, 717.9 ± 772.4 ind/m2 in agricultural streams and 531.3 ± 1051.8 ind/m2 in urban streams. Species abundant in forest streams included Epeorus nipponicus, Ecdyonurus kibunensis, and Epeorus pellucidus (Ephemeroptera: Heptageniidae), Hydropsyche kozhantschikovi (Trichoptera: Hydropsychidae), and Simulium sp. (Diptera: Simuliidae). In agricultural streams, abundant species were Baetis fuscatus (Ephemeroptera: Baetidae), Dugesia sp. (Tricladida: Planariidae), Caenis KUa (a provisional code used in Korean freshwater biomonitoring to denote a morphologically distinct but taxonomically unresolved Caenis form, Ephemeroptera: Caenidae), Asellus sp. (Isopoda: Asellidae), and Ecdyonurus kibunensis (Ephemeroptera: Heptageniidae). Urban streams were dominated by Baetis fuscatus (Ephemeroptera: Baetidae), Asellus sp. (Isopoda: Asellidae), Limnodrilus gotoi (Clitellata: Naididae), Cheumatopsyche brevilineata (Trichoptera: Hydropsychidae), and Antocha KUa (Diptera: Limoniidae). Based on species richness, collector-gatherers were the most species-rich functional feeding group (38.0%) in urban areas (Fig. 1b). In contrast, predators accounted for the highest species richness (34.8%) in agricultural streams, while scrapers and predators were notably species-rich (27.5% and 26.8%, respectively) in forested streams.

Species richness was significantly greater in forest streams (23.3 ± 4.8) than in agriculture (10.8 ± 5.9) and urban streams (7.9 ± 3.5) (Kruskal–Wallis test: H = 79.472, df = 2, p < 0.001). Shannon diversity (H = 72.943, df = 2, p < 0.001), evenness (H = 26.031, df = 2, p < 0.001), and the marginal index (H = 80.730, df = 2, p < 0.001) were also significantly greater in the forest streams than in the other streams (Fig. 2). Functional richness (Fric) was significantly higher in forest (21.7 ± 6.6) and agricultural streams (18.8 ± 7.2) than in urban streams (11.1 ± 5.5) (H = 44.184, df = 2, p < 0.001). Conversely, functional divergence (Fdiv) was significantly higher in agricultural (0.90 ± 0.09) and urban streams (0.85 ± 0.12) than in forest streams (0.77 ± 0.09) (H = 32.9357, df = 2, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons indicated that most differences were driven by contrasts involving urban streams, while forest and agricultural streams showed fewer differences, except for some indices such as functional richness (Supplementary Table S2).

Taxonomic and functional dissimilarities among land use types

Permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) revealed a significant difference in the composition of benthic macroinvertebrate communities (pseudo-F = 11.376, P < 0.001) and their biological traits (pseudo-F = 17.977, P < 0.001) across the different land use types (Table 1). Pairwise comparisons indicated that taxonomic composition differed significantly among all land use types (p = 0.003, Bonferroni-adjusted), with the largest differences observed between forest and urban streams (R² = 0.15, p = 0.003; Supplementary Table S3). Functional diversity also exhibited significant differences, particularly between forest and urban streams (R² = 0.20, p = 0.003) and agricultural and urban streams (R² = 0.29, p = 0.003; Supplementary Table S3), highlighting distinct trait reorganization patterns driven by land-use-specific environmental pressures.

Relationship between freshwater biodiversity and environmental factors

Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination based on the benthic macroinvertebrate taxonomic community had a fair goodness-of-fit with a stress value of 0.21 (Fig. 3a). The ordination plots revealed an apparent separation among macroinvertebrate communities for the three different land-use patterns. Forest streams were predominantly located in the right-hand side of the ordination space (positive axis 1), while agricultural streams were clustered in the lower-left quadrant (negative axis 1 and negative axis 2), and urban streams were mainly positioned in the upper-left quadrant (negative axis 1 and positive axis 2). The ordination based on biological traits (stress value = 0.14) also showed a clear separation, with forest streams primarily located in the upper-right quadrant (positive axis 1 and positive axis 2), whereas sites from agriculture and urban streams were primarily distributed in the lower part of the ordination space, showing partial overlap between the two groups (Fig. 3b).

In the NMDS ordination based on macroinvertebrate taxonomic communities, average temperature (R2 = 0.70), conductivity (R2 = 0.68), urban (%) land cover (R2 = 0.59), and minimum temperature (R2 = 0.54) were also major environmental factors structuring taxonomic diversity. Indicator species and traits among land use types

Indicator species analysis (IndVal) method identified specific indicator species and trait categories within each land use type. Of the 211 taxa, 65 species were selected as indicator species, characterizing each land-use type (p < 0.05) (Table 2). Only three taxa, Baetis pseudothermicus, Asellus sp., and Alboglossiphonia heteroclita, were selected as indicator species for the urban streams. Twelve taxa were indicator species in the agricultural stream, including Limnodrilus gotoi, Chironomidae spp., and Ephemera orientalis. Fifty-one taxa were indicator species in the forest streams, including Epeorus pellucidus, Rhyanocphila nigrocephala, Stenopsyche bergeri, Drunella ishiyamana, and Kamimuria coreana.

Discussion

In our study, despite lower taxonomic richness, the species sampled from agricultural and urban streams exhibited a more pronounced expression of specific functional traits (higher functional divergence)30,31. Barnum et al.32 observed similar findings where functional divergence increased with urbanization gradients at the state and ecoregion levels. They suggested trait redistribution from a configuration with more abundant taxa occupying the trait space to a distribution where a few abundant taxa occupy specialized regions of the trait space32. The enhanced functional divergence observed in agricultural and urban stream communities, despite lower species richness, reflects a reorganization of functional traits. This trait redistribution may reflect adaptive responses that promote ecological stability under disturbed conditions. Furthermore, Sarremejane et al.33 observed that drought events led to decreased species richness but did not reduce trait richness, indicating that trait-based indices such as functional divergence may still capture functional adaptations of communities even when taxonomic diversity declines. This redistribution highlights the importance of functional divergence as a metric for understanding community adaptation to disturbances. These findings are consistent with those of Matomela et al.34 and Paz et al.22, who underscored the significant impact of land-use changes on macroinvertebrate communities35 and advocated for integrating both taxonomic and functional approaches to improve bioindication and biodiversity assessment frameworks36. The distinct functional traits observed in forest, agricultural, and urban streams highlight how land use drives community adaptations to specific environmental pressures, such as sedimentation, nutrient enrichment, and habitat fragmentation. These adaptive traits not only reflect the ecological consequences of land-use changes but also provide a nuanced understanding of their impact on ecosystem functionality37.

In urban streams, indicator species such as Asellus sp. and Alboglossiphonia heteroclita exhibit traits that enhance survival under conditions of pollution and physical disturbance. For example, Asellus sp., known for its ecological flexibility and tolerance to low DO, is well-adapted to fragmented habitats and degraded water quality, which are general characteristic of urban environments38. Alboglossiphonia heteroclita, a predator, contributes to simplified food webs often observed in disturbed ecosystems, where trophic interactions are reorganized due to habitat alteration and reduced biodiversity39. These findings suggest that urban streams select species that can tolerate such conditions and play a crucial role in maintaining ecological functions and stability despite environmental stresses. Additionally, the prevalence of these traits highlights the ecological processes shaping community composition in urban streams, providing insights into how disturbance-driven environments influence both functional roles and species distributions40.

In agricultural streams, indicator species such as Limnodrilus gotoi, Ephemera orientalis, and Chironomidae spp. (non-biting midges) which prefer habitats with a higher ratio of sand in the substrate, a condition often resulting from sediment runoff from agricultural land were selected41,42. This substrate preference is crucial for survival, as it provides optimal conditions for burrowing behaviors that protect them from predators and harsh environmental conditions. Burrowers (favored regarding increasing sediment inputs) are notably more abundant in agricultural streams, indicating a predictable response to human-induced disturbance gradients43,44. In addition, these agricultural streams are characterized by larger species and collector-gatherers, which suggests a response to nutrient enrichment from agricultural runoff45. This response highlights their adaptive traits and underscores the broader ecological shifts within these communities, adjusting to altered nutrient cycles, and energy flows in the deforested ecosystem. The prevalence of species that utilize cutaneous respiration may reflect tolerance to depositional environments with fine sediments and high organic loads, where oxygen levels are variable but not necessarily persistently low46. This adaptation is particularly significant because it reflects the resilience of macroinvertebrates to hypoxic conditions, which are often exacerbated by agricultural runoff, enriching water bodies with organic materials and nutrients. Agricultural streams exhibit certain traits indicative of adaptation to altered environmental conditions, such as fully aquatic life stages and the prevalence of collector-gatherers6. The enrichment of streams with nutrients and organic matter from agricultural runoff likely promotes conditions that support species capable of exploiting abundant detritus and sediment, thereby maintaining crucial ecological functions, such as detritus processing and nutrient cycling, under disturbed environmental conditions7. Egler et al.47 observed similar patterns, highlighting that agricultural streams uniquely select for traits associated with sediment tolerance and organic matter utilization, which stabilize ecosystem processes despite ongoing disturbances. In contrast to urban streams, agricultural streams harbored a greater proportion of taxa associated with traits such as cutaneous respiration and collector-gathering behavior, which may reflect community-level responses to sediment-rich and nutrient-enriched conditions, rather than functional optimization.

Forest streams are characterized by species such as Epeorus pellucidus and Rhyacophila nigrocephala, which prefer habitats with high water velocity and DO levels, conditions indicative of minimal human disturbance48–50. Traits like gill respiration and clinging ability enable these taxa to thrive in fast-flowing, oxygen-rich environments26,51,52. Univoltine species, which reproduce once annually, dominate due to the stable and predictable conditions of forest streams. Additionally, perennial species, which exhibit longer life spans and reproduce over multiple years, also thrive in these environments, reflecting their ability to exploit the minimal disturbances and consistent resource availability. The dominance of traits such as weak armor, the life stages of eggs and larvae, and the presence of scrapers/grazers in forest streams can be attributed to the pristine conditions typically offered by these environments. In forested streams, complex and shaded habitats provided by dense riparian vegetation may reduce physical stressors, such as sediment abrasion or high flow exposure, thereby favoring a broader array of invertebrate morphologies and feeding strategies that reflect ecological integrity. These observations are supported by the findings of Jun et al.53 and Iñiguez-Armijos et al.54, who noted that forested streams support diverse macroinvertebrate communities with a range of feeding strategies, reflecting the availability of diverse food resources and high-quality habitats.

Procrustes analysis revealed significant congruence between the ordinations derived from taxonomic and functional diversity datasets for urban and agricultural streams, indicating that these diversity metrics share consistent spatial patterns under anthropogenic disturbances. This congruence highlights that taxonomic and functional composition, while capturing distinct ecological dimensions, are similarly influenced by environmental gradients such as land use changes and water quality. In contrast, forest streams exhibited no significant congruence between taxonomic and functional diversity, suggesting that their structural and functional attributes respond differently to environmental factors, likely due to minimal anthropogenic influence and more stable ecological conditions.

Our results suggest that taxonomic and functional diversity assessments are not merely redundant but rather synergistic, offering a more nuanced understanding of community responses to environmental pressures. The significant congruence in disturbed habitats demonstrates how functional traits adapt to maintain ecosystem functionality despite species turnover, while the lack of congruence in forest streams points to a more complex ecological interplay that warrants further exploration. Integrating both metrics is therefore essential for developing conservation strategies tailored to the specific ecological dynamics and stressors of each land-use type.

Our findings provide significant insights into biodiversity conservation by addressing how land-use changes influence the taxonomic and functional diversity of freshwater macroinvertebrate communities. This study underscores the complementary role of functional diversity in biodiversity assessments by highlighting patterns not fully captured by taxonomic data alone. While forest streams exhibited the highest taxonomic diversity, functional divergence (Fdiv) was elevated in agricultural and urban streams, suggesting trait specialization under disturbed conditions. Moreover, functional richness (Fric) was significantly higher in agricultural than in urban streams, despite similar taxonomic richness, indicating that functional metrics can uncover subtle but ecologically meaningful responses to land-use change. These trait distributions likely reflect ecological filtering, whereby only certain functional strategies persist under anthropogenic pressures.

Global biodiversity frameworks, such as the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (K-M GBF), primarily rely on taxonomic indicators to monitor and mitigate biodiversity loss. However, this taxonomic focus may not fully capture ecological dynamics that functional diversity can reflect. Our findings—particularly the trait reorganization observed across land-use gradients—highlight the interpretive potential of functional metrics in detecting ecological responses to disturbance. This perspective supports the integration of trait-based indicators into biodiversity assessments, offering added value in guiding restoration priorities and conservation strategies aligned with the goals of the K-M GBF.

Methods

Study area

This study focused on the extensive Han River Basin, which stretches over 32,971 km² and represents one of South Korea’s primary river systems, encompassing ~25% of the country (Fig. 4). This basin is located between the latitudes of 36.03° N and 38.55° N and longitudes of 126.24° E and 129.02° E55. The rivers converge and proceed towards Seoul, the country’s densely populated capital, with a population of ~10 million. The region experiences a four-season climate with notable monsoon rains from June to August, with annual precipitation ranging from 500 to 1500 mm. Seasonal temperature fluctuations are observed, with averages of 10.8 °C in spring, 23.6 °C in summer, 12.6 °C in autumn, and −2.9 °C in winter35. This pattern contributes to a predominantly moist environment, with precipitation heavily skewed towards the summer months (June–August), according to the Korea Meteorological Administration. The average altitude of the Han River Basin’s landscape is 404.7 m above sea level. Urban development accounted for 8.2% of the area, whereas forests and agricultural land accounted for 71.3% and 16.6%, respectively, including a small fraction (3.9%) of other uses.

Benthic macroinvertebrate sampling

Data on benthic macroinvertebrates within the Han River Basin were sourced from the National Aquatic Ecological Monitoring Program (NAEMP) database (http://water.nier.go.kr/). Biannual surveys were conducted in spring (April) and autumn (September) to coincide with river periods of water stability. These months were selected to mitigate the impact of severe summer flooding, which is commonly induced by monsoon weather patterns. September marks the onset of autumn and is particularly notable for the resurgence of aquatic life following flood disturbances. Benthic macroinvertebrates were collected using a Surber sampling net (30 × 30 cm, mesh size 1 mm) following the standardized protocol of the National Aquatic Ecological Monitoring Program (NAEMP) in Korea. While this mesh size may limit the collection of smaller organisms, it has been widely adopted for macroinvertebrate studies due to its suitability for long-term ecological monitoring and its alignment with the focus of this study. At each site, three replicate samples were collected from different locations within the riffle area to capture spatial variability during each sampling event. Sorting and species-level identification of the samples were subsequently performed, and the species abundance was quantified through direct observation or microscopic analysis. Most taxa were identified to the species level, except for certain groups such as Chironomidae (Diptera) and Oligochaeta (Clitellata), which were identified to higher taxonomic levels due to morphological constraints.

Environmental factors

Our study incorporated 24 diverse environmental parameters, including meteorological, geographical, land cover, and water quality factors (Table 4). Meteorological data, including average annual temperature, maximum and minimum temperatures, and annual precipitation, were obtained from the Korea Meteorological Administration (http://www.climate.go.kr) using 30-year climate averages to ensure comprehensive and representative environmental assessments. Geographic details were accurately mapped using ArcGIS digital mapping tools (version 10.1) with elevation data derived from digital elevation models (DEM). Land-cover data were extracted within a 500-meter radius from each sampling site, using a circular buffer approach via the Environmental Spatial Information Service (https://egis.me.go.kr/). Water quality data, including dissolved oxygen (DO), biological oxygen demand (BOD), ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N), nitrate nitrogen (NO3–N), total nitrogen (TN), phosphate-phosphorus (PO4–P), total phosphorus (TP), and chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) in the Han River Basin were sourced from Water Quality Monitoring Program database (http://water.nier.go.kr/).

Functional traits (diversity indices)

We classified nine functional traits and 28 categories into three distinct groups: life history, morphology, and habit (Table 5). These traits were selected using existing studies and data accessibility56,57.

The life history group comprises three traits: voltinism, life span, and aquatic stages. Voltinism, which refers to the number of generations a species produces annually, is associated with variation in size within a species and may influence intra-species competition and predation rates by altering life history strategies and temporal resource use patterns. Life span, which is indicative of the duration of a species’ life cycle, correlates with its reproductive capabilities. Species exhibiting shorter life spans typically show greater resilience to disturbances. Aquatic stages, which describe the dispersal abilities of a species, suggest that species with non-aquatic flying adults possess superior dispersal capabilities.

Morphology is characterized by four traits: maximum size, respiratory organs, shape, and armoring. Maximum size is directly linked to an organism’s fecundity58, position within the food web, and mobility. The type of respiratory organ possessed by an organism indicates its adaptability to various environmental oxygen levels. The shape of an organism influences its mobility and is an adaptation to the velocity of the water flow it experiences, whereas its armoring level indicates its ability to endure physical and environmental challenges.

Finally, the habit category comprises locomotion and functional feeding habits. The mode of locomotion and its relationship with the substrate play crucial roles in habitat connectivity and ecosystem resilience, influencing microhabitat choice. Functional feeding groups highlight the role of organisms in trophic dynamics and their reactions to environmental changes.

Data analysis (statistical analyses)

To compare the taxonomic and functional diversity of benthic macroinvertebrates among streams with different land cover types, we considered three categories: urban/built-up areas, agricultural areas, and forest areas. Urban and agricultural streams were defined as those where more than 60% of the buffer zone was urban or agricultural land, respectively, while forest streams were classified as those with more than 80% forest cover59–61. A total of 121 sites near rivers were surveyed, including 39 urban, 34 agricultural, and 48 forested sites.

Then, we applied for five steps of data analysis.

First, representative functional diversity (FD) indices, including functional richness (Fric), functional evenness (Feve), functional divergence (Fdiv), and Rao’s quadratic entropy indices (RaoQ), were computed62. These indices were derived using the “dbFD” function within the ‘FD’ package62 in R63, version 3.6.2. Fric measures the extent of functional traits present within species communities, with lower Fric values suggesting limited exploitation of available ecological niches and resources by the species64. Feve quantifies how evenly species are distributed across the functional trait space, where a high Feve value denotes more thorough and efficient use of available resources by the species. Fdiv assesses how species with higher abundances are spread relative to the center of the functional space, indicating the level of ecological niche differentiation and competition among species. A greater Fdiv value implies high niche differentiation among species, reduced niche overlap, and less resource competition. Moreover, RaoQ combines aspects of Fric and Fdiv and can help evaluate the degree of redundancy or complementarity among these functional diversity indices65,66.

Second, to assess the variations in community structures between the land use types, we applied the Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA). This analysis was performed using the adonis function of the vegan package67 in R63. PERMANOVA uses distance matrices to analyze variances across different sources, focusing specifically on the spatial positioning of group centroids rather than their dispersion. This method is highly effective in detecting differences in community composition resulting from various conditions68. By conducting PERMANOVA, we aimed to determine whether significant differences existed in the taxonomic and functional diversity of benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages across different land cover types based on the spatial distribution of centroids.

Third, to explore the distribution patterns of benthic macroinvertebrate species in relation to land-use types, we used NMDS with the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index to quantify compositional differences among communities. The NMDS method is well-suited for ecological community data, which are often characterized by many zeros and non-normal distributions, and is commonly applied to taxa such as benthic macroinvertebrates69. To identify the optimal configuration that reflects the true spatial distribution of species with minimal distortion, we employed the metaMDS function from the vegan package in R63, targeting the solution with the lowest stress value as the best representation of the data69,70. This allowed us to visually interpret the impact of land use on the community structure of benthic macroinvertebrates in a multidimensional space. Additionally, to understand the relationship between the observed patterns of benthic macroinvertebrate diversity and environmental variables within these land-use contexts, we conducted an analysis using the envfit function in the vegan package67 in R63. This analysis mapped environmental gradients onto the NMDS ordination results, providing insights into how specific environmental factors, such as water quality, are associated with the distribution of benthic macroinvertebrate species across different land-use types. This comprehensive approach enabled us to decipher the intricate interactions between land use, environmental conditions, and diversity of benthic macroinvertebrate communities.

Fourth, to ascertain the level of agreement between taxonomic and functional assemblages within the Han River Basin, we applied Procrustes Rotation Analysis (PRA) as outlined by Peres-Neto and Jackson71. This analysis used the NMDS ordination results obtained in the previous step, overlaying the NMDS ordination outputs of taxonomic and functional diversity. This analytical approach overlays two distinct sets of ordination data, adjusting one through various transformations, such as scaling, rotation, reflection, and expansion, to minimize the total squared deviations (m2) between their ordination points72. A reduced m2 value implies higher congruence between the two community datasets. Importantly, the statistical correlation coefficient (R) derived from this minimized m2 served as a quantitative indicator of the degree of community agreement, where a larger R-value signified stronger congruence between taxonomic and functional assemblages. Significance testing for PRA was conducted using the protest method73, which uses a permutation test (n = 999) to evaluate whether the observed m2 is significantly lower than expected by random chance. This involves randomly shuffling one of the ordination matrices, recalculating the m² for each permutation, and assessing whether the proportion of permutations with an m² greater than or equal to the observed m² provides a statistically significant result, as indicated by the calculated p-value. This comprehensive approach statistically validates the extent to which taxonomic and functional community structures reflect similar spatial patterns, strengthening interpretations of biodiversity responses to land-use changes74. Taxonomic data were log-transformed to minimize the influence of abundant species and subsequently standardized before NMDS analysis, which provided the ordination results for both the visualization of community-level patterns and subsequent Procrustes Rotation Analysis (PRA) to assess congruence between taxonomic and functional diversity. Functional diversity metrics were calculated based on standardized trait data without additional transformations.

Finally, we employed the Indicator Species Analysis (IndVal) method to extract indicator species and traits of benthic macroinvertebrates that were distinctively associated with different types of land use (forest, agriculture, and urban)75,76. The IndVal was calculated from the relative abundance and frequency of the species (or trait), producing a value scale ranging from 0 (no indication) to 100 (perfect indication)77,78. We considered species with an IndVal greater than 25, which also showed statistical significance (p < 0.05), as indicator species75,79. We conducted a site randomization test to assess the significance by redistributing sites across groups with 9999 permutations. This approach allowed us to pinpoint specific trait categories that were indicative of certain land-use groups based on their unique ecological characteristics. Only trait categories that demonstrated a significant (p < 0.05) maximal indicator value for a particular land-use type were designated as indicator trait categories for that specific group of sites. This strategy was instrumental in identifying the ecological signatures of benthic macroinvertebrate communities across various landscapes, thereby providing insights into how land use influences community composition and trait distribution. The ‘labdsv’ package80 for the indicator species analysis was applied in R63.
