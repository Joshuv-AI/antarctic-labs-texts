The impact of death attitudes on death anxiety among nursing interns: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Han Zhou, Yue Shen, Bo Zheng, Yonghui Wan, Xuezeng Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis multi-center, cross-sectional study investigated death attitudes and death anxiety among nursing interns in China, explored their internal correlations, and identified independent predictors of high death anxiety to provide an empirical basis for enhancing death education and end-of-life care quality.MethodsA survey was conducted among 400 nursing interns from four medical institutions using an online platform. Validated instruments included the Death Attitude Profile–Revised (DAP-R) and the Chinese Version of the Templer Death Anxiety Scale (CT-DAS). High death anxiety was defined as a CT-DAS score > 35. Multivariate logistic regression was employed to identify factors significantly associated with death anxiety.ResultsOf the participants, 27.5% were classified as having high death anxiety. Correlation analysis revealed that natural acceptance was negatively associated with death anxiety (r = -0.252, P < 0.001), while fear of death (r = 0.508), death avoidance (r = 0.274), approach acceptance (r = 0.200), and escape acceptance (r = 0.225) showed significant positive correlations (all P < 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified female gender as a significant risk factor (OR = 4.17, 95% CI: 1.45–12.02, P = 0.008). Fear of death (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.26–1.54, P < 0.001), approach acceptance (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84–0.96, P = 0.002)and escape acceptance (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.05–1.28, P = 0.003) were associated with death anxiety.ConclusionsNursing interns in China exhibit a relatively high prevalence of death anxiety, which is closely linked to gender, clinical knowledge, and cognitive orientations toward death. Higher levels of fear of death and escape acceptance are associated with increased death anxiety, whereas approach acceptance appears to be protective. It is imperative to integrate structured, experiential death education into nursing curricula to enhance interns’ psychological resilience and improve the delivery of end-of-life care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

High-quality end-of-life care is widely recognised as a core component of modern nursing practice and an essential indicator of healthcare quality. Beyond technical proficiency and symptom control, the delivery of effective end-of-life care critically depends on nurses’ psychological resilience and their capacity for emotional regulation when confronting death and dying [1]. Existing evidence suggests that repeated exposure to patient death imposes substantial emotional labour on nurses, often leading to heightened psychological stress, compassion fatigue, and professional burnout, which in turn compromise care quality and workforce sustainability [1, 2]. Empirical studies have shown that nurses who lack adequate preparation for end-of-life care are more likely to experience intense emotional labour, psychological distress, and diminished coping competence when exposed to patient death [3, 4]. These findings highlight that death-related psychological responses are not peripheral concerns, but central determinants of end-of-life care quality. Thus, the psychological responses of nursing personnel to death—particularly death attitudes and death-related anxiety—represent a fundamental, yet frequently underappreciated, determinant of end-of-life care quality.

Within this broader context, nursing interns constitute a uniquely vulnerable and understudied subgroup. Unlike fully qualified nurses, nursing interns occupy a critical transitional phase characterised by the rupture and reconstruction of knowledge as they move from classroom-based theoretical learning to real-world clinical practice. During this period, abstract concepts of death acquired through education are confronted for the first time with concrete clinical experiences of dying patients and death events [5]. This initial exposure often challenges interns’ pre-existing death attitudes, testing their emotional coping strategies and psychological preparedness. How nursing interns interpret, accept, avoid, or fear death at this formative stage may shape not only their immediate anxiety responses but also their long-term professional identity, emotional resilience, and trajectory of mental health within the nursing profession [6].

Previous studies have demonstrated that death attitudes are significantly associated with death anxiety among practising nurses and other healthcare professionals [7, 8]. Research among medical students has similarly reported elevated levels of death anxiety and its correlation with maladaptive death attitudes [9]. In addition, studies of newly graduated nurses suggest that inadequate coping-with-death competence and heightened death anxiety during early clinical practice may adversely affect clinical performance and emotional well-being [2, 10]. However, despite these findings, a critical knowledge gap remains. Existing literature has not sufficiently clarified how the distinctive transition from campus-based learning to first clinical exposure moderates or reshapes the relationship between death attitudes and death anxiety specifically among nursing interns [11, 12]. In other words, it remains unclear whether psychological mechanisms identified in experienced nurses or preclinical students operate similarly during this pivotal early clinical stage.

To address this gap, the present multi-centre cross-sectional study investigated death attitudes and death anxiety among nursing interns in China using validated instruments, including the Death Attitude Profile–Revised (DAP-R) and the Chinese Version of the Templer Death Anxiety Scale (CT-DAS) [13, 14]. The specific aims were: (1) to assess the prevalence and level of death anxiety among nursing interns across multiple clinical settings; (2) to examine the independent associations between the five dimensions of death attitudes and death anxiety; and (3) to identify key demographic and educational factors associated with death anxiety. We hypothesised that, after controlling for relevant covariates, a natural acceptance attitude toward death would be a significant negative predictor of death anxiety, whereas fear of death would emerge as the strongest positive predictor. Clarifying these relationships may provide an empirical foundation for optimising death education and psychological support strategies for nursing interns, thereby contributing to improved quality of end-of-life care.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multi-center, cross-sectional study was conducted across four tertiary medical institutions in China: Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University, West China Hospital of Sichuan University, Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital, and Tibet Autonomous Region People’s Hospital. Data were collected via an online survey between January 2025 and April, 2025. Nursing interns were eligible if they had completed at least two months of clinical rotation. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all respondents provided informed consent prior to accessing the questionnaire. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (No. 2025 K-K061 (C01)). I confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Data collection and instrumentation

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study variables, derived from the questionnaire, included the following: age (recorded and analyzed as a continuous variable); gender (male = 1, female = 2); household registration (rural = 1, urban = 2); educational level (junior college = 1, undergraduate = 2, postgraduate = 3); only-child status (no = 1, yes = 2); duration of clinical internship (≤ 6 months = 1, > 6 months = 2); family structure (nuclear = 1, extended = 2, single-parent or blended = 3); exposure to death education (no = 1, yes = 2); experience of danger within the past three years (no = 1, yes = 2); family attitude toward death (avoid discussion = 1, rarely discussed = 2, openly discussed = 3); knowledge of end-of-life care (unfamiliar = 1, familiar = 2).

Death attitude profile–revised (DAP-R)

Death attitudes were assessed using the five dimensions of the DAP-R (fear of death, death avoidance, natural acceptance, approach acceptance, and escape acceptance). Each dimension was analyzed separately as a continuous variable in subsequent analyses. The DAP-R was developed by Wong et al. in 1994 [14] and later translated and adapted into Chinese by Tang Lu et al. [15]. The scale comprises 32 items across five dimensions: fear of death, death avoidance, natural acceptance, approach acceptance, and escape acceptance. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Higher mean scores within each dimension indicate a stronger tendency toward the corresponding death attitude. The DAP-R has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.875 and a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.864, supporting its reliability and cultural appropriateness for assessing death attitudes among Chinese nursing personnel [16].

Templer death anxiety scale (CT-DAS)

The Death Anxiety Scale (DAS) was originally developed by Templer in 1967 [17], and subsequently revised and translated into Chinese as the CT-DAS by Yang Hong et al. [13]. The CT-DAS consists of 15 items across four dimensions: emotional response, stress and distress, time awareness, and cognition. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with nine items scored positively and six items scored in reverse (Items 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 15). Higher total scores indicate greater levels of death anxiety. A cutoff score of 35 was used to categorize participants into low and high death anxiety groups [18]. Scores > 35 have been considered indicative of high or intense death anxiety in Chinese populations, including nursing students and clinical populations [19, 20]. The Chinese version demonstrates satisfactory psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.71 and a test–retest reliability of 0.83 [13].

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using a power analysis for a multivariate logistic regression model, calculated with G*Power software (version 3.1) [21]. The analysis aimed to ensure sufficient statistical power (1-β) to detect a meaningful effect of key predictors on the likelihood of high death anxiety. Based on the methodology described by Hsieh et al. (1998) [22], we anticipated a medium effect size, corresponding to an Odds Ratio (OR) of approximately 0.5 for a protective factor (or 2.0 for a risk factor).

Setting the significance level (α) at 0.05 (two-tailed) and the desired statistical power at 80%, with an anticipated prevalence of high death anxiety (the event) of 25% in the study population, the calculation indicated that a minimum sample size of 279 participants was required. To account for potential invalid questionnaires and to increase the robustness of the model, we aimed to recruit a larger sample. Ultimately, a total of 400 complete questionnaires were collected, which provides over 90% power to detect the specified effect size, thus ensuring the study had adequate statistical power for the multivariate analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with ranges, while categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group differences between low and high death anxiety were assessed via chi-square tests, independent-samples t-tests, or Mann-Whitney U tests as appropriate. Spearman correlation was used to examine the relationship between death attitudes and anxiety. Independent predictors of death anxiety were identified using multivariable logistic regression with the enter method. All variables were included in the model based on their potential clinical relevance and evidence from previous literature. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model performance was evaluated using the Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients and Nagelkerke’s pseudo R2. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA), and two-tailed P values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics and death anxiety prevalence

A total of 400 nursing interns completed the survey (completion rate: 96.39%). The cohort had a mean age of 21.09 ± 1.76 years, with a predominant representation of female (88.5%) and Han ethnic (85.25%) participants. Based on the CT-DAS cutoff score of 35, 110 participants (27.5%) were identified as experiencing high levels of death anxiety.

Bivariate analysis (Table 1) revealed no significant differences in death anxiety across age, education level, or family structure (P > 0.05). However, participants in the low death anxiety group were more likely to be male (P = 0.020). Significant differences were also observed across all five dimensions of death attitudes. Compared with participants in the high death anxiety group, those in the low death anxiety group reported lower scores for fear of death (17.9 ± 4.2 vs. 21.9 ± 4.1, P < 0.001), death avoidance (14.2 ± 3.6 vs. 15.8 ± 3.2, P < 0.001), approach acceptance (25.5 ± 6.1 vs. 26.9 ± 5.6, P = 0.040), and escape acceptance (12.0 ± 3.8 vs. 13.2 ± 3.7, P = 0.006), but higher natural acceptance scores (19.2 ± 3.3 vs. 18.6 ± 2.8, P = 0.040).

Correlation between death attitudes and death anxiety

Spearman correlation analysis (Table 3) indicated that all five dimensions of death attitude were significantly associated with death anxiety (P < 0.001). “Natural acceptance” was the only dimension to show a significant negative correlation with death anxiety (r = -0.252, P < 0.001). In contrast, “fear of death” exhibited the strongest positive correlation (r = 0.508, P < 0.001), followed by death avoidance (r = 0.274), escape acceptance (r = 0.225), and approach acceptance (r = 0.200).

Female interns were significantly more likely to experience high death anxiety than males (OR = 4.17, 95% CI: 1.45–12.02, P = 0.008). Among the five dimensions of death attitudes, higher fear of death scores were independently associated with an increased likelihood of high death anxiety (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.26–1.54, P < 0.001). Similarly, higher escape acceptance scores were associated with greater odds of high death anxiety (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.05–1.28, P = 0.003). In contrast, higher approach acceptance scores were associated with a lower likelihood of high death anxiety (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84–0.96, P = 0.002). Death avoidance and natural acceptance were not independently associated with death anxiety after adjustment for potential confounders (both P > 0.05).

Discussion

This multi-centre cross-sectional study provides empirical evidence that death anxiety is a prominent psychological concern among nursing interns in China. Approximately 27.5% of participants exhibited high levels of death anxiety, and its key predictors included gender and specific dimensions of death attitudes. In particular, fear of death and escape acceptance were associated with a higher likelihood of death anxiety, whereas approach acceptance was associated with a lower likelihood of death anxiety. These findings extend existing research by clarifying how demographic characteristics, educational exposure, and cognitive–emotional orientations toward death jointly shape death anxiety at the early stage of clinical professionalisation.

The finding that female nursing interns exhibited significantly higher levels of death anxiety than male interns is consistent with previous studies among nursing and medical trainees [23]. Although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, several factors may contribute to this gender difference. Previous research has shown that women generally report higher levels of emotional sensitivity and empathy than men [24]. Greater emotional engagement with patients may increase vulnerability to distress when encountering suffering, dying, and death during clinical training. In addition, studies have suggested that women may be more likely to adopt emotion-focused coping strategies when facing stressful situations, which could influence their responses to death-related experiences [25]. Sociocultural factors may also play a role. In many healthcare settings, female nurses are often expected to provide substantial emotional support to patients and families, particularly in end-of-life care contexts. Such expectations may increase the emotional demands associated with clinical training. Furthermore, cultural attitudes toward death may influence how nursing interns perceive and cope with death-related situations [26]. However, because the present study did not directly assess empathy, coping styles, emotional labour, or cultural beliefs, these explanations should be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating psychological and sociocultural variables are needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying gender differences in death anxiety. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that female nursing interns may represent a group at increased risk of death anxiety and could benefit from targeted psychological support and coping-skills training during clinical education.

Although univariate analysis suggested familiarity with end-of-life care had differences between groups, this variable had borderline significant after controlling for covariates. This result still highlights the importance of death education but suggests that its effect may be more complex and potentially confounded by other psychological and attitudinal factors. In mainland China, death education and hospice care training remain largely theory-oriented, with limited opportunities for experiential learning or structured reflection [27, 28]. Consequently, nursing students often enter clinical placements with insufficient preparation for the emotional and communicative demands of caring for dying patients. In the present study, only about half of the interns reported being familiar with end-of-life care, a proportion lower than that reported in studies from countries where palliative care education is more systematically integrated into healthcare training [29]. This educational gap may exacerbate uncertainty, fear, and emotional distress when interns encounter death for the first time in clinical settings. Our findings still support the value of death education in principle [30], but suggest that knowledge alone may be insufficient to independently reduce death anxiety, and that integration with experiential and psychological training may be necessary.

Natural acceptance reflects the cognitive and emotional integration of death as an inevitable part of life, which has been associated in prior literature with emotional maturity and psychological adaptation [31]. However, in the present adjusted model, it was not associated with death anxiety, suggesting that its relationship with death anxiety may overlap with other death attitude dimensions rather than exerting an independent effect. Similarly, death avoidance did not demonstrate an independent association, indicating that avoidance may function more as a secondary or overlapping coping tendency rather than a distinct protective factor in this population.

In contrast, fear of death remained the strongest independent predictor of death anxiety. This maladaptive cognitive orientation, characterized by heightened threat appraisal and avoidance of mortality-related thoughts, has consistently been associated with elevated death anxiety in healthcare populations [32, 33]. In addition, escape acceptance was associated with death anxiety, suggesting that this dimension may reflect psychological distress or a desire to escape suffering rather than adaptive acceptance [34]. Approach acceptance was associated with death anxiety, which may reflect a more meaning-oriented or transcendental acceptance of death that reduces existential threat perception [35].

With regard to death avoidance, although it showed a positive correlation with death anxiety in bivariate analysis, it did not retain an independent protective effect in the fully adjusted model when fear of death was included. This finding suggests that death avoidance should not be interpreted as a protective psychological mechanism, but rather as a passive or maladaptive coping tendency closely intertwined with fear of death [36]. From the perspective of clinical training, this pattern may reflect a structural feature of internship education: nursing interns are often instructed primarily in technical procedures, while meaningful engagement with dying patients, emotional support, and end-of-life communication are frequently handled by senior staff. As a result, interns may learn to avoid direct confrontation with death-related experiences rather than develop active coping strategies.

Moreover, death avoidance may also be shaped by broader sociocultural influences. Traditional Chinese culture has long emphasised the avoidance of death-related discourse, viewing death as an inauspicious or taboo topic. Such cultural norms may implicitly reinforce avoidance-based coping strategies among nursing interns, particularly in the absence of systematic death education [37]. Therefore, death avoidance in this context is better understood as a culturally and institutionally shaped response rather than an independent protective factor. Educational interventions should aim to gradually reduce avoidance by fostering open discussion, reflective practice, and acceptance-based coping, rather than reinforcing silence or emotional disengagement.

The findings of this study have important implications for nursing education and internship management. At the educational level, nursing curricula should move beyond predominantly didactic instruction and incorporate experiential, student-centred approaches to death education [38]. Simulation-based learning, reflective group discussions, grief counselling workshops, and supervised exposure to end-of-life care have been shown to reduce death anxiety and promote adaptive death attitudes among nursing students [38, 39].Such approaches may facilitate the development of emotional resilience and more adaptive death-related cognitions. At the clinical management level, internship programmes should establish routine psychological support mechanisms for nursing interns, particularly for female interns who appear to be at higher risk of death anxiety. Structured supervision, peer support groups, and opportunities for guided reflection may help interns process emotionally challenging experiences and prevent the accumulation of unaddressed psychological stress. These measures may ultimately contribute to improved professional well-being and higher-quality end-of-life care.

From a neuropsychological perspective, death anxiety has been linked to hyperactivation of brain regions involved in threat detection and emotional regulation, including the amygdala and anterior cingulate cortex [40]. Educational interventions that increase familiarity with death-related processes may reduce the novelty and perceived threat of such stimuli, thereby attenuating amygdala reactivity and promoting cognitive reappraisal through prefrontal regulatory mechanisms. Accordingly, incorporating interactive and experiential learning approaches—such as simulation-based training, reflective exercises, mindfulness practices, and supervised clinical exposure—may not only enhance nursing interns’ professional preparedness for end-of-life care but also strengthen top-down regulation of fear responses and support more adaptive death-related cognition.

This study has several notable strengths. First, the multicenter design enhances the generalizability of findings across diverse clinical settings and educational environments in China. Second, the study specifically focuses on nursing interns at a critical transitional phase, capturing their unique experiences of encountering death for the first time in clinical practice—an understudied population in previous research. Third, validated measurement tools were used to assess death anxiety and death attitudes, ensuring reliability and internal consistency. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, limiting the ability to determine whether death attitudes directly influence death anxiety or vice versa. Second, non-probability sampling may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting external validity. Third, the study relied on self-reported questionnaires, which could be affected by social desirability bias. Despite these limitations, the study provides important empirical evidence to inform educational and psychological interventions for nursing interns.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that death anxiety is prevalent among nursing interns in China and is significantly associated with gender and multiple dimensions of death attitudes. Female interns were more likely to experience high death anxiety. Among the death attitude dimensions, fear of death and escape acceptance were independently associated with increased death anxiety, whereas approach acceptance was associated with a lower likelihood of high death anxiety. These findings underscore the importance of integrating structured death education and targeted psychological support into nursing training programmes. Promoting adaptive attitudes toward death and addressing death-related anxiety during this critical stage of professional development may strengthen psychological resilience and ultimately improve the quality of end-of-life care.
