Tumor Mass Is a Better Prognostic Factor Than Consolidation-to-Tumor Ratio in Invasive Lung Adenocarcinoma Manifest Difficult-to-Measure Solid Portion
Authors: Jialiang Wen, Yichen Dong, Juemin Yu, Tao Chen, Xinchen Shen, Wei Li, Xiwen Sun, Jiajun Deng, Yunlang She, Chang Chen, Dong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate the long-term prognosis and related factors in patients who manifest part-solid invasive lung adenocarcinoma with difficult-to-measure solid portions.MethodsPatients with resected clinical stage I part-solid invasive lung adenocarcinoma in a single institution were retrospectively enrolled. They were divided into measurable and difficult-to-measure groups according to whether the radiologically solid portion was difficult to measure. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to evaluate the outcomes. Cox analysis was used to explore the prognostic factors. Tumor nodules were segmented using 3D Slicer software to extract quantitative features.ResultsOf the 1240 patients, 225 (18.2%) were classified into the difficult-to-measure group. The intraclass correlation coefficient revealed that two readers had an excellent agreement on solid size in the measurable group (0.88), but not in the difficult-to-measure group (0.65). Compared with the measurable group, these tumors were larger and exhibited comparable overall survival (10 y 95.0% versus 94.2%; p = 0.341). The prognosis of the difficult-to-measure group was similar to that of cT1a or cT1b in the measurable group. consolidation-to-tumor ratio was an independent risk factor for patients with easy-to-measure solid components but not for patients whose solid parts were difficult to measure. Multivariate analyses revealed that tumor mass (>1.62 g) was an independent factor for worse recurrence-free survival in the measurable or difficult-to-measure group.ConclusionsPatients with invasive adenocarcinoma manifesting as part-solid nodules whose solid components were difficult to measure revealed favorable outcomes. Tumor mass was a better prognostic indicator than consolidation-to-tumor ratio for this population.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma that radiologically manifests as a part-solid nodule is common in clinical practice. In clinical stage IA patients with part-solid lesions, the prognosis is better than that of patients with pure-solid lesions because of the presence of ground-glass opacity (GGO) components.1,2 Compared with pure ground-glass nodules, part-solid nodules grow faster and have more malignant and invasive potential, which should be paid more attention to in the clinical follow-up.3,4 In 2016, the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer staging committee recommended that the solid or invasive size should be applied for the T descriptor rather than the whole length of the part-solid nodule.5 Several studies also reported that a larger consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) or solid component was associated with greater tumor aggressiveness, such as the development of lymph node metastasis and poorly differentiated patterns, leading to a worse prognosis.6, 7, 8

However, the solid component of some part-solid nodules exhibiting complex solid distributions is difficult to identify and measure in clinical work. For patients with these nodules, it is unclear whether clinical T categorization on the basis of the current method of measuring the maximum diameter of the solid component is appropriate. It has been reported by Matsunaga et al.9 that lung cancer that manifests radiologically difficult-to-measure consolidations and GGO components tended to be pathologically less invasive. Fukui et al.10 concluded that lymph node metastasis and lymphovascular invasion were rare for nodules for which the measurement of the solid part is challenging. To our knowledge, no study has described the long-term prognosis and explored the prognostic factors for this population.

In our research, we reviewed the patients with invasive lung adenocarcinoma with radiographic manifestations of part-solid nodules and investigated the outcome and prognostic factors on the basis of the nodules with measurable or difficult-to-measure solid parts.

Materials and Methods

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed clinical stage I patients with invasive lung adenocarcinoma undergoing complete surgical resection between 2011 and 2015 in Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. Chest computed tomography (CT) scans were used to evaluate the primary lesion and lymph node status. For patients with swollen lymph nodes (short-axis diameter greater than one centimeter), positron emission tomography–CT, or endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration was performed to confirm clinical N0 status. Brain magnetic resonance imaging, abdominal B-ultrasound, and isotopic bone scanning were performed as needed to exclude metastatic disease. The histologic subtypes and tumor stagings were based on the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer, American Thoracic Society, and European Respiratory Society classification of the invasive adenocarcinoma11 and the eighth edition of the TNM staging system,12 respectively. The ethics committee (Institutional Review Board of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital) approved this retrospective study (Ethical Batch Number: L22-219; 8 June 2022). Written informed consent in the study was waived. The surgical details and follow-up strategy were described in Supplementary Materials.

The exclusion criteria were the following: (1) pure-solid or GGO nodules; (2) no thin-slice CT images; (3) history of other malignancy within 5 years; and (4) incomplete clinical or survival record.

Radiologic Assessment

The CT system and scanning parameters were described in previous studies.13,14 The preoperative CT images of all patients were reviewed by two senior radiologists (Xiwen Sun and Wei Li). GGO was defined as a component with a mildly increased density that did not obscure the vascular and bronchial structures, whereas solid components completely covered the underlying lung parenchyma. The part-solid nodules have both GGO and solid components. The radiologists identified the GGO component and measured the longest diameters of tumors and solid portions under the lung window (level: −450 Hounsfield units [HU]; width: 1250 HU). The sizes of the tumor and solid portion were determined by the average values of the two observers. CTR was calculated as the ratio of the maximum length of consolidation to the maximum tumor size. According to Fukui et al.10 there were four reasons why the measurement of solid size was difficult to judge (except for severe diffuse lung diseases and emphysematous lung): (1) multifocal group (multifocal solid portion); (2) reticular group (solid portion in a reticular pattern); (3) gradation group (gradual change in density); and (4) vessel on border group (overlap of the solid portion with vessels in the peripheral area). If there were inconsistent observations on whether the solid portion was difficult to measure, three thoracic surgeons (Juemin Yu, Jialiang Wen, and Yichen Dong) joined them and reached a consensus after discussion. The clinical T stage of measurable nodules was classified according to the maximum length of the solid portion. The mediastinal window (level: 30 to 60 HU; width: 400 to 600 HU) was used to assess the lymph nodes and help evaluate the solid component.

CT Image Analysis

The thin-slice CT images (1 mm) were imported into the 3D Slicer program version 5.0.3.15 Two senior radiologists with experience in segmenting 2000 chest CT images by 3D Slicer manually segmented the tumor areas in three views (coronal, axial, and sagittal images). The margin delineation and exclusion of the results were double-checked by Dr. Wen and Dr. Chen. All readers were unaware of the clinical and prognostic data. The mean attenuation value was the average value, avoiding the large vessels and bronchi. The tumor volume (cm3) and CT mean attenuation (HU) were obtained using “Segment Statistics” in 3D Slicer. Because the CT attenuation value is roughly linear with the density,16 the tumor mass could be calculated as follows: mass (g) = volume × [(mean attenuation + 1000) × 0.001].17,18

Statistical Analysis

All statistical computations were carried out using R software version 3.6.3 (R Core Team, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), MedCalc software (version 20.218, Ostend, Belgium), and SPSS 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, NY). A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Continuous variables were described by interquartile ranges (first quartile to third quartile) and compared by the Mann-Whitney U test. The chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to evaluate categorical variables. Receiver operating characteristic analyses were performed to analyze the efficacy in diagnosing recurrence by calculating the area under the curve. Interobserver variability analyses for tumor and solid size measurements were conducted using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Bland-Altman plots. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from tumor resection to recurrence or death. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death. The analysis of RFS and OS was conducted using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. The p values of multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamin and Hochberg method. Multivariable Cox regression was used to identify the independent factors affecting patients’ survival.

Results

Survival Outcomes

The median follow-up duration was 98.8 months (range from the lower to upper quartiles: 88.0 to 110.2 mo). A total of 99 patients experienced relapse or death; the entire cohort’s 5-year and 10-year RFS rates were 94.4% and 90.0%, respectively. There were 61 patients who died; the 5-year and 10-year OS rates were 97.7% and 94.4%, respectively. The difficult-to-measure group exhibited comparable RFS (Fig. 3A) (5-y 95.3% versus 94.2%, 10-y 90.2% versus 90.0%, p = 0.470) and OS (Fig. 3B) (5-y 98.1% versus 97.7%, 10-y 95.0% versus 94.2%, p = 0.341) to the measurable group, even after adjusting for other clinical factors (Table 2). Prognostic Factors

CTR and solid size are deemed important in patients with lung cancer with radiologic part-solid lesions.19 The Cox analyses revealed that CTR could be an independent factor for prognosis in the entire and measurable cohorts, but not for the difficult-to-measure group (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2). The area under the curve of solid size and CTR for predicting recurrence was 0.659 and 0.644 in the entire cohort, 0.696 and 0.697 in the measurable group, whereas it was only 0.514 and 0.563 in the difficult-to-measure group (Supplementary Fig. 2). Limited predictive value for patients’ survival of CTR was observed in the patients whose solid portion was difficult to measure.

As for the operative procedure, we also found that there was no significant difference in long-term survival between lobectomy and sublobectomy in all patients, the measurable and difficult-to-measure groups (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Prognostic Value of Tumor Mass

To further explore the prognostic predictor suitable for the entire cohort, a quantitative CT analysis for volume, density, and mass was performed (Supplementary Table 3). Typical manual segmentation of nodules with difficult-to-measure solid components is illustrated in Figures 4A to D. We found that tumor mass could predict recurrence better than whole volume and mean attenuation in the entire, measurable, or difficult-to-measure cohort (Fig. 4E). The best cutoff value determined by the greatest Youden index in the entire cohort was 1.62 g. The worst RFS and OS were observed for patients with tumor mass greater than 1.62 g in the entire (p < 0.001), measurable (p < 0.001), and difficult-to-measure groups (RFS: p < 0.001; OS: p = 0.007) (Fig. 5). Discussion

In our cohort, 18.1% of patients exhibited the difficult-to-measure solid component with a larger whole size than the measurable group. Nevertheless, Fukui et al.10 reported that the difficult-to-measure population accounted for 25.4% of all part-solid nodules and had a smaller tumor size and less pathologic invasiveness than the easy-to-measure group. One possible reason for these discrepancies lies in the difference in the populations studied. Compared with Fukui’s research, we focused on patients with invasive patterns who might experience recurrence and excluded those diagnosed with nonmalignancy, adenocarcinoma in situ, or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, with a nearly 100% 10-year disease-specific survival rate.20 The proportion of benign lesions, adenocarcinoma in situ, or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma was higher in the difficult-to-measure group than in the measurable group,10 and the size of these lesions was generally smaller than that of invasive adenocarcinomas.21 Moreover, we found that the size of many nodules with reticular or multifocal solid composition (56% of the difficult-to-measure group) was generally large.

Our results revealed that the outcome of the difficult-to-measure group was comparable to that of the measurable group and did not differ from that of cT1a or cT1b easy-to-measure patients. A larger CTR was an independent risk factor for patients with measurable solid components but not for patients whose solid part was difficult to measure. In one way, measuring the maximum diameter of the solid size was a subjective and less accurate process, which might be responsible for the moderate interobservational reproducibility when evaluating these nodules. Because it was not easy for clinicians to evaluate the clinical T stage of tumors with difficult-to-measure consolidation size in practical work accurately, classifying this population as cT1a or cT1b might be effective and simple, just like the nodules with measurable solid portions. In another way, measurement inaccuracy might lead to the limitation of the predictive efficacy of CTR in the difficult-to-measure group. Interestingly, previous literature reported inconsistent results about the prognostic values of CTR in patients with part-solid nodules. Hattori et al.22 reviewed 448 patients with surgically resected NSCLC who manifested part-solid nodules in the CT images and found that neither solid component size nor CTR was an independent factor for OS. Ye et al.23 also reported limited prognostic values of CTR in patients with part-solid nodules. Several researchers also reported that all part-solid tumors have a comparable outcome with cT1a solid tumors.14,24 However, there were some studies supporting CTR as an important prognostic indicator in part-solid tumors.2,6,7 The differences in the proportion of nodules with difficult-to-measure solid size and measurement errors in different studies may explain the inconsistent findings above. According to our long-term survival results, there was no significant difference in the OS of cT1a and cT1b in the measurable group, and the prognosis of cT1c-cT2a was markedly worse than that of others. Therefore, we considered that the solid size does affect the invasiveness of part-solid tumors and, to some extent, the prognosis. However, the relationship between the CTR or solid size and tumor aggressiveness may not be simply linear. In addition, although CTR and solid size have an impact on tumor aggressiveness, they did not affect the independent predictive effect of GGO components on a favorable prognosis in clinical stage I NSCLC.1,14 In some part-solid nodules, the difficulty of CTR measurement mainly stems from challenges in delineating the consolidation portion, but the presence of GGO can still be clearly and objectively assessed.

Many clinical trials, such as Japan Clinical Oncology Group 0802, have explored the feasibility of sublobectomy on the basis of different tumor sizes and CTR,25, 26, 27 but have failed to focus on these tumors with poor CTR assessment, which cannot be classified as CTR greater than 0.5 or CTR less than or equal to 0.5. Our results suggested that, after adjusting for confounders, sublobectomy might also bring up long-term benefits comparable to lobectomy for this special group with a favorable prognosis. A prospective randomized controlled study was required to validate this finding.

The CTR has strong prognostic value for part-solid tumors with easy-to-measure solid components but not in the difficult-to-measure group. An interesting point is how to find a novel predictor suitable for all part-solid nodules that helps to improve risk stratification. It has been reported that the mass of nodules was correlated closely with high-grade pathologic subtypes and could predict prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma.28 Some studies also suggested that mass was an independent predictor of early growth17 and invasive adenocarcinoma in pure GGO nodules.29,30 Ying Zhu et al.30 reported that the prognostic values of solid mass and volume were more accurate than those of maximal solid size in part-solid or pure-solid lung adenocarcinoma. For the difficult-to-measure group, it was difficult to accurately determine the maximum diameter of solid size on a two-dimensional plane, whereas the mass on the basis of three-dimensional imaging, could better reflect the nodule size and density. Therefore, we hypothesized whether the tumor mass could predict the outcomes of patients with difficult-to-measure solid components, playing a role similar to that of CTR in the measurable group. Our results revealed that a tumor mass of more than 1.62 g could well discriminate the worst RFS or OS of patients. Tumor mass (>1.62 g) was an independent risk factor better than CTR in the entire, measurable, or difficult-to-measure cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the impact of tumor mass on the prognosis of populations with part-solid lung invasive adenocarcinoma. The quantitative CT factor could assist doctors in predicting the prognosis of these patients in clinical work, obtaining additional prognostic information beyond pathologic staging, especially for those with part-solid adenocarcinoma, where radiologic parameters play an important predictive role,19 and making appropriate treatment and follow-up strategies.

There are some limitations in our study. First, the reproducibility of mass measurements across observers and software platforms remains unproven, as it was not quantitatively assessed (e.g., by ICC). Future work must prioritize a rigorous multicenter, multiplatform, and multiobserver validation study. Artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning-based automated segmentation models, might be a promising tool to improve the consistency and efficacy of tumor mass measurement. Second, because this is a retrospective study on the basis of a single institution, selective biases and confounding are inevitable. Meanwhile, the proposed tumor mass cutoff (>1.62 g) is data-driven and may not be directly transferable to other populations. External validation is necessary to confirm its generalizability. Third, identifying the difficult-to-measure group is a subjective and visual process, although at least two researchers are involved in this evaluation, and the reasons for the measurement difficulties can be objectively explained.

In conclusion, patients with invasive adenocarcinomas radiologically manifesting as part-solid nodules with difficult-to-measure solid size have a favorable long-term prognosis. The prognostic value of CTR is limited for this population, whereas tumor mass is a better indicator for risk stratification.
