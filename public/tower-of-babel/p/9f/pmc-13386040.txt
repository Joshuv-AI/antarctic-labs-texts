Enhanced home-based transcranial direct-current stimulation (tDCS) for depression comorbid with borderline personality disorder (BPD): study protocol for a pilot double-blinded randomised controlled trial (TENTADIS)
Authors: Lionel Cailhol, Kamilia Soltani, David Benrimoh, Martin Blay, Sebastien Brodeur, Jérôme Brunelin, Caroline Dussault, Roxane Hoyer, Jillian Mills, Simon Poirier, Stephane Potvin, David Roy, Olivier Roy, Anthony C Ruocco, Claudia Savard, Samuel St-Amour, Steven Laureys

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is the most common comorbidity among individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD), affecting over 85% of patients and leading to high recurrence rates and resistance to treatment. Traditional pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions often show limited efficacy in this population, highlighting the need for innovative treatment strategies. Emerging evidence suggests that the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of both MDD and BPD. In addition, non-invasive brain stimulation, particularly transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS), has shown promising results in alleviating depression and improving BPD symptoms when targeting the DLPFC. The development of home-based tDCS presents new opportunities for accessible and cost-effective interventions. However, no study has specifically investigated its effects on MDD in the context of BPD.Methods and analysisThis double-blind randomised controlled trial will assess the feasibility and explore the efficacy of home-based tDCS in reducing depressive symptoms in BPD patients who are experiencing moderate to severe depressive episodes. A total of 60 participants will be randomised, following a 2-week online psychoeducation, to receive either active or sham tDCS in 14 sessions over 1 week. The primary outcome is the feasibility of home-based tDCS in remote and urban areas. The secondary aim is to obtain a preliminary assessment of the efficacy of 14 home-based tDCS sessions in reducing depressive symptoms in BPD patients with moderate to severe depressive episodes and BPD symptoms. Exploratory analyses will evaluate the impact of tDCS on neuropsychological functioning, physical activity and substance use. Sociodemographic variables will be considered in predicting treatment response. All assessments will be conducted at baseline, post-treatment and during follow-ups through 3 months after treatment ends.Ethics and disseminationThis study has received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et de services sociaux de l’Est-de-l’Île-de-Montréal (CIUSSS-EMTL) (approval number: MP-12-2025-4050). All participants will provide informed consent prior to inclusion. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and knowledge translation activities targeting clinicians, researchers and mental health stakeholders.Trial registration numberNCT06972368.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the most common comorbid conditions in individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD), affecting over 85% of patients and showing a 90% recurrence rate.1 Co-occurrence of depression and BPD is associated with an increased risk of psychosocial dysfunction and self-harming behaviours, and can hinder the patient’s ability to engage in treatment, compared with patients with BPD only.2 Moreover, patients with both MDD and BPD often present worse outcomes in MDD treatments, both psychotherapeutic and pharmacological, compared with individuals with MDD only.3 4 Despite this concerning situation, there is still a lack of effective treatment strategies targeting both disorders in comorbid cases, emphasising the urgent need for new therapeutic approaches. In this context, improving our understanding of the neurobiological bases could help develop new therapeutic strategies. For example, we can emphasise the specific role of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) in both pathophysiologies. Neuroimaging studies have identified abnormal activity in the DLPFC as a hallmark of dysfunction and part of a broader network dysregulation involved in mood regulation in both MDD and BPD.3–11 Specifically, in MDD, an imbalance between a hypoactive left DLPFC and a hyperactive right DLPFC has been consistently reported. In BPD, hyperactivation of the left DLPFC has also been observed in emotional contexts.5 The DLPFC is indeed a key node in affective regulation and executive control, emerging as a convergent site of dysfunction in both MDD and BPD. This shared pathophysiological substrate renders the DLPFC a strategic therapeutic target.

Non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) offers a means to modulate these dysregulated circuits. By leveraging the DLPFC’s central role in top-down regulation, neuromodulation may help recalibrate affective and cognitive processes that are disrupted across both MDD and BPD.

NIBS is an emerging therapeutic tool that can modulate neurophysiological activity in specific brain areas and networks.6 Some of these techniques have been used in targeting the DLPFC, showing their promising effectiveness for reducing the debilitating depressive symptoms in both MDD and BPD populations.7–11 These results were observed on depression scales following neuromodulation applied to both the right and left DLPFC, particularly using transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS). Stimulation of the left DLPFC with anodal tDCS has been related to favourable outcomes in MDD treatment during the acute phase (low to moderate effect size: 0.36 to 0.46)12 13 and follow-up assessments (large effect size: 0.81),14 regardless of the position of the cathode. Additionally, tDCS targeting the left DLPFC not only demonstrates moderate efficacy in alleviating depressive symptoms but also suggests a promising transdiagnostic neural mechanism underlying depression across clinical conditions, particularly in BPD.15 A large effect size on depressive symptoms was also reported in a recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) involving 50 individuals with BPD using anodal stimulation over F4.16 tDCS appears to be well accepted,12 with participants frequently reporting a sense of helpfulness and gratitude and experiencing few side effects.17 Dropout rates and the frequency of adverse effects are similar between the active and sham tDCS groups in RCTs. Only mild side effects are reported, such as itching, redness and tingling.18 Similarly, tDCS has been tested in individuals with BPD and shows promising effects in reducing affective instability and impulsivity and improving other associated neuropsychological deficits such as executive dysfunctions.8 19 20 Two primary anodal stimulation sites have been explored on the DLPFC: the left DLPFC and right DLPFC, with variations in intensity and duration, although most studies have used 2 mA for 20 min.13

Beyond its clinical promise, tDCS also offers distinct practical advantages that broaden its applicability in real-world contexts.21 22 These include its portability, ease of use, relatively low cost and favourable safety profile. Among the numerous strengths of this technique, one particularly compelling development is the evolution of tDCS into a home-based format, which increases access for people in rural areas. In these settings, access to specialised neuromodulation treatments is often limited by geographic isolation, a shortage of trained professionals and the logistical burden of repeated clinic visits, factors that disproportionately hinder individuals outside urban centres from receiving timely and consistent care. This development facilitates innovative new approaches in the field of NIBS by offering interventions that may be cost-effective, well-tolerated and seamlessly integrated into patients’ daily routines. Nonetheless, the use of home-based treatments warrants careful consideration, as the lack of supervision may elevate the risk of skin-related adverse effects.23

Overall, tDCS appears safe and shows promising effects on symptomatic and neuropsychological outcomes in both MDD and BPD. However, it currently lacks sufficiently powered studies to draw definitive conclusions or accurately estimate the magnitude of its clinical and functional effects, especially in BPD. Its ease of use, low physical demand and potential for autonomous administration make it particularly well-suited for individuals with MDD, whose symptoms often include fatigue, psychomotor slowing and a lack of initiative—factors that can hinder adherence to traditional, clinic-based treatments. Thus, home-based tDCS not only reduces logistical barriers but also aligns with the behavioural and motivational constraints imposed by the disorder itself, offering a practical pathway to sustained therapeutic engagement. Although the pooled effect of home-based tDCS is supported by existing studies, heterogeneity across studies remains high, likely reflecting differences in techniques and levels of supervision.24

This therapeutic potential, combined with the unique adaptability of home-based delivery, highlights the need to evaluate tDCS not only in isolated clinical populations but specifically in individuals facing the dual burden of MDD and BPD. Although anodal tDCS targeting the left DLPFC has shown efficacy for treating MDD and BPD individually, no study to date has examined its effects on MDD when it co-occurs with BPD. To address this critical gap, the present study aims to assess the feasibility of home-based tDCS in remote and urban areas (primary objective). The secondary aim is to obtain a preliminary assessment of the efficacy of 14 home-based tDCS sessions in reducing depressive symptoms in BPD patients with moderate to severe depressive episodes and BPD symptoms. Exploratory objectives include assessing the impact on neuropsychological and psychosocial functioning, anxiety, physical activity, sleep disorders and addiction. Additionally, we aim to investigate the sociodemographic and clinical factors that are linked to the most favourable response to tDCS in addressing both depressive and BPD symptoms. We also aim to assess the feasibility of using a smartwatch as an outcome measure in this population. Finally, we intend to gather preliminary data on the effectiveness of an online psychoeducational programme specifically designed for BPD patients.

Methods

Trial design

The study is a double-blinded, randomised, sham-controlled trial with two parallel arms. The protocol was developed by an international team of experts in BPD, depression and neuromodulation. Notably, an individual with lived experience, including prior exposure to tDCS, was actively involved in the study’s conception and implementation.

Randomisation procedure and blinding

Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the active or sham tDCS group. Stratification is applied based on treatment-resistant depression (stage 3 or more on Antidepressant Treatment History Form22 23 25), age (18–25, 26–45 and more than 45) and sex assigned at birth (male or female). A centralised, computer-generated blocked randomisation with randomly selected block sizes of 4 and 6 will ensure allocation concealment.

Participants, investigators involved in the assessment, and statisticians will be blinded to the treatment arm. The research assistant will preprogramme the tDCS devices to deliver either active or sham stimulation using an identical interface. For sham stimulation, the current will ramp up and down over the first 30 s but remain inactive thereafter, consistent with standard blinding procedures in tDCS trials.26

To systematically assess the integrity of blinding, both participants and assessors will be asked at each post-stimulation visit to estimate the assigned condition (active vs sham) using a forced-choice question, followed by a confidence rating. These responses will be analysed using blinding indices (eg, Bang’s Blinding Index27) to quantify the success of the masking procedure.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria include adults aged 18 to 65 years residing in Quebec, fluency in French or English, access to a smartphone and internet, meeting Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth edition (DSM-IV) diagnostic criteria for BPD as assessed by the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Axis II (SCID-II),28 a clinician-administered interview designed to assess personality pathology (κ=0.50–0.75) and α=0.78–0.85) and presenting with a moderate major depressive episode according to SCID-5,29 a clinician-administered, semistructured interview designed to generate DSM-5-based diagnoses of current and lifetime clinical disorders, and the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) (score ≥20 at both baseline (V1) and prerandomisation (V3)).

Participants must be under the care of a prescribing physician or mental health professional, on a stable psychopharmacological regimen (no changes in the 4 weeks before inclusion), and not have initiated or terminated psychotherapy during that same period (occasional missed sessions are acceptable). Participants must be capable of providing informed consent, be able to use a remote tDCS device independently following demonstration and with visual support, and travel to a designated location in Quebec to pick up and return the device (urban and rural local community services or outpatient clinics).

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria are as follows: any contraindication for tDCS medical devices (including metallic implants in the head or heart, or other implants that may interfere with electrical stimulation), prior cerebrovascular surgery, scalp conditions interfering with electrode placement, history of bipolar disorder, current pregnancy, undergoing other neuromodulation treatments (eg, repetitive Trancranial Magnetic Stimulation, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) or implanted neurostimulation devices) or any social or medical condition that may limit the safe or effective autonomous use of a remote tDCS device (eg, homeless without any plan to use the device properly). Participants are excluded if, in the investigator’s judgement, they have significant visual or hearing impairments (even with corrective devices) that would interfere with their ability to read instructions, navigate the app interface or communicate effectively during remote sessions. Patients currently taking benzodiazepines regularly will be excluded, as these medications may reduce the effectiveness of tDCS. However, participants using benzodiazepines on a pro re nata basis, fewer than four times per week, will remain eligible. In addition, individuals with recent suicidal behaviour, current acute psychiatric instability or a significant risk of clinical deterioration requiring intensive care or crisis intervention will be excluded to ensure participant safety in the context of home-based administration.

Intervention

Psychoeducation

All participants will receive a psychoeducational intervention during a 2-week lead-in phase prior to randomisation. This phase is designed to enhance engagement, provide nonspecific therapeutic support and help stabilise expectations, thereby ensuring that any subsequent effects observed during the tDCS phase can be attributed with greater confidence to the neuromodulation itself. The 2-week duration also serves to confirm the persistence of depressive symptoms and differentiate a major depressive episode from transient mood fluctuations commonly associated with BPD. Psychoeducation for individuals living with BPD is effective30 even if online,31 with statistically significant effects observable several months later. The same applies to depression.

The online psychoeducation programme will consist of educational videos ranging from 8 to 14 min, accompanied by multiple-choice questions to assess comprehension. These videos will cover topics such as BPD, critical elements for therapeutic success, depression in the context of BPD, neuromodulation and tDCS.

Remote-tDCS and remote-sham-tDCS

Participants will undergo 14 sessions of either active or sham remote-tDCS administered twice daily over 7 consecutive days. Each session will last 30 min, with a minimum interval of 2 hours between successive sessions, preferably scheduled at the same time each day. The research team will teach the participant how to use the tDCS device during the on-site equipment pickup. A standardised procedure will confirm their understanding, including correct electrode placement. All stimulation parameters will be preprogrammed into the device; the participant must only enter an activation code to initiate the session. A built-in safety mechanism will prevent stimulation if the impedance level is not within the acceptable range. Then, the research team will provide daily on-demand supervision on weekdays. They will focus exclusively on the technical aspects of tDCS. The first remote session will be scheduled early in the week, no later than Wednesday, to ensure the session can be monitored appropriately. Adherence will also be objectively monitored through device-embedded logs, which automatically record session completion, stimulation duration and impedance levels. These data will be reviewed weekly by the research team to ensure proper device use.

For the active group, tDCS will be administered at an intensity of 2 mA, a dosage commonly used in clinical trials for depression due to its demonstrated efficacy and favourable tolerability profile.32 The total charge delivered over 1 week will amount to 48 coulombs, which has been established as the optimal dosage in depression studies.10 The 25 cm2 anodal stimulation will target the left DLPFC (at the F3 electrode site, according to the 10/20 ElectroEncephaloGraphy international system). Each session will include a 30 s ramp-up and ramp-down period to ensure a smooth application of the current. The 25 cm2 cathode will be placed over the right DLPFC (F4 electrode site). The choice of F3 as the stimulation site is based on its established relevance in treating depression and its demonstrated efficacy in studies involving BPD patients. The other half of the participants in the sham-tDCS condition will receive only a 30 s ramp-up and ramp-down at the beginning of each session, followed by no active stimulation for the remainder of the 30 min. This follows the standard research procedure commonly adopted in this field.14

The research team will prioritise participant safety during the initial session by conducting a thorough safety screening that includes questions about prior experiences with transcranial electrical or magnetic stimulation and any adverse events. They will closely monitor the first three sessions to ensure the proper use of the remote tDCS device. The first session will take place on-site, during which a specific checklist will assess the participant’s ability to operate the device.

One of the key challenges in tDCS research, particularly in home-based protocols, is the variability in participants’ brain states during stimulation sessions. Because tDCS modulates ongoing neural activity, its effects are strongly influenced by the participant’s behaviour during stimulation. Participants may become idle, distracted or drowsy without structured engagement, leading to inconsistent or suboptimal cortical activation. To address this limitation and enhance stimulation efficacy, we have incorporated two structured, clinically relevant tasks into each stimulation session to activate target neural circuits and reduce inter-individual variability. By integrating two tasks—one designed to activate the emotional regulation network and one aimed at executive function—we aim to optimise tDCS’s state-dependent effects and enhance its impact on the targeted brain networks. While complete control over home-session conditions is not feasible, this dual-task approach provides a structured framework that increases the likelihood of consistent and functionally meaningful cortical engagement during stimulation.

Scripted emotion regulation exercise

At baseline (V1), a research team member will conduct a semistructured interview with each participant to identify a recent situation involving moderate emotional dysregulation (rated 5 out of 10 on a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)). The interviewer will work with the participant to isolate the ‘apex moment’, that is, the turning point from emotional regulation to dysregulation. Based on this narrative, and with input from a clinician trained in Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), the team will develop a personalised 5–10 min script in which the apex moment is rewritten using evidence-based DBT skills (eg, opposite action, distress tolerance or mindful self-soothing). The participant reads this personalised script at the beginning of each stimulation session. This approach serves multiple purposes: (1) it elicits the neural network associated with emotional regulation, notably the DLPFC—the target of stimulation33; (2) it introduces and reinforces adaptive self-regulation strategies throughout the intervention and (3) it draws on validated clinical paradigms in BPD research that demonstrate activation of regulatory prefrontal circuits during guided imagery of emotionally relevant scenarios.34 This paradigm is expected to increase both the specificity and the consistency of tDCS effects across participants.

Executive function activation

Following the emotion regulation script, participants are invited to complete a series of cognitive training exercises on the Lumosity platform. Lumosity is a digital cognitive training platform that delivers personalised, game-based exercises targeting core cognitive domains, including memory, attention, processing speed, cognitive flexibility and problem solving. The platform begins with a brief baseline assessment to characterise individual cognitive performance, after which it generates an adaptive training programme. Exercises are presented in short daily sessions and dynamically adjust in difficulty based on user performance to maintain an optimal level of challenge. Tasks are derived from established paradigms in cognitive psychology and neuroscience. These activities are conducted for the remainder of the 30 min stimulation period. Prior studies indicate that concurrent cognitive engagement during tDCS can enhance neuroplasticity and improve clinical outcomes, particularly when the task aligns with the stimulation’s neural target. This is especially relevant for individuals with BPD, in whom impairments in executive function are typical and contribute to emotional and behavioural dysregulation.

For the remainder of the session, participants are encouraged to engage in cognitive training using Lumosity, which aims to stimulate executive functions and memory. During the adverse-effect check, we will ask participants about their activities during the tDCS session.

Secondary outcome

Remission rate according to MADRS35: The MADRS is a clinician-administered scale with 10 items designed to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. Each item is scored from 0 to 6, yielding a total score of 0 to 60 (higher scores indicate greater severity). The assessment requires approximately 15 to 20 min to complete. It exhibits high internal consistency, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha (α) of 0.85 to 0.89, and demonstrates sensitivity to change, making it suitable for evaluating treatment efficacy. Remission will be defined as a MADRS total score ≤10. The proportion of participants meeting this criterion will be calculated separately for each treatment group. The scale has been validated in French36 and can be administered over the phone.37

Borderline Symptom List (BSL-23)38 39: The BSL-23 is a 23-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess the severity of BPD symptoms over the past week (higher scores indicate more severe symptoms). This shortened version of the BSL-95 is widely used for routine monitoring of BPD. The assessment takes approximately 10 min to complete. The BSL-23 demonstrates excellent internal consistency, with α of roughly 0.94 for the French version.39

Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (QIDS)40: The QIDS is a 16-item self-report questionnaire based on DSM-IV criteria for major depression. It assesses symptom severity over the past 7 days (higher is more severe) and takes approximately 5 to 10 min to complete. The assessment has good internal consistency, with α of 0.69 to 0.89.41 It has been translated into French.42

Borderline Personality Disorder Severity Index (BPDSI)43: The BPDSI is a 70-item semistructured interview designed to assess the frequency and severity of BPD symptoms over the past 3 months. It captures critical symptoms and behaviours associated with BPD, such as self-harm, emotional dysregulation and interpersonal conflicts. The interview takes approximately 45 to 60 min to complete. The BPDSI yields highly reliable (Intra-Class Correlation=0.93) and internally consistent (α=0.85) scores. The BPDSI has been translated into French and used in clinical research, although its psychometric properties have not yet been validated for the French version.44

The Personality Disorder Severity Scale-International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (PDS-ICD-11)45: The PDS-ICD-11 is a clinician-rated instrument designed to assess the severity of personality disorder features by the ICD-11. This scale evaluates impairments in self-functioning (identity and self-direction) and interpersonal functioning (empathy and intimacy), the core components of personality pathology defined by the ICD-11 framework. The PDS-ICD-11 provides a dimensional assessment, categorising severity into five levels: no personality dysfunction, personality difficulty, mild personality disorder, moderate personality disorder and severe personality disorder. This nuanced approach allows for a standardised and comprehensive evaluation of personality dysfunction, facilitating both diagnosis and the monitoring of treatment outcomes. A version has been translated into French.46 47

Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11)48: The BIS-11 is a 30-item self-report questionnaire that evaluates various dimensions of impulsivity, including attentional, motor and non-planning impulsivity. It takes approximately 10 min to complete. The BIS exhibits good internal consistency, with an α ranging from 0.69 to 0.83, indicating satisfactory scale homogeneity. A French version is available.49

WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0, 12-item version): The WHODAS 2.0 (12-item) is a brief self-report questionnaire that assesses disability and functioning across six domains: cognition, mobility, self-care, getting along with others, life activities and participation in society. Higher scores indicate greater functional impairment. Developed by the WHO, it has good internal consistency, with α ranging from 0.87 to 0.94, indicating excellent scale homogeneity. The scale is available in French and English and takes approximately 5–10 min to complete.

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)50 51: The BAI is a 21-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms experienced over the past week. The items focus on physical symptoms of anxiety, such as nervousness and heart palpitations. The BAI takes approximately 10 min to complete. It has excellent internal consistency, with an α of 0.91.

The Personality Inventory for DSM-5 (PID-5)52: The PID-5 is a validated psychometric tool designed to assess maladaptive personality traits described in the DSM-5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders. In clinical research, the PID-5 is an outcome measure by capturing dimensional traits across five broad domains: Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Antagonism, Disinhibition and Psychoticism. Each domain consists of more specific facets that provide a detailed profile of personality pathology. Using the PID-5, researchers can quantify changes in these traits over time, making it a valuable tool for evaluating the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions. Additionally, its use in cross-sectional and longitudinal studies enables the assessment of personality trait stability or improvement following treatment. The French version reported Cronbach’s α coefficients above 0.65.44

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics: We will collect comprehensive sociodemographic and clinical data to provide context for the analysis of outcomes: age, gender and biological sex, luteal phase if applicable, handedness, detailed medication history (including the name of medications, dosage and duration of use), daily consumption of caffeine and cannabis. From V3 onward, the research team will also record any changes in medications (name, dosage) and psychotherapy (therapist, frequency, duration, techniques). This will take approximately 1–5 min.

Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) 53is a widely used tool developed by the WHO for the screening and measurement of addiction across various substances, including alcohol, tobacco, cannabis and opioids. ASSIST is designed for use in clinical and research settings to assess both current and lifetime use and associated risks of substance use disorders. The tool consists of eight questions that measure the frequency of use, cravings and negative impacts of substances on physical, mental, social and legal aspects of the individual’s life, allowing for a quick and comprehensive screening. Psychometric evaluations of ASSIST demonstrate high reliability and validity across various settings and populations. Studies report good internal consistency, with α ranging from 0.80 to 0.88 and high test-retest reliability (ICC=0.90), indicating consistent results over time. It takes approximately 5 to 10 min. The instrument has been validated in French.54

Pittsburg Sleep Questionnaire Inventory (PSQI)55: A 19-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess subjective sleep quality across seven components, including sleep latency, duration, sleep disturbances and daytime dysfunction. Higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. The PSQI takes approximately 10 min to complete. It has good internal consistency, with a fair or reasonable α (ranging from 0.64 to 0.83). The instrument has been validated in French.56

Psychophysiological outcomes: In this study, we will use FitBit watches (Inspire 3) to objectively measure several key outcomes related to sleep, physical activity and physiological metrics. Sleep data will include the total sleep duration and the time spent in each stage of sleep (light, deep and REM). Physical activity will be assessed by tracking the number of steps taken per day and the number of active minutes. Additionally, we will monitor physiological activity, focusing on heart rate variability and heart rate frequency, providing insights into participants’ cardiovascular and autonomic functioning. These data will offer comprehensive, real-time monitoring of participants’ physical and physiological status throughout the study. These measures will be exploratory, as concerns remain regarding their validity in clinical research.57–59 However, their acceptability among participants appears to be good. The Fitbit watches (Inspire 3) will be provided to patients from Visit 3 (V3) to Visit 4 (V4), along with instructions on how to properly charge and maintain the devices throughout the study period. Participants will be guided on ensuring the watches are charged regularly to avoid data loss and monitor sleep, physical activity and physiological measures. Practically, the research team will create an account for each patient and record parameters on a weekly basis. While key usage statistics are accessible directly on the Fitbit device, all data for each participant will also be systematically entered into the RedCap database. Stimulation adherence data (eg, session completion, duration and impedance) will be integrated alongside Fitbit outputs, enabling concurrent monitoring of compliance and physiological indicators throughout the intervention phase.

Cognitive outcomes

N-back task: The N-back task is designed to evaluate working memory and will be administered online. Participants will view a sequence of stimuli and indicate when the current stimulus matches one presented ‘n’ steps earlier. The task duration is typically 15–20 min, depending on the difficulty level (eg, 2-back or 3-back).

Go/No-Go: The Go/No-Go task measures impulsivity and inhibitory control. Participants respond to ‘go’ stimuli while inhibiting responses to ‘no-go’ stimuli. This task generally takes around 10 min to complete. These assessments are integrated into the protocol to evaluate key cognitive domains (planning, working memory and inhibitory control). They will be conducted remotely (V2 and V4), ensuring accessibility and participant convenience while collecting valid and reliable data.

Sample size

Comparable studies in this field have used sample sizes ranging from 3 to 70 participants per arm. Our pilot study aims for a sample size of 30 participants per arm. This initial target will allow us to assess feasibility with respect to recruitment and adherence rates, participant satisfaction, tolerability, implementation and cost.

Moreover, this sample size of 30 participants per group (total N=60) will allow for a preliminary assessment of the efficacy of the tDCS stimulation for all secondary and exploratory outcomes. For depression, an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.4043 is expected.

Analyses

Descriptive statistics

Baseline characteristics will be summarised using means, SDs, medians and IQRs for continuous variables. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons at baseline will be conducted using independent t-tests for continuous variables, Mann-Whitney U tests, χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Primary outcome analysis

First, the recruitment response will be calculated as the proportion of eligible patients who agree to participate in the study. We will require a recruitment response rate of at least 30%. Second, the proportion of participants who completed at least 12 of the 14 tDCS sessions scheduled will be calculated. We will require at least 75% adherence. Participant satisfaction will be evaluated by determining the proportion of participants with a score of at least 70% on the VAS. Again, at least 75% satisfaction is required. Tolerability will be compared between the tDCS and sham-tDCS groups using the χ2 test on contingency tables that cross group with level of tolerability for each side effect. No statistical difference is expected.

Secondary outcome analyses

Outcome measure: Mean MADRS score from baseline to 6 weeks post-treatment initiation.

A longitudinal analysis of covariance will be conducted using a linear mixed model in SAS with the MIXED procedure, where the treatment ‘group’ (tDCS vs sham-tDCS) and ‘time’ are the two main factors. The interaction between group and time will be included to assess the treatment effect over time. Baseline MADRS scores will be included as a covariate.

A log-binomial regression will be performed using the GENMOD procedure in SAS, with the DIST=binomial and LINK=log options, to predict the rates of response and remission at week 6 while accounting for covariates (age, gender, baseline severity and location). Relative risks will be calculated to assess the impact of the tDCS. Similar analyses will be used for all other secondary and exploratory outcomes.

Handling of missing data

Missing data will be addressed using multiple imputation methods under the assumption of missing at random. Sensitivity analyses will be performed to assess the impact of missing data on the study findings.

Interim analysis

No interim analyses are planned for efficacy.

Statistical software

All analyses will be conducted using SAS/STAT software V.9.4 for Windows (SAS Institute).

Subgroup analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses will be performed to examine the treatment effect across age groups, genders, baseline severity levels and urban versus rural settings. The models will include interaction terms to assess subgroup effects.

Adjustments for multiple comparisons

The Holm-Bonferroni correction will be applied to control for the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons in secondary and exploratory analyses. This method sequentially adjusts the significance levels, balancing type I error control and statistical power.

Potential outcomes

This study will establish the potential efficacy of home-based tDCS as a novel intervention for depressive symptoms comorbid with BPD and provide proof-of-concept evidence for the feasibility of remotely delivered neuromodulation in Quebec. Results will generate a preliminary effect-size estimate, which will directly inform the design and sample-size calculations of a future, larger-scale RCT. Moreover, if active tDCS proves both efficacious and well tolerated, these results could position tDCS as a ‘starter’ treatment option—perhaps the first targeted neuromodulatory therapy—for depressed patients with BPD, who often exhibit poor responses to conventional pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. In the longer term, this work may pave the way for integrating home-based tDCS into stepped-care models, reducing barriers to access and offering an early-intervention strategy that can be rapidly deployed in community settings.

Dissemination policy

Trial results will be communicated to participants through social media, posters displayed in relevant community treatment centres and outpatient clinics, and reporting on ClinicalTrials.gov. Healthcare professionals will be informed through poster and oral presentations. Results will also be disseminated to the public and other relevant stakeholders via peer-reviewed publications and plain-language summaries. Following publication, study findings will be presented at scientific congresses and shared directly with participants.
