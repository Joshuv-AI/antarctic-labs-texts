MRI Evaluation of Glymphatic Dysfunction in Cerebral Small Vessel Disease: Hypertensive vs. Normotensive Patients
Authors: Mingming Huang, Xinyue lyu, Yong Xia, Bo Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe glymphatic system (GS) serves as the brain's waste clearance pathway, with dysfunction implicated in neurodegenerative disorders—particularly cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). As hypertension is a modifiable risk factor strongly linked to CSVD progression, we compared magnetic resonance imaging‐derived (MRI‐derived) GS metrics between hypertensive and normotensive CSVD patients. So, in this study, we retrospectively analyzed 72 clinically diagnosed CSVD patients (37 hypertensive [CSVD_HT+], 35 normotensive [CSVD_HT−]), and 40 age‐matched healthy controls (HCs). Key MRI‐derived GS indices included: perivascular space volume fraction (PVS‐VF), diffusivity along perivascular spaces (ALPS index), free water in white matter (FW‐WM), and choroid plexus volume (Chp). CSVD patients exhibited abnormal MRI‐derived indices, Compared to CSVD_HT− individuals, CSVD_HT+ were characterized by lower ALPS index, higher FW‐WM and hypertrophy of Chp. Random systolic blood pressure (SBP) negatively correlated with ALPS index (r = −0.220, p = 0.055), while positively correlating with FW‐WM (r = 0.460, p < 0.001), and Chp volume (r = 0.310, p = 0.006). Cognitive assessments revealed associations between ALPS index and memory scores (r = 0.280, p = 0.020). These findings suggest that elevated SBP exacerbates GS impairment in CSVD, underscoring the critical role of blood pressure management. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these observations.Keywords: ALPS index, cerebral small vessel disease, glymphatic dysfunction, hypertension, perivascular spaces

Introduction

The glymphatic system (GS) functions as the brain's waste clearance network, with growing evidence linking its dysfunction to neurodegenerative diseases and cognitive impairment (Jeffrey et al. 2012; Antoine et al. 2015; Maiken and Steven 2020). Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), a prevalent condition in aging populations, is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to cognitive decline and dementia (Olivia et al. 2020). Notably, hypertension emerges as a major modifiable risk factor for both CSVD onset and progression, with studies demonstrating its role in inducing pathological changes in cerebral microvasculature (Humberto et al. 2018; Kristian Nygaard et al. 2019). Compelling research further suggests that glymphatic dysfunction may accelerate CSVD progression (Hao et al. 2024). Given these interconnected mechanisms, a comprehensive elucidation of GS function holds critical promise for developing targeted prevention and therapeutic strategies for CSVD patients.

Given the limited accessibility of direct GS biomarkers, researchers are actively pursuing noninvasive imaging alternatives (Hao et al. 2024). Among these, MRI‐based techniques stand out as the most promising approach for assessing GS dysfunction (Koji et al. 2022). Current assessment strategies primarily rely on indirect measures, including volumetric analysis of perivascular spaces (PVS) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)‐based methods. Huang et al. (Peiyu et al. 2024) demonstrated that PVS quantification from 3D T1‐weighted images achieves robust segmentation accuracy. In the basal ganglia (BG), enlarged PVS are strongly linked to both aging and hypertension (Corey et al. 2022; Evans et al. 2023). Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of deep medullary veins (DMV) in CSVD pathogenesis (Xueyang et al. 2024), which led to the development of the ALPS index—a DTI‐derived metric evaluating diffusivity along these veins as a proxy for glymphatic clearance (Toshiaki et al. 2017). Concurrently, advanced algorithms now correct for free water (FW) interference in DTI analyses, with some studies proposing FW as a potential standalone biomarker in Alzheimer's disease (AD) (Ofer et al. 2009) (Koji et al. 2022). Additionally, choroid plexus volume (ChP) measurements demonstrate dual relevance, reflecting both Alzheimer's pathophysiology and glymphatic activity (Hui et al. 2024). When integrated, these multimodal MRI indices offer a comprehensive framework for in vivo GS assessment, enabling detection of widespread dysfunctions that might be missed by single‐metric evaluations.

Emerging evidence underscores hypertension as a pivotal risk factor for CSVD, with elevated blood pressure showing strong correlations with CSVD biomarkers (Tan et al. 2020). Rouhl et al. demonstrated that enlarged PVS reflect stagnant cerebrospinal fluid flow (CSF) and are associated with aging, hypertension, and other cerebrovascular risk factors (Rouhl et al. 2008; Marjolein et al. 2014). As critical components of the GS, PVS facilitate solute exchange between CSF and interstitial fluid (ISF), with preclinical models suggesting their fluid dynamics are driven by arterial pulsations and vasomotor oscillations (Joanna et al. 2020). Volumetric quantification of PVS—which encase cerebral microvessels and exhibit MRI signals akin to CSF—enhances measurement sensitivity (Farshid et al. 2019; Giuseppe et al. 2022). Notably, Zhu et al. identified hypertension as strongly linked to PVS enlargement (Yi‐Cheng, Carole, et al. 2010; Yi‐Cheng, Christophe, et al. 2010), while Kern et al. (2023) found intensive random systolic blood pressure (SBP) management could remodel these spaces. Shen et al. (2022) further revealed that 24‐h SBP variability and daytime mean systolic pressure independently predict CSVD burden in acute ischemic stroke patients, unlike diastolic pressure metrics (Xu et al. 2020). As CSVD progresses, DMV undergoes pathological changes from compensatory dilation to collagen deposition and potential occlusion. The ALPS index, developed to assess diffusivity along DMV‐associated PVS, provides a glymphatic function proxy. Wang et al. (Junjun et al. 2023) elucidated hypertension's dual impact on vascular health and cognitive decline, while Kikuta et al. confirmed its glymphatic‐disrupting effects via reduced ALPS values (Kikuta et al. 2022). Synthesizing these findings, we propose that hypertension—particularly elevated SBP—likely contributes to GS dysfunction in CSVD patients.

Building upon this foundation, we integrated four MRI‐derived GS indices to comprehensively evaluate GS alterations in clinical populations. This study pursued two primary objectives: (1) to investigate discrepancies in these potential GS biomarkers between CSVD patients with and without hypertension and (2) to systematically analyze relationships among GS biomarkers, their correlations with SBP, and associations with cognitive impairment.

Methods

Study Participants

CSVD patients were retrospectively enrolled from the Department of Neurology at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University between January 1, 2020, and January 1, 2023. All participants underwent comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and multimodal MRI examinations, with clinical data collected by board‐certified neurologists. CSVD diagnosis followed the 2016 American Heart Association (AHA) and the American Stroke Association (ASA) criteria (Leonardo 2010; Pantoni 2010) requiring MRI evidence of the following: (1) Moderate‐to‐severe white matter hyperintensity (Fazekas score 2–3); (2) lacunar infarcts (< 15 mm diameter); (3) with or without enlarged PVS; (4) cerebral microbleeds (< 5 mm); and (5) brain atrophy. Hypertensive CSVD (CSVD_HT+) was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg (confirmed on two separate measurements), or current antihypertensive medication use.

Healthy controls exhibited: No neurological/psychiatric disorders; the absence of white matter degeneration/lacunar infarcts on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) scans.

Clinical evaluations were performed jointly by trained neurologists and radiologists. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, with ethical approval granted by Guizhou Medical University's Institutional Review Board (Helsinki Declaration compliant).

Demographic and biochemical parameters recorded included as follows: Age/sex; lipid profile (total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL)); Evening blood pressure (SBP/DBP measured at 18:00–20:00).

MRI Acquisition

All imaging data were obtained using a 3 T GE Discovery MR750 scanner (GE Healthcare, USA). To mitigate motion artifacts: Head immobilization was achieved using foam padding; scans with framewise displacement > 0.5 mm were automatically excluded. MRI acquisition protocol: (1) T1‐Weighted Imaging (MPRAGE): Repetition time (TR): 12.07 ms; Echo time (TE): 5.97 ms; Field of view (FOV): 256 × 256 × 176 mm3; isotropic voxel size: 1.00 × 1.00 × 1.00 mm; slice thickness: 1.0 mm (no gap); acquisition matrix: 256 × 256; number of signal averages (NSA): 1. (2) DTI: TR: 4999 ms; TE: 70 ms; acquisition matrix: 160 × 160; diffusion gradients: 34 directions; b‐value: 1000 s/mm2; slice thickness: 3.0 mm (45 slices); in‐plane resolution: 1.38 × 1.38 mm2.

GM, WM, CSF Segmentation

T1‐weighted images (T1WI) were preprocessed and segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and CSF using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox 12 (CAT12). The T1WI images in native space were thereby partitioned into these three distinct tissue classes.

ALPS Calculation

In accordance with the methodology proposed by Taoka et al. (Toshiaki et al. 2017), we adopted the modified ALPS calculation approach introduced by Chang et al. (Hsin‐I et al. 2023). Preprocessing included the following steps: (1) denoising with Marchenko–Pastur principal component analysis; (2) removal of Gibbs ringing artifacts; (3) motion and distortion correction; and (4) bias field correction. Diffusivity maps along the x‐axis (Dxx, right–left), y‐axis (Dyy, anterior–posterior), z‐axis (Dzz, inferior–superior), as well as color‐coded fractional anisotropy (FA) maps, were generated using FSL (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/). The ALPS index was calculated based on projection and association fibers, represented by the corticofugal fibers in the corona radiata and the superior longitudinal fasciculus, respectively. Two spherical regions of interest (ROIs), each with a diameter of 5 mm, were placed on these fiber tracts at the level of the lateral ventricular body. The ALPS index was defined as: mean (Dxx_proj, Dxx_assoc) /mean (Dyy_proj, Dzz_assoc). ROIs were placed bilaterally, and the values from both hemispheres were averaged (Figure 1A).

Workflow of MRI‐derived GS indices. (A) Diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space (ALPS) index processing; (B) FW‐WM in white matter skeleton calculation; (C) PVS in cortex white matter and subcortex white matter; (D) choroid plexus volume segmentation.

FW in WM Skeleton Calculation

DTI images were initially corrected for susceptibility‐induced distortions using the FSL TopUp tool (version 6.0.1) along with additional b₀ images, followed by eddy‐current correction (Jesper and Stamatios 2015). Motion‐related signal dropout was mitigated by estimating and replacing slice‐wise outliers through Gaussian process prediction (Jesper and Stamatios 2015). Fractional volume maps of FW were then generated from the preprocessed DTI using the Diffusion Imaging in Python (Dipy) algorithm (dipy.org) (Eleftherios et al. 2014). A 3D Gaussian smoothing kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 1.25 mm was applied prior to model fitting. White matter skeletons were derived from fractional anisotropy (FA) maps, and mean FW‐WM fractions were computed within each resulting mask (Figure 1B).

PVS Volume Fraction in WM

PVS segmentation was conducted on T1WI using the Frangi filter algorithm, in accordance with previously established methods (Erin et al. 2021; Koji et al. 2022; Kirsten et al. 2023), The detailed procedure consisted of the following steps: (1) bias field correction, (2) spatial registration of the T1WI, (3) mask definition for WM and ventricles, (4) intensity normalization, (5) PVS definition (applying a threshold of 2.3) and generation of a binary mask, (6) registration of the PVS mask to FreeSurfer space, and (7) extraction of volume and volume fraction of dilated PVS in cortical white matter (PVS_Ctx), subcortical white matter (PVS_Sub), and hippocampal white matter (PVS_Hip). To account for inter‐individual variability in brain size, the PVS volume fraction (PVS_VF) within WM was estimated as the ratio of PVS volume in WM to the total intracranial volume (Figure 1C).

Chp Segmentation

Chp volume segmentation was performed using a deep learning approach, following previously established methodology (Jarrod et al. 2024). Model construction and training were implemented in PyTorch, while image registration was carried out using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). The processing pipeline consisted of the following steps: First, the T1WI was registered to MNI152 standard space. A probability atlas, generated from manually segmented Chp ground truth labels, was then cropped to focus on the Chp region. Next, the cropped images served as input for training a 3D U‐Net fully convolutional neural network. Each model was designed with a single‐input and single‐output architecture to segment the Chp. Finally, the cropped output map was restored to its original dimensions and transformed back into individual native imaging space. Chp volume was adjusted for total intracranial volume to account for individual differences (Figure 1D).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons of normally distributed continuous variables were conducted using one‐way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). An independent samples t‐test was applied for group comparisons. For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test was used. Categorical variables were compared across groups using the chi‐square test. Homogeneity of variances was assessed; if the assumption was met (p > 0.05), the Bonferroni correction was applied; otherwise, Dunnett's T3 test was used. The correlation analyses reported were conducted across all CSVD participants, pooling data from both with and without hypertension groups. Correlations between MRI‐derived indices, blood pressure, and cognitive scores were evaluated using Winsorized correlation analysis. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we employed noninvasive MRI‐derived GS indices to comprehensively evaluate GS function in patients with CSVD, with a particular focus on hypertension. Consistent with our initial hypothesis, the results demonstrated the following: (1) Significant alterations in the ALPS index, cortical white matter PVS fraction, and Chp volume were observed in CSVD patients, with more pronounced changes evident in those with hypertension (CSVD_HT+) compared to nonhypertensive individuals (CSVD_HT−); (2) close associations were identified between SBP and the ALPS index, FW‐WM, and Chp volume; however, no significant correlation was found with DBP; and (3) reciprocal relationships among GS indices and their associations with cognitive performance were also confirmed.

Alterations in GS Indices Differ Between CSVD_HT+ and CSVD_HT−

In this study, compared with HCs, both CSVD groups showed a higher PVS volume fraction (mainly located in the cortex), a lower ALPS index, and a larger Chp volume. However, more pronounced alterations were observed in CSVD_HT+ patients relative to the CSVD_HT−group. No significant differences in FW‐WM were detected among the three groups. These findings are consistent with previous studies. Recently, Hao et al. (2024) reported that increased BG‐PVS volumes, elevated FW fraction, and reduced ALPS index are associated with the progression of MRI markers of CSVD, as well as CSVD‐related cognitive decline over time. Similarly, Lan et al. (Haiyuan et al. 2022) demonstrated that changes in extracellular fluid, measured via DTI‐based FW metrics in white matter, correlate with CSVD markers and overall disease burden. Additionally, Tang et al. (2022) suggested that GS dysfunction, reflected by a lower ALPS index, is independently associated with cognitive impairment in CSVD patients.

PVS, recognized as a key component of the lymphatic system, plays a crucial role in glia‐mediated waste clearance (Iliff et al. 2012). Enlarged PVS in the white matter has been observed in patients with more advanced AD (Xue et al. 2023), as well as in those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), as reported by Kamagata et al. (Koji et al. 2022). In the context of CSVD, a previous neuroimaging study (Hao et al. 2024) also documented the presence of enlarged PVS in CSVD patients and its correlation with CSVD‐related cognitive impairment. PVS surround the small cerebral blood vessels and exhibit CSF‐like signals on MRI. Multiple studies have indicated that enlarged PVS in the aging brain are associated with both physiological changes and clinical outcomes (Corey et al. 2022; Qingze et al. 2022). According to existing literature, potential mechanisms underlying PVS enlargement in aging may include arterial stiffening and abnormal protein accumulation within vessel walls (Corey et al. 2022). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of PVS is essential for developing prevention and treatment strategies for CSVD patients, warranting further investigation.

The ALPS index is considered a measure of diffusivity along the PVS surrounding DMV. In Wang et al.'s study (Xueyang et al. 2024), it was suggested that a higher CSVD burden is associated with reduced DMV visibility and structural disruption, which may lead to a decrease in the ALPS index (Xu et al. 2020). Therefore, we propose that CSVD‐related changes in the DMV could contribute to the reduction in the ALPS index. Despite methodological limitations, the utility of the ALPS index as a noninvasive biomarker of GS function has been supported through comparisons with intrathecal gadolinium‐based contrast agent (GBCA) tracers (Toshiaki and Shinji 2019). Elevated venous pressure and compensatory dilatation may increase the prominence of DMVs, indicating that the ALPS index could serve as a potential marker for CSVD progression (Xu et al. 2020).

According to the glymphatic hypothesis (Lulu et al. 2013), FW‐WM reflects alterations in parenchymal microstructure and fluid content, offering insight into interstitial fluid dynamics (Matthieu et al. 2019; Koji et al. 2022), The choroid plexus, a key element of the brain's waste clearance system, serves as the blood‐CSF barrier and modulates CSF production and circulation (Choi et al. 2022; Jarrod et al. 2024). However, most previous studies employed only one or two of these measures within cross‐sectional designs, leading to inconsistent results. Our study did not identify significant differences in FW‐WM fractions among CSVD groups, but did observe enlarged Chp volumes in CSVD patients—particularly in the CSVD_HT+ subgroup. FW‐WM has been linked to radiological severity and cognitive impairment in CSVD, as reported by Lan et al. (Haiyuan et al. 2022). Similarly, Hong et al. proposed that choroid plexus volume is positively correlated with Aβ deposition and white matter hyperintensity burden in Alzheimer's disease patients with CSVD (Hui et al. 2024). These findings are supported by other studies indicating that Chp enlargement may be associated with worsening cognitive impairment across the spectrum of Alzheimer's disease and other neurodegenerative conditions (Choi et al. 2022; Jarrod et al. 2024). Our results align with these observations: in CSVD_HT+ participants, a larger choroid plexus was accompanied by poorer cognitive performance. Furthermore, elevated FW in white matter has been documented in AD, suggesting impaired fluid drainage likely due to glymphatic dysfunction (Matthieu et al. 2019; Koji et al. 2022), The FW‐WM index has consequently been proposed as a standalone biomarker for Alzheimer's‐related pathology (Pauline et al. 2019; Maurizio et al. 2021; Koji et al. 2022).

Collectively, these biomarkers may enable indirect in vivo assessment of the GS, which plays a vital role in metabolic waste clearance and maintaining fluid homeostasis in the brain. The discovery of the GS has revolutionized our understanding of CSVD pathophysiology, underscoring the importance of further investigating its function and pathogenesis through advanced MR imaging techniques.

Associations Between SBP and GS Indices

In our study, Winsorized correlation analysis revealed that random SBP was negatively correlated with the ALPS index and positively correlated with FW‐WM, Chp volume, and CSF volume. To some extent, our findings align with those of Kern et al. (Kern et al. 2023), who demonstrated that intensive SBP treatment can remodel brain PVS. Furthermore, a recent exploratory study by Xie et al. suggested that higher intracranial arterial palpability is associated with presumed neuroimaging markers of glymphatic dysfunction (Linyun et al. 2024), Hypertension is widely recognized as a major modifiable risk factor for CSVD, and growing evidence indicates that elevated blood pressure is positively correlated with the risk of individual CSVD markers. For instance, Shen et al. (2022) showed that 24‐h and daytime mean SBP are independent risk factors for CSVD burden in patients with acute ischemic stroke. Similarly, Li et al. (Tan et al. 2020), reported that H‐type hypertension is associated with both total CSVD burden and its spectrum of manifestations. In this study, we not only identified abnormalities in GS indices but also explored correlations between SBP and these MRI‐derived metrics, underscoring the importance of blood pressure control in maintaining glymphatic function. In addition, the role of the GS in CSVD has increasingly attracted research attention in the broader field.

However, most previous studies have focused on only one or two relationships between DMV and individual biomarkers of GS function. As CSVD represents a complex clinical syndrome, the limitations of any single imaging index suggest that composite measures—such as hypertension burden or other clinical factors—may better reflect the process of glymphatic dysfunction. This highlights the role of SBP as a potential risk factor affecting glymphatic function. Further investigation is warranted to determine whether SBP is indeed a modifiable risk factor for the maintenance of GS integrity.

Interrelationships Between GS Indices and Related Brain Structure

In this study, negative correlations were observed between the ALPS index and volume of CSF, while positive associations were identified with the ALPS index and GM volume. Additionally, the FW‐WM was positively correlated with CSF volume. An enlargement of the Chp was also associated with increased CSF volume. These findings are consistent with previous reports, including the study by Hsiao et al. (2023), which suggested that a higher ALPS index is linked to improved cortical reserve. In contrast to earlier research that primarily examined cross‐sectional relationships between the ALPS index and PVS, our study further investigated the potential cognitive implications of these associations. The results imply that a reduction in the ALPS index may precede increases in PVS volume, possibly indicating impaired brain waste clearance pathways. Notably, no significant association was found between FW‐WM and cognitive performance. Overall, these results support the notion that GS alterations are associated with neurodegenerative and CSVD‐related pathological processes. However, the observed variations among patient subgroups and the lack of correlation with cognitive scores merit further investigation.

Moreover, memory performance was positively correlated with the ALPS index, Accumulating evidence indicates that CSVD contributes to the incidence of stroke and dementia (Olivia et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2022). Our findings are partially consistent with previous studies and, in particular, underscore the association between the ALPS index and memory function. This supports the hypothesis that glymphatic dysfunction may represent a common pathway in the development of dementia (Maiken and Steven 2020).

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and all participants were recruited from a single center. Future studies should involve larger, multicenter cohorts with longitudinal designs to better elucidate the trajectories and interrelationships of these imaging indices. Second, although participants were confirmed to have stable blood pressure management, we relied on random systolic blood pressure measurements rather than continuous monitoring. Subsequent research should incorporate 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring alongside longitudinal cognitive evaluations to establish potential SBP thresholds for preserving glymphatic function (e.g., SBP < 130 mmHg). Third, the FW within WM was assessed using a skeleton‐based approach without excluding white matter hyperintensity regions. Limitations in vein visibility or signal continuity may also stem from elevated free water values or MRI slice orientation. Fourth, the study focused exclusively on MRI‐derived metrics and did not include biochemical measures such as vascular cellular markers or signaling peptides. Finally, the absence of long‐term follow‐up for cognitive decline and dementia outcomes represents an important constraint, underscoring the need for further prospective studies. Additionally, we did not control for cardiovascular risk factors or sleep disturbances, both of which are known to modulate glymphatic function.

Conclusion

This preliminary finding suggests that elevated SBP is closely associated with MRI‐derived GS indices. Specifically, hypertensive CSVD patients exhibited a lower ALPS index, enlarged PVS, and increased Chp volume. These observations provide novel insight into the pathophysiology of CSVD. Further research is warranted to investigate the potential relationship between SBP and impaired glymphatic clearance in CSVD patients.
