Going for Distance or Speed? Determining the Optimal Hospital Destination for Patients With Acute Stroke in the Prehospital Setting
Authors: Regina Royan, William J Meurer
Published: 2023

Outcomes following acute ischemic stroke are influenced by intrinsic biological factors and extrinsic process factors. The individual variability in the intrinsic factors is broad—including size and location of the vessel that is occluded, extent of collaterals, and the prestroke health of the existing brain cells inclusive of neurons, vasculature, and structural support. What is the current status of endogenous thrombosis versus fibrinolysis in the bloodstream? What existing medications may be altering blood pressure or clotting? At initial presentation, however, these complex factors manifest as a phenotype visible to bystanders and care providers only as severity, localization, and hemodynamics. To maximize the chance of a good outcome, blood flow needs to be restored as quickly and completely as possible. The extrinsic factors that comprise the process are similarly complex, and several are influenced by the intrinsic factors. First, how obvious is the presentation? Sudden right‐sided paralysis with loss of speech is generally not ignorable assuming another human is available to witness it and seek help. On the other hand, subtle vertigo with slight imbalance may not prompt a person or bystanders to seek care. Second, where is the patient? Are they close or far from a stroke center? What capabilities does that center have? Finally, how will the local system of health care evaluate and treat the patient with a sudden, new, neurological deficit?

The RACECAT (Transfer to the Closest Local Stroke Center Versus Direct Transfer to Endovascular Stroke Center of Acute Stroke Patients With Suspected Large Vessel Occlusion in the Catalan Territory) trial found no difference in outcomes when nonurban patients with suspected large vessel occlusion stroke in Catalonia were randomly assigned to either receive care at nearest stroke center or diverted to a more distant thrombectomy capable center (also referred to as mothership approach). 1 Given the complex interplay of intrinsic and extrinsic factors that contribute to outcome, perhaps it is not surprising that RACECAT was neutral on its primary outcome. Only 48% of the diversion group received systemic thrombolysis, whereas 60% of the closest hospital group did. A key (but oft forgotten) feature of the practice‐changing thrombectomy trials such as MR‐CLEAN (Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial of Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands) was early (and frequent) thrombolysis. 2

So, what data are necessary to provide a paramedic or stroke system of care with a destination for a given patient that maximizes the likelihood of a good outcome? One extrinsic factor influencing outcome is fixed (by weight) thrombolytic dosing. Intuitively, the larger the vessel containing the clot, the less likely it will be opened by a fixed dose of exogenous thrombolytic. Time clearly influences this as well. So, assuming there is no obvious contraindication such as anticoagulation, we do not have a meaningful model to balance several factors. First, knowable factors such as severity and geography could help guide destination. Arguably, a hospital's average door‐to‐needle time is knowable but typically unavailable to paramedics or prehospital systems of care in the moment. Next, consider the currently unknowable factors from the paramedic perspective: clot size, collateral status, and accessibility of a vessel via angiography. Finally, there are permanently unknowable factors including whether a fixed dose of a thrombolytic will actually open this vessel. An ideal future tool that made the most of the knowable factors would help a paramedic or stroke system of care determine whether an individual patient is best served by getting faster thrombolysis locally or diverting to reduce the time to thrombectomy. In addition, a patient across the street from a hospital with average door‐to‐needle times of 80 minutes would be better served traveling up to an hour to reach a hospital that treats within 20 minutes. In the end, getting a prehospital bypass procedure for stroke that can benefit more patients than it inconveniences (or worse, hurts) is immensely complicated. Or to paraphrase the band Cake, should we be going the distance? 3 Or should we be going for speed (to thrombolysis)?

In this issue of Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Schuler et al 4 investigated the applicability of the RACECAT study to 4 distinct stroke networks located in Southwest Bavaria, Germany; Switzerland; Northern France; and Catalonia, Spain. They examined 2 prehospital triage approaches for acute ischemic stroke. The drip‐and‐ship approach and the mothership approach were compared with respect to transport time saved for thrombolysis and endovascular therapy in regions where a local thrombolytic‐capable hospital could be reached faster than endovascular‐capable hospital.

Transport times showed greater benefit for thrombolysis under the drip‐and‐ship approach in stroke networks that were clustered versus those that were more dispersed. These findings highlight that population densities and geographic distributions of intervention centers must be taken into account when testing and applying prehospital triage algorithms.

Although the RACECAT study found no differences in the outcomes between the mothership and drip‐and‐ship approach, these conclusions were limited in generalizability as they were performed in a single stroke system in Catalonia, Spain. 1 The authors of the current study correctly point out the knowledge gap of broader applicability of the RACECAT trial, particularly in varied populations and hospital densities. Indeed, it is plausible to believe that RACECAT may have had positive findings if conducted in the United States given the long length of time that patients with acute ischemic stroke wait for interhospital transfer. A recent study of US patients with stroke showed that patients who required interhospital transfer for endovascular therapy had a median door‐in‐door‐out time of 132 minutes, suggesting that many US patients with stroke have significant delays in interhospital transfer. 5 This represents an administrative penalty on biology. The plausible benefit of initial transport to the closest center is faster systemic thrombolysis. This is an area yet unstudied, but it would seem that saving 10 to 15 minutes from onset to drug may not justify an additional 2 hours waiting for a hospital‐to‐hospital transfer for thrombectomy.

So, is it best to bypass local stroke centers in favor of direct transport to interventional‐capable hospitals? It remains unknown whether direct transport to hospitals capable of definitive care will lead to meaningful improvement in clinical outcomes. In fact, there may be unintended consequences of bypassing local stroke centers, as a recent secondary analysis of the RACECAT trial data showed that there were worse functional outcomes for patients with intracerebral hemorrhage. 6 In addition, most studies only focus on confirmed stroke patients, whereas patients with mimics are also transported. Mimic and nonthrombectomy patients alike will be farther from their home and family resources after diversion, and these effects are not measured in the vast majority of existing studies. In addition, both mimic and nonthrombectomy patients require a high level of emergency department resources, which can result in care delays for other patients. In some rural communities, longer transport times can tie up the only available ambulance.

When considering the applicability of these findings to other stroke systems of care, it is worth appreciating that hospitals and stroke systems in Western Europe may be located with greater consideration for their role as a public utility with an egalitarian approach to access. This is in great contrast to the United States, where hospital location and transfer agreement networks are largely based on economics, with resources clustered around wealthy populations while other areas are relatively underserved. 7 , 8 Since 2015, there has been an effort to expand access to endovascular therapy for all communities 9 ; however, this goal is yet to be realized, with 37% of Americans lacking 60‐minute ground or air access to endovascular‐capable stroke centers. 10

As those familiar with 1990s alternative music are aware, the protagonist in the Cake song was simultaneously going the distance and going for speed but spectacularly failed in the latter. We continue to do less than we can for many patients who could be getting better and faster care. Stroke systems of care are inherently variable with respect to local population density and resources, and we applaud the authors for conducting this study to understand the applicability of RACECAT findings in additional settings. To determine whether patients ultimately end up where they belong for optimal stroke treatment, further studies will be needed in other diverse stroke systems. Such studies should recognize the complexity of the problem and work to account for the externalities on other patients concurrently needing emergency care.
