Brain age gradients as intermediate phenotypes linking plasma p-tau217 to cognition in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Nicholas Riccardi, Ansley Martin, Dariusz Pytel, Sarah Newman-Norlund, Steven L. Carroll, Chris Rorden, Julius Fridriksson, Leonardo Bonilha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep learning-based brain age models quantify regional deviations from normative aging and may capture structural changes relevant to dementia risk. Plasma phosphorylated tau-217 (p-tau217) is a scalable Alzheimer’s disease biomarker, but its relationship to brain aging and cognition in cognitively unimpaired adults is unclear. In this cross-sectional study, we tested whether brain age patterns serve as indirect pathways linking plasma p-tau217 to cognition in the Aging Brain Cohort (ABC). Neuroimaging data from 518 adults (mean age = 43.7 years, 70.8% female) were analyzed using a validated deep learning brain age model, and decomposed via exploratory factor analysis into six gradients: frontal, dorsal, ventral, left frontotemporal, right frontotemporoparietal, and bilateral parietal. In a parallel mediation model including all six gradients as simultaneous mediators in adults aged ≥60 years (N = 71), a significant specific indirect effect of plasma p-tau217 on Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores was observed through accelerated right frontotemporoparietal aging (β = −0.111, 95% CI [−0.313, −0.010], p = 0.031). No other indirect pathways were significant, and neither the total nor direct effect was significant. These findings suggest a specific brain aging phenotype as a potential intermediate pathway linking tau-related pathology to cognition prior to clinical impairment.

Introduction

Neuroimaging-based brain age models are a powerful tool for quantifying deviations from normative brain structure1. Deep learning models trained on large datasets can estimate an individual’s chronological age directly from MR images, and the difference between predicted and actual age (brain age gap; BAG) indexes older- or younger-appearing brain structure. Higher BAG, indicating accelerated aging, has been linked to vascular risk, lower cognition, and mortality2–4, positioning brain age as a sensitive marker across both normative and pathological aging. Because these models are trained on population samples encompassing the full spectrum of age-related processes, BAG captures a composite of structural variance that includes normative changes, vascular burden, and age-correlated pathology, rather than isolating any single biological process5. Identifying which biological processes are associated with these deviations in independent samples is a central application of brain age and other biological clock frameworks1,2.

Next-generation approaches now provide region-specific age estimates6–8, enabling detection of spatially distributed patterns of accelerated aging that reflect heterogeneity in structural vulnerability. Due to their inherent micro- and macrostructural properties, certain regions are uniquely susceptible to age-related decline or molecular processes6,7, making this enhanced spatial resolution critical for identifying neurostructural phenotypes that may signal early disease risk. This framework provides an opportunity to test how peripheral biomarkers map onto topographic patterns of brain aging.

Among candidate peripheral markers, plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine 217 (p-tau217) has emerged as a sensitive and minimally invasive indicator of Alzheimer disease (AD) pathology9,10, reflecting abnormal tau phosphorylation processes that closely track with neurofibrillary tangle burden. Plasma p-tau217 increases with age, predicts future cognitive decline at the group level, and tracks with tau cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and positron emission tomography (PET) measures11–13, yet its behavior in community-dwelling adults without cognitive or neurological diagnoses is not well-characterized. Importantly, p-tau217 levels vary between individuals, ranging from ‘normal’ to intermediate or high14, but many people with elevated levels remain cognitively stable for years15,16. Identifying structural brain correlates that are associated with resilience or vulnerability across this spectrum is vital for understanding risk before clinically significant cognitive impairment emerges.

However, little is known about how plasma p-tau217 relates to in vivo brain structure in community-based samples15,17–19. Most prior work has examined plasma p-tau217 in cohorts enriched for AD or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or has focused on its relationship to CSF, PET, or postmortem brain structure20–22. Few studies have tested whether plasma p-tau217 corresponds to quantitative MRI in adults without cognitive diagnoses23,24. This is an important gap, as aging itself brings widespread structural changes, such as cortical thinning or regional atrophy, that vary in extent, topography, and range from normative to atypical25–27. Determining whether plasma p-tau217 relates to patterned deviations in brain aging may help identify intermediate neural phenotypes that link blood-based markers of pathology to cognition.

By integrating peripheral biomarkers with neuroimaging-derived brain age phenotypes, it may be possible to identify neural pathways that distinguish resilience from vulnerability to Alzheimer disease pathology. Brain age models provide a sensitive framework to capture early deviations in structural integrity that may precede overt symptoms, while plasma p-tau217 offers a minimally invasive index of tau-related molecular processes. Together, these approaches create a bridge between peripheral biomarkers and neuroimaging phenotypes, offering insight into why some individuals with molecular evidence of AD remain cognitively intact whereas others experience decline.

In this cross-sectional study, we utilized a validated, pre-trained deep learning model8 to characterize common spatial patterns of brain aging in community-dwelling adults aged 20–86 years without neurological or cognitive diagnoses. We then tested whether plasma p-tau217 was associated with cognition directly, or indirectly through the expression of these brain aging patterns. We hypothesized that (1) higher plasma p-tau217 would be associated with lower Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA28) scores, (2) individual expression of accelerated brain aging patterns centered around association cortices would relate to lower MoCA, and (3) plasma p-tau217 would relate to cognition indirectly through its relationship with regional brain aging.

Results

Sample characteristics

Overall, 518 neurologically intact adults were included in the initial sample. Of these, 354 completed the MoCA and 196 had available plasma p-tau217 (see participant flowchart in Supplementary Fig. 1). The mediation analysis sample comprised 71 adults aged ≥60 years (mean age = 67.2, 71.8% female) with complete data on plasma p-tau217, MoCA, brain age factor scores, and covariates (Table 1). Characterizing brain age gradients

Exploratory factor analysis (principal axis factoring, varimax rotation; see “Methods”) identified six gradients of accelerated brain aging that explained variance in the correlation structure of the BAGs (Fig. 1): (1) frontal (28.76%), (2) dorsal (28.25%), (3) ventral (18.96%), (4) left frontotemporal (13.75%), (5) right frontotemporoparietal (4.09%), and (6) parietal (1.29%). These patterns are consistent with prior work in subsets of the present cohort, which also showed that these six gradients replicated across two independent samples7. Pairwise Pearson’s correlations confirmed that participant-level factor scores were orthogonal (all p’s > 0.5). Parallel mediation: brain age gradients as indirect pathways linking p-tau217 to cognition

To test whether brain age gradients mediate the association between plasma p-tau217 and cognition, we fit a parallel mediation model with all six gradients as simultaneous mediators, z-scored log-transformed plasma p-tau217 as predictor, and MoCA total as outcome, controlling for age, sex, education, and dementia family history (see “Methods”).

In this parallel mediation model (N = 71; Fig. 3), neither the total effect (β = 0.043, p = 0.768; Table 3) nor the direct effect (β = 0.074, p = 0.637; Table 3) of plasma p-tau217 on MoCA was significant. However, a significant specific indirect effect was observed through the right frontotemporoparietal gradient (factor 5; β = −0.111, 95% CI [−0.313, −0.010], p = 0.031; Table 3). Higher plasma p-tau217 was associated with greater expression of accelerated right frontotemporoparietal aging (a-path: β = 0.280, p = 0.033), which in turn was associated with lower MoCA scores. The specific indirect effects through the remaining five gradients were not significant: frontal (factor 1; β = −0.004, p = 0.910), dorsal (factor 2; β = −0.001, p = 0.948), ventral (factor 3; β = 0.006, p = 0.884), left frontotemporal (factor 4; β = 0.021, p = 0.516), and bilateral parietal (factor 6; β = 0.056, p = 0.194). Discussion

The central aim of this cross-sectional study was to determine whether circulating tau pathology, indexed by plasma p-tau217, relates to cognition in community-dwelling adults through its association with regional brain aging patterns identified by a validated, pretrained, deep learning model. Our primary finding is that a specific indirect pathway through accelerated right frontotemporoparietal brain aging linked plasma p-tau217 to cognition in older adults without MCI or AD diagnoses. In a parallel mediation model that simultaneously included all six brain age gradients, higher plasma p-tau217 was associated with greater expression of accelerated right frontotemporoparietal aging, which in turn was associated with lower MoCA scores. This specific indirect effect was the only significant pathway among the six gradients tested, and it was robust to the removal of education as a covariate and to adjustment for cardiovascular risk (QRISK3). In contrast, a bilateral parietal accelerated aging pattern was related to lower MoCA but was not significantly associated with p-tau217, suggesting additional p-tau-independent mechanisms of cognitive vulnerability. Furthermore, higher p-tau217 levels were observed in individuals with older age and lower educational attainment; however, there was no significant total or direct association between p-tau217 and cognition. Notably, in the older subgroup, a minority of participants met recent thresholds14 for intermediate (16.9%) or high (9.9%) plasma p-tau217, underscoring that these tau-brain age effects were detectable even in a community-based sample that was not enriched for MCI or AD.

The finding that plasma p-tau217 was associated with cognition only indirectly, via a pattern of accelerated frontotemporoparietal aging helps refine how this plasma biomarker is interpreted in community-based aging. Most prior studies have linked p-tau217 to cognition in cohorts enriched for MCI or AD, or have focused on discriminating clinical diagnoses, leaving its role in non-diagnosed adults less clear15,17–19. Our cohort captures this space. While participants were free of neurological or cognitive diagnoses, cognitive variability was common, with 42% of our older adults scoring in the ‘at-risk’ MoCA range28. In this context, the absence of a significant total or direct association between p-tau217 and cognition but the presence of a significant specific indirect brain age pathway suggests that higher plasma p-tau217 is related to lower cognition primarily when accompanied by regionally patterned cortical vulnerability. These effects were robust to adjustment for QRISK3 cardiovascular scores, suggesting that the tau-brain age-cognition pathway is not attributable to vascular health burden. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify a specific indirect pathway of plasma p-tau217 via MRI-derived brain age phenotypes in community-dwelling adults, advancing efforts to integrate plasma biomarkers with neurostructural phenotyping to understand cognitive risk in the general population.

The right frontotemporoparietal pattern identified here aligns with tau-related temporoparietal vulnerability reported in prior tau-PET and CSF studies31–35, while extending this literature by linking plasma p-tau217 to MRI-derived brain age phenotypes. For example, LaPoint et al.35 found that elevated tau-PET was associated with thinning in temporal and parietal cortices among cognitively normal older adults, with a notable rightward bias. Although AD pathology typically shows bilateral involvement, asymmetrical structural patterning is well-documented in normative aging36,37, preclinical AD38–40, and lifespan studies of structural changes in association cortices41, providing a plausible scaffold for hemisphere-specific vulnerability. This convergence across modalities and cohorts suggests that such asymmetries reflect meaningful biological signal rather than methodological artifact. Our right-lateralized findings therefore align with independent evidence of asymmetric structural changes in aging and tau-related pathology, suggesting that plasma p-tau217 may index vulnerability in highly connected right hemisphere temporoparietal hubs.

A distinct pattern of accelerated bilateral parietal aging was associated with lower MoCA scores across the entire adult lifespan but was not significantly related to plasma p-tau217. This suggests different pathways of cognitive and neural vulnerability. In contrast, the right frontotemporoparietal pattern may reflect age-related susceptibility that becomes relevant in later life and is associated with tau accumulation. Thus, the bilateral parietal gradient may represent a more stable, trait-like feature of brain structure. Such trait-like features of brain aging may arise from enduring network architectures established during early-life development, rather than age-related processes during adulthood42.

Educational attainment also emerged as an important contextual factor in our cohort. Higher education was associated with better cognitive status and lower plasma p-tau217, consistent with hypotheses that education contributes to cognitive and neural reserve43,44. While most prior plasma p-tau217 studies have focused on diagnostic or prognostic value in enriched AD/MCI cohorts and treat education only as a nuisance covariate18,45,46, our data suggest that reserve-related factors relate to molecular and cognitive profiles even in the absence of clinical diagnoses. Prior tau-PET studies have reported mixed associations with education, ranging from null effects47 to positive48 or negative49 associations with tau burden. Our finding of lower plasma p-tau217 with higher education suggests that reserve may partially mitigate tau accumulation and its downstream impact on cognition. However, the role of education in dementia risk remains debated: recent evidence suggests that early-life cognitive ability, rather than education itself, may drive the education-dementia association50, and that education shows limited independent association with cognitive decline after accounting for baseline ability51. Notably, the indirect effect through factor 5 was significant both with and without education as a covariate (β = −0.111, p = 0.031 and β = −0.124, p = 0.024, respectively), indicating that the observed pathway is not contingent on educational adjustment.

A limitation of this study is the cross-sectional design, which precludes inferences about the temporal ordering between plasma p-tau217, brain aging, and cognition; the indirect pathway identified here is statistical, not causal. We also cannot determine which individuals will ultimately develop AD, and APOE genotype and PET imaging were not available to further clarify underlying mechanisms. The mediation sample (N = 71) is modest, which increases the risk of Type II error; however, the percentile bootstrap provides valid inference for indirect effects at this sample size52,53, and the consistency of results across sensitivity models supports the reliability of the observed pathway. More broadly, BAG is a composite biomarker that reflects the aggregate structural correlates of aging, including contributions from normative age-related change, vascular burden, and age-correlated pathology5. The present findings should therefore be interpreted not as demonstrating that p-tau217 drives “pure” brain aging, but rather that elevated p-tau217 is associated with a specific spatial pattern of structural deviation from expected aging that also relates to cognition.

Additionally, high age-prediction accuracy in brain age models can compress the range of BAGs and reduce clinical sensitivity, as accuracy-optimized models tend to down-weight features with high inter-individual variability that are often the most sensitive to disease-related changes54,55. The brain age model used here achieves high accuracy (MAE = 3.83 year in older adults), and our effect sizes may therefore be conservative estimates. However, the same pipeline has demonstrated significant clinical sensitivity across five neurological and psychiatric conditions, with regional brain structure ages substantially outperforming global brain age for disease classification8, suggesting that regional resolution partially mitigates the accuracy-sensitivity tradeoff even when prediction accuracy is high. While our cohort represents a community-based sample, these findings should be replicated in additional and diverse samples. The ABC is an ongoing study with continuous enrollment; as participants complete longitudinal follow-up assessments, future work will test whether the brain age gradients identified here predict cognitive change over time.

Our findings indicate that combining MRI-derived brain age with plasma p-tau217 reveals a specific frontotemporoparietal aging phenotype that is associated with both later-life cognition and elevated p-tau217. This phenotype was distinct from a bilateral parietal aging pattern that related to cognition across adulthood but was not significantly associated with p-tau217. That only one of six brain age gradients showed a significant indirect pathway underscores the neuroanatomical specificity of this association: rather than a diffuse relationship between p-tau217 and brain aging broadly, elevated p-tau217 is associated with accelerated aging in right-lateralized frontotemporoparietal regions that also relate to cognitive performance. Together, these results suggest that integrating minimally invasive plasma biomarkers with quantitative MRI can uncover neural phenotypes that relate to variability across the spectrum of p-tau217 levels and may ultimately aid in identifying early pathways to cognitive decline in non-diagnosed, community-based adults.

Methods

Participants

This was a cross-sectional observational study embedded within the ongoing University of South Carolina’s Aging Brain Cohort (ABC56). A total of 518 adults aged 20–86 years (mean = 43.7 years, 368 females) had neuroimaging data and met inclusion criteria and were therefore included, 196 of whom had available plasma (Table 4). 360 ABC participants were recruited from 2019 to 2025. ABC recruitment is continuous and based on community and media outreach; thus, the sample size reflects the number of eligible participants enrolled during the study period. An additional 158 participants were drawn from a public neuroimaging repository57 to ensure that observed brain age patterns were not cohort-specific and to support stable identification of shared spatial gradients. This approach ensured broad age coverage and sufficient sample size for subsequent data-driven brain age factor-analytics. All participants were neurologically intact, community-dwelling adults without diagnosed MCI. Exclusion criteria were history of stroke, diagnosis of neurodegenerative disease, serious acute or chronic conditions that would limit their ability to participate, current severe illness (e.g., cancer), psychiatric diagnosis (e.g., schizophrenia), or BMI > 42 kg/m2. Non-native English speakers completed cognitive testing but were excluded from cognitive analyses because lower scores in this group may reflect linguistic proficiency rather than true cognitive impairment. MRI processing and brain age estimation

Regional brain age estimates were calculated using BrainStructureAges8, an automated, publicly available pipeline that leverages T1-weighted MRI (Fig. 4). The pipeline and all constituent models were developed and trained by its original developers8 and applied as a pre-trained tool in the present study; no retraining or fine-tuning was performed by the authors. Five preprocessing steps are applied automatically by the pipeline: (1) denoising using a spatially adaptive non-local means (SANLM) filter58, (2) inhomogeneity correction using N4ITK59, (3) affine registration to MNI152 space (181 × 217 × 181 voxels at 1 mm isotropic) using ANTs60, (4) intensity standardization via piecewise linear mapping to fixed tissue-class intensity targets (CSF = 50, GM = 150, WM = 250) estimated using trimmed mean segmentation, and (5) intracranial cavity extraction using NICE61, a non-local multi-scale label fusion method. Automated quality control of the affine registration is performed using RegQCNET58. Brain age estimation workflow.

T1-weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) images are fed into a pretrained deep learning model (BrainStructureAges8), which parcellates each brain and bestows region-specific brain age estimates. Regional brain age gaps are then calculated (brain age minus chronological age) and entered into an exploratory factor analysis, identifying commonly expressed patterns of brain aging that are used in downstream analyses.

The pipeline then uses a multi-step deep learning process to parcellate brains and estimate regional brain ages (additional training details are provided in Supplementary Note 1). Preprocessed images are parcellated into 133 structures by AssemblyNet62, a large, pretrained ensemble of convolutional neural networks for whole-brain segmentation following the Neuromorphometrics atlas. The age-prediction models were trained on 2,887 images from eight publicly available datasets (including ABIDE I, ADNI, IXI, and OASIS1) and validated on two independent cohorts (ABIDE II, N = 580; UK Biobank, N = 29,251), and a built-in bias-correction procedure eliminates systematic age-related bias during regional age estimation. BrainStructureAges demonstrates excellent out-of-sample age estimation in both younger and older adults (young mean absolute error (MAE) = 1.88 y, R² = 0.91; older MAE = 3.83 y, R² = 0.62). Despite this high accuracy, which has been shown to occasionally limit clinical sensitivity in brain age models55, the pipeline has demonstrated clinical sensitivity across multiple neurological and psychiatric conditions, with regional brain structure ages significantly differentiating Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, multiple sclerosis, schizophrenia, and Parkinson’s disease from cognitively normal controls, substantially outperforming global brain age for disease classification8.

The model learns image-derived features (e.g., volume, shape/curvature, and intensity) directly from the MR data that are most predictive of chronological age, without relying on predefined morphometric features such as cortical thickness or volume. Each registered image is first downsampled by a factor of two (to 91 × 109 × 91 voxels), and a separate ensemble of 125 3D U-Net models generates voxel-wise age predictions from overlapping sub-volumes (32 × 48 × 32 voxels) evenly distributed across the downsampled image. Where sub-volumes overlap, predictions are averaged; the resulting age map is upscaled to native resolution via trilinear interpolation and then averaged within each parcellated structure to produce structure-level brain age estimates8. This approach can capture subtle, multivariate imaging features that may precede gross atrophy. It does not require pre-selecting specific morphometric measures or testing them separately. Instead, the model uses the combination of image-derived features that best predict chronological age in each brain region.

BAGs (brain age minus chronological age) were calculated for 104 regions (98 cortical regions from the Neuromorphometrics atlas and 6 subcortical structures: bilateral amygdala, hippocampus, and basal forebrain), retained to maintain consistency with prior work establishing the existence of brain age gradients6,7. This was done in each individual, resulting in heterogeneously distributed spatial maps indicating areas with accelerated or decelerated brain aging (Fig. 4).

Immunoassay measurements for p-tau217

196 ABC participants (mean = 46.9 ± 18.6 yo; 138 female) had available plasma p-tau217, cognitive and neuroimaging data, and met inclusion/exclusion criteria. Plasma p-tau217 concentrations (pg/mL) were measured using the commercially available Simoa ALZpath p-Tau 217 Advantage PLUS assay (#104570) with a 3-fold dilution on an HD-X Automated Immunoassay Analyzer, according to manufacturer’s instructions (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA)63,64. All measurements were performed by the Molecular and Genomic Biomarker Core of the South Carolina Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center (SC-ADRC) at the Medical University of South Carolina, Charleston, SC, USA. Higher plasma p-tau217 concentrations are indicative of greater tau pathology burden. P-tau217 levels were log-transformed prior to analysis.

Neuropsychological testing

354 participants completed the MoCA28, including 196 participants with available plasma. Trained research associates administered the MoCA after completing the official MoCA Training and Certification course. Although all participants were independent and community-dwelling, and free of an MCI or neurological diagnosis, scores ranged from 19 to 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. Among participants aged 60 years or older, 42% of participants scored in the at-risk range (MoCA ≤ 25; Table 1). Total MoCA was the primary measure of interest.

Statistical analysis

Plasma p-tau217 and cognition

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 29; IBM Corp.) and the Pingouin65 package in Python 3. Before neuroimaging analyses, we examined characteristics associated with p-tau217 in the 196 participants with available plasma. Linear regression (enter) predicted p-tau217 from age, sex, education, and dementia family history (yes/no).

To test the relationship between p-tau217 and cognition, MoCA score was predicted from the same variables with p-tau217 added as an additional predictor. This model was evaluated in all 196 and in the subset of those participants aged 60 or older (N = 71), who are the most likely to have elevated p-tau217 and lower cognitive scores.

Regression coefficients were tested for significance using bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrapping (1000 iterations). All models were inspected for collinearity, which was within acceptable limits (tolerance > 0.3).

Characterizing brain age gradients

To identify commonly expressed patterns of accelerated brain aging and reduce data dimensionality, we conducted exploratory factor analysis using principal axis factoring on the cortical BAG correlation matrix for all 518 participants with neuroimaging data. Factors were retained based on eigenvalues > 1, with a maximum of 25 iterations for convergence. Varimax rotation with Kaiser normalization was applied to improve interpretability and reduce the number of brain regions with high loadings on multiple factors.

The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure indicated excellent sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.982), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ²(5356) = 230,224.25, p < 0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Six factors were retained, explaining 95.1% of the variance in the BAG correlation matrix. Participant-level factor scores were computed using the regression method, providing continuous indices of each individual’s expression of the identified brain aging patterns.

For example, a participant with a high positive score on a given factor would have a pattern of accelerated aging that aligns with that factor’s spatial loadings. These participant-level scores were used in downstream statistical models to test associations with cognition and plasma p-tau217. Pairwise Pearson’s correlations confirmed that participant-level factor scores were orthogonal (all p’s > 0.5).

Mediation analysis

The mediation analysis was restricted to adults aged ≥60 years because plasma p-tau217 is strongly age-dependent, with concentrations near the lower detection range in younger adults12,18, and MoCA scores in the <60 group were near ceiling, leaving insufficient variance in both predictor and outcome to support mediation inference.

To test whether brain age gradients mediated the association between plasma p-tau217 and cognition, we conducted a parallel mediation analysis65 in which all six brain age gradients were entered simultaneously as mediators (M1–M6), with log-transformed plasma p-tau217 as the predictor (X) and total MoCA as the outcome (Y). All six gradients were included to avoid data-driven mediator selection that could inflate effect sizes66, and to present the complete pattern of indirect effects, including nulls. Plasma p-tau217 and MoCA total scores were z-scored prior to analysis; factor scores were already standardized (mean ≈ 0, SD ≈ 1). Chronological age, sex, education, and dementia family history were included as covariates. Significance of indirect effects was determined using percentile bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 iterations).

Following standard practice in parallel mediation, specific indirect effects were evaluated individually without correction for multiple comparisons, as these represent parameter estimates within a single unified model52,66. The primary focus was the indirect effect, representing the association between p-tau217 and cognition through brain aging patterns. Two-tailed 95% confidence intervals and p-values are reported for all effects. The primary model included the 71 adults aged 60 or older with complete data. All 71 participants in the mediation sample had complete data on all analysis variables (plasma p-tau217, MoCA, all six factor scores, and covariates). Participants outside this sample lacked entire measures (e.g., cognitive testing not administered or plasma not yet assayed) rather than individual item-level data points at random; complete-case analysis was therefore used.

As a sensitivity analysis, the mediation model was also evaluated without education as a covariate to assess whether results were robust to its inclusion, given ongoing debate about the role of education in dementia risk50,51. A second sensitivity analysis added QRISK330 cardiovascular risk scores as an additional covariate to assess whether cardiovascular burden accounted for the observed associations (N = 69 with available QRISK3 data).
