Single-cell-marker-based subtyping and multi-level analyses uncover the prognostic effects, dysregulations and therapeutic indicative potential of an eight-gene signature in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Xiuzhi Zhang, Fengqi Chen, Wenke Sun, Hanke Ma, Feifei Liang, Liping Dai, Chunyan Kang, Xiaoli Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the predominant subtype of non-small cell lung cancer, exhibits substantial molecular heterogeneity, complicating the identification of reliable prognostic and therapeutic markers. Single-cell transcriptome analysis, combined with other omics and multi-level analyses, offers a feasible solution to address this issue.MethodsWe clustered, annotated, and identified landmark genes for single cells in LUAD dataset. Using the expression profiles of these marker genes, we classified TCGA-LUAD samples via non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) clustering. The LUAD subtypes were compared for prognosis, tumor stage, gene expression, aging and stemness scores, and immunotherapy response (assessed by TIDE). LASSO-Cox regression analysis selected key genes for a prognostic model, which was validated in training and external datasets. RT-qPCR was used to validate mRNA expression levels. Protein-level dysregulation was confirmed through proteomics and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Additionally, the functional effects of the key genes and their drug-sensitivity associations were explored in LUAD cell lines.ResultsSingle cells were annotated into 12 types/subtypes, including three epithelial-associated and two fibroblast-associated subtypes. The top 100 marker genes for each cell type/subtype were identified. Based on these marker gene expressions, TCGA-LUAD samples were classified into C1 and C2 subtypes. Compared with C1 LUADs, C2 LUADs exhibited poorer prognosis, a higher proportion of late-stage tumors, higher MKI67 expression, and higher levels of aging and stemness, but lower MHC II gene expression and TIDE scores. LASSO-Cox regression analysis identified an eight-gene signature that effectively distinguished patient outcomes in both training and validation datasets. Dysregulation of these eight genes was confirmed at the mRNA level by RT-qPCR and at the protein level by proteomic and IHC analyses. Moreover, these eight genes were associated with sensitivity to 3–28 anti-cancer drugs in LUAD cell lines.ConclusionSubtyping based on single-cell derived marker genes and multi-level analyses provide a comprehensive understanding of LUAD biology. The distinct features of C1 and C2 subtypes highlight the need for tailored therapies. The eight-gene signature may serve as a novel prognostic, diagnostic, and therapeutic indicator.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04403-2.Keywords: Subtyping, Immune response, MHC II, Drug sensitivity, Multi-omics, Prognosis, Lung adenocarcinoma

Introduction

As the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1], lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is characterized by a high degree of heterogeneity, which contributes to its diverse clinical manifestations, therapeutic responses, and prognostic outcomes [2, 3]. Effective molecular subtyping of LUAD can reveal distinct expression patterns that are associated with differential tumor microenvironments. For instance, comprehensive multi-omics analyses have delineated distinct molecular subtypes in early-stage, poorly differentiated LUAD, each associated with unique prognostic outcomes, underscoring the critical need for precise molecular classification [4]. Moreover, research has demonstrated that LUAD cells are capable of undergoing phenotypic transitions, such as transformation into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) or lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), adding another layer of complexity to the disease’s molecular landscape [5]. Therefore, to enhance treatment efficacy and improve patient survival rates, it is of paramount importance to conduct comprehensive molecular subtyping and identify robust biomarkers for LUAD.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized our understanding of the cellular heterogeneity within LUAD by profiling gene expression patterns at single-cell level [6, 7]. This technique has the capacity to identify distinct cellular subpopulations, including tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cells, providing new insights into the tumor microenvironment and cell-cell interactions [8, 9]. In fact, the existence of tumor-specific T cells with higher expression of CXCL13 [10] and the immunosuppressive roles of PLK1 in LUAD microenvironment [11] were revealed by scRNA-seq analyses in recent studies. The intratumor heterogeneity was associated with tumor growth and the efficiency of the treatment. In LUAD, epithelial cell states were reported to be the root of tumorigeneses and KRT8+ alveolar intermediate cells (KACs) with KRAS oncogene mutations could promote the occurrence of tumor [12]. The pivotal roles of multigenic synergistic effects in the development and progression, prognosis and treatment have been recognized for decades [13]. Identification the key cell clusters and their maker genes through scRNA-seq analysis was crucial to illustrate the mechanisms of LUAD and identify new effective therapeutic targets.

Bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis has been widely used in the study of multiple diseases. It is instrumental in identifying the molecular characteristics and key signaling pathways of tumors as a whole, providing essential information for the classification and treatment of many malignancies, including LUAD [14–16]. The strategy of using scRNAseq-derived cell marker genes to analyze bulk transcriptomic data has been extensively applied across both neoplastic and non-neoplastic disease studies, enabling reliable evaluation of cell type enrichment and disease heterogeneity [17–19]. In many malignancies, this approach has been successfully utilized to achieve reasonable patient stratification [20–22]. Proteomics analysis has also been widely employed to validate the protein expression of identified genes, bridging the gap between gene expression data and actual protein levels [23, 24]. This validation step is critical, as it confirms the detected biomarkers are both transcriptionally active and translated into functional proteins, enhancing the credibility of the results. Multidimensional analysis integration helps reveal the complexity of LUAD, and identifies key molecular markers and potential therapeutic targets. However, most existing studies have focused only on specific sets of genes associated with particular immune cell types [25–28] or biological processes [29–31], and the protein-level validation of most identified genes has been lacking. Thus, there is an urgent need for in-depth LUAD studies based on global gene expression profiles derived from both scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq samples, with further validations and explorations.

This study aims to conduct an in-depth molecular typing of LUAD by integrating scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq and proteomic data. The primary objectives are to identify and validate key molecular markers and to explore their associations with LUAD development, prognosis, and therapeutic response. The flowchart of our study was shown in Fig. 1. We anticipate that our findings will provide a scientific basis for personalized LUAD treatment, thereby improving clinical outcomes for patients.

Methods and materials

Processing and analysis of LUAD scRNA-seq Data

The LUAD scRNA-seq dataset GSE131907 was obtained from gene expression omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The primary LUAD tumors (n = 11) and their paired adjacent tissue samples (n = 11) were included for investigation. The clinical feature of the 11 patients was shown in Supplementary Table S1. The Seurat package (v4) was used for scRNA-seq analysis in R. Following the widely adopted quality control (QC) criteria for scRNA-seq data [32], we retained genes detected in at least 5 cells and cells with ≥ 300 expressed genes. Cells exhibiting a mitochondrial transcript proportion above 20%—a threshold previously shown to efficiently remove stressed or apoptotic cells —were likewise excluded. After quality control, a total of 87,881 high-quality cells from 22 samples were retained for subsequent analyses. Unsupervised clustering was performed on the 10-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) space using a k-nearest-neighbor graph followed by modularity optimization (resolution = 0.8) to delineate transcriptionally distinct cell groups. For cell type annotation of the cell clusters, the reported cell markers from previous studies [33–35] and the CellMarker database (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/) were used. For visualization of the cell distribution, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was performed and the cells could be projected into two dimensions. Cell-type-specific gene expression was then visualized with the “dotplot” function, where dot size represents the fraction of expressing cells and color intensity represents the average scaled expression. The proportions of different epithelial cell subtypes and fibroblasts in LUAD and normal lung tissues were calculated and presented in a heatmap. Marker genes (top 100 per subtype) were identified using the COSG package, which detects cluster-specific signatures based on cosine similarity. Using tissue-specific genes as the background, tissue enrichment analysis was performed with the TissueEnrich package to examine whether the marker genes of epithelial-associated cell types were significantly enriched in lung tissue.

NMF clustering of TCGA-LUAD tumors with the marker genes deduced from LUAD scRNA-seq data

The maker gene expression profiles of the cell types deduced from the scRNA-seq data were than analyzed in the bulk-seq TCGA-LUAD dataset (n = 513, Supplementary Table S1) obtained from the GDC data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). We employed non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to stratify the primary tumors into distinct molecular subtypes to investigate their transcriptional heterogeneity. Notably, owing to both the algorithm of COSG and the inherent biological similarity between certain cell types/subtypes, some marker genes were shared across multiple cell type/subtypes. To avoid redundant influence in the clustering process, only unique marker genes were retained for patient stratification. Kaplan–Meier curves were then generated to compare overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) across these LUAD subtypes. Clinical variables—age, sex, and pathologic stage—were tested for association with subtype membership using the Chi-square test. Finally, multivariable Cox regression analysis, adjusted for age and sex, was performed to quantify the independent prognostic value of the subtyping scheme for both OS and DFS in LUAD patients.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identification between different LUAD subtypes and their potential functions

Limma R package was used to identify DEGs between different LUAD subtypes. Genes with ∣log2(fold change)∣ (|log2FC|) > 1 and adjusted p-value (adj.p) < 0.05 were defined as DEGs. Subsequently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted based on these DEGs to identify the disparities in Reactome pathways and molecular functions across different LUAD subtypes. The immunoregulatory genes were obtained from the cancer immunome atlas (https://tcia.at/home). The gene signature associated with aging in Soul study [36] was obtained and the gene set associated with stemness (BHATTACHARYA EMBRYONIC STEM CELL.v7.5.1) were downloaded from Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Based on these gene sets, aging and stemness scores were estimated using the ssGSEA method implemented in the GSVA R package. We further characterized the biological disparities between LUAD subtypes by comparing the expression of immunoregulatory, immune checkpoint, and proliferation marker genes (MKI67), as well as by assessing differences in aging and stemness scores. Based on the gene expression profiles of LUAD samples, immune dysfunction and exclusion levels were quantified by reproducing the core algorithm of the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) tool [37], and differences in these immune-related scores between LUAD subtypes (C1 and C2) were evaluated. The TIDE scores could provide supportive information for tumor immunotherapy.

Construction and validation of the prognostic signature based on the cell type-specific maker genes

Based on the identified DEGs, we extracted the cell-type marker genes that were significantly differentially expressed between the two subtypes. We then conducted Cox regression analysis on these genes, adjusting for age and sex as covariates, to explore their prognostic impacts. Subsequently, genes that demonstrated significant prognostic potential (after adjusting for age and sex) were subjected to LASSO Cox regression analysis. This step aimed to pinpoint independent prognostic factors and establish a risk model for LUAD OS. The discriminative ability of the risk model to predict the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS status of TCGA-LUAD patients was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Patients were divided into low- and high-risk groups based on the median risk score, and differences in OS and DFS were illustrated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Gene expression and clinical data for the LUAD dataset GSE31210 (n = 226, Supplementary Table S1) were acquired from the GEO database. Using the aforementioned risk model, we estimated patient risk scores and performed ROC analysis to validate model performance in this cohort. Gene expression values were z-score normalized across both datasets for risk model construction and validation.

Cell culture and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

The LUAD cell lines A549, PC-9, H1975 and human bronchial epithelial cell line Beas-2b (B2B) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). LUAD cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (BI, Israel) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), while B2B cells were maintained in DMEM medium (BI, Israel) with 10% FBS. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed with a cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). Subsequently, RT-qPCR analysis was carried out using the SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The gene primer sequences were provided in Table 1. The relative expressions of the genes were calculated according to previous studies [38, 39]. The experiments were performed in triplicate and GADPH was used as the endogenous control.

Proteomics analysis of the dysregulations of the key genes

The proteomics data of paired tumor and normal samples from 111 LUAD patients in CPTAC-LUAD dataset (https://proteomic.datacommons.cancer.gov/pdc/) and 103 LUAD patients in Xu study [40] were obtained and analyzed. The clinical features of the patients were shown in Supplementary Table S2. Proteins with “not available” (NA) values in over 85% of the samples were excluded from the analysis. The remaining NA values in the proteomics dataset were imputed using the impute.knn function from the impute R package. The expression differences of the proteins between paired tumor and normal samples were analyzed.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of the proteins in LUAD

Human tissue microarrays consisted of 49 pairs of LUAD tumor tissues and matched pare-tumor lung tissues were purchased from Shanghai Weiaobio Biotechnology Co., Ltd (LugA961, Shanghai, China). Standard IHC [41] was performed to investigate the expression of RHOV and SPOCK1 in LUAD. The anti-RHOV antibody (1:100, Cat No. 26620-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-SPOCK1 antibody (1:100, Cat No. 28203-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), and secondary antibody (anti-Rabbit) IHC Kit (Dako_K5007, DAKO, Denmark) were used for detection according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The clinicopathological features were described in Supplementary Table S2. The IHC analysis was conducted using Visiopharm, an artificial-intelligence pathology detection software, and the H-score for each sample was calculated. The H-score was based on the grayscale values of the slides (0-255, with higher values indicating lighter color). The color intensity was categorized as follows: negative (> 200, intensity: 0), weak (161–200, intensity: 1), moderate (100–160, intensity: 2), and strong (< 100, intensity: 3). Functional effects of key genes in LUAD cell lines

To further explore the functional essentiality of the identified key genes in LUAD and the potential association between their expression levels and the functional effects of other potential target genes, we utilized cancer cell line data from the DepMap portal (Release 25Q3, https://depmap.org/portal/), a resource widely used for investigating gene functional essentiality in cancer [42–44]. Specifically, we extracted CRISPR-based gene effect scores (reflecting the effects of knockout of the target genes) and the expression profiles of the key genes, as well as those of other potential target genes, from 51 LUAD cell lines. These data were used to: (1) assess the impact of individual key gene knockout on cell viability (a negative score indicates the gene promotes LUAD cell proliferation and growth, i.e., functionally essential for viability with knockout impairing growth; a positive score suggests growth inhibition or non-essentiality for survival); and (2) investigate the correlation between the expression levels of the identified key genes and the gene effect scores of other potential target genes. We prioritized target genes with substantial variations in gene effect scores (a gene effect range of > 2 across LUAD cell lines, n = 272), drawing on similar analytical frameworks from previous DepMap-based cancer studies that focus on identifying functionally relevant gene interactions and potential therapeutic targets [45–47]. The rationale underlying this selection is that such genes are more likely to exhibit a strong association between their functional essentiality and key gene expression (i.e., their regulatory effects on LUAD cell viability are more significantly modulated by the key genes), thereby facilitating the identification of clinically relevant combined therapeutic targets. This analysis aims to reveal whether the expression level of key genes is associated with the functional effects of potential target genes, thereby providing potential clues for targeted therapy (e.g., identifying such target genes whose functional essentiality is significantly correlated with the expression of key genes, which may serve as combined therapeutic targets or predictive biomarkers to optimize LUAD treatment strategies).

Investigation of the associations of the key gene expressions with anti-cancer drug sensitivities in LUAD cell lines

The gene expression profiles in cancerous cell lines were downloaded from Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle/datasets) and the key gene expression data in LUAD cell lines were extracted. The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) fitted dose response data (version: 25Feb20) were obtained from Cancerrxgene database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). A total of 54 LUAD cell lines and 175 anti-cancer drugs were included for analysis. Th correlations of the gene expressions with the LN_IC50 (natural log half maximal inhibitory concentration) values of the anti-cancer drugs in the LUAD cell lines were assessed.

Further exploration of drug and chemical interactions with the coding proteins of the key genes

The interactions between the proteins encoded by our key genes and drugs, as well as between these proteins and chemicals, were analyzed using NetworkAnalyst (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/). The relevant protein and drug target data were sourced from the DrugBank database, while the protein-chemical interaction data were retrieved from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). This analysis was conducted to identify potential drugs and influencing factors for these proteins.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1). For RNA-sequencing, proteomic, and IHC data, expression differences of individual genes or proteins between two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between two variables. Survival analyses were performed using univariable and multivariable Cox regression, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and LASSO regression. The predictive value of the model was evaluated using the ROC test. For RT-qPCR data, gene expression comparisons were made using the t-test. A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied to all analyses. For GO enrichment and GSEA, a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05 was used to determine significance.

Results

ScRNA-seq analyses revealed proportional imbalance and cellular heterogeneity in LUAD

After performing dimension reduction and clustering analyses using t-SNE, we identified a total of 27 cell clusters in both LUAD and normal lung tissues (Fig. 2A). Based on the expression patterns of known cell type markers (Supplementary Figure S1), these 27 cell clusters were categorized into nine major cell types (Fig. 2B). As illustrated in Fig. 2C-E, the distribution of epithelial clusters (clusters 9, 14, 15, 20, 21, 24, and 25) and fibroblast clusters (clusters 16 and 20) significantly differed between LUAD and normal cells. Specifically, cluster 15 was exclusively present in LUAD tissues, while cluster 14 constituted nearly one-third of the epithelial cells in LUAD tissues but only 0.21% in normal lung tissues (Fig. 2D), highlighting their strong association with LUAD development. In contrast, cluster 9 accounted for over half of the epithelial cells in normal lung tissues but only 22.24% in LUAD tissues. Similarly, clusters 21, 22, 24, and 25 had higher proportions in normal lung tissues than in LUAD tissues. For subsequent analyses, we designated the cells in clusters 15 and 14 as epithelial_1 and epithelial_2 cells, respectively. Other epithelial cells (clusters 9, 21, 22, 23, and 24), which had higher proportions in normal lung tissues, were collectively termed epithelial_normal cells. Regarding fibroblasts, 94.68% of those in LUAD tissues were in cluster 16, while cluster 20 accounted for over 70% of fibroblasts in normal lung tissues. In further analyses, fibroblasts were divided into different subgroups and named fibroblast_1 (cluster 16) and fibroblast_2 (cluster 20) cells, respectively (Fig. 2D-F). The expression levels of known marker genes in these cell types/subtypes are visualized in Fig. 2G. As shown in Fig. 2H, the heterogeneity of cell compositions among LUAD tumors is evident, underscoring the need for further investigation of cell characteristics in a larger LUAD dataset.

Using the cosg function in the COSG package, the top 100 marker genes for each cell type/subtype were identified and presented in Supplementary Table S3. The top three genes for each cell type/subtype are displayed in Supplementary Figure S2. Tissue enrichment analysis showed that lung was the most significant tissue term associated with the top 100 marker genes of epithelial_normal cells (Supplementary Figure S3A). For epithelial_1 and epithelial_2 cells, lung ranked as the 28th and fifth most enriched tissue terms, respectively (Supplementary Figures S3B and S3C). These findings suggested that epithelial_1 and epithelial_2 cells exhibited divergence from normal lung tissues.

LUAD stratification based on marker genes of the cell types derived from scRNA-seq analysis

Based on the expression profiles of 1102 unique cell type‑specific marker genes (Supplementary Table S3), TCGA‑LUAD patients were stratified into two molecular subtypes (clusters C1 and C2) (Fig. 3A-B). These marker genes showed substantial expression differences between the two subtypes, and 40 representative genes are shown in Fig. 3C. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that C1 patients had longer OS than C2 patients (Fig. 3D). Additionally, C1 patients exhibited a trend towards longer DFS (p = 0.057, p < 0.1, Fig. 3E). Significant differences in age (Fig. 3F) and sex distribution (Fig. 3G) were identified between the two LUAD subtypes. Following adjustment for age and sex, LUAD subtyping remained significantly associated with OS (Fig. 3H) and DFS (Fig. 3I), suggesting independent prognostic value. As presented in Fig. 3J, the C2 subtype had a higher proportion of late-stage (stage III/IV) tumors (25% versus 15% in C1), aligning with the inferior survival of C2 LUAD patients.

DEGs between LUAD subtypes revealed associations with immunoregulation, proliferation, aging, and stemness

Compared with C1 LUAD samples, 191 up-regulated and 379 down-regulated DEGs were identified in C2 tumors (Fig. 4A). Enrichment analyses revealed that the up-regulated DEGs were enriched in cell cycle-related processes, whereas the down-regulated DEGs were enriched in surfactant metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, and the transport of one-carbon compounds, fluids, and lipids (Supplementary Figure S4). Consistent with these findings, GSEA results using Reactome pathways (Fig. 4B-C) and GO terms (Fig. 4D-E) as backgrounds showed up-regulated activities in cell cycle-related processes and down-regulated activities in surfactant and lipid metabolism. Notably, GO molecular function analyses also indicated suppression of MHC class II complex binding in C2 LUAD (Fig. 4E). Compared with the C1 subtype, over half of the immunoregulatory genes exhibited higher or lower expression in the C2 subtype, with most MHC genes, particularly MHC II genes, being down-regulated in C2 LUAD tumors (Supplementary Table S4). Figure 4F shows the top 20 differentially expressed immunoregulatory genes, including eight MHC II genes downregulated in the C2 subtype. In addition, several immune checkpoint genes were found to be differentially expressed between C1 and C2 subtypes. As shown in Fig. 4G-K, CD274, CD276, PDCD1, and LAG3 were shown to be higher expressed while BTLA was lower expressed in C2 subtype than C1 subtype. Consistent with the upregulation of cell cycle processes in C2 subtype, the proliferation maker MKI67 was also higher expressed in C2 LUAD tumors (Fig. 4L). Furthermore, both the aging score (Fig. 4M) and stemness score (Fig. 4N) were shown to be higher in C2 subtype than C1 subtype, indicating difference in the levels of aging and tumor stemness.

Consistent with the distinct immunoregulatory gene expression profiles between the two subtypes, a significant difference in TIDE score was observed between C1 and C2 LUADs (Fig. 5). Given that a higher tumor TIDE score indicates poorer efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [48], the elevated TIDE score in C2 LUADs (Fig. 5A) suggests that these patients may have inferior responses to conventional immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. In addition, the C2 subtype exhibited a lower dysfunction score (Fig. 5B) and a higher exclusion score (Fig. 5C) compared with the C1 subtype, indicating divergent immune escape mechanisms between the two subtypes. Similarly, C2 LUADs presented higher IFNG scores (Fig. 5D), CD8 scores (Fig. 5E), and MDSC levels (Fig. 5F), as well as an upward trend in CAF levels (Fig. 5G), but lower TAM.M2 (tumor-associated macrophages, M2 phenotype) levels (Fig. 5H) relative to C1 LUADs. These findings further highlight the heterogeneous immune response regulation between the two subtypes.

The construction and validation of LUAD prognostic signature based on the heterogeneously expressed marker genes of the cell types

Based on the DEGs between the two subtypes, we identified 159 marker genes that were significantly differentially expressed with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 between the C1 and C2 subtypes (Supplementary Table S5). Notably, most of these genes (103/159, 64.8%) were traced to epithelial cell lineages. We further analyzed these epithelial‑sourced genes based on their epithelial subtypes, and identified distinct expression profiles between subtypes C1 and C2. Of note, seven genes annotated as epithelial_1 subtype marker showed significantly elevated expression in C2 relative to C1. In contrast, all other epithelial‑derived genes presented substantially lower transcript levels in C2 than in C1. These genes included those specific to epithelial_normal (n = 67) and epithelial_2 (n = 25), genes shared by epithelial_normal and epithelial_2 (n = 4), as well as genes with high expression in both epithelial_1 and epithelial_2 (n = 1). Beyond epithelial lineages, heterogeneous expression patterns were also found in genes from other stromal and immune cell populations. For fibroblast subsets, 11 fibroblast_1 subtype genes were upregulated in C2, while 4 were downregulated. By comparison, nine fibroblast_2 genes were downregulated and two were upregulated in C2. All 12 endothelial cell-associated genes were downregulated in subtype C2. Within immune cell populations, two B cell subset-specific genes were downregulated, and one plasma cell gene was upregulated. For mast cell-related genes, nine were downregulated and one was upregulated in C2. The numbers of NK cell, monocyte and macrophage-associated genes were relatively small, and both upregulation and downregulation were observed among these genes.

Through Cox regression analysis, 110 of the 159 genes were also shown to have significant prognostic effects on LUAD OS, independent of age and sex (Supplementary Table S6). Among them, 69 genes with p < 0.01 were investigated for their expressional difference between LUAD and normal lung tissue. Using the cutoff of |log2FC| >1, 61 genes were selected.

These genes were then subjected to LASSO Cox regression analysis, which identified eight independent prognostic factors (Fig. 6A-B). The eight genes (FAM83A, RHOV, SPOCK1, CX3CR1, TMEM125, CPA3, SFTPB, and SLC34A2) and their coefficients were shown in Fig. 6C. Through ROC analysis, the eight-gene signature demonstrated the ability to discriminate the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS status of TCGA-LUAD patients, with AUC values of 0.722, 0.687, and 0.656, respectively (Fig. 6D). Using the median risk score as a threshold, TCGA-LUAD patients were divided into low- and high-risk groups. Patients with higher risk scores exhibited shorter OS (Fig. 6E) and DFS (Fig. 6F) compared to those in the low-risk group. The predictive efficacy of the eight-gene signature was further evaluated in the GSE31210 dataset. The eight-gene model effectively discriminated the OS status of LUAD patients in GSE31210, with AUC values of 0.717, 0.702, and 0.737 for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival, respectively (Fig. 6G). Consistently, patients with high-risk scores were found to have shorter OS and relapse-free survival (RFS), thereby confirming the prognostic value of the eight-gene signature in the TCGA-LUAD dataset.

Dysregulation of mRNA expression of eight key genes in LUAD cell lines

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the expression levels of the eight genes were found to be dysregulated in LUAD cell lines. Functional effects of key genes and their expression-associated target gene functional correlations in LUAD

As shown in Fig. 10A, the identified key genes exhibited significant inter-cell line heterogeneity in their regulatory effects on LUAD cell viability based on CRISPR gene effect scores. Three distinct patterns were observed: (1) CPA3, CX3CR1, SLC34A2 and TMEM125 showed uniformly positive scores in vast majority of the cell lines, indicating these genes inhibit LUAD cell proliferation and growth, with their knockout exerting no adverse effect or even a slight promotive effect on cell viability; (2) SFTPB and FAM83A exhibited consistently negative gene effect scores in a notable proportion of LUAD cell lines, suggesting these genes promote the proliferation and growth of LUAD cells, and their knockout impairs cell viability; (3) RHOV and SPOCK1 had mixed positive and negative scores across different cell lines, reflecting a cell line-dependent regulatory effect—promoting cell growth in some lines but inhibiting it in others. Collectively, these results demonstrate the diverse functional roles of the key genes in regulating LUAD cell viability, and this inter-cell line heterogeneity should be considered when evaluating their potential for LUAD targeted therapy.

Discussion

LUAD is the most common form of lung cancer and exhibits remarkable molecular heterogeneity, which complicates its classification and treatment [51, 52]. The molecular profiling of LUAD has significant implications for the precision treatment of patients. In this study, we combined scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq analyses to dissect the molecular landscape of LUAD. We identified two LUAD subtypes with different prognosis, pathological features, and metabolic characteristics. Compared with the C1 subtype, the C2 subtype with poor survival exhibited higher activity in cell cycle-related processes, higher aging and stemness scores, and lower lipid metabolism activities. These distinct metabolic features may lead to different responses of LUAD patients to anti-cancer drugs.

Our stratification system based on single-cell-derived cell subtype-specific markers not only retained the biological relevance of cellular origins, but also uncovered prominent expression differences in genes derived from epithelial cell subsets, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, B cells and plasma cells between the two subtypes. Many such cell type-specific genes were differentially expressed between C1 and C2, and an eight-gene signature derived from these genes showed dysregulations, independent prognostic effects, and significant correlations with anti-cancer drug sensitivities in LUAD cell lines. These heterogeneous cellular expression patterns imply substantial disparities in the tumor microenvironment composition and biological behaviors of C1 and C2 subtypes. Collectively, our findings highlight the clinical significance of these subtype-specific molecular and cellular characteristics for LUAD diagnosis, prognosis prediction, and the development of personalized therapeutic strategies tailored to distinct subtype features.

Cell cycle checkpoints (CCIs) are pivotal in malignant development and progression, and their inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy for tumors [53, 54]. Aging is also linked to tumorigenesis; for instance, senescent alveolar macrophage accumulation promotes adenoma and LUAD progression in mice [55]. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) exhibit self-renewal and differentiation abilities, with tumor stemness affecting the efficacy of immunotherapy and chemotherapy in cancer patients [56]. In this study, the differences in cell cycle pathways, aging, and stemness levels between the two LUAD subtypes may contribute to heterogeneous treatment responses. These processes could serve as novel therapeutic targets for C2 LUAD.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated efficacy in multiple malignancies [57, 58] and represent a transformative treatment for advanced LUAD [59, 60]. However, ICI responses are highly heterogeneous due to tumor heterogeneity. Tumoral PD-L1 (CD274) expression is a key predictor of immunotherapy efficacy [61], with high expression linked to improved responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Our study revealed upregulated expression of CD274, CD276, PDCD1, and LAG3 in C2 LUADs, suggesting this subtype may be eligible for personalized immunotherapy. Moreover, distinct patterns of immune dysregulation and exclusion were observed between the two LUAD subtypes, and the lower TIDE score in C2 LUADs indicated a more favorable immunotherapeutic response.

MHC-II upregulation is critical for recruiting CD4+ cytotoxic lymphocytes in lung cancer [62], while reduced MHC-II expression diminishes tumor immunogenicity, impairs immune recognition, and decreases TIL infiltration. We identified significant differential expression of MHC-II genes between C1 and C2 LUADs. Downregulated MHC-II expression in C2 LUADs may drive immune escape and associate with poor prognosis. TIDE analysis further revealed that C2 LUADs had higher IFN-γ scores, increased CD8A/B expression and MDSC levels, and lower M2 macrophage infiltration than C1 LUADs, indicating distinct immune regulatory profiles between the two subtypes.

We constructed an eight-gene signature risk model for LUAD prognosis prediction, which demonstrated good efficiency in both the training and validation datasets. Unlike previous studies [63–65], our gene selection was based on differential expression between LUAD subtypes and their prognostic significance adjusted for age and sex. This model not only captures the heterogeneity of LUAD tumors but also reduces biases associated with age and sex. Moreover, the expression of these genes was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels, highlighting their diagnostic potential in LUAD.

The biological functions and prognostic potential of the eight genes in our LUAD prognostic signature have been well documented. FAM83A promotes LUAD stem cell-like phenotypes by stabilizing ErbB2 [66]; RHOV drives LUAD cell proliferation and metastasis via the JNK/c-Jun pathway, with its high expression predicting poor survival [67]. SPOCK1 is m6A-dependently silenced by RBM15, contributing to LUAD osimertinib resistance, and its prognostic and therapeutic value has been validated [68, 69]. These data underscore the complex crosstalk between genetic and epigenetic regulation in LUAD. CX3CR1 is a critical modulator of the LUAD tumor microenvironment [70], and the CXCL11-CX3CR1 axis is selectively dysregulated in smoking-related LUAD [71]. This suggests that cigarette smoking remodels the LUAD tumor microenvironment via this axis, potentially impacting disease progression and therapeutic efficacy. TMEM125 has been identified as a prognostic marker in a transmembrane protein-based LUAD risk model [72], implying its associations with immune infiltration and epigenetic modifications. Our results confirmed its prognostic value, yet its precise mechanisms in LUAD remain to be fully elucidated owing to limited research.

As a canonical marker of mast cell differentiation and maturation [73], CPA3 also shows prognostic significance in LUAD [74]. Our scRNA-seq analysis identified CPA3 as a mast cell marker and validated its independent prognostic role in LUAD, with differential expression observed between C1 and C2 subtypes. These findings support CPA3’s involvement in LUAD progression and treatment. Given mast cells’ key role in shaping the tumor microenvironment, CPA3 dysregulation may underpin the distinct immune landscapes of these LUAD subtypes. Our multi-level analysis validated SFTPB downregulation and its independent prognostic impact in LUAD, consistent with its reported status as an early-advanced stage-specific gene [75]. For SLC34A2, scRNA-seq revealed distinct expression patterns (low mRNA, high protein) in LUAD, corroborating previous reports of its downregulation in LUAD tissues and associations with tumorigenesis [76]. SLC34A2 also acts as a tumor suppressor in NSCLC by inhibiting the MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, suppressing tumor growth and metastasis [77]. Together, this eight-gene expression signature significantly improves the accuracy of LUAD prognosis prediction.

Our correlation analysis between gene expression and gene knockout effects provided novel insights into the functional interactions among key genes in LUAD cell lines. The distinct association patterns identified herein suggested that the candidate genes might actively modulate the biological functions of their target genes. While the functional effects of these candidate genes were measurable yet modest in LUAD cell lines, remarkable correlations were observed between their expression and the gene knockout effects of other target genes. Importantly, FAM83A and TMEM125 displayed more numerous and more significant correlations with other target genes compared with the remaining candidate genes, which may indicate synergistic interactions between these key genes and their corresponding targets in the pathogenesis of LUAD.

Furthermore, we integrated the expression data of eight key genes with the response data for 175 anti-cancer drugs across 54 LUAD cell lines. This analysis revealed significant correlations between the expression of these genes and the sensitivity of LUAD cells to anti-cancer drugs. Specifically, each gene was associated with the therapeutic response to at least three different anti-cancer drugs. For example, FAM83A, RHOV, and SFTPB exhibited correlations with the LN_IC50 values of 22, 18, and 28 anti-cancer drugs, respectively. These findings underscore the potential of these genes to serve as predictive markers for the efficacy of LUAD chemotherapy. We also investigated the interactions between the key proteins and various chemicals, identifying multiple interactions involving the eight proteins. These chemicals may serve as potential risk factors for LUAD development or as novel therapeutic agents for LUAD treatment.

This study has made significant progress in elucidating the molecular characteristics and precision treatment of LUAD. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite the large sample size, the study did not incorporate serum-based analyses. Given the accessibility and convenience of serum as a body fluid for detection, future studies should consider integrating serum research to enhance the comprehensiveness of LUAD molecular profiling. Second, although the eight-gene signature exhibited a significant correlation with anticancer drug sensitivity, these findings have yet to be validated in clinical samples. Additionally, the underlying mechanisms of drug action warrant further investigation. Future research should focus on in-depth mechanistic studies, integrating data from multiple molecular levels, and conducting rigorous validation to refine LUAD molecular classification and precision treatment strategies.

In summary, our comprehensive analysis has shed light on the intricate molecular pathways involved in LUAD. We have delineated two distinct subtypes of LUAD based on single-cell marker profiling. The C2 subtype, associated with a less favorable prognosis, may benefit from ICIs therapy. The dysregulation of cell cycle-related genes and those linked to aging and stemness in C2 LUAD suggests potential therapeutic targets. The enrichment of TAMs, particularly of the M2 phenotype, in the tumor microenvironment of the C1 subtype indicates that M2 macrophages could be a viable target for treatment. Moreover, our eight-gene prognostic signature offers a novel biomarker for predicting LUAD prognosis. The identified key genes, which are deregulated and correlate with anticancer drug sensitivity, may also serve as potential diagnostic markers and predictors for personalized chemotherapy strategies in LUAD.
