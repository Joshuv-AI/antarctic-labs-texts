Application of patient journey mapping in patients with breast cancer: a scoping review
Authors: Yiwen Duan, Jialin Chen, Yajuan Weng, Jiaojiao Zhang, Xuqian Zong, Tingting Cai, Changrong Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis scoping review synthesises the application of patient journey mapping (PJM) in breast cancer care to provide insights for enhancing patient-centred services and improving the quality of life for patients with breast cancer.DesignScoping review.Data sourcesWeb of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang and SinoMed were systematically searched for relevant studies published between 1 May 2005 and 1 May 2024.Eligibility criteriaStudies involving adults (≥18 years) with breast cancer that examined patient journeys were eligible, regardless of whether a formal PJM approach was used. All study designs in healthcare or community settings were considered. Exclusions included studies on other cancers, duplicates, inaccessible full texts, non-English/Chinese publications and non-original articles.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened and extracted data, with a third resolving discrepancies. Information on study characteristics, methods and PJM applications was narratively synthesised and tabulated.ResultsA total of 20 studies published from 2011 to 2024 were included. Four primary approaches to PJM in breast cancer care were identified, with cancer care stage mapping being the most prevalent. PJM illustrated patients’ trajectories, experiences and emotions, revealed critical interaction points such as diagnosis, decision-making and follow-up, and highlighted delays, coordination gaps and opportunities for service improvement. Most studies incorporated four core elements—patients, touchpoints, timelines and experiences—and emphasised patient perspectives across the care continuum, despite methodological heterogeneity.ConclusionsThis review shows that PJM has been applied to illustrate longitudinal healthcare experiences, identifies key touchpoints and supports the design and improvement of breast cancer care services. Most studies underscored patient perspectives and emotional needs, but current PJM applications remain fragmented, hospital-focused and weakly integrated across care settings and families. Future research should refine methodologies and apply digital technologies to develop personalised, dynamic maps that may enhance patient-centred care.Keywords: Patient Care Management, Patient-Centered Care, Breast tumours, Review

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer worldwide and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women.1 Despite advances in early diagnosis and treatment that have improved survival rates, patients with breast cancer continue to experience reduced quality of life due to the side effects and long-term consequences of surgeries, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and hormone therapies.2 3 Patients and their families face complex medical decisions, such as selecting appropriate treatment options and ensuring adherence to care plans, while also coping with the psychological and social challenges associated with the disease.4 Currently, there is a lack of continuity in medical care for patients with breast cancer, particularly for those in rural areas who must travel long distances to urban centres for treatment and seek care in multiple locations. This situation complicates the coordination of treatment, often leading to treatment delays and increasing the psychological distress of patients.5 The accessibility of continuous cancer care is further constrained by limited healthcare resources, inadequate healthcare provider-to-patient ratios and significant communication gaps between patients and healthcare professionals, all of which contribute to increased waiting times and the burden on patients.5 6 Moreover, conventional breast cancer care often fails to account for individual differences, negatively impacting the overall patient experience.7 Therefore, a comprehensive description of the treatment and personal experiences of patients with breast cancer not only enhances our understanding of their needs and challenges in the disease management process but also serves as a reference for optimising the overall quality of patient care and promoting more personalised and continuous medical services.

Journey mapping, which originated in market research, has increasingly been recognised as a valuable tool to investigate and capture the emotional, psychological and social experiences of individuals and groups within complex systems.8 With its growing application in healthcare, patient journey mapping (PJM) has emerged as a patient-centred approach that encompasses the entire trajectory from the discovery of symptoms and diagnosis through treatment and recovery.9 PJM enables detailed documentation of patient interactions with the complex healthcare system, including appointment reminders, prescription management, self-management, health monitoring and follow-up.10 Structurally, PJM is typically organised along two axes: the horizontal axis, which is divided according to research objectives and often represents diagnostic, treatment and follow-up phases or focuses on specific experiences within one stage; and the vertical axis, which reflects patients’ emotions, behaviours and perceptions.11 This framework facilitates visual analysis of various stakeholders, helping researchers identify gaps and opportunities for improvement in healthcare processes, redesign care pathways, reduce delays in consultations and symptom management, and provide more patient-centred, personalised healthcare solutions.12 13 PJM has already been applied in diverse disease contexts. For example, in cardiovascular care, it has revealed challenges in long-term medication adherence and lifestyle adjustments, thereby optimising follow-up strategies.14 In chronic disease management, it has been used to identify barriers in self-monitoring and interprofessional communication, promoting the adoption of digital solutions. Similarly, in burn and paediatric care, PJM has been applied to evaluate the cultural appropriateness and safety of healthcare services.15 Collectively, these applications demonstrate that PJM not only holds theoretical value but also offers practical guidance for advancing clinical practice.

Compared with other cancer populations, patients with breast cancer exhibit distinct characteristics in their disease trajectory and care needs. The course of breast cancer extends through multiple stages, including diagnosis, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine or targeted therapy and long-term follow-up, during which patients must cope with complex physical symptoms and evolving psychosocial challenges, resulting in highly dynamic and heterogeneous supportive care needs.16 Evidence from a cross-sectional study in Turkey indicated that postoperative needs vary by treatment and demographic factors: patients receiving chemotherapy and radiotherapy reported greater physical and daily life needs, while younger women expressed stronger psychological and intimacy-related needs.17 Similarly, a qualitative study from Chile revealed that young breast cancer survivors in the follow-up phase faced unmet needs for information, rehabilitation and psychological support.18 As a predominantly female group, patients with breast cancer frequently endure the lasting impact of mastectomy or altered appearance on body image and gender identity, while simultaneously shouldering family and social role pressures, making their care needs more complex than those of other cancer populations.19 20 Furthermore, the elevated risk of recurrence and metastasis leaves many patients with breast cancer with enduring fear of recurrence, while existing cancer management models inadequately address this long-term psychosocial burden and lack tailored support measures.21 Within this context, PJM provides a systematic framework to integrate patient experiences and needs across disease stages, uncover gaps between expectations and reality, and identify critical touchpoints and pain points, thereby guiding targeted interventions and optimising resource allocation.14 22 23 A qualitative study focusing on patients with breast cancer further demonstrated that PJM, when used as an assessment tool, not only outperforms generic approaches but also offers evidence-based support for refining care models and updating clinical practice guidelines.4

Although PJM has been explored in breast cancer care, inconsistencies remain in mapping methods, reported outcomes and reporting standards, and no systematic review has yet synthesised the available evidence.10 24 To address this gap, this study undertakes a scoping review to systematically analyse and integrate the applications and methodological features of PJM in breast cancer care, providing evidence-based insights to optimise clinical practice, support patient needs and guide future research.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was guided by the methodological framework of Arksey et al,25 which involves the following steps: identifying the research questions, locating relevant studies, selecting eligible literature, analysing the data and organising and summarising the findings.

Research questions

This review addresses the following research questions: how has PJM been carried out in studies involving patients with breast cancer? what purposes and applications of PJM have been reported in breast cancer care?

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in eight databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang Data and SinoMed. The search also included references from relevant articles. The time frame for the search spanned from 1 May 2005 to 1 May 2024. A combination of free-text terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) was used for the search strategy. For example, the PubMed search strategy was: (Patient journey OR Patient journey map OR Patient journey mapping OR Journey map OR Journey mapping OR Care journey) AND (Breast Neoplasms [MeSH Terms] OR Breast cancer OR Breast Tumors OR Breast Carcinoma OR Cancer of Breast). The full search strategies for all databases are provided in online supplemental file 2.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Based on the PCC (Participants, Concept, Context) framework,25 the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Participants: adults aged 18 years or older diagnosed with breast cancer; (2) Concept: original studies related to PJM in breast cancer, including randomised controlled trials, observational studies, retrospective studies, qualitative studies, descriptive studies and prospective studies. (3) Context: studies conducted in settings such as community health service centres, hospitals, communities or home environments. The exclusion criteria included: (1) studies involving participants with other concurrent cancers; (2) duplicate publications or studies without full-text availability; (3) letters, editorials, conference abstracts or protocol papers; (4) studies published in languages other than Chinese or English.

Study selection and data extraction

The retrieved records were imported into EndNote 20 for deduplication. Two researchers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the studies based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by a full-text review for a secondary screening. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers and cross-checked for accuracy, capturing information on study characteristics and key findings.

Data synthesis

The extracted data were synthesised narratively in accordance with scoping review methodology. Key study characteristics, including author and year of publication, country or region, research theme, research design, research object and sample size, were tabulated. Information on whether the study involved a formal PJM methodology, the specific mapping method applied, and the purpose of its application was also recorded. Findings were organised by study context, methodological approach and applications of PJM, with common patterns and differences across studies summarised to address the research questions.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Literature screening results

A total of 1208 articles were identified: 702 from Web of Science, 376 from PubMed, 93 from CINAHL, 35 from Cochrane Library and 2 from Embase. No relevant studies were found in SinoMed, Wanfang Data or CNKI. After removing 333 duplicates, 123 studies were screened by title and abstract. Following a thorough full-text review, 20 articles met the inclusion criteria. The detailed selection process is illustrated in figure 1.

Flow diagram of literature screening.

Basic characteristics of included studies

A total of 20 studies were included, published between 2005 and 2024, across diverse regions including Asia, Africa, Europe and North America. Research designs were mainly qualitative, with several cross-sectional, cohort or retrospective studies. Sample sizes ranged from small-scale qualitative studies with eight participants to large retrospective analyses of over 76 000 social media entries. Study populations encompassed patients at different disease stages, survivors and occasionally caregivers. Most studies explicitly applied PJM, most commonly stage-based or key event mapping, while a few involved multi-stakeholder approaches or lacked formal PJM methodology. Reported applications focused on treatment pathways, patient experiences, decision-making and access to care. Despite methodological heterogeneity, the studies consistently highlighted patient perspectives and emotional needs. Detailed characteristics are presented in online supplemental table 1.

Construction of PJM in patients with breast cancer

Mapping steps

Most included studies did not detail specific mapping steps. However, by summarising and analysing the literature, we identified six main steps: defining the problem in practice or research, consulting with healthcare providers and patients, designing research questions or project objectives, selecting the appropriate mapping type, collecting and analysing data and reporting findings.14 For example, Ciria-Suarez et al4 studied the experiences of women with breast cancer at various stages. They began by defining the patient journey, then set an objective to explore standard care pathways and mapped the patient journey framework accordingly. After consulting with oncologists and nurses, they developed an interview outline, structured the map around the stages of breast cancer care, recruited participants, conducted interviews, analysed qualitative data, and finally reported their findings, as well as the journey map.

Mapping methods

17 studies involved the construction of patient journey maps, of which 11 used the breast cancer care stage mapping as a framework,426,35 4 studies focused on key event mapping36,39 and 2 studies adopted multi-stakeholder mapping, such as patients, healthcare providers and navigators.40 41 No study used healthcare location-based mapping as a main framework.

In this review, journey maps constructed by breast cancer care stages typically feature clear transitions, with behavioural and emotional trajectories showing distinct changes at each phase.426,35 A study conducted in Spain exploring the patient journeys of female patients with breast cancer at different stages included interviews with 21 patients who had a clear medical process.4 The resulting PJM was divided into seven stages based on the breast cancer care stage mapping: diagnosis, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, follow-up care, recurrence and metastatic breast cancer. Throughout the patient journey, there are common emotional experiences such as feelings of loss and fear. However, the manifestation of these shared emotions may vary across different stages, and there are corresponding differentiated emotional experiences as the patient’s journey progresses. For instance, during the diagnostic phase, patients may encounter feelings of uncertainty, unreality, anger and denial. In the surgical phase, there may be uncertainty regarding the surgical plan and outcomes. During chemotherapy, patients often worry about adverse reactions, while the radiotherapy phase is marked by fatigue. In the follow-up care phase, patients may experience a sense of loss of control due to difficulties in returning to normal life. The recurrence phase is characterised by fear, and in the metastatic breast cancer phase, patients may feel isolation, suffering from treatment and a sense of devaluation stemming from not being understood by others. Similarly, Jacobs et al29 categorised the PJM of patients with breast cancer into four stages: screening and diagnosis, information seeking, emergency care and treatment, and no evidence of disease, each with unique responsibilities, challenges and personal journeys. For example, in the information-seeking phase, the responsibilities include information filtering and organisation, clinical decision-making and preparation, while the challenges involve comprehending the vast amount of information and treatment options, with the personal journey focused on coping strategies.

In studies mapping the patient journey based on key events, the stages of diagnosis and treatment may be less defined or incomplete, yet key events with significant impact on the patient or disease treatment are still identified.36,39 For instance, a qualitative study at a UK oncology centre analysed the journeys of newly diagnosed patients with breast cancer over a year, mapping key events such as interpersonal experiences, returning to work and symptom self-management.38 Additionally, key event mapping proved useful for analysing social media posts. A retrospective analysis across 14 European countries reviewed social media posts from Twitter, forums and blogs about the experiences of patients with metastatic breast cancer. By summarising key events such as treatment, diagnosis, examination and disease management, the study outlined the experiences and emotional changes of patients, revealing that despite the poor prognosis, advancements in detection and treatment have positively impacted overall survival rates.39

When patients with breast cancer interact with multiple stakeholders, mapping the patient journey from the perspectives of different perspectives is more feasible.40 41 Nightingale et al40 mapped journeys from both patient and staff viewpoints in a breast cancer assessment clinic, while Cherif et al41 analysed patient and healthcare provider interactions to highlight care delivery touchpoints.

Mapping tools

PJM approaches included formal journey mapping tools, qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods, each differing in application.14 This review found that the majority of past studies adopted qualitative research techniques. For example, a Malaysian study summarised early-stage breast cancer decision-making processes through semi-structured interviews based on breast cancer care stage mapping.26 However, qualitative methods relying on interviews and focus groups often led to variations in journey map structures and content. Ciria-Suarez et al4 and Haozous et al36 both employed qualitative interviews but differed in sampling and analysis methods. The former used purposive sampling across nine Spanish hospitals and analysed data by thematic analysis to construct stage-based maps, detailing emotional and experiential challenges at each stage.4 In contrast, Haozous et al36 developed a conceptual model of the cancer experience, named ‘Rough Waters’ by analysing data from two focus groups of cancer survivors, which identified barriers such as delays, isolation, communication challenges and financial concerns, while also highlighting that factors like advocacy, spirituality and family involvement could help make the cancer journey relatively quicker and easier for patients.

Quantitative methods provided objective data but also showed variability in mapping due to different research focuses. Boucheron et al27 used cohort analysis to identify delays among racially diverse patients with breast cancer, summarising the time points of diagnostic delays, including pre-contact, diagnostic and treatment intervals. Moodley et al32 conducted a cross-sectional survey of South African women, analysing patient data using rank-sum tests and regression models within a treatment pathway model framework that combines the patient journey to explore the pathway from the discovery of breast cancer symptoms to treatment, detailing the time intervals and related factors between symptom detection and treatment.

Current applications of PJM in patients with breast cancer

Understanding patients’ experiences

Patient journey maps provide insights into the experiences of patients with breast cancer. A year-long study at a cancer centre in the UK employed a ‘cancer journey’ heuristic tool to map the experiences of women during the first year following a breast cancer diagnosis, and through the analysis of interview transcripts, provided insights into patients’ relational experiences, return-to-work challenges and self-management of symptoms.38 Furthermore, applying PJM to the analysis of breast cancer-related information on social media offers a detailed portrayal of their medical journeys, as well as the evolving emotional and informational needs. Mazza et al39 conducted an in-depth analysis of posts related to metastatic breast cancer across platforms such as Twitter, forums and blogs, gaining valuable insights into treatment, diagnostic processes and disease management. Another qualitative study on the French patient forum ‘les Impatientes’ used journey mapping to segment the health trajectory into stages of discovery, examination, follow-up and survivorship, while also categorising patient groups into newcomers, altruists and self-advocates.41

Analysing healthcare-seeking behaviours

PJM for patients with breast cancer clearly illustrates the entire healthcare-seeking process, assisting in decision-making and the analysis of adverse healthcare behaviours. In Malaysia, researchers conducted qualitative interviews with early-stage patients with breast cancer, mapping the decision-making process into four distinct stages: awareness, diagnosis, deliberation and decision, which shed light on how patients interpret diagnostic information and make treatment choices.26

Beyond aiding patient decisions, journey mapping is also instrumental in identifying and analysing diagnostic and treatment delays. Mohd Mujar et al31 interviewed newly diagnosed patients with breast cancer and used a care timeliness framework to analyse intervals from symptom detection to treatment initiation, revealing significant diagnostic delays, especially in patients without a breast lump, highlighting symptom type as a key factor. Another interview-based study of newly diagnosed patients with breast cancer in South Africa used the model of the pathways to treatment framework to examine the patient journey, revealing that a lack of breast self-awareness and limited recognition of breast cancer symptoms delayed the time at which women perceived bodily changes as abnormal.37 A cross-sectional study in the same region quantified the time interval between symptom detection and breast cancer treatment through PJM, emphasising failure to recognise the severity of cancer symptoms was a significant risk factor for delays in seeking medical attention, with notable differences in presentation intervals between high- and middle-to-low-income countries.32 A cross-sectional study conducted in Morocco examining the patient with cancer journey identified that older age, higher educational attainment, access to patient navigation and higher household income were associated with shorter diagnostic intervals.42

Therefore, using PJM as an analytical framework helps identify factors influencing adverse healthcare behaviours, offering valuable guidance for improvement.

Identifying key touchpoints in the patient journey

PJM allows for the identification of key touchpoints in the care pathway from multiple perspectives—including patients, healthcare providers, navigators and caregivers—thereby offering critical moments for accurate information that enable patients to seek medical care more promptly and effectively, ultimately improving their quality of life.43 Taib et al33 interviewed 19 advanced patients with breast cancer in Malaysia, analysing delays from both patient and provider viewpoints. They developed the total breast cancer delay (TBCD) and breast cancer delay explanatory (BCDE) models. The TBCD model encompasses four components: a diverse healthcare system, delay points, patient or system delays and patient decision-making at each stage, with eight identified delay points. The BCDE model includes the assessment of severity and the process of understanding and selecting options, consisting of four main components: understanding the disease and its outcomes, understanding treatment and treatment outcomes, psychological resources and support, and the decision-making role. Each point of the TBCD model contributes to the emergence of the BCDE model. These two models help assess disease severity, understand and choose options, and deconstruct the reasons why women present with advanced breast cancer and resist treatment, thereby clarifying the issues and opportunities for intervention.

Identifying gaps and unmet needs in healthcare services

PJM provides a comprehensive analysis of patients’ healthcare experiences, allowing for the identification of gaps in current healthcare services and unmet patient needs. A Canadian interview-based study involving patients with cancer and caregivers analysed patients’ navigation of online information, developing an orientation theory based on six interconnected concepts: cancer challenges, orientation, consequences of challenges, information sources, human and external factors, and internet content design. This study revealed the significant challenges and unmet informational needs patients faced following a cancer diagnosis.34 In Europe, a content analysis of social media posts related to metastatic breast cancer examined patients’ shared experiences online, identifying the lack of effective treatments and side-effect management as two critical unmet needs.39 A qualitative study in the USA employing grounded theory and patient journey analysis examined the perspectives of patients with breast cancer on access and identified six core dimensions—information, psychosocial support, health insurance, financial resources, timeliness and emotional support—that together capture both opportunities for accessing care and the ongoing support required across treatment.44 Collectively, these studies highlight the multifaceted unmet needs of patients with cancer regarding information, treatment support and resource access, offering valuable insights for optimising patient support systems.

Informing healthcare service and tool design

PJM serves as a reference for designing and improving healthcare services and tools. A study conducted in the USA investigated the application of mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) therapy among breast cancer survivors, categorising the patient journey into cognitive transformation, treatment and recovery stages, and demonstrating that the integration of MBSR as a complementary therapy facilitated a shift in mindset, which helped participants to live in the present moment, enhance their sense of well-being and improve their self-care practices.35 Furthermore, PJM facilitates quality improvement initiatives for breast cancer care. For instance, a framework for timely breast cancer care has been proposed based on a study of three consecutive episodes of patient visits, diagnosis and treatment, encompassing visit, diagnostic and treatment intervals, along with four sub-intervals for diagnosis, to facilitate the examination of the patient’s disease course, identify barriers to early detection, diagnosis and treatment, and offer crucial insights for cancer control planning.31

Discussion

This study identifies both commonalities and distinctions in the construction of PJM for breast cancer across various research contexts. We identified four primary mapping approaches: by breast cancer care stages, key events, perspectives of different stakeholders (eg, patients, healthcare providers, navigators) and healthcare locations. PJM provides a visual representation of the patient’s journey, illustrating a comprehensive pathway across all stages of care and reflecting patients’ emotional experiences. It is commonly depicted through flowcharts, mind maps or other visual tools, with variations reflecting the specific nature of each study.45 Although the included studies employ various mapping approaches, the resulting maps, while not standardised replicas, collectively represent diverse forms, structures and focal points in illustrating patient journeys.46

Despite the variation in mapping approaches for the breast cancer population, the patient journey maps included in the studies generally encompass four key components: users (patients, healthcare professionals and healthcare managers), touchpoints, timelines and patient experiences. These maps often centre on the patient’s perspective, detailing emotional trajectories to visually track changes and provide appropriate support. In this review, most studies collected patient journey data through qualitative interviews, with a subset incorporating quantitative methods. Additionally, some studies employed flexible approaches, such as a study on depression and diabetes that used case tracking with quantitative and qualitative data from multiple sources, allowing patient journeys to be constructed by tracking individuals through primary care over defined periods.47 This review also includes studies that employ a combination of social media analysis and qualitative research to explore the journey of patients with metastatic breast cancer.41 Future PJM could integrate diverse methods, including qualitative, quantitative and case-tracking, alongside extensive internet data.

The mapping of patient journeys is characterised by specificity and diversity, with each study potentially selecting stages and perspectives suited to its research setting and goals, such as mapping by breast cancer care stages, key events or roles (eg, patient, provider, navigator), which contributes to the diversity of the resulting patient journey maps. Cancer care stage mapping, for example, provides a structured view of patients’ needs and issues, helping to understand their treatment experiences and identify critical issues and intervention points, but may overlook individual differences and stage-specific details.4 Key event mapping can help analyse the impact of pivotal events, such as surgery, chemotherapy or recurrence, on patients’ psychological and physical states, but may not capture long-term effects and continuity.37 Multi-stakeholder mapping provides a multidimensional analysis reflecting varied perspectives and needs, which can address potential conflicts, although it adds complexity and challenges for integration.41 Location-based mapping clearly highlights patient experiences in different settings, helping to optimise workflows and layouts, though it may neglect external influences.46 Hence, mapping approaches should be tailored to specific research contexts.

This study reveals that PJM for breast cancer is primarily applied to understand patient experiences, analyse healthcare-seeking behaviours, identify critical touchpoints to optimise care processes, detect service gaps and inform the design of healthcare tools. PJM places patients’ experiences at the centre, capturing their care trajectories and interactions with healthcare providers, offering physicians insights into patients’ recovery needs and facilitating compassionate care.38 48 49 PJM also provides valuable perspectives on how disease impacts different roles, as illustrated by a review of fibromyalgia patient journeys, which analysed negative effects on individuals, society and healthcare systems.50 Future PJM for breast cancer may integrate both individual and societal perspectives, aligning emotional and care needs across treatment stages to enhance overall care quality and patient satisfaction. PJM can pinpoint key issues in treatment delays and decision-making, providing targeted solutions. The included studies identified factors affecting delays by analysing the intervals between symptom onset, diagnosis and treatment, thereby offering a basis for optimising care processes.31 37 Similarly, Cunnington et al51 identified significant delays at each stage of the patient journey from symptom onset to pacemaker implantation, with a substantial number of patients experiencing delays due to failure to refer for implantation after documenting the indication, offering valuable insights for streamlining and optimising patient assessment and referral process. Moreover, by analysing the decision-making process, PJM facilitates transparent shared decision-making between patients and healthcare providers.26 52 In recent years, an increasing number of studies are incorporating digital technologies, such as artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things and telemedicine, into PJM development to use real-time data for mapping of patients with breast cancer journeys.53

In terms of identifying critical touchpoints and optimising workflows, PJM allows multifaceted analysis of care pathways, offering precise informational support. Studies included in this review identified key touchpoints from both patient and provider perspectives, proposing delay models to identify healthcare barriers, thereby enabling timely interventions and improved patient experiences.33 A study on work participation among patients with cardiovascular disease employed PJM to visualise work-focused healthcare trajectories, capturing changing experiences and needs across time and care settings, and delineated six key system-level stages together with nine patient-perceived opportunities for improvement, which align with the findings of this review.54 Thus, PJM for breast cancer may benefit from adapting delay models and system-level stage frameworks, integrating patient feedback and healthcare practices to streamline care pathways, enhance intervention strategies and improve overall care quality. The PJM for breast cancer highlights gaps in healthcare services and unmet patient needs, providing critical insights into the needs of patients and families during transitions and the factors influencing their quality of life, thereby informing future interventions to improve these transitions.13

PJM also offers crucial insights for designing and refining healthcare services and tools. By understanding patients’ emotional experiences and needs at different stages of care, more patient-centred and effective healthcare tools can be developed. Studies incorporated in this review identified that introducing psychological interventions into the PJM for patients with breast cancer can improve patients’ psychological well-being and treatment experience.35 One study, for example, employed a co-creation approach with patients and healthcare providers to develop a massive open online course for women with breast cancer, using PJM to tailor content to patients’ digital health literacy needs and care experiences, thereby enhancing both literacy and quality of care.55 Moreover, PJM can further support the development of evidence-based decision-support tools and safety monitoring systems to optimise cancer care pathways. This has been exemplified by the 4D PICTURE project, which created data-driven tools to improve shared decision-making and redesign treatment pathways, and by the implementation of an unobtrusive event-tracking system in operating rooms to enhance safety and care quality.56 57 Integrating data with information technology, PJM helps address healthcare challenges and advance overall care quality.58 Some studies have combined health information technology with PJM, developing integrated patient journey maps to analyse support needs and aid multidisciplinary teams in creating effective health information technology solutions to address quality issues.59 Therefore, future PJM for breast cancer may benefit from psychological interventions and co-creation approaches, enhancing evidence-based decision tools and information technology applications, advancing care quality and solutions to healthcare challenges.

In summary, PJM for patients with breast cancer presents the patients’ healthcare experience as a longitudinal and continuous journey and focuses on patient-centred care, which allows patients, families and healthcare providers to interact at each touchpoint and receive valuable feedback.60 61 The application of PJM in breast cancer management and care holds significant value, as it captures the complex experiences of patients across various treatment stages and contexts. This provides a framework for a deeper understanding of their experiences, needs and expectations, and aids in identifying pain points and opportunities for improvement in healthcare services. However, existing tools tend to focus primarily on institutional medical process design, with insufficient attention given to mapping the journey from the perspectives of patients and their families, or across multiple healthcare settings. Therefore, it is essential to integrate all these perspectives, emphasising the journey’s complexity and the need for high-quality, culturally competent care, including clinical nursing.62 In recent years, the emergence of smartphones, wearable devices and health informatics has enabled the real-time monitoring of the entire patient journey. In the future, the integration of these technologies can further enhance the PJM for patients with breast cancer.10

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. The variability in study methodologies and PJM approaches restricted direct comparisons. Most studies were qualitative, which, while valuable for understanding patient experiences, limits insights into the broader impact of PJM on patient outcomes. The exclusion of grey literature, dissertations and books may also have led to the omission of alternative perspectives. Finally, we were unable to include studies published in languages other than English and Chinese, which may have introduced language bias.

Conclusion

The construction of PJM for patients with breast cancer exhibits diversity, with mapping by cancer care stages, key events, stakeholder perspectives and healthcare locations, among which the first approach is the most common. Although various mapping methods were applied, some commonalities emerged, with method selection depending on specific research characteristics. PJM for patients with breast cancer is currently limited to understanding patient journeys and optimising care processes. Future research should focus on standardising construction steps, refining data collection and visualisation methods, and incorporating digital technologies, psychological interventions and co-creation strategies to enhance the design and application of PJM. Additionally, exploring the application of PJM in multidisciplinary collaboration and quality assessment can contribute to providing more efficient and high-quality healthcare services for patients with breast cancer.
