Influenza A as a Potential Mimic of Central Nervous System Infections in Pediatric Patients
Authors: Gulhadiye Avcu, Coskun Ekemen, Asli Arslan, Emine Cigdem Ozer, Zumrut Sahbudak Bal
Published: 2025

Influenza is a febrile acute respiratory illness caused by Influenza viruses (A, B, and C) and an important public health problem due to its seasonal epidemics worldwide.1 It has been reported to be responsible for 290 000-670 000 hospitalizations and 17 000-98 000 deaths in the 2022-2023 season in the United States.2

With the Influenza peak in mid-January 2024 in our country, an increase in the number of patients presenting with meningeal irritation findings was noted. The aim of this report is to describe pediatric patients who were hospitalized with a preliminary diagnosis of central nervous system (CNS) disease and diagnosed with Influenza A despite showing no symptoms of respiratory involvement.

Eight children were hospitalized consecutively between January and February 2024. Of the patients, four were male, with a median age of 71 months (18 months-15 years). All had a fever above 38.5°C that lasted more than 24 hours [median; 3 days (1-7 days)]. They presented with tonic–clonic convulsions, lethargy, and meningeal irritation findings. There were comorbidities in four patients (chronic neurological disorders and neurometabolic disease). No pathological enhancement or diffusion restriction was detected on contrast-enhanced cranial magnetic resonance imaging in any patient. Electroencephalography was performed in five patients during the acute period, and all showed regular activity. Lumbar puncture could be performed in six patients. No microorganisms grew in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) culture, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of CSF for Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) 1-2, Human Herpesvirus-6 (HHV-6), Varicella-zoster virus (VZV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Enterovirus, Adenovirus, and Parechovirus was negative. We could not perform a PCR test for Influenza on CSF. The patients’ symptoms completely resolved after 24 hours and simultaneously respiratory virus PCR panel revealed Influenza A in all patients without any co-infection. With these results, the treatments initiated for possible CNS infection were switched to oseltamivir and completed for five days. No other system involvement (muscle, myocardium, etc.) due to Influenza was detected. There were no complications during outpatient follow-up. None of the patients, including the patient with a previous history of HSV, limbic encephalitis, and epileptic seizures (patient 3), continued to have seizures after the Influenza ended (Table 1).

Influenza usually has a self-limiting and uncomplicated clinical course in healthy children; however, it can cause severe morbidity and mortality in at-risk groups.3 Central nervous system complications (e.g. febrile convulsion, encephalopathy, encephalitis, and aseptic meningitis) have been reported in approximately 8-10% of children hospitalized with Influenza. Other rarely seen neurologic complications include cerebrovascular accidents, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, and Guillain–Barré syndrome. In recent years, both Influenza A and B have been reported to be responsible for neurological complications.3-5

In summary, eight children with Influenza A presenting with symptoms mimicking CNS infections have been reported. Primary CNS involvement of Influenza was also considered in these patients however, Influenza PCR could not be performed on the CSF to confirm this. Additionally, the patients' clinical improvement within 24 hours suggested that their neurological symptoms may have been a temporary effect of Influenza A, the exact mechanism remains unclear. Even in the absence of symptoms of respiratory disease, Influenza should be kept in mind before proceeding with further investigations and treatments, especially during seasonal outbreaks.
