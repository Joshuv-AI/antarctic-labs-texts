Longitudinal multisource clinical model for early lung cancer risk stratification and screening
Authors: Chia-Hui Chien, Shih-Chuan Chang, Yung-Chun Chang, Yu-Chuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with poor prognosis largely due to late-stage diagnosis. Current screening methods such as low-dose CT face accessibility and cost barriers in resource-limited settings. This study develops a lightweight multichannel convolutional neural network for lung cancer screening support through longitudinal risk stratification using routine pre-diagnostic healthcare data.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database, comprising 99 615 individuals (575 lung cancer cases; 99 040 non-cancer controls). Diagnostic codes, medication records and medical orders within a 36-month observation window were extracted. Log-likelihood ratio feature selection was implemented to reduce dimensionality, achieving 99.8% reduction in computational requirements while retaining clinical relevance. A multichannel Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture was designed to process these heterogeneous data modalities simultaneously.ResultsThe proposed method achieved an F₁-score of 0.5738, precision of 0.7149, Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) of 0.8316 and Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) of 0.1617, outperforming baseline methods in precision and F₁-score. Ablation studies confirmed that medical orders provide primary predictive value, while medication features contribute limited discriminative signal in the pre-diagnostic phase. SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis revealed that routine healthcare utilisation patterns, rather than cancer-specific features, drive risk stratification.DiscussionThe lightweight architecture enables deployment in resource-constrained clinical environments while maintaining robust performance, offering potential as a preliminary screening tool to identify high-risk individuals for further diagnostic examination.ConclusionEfficient deep learning models using routine clinical data can facilitate lung cancer risk stratification and screening, providing a scalable solution for clinical implementation.Keywords: Decision Support Systems, Clinical; Electronic Health Records; Health Information Interoperability; Medical Informatics Applications; Deep Learning

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. In 2022, approximately 2.5 million new cases were diagnosed globally, resulting in 1.8 million deaths.1 The situation is particularly concerning in Taiwan, where lung cancer accounts for approximately 15 000 new diagnoses and over 9000 deaths annually.2 The fundamental challenge in lung cancer management lies in the timing of diagnosis. Early-stage detection significantly improves patient prognosis, with survival rates exceeding 80% when tumours are identified before metastatic spread.3 However, the asymptomatic nature of early-stage disease creates a detection paradox where patients typically present with symptoms only after progression to advanced stages. Low-dose CT (LDCT) has shown promise in reducing lung cancer mortality in high-risk populations, with the National Lung Screening Trial demonstrating approximately 20% mortality reduction compared with chest radiography.4 5 Yet widespread LDCT implementation faces substantial barriers including high operational costs, limited accessibility in resource-constrained healthcare systems and false-positive rates reaching 24% in some screening programmes.6 These limitations highlight the need for efficient preliminary screening tools that can identify high-risk individuals who would benefit most from further diagnostic examination.

Artificial intelligence has created opportunities to transform medical diagnostics through analysing complex clinical data patterns.7 8 Deep learning architectures have achieved remarkable performance in medical applications, while electronic health records and administrative healthcare databases offer rich longitudinal information including diagnostic histories, medication patterns and medical orders.9 10 Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) represents one of the world’s most comprehensive population-based healthcare datasets, containing longitudinal clinical information for virtually the entire Taiwanese population and providing an exceptional foundation for developing AI-driven prediction models.11 12 However, realising the potential of such heterogeneous clinical data requires analytical frameworks capable of processing multiple data modalities, while preserving temporal relationships. Traditional machine learning approaches often struggle with the complexity and dimensionality of longitudinal clinical data. Moreover, computational demands of sophisticated deep learning models may limit their practical deployment in resource-constrained clinical environments, where efficient preliminary screening tools are most needed.

In this study, we focus on longitudinal risk stratification using routine pre-diagnostic healthcare data to support lung cancer screening, rather than direct detection of cancer presence at the time of inference. Our approach integrates 3 years of longitudinal patient data from NHIRD, incorporating diagnostic codes, medication records and medical orders to create comprehensive clinical profiles. A systematic feature selection strategy based on log-likelihood ratio was integrated with the CNN architecture to substantially reduce computational requirements while retaining clinical relevance, enabling deployment in resource-limited settings. Through this integration of efficient deep learning and population-scale healthcare data, we aim to explore whether lightweight CNN architectures can effectively extract temporal patterns from routine clinical records for lung cancer risk stratification, while maintaining the interpretability essential for clinical adoption.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study used Taiwan’s NHIRD, encompassing over 99% of Taiwan’s 23 million residents.11 The NHIRD contains longitudinal diagnostic codes, prescription records and medical orders. Data are deidentified and maintained by the Health and Welfare Data Science Center.

The study population was derived from the NHIRD 100 000-participant sampling file (1999–2013). The lung cancer cohort comprised patients with confirmed diagnoses identified (International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 codes 162 .xx). The index date was the first lung cancer diagnosis, with a 36-month observation window extracted retrospectively. The non-cancer cohort consisted of patients without any cancer diagnoses, with the latest healthcare record as index date.

Inclusion criteria required continuous National Health Insurance enrolment for 3 years prior to index date, age of ≥18 years, and at least one healthcare encounter per year. Patients with incomplete coverage, inconsistent codes or insufficient data were excluded. The final population comprised 99 615 individuals (575 lung cancer cases; 99 040 non-cancer controls). Data preprocessing and feature selection

Clinical data from the 36-month observation window encompassed diagnostic codes, medication prescriptions and medical orders. The 36-month period was selected based on clinical evidence that pre-diagnostic utilisation changes emerge 2–3 years before diagnosis, alignment with routine care patterns and NHIRD data availability.13 Medical orders predominantly represent outpatient pre-diagnostic investigations (chest X-ray, laboratory tests, imaging) rather than inpatient procedures. Diagnostic data used ICD-9 codes, medications used Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes14 and medical orders used standardised procedure codes.

Feature selection employed Log-Likelihood Ratio (LLR) to identify differential occurrence patterns between cohorts.15 The top 10 features per data modality per cohort were selected, yielding 60 features total (10 features×3 modalities×2 cohorts; online supplemental table 1). This achieved a 99.8% reduction in computational requirements while retaining clinically relevant predictors. Each feature was mapped across 36 monthly intervals to create temporal matrices capturing clinical event evolution.16

Model architecture

We employ a multichannel CNN architecture, termed Longitudinal Multisource Clinical Model (LUMIC), to jointly model heterogeneous clinical data while preserving temporal structure. The architecture integrates six input channels from LLR-selected features across three data modalities (diagnostic codes, medications, medical orders) for both cohorts, yielding an input tensor of dimensions (batch, 6, 10, 36) representing channels, features and monthly intervals.

The convolutional architecture uses two layers optimised for temporal clinical data. The first layer processes 6-channel input with 32 output channels using 3×3 kernels, followed by Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation and 2×2 max-pooling. The second layer expands to 64 channels with identical configurations.17 After convolution-pooling, feature maps are flattened for dense layer processing. The architecture incorporates dropout and batch normalisation to prevent overfitting. The final layer employs softmax activation for binary classification (figure 1).

Model training and evaluation

Model training employed stratified 10-fold cross-validation maintaining proportional cohort representation. Hyperparameter optimisation used grid search targeting macro-averaged F1-score maximisation. Key hyperparameters included learning rate, batch size, dropout rate and epochs, with early stopping to prevent overfitting.

Performance evaluation employed metrics for imbalanced classification: precision, recall, macro-averaged F₁-score, Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) and Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), with AUPRC emphasised given 0.58% positive cases. AUROC and AUPRC are threshold-free; threshold-dependent metrics used 0.5 default, adjustable for deployment. Comparative analysis evaluated the framework against traditional machine learning (Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, Linear SVM, Decision Tree, Random Forest, KNN), DeepFFM for factorisation-based modelling, and ConvBERT for transformer transferability.18 19 LUMIC integrates Electronic Medical Record (EMR) input, LLR feature selection and multichannel CNN as a unified system. Baseline models used the complete original feature set without LLR. Traditional models aggregated temporal features into summary statistics; DeepFFM and ConvBERT used temporal sequences with embeddings.

Model interpretability used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis to quantify feature contributions.20 21 SHAP values were computed across all six input channels, generating temporal importance matrices (6 channels×10 features×36 time periods) visualising how clinical events influenced classification decisions.

Among traditional machine learning approaches, Logistic Regression achieved the highest F₁-score of 0.5693 with balanced precision-recall trade-off, while Random Forest and Linear SVM demonstrated strong discriminative ability (AUROC: 0.8587 and 0.8400, respectively) but minimal sensitivity. Decision Tree and KNN exhibited weak discriminative performance with AUROC values below 0.55. Among deep learning baselines, DeepFFM achieved the highest recall (0.8413) and AUROC (0.9137) but with modest precision (0.5188), while ConvBERT performed poorly across all metrics (F₁-score: 0.2029, AUROC: 0.5435), suggesting transformer-based architectures require substantial adaptation for structured longitudinal clinical records.

The proposed LUMIC achieved the highest precision (0.7149) and F₁-score (0.5738) among all methods, with AUPRC of 0.1617 representing optimal performance for imbalanced classification. While DeepFFM achieved higher recall and AUROC, its lower precision would generate more false positive referrals in clinical deployment. In terms of computational efficiency, the runtime per cross-validation fold for LUMIC (54.57 s) was comparable to that of DeepFFM (47.15 s) and substantially faster than that of ConvBERT (756.77 s) and most traditional approaches.

Ablation analysis examined each data modality’s contribution, as shown in table 3. Medical order omission caused the most substantial degradation, with F₁-score decreasing to 0.5345 and AUPRC dropping to 0.1045, confirming procedural data as essential for capturing clinical evaluation pathways preceding formal diagnosis. ICD omission resulted in a moderate reduction (F₁-score: 0.5452). Interestingly, medication omission yielded marginally higher F₁-score (0.5775) and AUPRC (0.1670) compared with the full model. This finding suggests that pre-diagnostic medication patterns may share similar characteristics across various conditions, including symptomatic treatments common to early-stage respiratory complaints and other potential malignancies, thereby introducing noise rather than discriminative signal for lung cancer-specific detection. Diagnostic codes and medical orders thus carry primary predictive value in the pre-diagnostic phase, while medication features may become more discriminative only after cancer-specific pharmacological interventions commence.

Discussion

The proposed LUMIC demonstrates that lightweight deep learning architectures can effectively leverage routine healthcare data for lung cancer risk stratification. By achieving the highest precision (0.7149) and F₁-score (0.5738) among evaluated methods while maintaining competitive inference time, this approach offers a practical balance between predictive performance and clinical deployability. While DeepFFM achieved superior recall (0.8413) and AUROC (0.9137), its lower precision (0.5188) would generate approximately 37% more false positive referrals. In cancer screening contexts, false positives lead to unnecessary diagnostic procedures, patient anxiety and healthcare resource consumption.22 LUMIC prioritises precision while maintaining reasonable sensitivity, reducing follow-up burden for patients without disease. Although LUMIC outperformed baseline models, its moderate F₁-score (0.5738) with higher precision (0.7149) than recall (0.4733) reflects deliberate prioritisation of precision appropriate for population-level screening. This performance profile would be insufficient for standalone diagnostic decision-making, which requires higher sensitivity to minimise false negatives. These trade-offs reflect fundamental differences between screening contexts, where moderate false positive rates are acceptable, and diagnostic contexts, where missed cancers carry severe consequences. The substantial baseline differences in table 1 (age, sex, insured salary) represent established risk factors whose effects may be partially captured through healthcare utilisation patterns, enabling risk identification beyond demographic factors alone.

ConvBERT’s poor performance (F₁-score: 0.2029, AUROC: 0.5435) demonstrates that transformer architectures designed for natural language processing require substantial adaptation for structured longitudinal clinical records.18 19 Its inferior performance reflects architectural limitations including absence of explicit temporal alignment and reliance on dense token representations incompatible with sparsely coded, event-based clinical records. These findings support CNN-based approaches for modelling localised temporal patterns in administrative healthcare data.

The ablation study provides clinically interpretable insights into data modality contribution. Medical order omission caused substantial performance degradation (F₁-score: 0.5345, AUPRC: 0.1045), confirming procedural data capture critical information about clinical evaluation pathways preceding diagnosis. In contrast, medication omission marginally improved performance (F₁-score: 0.5775 vs 0.5738), suggesting pre-diagnostic medication patterns may introduce noise rather than discriminative signal. Symptomatic treatments and medications common to multiple conditions likely share similar characteristics across patient populations, limiting lung cancer specificity prior to definitive diagnosis. The marginal improvement after removing medication features likely reflects noise reduction under LLR–based top-K selection, where heterogeneous pre-diagnostic medication patterns may be under-represented when only the top-ranked features are retained. These findings suggest selectively including clinically informative medication categories may be preferable for lightweight screening models.

SHAP analysis revealed distinct temporal patterns validating the model’s clinical reasoning. The identified top-ranked predictors across diagnostic codes, medical orders and medications provide insight into early lung cancer risk assessment. High-weight features reflect increased diagnostic testing, respiratory-related encounters and intensified healthcare utilisation, rather than cancer-specific confirmation, consistent with prior EHR-based cancer prediction studies showing utilisation trajectory changes precede formal diagnosis and capture pre-diagnostic evaluation processes. The framework is intended to operate upstream of LDCT during routine healthcare encounters, performing longitudinal risk stratification to identify individuals at elevated future risk who may benefit from prioritised LDCT referral. By functioning as a screening support rather than a diagnostic tool, the model aims to improve screening programme efficiency by focusing diagnostic resources on individuals most likely to benefit. Non-cancer cohort features demonstrated substantially greater contributions than cancer cohort features, with the examination channel exhibiting strongest temporal dynamics. This indicates the model primarily leverages routine healthcare utilisation patterns (chest radiography, laboratory tests, outpatient consultations) rather than cancer-specific features. The sparse contributions from cancer cohort features confirm cancer-specific patterns emerge only proximal to diagnosis, supporting the framework’s design rationale of capturing pre-diagnostic healthcare engagement.

LLR-based feature selection achieves critical computational efficiency for clinical implementation. The 99.8% reduction in memory requirements (from 1163.44 GB to 2.07 GB) enables deployment in resource-constrained environments where sophisticated deep learning models would be impractical.23 Combined with competitive runtime (54.57 s), this positions the framework for EHR system integration supporting real-time risk assessment during routine encounters. Our findings have implications for lung cancer screening programmes. Current LDCT screening faces implementation challenges including high costs, limited accessibility and 24% false positive rates.6 LUMIC offers a complementary strategy for identifying high-risk individuals who would benefit most from imaging studies, potentially optimising resource allocation.24

Several limitations warrant consideration. The reliance on NHIRD data through 2013 may not reflect recent advances in lung cancer diagnosis and treatment, and ICD-9 coding has since transitioned to ICD-10. This study uses ICD-9 because the NHIRD dataset spans 1999–2013. Notably, the majority of high-weight diagnostic features—including malignant neoplasms, chronic respiratory diseases and systemic comorbidities—map directly to established ICD-10 codes, demonstrating feature representation generalisability to modern coding systems. Nevertheless, evolving clinical practices, diagnostic workflows and coding standards may introduce concept drift, potentially affecting model calibration. This study should be viewed as methodological proof-of-concept demonstrating feasibility of longitudinal, multisource modelling using administrative healthcare data, not intended for immediate deployment without local retraining. Future research should prioritise validation on contemporary ICD-10 datasets to ensure temporal robustness and clinical utility.

The absence of lifestyle and environmental risk factors, including smoking history and occupational exposures, limits incorporation of established risk factors substantially influencing lung cancer development.25 The geographic specificity of our Taiwanese population raises generalisability questions regarding populations with different demographics, healthcare structures and disease epidemiology.26 External validation using diverse international datasets will be essential for establishing broader applicability. The substantial class imbalance (0.58% positive cases) presents inherent challenges for model training and evaluation.

Despite these limitations, this study establishes a foundation for leveraging routine clinical data in cancer risk prediction. The combination of robust predictive performance, computational efficiency and clinical interpretability positions LUMIC as a valuable complement to existing screening strategies. Future research should focus on external validation, incorporating additional risk factors and prospective clinical utility evaluation.27

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that lightweight deep learning architectures can effectively leverage routine clinical data for early lung cancer detection. The proposed LUMIC achieved the highest precision (0.7149) and F₁-score (0.5738) among evaluated methods, with AUPRC of 0.1617 representing optimal performance for imbalanced classification. The multimodal approach successfully integrated 3 years of diagnostic codes, medication records and medical order information, with ablation studies confirming that procedural data provide primary predictive value while pre-diagnostic medication patterns contribute limited discriminative signal.

The SHAP interpretability analysis validated the model’s clinical reasoning by revealing that routine healthcare utilisation patterns, rather than cancer-specific features, drive risk stratification in the pre-diagnostic phase. This finding supports the framework’s potential as a preliminary screening tool that identifies high-risk individuals through existing healthcare engagement patterns. The computational efficiency achieved through systematic feature selection, reducing memory requirements by 99.8%, enables deployment in resource-constrained clinical environments, where sophisticated deep learning models would otherwise be impractical.

Future implementation will require external validation using diverse international datasets and incorporation of additional risk factors including lifestyle and environmental exposures. Despite these considerations, the demonstrated feasibility of leveraging routine healthcare data for lung cancer risk prediction represents a step towards more accessible and cost-effective approaches to early cancer detection.
