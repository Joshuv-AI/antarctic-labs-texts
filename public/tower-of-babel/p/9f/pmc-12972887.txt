Long-term outcomes of patients with esophageal cancer recommended for surgery but treated non-surgically: a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results analysis
Authors: Ruijiang Lin, Minjie Ma, Hongbin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal cancer is a highly aggressive malignancy with a poor prognosis, particularly in regions such as Asia and Africa. Although surgery remains the standard treatment for early- to intermediate-stage esophageal cancer, a substantial proportion of patients who are recommended for surgery in real-world practice ultimately do not undergo cancer-directed resection. This study aimed to compare long-term survival outcomes between patients who were recommended for surgery but did not undergo it and those who did undergo surgery using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized data from the SEER database covering the period 2004 to 2021. A total of 7,106 patients with esophageal cancer were enrolled: 5,138 in the surgical group and 1,968 in the nonsurgical group. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to adjust for confounding variables. Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox regression analyses were used to compare overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) between the two groups.ResultsAfter PSM, patients who underwent surgery had significantly better median OS and CSS compared with the non-surgery group, across multiple factors including age, year of diagnosis, sex, race, income, and tumor location. Notably, surgery was associated with improved survival outcomes in both male and female patients, as well as those with lower esophageal tumors and those diagnosed later in life. The surgical group consistently demonstrated a higher probability of survival, with a significant benefit in both OS and CSS (P<0.001).ConclusionsSurgery was associated with superior survival in the overall matched cohort and across most subgroups, although this benefit was not observed in selected stages such as T2 and N2 disease. These findings support the importance of surgical intervention in improving long-term survival outcomes for patients with esophageal cancer and highlight the need for better patient selection and management to reduce the number of patients who do not undergo recommended surgery.Keywords: Long-term survival; esophageal cancer; nonsurgical treatment; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database (SEER database)

Key findings

• Esophageal cancer patients who underwent surgery had significantly better median overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) than those who did not.

What is known and what is new?

• Surgery is the standard treatment for early to mid-stage esophageal cancer, but a significant proportion of patients recommended for surgery do not undergo the procedure. Meanwhile, non-surgical treatments such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy have made progress, but their efficacy compared to surgery in this particular patient population remains unclear.

• Studies have confirmed that surgery significantly improved median OS and CSS in most subgroups (including age, sex, race, income, tumor location, and histological type, P<0.001), with the most significant benefit observed in patients with localized disease; however, no survival advantage of surgery was observed in patients with T2/N2 stage disease and Black patients.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Surgery remains the cornerstone for improving long-term survival rates in esophageal cancer patients, while also highlighting the need for individualized treatment stratification and addressing healthcare disparities.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer remains a highly lethal malignancy worldwide, with poor long-term survival despite advances in multimodality therapy (1-3). Recent U.S. mortality trend analyses have shown that esophageal cancer mortality has declined only modestly from 1999 to 2024, with marked disparities by sex, race, and urbanization status, highlighting persistent real-world inequities in outcomes (4). For patients with potentially resectable disease, surgery-based treatment (esophagectomy, often combined with perioperative systemic therapy and/or neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy) is commonly recommended, whereas definitive nonsurgical strategies such as concurrent chemoradiotherapy are standard for unresectable disease or for patients who are medically inoperable (5,6).

In real-world practice, a substantial proportion of patients who are initially recommended for surgery ultimately do not undergo esophagectomy (7,8). Importantly, this creates a unique clinical cohort: patients who, by clinical assessment, were considered suitable for surgery, but for whom surgical resection was not performed during the initial course of treatment (9). This group differs fundamentally from the broader population of non-surgically treated patients, which includes individuals who were never considered surgical candidates.

Meanwhile, improvements in radiotherapy techniques, concurrent chemoradiotherapy regimens, and the emergence of immunotherapy and targeted approaches have expanded the therapeutic potential of nonsurgical strategies (10-14). These developments raise a key question: in contemporary practice, does surgery still confer a consistent incremental survival benefit over nonsurgical treatment across patient subgroups?

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, an authoritative cancer registry platform under the U.S. National Cancer Institute, provides information on over 26.5% of American cancer patients over many years, making it an important tool for studying prognostic factors, changes in treatment patterns, and subgroup survival differences (15). Therefore, using the SEER database, we specifically compared long-term overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) between patients who were recommended for surgery but did not undergo resection and those who did undergo surgery, providing a focused evaluation of the survival impact of non-surgical management within a clinically comparable population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2312/rc).

Methods

Data source and study design

This study is a retrospective, population-based study using the U.S. National Cancer Institute’s (NCI) SEER database. Data were extracted using SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.2) for the period from January 2004 to December 2021. The database provides high-quality, de-identified public data, including demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment modalities, and prognostic information. Therefore, this study was exempt from ethical approval and patient informed consent requirements. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pathologically confirmed esophageal cancer (ICD-O-3 Site Code: C15.0–C15.9); (II) age ≥18 years; (III) diagnosis between 2004 and 2021; (IV) complete information on survival time, survival status, and major covariates; and (V) no history of other primary malignant tumors.

Exclusion criteria included: (I) patients without pathologically confirmed esophageal cancer; (II) patients not recommended for surgery; (III) lymphomas, Kaposi’s sarcoma, and other tumors of unknown origin located in the esophagus; (IV) patients with other concurrent tumors; (V) patients with incomplete follow-up data or zero survival days; and (VI) patients who received preoperative or intraoperative radiation therapy.

Grouping and variable definitions

The study population was divided into two groups based on whether they underwent surgery: the non-surgery group (recommended for but did not undergo surgery) and the surgery group (underwent recommended surgery). Surgical recommendation and receipt were defined using SEER treatment variables. Patients were classified as “recommended for surgery but not performed” if the variable “Reason no cancer-directed surgery” indicated that surgery was recommended but not carried out. Patients in the surgical group were identified using the SEER variable “RX Summ—Surg Prim Site”, indicating receipt of cancer-directed esophagectomy as part of the first course of therapy. In SEER, surgical treatment is recorded as part of the first course of therapy and reflects whether cancer-directed surgery was performed during the initial planned treatment period after diagnosis; surgeries performed after disease progression or outside the initial treatment course were not captured in this variable. Because surgical intent is determined at the time of initial treatment planning rather than after complete pathologic staging, some patients later found to have distant metastases may still be recorded as having been recommended for surgery (e.g., when metastases are detected intraoperatively or when surgery is planned for cytoreductive or palliative purposes). The comparison in this study is therefore not between surgery and any specific non-surgical modality, but between patients who completed a recommended surgical course and those who did not, regardless of the alternative management received. The extracted variables included: demographic information (age, sex, race, marital status, median household income, year of diagnosis), tumor characteristics [histologic type, grade, American Joint Committee on Cancer tumor-node-metastasis (AJCC TNM) stage], treatment modalities (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy status), and prognostic information (OS and CSS). Radiotherapy and chemotherapy variables captured both perioperative and non-surgical use and were incorporated as covariates in the propensity score model and subgroup analyses. OS was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. CSS was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to death attributed to esophageal cancer; deaths from other causes were treated as censored. Survival time in SEER is recorded in months from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or last contact, and patients alive at the last follow-up were censored at that time.

Patients in the non-surgical group were defined according to the SEER variable “Reason no cancer-directed surgery”, which includes the categories “Not recommended”, “Recommended but not performed, unknown reason”, and “Recommended, unknown if performed”. Due to the limited granularity of SEER coding, specific reasons such as patient refusal, frailty, or operative risk could not be distinguished. Therefore, we did not conduct further research on non-surgical causes.

Propensity score matching (PSM)

To reduce confounding bias, this study employed a 1:1 PSM method between the surgery and non-surgery groups. Matching was performed using the MatchIt package in R (version 4.3.1) with the nearest neighbor method, a caliper value of 0.02, and without replacement. The matching covariates included: age, sex, race, marital status, income, stage (T/N/M), histologic type, radiotherapy status, and chemotherapy status.

Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model including age, sex, race, marital status, income, T stage, N stage, M stage, histologic type, and receipt of radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values <0.10 indicating adequate balance. Patients with unknown T, N, or M stage were retained in the analysis to preserve real-world representativeness, and an “unknown” category was included for each staging variable in the propensity score model to partially account for differences in staging completeness between groups.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and proportions were used to analyze demographic and clinical variables. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using t-tests, while non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median [interquartile range] and compared using non-parametric tests. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test to assess baseline differences between the two groups. Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate OS and CSS curves, with log-rank tests for inter-group comparisons. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to calculate hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and to identify independent predictors of survival. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and log-log survival plots, and no major violations were detected. In the PSM cohort, multivariable Cox models included the treatment group (surgery vs. non-surgery) and a limited set of key prognostic variables to account for residual imbalance and improve precision, rather than re-adjusting for all variables used in the propensity score model. In the matched cohort, stratified analyses were conducted by variables such as age, diagnosis period, and T/M stage to further explore the survival performance of the non-surgery group in different subgroups and identify potential subgroups with survival not inferior to the surgery group. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23.0) and R software (version 4.3.1), with a two-sided P value <0.05 considered statistically significant. Patients with missing data for key covariates required for matching or survival analyses were excluded; remaining analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 7,106 patients with esophageal cancer who were recommended for surgery in the SEER database, 1,968 (27.7%) did not undergo cancer-directed surgery, whereas 5,138 (72.3%) received surgery. The specific inclusion and exclusion criteria and the analysis process are shown in Figure S1.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table S1. Significant differences were observed between the two groups in all listed characteristics (all P<0.05). Patients in the surgery group were more likely to be male (82.27% vs. 75.61%), younger (32.04% aged 41–60 vs. 20.53%), and married (62.03% vs. 44.97%) compared with those in the non-surgery group. In terms of socioeconomic factors, surgery group patients were more often urban residents (86.84% vs. 81.10%) and had higher median household income. Regarding tumor characteristics, the surgery group had a higher proportion of lower esophageal tumors (72.87% vs. 57.37%) and adenocarcinoma histology (75.46% vs. 56.81%), while the non-surgery group had more squamous cell carcinoma (30.34% vs. 14.13%). The surgery group had a significantly higher percentage of patients diagnosed at early stages, including localized disease (56.25% vs. 24.19%), T1 stage (40.54% vs. 21.49%), and AJCC Stage I (49.38% vs. 16.67%). Conversely, the non-surgery group had higher proportions of distant metastasis (M1: 20.33% vs. 6.31%), AJCC stage IV disease (20.33% vs. 6.95%), and unknown staging data. As for treatment modalities, radiotherapy and chemotherapy were more frequently used in the non-surgery group (46.70% vs. 18.12% and 47.97% vs. 30.28%, respectively), suggesting a potential reliance on non-surgical management strategies in this population. To address these baseline imbalances, PSM was performed; the matched baseline characteristics demonstrating balance after PSM are summarized in Table 1.

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis of factors associated with OS

Univariable Cox regression analysis (Table S2) showed that year of diagnosis, race, age, marital status, residential location, median income, tumor primary site, and histologic type were significantly associated with OS (all P<0.05). In multivariable Cox regression analysis (Table S2), several variables remained independent prognostic factors. Compared with male patients, female patients had a significantly lower risk of death (HR =0.82, 95% CI: 0.76–0.89). Patients diagnosed in 2010–2015 (HR =0.79, 95% CI: 0.73–0.85) and 2016–2021 (HR =0.63, 95% CI: 0.56–0.71) had better survival than those diagnosed in 2004–2009. Increasing age was strongly associated with worse prognosis, especially in patients aged ≥80 years (HR =3.17, 95% CI: 2.38–4.21). Being married (HR =1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.22), living in urban areas (HR =0.86, 95% CI: 0.79–0.94), and having higher household income (≥$90,000: HR =0.79, 95% CI: 0.73–0.86) were associated with improved survival. Regarding tumor characteristics, lower esophageal location (HR =0.92, 95% CI: 0.79–1.07) and adenocarcinoma histology (HR =0.80, 95% CI: 0.73–0.87) were also favorable prognostic factors.

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis of factors associated with CSS

Univariable Cox regression analysis (Table S3) showed that year of diagnosis, race, age, marital status, residential location, median income, tumor primary site, and histologic type were significantly associated with CSS (all P<0.05). In multivariable Cox regression analysis (Table S3), several variables remained independent prognostic factors. Compared with patients diagnosed between 2004 and 2009, those diagnosed between 2010 and 2015 (HR =0.75, 95% CI: 0.69–0.81) and 2016–2021 (HR =0.60, 95% CI: 0.52–0.68) had better survival. Older age was strongly associated with worse prognosis, particularly in patients aged 61–80 years (HR =1.38, 95% CI: 1.04–1.84) and ≥80 years (HR =2.32, 95% CI: 1.72–3.13). Black patients had a significantly worse prognosis compared with white patients (HR =1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.31). Being married was associated with improved survival (HR =1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.20), whereas living in urban areas (HR =0.86, 95% CI: 0.78–0.95) and having higher income (≥$90,000: HR =0.77, 95% CI: 0.70–0.85) were favorable factors. Regarding tumor characteristics, higher tumor stage (T3/T4) and M1 stage were associated with worse survival, while adenocarcinoma histology (HR =0.79, 95% CI: 0.71–0.87) and lower esophageal location (HR =0.88, 95% CI: 0.75–1.04) were favorable prognostic factors.

The Kaplan-Meier curves before PSM are illustrated in Figure 1, and show a significant survival benefit for the surgery group compared to the non-surgery group. In both OS and CSS, the surgery group consistently demonstrated higher survival probabilities, with a significant difference (P<0.001). Even when non-surgery patients were categorized into those who refused treatment or had unknown reasons for not undergoing surgery, the surgery group still exhibited markedly better survival outcomes. These findings highlight the clear survival advantage of surgery over non-surgery before PSM adjustments.

PSM

The matched cohort of 1,353 pairs represented 26.3% of the original surgical cohort and 68.8% of the non-surgical cohort. Before PSM, there were significant differences in multiple variables between the surgery group (n=5,138) and the non-surgery group (n=1,968). The proportion of males was higher in the surgery group (82.3%) than in the non-surgery group (75.6%), and the proportion of White patients was also higher in the surgery group (89.5%) compared to the non-surgery group (84.3%). Furthermore, the age of patients in the surgery group was predominantly concentrated between 40–79 years, whereas the non-surgery group had more patients aged 80 and above (19.8%). In terms of income, the surgery group had a larger proportion of high-income individuals (≥$70,000) and a higher percentage of urban residents compared to the non-surgery group. After PSM, which resulted in 1,353 patients in both the surgery and non-surgery groups, the distribution of sex, race, age, marital status, residential area, and income became consistent across groups. For example, after matching, the proportions of males were 78.9% and 78.0%, and the proportions of White patients were 85.6% and 85.7% in the surgery and non-surgery groups, respectively. Differences in age and income were significantly reduced, especially in the distribution of household income and residential area, where inter-group differences nearly vanished after matching. Overall, PSM effectively balanced the main demographic and clinical characteristics between the surgery and non-surgery groups, allowing for a more equitable comparison, with detailed results presented in Table 1.

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis of factors associated with OS after PSM

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses identified several independent prognostic factors associated with OS (Table 2). Female sex, higher income, urban residence, and adenocarcinoma histology were found to be favorable prognostic factors, with patients in these groups exhibiting improved survival. Conversely, older age (particularly 80 years and older) and Black race were associated with a higher risk of death. Marital status and more recent years of diagnosis (2010–2021) were also identified as factors that contributed to better survival.

This survival advantage of surgery was observed across age categories: in patients aged 41–60 years, OS was 22 vs. 12 months; in those aged 61–80 years, 28 vs. 12 months; and in those aged ≥80 years, 20 vs. 6 months (all P<0.001).

Similarly, surgery conferred longer survival across diagnostic periods (2004–2009: 18 vs. 8 months; 2010–2015: 28 vs. 10 months; 2016–2021: 43 vs. 18 months; all P<0.001).

Consistent benefits of surgery were also seen across race and socioeconomic strata. Among White patients, OS was 25 months with surgery vs. 11 months without surgery (P<0.001), and among patients with annual median income ≥$90,000, OS was 27 vs. 12 months (P<0.001). In contrast, no significant OS difference was observed between surgery and non-surgery in Black patients (9 vs. 15 months, P=0.80). Surgery was also associated with longer OS across tumor characteristics. For adenocarcinoma, OS was 29 vs. 12 months, and for squamous cell carcinoma, 19 vs. 10 months (both P<0.001). Patients with localized disease had a particularly large benefit (63 vs. 12 months, P<0.001), while even patients with distant metastases had longer OS with surgery (10 vs. 5 months, P<0.001).

Patients diagnosed more recently (2010–2021) had significantly better CSS compared to those diagnosed in 2004–2009. Discussion

Esophageal cancer remains one of the deadliest cancers globally, with a poor prognosis, especially in high-incidence regions like Asia and Africa. Although surgery is considered the gold standard for early- and intermediate-stage patients, a significant proportion of patients do not undergo surgery for various reasons, including comorbidities, refusal, and poor nutritional status. The long-term prognosis for esophageal cancer patients who are recommended for but do not receive surgery is largely unknown. In this study, we used a large cohort from the SEER database to investigate the long-term survival outcomes of patients who were recommended for surgery but ultimately did not undergo it.

Our findings indicate that after adjusting for confounding variables using PSM, patients who underwent surgery had significantly better median OS and CSS compared to those who did not. The novelty of this analysis lies not in demonstrating the general benefit of surgery, but in quantifying the real-world survival penalty associated with failing to complete a recommended surgical course in a clinically comparable population. The survival advantage of the surgical group was consistent across multiple subgroups, including those based on age, sex, race, and tumor location. These results are consistent with previous studies showing the survival benefit of surgery for esophageal cancer patients, even when combined with other treatments like radiotherapy or chemotherapy (16-20).

In our PSM cohort, patients who were recommended for surgery but managed without surgery had significantly inferior overall and CSS compared with those who underwent surgical resection, despite receiving non-surgical therapies. This aligns with recent advancements in radiation technology, such as three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) (21,22) and intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) (23,24), as well as promising results from immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as programmed cell death-1/programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors (25-27) in advanced esophageal cancer.

However, despite the progress in non-surgical treatments, the survival outcomes for non-surgical patients in our study remained inferior to those of surgical patients. This underscores the importance of surgical intervention, especially for patients who are otherwise suitable for surgery but face barriers such as age, comorbidities, or socioeconomic factors. It also highlights the need for better preoperative assessment and patient counseling to address concerns and encourage more patients to undergo surgery (9-11,28,29).

Our study found no significant difference in median OS between the surgical and non-surgical cohorts for patients with T2 stage disease. This may be because, in some cases, with advancements in medical technology, definitive chemoradiotherapy and surgical treatment may be alternative therapeutic options (30). For patients with N2 stage disease, there was no significant difference in OS and CSS between surgical and non-surgical treatments. This could be because N2 stage indicates that the cancer has invaded regional lymph nodes but has not yet spread to distant organs. At this stage, the tumor is not only growing locally but also involves multiple lymph nodes, making treatment more difficult and challenging. In such cases, surgery alone may not be able to completely remove all cancer cells, especially those involving lymph nodes, leading to a higher risk of recurrence. Therefore, surgery alone may not significantly improve the long-term survival rate for these patients (31-33). In addition, the absence of a significant CSS benefit associated with surgery among Black patients should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect smaller sample size, differences in access to perioperative and adjuvant therapy, comorbidity burden, and unmeasured social determinants of health that are not captured in SEER.

In conclusion, surgery was associated with improved long-term survival in this cohort, although this benefit was heterogeneous across subgroups and should be interpreted in the context of potential residual confounding. For patients who cannot undergo surgery, alternative therapies such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy can still offer survival benefits, but these should be considered based on specific patient factors. Future research should focus on refining patient selection criteria for surgery and exploring the optimal combination of surgical and non-surgical treatments to further improve patient outcomes.

In addition, the therapeutic landscape of esophageal cancer has continued to evolve in recent years. Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have increasingly been incorporated into first-line and perioperative treatment paradigms, supported by phase III evidence in advanced disease and in the post-neoadjuvant setting (34-36). In parallel, immune checkpoint blockade is being actively evaluated in combination with definitive chemoradiotherapy in locally advanced, unresectable disease (37). Emerging targeted therapies and biomarker-guided strategies (e.g., HER2-directed therapy and CLDN18.2-targeted agents in gastroesophageal/GEJ adenocarcinoma) are also expanding systemic options for selected patients (38-40). These advances, together with ongoing refinements in radiotherapy techniques and multimodality combinations recommended in contemporary guidelines, may further improve outcomes for patients managed without surgery in contemporary practice. However, mature population-level survival data reflecting these newer approaches are not yet available in SEER, and their long-term impact on the relative effectiveness of surgical versus non-surgical strategies remains to be determined (41).

Furthermore, there are certain limitations in this study. Firstly, the SEER database lacks specific details on esophageal cancer treatment, such as radiation dosage, equivalent dose in 2-Gy fractions (EQD2), detailed chemotherapy information, total treatment duration, and tumor size after external beam radiation therapy. Secondly, since we could not balance all covariates using a standard matching method, we adopted a precise matching strategy. Although this strategy controls for some bias, it resulted in a reduced post-matching sample size and the potential for residual bias due to possible observed covariates. Thirdly, although SEER records whether surgery was recommended or performed, it does not provide specific reasons why surgery was not ultimately received, such as refusal, frailty, comorbidity burden, or access to care. As a result, residual confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded. In particular, key determinants of surgical eligibility such as comorbidity burden, frailty, and performance status are not available in SEER and could not be included in the propensity score model. Because surgical recommendation and receipt are registry-coded variables rather than detailed clinical decisions, some degree of misclassification is possible; however, use of the “Reason no cancer-directed surgery” field reduces contamination from patients who were never considered surgical candidates. In addition, immortal time bias cannot be fully excluded, and SEER lacks detailed information on radiotherapy fields, treatment timing, and chemotherapy intent, which may limit precise characterization of treatment effects.

In summary, compared to the surgical group, patients recommended for surgery who did not undergo it generally had poorer clinical outcomes. With continuous advancements in non-surgical treatment technologies, the survival gap between surgical and non-surgical treatments is gradually narrowing, especially for patients with T2 and N2 stage disease.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that surgery significantly improved long-term survival outcomes in patients with esophageal cancer compared to those who were recommended for surgery but did not undergo it. After adjusting for confounders, the surgical group showed better OS and CSS across most subgroups. However, no survival benefit was observed in stages such as T2 and N2 disease. These findings highlight the importance of surgery in improving survival and emphasize the need for better patient selection to reduce the number of patients who do not undergo recommended surgery.
