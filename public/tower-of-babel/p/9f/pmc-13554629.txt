Exploring candidate biomarkers for nonobstructive azoospermia: A bioinformatics and machine learning approach
Authors: Mingming Liang, Yan Chi, Hong Pan, Chengqi Wu, Jinjiang Zeng, Weiyou Lv, Xi Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveNonobstructive azoospermia (NOA), a severe male reproductive disorder, has a complex pathogenesis. This study aimed to identify novel oxidative stress potential biomarkers for NOA using bioinformatics.MethodsDatasets GSE9210, GSE108886, and GSE45885 from the GEO database were used for training, while GSE145467 supported weighted gene coexpression network analysis. The limma package identified differentially expressed genes, cross-referenced with oxidative stress genes from GeneCards for drug prediction. Three machine learning algorithms identified potential hub genes, validated by GSE45887 and GSE216907. Advanced analyses focused on these hub genes, including regulatory networks and single-gene enrichment studies. qRT-PCR validated hub gene expression, and single-cell RNA sequencing (GSE149512 and GSE202647) explored cellular expression patterns.ResultsIL1RN, NOSIP, PDYN, PRKCZ, and UTRN were potential NOA markers. qRT-PCR validation confirmed IL1RN, NOSIP, PRKCZ, and UTRN align with bioinformatic results. Single-cell analysis showed stage-specific dysregulation of these four genes in NOA.ConclusionsThis study broadens the spectrum of known NOA candidate biomarkers and enhances the comprehension of NOA.

Introduction

Non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) affects about 10% of infertile men and is marked by failed spermatogenesis, 1 often due to genetic mutations, 2 disrupted cell adhesion, and a weakened blood-testis barrier. 3 Although stem cell therapies and their exosomes have demonstrated promise in some preclinical models, they are not expected to address NOA resulting from inherent genetic issues in meiosis. There are still many cases of NOA with unknown etiology. 4 To better understand its molecular mechanisms, studies using high-throughput omics techniques—such as transcriptome,5–9 single-cell transcriptome,10,11 proteomic, 12 and metabolomic 13 analyses—have identified key pathways and markers, aiding in clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Oxidative stress (OS) plays a significant role in male reproductive disorders by harming sperm quality and function, leading to DNA damage and infertility. 14 It is particularly high in nonobstructive azoospermia, impairing spermatogenesis. OS damages sperm membranes, causes DNA breaks and reduces viability, all contributing to infertility. 15 It is also linked to inflammation, which can worsen OS in a vicious cycle. Antioxidant therapy is considered a potential treatment to enhance sperm quality and fertility, with studies exploring the benefits of exercise 16 and oral antioxidants. 17 Understanding OS in NOA is essential for developing new fertility treatments.

To investigate diagnostic genes and potential mechanisms in azoospermia, datasets GSE9210, GSE45885, GSE145467, GSE45887, GSE108886, GSE216907, GSE149512 and GSE202647 were obtained from the GEO database. Various bioinformatics methods, including DEGs analysis, molecular docking and machine learning, were employed. Hub genes were validated through qRT-PCR and single-cell analyses. This study might improve the comprehension of the pathophysiology related to spermatogenesis disorders.

Materials and methods

Gathering and initial processing of data

Six high-throughput RNA sequencing datasets of testicular tissue associated with NOA were obtained from the GEO database, specifically GSE9210, GSE108886, GSE145467, GSE216907, GSE45885, and GSE45887. Detailed information regarding these datasets was presented in Table 1. The comprehensive analysis encompassed a total of 154 samples, comprising 38 obstructive azoospermia (OA) and 116 NOA samples. The OA samples, representing normal spermatogenesis, were served as the control samples. NOA was clinically diagnosed through testicular biopsy, with microscopic confirmation of the absence of spermatozoa in the seminiferous tubules. OA samples were obtained from patients who have normal spermatogenic function following a biopsy. Although specific histopathological subcategories, such as Sertoli cell-only syndrome or maturation arrest, could not be further delineated due to limited publicly available metadata, the samples utilized for our quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) validation strictly conformed to the same diagnostic criteria to ensure consistency. Furthermore, 1,399 oxidative stress-related genes (OSGs), each with a relevance score of 7 or higher, were identified from the GeneCards database. 18 To prevent subjective selection bias or human interference, the eligible NOA datasets retrieved were randomly divided into either the discovery set for training or the independent validation set. The gene expression matrices from GSE9210, GSE45885 and GSE108886 were combined to establish the training set, while GSE45887 and GSE216907 were utilized as the validation set. To mitigate batch effects across the integrated GEO datasets, the ComBat function from the R package “sva” (version 3.54.0) was applied. A model matrix was created based on disease status (OA vs NOA) to maintain the biological variation of interest, with GSE accession numbers specified as the batch variable. Identification of DEGs

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the NOA and OA groups, the ‘limma’ package in R software was employed. The gene expression data was log2-transformed, and a linear model was applied with empirical Bayes moderation to ensure robust standard errors. P-values were adjusted for false discovery rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The threshold for DEGs was established at |log2-fold change| > 0.585 with a P-value < 0.05.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) for pinpointing pathogenic genes

The R package “WGCNA” was employed to identify potential NOA-associated genes. Initially, the top 50% of genes were selected using variance analysis. The soft-thresholding power β = 8 was selected via the ''pickSoftThreshold'' R function. 20 Hierarchical clustering was performed with a modularity cutoff of 50 and a merge height of 0.25. Modules with a correlation coefficient (r > 0.7, P < 0.001) with NOA were identified as NOA-related.

Initial identification of signature genes and assessment of functional enrichment

The signature genes of NOA were identified as the intersection of genes from DEGs, OSGs, and cluster WGCNA. The R package “clusterProfiler” was used to perform analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). 21 P value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Identification of hub genes via machine learning

To identify potential diagnostic biomarkers, three distinct machine learning algorithms—Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine with Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE)—were guided by the core principles of TRIPOD reporting guidelines for predictive models in biomedical research. 22 All feature selection and hyperparameter tuning steps were performed exclusively on the training cohort to avoid data leakage. For the training cohort, batch-corrected integrated GEO datasets were utilized, and two independent external validation datasets, excluded from the integrated GEO data, were reserved for the ultimate evaluation of the model. LASSO regression analysis was conducted utilizing the glmnet package (version 4.1-8) to identify potential biomarkers, employing a binomial family for the model. A ten-fold cross-validation approach was implemented to ascertain the optimal regularization parameter (λ.min) that minimized binomial deviance. Genes exhibiting non-zero coefficients at λ.min were designated as LASSO candidate genes. RF analysis was executed using the randomForest R package, where the out-of-bag (OOB) error was monitored to ensure model stability, with the number of trees (ntree) ultimately fixed at 500. Feature importance was assessed via the Mean Decrease Gini index, and genes were ranked in descending order based on their Mean Decrease Gini scores. The top 20 genes with the highest scores were selected as RF candidate biomarkers. SVM-RFE was carried out using the e1071 R package with a radial kernel. A 5-fold cross-validation framework was employed during recursive feature elimination to iteratively remove the least significant features until the minimum classification error was achieved. The optimal feature subset was identified by identifying the lowest cross-validation error rate. To ensure reproducibility, a random seed was established prior to executing each of the three machine learning algorithms. Finally, the intersecting features identified by all three independent algorithms were deemed the definitive key diagnostic genes for subsequent model development.

Identification of potential diagnostic biomarkers via ROC analysis

The identification of potential diagnostic biomarkers was conducted through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Initially, candidate genes were identified by intersecting feature sets derived from the LASSO, RF, and SVM-RFE algorithms. To assess the individual discriminative capabilities of these intersecting genes, ROC curve analysis was employed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify their diagnostic significance in differentiating NOA from OA. 23 In this study, genes with an AUC greater than 0.7 were classified as optimal core potential biomarkers, indicating their robust and independent diagnostic value. These final screened genes were subsequently used as independent variables in the construction of the multivariate diagnostic model.

Construction and evaluation of a diagnostic nomogram

A logistic regression-based diagnostic model was created to predict NOA probability in infertile males, visualized as a nomogram. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test, using the “ggpubr” R package, compared gene expression levels and diagnostic scores between NOA and OA groups. The model’s predictive performance was evaluated with a calibration curve from 1,000 bootstrap resamples and decision curve analysis (DCA) for clinical net benefit. External validation datasets confirmed the potential biomarkers’ differential expression and the nomogram’s predictive accuracy.

Diagnostic gene regulatory network prediction

Using the NetworkAnalyst 3.0 platform, information of miRNA‒mRNA target interactions was retrieved from the miRTarBase 24 and JASPAR databases 25 for TF‒mRNA interactions.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Using the R packages “enrichplot”, “clusterProfiler” and “pathwork”, the single-gene GSEA was carried out for diagnostic genes to examine their possible functions in NOA. Each diagnostic gene’s median expression level was used to classify the samples into high-expression and low-expression categories. Once identified, the DEGs underwent GO and KEGG enrichment analysis.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

To validate the bioinformatics findings, mRNA expression levels of five hub genes were analyzed using testicular tissues from twelve patients (six with NOA and six with OA as controls) at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center Liuzhou Hospital between August and December 2024. Tissue collection occurred during routine clinical procedures, using only residual tissues after fulfilling clinical needs. The study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024-049, February, 28, 2024), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. To maintain confidentiality, all samples and clinical data were de-identified and assigned unique research identifiers.

Total RNA was extracted from testes using the TRIzol protocol and the TaKaRa RNAiso Kit (TaKaRa, Code No. 9108, Shanghai, China). RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a microspectrophotometer (OSE-260-03, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Then, 1 µg of RNA was converted to cDNA using the HiScript II Kit. RT-qPCR was performed with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix on a QuantStudio™ 5 system (Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher, USA). Primers were designed by gensys Biotech, and sequences were showed in Supplementary Table S1. The PCR protocol included a 5 min denaturation at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 40 s. Melting curve analysis confirmed amplification specificity. The 2–(ΔΔCT) method determined relative gene expression, using ACTB as the reference. Data are presented as mean ± SD based on 3 independent experiments (n = 6 biologically independent samples per group, with 3–4 technical replicates per sample).

Data preprocessing and batch effect integration of single cell sequencing

Two single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of testicular tissue related to NOA and OA, GSE149512 10 and GSE202647 11, were obtained from the GEO database. Detailed information regarding these datasets was presented in Table 1. Variations in reference genome annotation and sequencing depth resulted in alterations in baseline mitochondrial gene expression; consequently, a stringent, tailored filtering strategy was implemented based on library size and detected gene count. Specifically, cells were retained if they exhibited nFeature_RNA values greater than 200 and less than 6,000, and nCount_RNA values less than 20,000, which effectively eliminated low-quality cells, debris, and doublets across all samples. To address technical and biological variances without biased cell exclusion, cell cycle progression scores (S.Score and G2M.Score) were calculated using Seurat’s standard gene panel. The dataset was log-normalized, and the top 2,000 highly variable features were identified using the variance-stabilizing transformation method. During data scaling, both the mitochondrial proportion (percent.mt) and cell cycle scores were regressed out to reduce background noise (ScaleData). Subsequently, using the Harmony package in R (RunHarmony), batch effects were eliminated based on the top 30 PCA components identified.

Unsupervised clustering and cell type annotation

A K-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph was created using the first 15 eigenvectors, and cells with similar accessibility profiles were clustered via the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.2. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was then used for non-linear dimensionality reduction and visualization in these 15 dimensions. To validate spermatogenic lineage annotations, a germ cell identity score was calculated using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function with a curated signature of germline genes (DDX4, DAZL, STRA8, SYCP3). The phenotypic scores effectively distinguished spermatogenic stages from testicular somatic cells.

The identification of cell subpopulations was achieved by annotating clusters with marker genes, including spermatogonial stem cells (OCT4, NANOG), spermatogonia (UTF1, UCHL1), spermatocytes (SYCP3, DMC1), spermatids (ACRV1), sertoli cells (SOX9, WT1, AMH), leydig cells (CYP11A1, DLK1, STAR), peritubular myoid cells (ACTA2, TAGLN, MYH11), macrophages (CD163, CD68, C1QA) and endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF). To verify the results of annotation, the top 200 genes of each type were identified, and the heatmap of top 5 genes was visualized using the ComplexHeatmap package. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was then conducted on cluster-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to validate the clustering results. Finally, the relative proportions of each annotated cell type between the OA and NOA groups were calculated and visualized. Collectively, the combination of canonical marker anchoring, germ_score lineage tracking, and GO functional enrichment provided robust verification for all annotated single-cell clusters and developmental stages.

Differential expression and trajectory inference

To identify transcriptomic changes between OA and NOA states in specific cell subpopulations, DEG analyses was performed using the FindMarkers function, focusing on hub genes. Pseudotime trajectory analyses was conducted with the slingshot R package to trace the developmental progression of the spermatogenic lineage, specifically analyzing spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids, with spermatogonia as the starting point. The tradeSeq package was used to model dynamic gene expression along the pseudotime, fitting the raw count matrix of target genes with Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) using pseudotime coordinates and cell weights from Slingshot, and assessing significance with the associationTest function.

Exploratory in silico screening of small molecule-hub protein interactions

The DSigDB database was accessed via Enrichr for small molecule screening. 26 Crystal structures of potential diagnostic biomarkers—Interleukin 1 Receptor Antagonist (IL1RN), Prodynorphin (PDYN) and Utrophin (UTRN) were sourced from the Protein Data Bank, while Protein kinase C/zeta (PRKCZ) and nitric oxide synthase–interacting proteins (NOSIP) structures came from Alphafold at EMBL-EBI (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/). Small molecule structures were obtained from PubChem. Autodock Vina was used for molecular docking, and Pymol for visualization. 27

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software. Bulk RNA-seq data were analyzed with R version 4.1.1, and scRNA-seq data with R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Cytoscape was performed for network plots. SPSS handled one-way ANOVA on quantitative RT-PCR data, visualized with GraphPad, with all analyses at a significance level of P < 0.05.

Identification of hub genes via machine learning

In this study, three machine learning methodologies were performed to identify core diagnostic genes. The LASSO regression analyses achieved convergence to an optimal λ value via 10-fold cross-validation, utilizing the criterion of minimum binomial deviance. This process effectively identified and retained 11 features with non-zero coefficients (Supplemental Figure 3A). Subsequently, the SVM-RFE algorithm was employed to assess the feature subsets. The 5-fold cross-validation results indicated that the model achieved its highest accuracy of 0.97 and its lowest cross-validation error of 0.0298 when exactly 65 features were included (Supplemental Figure 3B). Additionally, a Random Forest classifier was developed. The out-of-bag (OOB) error rate curve demonstrated a significant reduction and stabilization of the model’s error when the number of trees reached 500. Further evaluation of the variables’ significance led to the identification of the top 20 genes, such as STAT4, GPX4, GLRX2, PDYN, and PRKCZ, based on the highest Mean Decrease Accuracy and Mean Decrease Gini values (Supplemental Figure 3D).

The intersection of these selections showed six potential diagnostic markers (IL1RN, NOSIP, PDYN, PRKCZ, UTRN, and GLUL) for NOA patients (Figure 4(A)), which were chosen for further analyses. Validation of hub genes along the spermatogenic trajectory at single-cell resolution

Figure 9(B) illustrated the expression of hub genes in the testes: IL1RN was predominantly found in Macrophages, UTRN in Endothelial cells, and NOSIP and PRKCZ were mainly expressed in germ cells, especially Spermatids.

To understand hub genes’ roles in spermatogenesis, Pseudotime trajectory analysis was used to map development from spermatogonia to spermatids. In normal spermatogenesis (OA group), all four hub genes showed distinct stage-specific expressions, unlike in the NOA group. IL1RN remained consistently low during spermatogenesis (Figure 9(C)). The pseudotemporal expression kinetics of NOSIP showed a loss of expression peaks in late spermatids (Figure 9(D)). The trajectory of PRKCZ demonstrated a gradual upregulation peaking at the spermatocyte stage (Figure 9(E)). Figure 9(F) showed that UTRN expression was suppressed throughout spermatogenesis.

Discussion

This study investigated the role of oxidative stress in NOA, a severe male infertility condition, by analyzing a dataset from the GEO database. It identified 1,285 differentially expressed genes related to glutathione metabolism and other pathways. IL1RN, NOSIP, PDYN, PRKCZ, and UTRN emerged as potential NOA markers. Single-cell analysis confirmed the critical regulatory roles and stage-specific dysregulation of PRKCZ, UTRN, and NOSIP in spermatogenesis, indicating them as key pathological features of NOA. This research enhances the understanding of NOA.

The IL1RN gene encodes the Interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), which functions by binding to the interleukin-1 receptor without activating it, thereby inhibiting IL-1 activity. Owing to its anti-inflammatory properties, IL-1Ra is a promising candidate for the treatment of various diseases. 28 Studies on male reproductive health indicate that IL-1Ra influences the growth and differentiation of prostate stem cells by modulating androgen receptor signaling in chronic prostatitis, consequently affecting the development of prostate hyperplasia. 29 Testicular inflammation is known to compromise reproductive function and can lead to infertility. Macrophages facilitate inflammation through the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, which IL1RN can inhibit. 30 The study observed that IL1RN levels are reduced in testes affected by NOA compared to those with OA. This diminished expression of IL1RN may be insufficient to mitigate inflammation resulting from oxidative stress. Although some protein databases may not detect IL1RN in testicular tissue due to technical constraints, transcriptomic analyses confirm its fundamental transcription in human testicular tissue. These findings are consistent with previous research, 31 which demonstrated the expression of IL-1Ra in testicular cells.

Oxidative stress is a key cellular response to excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), activating pathways like MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and JNK, which influence cell survival, growth, and death. The PRKCZ gene encodes PKCζ, a kinase that responds to oxidative stress by modulating the MAPK 32 and PI3K/Akt 33 pathways, affecting cell fate. Additionally, NOSIP regulates nitric oxide (NO) production by interacting with nitric oxide synthase (NOS). 34 NO helps reduce oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals 35 and inhibiting lipid peroxidation. 36 In this study, we demonstrated that both PRKCZ and NOSIP exhibited significantly reduced expression levels and abnormal expression patterns in spermatogenic cells compared to the OA group. Despite the presence of intrinsic antioxidant defense systems in the human body, we hypothesize that the downregulation of these hub genes may impair the initiation or execution of these protective mechanisms, thereby exacerbating oxidative damage in the testicular microenvironment. Future research efforts will prioritize in-depth experimental investigations into these potential underlying mechanisms. This study highlights OS’s role in NOA but acknowledges the condition’s complexity. OS effects are linked with genetic and hormonal factors, such as Y-chromosome microdeletions causing spermatogenic arrest and hormonal imbalances weakening testicular defenses. Thus, the identified hub genes should be considered within this intricate biological framework. This study also identified several hub genes known as key regulators of oxidative stress in various physiological contexts. While their roles in male germ cell biology are not yet documented, our findings suggest they could be new therapeutic targets for NOA, meriting further experimental validation.

RSV is a naturally occurring polyphenol recognized for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Although its protective effects on male infertility have been investigated in previous animal models. 37 Its clinical application remains a subject of considerable debate. In silico docking analysis identified RSV as a computational candidate with a high structural affinity for hub target proteins; however, this geometric compatibility does not necessarily translate into direct therapeutic efficacy. Current literature indicates that while multi-ingredient supplements containing RSV may enhance certain sperm parameters, studies evaluating RSV as a monotherapy have produced inconsistent outcomes. 38 Additionally, our computational screening unexpectedly identified strong theoretical binding affinities between these target genes and certain pesticides and environmental toxicants, suggesting their potential vulnerability to environmental reproductive toxicity. Future empirical research is required to explore how these toxicants affect these genes, potentially leading to oxidative stress and NOA.

This study on NOA advanced understanding of the testicular microenvironment through comprehensive transcriptomic analysis and single-cell resolution of hub genes. The study used qRT-PCR to verify gene expression and applied molecular docking as an exploratory in silico screening method, suggesting new structural insights into potential NOA mechanisms and interactions with small molecules. By employing a two-step approach—identifying systemic DEGs and hub genes, then mapping them with scRNA-seq data—it linked tissue-level changes to specific cell types and identified candidate NOA biomarkers. Additionally, the study’s diagnostic model demonstrated robust statistical validation and generalization, maintaining significant discriminative ability across multiple independent datasets, despite inherent batch effects.

This study has several limitations. First, analyzing NOA patients as a single group without detailed sub-stratification may introduce heterogeneity bias; future research should involve rigorously stratified cohorts. Second, sample size constraints and group imbalances could lead to statistical bias. Third, despite normalization, residual biases from different technologies may remain. Fourth, our findings are primarily based on transcriptomic correlations; while our machine learning models show strong predictive value, the hub genes identified are highly correlated candidates rather than definitive clinical biomarkers. Validating their clinical utility and our hypothetical small-molecule predictions requires large-scale prospective cohorts and protein-level functional assays. Lastly, although we used single-cell transcriptomic validation for cell-type specificity, this approach lacks spatial context, highlighting the need for spatial transcriptomics to accurately map these hub genes within the testicular microenvironment.

Recognizing these constraints outlines a clear path for future research. Identifying hub proteins in testicular tissue could enhance idiopathic NOA diagnostics by incorporating molecular insights into the traditional histological approach. This would allow for better patient stratification by subtyping NOA based on molecular signatures rather than morphology, providing deeper insights into the disease. To translate these in silico findings into clinical practice, future studies must focus on key steps: rigorously validating diagnostic biomarkers in diverse human testicular biopsies, confirming the diagnostic nomogram’s accuracy in large clinical cohorts, and thoroughly validating small-molecule candidates like resveratrol experimentally. Before moving to in vivo bioavailability tests or randomized trials, future wet-lab studies must verify physical interactions with biophysical assays and demonstrate biological relevance through in vitro evidence. Only candidates that meet these criteria can proceed to preclinical and clinical testing.

Conclusion

This study identified IL1RN, NOSIP, PRKCZ and UTRN as oxidative stress-related candidate biomarkers and enhanced the comprehension of NOA.
