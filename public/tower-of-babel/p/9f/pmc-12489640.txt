Effect of Layer Sequence in Ce–Ni Bilayer Films on Catalytic Ozonation of Methylparaben
Authors: Daynahi Franco Peláez, Julia Liliana Rodríguez Santillán, Tatyana Poznyak, Hugo Martínez Gutiérrez, Jorge L. Vazquez Arce, Luis Alberto Moreno Ruiz, J. Alberto Andraca Adame, Claudia Jazmín Ramos Torres, Claudia Marissa Aguilar Melo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), particularly catalytic ozonation, offer effective strategies for the degradation of contaminants of emerging concern (CEC). This study evaluates the role of layer sequence in Ce–Ni bilayer thin films synthesized via ultrasonic spray pyrolysis (USP) in the catalytic ozonation of methylparaben (MePB), a widely used antimicrobial preservative and representative CEC. Using water as the only solvent and benign precursors, the films were directly deposited on glass substrates. While ozonation alone led to full MePB degradation, it achieved limited mineralization. The inclusion of Ce–Ni bilayer catalysts significantly enhanced total organic carbon (TOC) removal. Notably, the Ce–Ni (L–L) configuration outperformed Ni–Ce (L–L), achieving 58.5% mineralization vs 38.6%, respectively, after 120 min. Radical scavenger experiments confirmed the generation of multiple reactive oxygen species (•OH, 1O2, •O2 –), with catalytic activity linked to surface oxygen vacancies and Ce3+ concentration as determined by XPS analysis. The Ce–Ni (L–L) thin film exhibited excellent stability over five reuse cycles and maintained activity in tap water, highlighting its practical robustness. Toxicity assays using Lactuca sativa confirmed the nontoxic nature of treated effluent. These results denote Ce–Ni thin films as a reusable, scalable, and sustainable platform for water decontamination applications.

Introduction

Climate change and environmental pollution (air, water, and soil) have severely impacted health human and economic development. In response, governments and research sectors have proposed sustainable and environmentally friendly solutions. In the field of environmental remediation, advanced water treatment methods have demonstrated high efficiency in pollutant removal, supported by analytic techniques capable of detecting trace-level contaminants. ,

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) comprise a group of technologies that generate reactive radicals capable of breaking down complex organic compounds into smaller or biodegradable byproducts. , Typically, AOPs employ primary oxidants (e.g., ozone, hydrogen peroxide, oxygen, peroxymonosulfate, persulfate) combined with energy sources (e.g., visible or ultraviolet light) or catalysts. , In the case of catalytic systems, researchers have developed novel synthesis approaches to improve the mechanical, chemical, thermal, and magnetic properties of these materials. Based on their mobility, catalysts can be classified into two types: mobile (powder form) and immobilized (thin films). Immobilized catalysts offer advantages such as ease of reuse, reduced material consumption (from nanometers to a few micrometers), enhanced durability, and facile integration into various devices.

Among the techniques for fabricating multilayer thin films, the layer-by-layer (LbL) approach enables the deposition of materials with precise control over composition and thickness. LbL films have attracted attention in pharmaceutical, electronic, and environmental applications. , This method involves multilayer of ultrathin structures formed through the adsorption of materials onto the substrate surface, enabling intermolecular interactions, such as electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, covalent bonding, van der Waals force, or host–guest interaction.

Ultrasonic spray pyrolysis (USP) is a versatile method for constructing LbL structures. In this process, precursor droplets generated by ultrasonic waves are sprayed onto a heated substrate. , Upon impact, the droplets flatten into disk-like shapes and undergo thermal decomposition, forming a thin film. The overlapping disks result in a continuous layer with advantages such as simplicity, low cost, scalability, process tunability, and operation under atmospheric conditions.

Despite the potential of USP, few studies have explored its use in the fabrication of multilayer films for environmental application. Mahadik et al. reported the deposition of TiO2/α-Fe2O3 composites on glass and FTO-coated substrates; achieving 98% removal of Rhodamine B in 20 min, with a mineralization rate of 40%. Similarly, Polat Gonullu demonstrated 72% removal of methylene blue using a ZnO/Al2O3 bilayer film after 90 min of photocatalysis. These findings highlight the potential of multilayer films for water treatment. However, most previous studies focused exclusively on photocatalysis.

Recently, catalytic ozonation has emerged as a promising AOP for the degradation of organic pollutants, offering high oxidation potential without additional energy input. , To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has reported the design, synthesis, and characterization of a single bilayer film via USP for catalytic ozonation applications. Cerium oxide (CeO2) is a particularly effective material for catalytic ozonation due to its Ce4+/Ce3+ redox couple, which promotes the formation of unstable oxygen vacancies and reactive oxygen species (ROS). CeO2 has shown excellent performance in the ozonation of oxalic acid, phenol, ,, and p-nitrophenol. Nickel oxide (NiO), a key transition metal oxide, has also demonstrated synergistic effects with ozone. Literature suggests that combining two metal oxides may enhance ROS formation and, consequently, improve mineralization efficiency. −

In this study, we synthesized two LbL bilayer films CeO2/NiO (Ce–Ni (L–L)) and NiO/CeO2 (Ni–Ce (L–L)) via USP. The objective was to investigate the influence of the layer sequence on the catalytic ozonation performance. The films were characterized in terms of their crystalline structures, morphologies, and chemical composition. Catalytic activity was evaluated using methylparaben (MePB), which is considered an emergent concern due to its persistent occurrence in water sources and extensive use in personal care products and pharmaceuticals.

Experimental Section

Material Preparation

Bilayer thin films were deposited onto a glass substrate (“Lauka” 2.5 cm × 7.5 cm) using USP. The synthesis procedure was as follows: (a) substrates were sequentially cleaned with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water in an ultrasonic bath for 20 min each, (b) a 0.1 M solution of nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni­(NO3)2 ·6H2O, Sigma-Aldrich) was sprayed onto the substrate to form a nickel oxide (Ni) layer, (c) the film was then cooled for 12 h, and (d) the substrate was reheated to 450 °C, after which a 0.1 M solution of cerium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce­(NO3)3 ·6H2O, Sigma-Aldrich) was sprayed under identical condition to deposit CeO2 layers. This configuration was designated Ce–Ni (L–L). Reversing the deposition order yielded the Ni–Ce (L–L) configuration.

The nozzle-to-substrate distance was selected based on optimal conditions reported in a previous study. All films were stored under ambient conditions for subsequent characterization.

Material Characterizations

The crystalline structures of the thin films were analyzed using Grazing Incidence X-ray Diffraction (GIXRD, XPERT-Pro) with Cu Kα irradiation (λ = 1.54183 Å, 40 mA and 45 kV), in the range 20°≤ 2θ ≤ 70°, with a fixed incidence angle of 0.5°. Surface morphology was examined by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM, Jeol-JSM 7800 F) at 1.5 kV.

X-ray photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific, USA) was employed to determine the surface chemical composition. A K-Alpha instrument equipped with an Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV, 150 W) was used. Survey and high-resolution scans were collected with pass energies of 200 and 40 eV, respectively, and a spot size a 400 μm2.

Surface roughness was evaluated via an Atomic Force Microscope (AFM, MultiMode V, Bruker USA) with a NanoScope V controller. Film thickness was measured using an Uvisel spectroscopy ellipsometer (model LT M200AGMS, HORIBA) and data were modeled using the Tauc Lorentz dispersion formula (Delta Psi software, version 2.1). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) was carried out using a JEOL JEM2100F equipped with a STEM unit at 200 kV. Samples were prepared by scraping the films with a razor, dispersing them in isopropanol, and depositing them on copper grids.

Evaluation of Thin Films

The catalytic ozonation performance of the thin films was evaluated in a semicontinuous batch reactor for MePB removal. Initial tests were performed in distilled water, followed by experiments in tap water from Azcapotzalco, Mexico City. In a typical test, 500 mL of MePB solution (20 ± 2 mg L–1) was added to the reactor and ozonized for 120 min at room temperature. Ozone (15 mg L–1 in the gas phase) was introduced through a porous glass filter at a constant flow rate of 0.2 L min–1. Six glass plates coated with either Ni–Ce (L–L) or Ce–Ni (L–L) thin films were placed in the reactor. Samples were collected at regular intervals for analysis.

Ozone was generated by using a laboratory-scale ozonizer (HTU500G, Azco Industries, Canada) with high-purity oxygen as the feed gas. Residual ozone in the gas phase was measured with a BMT 946 BT (BMT Messtechnik, Berlin) and destroyed using an activated carbon filter. Addition experiments were conducted in the presence of radical scavengers: tert-butanol (20 mg L–1), sodium azide (0.11 M), and p-benzoquinone (0.2 M).

Analytical Procedures

MePB concentrations were determined using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC, PerkinElmer Flexar) with a diode-array detector at 254 nm, equipped with a YMC C18 column. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile:water (50:50 v/v), adjusted to pH 2.5 with phosphoric acid, at flow rate of 0.5 mL min–1. Organic acids were analyzed using a Prevail Organic Acid column (Grace, 150 mm × 4.6 mm) with a mobile phase composed of KH2PO4 (0.25 mM, pH 2.5 with phosphoric acid) and acetonitrile (85:15 v/v) at a flow rate of 1 mL min–1 and detection at 210 nm. Total organic carbon (TOC) was measured with a Shimadzu TOC-500 A analyzer. Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS) was employed to assess the stability of thin films. Samples collected after 120 min of ozonation treatment were analyzed using a Perkin Elmer AAnalyst 100 Spectrometer equipped with a nickel lamp, operating with an acetylene–oxygen flow rate of 0.3 L min–1. Prior to analysis, each sample was digested with HNO3 under heating for 1 h with constant agitation. The digested solution was then filtered through Whatman 541 filter paper, and the filtrate was collected for measurement.

Toxicity Testing of Ozonized Effluents

Toxicity of effluents collected after 120 min of ozonation was evaluated using Lactuca sativa (lettuce) seed germination assays. In each test, 8 mL of treated effluent was placed on filter paper in a 90 mm Petri dish containing 20 seeds. The dishes were incubated at 24 °C in the dark for 5 days. According to US EPA recommendations, ecotoxicological predictions were supported using ECOSAR (Ecological Structure Activity Relationships) software, version 2.2.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Thin Films

The crystalline structure of Ni–Ce (L–L) and Ce–Ni (L–L) films was investigated by XRD, as shown in Figure a. Both materials exhibited diffraction peaks corresponding to the cubic fluorite structure of CeO2, with characteristic planes indexed to (111), (200), (220), and (311), regardless of whether CeO2 or NiO was the top layer. The diffraction patterns matched the reference data for CeO2 (JCPDS No. 01-081-0792). The layer sequence of Ce–Ni (L-L) or Ni–Ce (L-L) affects the intensity of the diffraction peaks in GIXRD measurements. Additionally, the technique influences the broadening of the diffraction peaks. Another very important factor is that the structure factor of the material in this case, the structure factor of the CeO2 film is much higher than that of NiO. For this reason, the CeO2 peaks are clearly visible, while the NiO peaks are barely distinguishable from the background noise.

(a) XRD patterns, (b) Raman spectra, and (c) magnified Raman spectra of synthesized thin films.

Raman spectra were collected in the range of 180–1500 cm–1, Figure b. Three prominent bands were observed at 460, 600, and 1106.6 cm–1 corresponding to the F2g mode (symmetric breathing of O atoms in the fluorite-type CeO2 structure), a defect-induced (D) mode, and the second-order longitudinal optical (2LO) mode, respectively. , While both films exhibited these bands, their intensity was significantly lower in Ni–Ce (L–L) than in Ce–Ni (L–L). This difference could potentially influence the catalytic activity of Ni–Ce (L–L), as it may reflect a reduced concentration of vacancies (Frenkel-type and oxygen), which are generally recognized as active sites in catalytic ozonation. ,

Figure c shows a magnified view of the D band, which comprises three main components: (a) Frenkel-type oxygen vacancies (D1) at 596 cm–1, caused by oxygen displacement to octahedral interstitial sites; (b) an extrinsic defect mode (D2) at 640 cm–1 due to dopant incorporation into the Ce lattice; and (c) an intrinsic defect (D3) mode at 555 cm–1 assigned to oxygen vacancies (Vo) associated with Ce3+. These features were more pronounced in the Ce–Ni (L–L) film, consistent with the higher contribution of CeO2 at the surface.

The lower intensity of the F2g band in Ni–Ce (L–L) is attributed to the CeO2 layer being at the bottom, limiting the detection of defect contributions by Raman spectroscopy. Consequently, ozone may not initially interact with Frenkel defects in Ni–Ce (L–L), potentially reducing the catalytic activity.

To corroborate the Raman data, surface chemical composition was analyzed by high-resolution XPS, Figure a–d. The Ni 2p and Ce 3d regions are shown in Figure a and 2b, respectively. The Ni–Ce (L–L) film did not show Ce 3d signals, likely because the NiO layer was thick enough to mask the underlying CeO2. In contrast, both Ce 3d and Ni 2p signals were detected in the Ce–Ni (L–L) film, indicating a thinner CeO2 layer (<10 nm), consistent with ellipsometry results. A higher Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio was observed in the Ce–Ni (L–L) film, suggesting a greater number of oxygen vacancies that may enhance the catalytic activity.

High-resolution XPS spectra in the (a,b) Ni 2p and Ce 3d regions and (c,d) O 1s for (a,c) Ni–Ce (L–L) and (b,d) Ce–Ni (L–L) films.

The O 1s spectra were deconvoluted into three components: a lattice oxygen peak at 529.08 eV, a hydroxyl group signal at 531.88 eV, and a component at 530.08 likely associated with adsorbed oxygen (Figure c and 2d). Both films showed significant contributions from surface –OH and adsorbed water species, which play a role in ozone decomposition.

In addition, atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis revealed both films consisted of spherical grains with average sizes of 7.35 and 8.15 nm for Ce–Ni (L–L) and Ni–Ce (L–L), respectively, Figure . The root-mean-square (RMS) surface roughness was 2.25 nm for Ce–Ni (L–L) and 1.02 nm for Ni–Ce (L–L) (see Table ). The increased roughness in Ce–Ni (L–L) is attributed to smaller grains and a more porous structure, which may improve catalytic performance.

Main Characteristics of As-Prepared Films

The surfaces of Ni–Ce (L–L) and Ce–Ni (L–L) thin films obtained from SEM analysis are compared in Figure . Ni–Ce (L–L) displayed densely packed spherical agglomerates, while Ce–Ni (L–L) exhibited a more uniform grain distribution with visible pinholes and voids, potentially enhancing the accessible surface area.

SEM images of Ni–Ce (L–L) and Ce–Ni (L–L) thin films.

Certainly, the configuration of bilayer Ce–Ni (L–L) favored the formation of a rough surface, which most likely results in a large specific surface area and greater catalytic activity. Ellipsometry confirmed that the total film thickness was lower for Ce–Ni (L–L) (Table ), likely due to reduced cerium precursor loading, yet this configuration achieved the highest MePB mineralization.

To further investigate the microstructural characteristics of as-prepared bilayer thin films, TEM and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analyses were performed, as shown in Figure . Figure a displays the Ni–Ce (L–L) thin film, revealing nanocrystallites with an average size of approximately 10 nm aggregated into compact structures. Variations in image contrast are attributed to differences in thickness and the atomic number of the constituent oxide layers. HRTEM images (Figure b and c) show well-defined lattice fringes on the film surface, with interplanar spacings of 0.31 and 0.24 nm corresponding to the (111) planes of CeO2 and NiO, respectively. Figure d presents a TEM image of the Ce–Ni (L–L) thin film showing distinct nanocrystalline domains. HRTEM analysis revealed lattice fringes with interplanar distances of 0.31 and 0.241 nm, consistent with the (111) planes of CeO2 and NiO, respectively, Figure e and 5f. Structural defects such as misalignments and vacancies were also observed, suggesting a high density of crystal imperfections that may contribute to enhanced catalytic activity.

Catalytic Performances of the Thin Film

Figure a shows the MePB degradation profiles during ozonation in the presence of synthesized bilayer thin films. No significant improvement in MePB removal was observed within the first 20 min compared to ozonation alone. However, TOC removal was markedly improved in the presence of the catalysts. After 120 min of reaction, Ce–Ni (L–L) and Ni–Ce (L–L) films achieved 58.5% and 38.6% mineralization, respectively (Figure b). The film with CeO2 as the top layer exhibited superior TOC removal, which is likely due to the generation of more active surface sites during the synthesis.

(a) MePB degradation curves and (b) the corresponding TOC removal by catalytic ozonation in the presence of the synthesized thin film.

As shown in Figure a, the addition of scavengers helped to elucidate the ROS involved in the catalytic ozonation process. The presence of tert-butanol (TBA) and p-benzoquinone (p-BQ) caused a substantial reduction in the level of TOC removal (58%), indicating that •OH and •O2 – were the dominant reactive species. The addition of sodium azide (NaN3) reduced the level of TOC removal to 75% at 120 min, suggesting that 1O2 also contributed to MePB degradation. These results confirm that •OH, •O2 –, and 1O2 play synergistic roles in the catalytic ozonation mechanism promoted by Ce–Ni (L–L). Considering that the Ce–Ni (L–L) sample exhibited the best catalytic performance, it is plausible to infer that the high content of oxygen vacancies promoted the formation of •OH, thereby enhancing the catalytic activity. This inference is further supported by Raman spectra, which indicate a higher density of oxygen vacancies in the Ce–Ni (L–L) film.

The overall trend in scavenger-induced was p-BQ ≈ TBA > NaN3, confirming the combined involvement of multiple ROS. Reusability tests showed that the Ce–Ni (L–L) film maintained the percentage of TOC removal as is showed in Figure b and its crystalline structure after five cycles, as confirmed by XRD (see Supporting Information, Figure S1). In fact, a slight improvement in crystallinity was observed after five uses, indicating a transition from a more amorphous to a more ordered phase under oxidative conditions. The slight decrease in catalytic efficiency observed in Figure a may be attributed to the accumulation of surface impurities rather than catalyst degradation.

XPS spectra of Ni 2p, Ce 3d, and O1 regions before and after the catalytic reaction are shown in Figure . The Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio increased from 0.86 to 6.62 (Figure a and 8b), indicating a relative depletion of Ce3+ species, which is associated with a reduction in the oxygen-vacancy concentration on the catalyst surface (Figure c and 8d). Oxygen vacancies are known to facilitate ROS generation during catalytic ozonation; therefore, their partial loss could, in principle, diminish catalytic performance. However, the TOC removal efficiency remained essentially constant (∼58.5%) over five consecutive cycles, implying that Ni and Ce sites contribute cooperatively to ozone decomposition and ROS formation, and the overall catalytic performance is not solely dependent on Ce3+-derived vacancies.

The quantification of the Ni2+/Ni3+ ratio was not feasible, the predominant oxidation state observed in the Ni 2p region before and after the reaction was Ni2+, Figure a and 8b. Nevertheless, Li et al. reported a minor presence of Ni3+ species during catalytic ozonation, which could contribute to ROS generation, as described in reactions 15–17. In the XPS analysis of the Ni 2p region, no distinct contribution from Ni3+ could be identified; however, it is plausible that Ni3+ species are transiently generated as reaction intermediates and thus remain undetectable in the post-reaction spectra. Additionally, AAS of the postreaction solution revealed negligible Ni leaching (<0.1 ppm), confirming the structural robustness of the catalysts under reaction condition.

Toxicity Assessment of the Ozonized Effluent

According to the TOC results, complete mineralization was not achieved in the presence of Ce–Ni (L–L) films. Therefore, it was necessary to assess whether the oxidation intermediates were less toxic than the parent compound (MePB). For this purpose, toxicity tests were conducted using effluents from both conventional and catalytic ozonation in distilled (DW) and Azcapotzalco water (AW). Seed germination and growth test were carried out using L. sativa (Simpson variety).

Figure a shows the hypocotyl growth of seedlings exposed to effluents collected after 120 min of ozonation, with and without Ce–Ni (L–L) catalysts. The average hypocotyl length was approximately 15 ± 1 mm for both DW and AW (control), and all treated samples showed similar lengths, indicating no adverse effects. As shown in Figure b, a slight increase in root length was observed in AW samples treated with ozone compared to that treated with conventional ozonation, but this was not statistically significant. It is worth noting that hypocotyl elongation is considered a more reliable toxicity indicator than root growth; in this case, treated effluents exhibited no inhibitory effects compared to the control.

(a) Sprout growth and (b) root of L.sativa in the toxicity bioassay of ozone samples with the Ce–Ni (L–L) film and (c) the predicted chronic toxicity for MePB and oxalic acid by the ECOSAR program.

In addition, the ECOSAR program (version 2.2) was used to predict the chronic toxicity of MePB and oxalic acid (a recalcitrant byproduct often generated during conventional ozonation (Figure S2)). Figure c presents the LC50 values for both compounds against three aquatic species: fish, daphnids, and green algae. The initial MePB concentration (20 mg L–1) was considered toxic only to fish (LC50 = 18.4 mg L–1), while it posed no threat to daphnids or algae. However, MePB was fully degraded in all of the treated samples. The predicted toxicity of oxalic acid was also low, even at its maximum concentration (16.3 mg L–1) observed under conventional ozonation. In catalytic ozonation, oxalic acid levels were significantly lower, indicating a reduced toxicity of byproducts.

These findings demonstrate that the degradation intermediates produced by both ozonation processes were not harmful to aquatic organisms, confirming that the catalytic system studied here can contribute to the ecological safety of the treated waters.

The catalytic activity of the Ce–Ni (L–L) film is comparable to or even superior to that of other materials employed in AOPs. Notably, most of the studies cited used powder-form catalysts, which required additional post-treatment steps for separation and recovery. In contrast, the thin films developed in this work offer the advantage of easy handling and reuse without the need for complex separation procedures.

Furthermore, this study presents an effective and reusable catalytic material with potential for application in water treatment technologies based on catalytic ozonation. The environmentally friendly synthesis route employed here could also serve as a model for the development of new thin-film materials for environmental remediation purposes.

Conclusions

Bilayer thin films of cerium oxide and nickel oxide were successfully deposited on glass substrates via a simple and environmentally friendly synthesis method. The sequence of layer deposition by USP significantly influenced the physicochemical properties of the thin films. The Ce–Ni (L–L) configuration, obtained by depositing nickel oxide first followed by cerium oxide, exhibited enhanced crystalline, improved nanoparticle dispersion, low surface roughness, and thickness below 80 nm. XPS and Raman analyses showed a higher content of Vo in Ce–Ni (L–L) compared to the reverse configuration.

In terms of the catalytic performance, the bilayer films were evaluated in the catalytic ozonation of methylparaben in water. The Ce–Ni (L–L) film achieved the highest mineralization efficiency, significantly outperforming the Ni–Ce (L–L) film. This enhanced activity was attributed to the greater generation of reactive oxygen species, including •OH, •O2 –, and 1O2.

Moreover, the Ce–Ni (L–L) catalyst demonstrated excellent reusability and stability, maintaining high performance over five reuse cycles. It also showed promising activity in tap water, indicating its potential for use in real-world water treatment applications. Finally, toxicity bioassays confirmed that the treated effluents were nontoxic to aquatic organisms, highlighting the environmental safety and applicability of the system studied.
