Association between normal-weight central obesity and asymptomatic hyperuricemia in Korean adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Minok Shim, Hyun Joe, Jung-Eun Oh, Yong-Jin Cho, Hwang-Sik Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity increases the risk of hyperuricemia. Recent studies have shown that normal-weight central obesity increases the risk of cardiometabolic disease. In this study, we examined the relationship between normal-weight central obesity and asymptomatic hyperuricemia in Korean adults aged > 20 years.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 14,501 adults who visited the health checkup center at a university hospital in 2021. The participants were classified based on sex, and were divided into four groups according to their body mass index and waist-to-height ratio as follows: normal weight, normal-weight central obesity, obesity, and obesity central obesity groups. The odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for asymptomatic hyperuricemia were analyzed separately using logistic regression models.ResultsRegardless of sex, the prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia was higher in the central obesity groups than in the normal weight group. The odds ratios for asymptomatic hyperuricemia were higher in the normal-weight central obesity group than in the normal weight group, even after adjusting for confounding factors (p < 0.05).ConclusionsIn Korean adults aged over 20 years, normal-weight central obesity was significantly associated with asymptomatic hyperuricemia. Therefore, screening tests and proper management of asymptomatic hyperuricemia in normal-weight central obesity individuals are necessary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24756-z.Keywords: Asymptomatic hyperuricemia, Normal-weight central obesity, Obesity, Central obesity

Background

Asymptomatic hyperuricemia is characterized by elevated serum uric acid levels without the clinical manifestations or symptoms of gout [1]. Serum uric acid levels are regulated by a balance between uric acid production and excretion [2]. Hyperuricemia occurs when uric acid production increases, or its excretion becomes impaired. Hyperuricemia has been recognized and managed as a precursor to gout and nephrolithiasis due to urate crystal deposition. However, recent studies have revealed its association with an increased risk of chronic metabolic disorders, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and metabolic syndrome [3–5]. Therefore, the identification and management of asymptomatic hyperuricemia have emerged as significant societal challenges in chronic disease management.

Obesity, defined as excessive fat accumulation that negatively impacts health, is on the rise both globally as well as in South Korea. It is associated with several conditions, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and metabolic syndrome, which can collectively increase socioeconomic burdens [6]. Many other epidemiological studies have demonstrated that obesity increases the risk of asymptomatic hyperuricemia [7–9].

The body mass index (BMI) is commonly used to define obesity. However, BMI use has limitations, as it is influenced by several factors such as age, sex, and ethnicity, making it an imprecise measure of body fat distribution [10]. Central obesity, often indicative of visceral fat accumulation, has been established as a risk factor for metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and cerebrovascular diseases [11–14]. Additionally, central obesity indicators such as waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) have shown stronger associations with cardiovascular metabolic risks compared to BMI, in previous research [15, 16]. Therefore, assessing central obesity is essential when evaluating its health impacts. There is a growing interest in conducting research on individuals classified as having normal weight but exhibiting central obesity (normal-weight central obesity) [17]. Although previous domestic studies have investigated the relationship between normal-weight central obesity and cardiovascular risk factors [18, 19], no research has examined its association with asymptomatic hyperuricemia. Among the various indicators of central obesity, WHtR is particularly notable for its ease of calculation using standard anthropometric data and its strong correlation with cardiovascular metabolic diseases [15].

We investigated the association between asymptomatic hyperuricemia and normal-weight central obesity using BMI and WHtR among participants undergoing health checkups at a university hospital’s health checkup center.

Methods

Study population

This study included 16,666 adults aged 20 years or older who underwent health check-ups at a university hospital’s health checkup center between January 2021 and December 2021. Subsequently, we excluded 1,361 individuals with missing data on medical history and Lifestyle questionnaires, 364 individuals with missing serum uric acid or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, 352 individuals with a BMI < 18.5, and 88 individuals diagnosed with gout or currently taking urate-lowering medications. A total of 14,501 participants were ultimately included. Approval from the hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB No. 2023-05-002) was received.

Variables and measurements

The participants completed a questionnaire during their visit and signed informed consent forms. The questionnaire focused on medical history, medication use, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and physical activity. The questionnaire form was originally used internally by our health checkup center for data collection. Its English version has been provided as Additional file 1. The smoking status was classified into three groups: current, former, and never smokers. Alcohol consumption was assessed based on the average weekly alcohol intake over the past year and converted into standard drinks (14 g of alcohol per drink) according to the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) guidelines. Men consuming more than 14 drinks per week and women consuming more than seven drinks per week were classified as heavy drinkers, those consuming fewer were classified as moderate drinkers, and those who abstained were classified as non-drinkers. Physical activity was categorized based on the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for adults: participants engaging in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity exercise per week or at least 75 min of vigorous-intensity exercise per week (or an equivalent combination) were classified as Sufficiently active, whereas others were classified as insufficiently active. Height and weight were measured to the nearest decimal point using an automated body measurement device, and BMI was calculated. WC was measured following exhalation at the midpoint between the lowest rib and iliac crest while the participants stood with their feet approximately 30 cm apart. After at least 5 min of rest, blood pressure was measured using an automated device. Venous blood samples were collected, following a fast of at least 10 h, to measure serum uric acid, creatinine, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides, fasting glucose, and HbA1c levels.

Asymptomatic hyperuricemia is defined as serum uric acid levels exceeding 7 mg/dL in men and 6 mg/dL in women [2, 8]. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using serum creatinine levels according to the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) Eq. [20] Hypertension is defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or the current use of antihypertensive medications [21]. Dyslipidemia is defined as LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, or the current use of lipid-lowering medications [22]. Diabetes mellitus is defined as fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or the current use of antidiabetic medications [23]. WHtR was calculated by dividing WC by height, with a value of ≥ 0.5 indicating central obesity [24]. The participants were categorized into four groups based on their BMI and WHtR:

Normal weight group (NW): BMI within the normal range (18.5–24.9) and WHtR < 0.5.

Normal weight group (NW): BMI within the normal range (18.5–24.9) and WHtR < 0.5.

Normal-weight with central obesity group (NWCO): BMI within the normal range and WHtR ≥ 0.5.

Normal-weight with central obesity group (NWCO): BMI within the normal range and WHtR ≥ 0.5.

Obesity group (OB): BMI ≥ 25 and WHtR < 0.5.

Obesity group (OB): BMI ≥ 25 and WHtR < 0.5.

Obesity with central obesity group (OBCO): BMI ≥ 25 and WHtR ≥ 0.5.

Obesity with central obesity group (OBCO): BMI ≥ 25 and WHtR ≥ 0.5.

Statistical analysis

Among the study participants, missing data were present for several key variables. Therefore, we conducted the analysis of missing values and their patterns. Age and sex variables were completely observed with no missing data. The overall missing rate was below 6% for anthropometric and blood test variables, whereas chronic disease status and Lifestyle-related variables exhibited missing rates between 7% and 13%. Detailed missingness for each variable is presented in [Additional file 2]. Little’s MCAR test (χ²=265.897, df = 76, p < 0.001) confirmed that the missing data did not follow a completely random pattern. To reduce potential bias, multiple imputation was performed using fully conditional specification (FCS), resulting in five imputed datasets. Continuous variables were imputed by linear regression, and categorical variables by logistic regression. All subsequent analyses were conducted using these multiply imputed datasets.

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were summarized as pooled means and effective sample sizes, with pooled standard deviations calculated according to Rubin’s rules. Group comparisons of means were conducted using independent two-sample t-tests. For categorical variables, valid sample sizes (n) were obtained through cross-tabulation, and frequencies were expressed as percentages. Group differences in categorical variables were assessed using logistic regression analysis.

The association between four groups classified by obesity and abdominal obesity status and asymptomatic hyperuricemia was evaluated via logistic regression. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were pooled across imputed datasets. Analyses were stratified by sex, presenting both unadjusted models and adjusted models controlling for age, estimated glomerular filtration rate, alcohol consumption, smoking, physical activity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus. Furthermore, interaction analysis was conducted to examine whether the association between obesity type and hyperuricemia differed by sex. To assess the appropriateness of our adjusted logistic regression models, we calculated the events per variable (EPV) based on the female sample size and prevalence of hyperuricemia. After multiple imputation, 6,146 women were included, with approximately 8.33% (n ≈ 512) exhibiting hyperuricemia. The adjusted model incorporated 12 covariates (three dummy variables for obesity type, age, estimated glomerular filtration rate, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and exercise). The calculated EPV was approximately 43.0, which significantly Surpasses the recommended minimum of 10, minimizing risks of overfitting and providing stable estimates in the adjusted models.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Standard (version 31.0.0.0; IBM Corp.), with statistical significance set at a p-value less than 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between asymptomatic hyperuricemia and different obesity types. Four groups were included: NW, NWCO, OB, OBCO, based on BMI and WHtR, among individuals undergoing health checkups at a university hospital. Even after adjusting for confounding variables, such as age, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity, the central obesity groups demonstrated statistically significant higher risks of asymptomatic hyperuricemia compared to the normal weight group (p-value < 0.001). Particularly, individuals with a normal BMI and central obesity also exhibited a significantly higher risk of asymptomatic hyperuricemia compared to the normal weight group. Obesity is often defined solely by BMI, and individuals with a normal BMI are usually not considered to require lifestyle education or intervention. However, our findings suggest that individuals with WHtR ≥ 0.5, indicating that their WC exceeds half their height, should undergo screening for asymptomatic hyperuricemia, and appropriate interventions, such as lifestyle modifications, may be necessary. Therefore, assessment of central obesity using WHtR and BMI could play a pivotal role in reducing the prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia.

Previous studies have established a relationship between obesity, central obesity, and hyperuricemia. For instance, in twin studies, Tanaka et al. [7] demonstrated an association between BMI and hyperuricemia even after adjusting for genetic predisposition. Yamashita et al. [25] found that weight loss in obese populations normalized serum uric acid levels. Yamada et al. [26] revealed that visceral and hepatic fat were independently correlated with hyperuricemia in men. A large cross-sectional study of U.S. adults [27] also revealed significant correlations between visceral adiposity indices and the prevalence of hyperuricemia and gout. Although the exact mechanisms remain unclear, obesity —and especially central obesity— may contribute to hyperuricemia by increasing levels of free fatty acids in the bloodstream, promoting triglyceride synthesis, enhancing uric acid production, and inhibiting uric acid excretion [9]. In addition, elevated serum uric acid levels have been implicated in developing metabolic abnormalities and insulin resistance [28]suggesting that hyperuricemia may itself contribute to the risk of obesity and central obesity. Thus, hyperuricemia and obesity including central obesity may act as reciprocal risk factors, mutually increasing the likelihood of each other’s occurrence. Clinically, this underscores the importance of an integrated approach that considers both conditions when managing patients.

Meanwhile, the odds ratios for hyperuricemia were higher in women than in men within both the NWCO and OBCO groups. Interaction analysis confirmed that these sex differences were statistically significant, indicating that women in the central obesity groups have a greater risk of hyperuricemia compared to the men in these groups. Similar sex-specific patterns were observed in a population-based study conducted by Su et al. in Taiwan [29]. These differences are presumed to be mainly due to estrogen. In women, estrogen levels gradually decline with aging, with a marked decrease after menopause. This decline contributes to a reduction in subcutaneous fat and an increase in abdominal fat accumulation [30]. The mean and median ages of women included in this study were 44.26 and 44.00 years, respectively. Considering that the average age of natural menopause in Korean women is approximately 49.3 years [31]it is plausible that a substantial proportion of postmenopausal women were included in the sample. The accumulation of abdominal fat is associated with elevated serum uric acid levels, as discussed in the previous paragraph. Moreover, estrogen is known to promote uric acid excretion, thereby lowering circulating uric acid concentrations [32]. Therefore, the elevated risk for hyperuricemia observed in abdominally obese women in this study may be partially attributed to the effects of estrogen decline—especially after menopause—leading to increased abdominal fat accumulation and subsequent elevation of serum uric acid levels.

In this study, the overall prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia was 20.13%, 27.81% in men and 8.33% in women. These rates are higher than those reported in earlier studies using health check-up data. Kim et al.‘s study in 2004[33] reported an overall prevalence of 9.3% (14.3% and 2.2% in male and female participants, respectively), whereas Hong et al.‘s study in 2012[34] reported a prevalence of 9.8% (15.0% and 4.1% in male and female participants, respectively). Although our findings may not represent the entire Korean population, they still suggest an increasing trend in the prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia in South Korea. Globally, hyperuricemia prevalence shows an upward trend in developing countries such as China and Thailand [35, 36], but remains stable in developed nations like the United States and Japan [37, 38]. This pattern may reflect socioeconomic development with increased meat consumption, alcohol intake, and sedentary lifestyles, all contributing to increasing rates of hyperuricemia until they reach a plateau. Efforts to monitor and conduct research on the prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia should continue in South Korea while promoting dietary education and lifestyle modifications to prevent further increase.

This study has several limitations:

As this was a cross-sectional study, a causal relationship between normal-weight central obesity and asymptomatic hyperuricemia could not be established, and prospective studies are needed.

Despite these Limitations, this study provides meaningful evidence on the association between normal-weight central obesity and asymptomatic hyperuricemia in a large sample of Korean adults. This underscores the importance of screening for asymptomatic hyperuricemia when WHtR exceeds 0.5, even among individuals with normal BMI, and highlights the need for appropriate intervention.

Conclusions

Normal-weight central obesity was associated with a higher risk of asymptomatic hyperuricemia compared to normal weight in Korean adults, and large-scale prospective studies are necessary to investigate the causal relationship between central obesity and asymptomatic hyperuricemia.
