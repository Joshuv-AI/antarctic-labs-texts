Cerchar abrasiveness index prediction based on rock properties leveraging hybrid soft computing techniques
Authors: Mohammad Matin Rouhani, Alireza Dolatshahi, Mahdi Hasanipanah, Yewuhalashet Fissha, N Rao Cheepurupalli, Taoufik Saidani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Cerchar Abrasiveness Index (CAI) is a vital parameter in geotechnical engineering, especially when it comes to tunneling and mechanized excavations. The study employed a comprehensive dataset of 163 samples representing various rock types, including igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic formations. The methodology included three base algorithms (XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest), improved by three distinct metaheuristic techniques: Arithmetic Optimization Algorithm (AOA), Reptile Search Optimization (RSO), and Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO). The Brazilian tensile strength (BTS), uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), equivalent quartz content (EQC), and brittleness index (BI) were the four main rock parameters used to make the predictive models. The model was further evaluated by splitting the data into 80% training and 20% testing sets. Subsequently, the model was compared to 17 real-world hard rock TBM projects in different countries and geological conditions. The AOA-optimized versions performed nicely, with AOA-LightGBM doing the best on the held-out test set (R² = 0.952, RMSE = 0.290, MAE = 0.208, VAF = 0.952). External validation showed that AOA-XGBoost performed properly, with the highest correlation coefficient of 0.8308 compared to field measurements from international tunneling projects. Also, the AOA-XGBoost did well on tests with R² = 0.951, RMSE = 0.296, MAE = 0.223, and VAF = 0.951. Using SHAP values to examine feature importance revealed unique parameter influence signatures. EQC was the most important parameter in XGBoost models, while UCS had the greatest impact in LightGBM and Random Forest-based models. The new method described here is an important advancement in CAI prediction methodology. It is more accurate and efficient than traditional experimental testing methods, and it works well on different types of rock. Introduction

One of the most critical considerations in drilling projects within civil and mining engineering, particularly in underground space and tunnel engineering, is acquiring comprehensive geological information about the project’s zones. A thorough understanding of the geological composition significantly aids in the tunnel excavation process. The utilization of mechanized cutting tools, such as; chisel bits and disc cutters in the cutter head, directly influences key parameters, including excavation speed, energy consumption, penetration ratio of the mechanized machine, and the overall time and costs associated with the project1–4. These crucial factors are contingent upon a multitude of subjects, including the geological characteristics and tectonic conditions of the region, as well as the operational specifics of the drilling machinery, such as the driving forces of the mechanized tunnel boring machine and the selection of an appropriate alloy for the cutting tools5.

Moreover, an essential aspect of managing tunneling project costs in rocky environments is the understanding of rock abrasion. Several experimental methodologies have been proposed to assess rock abrasion, including the Abrasion value of steel (AVS test), the Laboratoire Central des Ponts et Chaussées (LCPC) abrasion test, the Cerchar abrasiveness index test (CAI), the Vickers hardness number test (VHN), the wide wheel abrasion test (WWA), the Bohme test (BAT), and the rolling indentation abrasion test (RIAT)5,6.

The Abrasive Value of Steel (AVS) test assesses the abrasive influence of rock particles on a standardized steel surface under controlled conditions. This procedure entails the application of crushed rock fragments against a steel plate or disk at a consistent pressure and speed. The steel plate’s weight loss following a predetermined testing duration is measured and subsequently utilized to calculate the Abrasive Value of Steel (AVS)7. Also, the Vickers Hardness Test is one of the most widely used methods for evaluating the hardness of materials, including metals, ceramics, and rocks. This test provides a standardized method for quantifying a material’s resistance to indentation. In the fields of geotechnical engineering and mining, the Vickers Hardness Number (VHN) is crucial for characterizing rock properties, forecasting tool wear, and evaluating material behavior under mechanical stress8,9. The Laboratoire Central des Ponts et Chaussées (LCPC) test represents a method specifically designed to assess the abrasiveness of rocks, particularly within the context of road construction. In contrast to the Norwegian test, the LCPC test concentrates on the interaction between rock particles and a steel plate6,10. Among the various indices developed to quantify rock abrasiveness, the CERCHAR Abrasiveness Index (CAI) has emerged as one of the most widely recognized and standardized methodologies globally. The CAI was developed by the French organization CERCHAR (Centre d’Études et de Recherches sur les Matériaux pour la Construction Hydraulique et Routière) in the 1970s. This index was specifically designed to provide a straightforward yet effective means of assessing the abrasive properties of rocks, thereby proving particularly beneficial in tunneling, quarrying, and road construction contexts. Unlike other abrasiveness indices, the CAI emphasizes the mechanical interaction between rock particles and cutting tools, rendering it highly pertinent for applications in which material durability and tool performance are critical5,11. The Rolling Indentation Abrasion Test (RIAT) is an effective method for determining the abrasivity index of materials using actual discs. This evaluation analyzes the volume loss, wear scars’ depth, or surface roughness alterations12. The Wide Wheel Abrasion Test, also known as the Capon Test, is a standardized method for measuring the abrasion resistance of materials under controlled conditions. It is commonly used in various industries to evaluate the durability of materials. The Böhme Abrasion Test (BAT) is a widely recognized method for assessing the abrasion resistance of materials. This test specifically simulates conditions involving flat-on-flat abrasion, providing valuable insights into the durability of various substances13. It should be noted that although various petrographic, geomechanical, and environmental factors, including loading rate, moisture, and temperature, are known to influence the Cerchar Abrasiveness Index (CAI), a comprehensive evaluation of these influences was beyond the scope of this study. The present work focuses on the most widely reported mechanical and mineralogical parameters available across a broad range of rock types.

The empirical models (Table 2) often exhibit insufficient accuracy and are primarily applicable to specific rock types, which limits their effectiveness across diverse rock layers. Machine learning (ML) techniques and artificial neural networks (ANN) are recommended to mitigate these challenges19.

By leveraging a comprehensive database of rock properties, it becomes feasible to estimate the CAI of various rocks with greater precision. In a study by Tripathy et al., the authors investigated the estimation of the saturation index and penetration rate in specific Indian rock formations utilizing artificial neural networks. To determine the CAI, the study incorporated various parameters, including the Brazilian tensile strength, the uniaxial compressive strength, Young’s modulus, and the longitudinal wave velocity. The results indicated that the application of artificial neural networks yielded an R² of 0.97 for the estimates. In contrast, a multivariate linear regression approach, which relied on the same four parameters, tensile strength, fracture toughness, longitudinal wave velocity, and the point load index, resulted in a significantly lower coefficient of determination of 0.6320. Perez et al. investigated the use of acoustic emission (AE) as a tool condition monitoring technique to predict the Cerchar Abrasivity Index (CAI) of rocks. They presented a new ANN model for predicting the CAI based on rock properties such as tensile and compressive strength, equivalent quartz content, etc., with AE parameters as six inputs, 24 hidden neurons, and one output as the CAI. The coefficient of determination of their model was 0.8721. Sirdesai et al. employed Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS) to develop innovative predictive and correlation models. The physico-mechanical properties, including the Brazilian tensile strength (BTS), porosity, uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), and dry density, were utilized as input parameters for the prediction of the CAI22. Garzon-Roca et al. used the ANN technique to evaluate the andesitic rocks’ CAI based on petrographical properties, their dataset number was 73 and their model’s R2 was 0.9723. Kwak et al. conducted a study in which they predicted CAI, using various parameters such as the BTS, UCS, EQC%, and brittleness indexes in the forms of (, and ). They employed machine learning-based regression techniques, specifically support vector regression (SVR), regression tree regression (RFR), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN). Their findings indicated that the KNN model demonstrated superior performance, achieving a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.91924. Ghadernejad and Esmaeili presented new models based on a Random Forest (RF) regressor and utilizing hyperspectral imaging information for forecasting rocks’ Abrasivity and hardness; their models’ R2 were in the range 0.74 to 0.7925.

Research utilizing machine learning techniques for estimating rocks’ CAIs remains limited. Conversely, there exists a considerable body of work that applies the CAI in conjunction with machine learning to assess and predict the Cutter Life Index (CLI) of tunnel boring machines (TBMs). Notably, some studies exemplify this intersection of methodologies26–32. Therefore, this study systematically addresses existing methodological gaps by establishing a unified framework for CAI prediction using hybrid algorithms. Additionally, sophisticated feature importance analyses are conducted to identify the key rock parameters that significantly impact prediction accuracy.

Methodology

Figure 1 shows a schematic representation of the systematics used in this work to predict the Cerchar Abrasiveness Index (CAI). The methodology includes several major steps, starting with data collection and preprocessing. The dataset includes 163 samples of different types of rocks and four main input parameters: the Indirect tensile strength (BTS), equivalent quartz content (EQC), unconfined compressive strength (UCS), and brittleness index (BI). Before model development, the dataset undergoes comprehensive preprocessing. The data is then systematically divided into training and testing sets using an 80/20 split ratio, where 80% of the samples (approximately 130 samples) are allocated for training and hyperparameter optimization, while the remaining 20% are reserved for final model evaluation to ensure unbiased performance assessment. Before feature separation, they are normalized using standard Scaler from Scikit-Learn library33, which transforms all input features to a [0,1] range to ensure equal contribution from all features and prevent scale-dependent bias during model training.

Framework of the learning-based part of the study.

After the data preparation step, the method splits into two parallel streams implementing machine learning. The first stream is based on three unique base algorithms: XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. Previous studies have shown the superiority of GBDT models in comparison with other supervised ML models34. The second stream runs concurrently while using three metaheuristic optimization algorithms: the Arithmetic Optimization Algorithm (AOA), Reptile Search Optimization (RSO), and Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO). These algorithms are mathematically-based and nature-inspired ones that have been developed recently. The integration of these optimization techniques follows a systematic approach where each optimization algorithm is specifically designed to explore the hyperparameter space of each base model. The AOA employs mathematical operators to search through hyperparameter configurations, RSO mimics the hunting behaviors of crocodiles for efficient parameter exploration, and HHO utilizes the cooperative behavior and chasing strategies of Harris hawks for optimization. Each optimization algorithm operates with a population size of 50, 100, and 150 individuals across 1000 iterations, using cross-validation MAE as the fitness function (or called the Objective function) to guide the search process. Nine enhancement models are generated as an exhaustive combination of these optimization algorithms and each base algorithm, creating hybrid configurations that leverage both the predictive power of GBDT models and the optimization capabilities of metaheuristic algorithms.

The performance of the hybrid models is thoroughly evaluated using various metrics such as Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Variance Accounted For (VAF), and coefficient of determination (R²), among others. The evaluation framework employs the reserved test set for final performance assessment, ensuring that model comparison is conducted on completely unseen data to provide reliable estimates of generalization capability. In addition, it includes various feature analysis tools such as SHAP values for feature importance, a learning curve to overview whether the model converges, and residuals to check how good the prediction is for different values. Statistical significance testing is performed to ensure reliable comparisons between different hybrid model configurations. Analyses are thus undertaken across multiple dimensions, allowing for the robust comparison of the predictive and generalization properties of the hybrid models. Finally, a real-world tunneling project’s database is also evaluated using the developed ML models to determine their applicability to real-world projects.

Learning-based algorithms

Extreme gradient boosting

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) creates a new tree on an iterative basis to capture the residuals from the previous tree, leading to improved accuracy with an increasing number of iterations35,36. To mitigate overfitting, regularization terms are incorporated into XGBoost’s objective function. Additionally, XGBoost improves its target function using a second-order Taylor expansion, resulting in a more precise loss function36,37. XGBoost distinguishes itself in the optimization domain by integrating a regularization mechanism within its objective function to minimize overfitting, alongside employing a second-order Taylor series for defining its loss function38–43.

Random forest

The Random Forest (RF) model is an integrated predictive algorithm based on decision trees and is part of the Bagging algorithm framework44. RF is an ensemble machine learning method that combines the outputs of multiple learners to enhance overall performance45. Once the data subsets are prepared, the algorithm continuously trains decision trees on these portions of the dataset. Each tree is trained independently on different subsets, allowing for the construction of individual trees that follow the same decision tree-building principles while benefiting from parallelization44.

Light gradient boosting machine

Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) is a state-of-the-art algorithm developed to improve Gradient Boosting, especially when dealing with large-scale datasets46. LightGBM also adopts multi-thread parallel histograms to speed up the training process, and stepwise data pre-processing method (gradient-based one-side sampling and exclusive feature bundling) to enhance the computational efficiency. The algorithm discretizes the values of continuous floating-point variables into K integers which are used to build a histogram of K width. While traversing, the discretized values are treated as indices and accumulated in the histogram to find an optimal tree split point47.

Meta-heuristic optimization methods

Reptile search optimization (RSO)

The Reptile Search Optimization (RSO), which is a recently devised metaheuristic algorithm, is inspired systematically by the hunting of crocodiles (specifically, surrounding and circular habits). The phase of exploration by the algorithm mimics stalking crocodiles, surrounding their prey for a more well-rounded search territory and a look at where the fields of interest may be. During its exploitation phase, it mimics the cooperative hunting techniques of crocodiles, directing the search towards specific areas and complex solutions48,49.

Arithmetic optimization algorithm (AOA)

The Archimedean Optimization Algorithm (AOA), which was derived mainly from the basic principle of physics, is a powerful addition to the available metaheuristic approaches50,51. This specific algorithm is a population-based algorithm, permitting many candidate solutions to process evolutionarily toward optimization objectives52. It starts by initializing a diverse population of solutions where each solution is represented by three relevant physical parameters: acceleration, density, and volume, which are all assigned randomly. The algorithm then undergoes several iterative cycles until the termination condition is satisfied, after an initial evaluation of fitness. In each of these iterations, the density [D] and volume [V] parameters are successively modified, and the acceleration vectors [A]are computed based on the interactions taking place in the solution space among the particles (the measuring [m] particles). These updates allow pieces of the solution to be reshuffled throughout the optimization landscape. The AOA design incorporates cutting-edge exploration-exploitation balancing mechanisms that position it uniquely in its ability to apply to highly complex optimization problems. This holistic strategy has shown great versatility in different branches of engineering, making the AOA a powerful optimization technique50,53.

Harris hawks optimization (HHO)

Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) is a metaheuristic algorithm inspired by the cooperative hunting strategies of Harris Hawks. It is recognized for its effectiveness in navigating complex optimization challenges and excels at avoiding local optima, making it applicable in various fields such as engineering, machine learning, and resource allocation54. The algorithm’s adaptive characteristics enable it to efficiently manage high-dimensional spaces, continuously exploring new areas while capitalizing on previously identified good solutions55. This approach is particularly useful for optimizing critical parameters, such as learning rates or batch sizes in deep learning models4,56.

Materials and database description

One of the distinguishing characteristics of the present study is its attempt to encompass a broad spectrum of rock types, specifically igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic, rather than focusing solely on statistical models limited to a single rock type. In this research, a total of 163 data points on the Brazilian Tensile Strength (BTS), uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), brittleness index (BI), the CERCHAR Abrasivity Index (CAI), and the equivalent quartz content percentage (EQC%) across various rock types have been compiled from numerous scholarly articles. The Brittleness Index (BI) can be defined through various parameters and classified according to mechanical elasticity, mineralogical characteristics, strain-based, or energy-based criteria57. In this study, a simplified version of the BI is employed based on the methodology established by Hucka et al. (UCS/BTS)58. Also, the EQC can be calculated as , where is minerals’ percentages, and is Rosiwal abrasivity index which can be defined as Mohs hardness (M) as 10.

The utilization of UCS, BTS, EQC, and BI as input parameters for predicting abrasive indexes such as CAI is substantiated not only by statistical performance metrics but also by their physical significance concerning rock-tool interactions. The UCS quantifies the compressive resistance of the rock, which fundamentally governs the stress levels required for tool penetration59,60. An increase in UCS typically correlates with elevated cutting forces and accelerated wear on drilling or cutting tools59,61. The BTS serves to reflect the tensile failure characteristics of the rocks, which are critical as tensile microcracking predominantly influences the chipping and spalling phenomena occurring around the tool-rock contact zones62, thereby affecting the rate of abrasive particle generation. The EQC provides an understanding of the rock’s intrinsic fracture toughness and the ease of crack propagation across grain boundaries63. Rocks characterized by low EQC are inclined to produce finer debris, which can exacerbate abrasive wear64. Additionally, BI captures the quartz content intrinsic to the rock, which is directly associated with mineral hardness and scratching propensity; an elevated BI signifies a higher proportion of hard, angular mineral grains that intensify mechanical wear on tool surfaces65. Collectively, these parameters establish a mechanistic foundation for predicting CAI: the UCS and BTS elucidate macroscopic strength-controlled failure modes43,66, the EQC pertains to microstructural fracture processes, and BI incorporates considerations of mineralogical hardness and abrasivity67. Therefore, the incorporation of these parameters as predictors ensures that the model is firmly anchored in the principles of fracture mechanics and microstructure-informed reasoning, rather than relying solely on data-driven feature selection.

Cross-validation using a real-world database

A total of 17 hard rock TBM projects were collected for the cross-validation stage. These include four tunnels of Ghomroud, Karaj, Zagros, and Golab in Iran; Milyang tunnel in South Korea; Queens tunnel in New York; Manapouri tunnel in New Zealand; three tunnels of Maen, Pieve, and Varzo in Italy; South Manhattan tunnel in Manhattan; two tubes of KCRC tunnels in Hong Kong; two tunnels of Frasnadello and Antea in Italy both for pilot and main tunnels75. All of these projects utilized open or double shield hard rock TBM in hard rock masses. Machine diameter for these projects ranged from 2.6 m to 11.8 m. The format and extent of machine and geological data collection varied for each project75.

To address the practical validation concerns and demonstrate the engineering applicability of the developed hybrid models, an independent validation dataset was utilized to assess model performance against real-world operational scenarios. Figure 21 shows a comparison of the three base algorithms (XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest) and their optimization variants with all of the CAI values that are obtained through tunneling operations. The R² values for the XGBoost variants were between 0.8215 and 0.8308, with AOA-XGBoost having the highest correlation coefficient of 0.8308. This suggests that the model was very close to the actual field measurements in this important operational range. The LightGBM implementations additionally performed well, with R² values between 0.8206 and 0.841. The Random Forest variants also worked well, with coefficients between 0.8382 and 0.8482. The validation analysis shows that the biggest prediction errors happen when the Cerchar value is high (CAI > 4.0). This indicates that the rocks are very abrasive, like highly quartzitic formations or heavily weathered metamorphic rocks. This pattern of errors is in line with the difficulties of defining extreme abrasivity conditions, where small changes in mineral composition and microstructural features can cause big changes in how tools wear. The clustering of data points around the 1:1 line for lower CAI values shows that the models are useful for regular engineering tasks, where accurate prediction of moderate abrasivity conditions is important for choosing a TBM, optimizing cutter design, and planning operational parameters.

Conclusion

This research, incorporating sophisticated machine learning algorithms and metaheuristic optimization techniques, is aimed at predicting the Cerchar Abrasiveness Index (CAI). A total of 163 samples (from different rock types: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic) data were used in the research, including four pivotal parameters: Brazilian tensile strength (BTS), uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), equivalent quartz content (EQC), and brittleness index (BI).

The systematic evaluation of nine hybrid models, created through the combination of three base algorithms (XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest) with three metaheuristic optimization approaches (AOA, RSO, and HHO), revealed several significant findings. AOA-LightGBM demonstrated the best performance of the AOA-optimized implementations, with a testing phase score of 39, followed by AOA-XGBoost with 36. Testing and training these models showed that they performed well, with R2 values over 0.97. The thorough evaluation of 17 actual TBM projects verified the engineering relevance of these models, with AOA-XGBoost exhibiting the highest correlation coefficient of 0.8308 in comparison to field measurements, thus proving its superiority as the most dependable predictor for practical tunneling applications. The statistical significance analysis through Friedman and Nemenyi tests confirmed that AOA-optimized variants significantly outperformed their counterparts, with effect sizes ranging from small to medium (Cohen’s d between 0.359 and 0.467), providing robust evidence for model selection in engineering practice.

Analysis of feature importance through the SHAP values showed distinct patterns of how various parameters impacted different model configurations. The EQC was discovered to be the most important predictor in XGBoost models, while UCS was by far the most influential in the LightGBM and Random Forest models. The variance in feature importance indicates these complex interactions between the rocks’ properties and their ablative properties. Analysis of the relative absolute error (RAE) confirmed these results. The error analysis showed that the greatest number of prediction errors occurs when the Cerchar value is large (CAI > 4.0). This indicates that the models work most effectively within the operational range that is most common in regular tunneling projects.

This study shows that the suggested hybrid models, particularly those that utilize AOA, are very promising and useful for dealing with different types of rocks and their properties. This work has important practical engineering usages since accurate CAI prediction makes it possible to design better cutters and estimate out how much wear they will get on tunneling projects. However, several limitations must be acknowledged to ensure appropriate application of these models. The dataset’s range distribution may limit generalizability to the lower CAI values. Indeed, the developed model may face difficulties in extreme conditions.

The computational complexity associated with metaheuristic optimization is a trade-off between how well the model works and how easy it is to put into practice. XGBoost models, due to their complexity, took a longer time to solve. On the other hand, with the same search space, HHO algorithms took the longest time to find the optimum answer. Considering this computational cost, AOA-LightGBM was the most optimal answer.

Although BI was shown to have a comparatively minor effect on predictive performance, it was retained in the model because of its mechanistic importance in describing brittleness-driven rock-tool interactions. Future studies may consider simplified models excluding BI when computational efficiency is prioritized, while still recognizing its conceptual relevance. This advancement has the potential to reduce the time and costs associated with experimental testing. Future research should concentrate on expanding the dataset to encompass a variety of geological formations from distinct geographical regions, exploring the integration of supplementary rock parameters, and formulating streamlined model variants that reconcile computational efficiency with predictive precision. Combining TBM operational parameters with real-time monitoring data could make the model even more useful for adaptive control systems used in tunneling operations.
