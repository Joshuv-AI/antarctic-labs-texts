The gut-heart dialogue: an epigenetic perspective on myocardial infarction
Authors: Jie Song, Shuai Tang, Yan Guo, Chenyi Hong, Tongtong Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myocardial infarction (MI) remains a leading cause of global mortality, with adverse cardiac remodeling and heart failure presenting persistent metabolic and clinical challenges. While traditional pharmacotherapies have improved outcomes, there is an urgent need to elucidate how lifestyle and nutritional factors influence disease progression. Emerging evidence underscores the critical interplay between natural bioactive compounds (e.g., dietary fibers, polyphenols), the gut microbiota, and host metabolic regulation. This review systematically clarifies the “gut microbiota-epigenetic axis” as a pivotal mechanism linking nature-derived bioactives to cardiac repair. We delineate how the gut microbiota transforms specific bioactives into functional metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) from fibers and urolithin A from ellagitannins, which act as potent epigenetic modulators. These microbial metabolites remodel the epigenetic landscape of the host heart via histone modification and DNA methylation, thereby regulating gene networks governing inflammation, energy metabolism, and fibrosis. Synthesizing evidence from in vivo animal studies and clinical observations, we explore therapeutic strategies centered on natural bioactives, including high-fiber dietary interventions and polyphenol supplementation, alongside probiotics and postbiotics. By integrating the metabolic potential of the gut microbiome with epigenetic regulation, this review offers novel insights into how natural bioactive compounds can be leveraged for precision prevention and therapeutic strategies in myocardial infarction.

Introduction

MI, representing the most critical phenotype of ischemic heart disease, is pathologically characterized by irreversible cardiomyocyte necrosis resulting from coronary artery occlusion and remains a leading cause of global mortality and morbidity1,2. Although reperfusion strategies, typified by percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), alongside modern pharmacotherapies, have significantly improved acute survival rates, patients post-MI continue to face severe long-term challenges3,4. Adverse cardiac remodeling is prevalent among survivors. This pathological process encompasses myocardial fibrosis, ventricular dilation, and functional decline. Ultimately, it drives the progression of a substantial proportion of patients to heart failure (HF), thereby sustaining high rates of rehospitalization and mortality5,6. This persistent clinical dilemma underscores the limitations of current therapeutic paradigms and highlights our incomplete understanding of the complex regulatory networks governing cardiac injury and repair following MI, necessitating the urgent elucidation of novel pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic targets.

In recent years, an emerging field of research has focused on the vast microbial community residing within the human host: the gut microbiota. With the introduction of the “heart-gut axis” concept, the intimate association between intestinal health and the cardiovascular system has gained widespread recognition7. Substantial evidence indicates that gut dysbiosis acts as a critical driver in the onset and progression of MI8,9. In the setting of MI, intestinal barrier function is compromised, facilitating the translocation of microbiota and their metabolites—such as the pro-atherosclerotic Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS)—into the systemic circulation. This process triggers and exacerbates systemic inflammatory responses, thereby aggravating myocardial injury10. Concurrently, a reduction in the abundance of commensal bacteria capable of producing beneficial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), diminishes their protective effects on the host, effectively transforming the gut microbiota from a symbiotic partner into a potent risk modulator for cardiovascular disease11–13.

Concurrently, epigenetics serves as a critical molecular bridge for elucidating how environmental factors influence disease progression. Epigenetics is defined as the modulation of gene expression without alterations to the underlying DNA sequence, mediated through reversible mechanisms such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and regulation by non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs)14. Epigenetic regulation plays a central role in the pathophysiology of MI15. The stressful microenvironment characterized by myocardial ischemia and hypoxia induces extensive and dynamic epigenetic reprogramming within cardiac tissue; these modifications precisely orchestrate gene networks associated with inflammatory responses, apoptosis, energy metabolism, and fibrosis, thereby ultimately dictating the outcomes of cardiac repair and remodeling following infarction16–18. Consequently, targeting specific epigenetic-modifying enzymes or identifying critical epigenetic markers has emerged as a highly promising avenue for novel cardiovascular therapeutics.

Increasingly distinct evidence suggests that gut microbiota and epigenetics do not function as isolated regulatory systems but rather constitute an integrated, reciprocal regulatory network: the “gut microbiota-epigenetics-heart axis.” Acting as a pivotal mediator between environmental factors and the host genetic background, the gut microbiota produces metabolites that serve directly as substrates or regulators to remodel the epigenetic landscape of host cardiomyocytes. The identification of this axis provides a novel mechanistic explanation for the fundamental paradigm that MI is not merely a localized ischemic event but a disease subject to systemic regulation. Consequently, this review aims to systematically elucidate how the gut microbiota remotely modulates the pathological progression of MI via epigenetic mechanisms and to synthesize potential intervention strategies targeting this critical axis, to provide a theoretical foundation for the development of more precise and effective approaches for the prevention and treatment of MI.

Gut microbiota in healthy individuals and patients with MI

The human gut microbiome constitutes an intricate ecosystem whose physiological functions are increasingly recognized as pivotal in cardiovascular health. In healthy individuals, a stable commensal community maintains homeostasis through the production of protective metabolites—such as antioxidants and SCFAs—that mitigate intestinal inflammation and optimize lipid metabolism12,19. Conversely, this equilibrium is profoundly disrupted following myocardial infarction. Gut microbiota in healthy individuals

The gut microbiota constitutes a vast and intricate ecosystem, harboring ~1013–1014 microorganisms22, and is consequently referred to as the human second genome. Functioning as a highly sophisticated anaerobic bioreactor, the healthy gut microbiota exists in symbiosis with the host23, conferring additional energy harvesting and metabolic capabilities. Following extensive co-evolution, broad interactions have been established between gut microbes and the host24, rendering this microbial community an integral component of human physiology.

The gut microbiota fulfills four cardinal functions essential for maintaining host health: metabolism, immune protection, structural maintenance, and neural regulation25. Metabolically, the microbiota facilitates the catabolism of complex carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids via the production of digestive enzymes, while also synthesizing vitamin K and B-complex vitamins26,27. Regarding immune protection, early microbial colonization is pivotal for immune system maturation; these microbes synergize with gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) to constitute the primary line of intestinal immunity, inhibiting pathogen colonization through resource competition28,29. SCFAs derived from microbial metabolism—principally acetate, propionate, and butyrate—not only serve as a primary energy source for intestinal epithelial cells but also exert anti-inflammatory effects and maintain intestinal barrier integrity13,30. Specific strains, such as certain Lactobacillus and Escherichia coli, have been shown to enhance the expression of tight junctions. Similarly, Akkermansia muciniphila reinforces barrier function through the degradation of mucin proteins31–33. In the domain of neural regulation, the gut and brain engage in bidirectional communication via the gut-brain axis34; SCFAs are capable of traversing the blood-brain barrier to modulate central nervous system (CNS) activity, while dysregulation of microbiota-derived neurotransmitters, such as gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and dopamine, is implicated in CNS disorders, including schizophrenia35. The composition and function of the microbiota are susceptible to environmental factors, such as diet and antibiotics, acting as critical mediators of host defense strategies36.

The adult gastrointestinal microbiome encompasses all three domains of life: bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes. The intestinal microenvironment primarily favors the proliferation of seven dominant phyla—Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Fusobacteria, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Cyanobacteria—with Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes constituting over 90% of the total microbiota25. Although the composition of core phyla remains relatively conserved37, the proportions and presence of specific species exhibit significant inter-individual variability and fluctuate across developmental stages, regulated by multifactorial influences such as age, diet, environment, health status, and medication use. Consequently, alterations in the ratio of a single phylum do not constitute a reliable biomarker for disease. However, despite this diversity, certain core species remain ubiquitous across populations, suggesting they possess critical, shared functions essential for human health38–41. A healthy gut microbiota is characterized by a degree of stability and resilience. In adulthood, assuming stability in diet, antibiotic usage, and physiological state, microbial composition and function can be maintained long-term42. In the face of short-term perturbations, such as infection or antibiotic therapy, the microbiota is capable of gradually restoring homeostasis by preserving key species43,44. Conversely, chronic exposure to stressors—including poor diet, food additives, environmental pollutants, or frequent antibiotic use—can induce persistent dysbiosis, promoting the proliferation of potentially pathogenic bacteria and exerting deleterious effects on host health38,45,46. Notably, stability is not synonymous with health; certain highly stable yet unhealthy microbial profiles may be associated with chronic conditions such as obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes mellitus47,48.

High microbial diversity is generally regarded as a hallmark of intestinal health, as it confers enhanced stability and resilience to the microbiota41. Increased diversity facilitates improved digestion and absorption, metabolite production, and immune modulation, while also bolstering resistance to and recovery from perturbations. Conversely, reduced diversity is associated with an elevated risk of inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancers49. Healthy dietary habits and physical activity promote diversity; for instance, athletes typically exhibit greater gut microbial diversity50.

Compared to taxonomic composition, functional integrity constitutes a more critical metric for the assessment of intestinal health. Given the existence of functional redundancy within the microbiota, metabolic pathways exhibit a high degree of commonality across individuals despite substantial diversity in species composition51,52. Consequently, functional diversity provides a more comprehensive reflection of gut health status than compositional diversity. Metabolism represents a core microbial function; metabolites such as SCFAs, bile acids, and tryptophan derivatives are frequently utilized as biomarkers for gut health assessment, with particular emphasis on SCFAs30,53,54. However, owing to the complexity of dietary influences, inter-individual variability, and host-microbe interactions, the interpretation of these metabolic profiles warrants caution. Beyond metabolites, parameters including gas production, pH levels, and fecal inflammatory markers also contribute to defining the state of a healthy microbiota55,56.

The relationship among the host, gut microbiota, and health is profound and complex. Although a definitive consensus on the definition of a “healthy gut microbiota” remains elusive, current knowledge has provided a critical foundation for understanding the association between dysbiosis and disease.

Gut microbiota in patients with MI

Pre-existing dysbiosis: a risk factor for atherosclerosis and thrombosis

Atherosclerosis (AS) constitutes the primary etiology of MI, and gut dysbiosis is implicated in its initiation and progression57–60. The gut microbiome in patients with AS is often characterized by a significant depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria compared to healthy adults61. Butyrate is essential for stabilizing plaques and inhibiting vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation62. Furthermore, specific microbial metabolites directly contribute to thrombotic risk. Research has demonstrated that the transplantation of gut microbiota producing phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln) into germ-free mice enhances platelet function and promotes arterial thrombogenesis63. Additionally, the microbial conversion of dietary choline to TMAO has been identified as an independent risk factor that accelerates atherosclerosis and predicts major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)64. These findings suggest that a “pro-thrombotic” and “pro-inflammatory” microbial profile exists long before the acute infarction event.

The acute MI phase: barrier disruption and systemic inflammation

The onset of AMI precipitates a rapid and dramatic shift in the gut ecosystem. The physiological stress of AMI compromises gut barrier integrity, increasing permeability (“leaky gut”). This facilitates the translocation of bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and other metabolites into the systemic circulation, triggering severe inflammatory responses that exacerbate myocardial injury19,65. Clinically, the microbiota of patients with AMI exhibits distinct structural alterations. Dong et al. identified specific genera significantly enriched in AMI patients, noting that species such as Alistipes shahii and Streptococcus salivarius demonstrated high predictive value for AMI diagnosis66. Similarly, Liu et al. revealed an enrichment of Streptococcus spp. and Klebsiella pneumoniae in AMI patients, contrasting with a depletion of the beneficial Roseburia hominis67. Li et al. further reported that gut colonization by oral streptococci could exacerbate infarct size in mice, highlighting a direct pathogenic role for specific taxa during the acute phase68. Functionally, this phase is often marked by a suppression of SCFA-producing genera (e.g., Selenomonadales), potentially depriving the heart of protective metabolites during a critical window of injury69.

The chronic phase: post-MI remodeling and heart failure

Following the acute event, the gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in determining the trajectory of cardiac repair and ventricular remodeling. Persistent dysbiosis can drive adverse outcomes, including fibrosis and HF. Tang et al. demonstrated that the depletion of gut microbiota via antibiotics in mice resulted in increased post-MI mortality, associated with significantly reduced levels of SCFAs70. In the chronic phase, the loss of microbial diversity often persists. Han et al. observed decreased richness in patients post-MI, characterized by a reduction in beneficial genera such as Tyzzerella 3 and Dialister, which correlated with declining cardiac function and chronic inflammation71. Conversely, therapeutic interventions during this phase show promise; for instance, Chen et al. reported that restoring the abundance of butyrate- and propionate-producing bacteria attenuated myocardial fibrosis and improved cardiac structure72. This indicates that the long-term prognosis of MI is intimately linked to the re-establishment of a symbiotic “gut–heart” axis.

Gut microbiota-regulated epigenetics impacts the progression and prognosis of myocardial infarction

The characteristic alterations of the gut microbiota in patients with MI and their roles in the onset and progression of the disease are becoming increasingly elucidated. Acting as a dynamic environmental factor influenced by host genetics and long-term lifestyle, the gut microbiota significantly impacts the severity and clinical outcomes of MI. DNA methylation

DNA methylation constitutes a critical component of the epigenetic landscape in MI. This process involves the pre-transcriptional addition of a methyl group to the cytosine base at CpG dinucleotide sites, significantly influencing gene transcriptional activity73. DNA methylation is intimately associated with a spectrum of cardiovascular diseases, including MI74 (Table 1).

As a pivotal epigenetic modification mechanism, DNA methylation exerts crucial regulatory functions in the pathophysiology of MI. Under conditions of acute ischemia and hypoxia, the myocardial genome undergoes extensive remodeling of DNA methylation patterns75. These aberrant modifications deeply engage in the inflammatory cascade, oxidative stress, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and subsequent ventricular remodeling primarily by modulating the transcriptional activity of specific genes76. Specifically, hypomethylation in the promoter regions of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) frequently leads to the overexpression of inflammatory mediators, exacerbating myocardial injury77,78. Conversely, protective genes involved in mitochondrial function or angiogenesis may be transcriptionally suppressed due to hypermethylation, thereby attenuating the heart’s self-repair capacity79,80. As a key regulator of host epigenetics, the gut microbiota profoundly influences the pathophysiology of MI through the precise modulation of DNA methylation and modifications, emerging as a critical regulatory hub in this field. The mechanisms underlying these regulatory networks fundamentally revolve around two major axes: the availability of methyl donors and the direct interference with epigenetic enzyme activity. Classic examples include the imbalance in the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) ratio81,82: for instance, a reduction in intestinal Bifidobacterium can lead to folate and vitamin B12 deficiency, ultimately triggering global hypomethylation and the reactivation of retrotransposons (e.g., LINE-1)83. Conversely, microbial metabolites such as TMAO can induce site-specific hypermethylation by upregulating DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), thereby silencing protective genes like Sirt1, Nrf2, and Bcl2, and compromising the heart’s antioxidant and anti-apoptotic defenses84,85. Furthermore, the accumulation of bacteria-derived metabolites can competitively inhibit TET dioxygenases, thereby impeding the process of active DNA demethylation86,87.

Beyond these established pathways, recent investigations have unveiled more sophisticated and dynamic layers of epigenetic regulation. Foremost is the generation of advanced demethylation intermediates—5-formylcytosine (5-fC) and 5-carboxylcytosine (5-caC)—which function not merely as transient states but as novel, functionally active epigenetic markers88,89. Alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG), produced by the gut microbiota, serves as an essential cofactor for TET enzymes in the oxidation of 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) to these variants90. 5-fC is capable of specifically recruiting DNA repair proteins89. Consequently, it is theoretically plausible that α-KG deficiency resulting from dysbiosis could impair this repair pathway, potentially exacerbating cardiomyocyte death. However, this specific mechanistic link remains to be experimentally validated in the context of MI. This epigenetic regulation extends further to the mitochondrial genome. Gut-derived SCFAs can be transported into mitochondria and modulate the subcellular localization of the mitochondria-specific DNMT1 (mtDNMT1). This mechanism precisely regulates the methylation levels of the mitochondrial DNA D-loop region91,92, thereby influencing mitochondrial biogenesis and ultimately determining the heart’s capacity to effectively restore energy supply post-infarction. Histone modifications

Histone modifications, through the dynamic remodeling of chromatin structure, exert multidimensional regulatory effects across the various stages of acute injury, metabolic reprogramming, and chronic ventricular remodeling in MI, serving as critical nodes determining cardiomyocyte fate and tissue repair93–95.

Histone modifications constitute the central hub of the “Metabolism-Epigenetics Coupling” between the gut microbiota and the host, wherein microbial metabolites serve directly as substrates for chromatin modification or regulators of enzyme activity. Among these modifications, histone acetylation has been most extensively characterized30,96. SCFAs—primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate—play a pivotal epigenetic regulatory role97. Following MI, myocardial ischemia and hypoxia trigger an aberrant elevation in histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity, leading to the overexpression of pro-inflammatory genes and increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, thereby exacerbating myocardial injury and remodeling98,99. Crucially, SCFAs, particularly butyrate, function as direct HDAC inhibitors (HDACi). By suppressing HDAC activity, they promote chromatin relaxation, which subsequently upregulates anti-inflammatory gene expression, inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory factors, and suppresses excessive cardiomyocyte apoptosis and myocardial fibrosis100.

Furthermore, SCFAs modulate macrophage polarization via histone acetylation regulation, promoting the activation of the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This further attenuates post-MI myocardial inflammation and sympathetic nerve remodeling, reducing the risk of malignant arrhythmias97,100–102.

Beyond classical acetylation, a diverse family of “acylation modifications,” driven by microbial metabolism, is emerging as a frontier field. For instance, the accumulation of lactate, resulting from the confluence of hypoxia and dysbiosis, can catalyze the generation of histone lactylation (H3K18la) via p300. This modification plays a dual role in regulating angiogenesis (Vegfa) and tissue fibrosis (Tgf-β), potentially controlling the “phenotypic switch” governing the transformation of cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts103. Furthermore, catabolism involving lysine and fatty acid β-oxidation generates crotonyl-CoA, which drives crotonylation (Kcr)—a transcriptional activation modification more potent than acetylation104,105. Systemic Kcr modification induced by sodium crotonate (NaCr), as well as Kcr-mimetic mutants of isocitrate dehydrogenase 3a (IDH3a), have been shown to reduce cardiomyocyte death by inhibiting BCL2 Interacting Protein 3 (BNIP3)-mediated mitophagy, thereby preserving cardiac function post-MI106. However, crotonylation modifications (including histone H3K18cr, H2BK12cr, and non-histone sites such as NAE1 K238cr) can also promote pathological cardiac hypertrophy through mechanisms involving the regulation of Nuclear Factor Of Activated T Cells 3 (NFATc3)-mediated hypertrophic gene expression and gelsolin (GSN) protein stability, whereas Enoyl-CoA Hydratase, Short Chain 1 (ECHS1) exerts anti-hypertrophic effects by inhibiting histone crotonylation107. This dichotomy suggests that histone crotonylation plays a dual role at different stages post-MI, indicating that specific crotonylation sites may represent potential therapeutic targets for cardiac hypertrophy. Similarly, ketone bodies (e.g., β-hydroxybutyrate) produced by the liver under microbial regulation serve not only as energy substrates but also remodel the epigenetic landscape of pro-angiogenic genes by increasing H3K9bhb levels and chromatin accessibility, and upregulating Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) and associated genes, thereby conferring cardioprotection108.

Beyond serving as acyl donors, the influence of the microbiota extends to a more diverse array of chemical modifications intricately linked to MI pathology. For instance, alterations in gut microbiota composition induced by a high-sugar diet can lead to excessive O-GlcNAc glycosylation via the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway; this modification on histone H2B has been shown to impair the transcription of calcium handling genes Sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2a (Serca2a) in diabetic cardiomyopathy, thereby exacerbating heart failure109–111. Another novel mechanism is citrullination: LPS release resulting from compromised gut barrier function (“Leaky Gut”) activates the Peptidyl Arginine Deiminase 4 (PADI4) enzyme in neutrophils, catalyzing histone H3 citrullination (H3Cit). This leads to chromatin decondensation and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which have been confirmed as key drivers of microvascular obstruction and the “no-reflow” phenomenon in the infarct zone112,113. Furthermore, a complex and precise regulatory network is constituted by additional modifications, including gut-derived dopamine-driven dopaminylation (H3Q5dop), SUMOylation regulated by SCFAs and branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs) in a pH-dependent manner, and methylation modulated by polyamines. Emerging evidence also points to dopamine-driven H3Q5dop; while this mechanism has been characterized in neuronal contexts, its potential role in regulating cardiomyocyte gene expression via gut-derived dopamine represents a compelling hypothesis that warrants further investigation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the immense potential of the gut microbiota to profoundly influence MI outcomes by remodeling the chromatin language114–116. ncRNAs

Beyond the chemical modification of chromatin, the gut microbiota also exerts post-transcriptional control through a diverse network of non-coding RNAs. Although over 90% of the human genome does not encode proteins, it exhibits high transcriptional activity. The transcripts generated from these regions, known as ncRNAs, represent a class of RNA molecules with critical regulatory functions, primarily encompassing microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs)117.

The gut microbiota orchestrates a cross-kingdom regulation of the pathophysiological processes of MI by modulating a vast and intricate network of ncRNAs118,119. Among the diverse ncRNAs, miRNAs and lncRNAs have been the most extensively characterized, collectively functioning as regulators of inflammatory and injury responses120,121. For instance, LPS derived from the microbiota can induce the expression of the pro-inflammatory miR-155 and the pro-fibrotic miR-21 via the TLR4-NF-κB pathway122,123. Conversely, indole derivatives produced from microbial tryptophan metabolism can inhibit miR-196 by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), thereby upregulating the cardioprotective protein HO-1124–126. Similarly, the pro-atherosclerotic metabolite TMAO has been shown to upregulate the lncRNA Malat1, which activates the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome by sponging miR-22, leading to endothelial cell pyroptosis127,128; whereas beneficial butyrate exerts anti-pyroptotic effects by downregulating lncRNAs such as KCNQ1OT1128.

Recent investigations have further elucidated the critical roles of novel ncRNAs within this regulatory axis, extending their functions beyond traditional “sponging” or transcriptional modulation. For instance, certain circRNAs, induced by dysbiosis, can be translated into functional microproteins; these peptides directly interfere with chromatin remodeling complexes, thereby impeding the transcription of genes essential for cardiac repair129,130. Moreover, the gut microbiota exerts direct control over cellular “life and death” decisions under stress—specifically, protein translation and genomic stability—by influencing two distinct classes of ncRNAs. At the level of protein translation, stress induced by MI activates angiogenin, which cleaves tRNAs into tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs/tiRNAs). These fragments can displace mRNAs from ribosomes, inducing a global arrest of protein translation. tiRNAs exert protective effects in the pathological progression of MI through core mechanisms such as targeting pro-MI molecules, inhibiting cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and regulating vascular function131. The potential for MI-induced gut dysbiosis to trigger tRF/tiRNA activation warrants further investigation. A more sophisticated layer of regulation involves small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs). By guiding the methylation of ribosomal RNA (rRNA), snoRNAs determine ribosomal translation fidelity and preference for specific mRNAs, thereby influencing the course of MI132–134. Notably, studies in avian models have shown that snoRNA expression can be modulated by the microbiota144; however, whether this regulatory axis is conserved in human cardiac pathophysiology remains to be determined135. Regarding genomic stability, dysbiosis can disrupt the expression profile of PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), releasing their suppression of retrotransposons86,136, which may precipitate genomic instability in cardiomyocytes. The gut microbiota thus functions not merely as a distal metabolic hub but, through a multi-layered and functionally diverse ncRNA network, profoundly participates in and shapes the ultimate fate of essential myocardial processes post-infarction, ranging from energy metabolism and protein synthesis to genomic integrity. Epigenetic crosstalk: an integrated regulatory network

It is crucial to recognize that DNA methylation, histone modifications, and ncRNA regulation do not operate in isolation but rather function as a highly integrated and reciprocal network. Gut microbiota-derived metabolites often trigger a cascade of events across these layers. For instance, SCFAs can simultaneously inhibit HDACs to increase histone acetylation while also modulating the intracellular pool of methyl donors, thereby influencing DNA methylation patterns. Furthermore, ncRNAs can serve as scaffolds to recruit chromatin-modifying complexes to specific genomic loci, precisely orchestrating gene expression during cardiac repair. This multi-layered ‘crosstalk’ amplifies the regulatory signals from the gut microbiome, ensuring a robust and fine-tuned response to myocardial stress.

Other modifications

Beyond canonical histone and DNA modifications, the regulatory network of the gut microbiota extends into frontier domains such as RNA chemical modifications and three-dimensional chromatin architecture, mechanisms that collectively dictate the final output of gene expression programs. At the level of epitranscriptomics, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methylation represents an extensively investigated, pivotal regulatory node. Microbial metabolites, such as TMAO or secondary bile acids, can modulate the m6A modification landscape of target gene mRNAs by regulating the activity or stability of methyltransferases like METTL3137–139. The consequences typically involve altered mRNA stability, such as accelerated degradation, thereby suppressing cardioprotective autophagy processes140. Furthermore, the influence of the microbiota encompasses a spectrum of RNA modifications, including 5-methylcytosine (m5C) RNA methylation141,142 and the emerging N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) RNA acetylation143,144.

Another nascent area of investigation elucidates the interplay among dysbiosis, aberrant DNA structural conformations, and cell fate. Severe dysbiosis fosters a pro-inflammatory, hyperosmotic systemic environment, which favors the transition from canonical B-DNA (right-handed helix) to unstable Z-DNA (left-handed helix) conformations145,146. The RNA/DNA editing enzyme ADAR1 is indispensable for binding and stabilizing Z-DNA to avert aberrant immune activation; however, the depletion of microbial metabolites (e.g., polyamines) impairs ADAR1 function147. This impairment allows Z-DNA structures to trigger a potent form of programmed necrosis known as necroptosis148–151.

In summary, spanning from chemical RNA tagging to genomic spatial conformation and dynamic condensation, the gut microbiota exhibits a central regulatory role within the pathophysiology of the heart across multiple frontier dimensions.

Therapeutic intervention strategies targeting the “gut microbiota–epigenetics” axis in myocardial infarction

The pivotal role of the “gut microbiota–epigenetics axis” in the pathophysiology of MI establishes a robust theoretical foundation for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies. Modulating the composition and function of the gut microbiota, or directly mimicking the epigenetic effects of its beneficial metabolites, holds the potential to remodel the host gene expression network, thereby attenuating myocardial injury, inhibiting adverse remodeling, and enhancing clinical prognosis. Pharmacological interventions

This strategy aims to achieve rapid and efficacious cardioprotection by directly intervening in critical gut microbiota-derived metabolite pathways via pharmacological agents. Specifically, given that SCFAs function as natural inhibitors of HDACs (as detailed in Section “Histone Modifications”), the direct administration of pharmacological HDAC inhibitors can simulate the epigenetic effects of butyrate100,154,155. Substantial evidence indicates that HDACi exhibit significant cardioprotective effects in animal models of MI. For instance, broad-spectrum HDACi, such as vorinostat (SAHA) and valproic acid (VPA), are capable of inhibiting myocardial fibrosis, attenuating inflammatory responses, and improving cardiac function. Mechanistically, by inhibiting HDAC activity, these agents augment histone acetylation levels (specifically H3 and H4), thereby activating the transcription of cardioprotective genes, including those regulating anti-apoptotic and antioxidant processes152,155. Furthermore, selective HDAC inhibitors (e.g., those targeting HDAC6 or HDAC9) have demonstrated potential for more precise therapy, potentially minimizing off-target effects156,157. These findings corroborate that pharmacologically mimicking the epigenetic functions of microbiota metabolites represents a viable therapeutic avenue for MI.

Given the deleterious role of TMAO in promoting atherosclerosis and exacerbating post-MI cardiac injury, inhibiting its generation constitutes a critical therapeutic target. Pharmacological interventions primarily focus on inhibiting a key microbial enzyme: TMA lyase. 3,3-dimethyl-1-butanol (DMB), a choline analogue, effectively inhibits the CutC/D enzyme complex, thereby reducing the production of TMA from choline158. In ApoE−/− mouse models, DMB intervention significantly lowered plasma TMAO levels and retarded atherosclerosis159. Furthermore, recent research has highlighted that TMAO production also arises from carnitine and γ -butyrobetaine metabolism, involving pathways such as the gbu gene cluster (including CntA/B and YeaW/X)160,161. This suggests that therapeutic strategies must broaden their scope to target these alternative metabolic pathways for comprehensive TMAO suppression.

Additionally, the utilization of bile acid receptor agonists exemplifies the potential of this strategy. The gut microbiota modifies primary bile acids via enzymes such as bile salt hydrolase (BSH) to generate signaling-active secondary bile acids162. These serve as potent ligands for the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and the G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 5 (TGR5), the activation of which elicits anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulatory effects163. For instance, the FXR agonist obeticholic acid (OCA) has been demonstrated to attenuate systemic inflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, while TGR5 activation similarly suppresses macrophage inflammatory responses164–166. In the cardiovascular domain, modulating bile acid signaling pathways is postulated to confer indirect cardioprotection by improving lipid metabolism and inhibiting inflammation, constituting an integral component of the microbiota-metabolism-epigenetics regulatory network167.

Despite the theoretical appeal, pharmacological interventions face substantial hurdles. First, for HDAC inhibitors, “off-target” effects are a major concern. Pan-HDAC inhibitors lack isoform specificity and may induce cytotoxicity or even cardiotoxicity (e.g., QT prolongation) at high doses, and currently, no HDACi is approved specifically for cardiovascular indications156,168. Second, regarding TMAO inhibition, DMB is primarily effective against choline-induced TMA production but less effective against carnitine-derived TMA pathways. Furthermore, long-term non-lethal inhibition of gut microbial enzymes raises concerns about the potential evolution of resistant strains or the disruption of essential microbial metabolic functions, the long-term systemic consequences of which remain unknown.

Dietary and nutritional interventions

Diet constitutes the most critical environmental determinant shaping the structural and functional composition of the gut microbiota. Adjusting dietary patterns can effectively steer the microbiota toward a beneficial profile, thereby conferring cardioprotection by remodeling the epigenetic landscape of the host myocardium169,170. Fiber-rich diets (prebiotics) serve as primary energy substrates for beneficial gut bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Roseburia). High-fiber intake—characterized by whole grains, legumes, fruits, and vegetables—significantly elevates the concentration of intestinal SCFAs171,172. In animal models of MI, supplementation with prebiotic fibers not only augmented serum and cardiac levels of butyrate but also significantly reduced infarct size and suppressed cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis100. As discussed, gut-derived butyrate functions as a natural HDACi. By preventing histone deacetylation (e.g., H3K9ac)173,174, it maintains chromatin in a relaxed state, thereby specifically enhancing the transcriptional accessibility of anti-inflammatory and antioxidant genes downstream of GPR43/109A173–175.

The cardiovascular benefits of the Mediterranean diet—a paradigm of a healthy diet rich in fiber, unsaturated fatty acids, and polyphenols—are similarly attributed to the positive modulation of the “microbiota-epigenetics axis”176–178. Dietary polyphenols possess potent antioxidant properties and are biotransformed by the gut microbiota into small-molecule phenolic acids with enhanced bioavailability. These metabolites not only modulate microbial composition but also act as “Epi-drugs”179,180. For instance, resveratrol and its microbial metabolites can function as DNMT inhibitors, reversing pathological hypermethylation in the promoter regions of critical cardioprotective genes (e.g., PGC-1α) post-infarction, thereby restoring gene expression. Concurrently, resveratrol activates SIRT1—an NAD+-dependent class III histone deacetylase—which precisely regulates apoptosis and inflammation networks at the post-translational level via the deacetylation of non-histone proteins such as p53 and NF-κB179. Furthermore, urolithin A, generated from the microbial metabolism of ellagitannins, has been confirmed to enhance gene expression related to mitophagy through epigenetic mechanisms181,182.

Alterations in dietary patterns such as caloric restriction (CR) and intermittent fasting (IF) trigger novel epigenetic modifications via metabolic reprogramming. During fasting, adaptive changes in the gut microbiota promote ketogenesis, wherein β-hydroxybutyrate (β-OHB) serves not only as an energy substrate but also as a critical donor for epigenetic modification. β-OHB directly drives a novel histone modification: histone lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb). Research indicates that the enrichment of H3K9bhb at gene promoter regions activates gene transcription more effectively than traditional acetylation183. In the context of MI, this modification specifically upregulates the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and antioxidant enzyme genes. Concurrently, acting as an HDAC inhibitor, β-OHB suppresses the transcription of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby accelerating microvascular angiogenesis and improving cardiac function. Additionally, indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), modulated by intermittent fasting, not only inhibits thrombosis via the Pregnane X receptor (PXR) signaling pathway but is also postulated to further attenuate myocardial reperfusion injury through the regulation of microRNA expression profiles mediated by the AhR184.

The efficacy of dietary interventions is highly variable and individualized (“responder vs. non-responder” phenomenon), largely depending on the host’s baseline microbiome. For example, the conversion of dietary precursors into bioactive metabolites like equol or urolithin A requires the presence of specific microbial species; absent these bacteria, the dietary intervention is ineffective181. Furthermore, achieving therapeutic concentrations of metabolites solely through diet is difficult, and rigorous adherence to strict dietary protocols (like CR or IF) is challenging for patients with MI, particularly those with comorbidities such as diabetes or frailty.

Microecological interventions

Microecological interventions aim to rectify dysbiosis by directly supplementing beneficial microorganisms or their growth substrates, or by globally replacing the microbiota. Probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host when administered in adequate amounts, have demonstrated therapeutic potential in the context of MI, particularly specific strains within the Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium genera185–187. For instance, supplementation with Lactobacillus plantarum in rat models of MI modulated gut dysbiosis, reduced serum levels of LPS and TMAO, suppressed myocardial inflammation and apoptosis, and ultimately improved cardiac function. Lactobacillus species exert cardioprotective effects by activating the Sirt1/Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis during myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury187. Concurrently, certain probiotics are capable of endogenously producing SCFAs, directly contributing to epigenetic regulation. The composite probiotic formulation VSL#3 has also been shown to lower blood pressure and improve endothelial function in animal studies188,189. Furthermore, Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT)—the transfer of fecal microbiota from a healthy donor to a patient’s gastrointestinal tract—serves as a robust intervention designed to fundamentally reconstitute a healthy microecosystem190,70. FMT has yielded encouraging results in MI models; studies indicate that transplanting fecal microbiota from healthy mice to those with MI significantly reduced infarct size, attenuated cardiac fibrosis, and enhanced cardiac function. Mechanistically, FMT successfully reversed MI-induced dysbiosis, significantly elevated SCFA levels, decreased TMAO concentrations, and suppressed systemic inflammation70,191,192. These findings suggest that the holistic restoration of gut microbiota homeostasis can systemically reset the host-microbiota interaction network, including downstream epigenetic regulatory pathways, thereby eliciting potent therapeutic effects.

While promising in animal models, the clinical application of FMT for cardiovascular disease remains controversial and experimental. Evidence for cardiovascular benefit in humans is mixed, with some studies showing limited or transient engraftment192. Key safety concerns include the risk of transferring multi-drug resistant pathogens or occult viruses, which could be fatal in immunocompromised post-MI patients. Additionally, the lack of standardization regarding donor selection, preparation protocols, and optimal delivery routes creates significant regulatory barriers. For probiotics, the “transient” nature of colonization means that effects often disappear after cessation of supplementation, limiting their long-term therapeutic value.

“Postbiotics”—direct supplementation of beneficial metabolites

Postbiotics, defined as “preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host,” encompass SCFAs, bacterial cell wall components, and functional proteins. In contrast to live bacteria (probiotics), postbiotics offer distinct advantages, including defined composition, enhanced stability, and ease of standardization. Furthermore, they obviate the risks associated with colonization failure or infection, thereby possessing superior potential for pharmaceutical development97,193,194. The direct supplementation of SCFAs represents the most straightforward and extensively investigated postbiotic strategy. The administration of butyrate, propionate, or acetate via drinking water or gavage has consistently demonstrated definitive cardioprotective effects across various animal models of MI102,195,196. Butyrate supplementation, in particular, effectively attenuates post-MI myocardial inflammation and fibrosis197,198; its core therapeutic mechanism is intimately linked to its epigenetic function as an HDACi199.

Beyond SCFAs, other molecules generated or metabolized by the microbiota exhibit postbiotic potential. For instance, urolithin A, derived from the gut microbial metabolism of ellagitannins found in pomegranates and berries, serves as a potent mitophagy inducer. By facilitating the clearance of damaged mitochondria, urolithin A enhances cardiomyocyte energy metabolism and viability. While its benefits have been established in models of aging-related cardiac decline, its application in MI also holds significant promise1181,200. Additionally, specific secreted proteins or cell wall components derived from probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG) have been observed to directly interact with host cells, modulating immune and epigenetic pathways to exert anti-inflammatory effects201.

In summary, targeting the “gut microbiota-epigenetics axis” provides a multidimensional, multi-target intervention strategy for the prevention and treatment of MI. Spanning from upstream dietary adjustments and microbiota transplantation, to midstream pharmacological modulation, and finally to the downstream direct supplementation of effector molecules, these strategies collectively constitute an integrated therapeutic framework. This paradigm holds promise for translation into effective clinical practice, offering novel avenues for patient care in MI.

The primary challenge for postbiotics lies in pharmacokinetics and delivery. SCFAs have a very short half-life and are rapidly metabolized by colonocytes or the liver before reaching the systemic circulation or the heart. Achieving an effective concentration in cardiac tissue via oral administration is difficult without advanced delivery systems. Furthermore, the systemic administration of high-dose epigenetic modulators carries the risk of pleiotropic effects, potentially altering the expression of non-target genes in other tissues, which necessitates the development of heart-targeted delivery mechanisms.

Summary and perspectives

Current status and mechanistic gaps

While the concept of the “gut microbiota-epigenetics axis” provides a highly promising theoretical framework for understanding the pathophysiology of MI, it must be noted that current research in this domain remains largely at the stage of phenomenological description. Existing evidence primarily reveals correlations between dysbiosis, altered metabolic profiles, and myocardial epigenetic remodeling, whereas a systematic elucidation of the precise molecular mechanisms and causal chains linking these entities is currently lacking.

Elucidating the precise molecular trajectory from “microbial metabolism” to “host gene regulation” constitutes the primary scientific challenge for future research. A critical knowledge gap persists regarding how non-specific microbial metabolites—such as SCFAs acting as HDACi—achieve locus-specificity within the cardiomyocyte nucleus. How do these metabolites precisely identify and regulate specific cardioprotective or deleterious genes genome-wide without precipitating global transcriptional chaos? Does this process depend on specific transcription factor recruitment, alterations in chromatin accessibility, or distinct signal transduction cascades? Addressing these questions requires transcending simple supplementation experiments to deeply dissect the direct molecular interactions between metabolites and host chromatin remodeling complexes.

Furthermore, the multidimensionality of epigenetic regulation and its interactive network with the microbiota require urgent expansion. Current research focuses heavily on classical DNA methylation and histone acetylation, with significantly insufficient attention paid to epitranscriptomics (e.g., m6A and m5C RNA modifications) and atypical histone modifications (e.g., crotonylation, lactylation). Given the critical role of ncRNAs in cardiac remodeling, understanding how the gut microbiota modulates ncRNA biogenesis and function via novel epigenetic modifications represents a highly promising yet underexplored domain.

Clinical relevance and translational challenges

Despite the promising mechanistic insights derived from animal models, translating findings from the “gut microbiota-epigenetics axis” into clinical practice for MI patients remains a significant challenge. While observational studies have successfully identified distinct microbial signatures and metabolic markers (like TMAO) associated with adverse cardiovascular events, interventional trials in humans remain limited and have yielded mixed results. Most successful interventions to date have focused on metabolic precursors or heart failure, rather than the acute phase of MI.

Bridging this translational gap requires addressing fundamental biological and clinical disparities. First, there are significant anatomical and physiological differences between rodents and humans. The murine microbiome is dominated by different phyla compared to humans, and their cardiac physiology (e.g., heart rate, ion channel distribution) differs, limiting the direct extrapolation of epigenetic findings. Second, clinical heterogeneity complicates human studies. Unlike controlled laboratory animals, MI patients present with complex comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, all of which independently alter the gut microbiome and epigenetic landscape. Third, the confounding effect of standard-of-care medications is profound. Drugs routinely administered post-MI—including statins, antiplatelets, and beta-blockers—have been shown to possess antibiotic-like properties or directly modulate the microbiome, potentially masking the effects of microbiota-targeted therapies.

Consequently, future research must move beyond standard rodent models to utilize humanized microbiota mice and incorporate large-scale clinical cohort data. Clinical trials must be rigorously designed to account for diet and medication use. Only by validating whether specific epigenetic mechanisms (such as SCFA-mediated HDAC inhibition) hold true in the complex physiological environment of human patients can we develop effective microbiome-based therapeutics.

Conclusion

In conclusion, future research on the “gut microbiota-epigenetics axis” must achieve a paradigm shift from establishing “correlational associations” to elucidating “mechanistic causality.” Leveraging single-cell multi-omics, spatial transcriptomics, and epigenetic editing technologies to precisely map the spatiotemporal regulatory landscape of gut metabolic signals within cardiomyocytes will be key to unraveling this complex interaction network. This will not only fill cognitive gaps in basic biology but also lay a solid theoretical foundation for the development of precision cardiovascular therapeutics targeting the microbiota-epigenetics interface.
