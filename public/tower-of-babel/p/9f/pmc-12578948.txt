Novel strategies for enhancing lap spliced reinforced concrete beam behavior for mitigating failure risk
Authors: Sabry Fayed, Moataz Badawi, Mohamed Ghalla, Rabeea W Bazuhair, Ehab A Mlybari, Abdulaziz Alaskar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn addition to one control beam with continuous tensile bars and one control beam with splice, nine reinforced concrete (RC) beams with lap-spliced bars were constructed and strengthened. This was carried out in order to enhance the flexural performance of RC beams that had a short tension reinforcement splice. Spliced beams were strengthened by the use of external post-tensioned rebars (EPTR), near surface mounted (NSM), and strain hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) confinement. The variables in this study include performing a short splice, using one strengthening technique (SHCC, NSM, or EPTR), combining two strengthening techniques, changing the diameter of the NSM rod that is horizontally implanted at the beam soffit, and using a novel end anchorage in the NSM rod. The failure pattern, load-deflection curve, ultimate capacity, ultimate deflection, stiffness, and ductility of the strengthened spliced beams were all examined, and the results were given. The ultimate load of spliced beams increased by 45, 55, and 58%; the ultimate deflection increased by 45, 68, and 75%; and the ductility increased by 111, 120, and 159% as a result of the NSM, SHCC, and EPTR systems, respectively. Conducting SHCC alone produced an ultimate load of 55.2%, an ultimate deflection of 68%, and ductility of 120%; however, functioning both SHCC and EPTR systems produced an ultimate load of 137%, an ultimate deflection of 146%, and a ductility of 771%. Ultimate load, ultimate deflection, and ductility are all increased by 40–60%, 37–84%, and 41–57%, respectively, due to NSM strengthening at the spliced beam soffit.Keywords: RC beams, Lap splice, Post-tension, Near surface mounted, Strain hardening cementitious composites, Anchorage, Bond strength, Construction riskSubject terms: Engineering, Civil engineering

Introduction

With little to no seismic detailing, numerous of the reinforced concrete structures that are still in use today in a number of nations were created solely to withstand gravity forces. As a result, these structures are particularly vulnerable to seismic activity. Numerous catastrophic collapses in these kinds of structures during the last large earthquakes have been attributed to poor quality short lap spliced reinforcement, which is typically positioned above beam-column connections or at high stress regions like the column-footing interface (e.g., Morocco 2022, Italy 2012, China 2008). Often, these lap splices have a distance of 20–24 times bar diameter (d), which is shorter than the splice distances mandated by bond rules and modern design recommendations (ACI Committee 4081; fib 20132. These guidelines often need a splice length more than (40–60) d. They are therefore insufficient to fully mobilize the spliced bars. Moreover, shipping and handling constraints place a limit on the maximum length of the reinforcing bars, and sometimes the bars must be spliced into the structure on site. Other times, for a variety of reasons, including as mistakes in design and execution, a lap splice has an inadequate connection and requires strengthening. One of these three techniques—lap splices, welded splices, or mechanical couplers—is often used to transmit tension from one bar to another. Lap splices are the most often used. Fortification procedures can extend the life of existing structures and increase their resilience to natural disasters like earthquakes. Structural strengthening may improve a structure’s efficiency and usefulness while also assisting it in adjusting to the shifting demands of society. Lap splices were discovered in the beams, slab-to-column joints, and column-footing joints of reinforced concrete structures. The focus of this study was on the tension side lap splices in RC beams. Strengthening techniques that enhance the bond behavior of lapped-spliced steel bars result in enhanced beam behavior for reinforced concrete beams. By strengthening the lap-spliced concrete elements, the earthquake risk associated with existing insufficient reinforced concrete members may be reduced, hence reducing social and financial concerns3. By acting as an external confinement at the splice area, all strengthening techniques applied to the existing RC beams with flaws serve to postpone or stop the failure of the lapped rebars at the concrete cover splitting of the beam soffit.

Lap splices are required to transfer the tension force and provide an appropriate deformation capacity if the lap is located near a critical portion of the maximum moment4,5. Numerous investigators looked at the impact of the bar diameter and geometry, the compressive strength of the concrete, the lap length, and the confinement effect of the transverse reinforcement and concrete on the lapping rebar capacity6–8. To strengthen these splices, a variety of techniques have been devised and put into practice, including fiber reinforced polymers, near surface mounted, external post tensioning processes, and strain hardening cementitious composites. A self-regulating small fracture width and ductile tensile strain-hardening performance are two of the unique features of strain-hardening cementitious composites9–11. In many applications, such as repair, strengthening, durability, and protection, engineers are now considering using SHCC in place of traditional concrete due to its exceptional qualities, which include high ductility, resistance to spalling, wide energy absorption capacity, and strong interfacial bond strength12. Furthermore, debonding failure at the concrete-SHCC interface was avoided by adding stirrups and a mesh grid to the SHCC layer13. The following repair features have been used in a number of investigations to increase the bonding capacity in rod splices: fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) sheets14–17, ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC)18, and post-tensioned metal straps3. The impact of wrapping carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) sheets on the capacity of rebar to join was examined by Garcia et al.15. The test findings demonstrated that strengthening improved the bonding strength of the rebar. Recently developed, UHPC is an innovative cementitious composite with excellent strength in both compression and tension, ductility, and durability19.

Fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC) can enhance the binding process in lap-splice zones and minimize or completely eliminate concrete splitting20. It seems possible to enhance the behavior of lap splices by using FRC to control the opening of splitting fractures caused by the pressure of the bond stress component operating perpendicular to the bar axis21.

The two most popular methods of strengthening RC beams are near-surface mounting (NSM) and external bonding (EB). The NSM and EB systems are the most widely utilized methods for improving the declining RC participation22–24. The EB approach offered flexural reinforcement for RC beams. The crack induced debonding of the RC structures reinforced with EB FRP was advantageous25. Using the EB approach, FRP was implanted in drilled grooves in the side and/or bottom concrete coverings of the beam in a variety of forms, including bars and strips, using epoxy glue or mortar26.

In order to improve the efficacy of NSM applications, pre-stress has been increasingly popular recently27,28. Pre-stressed external bonded (EB) and pre-stressed NSM systems were used by Casadei et al.29 to reinforce RC beams; factors that were investigated included groove form, pre-stress, and FRP bar type. Lee et al.30 looked at the impact of strengthening RC beams using a pre-tension and a post-tension NSM mechanism on repair. The findings showed that the pre-tension NSM mechanism caused pre-stress loss, which resulted in a weaker strengthening effect than the post-tension NSM approach. In order to investigate the fatigue effectiveness of the beams, Ouadah and ElHacha31 strengthened them with NSM after pre-stressing at 0-57.4% of the CFRP’s maximum capacity. The findings demonstrated that the NSM-strengthened beam had a smaller shift than the non-strengthened beam. Additionally, the CFRP form, pre-stressing dimensions, and groove dimensions all improved the strengthening effect.

NSM, SHCC, and external post-tension techniques are still not being used enough to strengthen RC beams, especially ones with short splices. Further data on NSM strengthening schemes and the combination of NSM, SHCC, and external post-tension techniques still need to be discovered. Therefore, the current work focuses on using a combination of near-surface mounted steel rods, post-tensioned steel rods, and strain-hardening cementitious composites to strengthen short lap splices in reinforced concrete beams.

Aim of the research

The innovation of this research is the use of the Near Surface Mounted (NSM) bars to strengthen short splices in reinforced concrete beams, as no study has used it before in this subject. Additionally, creating tension force in the NSM rods is considered as an innovation. The post-tension NSM system will be able to better external confinement of the area around the splice. The advantages of external post-tension systems and NSM were merged in this technology. Furthermore, strain hardening cementitious composite (SHCC) has improved the strengthening of reinforced concrete (RC) beams; yet, there is still relatively little use of this approach for strengthening RC beam with short splices. The effectiveness of a single strengthening technique was examined in the prior studies. The characteristics of the concrete element are different from those of any other study that is conducted. Additionally, the lap-splice test specimen was used for this investigation because a realistic bonding distribution of stress between the surrounding concrete and the reinforcing rebars may be produced using the lap-splice test specimen. Comparing different strengthening techniques using construction material, therefore, is invalid unless they are used to the same concrete element in the same circumstances. Thus, the main objective of this study was develop strengthened-spliced RC beams using the NSM, post-tension, and SHCC methods separately, and then combine them under the same circumstances. The fact that this study welds NSM rod with a perpendicular rod implanted in the RC beam web offers a definitive answer to the issue of premature debonding at the end of NSM rods at high loading levels.

Experimental work

Materials

Materials used in beams

The normal concrete (NC) mix was used to cast the examined beams. As listed in Table 1, the ingredients of the concrete mix were water, Portland cement with a 42.5 grade, graded crushed basalt dolomite with a maximum size of 15 mm as a coarse aggregate, and sand that was used as a fine aggregate. As shown in Fig. 1, mechanical sieve analysis was performed on the sand and basalt stone. Particle size of the sand was 0.075 mm to 2.36 mm while fineness modulus of it was 5.52. The specific gravities of the basalt, sand, and cement utilized were 2.4, 2.7, and 3.12, respectively, while their unit weights were 1305 kg/m3, 1234 kg/m3 and 1200 kg/m3. The concrete mix’s compressive strength was calibrated to be 25 MPa. The concrete’s cubic compressive strength was 27.5 MPa after 28 days. HTS were 10 mm deformed bars, whereas NMS were 8 mm smooth surface bars. To ascertain the employed steel bars’ mechanical characteristics, tension tests were carried out. Materials used in strengthening

In external post-tensioned rebar (EPTR) techniques, steel plates were employed. The steel plates’ average yield strength, ultimate strength, and average elongation were 245 MPa, 336 MPa, and 19.8%, respectively. Screw bolts were employed in EPTR and near surface mounted (NSM) techniques. An 8.8 grade high-strength friction-grip threaded bolts were utilized. These bolts are made of medium carbon steel and they were without heads. They have a continuous spiral of ribs on their surface. These bolts measured 10.3 mm in total diameter and 10 mm in net diameter no including rib height. The bolt ribs measured 1 mm in width, 0.17 mm in height, 1.67 mm in spacing, and 800 in angle. Threads of these bolts were distributed on whole length of the bolts. Uniaxial tension tests were carried out to ascertain the bolts’ mechanical properties. The results showed that the elasticity modulus was 200 MPa, the yield stress was 400 MPa, the rupture strength was 800 MPa, and the elongation was 15%. In EPTR technique, steel nuts, with 17 mm in external diameter, were used to interconnect the bolt in the steel plates. In NSM method, Sikadur 31CF32 was used as epoxy agent.

These bolts are attached to the pre-existing concrete using this substance, which is known as a high strength epoxy glue. The following benefits come with using this type of resin: it does not shrink or slump, has good initial and final capacity, is easy to use, and doesn’t require priming construction material. These goods have a 60–80 min first cure period, and the service lasts for seven days. Part A and Part B are the two portions that make up this agent. To create components with a consistent color, parts A and B were combined for five to ten minutes at a moderate speed drill.

As shown in Table 3, polypropylene fiber was mixed with Portland cement, quartz, sand, silica fume, superplasticizer, and water to create the strain-hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) that are utilized in this work. The rigidity of the SHCC is greatly increased by the polypropylene fiber. The geometrical diameter and length of the polypropylene fibers were 0.012 mm and 12 mm, respectively. The identical construction materials utilized in SHCC were the sand and cement of the tested beam castings. Quartz was used in place of coarse aggregate. To obtain SHCC mix, there must be a large volume of cement and a low water to cement ratio (between 0.25 and 0.35). A low water/cement (w/c) ratio will produce a dry mix unless admixtures of the chemical type (superplastizer) or the mineral type (silica fume) are utilized. In this work, the proportion of water to binder (cement, quartz, and silica fume) was 0.2. This low water ratio is thought to be a beneficial choice for SHCC. In the event of a low w/c ratio, this addition was utilized to give a practical level of concrete. In addition to being a cement additive, silica fume was employed to improve the properties of both newly poured and fully cured concrete. Silica fume is a reactive pozzolan that also serves as a small void filler. Silica fume increases the strength of the attachment between the particles. With tensile and compressive strengths of 8.1 MPa and 60 MPa, respectively, SHCC was used.

Beam specimens

In addition to one control beam with continuous tensile bars, there are ten identical lap-spliced beams. For casting all beams, the NC mix described in Sect. 3.1.1 was utilized. Every beam is the same size and same internal reinforcement (Fig. 3a). The beams measured 1500 mm in length and 120 × 200 mm in section. The effective loaded span of the beams was 1400 mm. Two HTS 10 mm bars served as the beams’ tension reinforcement, and two NMS 8 mm bars were positioned on the compression side. In the pressure areas at the top of the beam section, there is no need for longitudinal compression reinforcement bars, but only two NMS 8 mm bars were placed to suspend the stirrups. At the middle of the beam, two tension bars were spliced together with a splice length that was 25 times the bar diameter. In addition, the center portion (pure bending moment) of the beam, the length is separated into two equal shear spans. The minimum suggested splice length of reinforcing bars in the tension zone is 40 times the bar diameter, as per practically national regulations. In this investigation, the splice length was decided to be 25 times the bar diameter, which is deemed a short splice or substandard splice. Furthermore, the four-point bending test was chosen to provide a pure bending moment span at the splice location; as a result, only tension force will be applied to the lap-spliced bars. The splice length was chosen to be shorter than the pure bending moment span for the same reason. Additionally, the splice length was chosen to be less than the pure bending moment span in order to increase splice efficiency without increasing the beam’s flexure capacity. Two legs-ties composed of NMS 8 mm bars positioned 50 mm apart along the shear spans serve as shear reinforcement. These smooth-surfaced bars were chosen for the construction material of the stirrups because of their ease of forming without cracks at bents. Also, their strength is also proportional to the shear stress on the beam. Because the beams were protected from shear failure, strengthening techniques could be used to improve splice efficiency and stop shear failure before reinforced beams would flexure. In order to isolate the role of SHCC, NSM, or EPTR strengthening techniques in enhancing the effectiveness of the short splices in RC beams, the bending moment span did not include any stirrups. The tie’s clear cover measured 15 mm on each of the beam section’s four edges. The tension bar’s side and bottom covers (c) measured 23 mm. As advised by the literature34, the clear distance between the two pair bars at the splice location was 34 mm greater than three times the bar diameter. Strengthening techniques

Two control beams, one with spliced bars (beam C) and the other with continuous tension bars (beam C0), were constructed without strengthening. In C0, tension bar continuity was achieved in order for the tested beams to operate to their full abilities. NSM steel rods were used for supporting three spliced beams (HN10, HN12, and HN16) horizontally. In addition, three distinct strengthening techniques—SHCC layers (S), NSM steel rods (N), and EPTR (P)—were used to vertically strengthen six spliced beams (VS, VN, VP, VSN, VSP, and VNP). A “V” may occasionally appear as the first letter in the beam notation, denoting strengthened confinement at the splice region at the beam sides’ vertical direction. Occasionally, the first letter in the beam designation is “H,” denoting the use of a horizontally implanted NSM rod for strengthening at the splice zone of the beam soffit. The remaining letters “S, N, or P” stand for strengthening techniques. The two numerals (10, 12 or 16) indicate the NSM rod diameter that is being used. The length of the strengthening region in all strengthened beams was 345 mm, which was greater than the splice length and less than the bending span. Drilling was done in the concrete cover to create either vertical or horizontal grooves at the beam surface. The vertical grooves’ cross-section measured 15 mm by 15 mm and was consistent across all specimens that vertically strengthened by NSM or EPTR techniques. According to ACI 440.1R-1535, the groove diameter in the NSM strengthening method should be at least 1.5 times the diameter of the reinforcement bar. So, diameter of NSM was selected to be 10 mm and groove 15 mm. Also, all specimens strengthened at the soffit of the beam had a constant 20 × 20 mm horizontal groove cross-section. The groove’s diameter was chosen to be more than that of the EPTR and NSM steel rods (10 mm, 12 mm and 16 mm).

For the NSM rod to be embedded inside the beam surface, these grooves are crucial. The same groove geometry was used in all strengthened beams in order to provide an equitable and reliable comparison between the three strengthening techniques. Furthermore, grooves will strengthen the link between the concrete and SHCC layer and stop debonding at the extremities of the SHCC layer. There were four vertical grooves made on the beam’s sides. At the end of the spliced bar, a pair of grooves was placed to strengthen the confining effect of NSM or EPTR techniques or to function as the end anchorage of the SHCC layer. Individual strengthening using the S, N, and P-methods were carried out in the VP, VN, and VS beams, respectively. A thin layer of SHCC was cast at the splice location on both sides of beam VS (Fig. 4). The SHCC layer had a thickness of 20 mm.

Using an electrical ripsaw, the grooves at the ends of the splice were vertically slotted in beam VN (Fig. 5). These slots were then thoroughly cleaned. After partially filling the slot with epoxy, the steel rod is forced into the center of the slot to force the epoxy to flow around it and fill the whole space between it and the rod. Subsequently, epoxy is poured into the slot entirely, leveling the surface.

Beam VN strengthened vertically with NSM steel rods at the splice region, cm.

The external post-tensioned rebar (EPTR) method was used in beam VP (Fig. 6). Slots were present in the grooves. There was no epoxy in the slot. Next, the steel rods are inserted vertically into the slots and into the steel plates that are positioned on the top and bottom surfaces of the beam. Two nuts were then tightened to the necessary amount. The strain during the EPTR tension procedure was recorded by attaching a strain gauge to the rod and connecting it to a data logger that was connected to a computer. Every steel rod in every beam strengthened using the EPTR method was tensioned to a maximum of 30% of its yield stress.

Beam VP strengthened vertically with EPTR at the splice region.

In the beam VSN, a combination of the SHCC (S) and NSM (N) procedures were used. The NSM strengthening process was initially carried out using the same method as for beam VN, and following epoxy hardening, the SHCC layers were cast using the same method as for beam VS. In the beam VSP, a combination of the SHCC (S) and EPTR (P) approaches were used. The same process used for beam VP was initially used for EPTR strengthening, and then same procedure of the beam VS was used to cast the SHCC layers. In the beam VNP, a combination of the NSM (N) and EPTR (P) approaches were used. As previously mentioned, the grooves were cleaned thoroughly and arranged vertically. Partially filling the slot with epoxy, the EPTR is fitted into the slot and into the steel plates that are positioned at the top and bottom of the beam. Subsequently, two nuts were secured to the necessary extent (30% of their yield stress). Subsequently, epoxy is poured into the slot entirely, leveling the surface.

The NSM technique is depicted in Fig. 7 for the three spliced beams (HN10, HN12, and HN16) that were strengthened horizontally at the splice zone using a single NSM steel rod. Variable of this NSM technique was bar diameter. The NSM rod diameter that was utilized in the HN10, HN12, and HN16 beams was 10, 12, or 16 mm, in that order. The NSM rod had a 400 mm length. Two vertical rods were attached to the ends of the NSM rod to join it. The two vertical rods with diameter of 10 mm were completely welded with NSM rod at distance of 330 mm. The purpose of these two vertical rods is to keep the ends of the NSM rod from separating especially at the maximum load. The beam was drilled in two stages in order to construct the NSM rod inside the beam soffit. First, one groove (20 × 20 mm) was drilled along distance of 400 mm in the bottom surface of the beam between two pairs of lapped-spliced tension rebars. Second, at the designated locations, two circular vertical holes of 12 mm in diameter were bored. The NSM process will begin once the drill groove and two holes are finished. After partially filling the groove and both holes with epoxy, the rods are fitted into the steel plates that are positioned at the top of the beam. Subsequently, the nuts were secured to the necessary extent (30% of their yield stress). Next, epoxy is poured into the groove all the way to the surface, leveling it.

NSM technique that used in the three spliced beams (HN10, HN12 and HN16).

Results and analysis

Failures and load-deflection response

Tested beam failure types and load-mid span deflection curves are displayed in Figs. 9 and 10, respectively. In beam C0 without a splice, flexural cracks first developed in the mid-span area under load start. As the loading increased, these cracks expanded, leading to flexure failure and the occurrence of a few simple shear cracks (Fig. 9a). As can be seen in Fig. 10, ductile flexure failure resulting from high elongation of the two continuous (unspliced) tension bars occurred a long horizontal distance after beam C0’s peak load. The concrete cover splitting (CCS) at the lap-splice zone was the cause of the failure in control specimen C (Fig. 9b). CCS occurred at both beam sides and its soffit. Because of a short, inadequately lapped splice (25 times the diameter of the tension bar), the early failure happened. It was observed that the tested spliced beams’ breaking fracture trend mirrored that reported by Gambarova et al.33.

Failure patterns of tested beams.

Load-deflection curves of tested beams.

Notable are the two large vertical cracks that can be seen near the end of the lap-splice zone. This failure happened quickly, producing a loud noise, and the applied load was abruptly lost, as seen by the vertical decrease that followed the peak load on curve of the beam C (Fig. 10). The CCS failure in beams VN (Fig. 9c), VS (Fig. 9d), VP (Fig. 9e), and VNS (Fig. 9f) happened at higher loads than in control beam C, indicating that strengthening techniques increased capacity but were unable to alter the failure mode from CCS to flexure one. The post-peak fall curve of these beams was shown to be more ductile than that of beam C, indicating that the three strengthening methods (N, S, and P) resulted in a minor delay in CCS due to external confinement. In beam VN, because there were two vertical NSM rods before and after the lap-splice rebars, which prevented side CCS, the CCS only happened at the bottom of the beam and did not appear at the sides. The CCS failure in beam VNS (Fig. 9f), which was strengthened utilizing a combination of NSM and SHCC confinement techniques, happened at the beam soffit because the lap-splice zone’s bottom surface was left unsupported because the strengthening was only done on the beam’s sides.

It was possible to convert CCS into flexure failure mode, as happened in control beam C0 with continuous rebars, by combining NSM and EPTR confinement techniques in beam VNP (Fig. 9g) and SHCC and EPTR confinement techniques in beam VSP (Fig. 9h). In addition, both control beam C0 and strengthened beam VNP showed same cracking map, failure mode and load-deflection relationship.

The maximum carrying load in these two beams approached control beam C0’s peak load of 90 kN. The well strengthening confinement avoided the occurrence of both CCS and/or pull out failure of the lapped rebars, despite the occurrence of a vertical crack at the end of the lap-splice zone prior to the peak load. The beam reached its maximum capacity (90 kN) as the loading increased and the splice could withstand more loads. In addition to the previously positive results of these two beams, it was seen that, in comparison to all other single strengthening methods utilized in the spliced beams, the combination of the EPTR system and SHCC/NSM systems produced more ductile behavior following the peak point of the load-deflection curve.

The CCS failure happened at the splice zone on the sidewalls of beams HN10 (Fig. 9i), HN12 (Fig. 9j), and HN16 (Fig. 9k). The HN10, HN12, and HN16 beams’ soffit was equipped with a single horizontal bottom NSM rod, which prevented CCS at the beam’s bottom side and increased the strengthened beam capacity. However, the rod did not prevent side CCS, which led to an early failure before the full capacity (90 kN). The curves for beams HN10, HN12, and HN16 in Fig. 10 demonstrate how, as soon as the load reaches its peak, the curve abruptly drops because of side CCS. However, because end anchorages were utilized in this study, there was no end debonding in the NSM rod and vertical cracks only developed at the ends of the rod rather than at the ends of the splice in these beams. It was observed that the NSM rod-epoxy interface was free of fractures. The failure was transferred outside of the lap-splice zone by the NSM rods implanted in the beam soffit at the splice. Because side CCS occurs, increasing the NSM rod did not increase the strengthened beams’ capacity. It is therefore advised to use SHCC, NSM, or EPTR systems for confinement at the beam sides in addition to the single horizontal bottom NSM rod at the beam soffit. These beams will thereafter operate at their maximum capacity of 90 kN.

Figure 10 shows the curves for unstrengthened spliced beams C and C0, which are the lowest and upper bounds, respectively, for all strengthened spliced beams. The load-deflection curves were seen to be nonlinear until they reached the peak point, after which they were linear up to about 20 kN. When the load reached its peak in all strengthened spliced beams but VNP, the curve immediately dropped, exhibiting brittle behavior. Conversely, as compared to control C, all strengthening techniques that were applied in either a single (Fig. 10a) or double (Fig. 10b) mode produced greater carrying loads and corresponding ultimate deflection. When the post-peak curve portion of the three beams—HN10, HN12, and HN16—vertically declined after the peak load, it was observed that the usage of bottom NSM rods at the splice had the most brittle mode (Fig. 10c). The curves sloped similarly because the strengthening methods applied to all tested beams did not result in an increase in flexure reinforcement at the splice zone.

The load-deflection curve of every beam was used to determine the ultimate load (Pu), corresponding deflection (Δu), initial stiffness (k), and energy absorption capacity (EAC) of strengthened spliced beams. These values were then summarized in Table 5. The slope of the curve’s first linear portion determined the k, whereas the curve’s peak position determined the Pu and Δu. The area beneath the curve is called EAC. In addition to examining the ultimate load and associated deflection, it is crucial to investigate the impact of strengthening techniques on the ductility of the spliced beams. Energy absorption capacity (EAC), is a key indicator of ductility and when it is higher, the structure performs better. Gain rates were computed for each strengthened beam in the Pu, Δu, k, and EAC, compared to spliced control beam C that had not been strengthened. The impacts of strengthening techniques on the Pu, Δu, k, and EAC of spliced beam structural performance are discussed in the following sections.

Effects of short lap-splice

Ultimate load, deflection, stiffness and energy absorption capacity

The ultimate load (Pu), corresponding deflection (Δu), initial stiffness (k) and energy absorption capacity (EAC) of spliced beam C was 58, 73, 4 and 93% less than those of unspliced beam C0, listed in Table 5. Aside from stiffness, the splice length of 25 times the bar diameter had a significant detrimental impact on the structural performance in terms of Pu, Δu and EAC of the spliced beam C. Additionally, the brittle failure of beam C was caused by CCS at the splice while ductile tension-flexure failure occurred in beam C0 due to continuity of the tension bars. Because the slope of the early linear part of the load-deflection curve of beam C occurred before the first crack load at the splice zone, insufficient length of the lap-splice does not appreciably modify the initial stiffness. The lower bars in the beam are required to bear the tensile force resulting from the external bending moment. When the connection is made in a tension region, the tensile force is transmitted from one bar to the other through cohesion with the surrounding concrete. Due to the short bond length with the concrete, the bar may slip out of the concrete. Because of the small thickness of the concrete around the bar, a split may occur, and this is what happened in the beam C in the presented work. Once the beam C reached a load of 37 kN, a sudden collapse occurred due to concrete cover splitting and the beam lost directly its resistance.

Effects of single strengthening method

Ultimate load

Using NSM, SHCC and EPTR systems enhanced the Pu by 45, 55 and 58% compared to control C, listed in Table 5. NSM strengthening method using four steel rods embedded vertically at both splice sides causes the smallest increase in the flexural strength. While SHCC strengthening method using one continues layer at each splice side achieved gain in the Pu more than that of NSM strengthening method. Moreover, EPTR system using additional tension forces in the NSM rods causes the largest increase in the flexural strength. All three strengthening systems make external confinement to the concrete around the lap-splice zone hence the bond strength of the concrete enhanced and delayed the concrete cover splitting (CCS) at the beam soffit. Additionally, when these strengthening systems were performed at the splice sides, the CCS did not occurred at the strengthening position hence the failure delayed hence the flexural capacity increased. No strengthening systems used could achieve the full flexural capacity of beam C0 (90.2 kN).

Ultimate deflection

The ultimate deflection of beams strengthened with any strengthening system is greater than that of control C. The Δu was enhanced by 45, 68, and 75% using NSM, SHCC, and EPTR systems in comparison to control C. Based on the ultimate load and corresponding ultimate deflection, it was observed that EPTR system performed better than SHCC system, which performed better than NSM system under the same conditions. Demonstrating that the SHCC system outperformed the NSM system in terms of achieving confinement at the lap-splice zone, whereas the EPTR system did so more successfully.

Stiffness

Strengthening techniques do not significantly alter the initial stiffness (k), as Table 5 illustrates. No clear change occurred in the k of all tested beams as shown in Fig. 11a. Because every strengthening technique employed in this study merely created a confinement at the splice and did not boost the flexural reinforcement, the initial stiffness (k) did not change.

Stiffness and energy absorption capacity of tested beams.

Energy absorption capacity

Table 5; Fig. 11b illustrated values of the EACs of tested beams. The EAC was increased by 112, 120, and 159% when using NSM, SHCC, and EPTR strengthening methods in comparison to C. The NSM strengthening method, which uses four steel rods embedded vertically at both splice sides, causes the least amount of ductility increase. The SHCC strengthening method, which uses one continuous layer at each splice side, achieves a greater amount of ductility than the NSM strengthening method. Additionally, the EPTR system, which uses additional tension forces in the NSM rods, causes the largest amount of ductility. These systems delayed the failure, which increases the area under the beam curve, which is referred to as ductility. However, no strengthening systems were able to achieve the EAC of beam C0 with no splice (1823.5 kN.mm) due to brittle splitting at the splice of spliced beams. All spliced reinforced beams had poorer total energy absorption capacities than control beam C0, which did not have a splice. These results were in agreement with spliced beam observations reported in34.

Effects of double strengthening methods

Ultimate load

This section explains how combining two strengthening techniques affects spliced beam performance. Combining approaches aims to maximize control beam C0 efficiency in addition to increasing link capacity as compared to employing a single method. Additionally, by changing the collapse from CCS to flexure mode, the objective is to reach the deformation capacity (ultimate deflection and EAC) of beam C0 rather than only the maximum load. In comparison to splicing control C, using both NSM and EPTR systems in beam VNP, both NSM and SHCC systems in beam VNS, and both SHCC and EPTR systems in beam VSP increased the Pu by 134, 88, and 137%. It was evident that combining two procedures produced gain ratios that were significantly larger than those of employing just one strengthening technique because applying two methods to the sides of the lap-splice zone improved confinement; hence, each method played a separate function without adversely affecting the other.

Moreover, the gain in the Pu of beams strengthened with post-tension techniques (VNP and VSP) was larger than that of beams not equipped with post-tension technique (VNS). In other words, the post-tension approach obtained the highest confinement effect for two reasons. By imparting tension stress to the exterior rods prior to loading, the EPTR technique offers an advantage over the other two ways. As a result, more confinement was formed in the surround concrete along the lapped rebars both before and during loading. By adding steel plates to the top and bottom surface of the beam at the splice zone, this technique also has an advantage over the other two ways. This produced additional pressure on the surrounding concrete along the lapped rebars before and during the loading. Consequently, 88.8 kN was the ultimate load achieved by the beam VSP and 88 kN by the beam VNP, almost matching the full capacity of control C0 (90.2 kN).

However, the NSM method used four vertically embedded steel rods at both splice sides, and the SHCC method cast one continuous layer at each splice side in the beam VNS. This increased confinement on the beam sides in both the vertical and horizontal directions, delaying the opening of cracks at the ends of the splice zone. Hence the capacity of the beam VNS increased over the beams VN strengthened with NSM and VS strengthened with SHCC. Three beams, VS, VP, and VN, with a single strengthening system had an average Pu of 57.2 kN. Using both NSM and EPTR systems in beam VNP, both NSM and SHCC systems in beam VNS, and both SHCC and EPTR systems in beam VSP raised the Pu by 53, 22, and 55%, respectively, in relation to the average Pu.

The load capacity (Pu) of the beam VNS increased over the beams VS strengthened with SHCC alone (Fig. 12a). The improvement in Pu of VNS was 21% higher than that of beam VS. NSM rods works as an additional reinforcement inside SHCC layer hence the strengthened beam VNS resisted more carrying load capacity more than beam VS. The Pu of beam VSP provided by combining SHCC and EPTR systems increased by 56% when compared to beam VS strengthened with SHCC alone. To put it another way, compared to control C, executing SHCC alone in beam VS produced a Pu of 55%, however executing both SHCC and EPTR systems in beam VSP produced a Pu of 137%.

Effect of double strengthening methods on beams performance referenced to one strengthening method.

Ultimate deflection

It was found that beams strengthened with two different strengthening systems (VSP, VNS, or VNP) had a bigger ultimate deflection than beams strengthened with one strengthening system (VS, VN, or VP). Demonstrating how combining two approaches greatly improves both the Pu and the spliced beams’ deformation. The Δu was enhanced by 331, 137 and 146% when utilizing both NSM and EPTR systems in beam VNP, both NSM and SHCC systems in beam VNS, and both SHCC and EPTR systems in beam VSP in contrast to splicing control C. In beam VS, running SHCC alone resulted in a Δu of 68% compared to control C. In beam VSP, executing both SHCC and EPTR systems yielded a Δu of 146%. In beam VN, running NSM alone resulted in Δu of 45% compared to control C; in beam VNP, running both NSM and EPTR systems yielded Δu of 331%.

Beams reinforced with two different strengthening systems (VSP, VNS, or VNP) exhibit larger ultimate deflections than those reinforced with only one system (VS, VN, or VP), as shown in Fig. 12b. This suggests that combining two methods can significantly improve the ultimate load-bearing capacity and deformation performance of beams, potentially indicating a pattern in the combined effect on deflection.

Stiffness

As previously demonstrated, the initial stiffness (k) is not considerably changed by strengthening approaches. The initial stiffness (k) remained unchanged since all of the strengthening methods used in this investigation only improved the flexural performance by causing a confinement at the splice (see in Table 5; Figs. 11a and 12c).

Energy absorption capacity

It was found that beams strengthened with two different strengthening systems (VSP, VNS, or VNP) had bigger EACs than beams strengthened with one strengthening system (VS, VN, or VP). Demonstrating how combining two approaches greatly improves both the capacity and the spliced beams’ ductility. The EAC was enhanced by 1385, 203, and 771% when utilizing both NSM and EPTR systems in beam VNP, both NSM and SHCC systems in beam VNS, and both SHCC and EPTR systems in beam VSP in contrast to splicing control C (see in Table 5; Fig. 11a). In beam VS, running SHCC alone resulted in an EAC of 120% compared to control C while in beam VSP, executing both SHCC and EPTR systems yielded an EAC of 771.1%. In beam VN, running NSM alone resulted in EAC of 111% compared to control C; in beam VNP, running both NSM and EPTR systems yielded EAC of 1385%. As seen in Fig. 12d, beams reinforced with two distinct strengthening systems (VSP, VNS, or VNP) have greater EACs than beams reinforced with only one system (VS, VN, or VP). This implies that beams’ energy capacity may be greatly increased by combining two techniques.

Effects of NSM system at the splice soffit

Ultimate load

When compared to specimen without strengthening, the NSM strengthening at the beam soffit raises the ultimate load of spliced beams by an average of 40–60%. When a 10 mm NSM rod was longitudinally inserted at the beam soffit at the lap-splice zone in beam HN10, the Pu increased by 60% in comparison to control C. When a 12 mm-NSM rod was inserted longitudinally at the beam soffit at the lap-splice zone in beam HN12, the Pu increased by 38% in comparison to control C. When a 16 mm NSM rod was inserted longitudinally at the beam soffit at the lap-splice zone in beam HN16, the Pu increased by 58% in comparison to control C. Since the lowest diameter (10 mm) was able to transfer failure from the lap-splice zone to the ends of the NSM rod, it was seen that variations in NSM rod diameter had no effect on Pu and that increasing NSM rod diameter was thus useless. Two vertical rods were welded into the beam web and inserted at one end of the NSM rod to prevent debonding, particularly when the beam reaches its peak stress. The tension stress was spread throughout all lapped rebars and NSM rod when the rod was glued adjacent to lapped tension rebars. Consequently, the NSM rod assisted the rebars in carrying a portion of the tension force; as a result, the tension force in the lapped rebars decreased, delaying failure and increasing Pu. Because the NSM rod length (400 mm) was smaller than the bending moment span (500 mm), the splitting failure happened outside the splice zone but inside the bending moment span, preventing the NSM approach from achieving the full capacity of control C0.

As shown in Fig. 13a, it was seen that ultimate load of beams strengthened with horizontal and single vertical retrofit techniques (HN10, HN12, HN16, VP, VS and VN) was equal approximately, which indicates the equivalence of the horizontal and single vertical methods of strengthening. On the contrary, combining the two vertical methods greatly improved the maximum load of the beams.

The difference between vertical and horizontal retrofit techniques.

Ultimate deflection

A greater ultimate deflection was observed in the NSM strengthening system at the soffit of the beams HN10, HN12, and HN16 compared to control C. Illustrating the significant improvement in both the Pu and the spliced beams’ deformation that results from incorporating NSM rod at the splice zone. When using NSM rods with diameters of 10, 12, and 16 mm, the Δu was increased by 84, 37, and 76% in comparison to splicing control C. The improvement in the deflections occurred when the NSM rod lengthened the splice length from 250 mm (twenty-five times the bar diameter) to 400 mm (NSM rod length), hence improving the beam’s deformation.

The ultimate deflection of beams reinforced using horizontal and single vertical retrofit approaches (HN10, HN12, HN16, VP, VS, and VN) was about comparable, as illustrated in Fig. 13b, indicating the equivalency of the two strengthening techniques. On the other hand, the maximum deflection of the beams was significantly increased by combining the two vertical approaches.

Stiffness

The application of NSM strengthening technique does not significantly alter the initial stiffness (k). Since NSM strengthening merely enhanced the short splice’s performance and did not increase the flexural reinforcement, the initial stiffness (k) stayed constant.

Energy absorption capacity

The NSM strengthening system at the beam soffit of HN10, HN12, and HN16 was found to have larger EACs than control C. Illustrating the significant improvement in ductility and the Pu of the spliced beams that results from incorporating NSM rod at the splice zone. When using NSM rods with diameters of 10, 12, and 16 mm, the EAC was increased by 56, 41, and 51% as compared to splicing control C. The strengthened beams’ deformation improved as a result of the NSM rod’s increase in splice length from 250 to 400 mm, which led to gains in EACs. Additionally, while using NSM rods, the strengthened beams’ load-deflection curve’s lengthened drop part increased.

Applications and structural risk mitigation strategies

The outcomes of this study are of significant relevance to construction engineering and project management. The quantifiable improvements in load capacity, ductility, and energy absorption through the application of NSM, SHCC, and EPTR systems directly inform structural strengthening strategies for existing RC infrastructure. These enhancements not only reduce the risk of premature failure in critical structural members, particularly those with inadequate lap splices, but also improve resilience and service life, factors that are essential in the planning and risk management of construction projects. For construction project managers, the findings provide clear technical justification to prioritize combined strengthening solutions in high-risk or retrofitted structures, supporting more efficient allocation of resources, better scheduling control, and safer delivery of infrastructure assets.

Economic assessment and performance-to-cost implications of strengthening techniques

From an economic perspective, the selection of appropriate strengthening techniques—such as NSM, SHCC, and EPTR—must be guided not only by their technical performance but also by their cost-efficiency. While SHCC and EPTR systems demonstrated superior gains in ultimate load and ductility, their implementation involves higher material and labor costs compared to NSM. However, when assessed in terms of performance-per-cost ratio, combined systems (e.g., SHCC + EPTR or NSM + EPTR) offer compelling value by significantly extending structural service life and minimizing the likelihood of costly failure during operation. For project managers and decision-makers, this performance-to-cost trade-off is essential in lifecycle cost analysis (LCCA), as initial investments in advanced strengthening can lead to substantial long-term savings in maintenance, downtime, and risk mitigation.

Conclusion

The flexural performance of reinforced concrete (RC) beams with short splice in the tension reinforcement was improved by the application of strain hardening cementitious composites (SHCC), near surface mounted surface (NSM), or external post-tensioned rebars (EPTR) strengthening techniques, either separately or in combination. Ten identical lap-spliced beams with the same internal reinforcement and dimensions, measuring 1500 mm in length and 120 × 200 mm in section, in addition one control RC beam with continuous tensile bars were performed. At the middle of the beam, two tension bars were spliced together with a substandard splice length that was 25 times the bar diameter. The bending moment span did not include any stirrups in order to isolate the contribution of SHCC, NSM, or EPTR strengthening approaches in improving the effectiveness of the short splices in RC beams. Many construction materials were used in this study. This study’s variables include the short splice that is now in place, the use of one strengthening technique (SHCC, NSM, or EPTR), the combination of two strengthening techniques, the modification of the NSM rod’s diameter that is horizontally implanted at the beam soffit, and the use of end anchorage in the NSM rod. Key parameters were examined and their effects on the strengthened spliced beams’ failure pattern, load-deflection curve, ultimate capacity, ultimate deflection, stiffness, and ductility were discussed. The results are displayed with the following comments:

1- Comparing RC beams, with no splices in the tension reinforcement, to those with a short splice length of 25 times the bar diameter resulted in decline ratios of 58, 72, 4, and 93% in the ultimate load, corresponding deflection, initial stiffness, and energy absorption capacity, respectively.

2- In comparison to a control spliced unstrengthened beam, the NSM, SHCC, and EPTR systems increased the ultimate load of spliced beams by 45, 55, and 58%; increased the ultimate deflection by 45, 67, and 74%; and increased the ductility by 111, 120, and 159%. No single strengthening system used was able to achieve the full flexural capacities (ultimate load, deflection, and ductility) of an unspliced unstrengthen beam. Neither single nor double strengthening techniques significantly changed the initial stiffness.

3- Conducting SHCC alone resulted in an ultimate load of 55%, an ultimate deflection of 68% and ductility of 120% when compared to the control spliced unstrengthened beam; however, operating both SHCC and EPTR systems yielded an ultimate load of 137%, an ultimate deflection of 145% and a ductility of 771%.

4- In comparison to splicing control beam, using both NSM and EPTR systems and both NSM and SHCC systems increased the ultimate load capacity by 134 and 88%, respectively.

5- The ultimate deflection of strengthened beams was improved by 330, 137, and 146% when utilizing both NSM and EPTR, both NSM and SHCC, and both SHCC and EPTR systems in contrast to splicing unstrengthen beam.

6- The load capacity of the beam that provided with both NSM and SHCC increased by 21% over the beam strengthened with SHCC alone.

7- Compared to specimen with no strengthening, the NSM strengthening at the spliced beam soffit increases ultimate load by 40–60%, ultimate deflection by 37–84% and ductility by 41–57%.
