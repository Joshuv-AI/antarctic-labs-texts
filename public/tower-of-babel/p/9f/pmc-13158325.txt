Deceased donor ABO discrepancy due to a rare AwB subgroup: Implications for organ allocation
Authors: Sara O. Dionne, Kelsey Smith, Luis Santiago, Brenden O'Neale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate ABO determination is critical for safe deceased donor organ allocation. Weak or variant ABO subgroups may not be reliably detected by routine serologic testing, especially under urgent conditions or following transfusion. Such limitations can result in discrepant ABO assignments with potential implications for transplant safety.Study Design and MethodsWe describe a deceased organ donor with discordant ABO typing results reported by two laboratories. Serologic testing was performed using gel agglutination methods on independently collected specimens. Due to persistent discrepancies, ABO genotyping was performed using real‐time polymerase chain reaction to resolve the donor's blood group.ResultsInitial serologic testing identified the donor as AB RhD+ at one laboratory, while a second laboratory reported B RhD+ results. Repeat serologic evaluation demonstrated weak and variable A antigen reactivity, suggestive of a weak A subgroup. Molecular genotyping performed on specimens from both laboratories identified an AwB genotype, confirming the presence of a weak A allele. Because molecular results were not available prior to organ allocation and the discrepancy could not be resolved in real time, the donor was conservatively classified as AB for allocation purposes.DiscussionThis case highlights the vulnerability of routine serologic testing to misclassification of rare weak ABO subgroups. Integration of molecular genotyping with serologic testing can provide resolution of ABO discrepancies and enhance transplant safety. This case demonstrates a policy‐relevant gap in current ABO determination workflows for deceased donors. Lack of standardized inter‐laboratory data sharing and limited access to rapid molecular testing may unnecessarily restrict organ utilization.

BACKGROUND

In 2003, an accidental ABO‐incompatible transplant resulted in a patient death, which made national headlines and prompted additional safeguards to prevent future occurrences. 1 , 2 ABO subgroups and weak variants can complicate the determination of a deceased donor's blood type in a STAT fashion for organ allocation. Subgroups arise due to mutations that affect the activity of A and B glycosyltransferase enzymes, responsible for A and B antigen expression. 3 Group A exhibits the most heterogeneity of expression of A1, A2, and other weaker variants (e.g., A3, Ax, and Am). 4 These weaker subgroups may exhibit reduced or undetectable antigen expression during serological testing. 5

Many studies do not distinguish weak A subgroups (e.g., AwB) from other weak ABO subgroup combinations leading to the underestimation of their prevalence. Weak antigens are often not detected or under‐detected by routine blood typing, leading to underestimation of their true prevalence. 6 While AB is the rarest of blood groups (~4%–5% worldwide), AwB is even rarer and estimated to be less than 1% of the A and AB population. 7 Forward typing may not detect the A antigen, showing only B, which can mistakenly classify the person as group B instead of AB. Reverse typing may not detect anti‐A antibodies either, leading to inconsistent results. The use of molecular genotyping can help to identify these subgroups at the DNA level and help clarify discrepancies. 8 , 9

The determination of blood groups is particularly challenging in the deceased organ donor setting due to the STAT (urgent) nature of transplantation, along with prior transfusions that may have been administered to stabilize the donor. In the United States, the Organ Procurement Transplant Network (OPTN) published a guidance requiring the confirmation of ABO results using two independently drawn specimens. 10 Organ Procurement Organizations (OPO) must have a process to address conflicting or indeterminate primary blood type results. 11

Here we report a case involving a deceased organ donor with discordant ABO typing results between two laboratories. Our findings underscore the importance of confirming ABO results using both serological and molecular methods when discrepancies arise. This case also supports the cautious approach of assigning donors as AB when timely resolution is not possible.

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

Serological typing

Serological typing was performed using the IH‐24 platform (BioRad) with gel agglutination cards (BioRad).

DNA isolation and ABO genotyping

DNA was extracted using the EZ1 Advanced instrument (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's protocol. ABO genotyping was performed by real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) using the LinkSēq ABO Typing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Amplification and detection were conducted on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio™ 7 Pro Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data analysis was performed using the SureTyper software provided by the manufacturer (ISBT ABO database release version 1.1, January 2017).

The second laboratory had tested four specimens with distinct draw times.

To investigate further, we repeated ABO typing on all parent tubes of Specimen A (labeled A2–A5). Consistent AB RhD+ results were obtained for tubes A2, A3, and A4; however, tube A5 yielded discordant forward and reverse typing results that precluded a definitive ABO interpretation.

Weak A subgroups sometimes show the presence of naturally occurring anti‐A1 antibodies which further helps in classifying it as a weak “A” phenotype. In this case, we found a 2+ initial agglutination reaction at room temperature with anti‐A1 cells. When typing was repeated due to the reported result discrepancy, 2 of 4 tests displayed 1+ agglutination reactions (Table 1 A2–A4). In three of five tests performed, a 1+ agglutination reaction was observed with anti‐A reagent during forward typing, suggesting weak A expression (Table 1).

To further investigate the discrepancy, genotyping was performed using RT‐PCR using extracted DNA. Two draw times were chosen (Specimens A3 and E), one specimen from each laboratory. Both the assigned genotype and phenotype were found to be AwB which supported our laboratory's serological results. Weak A phenotypes such as Aw typically arise from one or a small number of nucleotide substitutions within the ABO glycosyltransferase gene that significantly reduce, but do not eliminate, enzymatic activity. 12 , 13

Because molecular typing could not be completed prior to organ allocation, and due to the unresolved ABO discrepancy, the donor was allocated as AB to avoid the risk of ABO‐incompatible transplantation, reinforcing the current OPTN guidance and supporting the integration of both serologic and molecular testing, when feasible, into donor evaluation workflows. 14 The donor remained on life support until recipients were determined. While the ABO discrepancy did not adversely impact the organs, it created unneeded confusion during this case, and several ABO tests were performed by multiple laboratories in an attempt to find a resolution.

We reviewed our laboratory quality data over a three‐year time frame. There was a 0.04% discrepancy rate as reported to us by OPOs, none of which were tied to laboratory error, further suggesting that STAT alternative typing methods should be implemented in labs that perform deceased donor testing.

DISCUSSION

Despite the critical importance of accurate blood group determination in organ transplantation, there is little published literature describing the real‐world challenges of resolving ABO discrepancies in deceased organ donors, particularly those arising from weak or variant ABO subgroups. Existing reports largely emphasize catastrophic ABO‐incompatible transplants or downstream policy responses rather than the analytical complexities that can produce ambiguous or conflicting serologic results in routine donor evaluation. This gap is significant because donor evaluation occurs under time constraints, many times after transfusion, and relies primarily on serologic methods that may not reliably detect weak antigen expression. This present case directly addresses this unmet need by documenting how a rare AwB phenotype produced discordant ABO results between two laboratories, highlighting the inherent vulnerability of standard serologic testing in the deceased donor setting.

Molecular typing has been proven to be a reliable method for the determination of blood groups. 15 , 16 By integrating serologic findings with confirmatory molecular genotyping, this case underscores the practical limitations of existing STAT testing workflows, in which molecular testing is often unavailable in real time. This case therefore, advances the field by illustrating how weak ABO subgroups can influence real‐time organ allocation decisions, exposing limitations in current donor testing paradigms and reinforcing the need for integrated serologic and molecular strategies to enhance transplant safety.

From a regulatory perspective, the AABB Standards for Blood Banks and Transfusion Services require confirmation of the ABO group of donor blood components by serologic testing from an integrally attached segment prior to release, with mandatory resolution of discrepancies before transfusion. 17 In the transplant setting, OPTN data highlights the clinical relevance of these safeguards. Between January 2012 and June 2013, 12.3% of reported safety events involved errors in processes related to ABO compatibility (n = 349 situations). 18 In response, OPTN implemented policy changes in 2016 mandating dual‐sample verification for deceased donors. 10 These safeguards were further strengthened in 2024, when ABO typing errors and discrepancies were formally incorporated into patient‐safety event reporting requirements, reflecting ongoing efforts to improve accuracy and reduce risk in organ allocation. 19

Biologically, this case highlights the challenges posed by weak A subgroups. While A1 and A2 phenotypes account for ~99% of “A” antigen phenotypes, the rest are weaker variants (e.g., Ax, A3, etc.) that are difficult to type serologically. In the context of AwB, weak A expression may yield absent or weak agglutination with anti‐A reagents during forward typing, resulting in apparent group B classification. Reverse typing may also fail to demonstrate expected anti‐A reactivity, further complicating interpretation. These serologic characteristics explain how AwB phenotypes can evade detection using routine testing algorithms and reinforce the need to consider weak subgroups when unexplained ABO discrepancies arise.

In the context of organ donation, timely and accurate ABO determination is critical to ensuring safe and appropriate organ allocation. When discrepancies cannot be resolved promptly, assigning the donor as AB remains the safest option to prevent ABO‐incompatible transplantation, even though this approach restricts the recipient pool and may reduce organ utilization. In the present case, misclassification of the donor as group B could have resulted in transplantation into an A or AB recipient, with the potential for severe hemolytic complications. This underscores the importance of prioritizing recipient safety in the face of unresolved ambiguity, while also recognizing the downstream consequences of conservative allocation strategies.

This case study exposes important limitations. We did not have access to the raw serologic data generated by the second testing laboratory; only final ABO interpretations were provided to our laboratory through the OPO. As a result, a detailed comparison of methodologies, reagent performance, and reaction strengths was not possible. More broadly, the responsibility for reconciling laboratory discrepancies should not rest with OPOs, whose role is to coordinate donor evaluation and allocation rather than to function as intermediaries for technical laboratory data exchange. Reliance on indirect communication introduces the risk of incomplete information transfer and interpretive loss at a critical decision point.

Notably, OPTN policy requires histocompatibility laboratories to resolve human leukocyte antigen (HLA) discrepancies. 20 No comparable requirement exists for laboratories performing ABO testing, despite the central role of ABO determination in transplant safety. The absence of standardized, mandatory inter‐laboratory communication for ABO discrepancies represents a modifiable system‐level vulnerability that can delay or preclude definitive resolution prior to organ allocation. The establishment of formal policy for transparent, bidirectional information exchange among laboratories performing ABO testing would strengthen safeguards and better ensure safe and equitable transplantation.

Although molecular genotyping provided definitive resolution of the discrepancy in this case, it was performed only after organ procurement and therefore could not inform real‐time allocation decisions. Had genotyping been available at the laboratory, results could have been issued within 3 h of test commencement. Broader access to rapid molecular testing technologies may help address this limitation, particularly given evidence that weak A subgroups are more prevalent than previously estimated when assessed by genotyping rather than serology alone. 13 Without molecular confirmation, donors with weak ABO subgroups may be misclassified, leading either to unsafe allocation, or unnecessary restriction of organ use.

In the time‐sensitive environment of deceased organ donation, accurate and timely ABO determination is essential to ensuring recipient safety and appropriate organ allocation. When serologic discrepancies cannot be resolved promptly, assigning the donor as AB remains the safest course of action; however, this conservative approach may restrict organ utilization when definitive resolution could otherwise be achieved. This case illustrates that weak ABO subgroups, while rare, pose a disproportionate risk for misclassification and allocation inefficiency when testing data are fragmented across laboratories. The absence of standardized, mandatory laboratory‐to‐laboratory data sharing for ABO discrepancies, particularly when contrasted with existing requirements for histocompatibility testing, represents a modifiable system‐level vulnerability. Broader access to rapid molecular genotyping, coupled with transparent information exchange among testing laboratories, would strengthen safeguards, reduce ambiguity, and better align ABO determination practices with the overarching goal of safe and equitable transplantation.
