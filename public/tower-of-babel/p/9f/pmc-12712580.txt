Urine Screening for Early Diagnosis of Young Individuals With Alport Syndrome: A Call for Action

Alport syndrome is an inherited disorder of basement membranes leading to progressive chronic kidney disease, hearing loss, and ocular abnormalities in many affected patients. When the authors started treating patients and studying Alport syndrome over 25 years ago, many nephrologists argued that it did not matter if you diagnosed the disease early because there was nothing you could do to treat it anyway. Since then, we have learned a significant amount about the role of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade in preserving kidney function in patients with Alport syndrome treated early through work with animal models and human registries. In 2003, it was reported that treatment of Alport mice with ramipril led to doubling of lifespan with the most benefit seen in mice treated before the onset of chronic kidney disease. The first evidence for benefit of RAAS inhibition in humans with Alport syndrome was reported in 2012, showing a correlation between earlier treatment with later onset of kidney failure.1 This finding has been replicated in a number of additional cohorts around the world, showing a similar benefit for treatment of patients with all genetic subtypes with the strongest benefit for patients treated early.2 Given these findings, in children aged ≥ 2 years, recent European clinical practice guidelines recommend RAAS blockade for males with X-linked Alport syndrome and all patients with autosomal recessive Alport syndrome at the time hematuria is detected and treatment when microalbuminuria is detected for girls with X-linked Alport syndrome or all patients with autosomal dominant Alport syndrome.3 Unfortunately, despite robust evidence of benefit, not all patients diagnosed with Alport syndrome are receiving recommended treatment. In a prospective natural history study of patients with Alport syndrome, only 81% were receiving RAAS inhibition at enrollment.4 In a randomized controlled trial of a novel drug to treat Alport syndrome, only 78% of enrolled patients were receiving RAAS inhibition.5 Although this is a major deficiency in the field, an equally important barrier to early treatment is lack of diagnosis or misdiagnosis. The manuscript by Kitakado et al.6 suggests a strategy of urine screening at the age of 3 years to identify patients with Alport syndrome and initiate treatment to preserve kidney function.

Different regions of the world have different recommendations for routine urine screening of asymptomatic children. In the United States, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended routine urine screening once at the age of 5 years and once in adolescence until 2007 when this was retired primarily because of concerns about cost and lack of potential for actionable results and benefits.7 Germany has a nationwide routine urine screening test for every child at the age of 4 years. However, 54 years after its implementation in 1971, the information provided to parents and pediatricians about the rationale for urine screening is completely outdated. Although a Germany-wide guideline from > 10 medical societies regulates the pathway of early detection of kidney disease in children and young adults in a legally secure manner since 2024,8 it has not yet found its way into the information used by health insurance companies and health lobbyists. In Japan, urine screening for children at the age of 3 years has been recommended since 1961. Kitakado et al.’s6 report on 356 children with genetically confirmed Alport syndrome who were diagnosed at a single large pediatric center between 2015 and 2024 and determined that 31.7% of them were initially diagnosed through the routine 3-year urine screening. Remarkably, 60.2% of those children diagnosed at the 3-year screen already met requirements for treatment per current guidelines. If diagnosis was not made at this routine screening, then patients were diagnosed when they developed gross or microscopic hematuria or were identified during clinical evaluation for other complaints. Importantly, 20.5% of patients had no family history of hematuria or kidney disease, thus Alport syndrome would have been unexpected in the children and likely diagnosed late. Treatment of children with Alport syndrome with RAAS inhibition at early stages of disease has the potential to delay the onset of kidney failure by ≥ 20 years. Registry data in the USA and Europe and experience in our own Alport outpatient clinics show that almost one-third of young adults still present for the first time with an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 ml/min and only then begin nephroprotective therapy, even though they have had hematuria or proteinuria diagnosed but not treated for a decade. In contrast, there is a small, very positive and motivating wave of patients who still have preserved kidney function in young adulthood, thanks to early diagnosis and treatment by pediatric nephrology. Many of these are former EARLY PRO-TECT patients with Alport syndrome. Now that effective treatments are available for this genetic condition, the economic case for routine urine screening needs to be reevaluated. A year of dialysis typically costs between $20,000 to $80,000 per person depending on what country you live in9; thus delay of kidney failure for ≥ 20 years for this cohort of patients could potentially reduce overall health care costs, not to mention the human benefits of delaying kidney failure on quality of life for affected patients.

Of course, universal screening of urine in children has well-documented potential downsides. Transient hematuria and proteinuria are common, and repeated sampling is required to identify patients most at risk for chronic kidney disease. Identification of hematuria or proteinuria in a child may lead to anxiety in parents or family members while evaluation is ongoing. This can be compounded by long waits for pediatric nephrology consultation because of workforce shortages. In addition, there are costs involved in screening and follow-up of patients with abnormal results. Despite these downsides, we strongly believe that early diagnosis of Alport syndrome through universal childhood urine screening is justified. Further prospective evaluations are needed to clarify the frequency of abnormal screening results in the general population, the frequency of Alport syndrome, and other potentially treatable diagnoses in screen-positive children, and the health economic evaluation of a childhood urine screening program.

With RAAS inhibition, Alport syndrome is now a treatable disease with an important treatment initiation window in childhood before the onset of significant kidney scarring. Multiple other therapeutic agents are currently being evaluated as add-on therapy. We are approaching the time when a diagnosis of Alport syndrome is no longer a sentence to eventual kidney failure. Early diagnosis and treatment are the key to making this a reality.
