Review of recent advances in the diagnosis and management of periprosthetic joint infection after total knee arthroplasty part 2: single-stage or two-stage surgical technique?
Authors: Jameel Suliman, Hamza Warda, Michel Samaan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriprosthetic joint infection (PJI) after total knee arthroplasty is a complication that affects approximately 2–3% of patients. The management of this issue is complicated and expensive for both the patients and the healthcare system. Multiple management options are available including antibiotic suppressive therapy, surgical management, and salvage procedures. Surgical management is considered a popular option for treating PJI, with multiple available surgical techniques, including single-stage revision arthroplasty and two-stage revision arthroplasty among others. Two-stage revision has been considered the gold standard for treating PJI. This method consists of two surgical procedures with a time interval in between, the first procedure aims to eradicate the infection along with implanting either a static or a mobile spacer, while the second intervention aims to remove the spacer and implant a new prothesis. During the interval period the patient is closely monitored through a handful of laboratory tests and clinical signs that help in assessing the optimal time of undertaking the second stage. However, in recent years, the single-stage method has gained much attention for its comparable outcomes and fewer complications. Contrary to the two-stage method, the single-stage approach consists only of one procedure in which the old infected prosthesis is removed and a new one is implanted. Many articles have compared the two methods over the years but have not agreed on a particular approach to be more potent in eliminating infection and providing better outcomes. Plenty of questions are yet to be answered regarding the two methods, including the superior type of spacer, interim period duration, and single-stage revision inclusion criteria. We herein, aim to address these issues, highlighting recent advances in managing this morbid complication and discussing controversial topics in the staged procedures.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Single-stage revision arthroplasty, Two-stage revision arthroplasty.

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common disease that affects 4–13% of the population [1]. A prevalence projected to increase in the coming decades, to affect approximately 25% of the adult population in the United States [2]. Severe cases of OA are usually managed with total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1], which is a procedure that aims to reconstruct the knee joint, thus restoring the normal weight-bearing physiology of its components [3]. Total knee arthroplasty is not only one of the most frequently performed elective surgeries worldwide [4], but is also speculated to only increase in popularity in the upcoming decades in most areas of the world [5–7]. Plenty of complications can follow TKA, however, aseptic loosening and infections remain the main two reasons for revision total knee arthroplasty (rTKA) [8, 9].

Multiple attempts have been made to define periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), the most prominent of which was made by the International Consensus Meeting (ICM), but a definite one is yet to be agreed upon in the literature [10–13]. PJI has a prevalence of 2–3% of TKA cases, which is suspected to increase in the coming years with the upward trend in the number of preformed arthroplasties [12]. PJI is also a costly complication for it sometimes necessitates multiple surgical procedures, prolonged hospitalization and antibiotic therapy [14]. Common manifestations of PJI are pain, erythema and swelling, while prosthetic loosening and sinus tract drainage are common only in chronic cases [15]. Periprosthetic joint infection is usually caused by bacteria, most commonly Staphylococcus epidermidis, with some cases reporting fungi-caused infections, which are in most instances, harder to eradicate than bacteria-caused ones [15].

Early diagnosis is crucial in cases of PJI as it can drastically change the prognosis and the method of treatment [15]. However, the diagnosis of periprosthetic infections is complicated by many factors and is yet to be ultimately determined despite multiple criteria having been suggested on several occasions. These criteria are based upon many factors such as clinical manifestations, blood tests (C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), D-dimer… etc.), imaging techniques, arthrocentesis, and intraoperative assessment [12, 15].

In general, PJI treatment is deemed complicated and should be planned for each patient, involving a multidisciplinary approach. The treatment is furthermore time-consuming and expensive [15], as a recent study showed that PJI costs will reach 1.1 billion dollars by the year 2030 in the United States [16]. Treatment options for PJI are plenty, including antibiotic suppressive therapy, which is a low-effect method that is only applied in a small cohort of patients who are unable to endure invasive surgical treatment methods [14]. Debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) is another viable method for treating PJI which is only indicated for acute cases in which symptoms have not been ongoing for more than four weeks [14].

Other treatment methods include single-stage exchange arthroplasty and two-stage exchange arthroplasty. Salvage procedures (resection, arthrodesis, amputation) on the other hand are another option for treatment, but they are usually preserved for patients unfit for surgery, or when one of the abovementioned methods fails to eradicate the infection [12].

Despite all the efforts to come up with new methods for treating PJI, two-stage exchange arthroplasty has always been considered the absolute gold standard. However, the single-stage approach has gained widespread attention recently [17, 18]. The main aim of this review is to offer a deep understanding of both the single and two-stage exchange procedures for treating knee PJI, delving into detail regarding controversies in those two approaches and comparing one to another.

Two-stage exchange procedures

A brief history and definition

Since it was first described by Insall et al. [19], many articles have confirmed the efficacy of the two-stage method in managing both early and late PJI cases [20, 21]. Two-stage revision arthroplasty consists of two surgical interventions, with a time interval that differs depending on serological and clinical signs of infection settlement. The first intervention aims to eradicate all signs of infection in the surgical area through implant removal and extensive debridement of all infected bone and soft tissue, followed by the implantation of either a static or a mobile spacer. During the time interval, the patient should be closely monitored both clinically and serologically. Then, only after the confirmation of infection eradication, the second stage takes place. During the second stage, the spacer is removed and a new prothesis is implanted [22]. This method offers plenty of advantages, such as the ability to provide a high localized antibiotic dosage through the implanted spacer and to perform a second debridement if needed [23]. In a recent systematic review, the rate of infection eradication using the two-stage exchange procedure ranged between 45% and 100%, averaging over 84%, while functional outcomes averaged around 77.8 on the Knee Society Score [21].

Preoperative planning

It is crucial to identify the causative organism preoperatively in PJI cases, for it influences the decision of whether to perform a single or a two-stage procedure, and the selection of the appropriate antibiotics utilized in the cement spacer [21]. This identification can be done by either joint aspiration, or biopsy (open or arthroscopic). Both methods are similarly effective. However, the combination of both increases the accuracy up to 90%. Thus, it is now recommended to combine both methods to obtain more accurate results [24–26].

Surgical technique of the first stage

First, the exposure of the infected joint is achieved using the original incision [23]. It is recommended to remove both the previous scar and the fistula, if present [18]. Then, all signs of infection should be eradicated, which requires vigorous surgical debridement and removal of all foreign materials, membranes, and all the tissue that is macroscopically involved [22]. Furthermore, copious irrigation and intramedullary reaming should be performed to ensure that no sign of infection is longer present [18]. A minimum of 6 L of normal saline is used with a high-pressure lavage system to eliminate necrotic tissue and microorganisms [18]. The surgeon should try to remove all the materials and involved tissues, without causing an extensive bone loss that can occur by violent extraction [18, 23]. If possible, antibiotics are withheld during the procedure up to the time of harvesting at least 3 and up to 5 or 6 intraoperative tissue samples [27], which then should be sent for aerobic and anaerobic cultures for a definitive diagnosis [23]. It’s important to take samples from different areas of the surgical field [27]. Additionally, sending the prosthesis itself to be analyzed can amplify the chances of isolating the causative organism [23]. Once debridement is complete, the re-draping and the changing of gloves, gowns, and surgical instruments should take place to prepare for the next and last step of the first stage, which is the installation of the spacer [18, 23].

The spacer

The main aim of using an antibiotic-loaded cement spacer is to deliver a high localized dose of antibiotics to the infected area, and fill the joint cavity between stages. Thus, maintaining the well-being of both the bone and soft tissue of the infected joint [28]. There are two main types of spacers; articulating (dynamic) and non-articulating (static). The decision of whether to use a static or a dynamic spacer is complicated, but should be made considering the individuality of each case along with the following factors [29]:

Most current evidence suggests the general utilization of a dynamic spacer, with keeping static spacers to be indicated only in specific situations such as uncontrolled severe infections, ligamentous laxity (compromise of the collateral ligament), extensor mechanism insufficiency and severe bone loss or soft tissue compromise [30].

The main advantage of a static spacer is that it guarantees more stability to the knee joint, especially in cases where severe bone loss is present. Whereas an articulating spacer guarantees better motion between the two stages, causing fewer complications and faster recovery [31, 32]. Some papers reported similar functional scores using the two types of spacers [28–30, 33]. While others reported better outcomes with dynamic ones [31, 32, 34].

Concerning infection eradication, most reports have shown similar results between articulating and static spacers [28–31, 34–36], but some papers have reported better outcomes when using articulating ones [30, 37].

Finally, although dynamic spacers offer less bone loss [28, 30, 32, 37], fewer complications [30, 32], and more patient satisfaction postoperatively [31], static spacers still offer some advantages, including cheaper costs [30–32], better joint fixation due to the use of intramedullary rods [32], and easier implantation intraoperatively [32]. It is worth mentioning that most of these results are prone to bias because cases, where static spacers are used, are generally more complicated and accompanied with worse bone stock and greater infection severity. It is also worth mentioning that in 2003, Jhao et al. reported good results when performing two-stage revision without using cement spacers in between stages [38].

The interim period

The main purpose of the interim period is to confirm the eradication of infection prior to reimplantation [39]. “How long is an interval period?” is a question that is yet to be answered in the literature, though it is commonly suggested to range between a couple of weeks and up to a few months [40]. Herein, we will discuss two of the most controversial topics regarding the interim period.

The optimal duration of the antibiotic regimen

When Insall et al. first described two-stage revision, a prolonged 6-week intravenous antibiotic regimen was suggested during the interim period [19]. However, the duration of this regimen is nowadays dependent on clinical progress, and laboratory values, and is individualized according to each patient’s case [18]. A prolonged systemic antibiotic therapy carries a lot of concerns, such as higher costs, medication-related side effects (i.e., nephropathy) [41], and increased antibiotic resistance. The benefit of this regimen is also questionable due to the postoperative reduced blood supply to the operated area [42].

Using a combination of short-term systemic antibiotic therapy along with an antibiotic-loaded spacer was proven to be equivalent in most reports to the results seen when using a prolonged systemic antibiotic regimen [41, 43]. The optimal duration of interim antibiotic regimens is yet to be determined but commonly ranges between two and six weeks, taking into consideration the specificity of each case [18, 44].

Antibiotic therapy is usually followed by an antibiotic-free period of around four weeks, during which the patient is also monitored serologically and clinically [18]. This period is known as a “drug holiday”, and it aims to manifest the persistent infection if present [23, 39]. Although the drug holiday is a common practice, much evidence has recently shown no significant difference if no drug-free period is applied [39].

The optimal time for prosthesis reimplantation

Despite all the efforts to identify one test or marker that can reliably determine the optimal timing of reimplantation, we have yet to find one [40]. Inflammatory markers such as the ESR, CRP, and white blood cell (WBC) count are currently used the most to monitor patients during the interim period [39]. Traditionally, a downward trend of these markers is used to predict the eradication of infection [39], however, more trials with larger sample sizes should be conducted to confirm their efficacy. These markers have plenty of shortcomings such as their low sensitivity and specificity [45, 46], and the absence of specific thresholds to make accurate predictions [39]. Moreover, it was shown in a recent study that delta changes in CRP and WBC count are inadequate for deciding to perform a reimplantation [47].

Some reports have suggested the use of coagulation-related markers, such as D-dimer and fibrinogen to predict infection eradication, but both have shown unsatisfactory results [39]. However, recent papers have shown more promising outcomes [48, 49], yet more trials are still needed to confirm their efficacy [48].

Synovial fluid investigations such as synovial fluid culture, synovial leukocyte count, and polymorphonuclear neutrophil percentage have also been suggested to determine infection eradication. Despite the importance of synovial fluid culture in the preoperative workup, it has shown disappointing results in this context due to its poor sensitivity [46, 50, 51]. Conversely, the results for synovial fluid leukocyte counts and polymorphonuclear neutrophil proportions (PMN)% are conflicting, as the sensitivity of the leukocyte count ranged between 10% and 50% in some papers and between 75% and 82% in others, with a higher specificity of 70-79%. Research regarding PMN% have shown similar results, following a similar pattern as leukocyte count, resulting in suboptimal accuracy for both tests in predicting the eradication of infection [39].

The use of alternative biomarkers such as alpha-defensin and leukocyte esterase has also been suggested but has been associated with poor and unsatisfactory outcomes [39].

A more recent systematic review showed that among all the markers available to predict infection persistence between the two stages, synovial leukocyte esterase, serum interleukin 6, synovial polymorphonuclear percentage, synovial fluid culture, and spacer sonication fluid culture were the tests that proved to have the highest accuracy in making optimal decisions [52].

To conclude, the best current approach we possess to decide the optimal timing of reimplantation necessitates the evaluation of each patient individually, depending on the disappearance of clinical signs of infection along with utilizing a combination of the locally available diagnostic tests [52]. A downward trend in serological tests that do not need to reach normal values can be of benefit, however, the poor accuracy of such markers should be kept in mind [39]. More trials are needed to prove the efficacy of some markers, especially more promising ones such as serum IL-6 and synovial leukocyte esterase [52].

Finally, the interim period should not be prolonged, as a large body of evidence shows good results by using a shorter 2–4-week interval period [39, 53]. It has also been suggested that a longer interval can lead to a greater risk of reinfection, higher costs, and worse functional and clinical outcomes compared to a shorter interval [39, 54–57].

Surgical technique of the second stage

The objectives of the second stage are removing the spacer, performing debridement and lavage, collecting tissue samples, and then eventually reimplantation [23]. As mentioned previously, the performance of the second stage is a decision that should be made only when complete infection eradication is confirmed [18].

The old scar is used to inter the knee joint [23, 58]. Synovectomy is performed if necessary, and samples are taken using clean instruments for examination [58]. The spacer is then removed carefully to ensure the preservation of the maximum amount of the surrounding bone [23]. Then careful examination of the knee joint for any visible sign of infection should take place, with performing another round of debridement and irrigation with pulsed lavage [23, 58]. Bone defects should be addressed, and the use of cement augmentation, bone grafts, or metal augments should be taken into careful consideration [58]. Finally, the implantation of the new prosthesis should be done [58]. Choosing the type of prosthesis for reimplantation (cemented or uncemented) is not always simple. It’s true that cemented fixation has been more popular [23], for it guarantees better local antibiotic delivery [58], though uncemented stems also offer some advantages, including better biological integration and easier reimplantation [58]. Furthermore, infection rates and revision for aseptic loosening were recently found to be higher in cemented revision arthroplasties when compared to uncemented ones [59, 60].

Postoperatively, antibiotic therapy should be continued until intraoperative cultures are examined. The results of these cultures are then assessed by a multidisciplinary team which advises on the optimal antibiotic therapy [58]. However, a shorter oral antibiotic therapy was proven to be superior after a two-stage exchange as it decreases reinfection rates at one-year follow-up, as reported recently [61].

It is highly important to note that infection persistence and severe comorbidities are contraindications to proceeding to the second stage [23]. Furthermore, if there is a high suspicion of infection persistence intraoperatively, another aggressive debridement should be carried out with the reimplantation of another cemented spacer, thereby, repeating the first-stage procedure [44].

Limitations of the two-stage revision arthroplasty

There are still plenty of disadvantages that should be addressed regarding the two-stage method, the most prominent of which is the high reinfection rates following the procedure. A recent trial demonstrated that the success rate of two-stage exchange in both the hip and knee joints was lower than expected, averaging around only 80.6% for patients undergoing the two stages [62]. These numbers are suspected to decrease with the continuous emergence of antibiotic-resistant organisms [63]. Failure of the two-stage method is more likely in patients with specific risk factors, such as male sex, positive intraoperative samples, and fungal and mycobacterial infections [64]. Furthermore, no management method of a failed two-stage revision shows consistent results, since although arthrodesis lowers the risk of repeated infection, it can lead to more complications and worse functional outcomes compared to other methods [63]. The emergence of more preferable alternative modalities such as the single-stage method can also be considered as a disadvantage to the two-stage procedure.

Single-stage exchange arthroplasty

Brief history and definition

Despite that the single-stage exchange has only recently drawn attention, it was first described by Buchholz as early as the 1970s and later reported by Borden and Gearen in 1987[65, 66]. This method follows a “2-in-1” approach, which indicates that two procedures are performed under one anesthetic with a break in between [64]. Some other protocols suggest using a “pure single-stage method” where no break is taken between implant removal and reimplantation [67].

The infection eradication rates of the single-stage approach were reported to average around 87.1%[21]. In 2014, Haddad et al. even reported a reinfection rate of (0%) after a mean follow-up of 6.5 years for carefully selected patients treated with the single-stage approach [68].

Indications and contraindications

Haddad et al. showed in their paper that strict patient selection criteria can significantly affect the outcomes of a single-stage procedure [68]. For that very reason, indications and contraindications for the single-stage method are of high importance and should always be taken into consideration to reach optimal results. A recent systematic review has summarized the complete list of indications and contra-indications for the single stage approach [69], which were described on many occasions and by multiple institutions. Their results showed better infection eradication in the group with no inclusion criteria, contradicting other results reported in the literature [70].

Surgical technique

To guarantee the success of this surgical technique it’s crucial to identify the causative organism, administer tailored preoperative intravenous antibiotics [71], and ensure the complete removal of all foreign and infected materials and tissue [66].

The surgery should be performed under general, spinal, or subdural anesthesia [72], and is classically divided into two main parts, debridement and reconstruction, with a small 20-minute interval in between [72]. A thigh tourniquet is used during the procedure and should be inflated before initiation [71, 72]. The previous most lateral scar is used to expose the joint using a medial parapatellar approach. The sinus tract should be excised if present along with the scar [71]. It has been reported that both a tibial crest ostomy and an extensile medial parapatellar approach can be used whenever access is complicated [72]. Antibiotics should be withheld initially until intraoperative fluid and tissue samples are collected and sent for microbiological and histological assessment [72]. After the tissue samples are collected, antibiotics should be administered according to the preoperative plan, which was previously determined by the microbiologist [73]. The removal of the existing implant should then take place using instruments that minimize bone loss. After the implant is fully removed, radical debridement should be initiated, any infected or necrotic tissue should be removed, and foreign materials should also be taken out along with any infected bone parts [71, 72]. This aggressive debridement is highly important because it is considered the cornerstone for the success of the procedure [74]. Pulsatile lavage should be undertaken throughout the procedure [66, 74], especially after debridement and implant removal are finished [71–73]. Once all macroscopically infected and necrotic parts are removed, the wound should be loosely closed and peripherally bandaged [68, 69]. Tranexamic acid is intravenously administered, and the tourniquet is deflated prior to the 20-minute interval [71, 72]. During this interval, the surgical team should re-scrub, new instruments and materials should be introduced, and the operation theater should be cleaned [71, 72]. The sterility in this technique is ensured by re-draping and changing the surgical instruments within the break between the two parts [19]. The tourniquet is re-inflated prior to the initiation of the second part of the procedure [71, 72]. If needed, another round of irrigation and debridement is performed prior to reimplantation. Bone loss is assessed by the primary surgeon, and then the bone is reconstructed accordingly [72]. Finally, definitive implantation is performed, and antibiotics are again delivered [71–73]. The components are then cemented in place, and another dose of tranexamic acid is administered [72].

The postoperative antibiotic regimen includes systemic antibiotic administration for 10 to 14 days, which is a short period compared to the popular two-stage’s 6 weeks, and that, in itself, is another advantage for single-stage revision. Finally, physiotherapy is recommended during the first eight days to encourage an early aggressive mobilization while keeping in mind the individuality of each case according to the integrity of the surrounding bone and soft tissue [66].

Comparison between single-stage and two-stage methods

Traditionally, two-stage revision arthroplasty has been considered the gold standard for treating knee PJI [17, 18]. Limitations of this method are of high importance, and should be considered and discussed with patients before the procedure. A viable alternative to this approach is the single-stage revision method, and multiple papers have been published comparing the outcomes of single and two-stage revision arthroplasties [17, 21, 75–78]. In 2009, Jämsen et al. published a systematic review in which they found better eradication of infection rates using two-stage revisions, they also found no difference between both types of revision regarding clinical outcomes [75]. Similar results were reported by Romanò et al. in 2012, showing that two-stage revisions lead to better rates of infection eradication [76]. In 2016, Nagra et al. published a systematic review that revealed no significant difference between the single-stage and two-stage revision groups regarding infection eradication. Nevertheless, they conducted a subgroup analysis in which they only included papers published after the year 2000 and found significantly lower reinfection rates when using the single-stage approach [77]. They also reported the functional results of Haddad et al. favoring the single-stage approach [68]. In 2016, Kunutsor et al. conducted another meta-analysis and found that reinfection rates were higher in two-stage revisions, and functional outcomes were similar between the two approaches [78]. More recently, in 2019, another systematic review found no difference regarding infection eradication or functional outcomes between the two procedures [21]. Finally, a systematic review was published in 2024 and revealed no difference in reinfection or reoperation rates between single and two-stage revision arthroplasties [17]. Table 3.

The results of all the above-mentioned studies are indeed conflicting, which is why it is really important to conduct randomized clinical trials comparing the efficacy of the two approaches. It is clear that more recent papers have reported better outcomes when using the single-stage revision. This can be due to a better patient selection in recent years. Notably, a randomized multicenter clinical trial study protocol was published in 2021 aiming to compare the two approaches [79]. Finally, considering the similarity between the two surgical methods regarding reinfection rates and functional outcomes, along with the limitations of the two-stage revision including longer hospitalization, higher costs, more complications, worse patient satisfaction, longer immobilization time, and greater risk and exposure to surgical trauma [17], we may say that the single-stage method represents a viable alternative to the two-stage approach with similar results and fewer complications. The process of choosing a surgical approach, however, should be made on a case-by-case basis considering the patient’s details and preferences, along with the characteristics of the surgical site [17], and the abovementioned patient selection criteria for single-stage procedures [21].

1.5 Stage exchange arthroplasty

In 2021, Hernandez et al. described the 1.5-stage exchange approach to treat knee PJI. In this approach, the infected prosthesis is removed, and an articulating spacer is implanted with the intent of keeping the spacer in situ as long as the patient can tolerate it. The advantages of such a procedure are dependent on whether the patient requires a second surgery or not. The patient in this case can either avoid undergoing a second procedure if tolerated the spacer, or can undergo a spacer removal if not. The spacer removal procedure here would be way less invasive and costly compared to an implant removal in case of the alternate failed traditional staged revision. Furthermore, the patient is usually functional between the two procedures which helps in choosing a time that fits their schedule for the second surgical intervention [80]. Furthermore, when the 1.5-stage method was compared to the outcomes of the two-stage procedure in 2022, a difference of 10.1% regarding infection-free survival was reported in favor of the 1.5-stage method [81].

Conclusion

Surgical management remains the predominant approach for treating knee periprosthetic joint infections. Although multiple surgical methods have been proposed, two-stage revision arthroplasty is still considered the gold standard for PJI management. One key factor for the success of the surgical approach is the preoperative identification of the causative organism, which is recommended to be obtained by a combination of biopsy and aspiration. The interim period of the two-stage approach should not be prolonged with most evidence also leaning towards a shorter antibiotic regimen. Additionally, re-implantation optimal timing should depend on local manifestations of infection settlement, in conjunction with a downtrend of the results of locally available serological and biological laboratory tests. On the other hand, recent evidence suggests that single-stage revision may yield comparable or even superior infection eradication and functional outcomes (in some cases), with fewer complications and reduced costs. The choice between these methods should be carefully deliberated by a multidisciplinary team, considering each patient’s specific requirements and circumstances, based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria for each approach.

More trials are warranted to directly compare the outcomes of the two procedures, explore reliable diagnostic tests to confirm infection persistence during the interim period and look into newer treatment modalities such as the promising 1.5-stage exchange arthroplasty.
