Management and outcomes of people presenting with new diabetes-related foot infections: a prospective observational study from Australia and New Zealand
Authors: Edward Raby, Laurens Manning, Kate McCarthy, Emma J Hamilton, Ayesha Akram, Natasha Bertschi, Hasan Bhally, Keat Choong, Annabelle Donaldson, Stephen Guy, Katy Lai, Chris Lemoh, Lyn-Li Lim, Christian McGrath, Benjamin Mulo, Ohide Otome, Marjoree Sehu, Omar Shum, Parind Vora, Cara Westphal, Jennifer Wong, Peter A Lazzarini, Robert J Commons, On behalf of DINGO Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInfections precede nearly all hospitalizations and amputations related to diabetes-related foot disease. Focusing on antibiotic management, we aimed to report the outcomes of people presenting with a new diabetes-related foot infection (DFI).Research design and methodsIn this prospective study conducted in Australian and New Zealand hospitals between 2018 and 2020, patients with diabetes, a foot ulcer and a newly diagnosed DFI were eligible for enrollment. DFI was categorized as mild or moderate/severe DFI, with the latter further categorized according to the presence of osteomyelitis. The primary outcome was healing of the index ulcer or amputation site at 6 months. Multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusted for potential confounders were undertaken to investigate the relationship between ulcer healing and infection severity, intravenous antibiotic duration and total antibiotic duration.ResultsOf 234 included patients presenting to 20 centers (mean age 61 years, 77% male), 15% had mild DFI, 31% moderate or severe DFI without osteomyelitis, and 54% moderate or severe DFI with osteomyelitis. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus was identified in 7% (16/214) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa in 3% (7/214). Median (IQR) days of total antibiotic management were 18 (10, 22) for mild DFI, 20 (11, 40) for moderate or severe DFI without osteomyelitis and 34 (15, 51) for moderate or severe DFI with osteomyelitis. Healing at 6 months was 73% (22/30) in those with mild DFI, 68% (42/62) in moderate or severe infections without osteomyelitis and 62% (69/111) in moderate or severe DFI with osteomyelitis. After adjusting for confounders, none of infection severity, intravenous antibiotic duration or total antibiotic duration were associated with ulcer healing.ConclusionsHealing at 6 months following DFI does not appear to be associated with infection severity or antibiotic management. To plan clinical trials of antibiotic therapy for DFI, further work is required to define target subgroups and meaningful trial endpoints.Keywords: Diabetic Foot, Infections, Wound Healing, Foot Ulcer

Introduction

Globally, diabetes-related foot ulcers (DFU) affect around 20 million people each year and are the leading cause of hospitalization, amputation, and disease burdens in people with diabetes.1,3 In Australia, there are an estimated 51 000 people with DFU, resulting in 47 100 hospitalizations, 5250 minor amputations (below ankle), 1050 major amputations (above ankle), and 2500 deaths each year, at a direct annual cost of $A2.7 billion.4 Australia has comparatively high rates of DFU-related hospitalizations and minor amputations in people with diabetes, but low rates of major amputations.3 5 6

The likelihood of healing DFU is dependent on the evidence-based multidisciplinary management of infection, ischemia, pressure offloading, debridement and dressings.7 8 Around 40% of DFUs are complicated by incident diabetes-related foot infections (DFI),9 and infections precede nearly all DFU-related hospitalizations and minor amputations, and most major amputations.310,12 DFI are also associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events13 and significantly worse quality of life than DFU without infection.14 Additional challenges are faced by First Nations peoples of Australia and New Zealand, who suffer higher rates of major limb amputation,15 16 highlighting the urgent need to address the systemic factors and historical impacts that contribute to disparities in health and well-being while supporting inherent strengths in culture and community to achieve health equity.11

DFI range in severity from mild soft tissue infections surrounding an ulcer through to severe sepsis and necrotizing infections of deep tissue structures with osteoarticular involvement.17 The DFI classification system from the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF)/Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) is the most widely used classification system for DFI and classifies severity into four strata: (1) no infection; (2) mild infection, not extending ≥2 cm from wound margin or involving tissues deeper than skin and subcutaneous tissue; (3) moderate infection, extending ≥2 cm from wound margin and/or involving deeper structures; and (4) severe infection, any foot infection with associated systemic manifestations of sepsis.10 11 Moderate and severe infections are further delineated by the presence of osteomyelitis. Amputation rates have consistently been found to increase with worsening severity scores from approximately 3% for mild infections to 70% for severe infections.1018,20

For practicing infectious disease (ID) physicians, DFI accounts for up to 20% of all consultations and are considered core business.21 In spite of the heavy disease burden, current guidelines10 11 are limited by a lack of published clinical trials comparing standard antibiotic regimens22 and substantial variation in causative organisms with differing antimicrobial resistance profiles.11 23 24 Empiric antibiotic choices are dependent on local guidelines, but are also influenced by a trade-off between the clinical imperative to prescribe ‘broad spectrum’ antibiotics that are active against the majority of infecting pathogens and a desire to prescribe the narrowest spectrum antibiotic possible to reduce the impact on the intestinal microbiome and/or selection of more resistant pathogens.23 25 As a consequence, antibiotic prescribing practice for DFI is heterogeneous locally and globally. The heterogeneity of management was highlighted in a study where 106 Australasian ID physicians were asked to provide their management of a standardized hypothetical scenario. 82 distinct antimicrobial regimens were recommended, with variations in the total duration of therapy, duration of intravenous antibiotics and the choice and spectrum of antimicrobial activity.21

There is a need to understand current prescribing practices for DFI and their correlation with clinical outcomes. In this multicenter, prospective observational study, we aimed to describe DFI management in an Australasian context, including prescribing practices and outcomes. The findings of this study will help identify research questions with appropriate equipoise which could inform the design of future randomized controlled trials, in addition to shedding new light on key controversies about DFI identified in the IWGDF/IDSA 2023 guidelines.10

Methods

Study sites and ethics

The Diabetic Foot Infection Longitudinal Outcome study was a prospective binational multicenter cohort study of patients presenting with a new DFI run under the auspices of the Australasian Society of Infectious Diseases Clinical Research Network across 20 sites in Australia and New Zealand between 18 August 2018 and 29 November 2020. Each site was permitted to recruit up to 50 participants. The study was closed following diversion of resources to COVID-19. Data were collected on an electronic database (REDCap v10.6.28, Vanderbilt University) and reported according to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (online supplemental file 2).

Participants

Patients were eligible for enrollment if they were adults (aged 18 years or older) and had diabetes mellitus, a foot ulcer of any duration, and acute signs or symptoms of foot ulcer infection that had been present for less than 2 weeks. A DFU was defined as a break in the skin of the foot beyond the epidermis in a person with diabetes mellitus.17 Patients could be either inpatients or outpatients but were excluded if the infection was a recurrent or relapsed infection of the index foot wound. Other exclusion criteria included hospitalization at the time of recruitment for an unrelated illness, pregnancy or breastfeeding, inability to consent or declined consent, or participation in a blinded or experimental arm of an interventional trial.

Variables

Over 30 explanatory variables were collected at baseline using a combination of physical examination of the patients, patient interview and medical record review. Variables were grouped into the domains of demographics, medical history, lower limb history, ulcer and infection, and management characteristics (fully defined in online supplemental table S1). Subsequent management variables were collected from patient interview and medical records at the time of outcome assessments (online supplemental table S1).

Explanatory variables of interest included infection severity, intravenous antibiotic duration, and total antibiotic duration (online supplemental table S1). At the time of study enrollment, infection severity was classified using the IWGDF/IDSA guidelines as mild, moderate or severe (online supplemental table S1).10 For this study, three categories were used: mild, moderate or severe without osteomyelitis, and moderate or severe with osteomyelitis (online supplemental table S1). Moderate and severe infections were considered as one group of patients given that the presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome commonly settles within 48 hours. The combined group of moderate and severe infections was further classified according to the presence of presumed or proven osteomyelitis at the time of first presentation or within 7 days.

With intravenous antibiotics, a short duration was defined as less than 7 days and extended duration as 7 days or more. For the total antibiotic duration, a duration less than 2.5 weeks (ie, <18 days) was considered to be a short course, a duration of 2.5 to <6.5 weeks was considered a medium course and a duration of 6.5 weeks or more was considered a long course. These durations were chosen to be consistent with the contemporaneous Australian recommendations for treatment of mild DFI, complicated mild or moderate DFI or an extended duration beyond most recommendations, respectively.11

Outcomes

The primary outcome was healing of index ulcer with or without minor amputation at 6 months. Healing was defined as epithelialization sustained for 2 weeks at either the index ulcer or the site of a minor amputation.17 26 Secondary outcomes were (1) minor lower extremity amputation (through or distal to the ankle), (2) major lower extremity amputation (proximal to the ankle) at 6 months,17 (3) healed index ulcer without amputation site at 6 months (defined as complete epithelialization and absence of infection), (4) resolution of infection at 6 weeks (defined as an IWGDF/IDSA infection score of 1 with no clinical signs or symptoms of infection in the index ulcer, or surgical wound if an amputation has occurred) or (5) death at 6 months.

Outcomes were collected at 6 weeks (±2 weeks) and 6 months (±1 month) from index presentation using patient interview, patient examination and medical records recorded as part of usual clinical care. If no medical records were available at 6 months, the patient and/or their general practitioner was contacted to complete the outcome assessment through a verbal report to the investigator.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was undertaken using R V.4.3.1. There was no imputation for missing data, with the exception of death and major limb amputation being carried forward. Only patients with outcomes recorded at the time of follow-up were analyzed. Categorical variables were described by frequency, and continuous variables were described by central tendency and dispersion depending on normality assumptions. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were undertaken for healing of the index ulcer at 6 months for infection severity. The model was adjusted for age, sex, presence of prior infection, presence of minor amputation within 6 months, the wound, ischaemia, foot infection classification system (WIfI) wound grade and WIfI ischemia grade with random effects for study site, based on a directed acyclic graph (online supplemental figure S1). Antibiotic route and duration were assessed separately for each infection severity category due to expected differences.

Results

Overall, 238 patients were enrolled from 20 sites across Australia and New Zealand (online supplemental figure S2), with four patients excluded due to missing data for DFI severity. Of the remaining 234 included patients, mean age was 61 years (SD 12), 180 (77%) were male, and 9 (4%) were identified as First Nations peoples of Australia, 7 (3%) as Māori and 6 (3%) as Pacific peoples. There were 215 (92%) patients enrolled in Australia, 188 (80%) were inpatients at the time of enrollment and 205 (88%) had type 2 diabetes. Methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) was cultured in 89 (42%) of 214 patients, compared with methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) in 16 (7%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa in 7 (3%). A 6-month outcome was recorded for 203 (87%) patients.

Severity of infection

At enrollment, 34 (15%) had mild DFI, 73 (31%) moderate or severe DFI without osteomyelitis, and 127 (54%) moderate or severe DFI with osteomyelitis. Patients with different infection severities had similar baseline characteristics, except patients with mild infections, who had lower HbA1c measurements at enrollment and more prior revascularization (table 1).

Of 127 patients with osteomyelitis at enrollment, 80 (63%) had a positive probe to bone test. Osteomyelitis was diagnosed after 7 days in 4 (12%) patients with mild infections at enrollment and 14 (19%) patients with moderate to severe infections. Patients with osteomyelitis were more often treated with extended intravenous antibiotics (64/125 (51%) vs without osteomyelitis 29/98 (30%)) and with a long total duration of antibiotics (65/125 (52%) vs without osteomyelitis 30/98 (31%)).

Arterial revascularization was more commonly performed in patients with mild infection (6/34 (21%) vs moderate to severe infection 19/175 (11%)), whereas surgery within 7 days was more common in patients with moderate to severe infection (102/219 (47%) vs mild infection 5/34 (15%)).

Healing of the index wound with or without minor amputation at 6 months occurred in 22 (73%) of 30 patients with mild infections, 42 (68%) of 62 patients with moderate or severe infections without osteomyelitis and 69 (62%) of 111 patients with osteomyelitis at baseline.

After adjusting for confounders, infection severity was not associated with healing of the index ulcer or minor amputation site at 6 months. The only factor associated with non-healing at 6 months was a severe WIfI wound grade compared with a mild WIfI wound grade (adjusted OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.90; online supplemental table S2).

Resolution of infection at 6 weeks occurred in similar percentages of mild severity infections (18/34 (67%)), moderate or severe without osteomyelitis (40/73 (69%)) and moderate or severe with osteomyelitis (76/127 (68%)). Minor amputations were more common in patients with osteomyelitis (68/127 (57%)) than those without (19/106 (18%)), and the only two major amputations were in patients with osteomyelitis. Intravenous antibiotic duration

A short course of intravenous antibiotics (≤7 days) was given to 130 (60%) of 218 patients versus extended therapy to 88 (40%; online supplemental table S3). Of patients receiving extended intravenous antibiotics, the majority were inpatients (84/93 (88%)) and diagnosed with osteomyelitis (64/93 (69%)). Intravenous antibiotics were rarely used for mild infections, and typically a short course was given (23/32 (72%)). Patients with moderate to severe infections without osteomyelitis were more often given short intravenous antibiotics (46/66 (69%)) than those with osteomyelitis (61/125 (49%)). Patients receiving extended intravenous antibiotics were more likely to undergo surgical debridement within 7 days (49/88 (56%) vs no surgery 53/130 (41%)).

Healing of the index ulcer with or without amputation at 6 months occurred in 75 (67%) of 112 patients receiving short course and in 56 (66%) of 85 patients receiving extended intravenous antibiotics. Resolution of infection at 6 weeks occurred in similar percentages of patients receiving a short duration (74/111 (67%)) and long duration of intravenous antibiotics (55/80 (71%)). Those who required amputations were more often prescribed long-duration intravenous antibiotics (minor: 43/88 (49%) vs 43/130 (33%); major: 2/88 (2.3%) vs 0/130 (0%)) (table 4).

Due to expected differences in treatment, total intravenous antibiotic duration was assessed by infection severity. On univariable analysis, there was no significant association between total intravenous antibiotic duration and outcome stratified by infection severity (online supplemental table S4).

Total antibiotic duration

A short duration (≤2.5 weeks) of total antibiotics was given to 130 (36%) of 223 patients compared with a medium duration (>2.5 to ≤6.5 weeks) to 47 (21%) and long duration (>6.5 weeks) to 95 (43%; online supplemental table S5). Of the patients given a long total duration of antibiotics, 17/95 (18%) had a severe WIfI wound grade compared with 2/47 (4%) given moderate durations and 2/81 (2%) given a short total duration of antibiotics. Osteomyelitis was present more often in those receiving a long total duration (65/95 (68%) vs 24/47 (51%) for moderate and 36/81 (44%) for short). Short-duration antibiotics were more common in mild infection (16/32 (50%)) than in patients with osteomyelitis (36/125 (29%)). Surgical debridement was more common in those receiving moderate-duration (14/47 (30%)) and long-duration (22/95 (23%)) compared with short-duration antibiotics (11/81 (14%)), and early MRSA therapy was associated with long-duration antibiotics (31/95 (33%)).

Healing of the index ulcer with or without amputation at 6 months was observed in 40 (61%) of 66 patients receiving a short total duration, 32 (74%) of 43 receiving a medium total duration and 59 (67%) of 88 receiving a long total duration of antibiotics. Similar percentages of patients receiving short, medium or long total duration of antibiotics had resolution of infection at 6 weeks, amputations and healing of the index ulcer without amputation at 6 months (table 5).

Discussion

This prospective study describes a geographically diverse cohort of patients with acute DFI from Australia and New Zealand. Despite inclusion in the study being restricted to patients with acute signs or symptoms, more than 30% of patients did not have infection resolution at 6 weeks, and a third of patients did not have a healed wound at 6 months. After adjustment for confounders, none of infection severity, the use of intravenous antibiotics or total antibiotic duration were associated with ulcer healing at 6 months.

The proportion of unhealed wounds 6 months after initial infection was similar in patients with mild severity infection (27%) and moderate or severe infection without osteomyelitis (32%) or with osteomyelitis (38%). The ulcer healing rates reported are similar to those reported in cohorts following patients with DFU with infection.27 28 However, a minor amputation was undertaken more commonly in patients with moderate to severe infection with osteomyelitis than without (57% vs 19%). The 15% prevalence of new minor amputations was less than previous Australian cohorts,24 29 which may reflect the acute nature of the initial infection. Finally, the need for a major amputation was rare, occurring in just two patients with moderate or severe infection with associated osteomyelitis. This is less than the prevalence of major amputations reported in other Australian cohorts of patients with diabetes-related infection, although these cohorts included chronic infection and had the potential for longer follow-up.24 29

There has been increasing evidence to support the early switch of intravenous to oral antibiotics in a variety of bone and joint infections including DFI.30 The current study suggested that there was substantial heterogeneity in the duration of intravenous and total antibiotics in each of the different infection severity categories. After adjusting for potential confounders, there was no apparent association with intravenous duration (or total antibiotic duration) and ulcer healing at 6 months in mild severity infections or infections with osteomyelitis. While not statistically significant, the point estimate of the odds for ulcer healing was reduced in moderate to severe infections without osteomyelitis given a short intravenous duration or a short total duration of antibiotics. This reduction may reflect a high proportion of patients classified as moderate or severe infection without osteomyelitis initially being subsequently diagnosed with osteomyelitis (19% (14/73)) and those with a late diagnosis of osteomyelitis having a reduced chance of healing at 6 months (54% (7/13); no outcome available for one patient). It is unclear whether late diagnosis of osteomyelitis was due to disease progression or inaccuracies of clinical and radiological diagnostic strategies.

The study also provides important binational data regarding the microbiology of DFI in the Australian and New Zealand contexts and an ability to validate the appropriateness of national guideline-recommended empiric antibiotic regimens for DFI. Consistent with other data from Australia, P. aeruginosa infections were uncommon and apparent overuse of antipseudomonal therapy was frequent. This finding has important implications for antimicrobial stewardship of DFI.23

The study had a number of limitations. Of the 234 patients included in the analysis, 13% (31 patients) were not followed through to 6-month outcomes. In addition, the included population was heterogeneous with mild to severe severity infections and a mix of inpatients and outpatients, making subgroups small and hampering direct comparisons to specific populations. Furthermore, bias between comparisons may be present due to the observational study design. Enrollment was pragmatic and not sequential, which may have introduced selection bias. Many of the outcome variables were collected from medical record review, where they were recorded as part of usual clinical care. This potentially increases the variation in timing of recorded outcomes and reduces outcome availability at all time points. While acknowledging the challenges of inequitable access to healthcare, we did not have sufficient data to explore the impact of socioeconomic determinants of health or the contribution of multidisciplinary models of care.

The primary outcome of healing at 6 months is relevant for all patients with DFU and aligns with best practice for trials investigating osteomyelitis.30 Consistent with this being a clinically valid endpoint in this cohort, a decreasing odds of healing was observed with increasing WIfI wound grade at presentation (online supplemental table S2). However, the results suggest healing may be less directly influenced by infection management than other aspects of multidisciplinary care. We included resolution of infection as a secondary outcome, recognizing that while this may seem intuitively important in studies of antibiotic treatment, its validity and utility as a clinical endpoint remain uncertain. In our study, resolution of infection signs and symptoms at 6 weeks did not correlate with the initial infection severity classification, nor with any specific antibiotic strategy. Furthermore, it did not predict healing at 6 months, suggesting that factors other than infection, possibly those not explored in this study, may play a more significant role in determining wound healing outcomes.

This study provides evidence of substantial variation in antibiotic prescribing for acute DFI. This observed variation in practice mirrors the findings of the earlier hypothetical survey21 and may be attributed to the lack of high-quality evidence to inform clinical management of DFI and optimal strategies to improve patient-centered outcomes. There is a considerable burden on patients prescribed prolonged courses of antibiotics, especially extended intravenous therapy, with implications for healthcare resource allocation and antimicrobial stewardship. The current data suggest uncertainty of clinical benefit of an intravenous or prolonged therapy course compared with an oral or short course. This supports a path to clinical trials of antibiotic strategies (short vs long, broad vs narrow, early vs late oral switch). Given the heterogeneity of the population, morbidity and infection subtypes, and the broad applicability of strategic questions, these may be best tested in the format of a platform trial with clear definitions of treatment domains and phenotypic silos.

The study provides an opportunity to re-evaluate outcome measures for trials that enroll patients with DFI. The majority of outcomes have focused previously on amputations or resolution of infection; however, there is a need to better understand the relative importance of outcomes to patients suffering from and clinicians managing this disease. A recent study31 has developed a set of core outcome measures for studies of interventions for DFU, which includes infection as a critical outcome, although the expert group highlighted the challenges of diagnosis of infection and the heterogeneity of infection subtypes. A similar consensus on the key outcome measures for DFI has not been achieved. The lack of a definition as to how and when to determine whether soft tissue infection and/or osteomyelitis of the foot has resolved has been identified as a key controversy in the assessment and management of DFI,10 so determining or measuring the resolution of DFI as an outcome in both clinical practice and clinical trials remains challenging. Data from the current study can help inform development of an optimal endpoint which should also take into account patient preferences, adverse events and antibiotic stewardship priorities, through their incorporation into a desirability of outcome ranking with or without response adjustment for days of antibiotic risk.32 33

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large prospective study of patients with acute DFI from 20 sites across Australia and New Zealand demonstrated a high proportion of patients with residual infection at 6 weeks and lack of wound healing at 6 months. Presentations were diverse and managed with a broad range of antibiotic strategies. Neither infection severity nor antibiotic duration was associated with the likelihood of wound healing at 6 months. As the diabetes epidemic grows, the study highlights the urgent need to achieve consensus on the methodology and outcome definitions for DFI trials and the need for high-quality evidence from pragmatic clinical trials that include assessment of socioeconomic determinants, to improve meaningful outcomes for patients with DFI over the coming decades.
