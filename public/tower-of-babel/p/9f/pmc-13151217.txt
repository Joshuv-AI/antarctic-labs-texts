Unintentional paediatric paracetamol overdose: a retrospective observational study of dosing errors reported to an Australian Poisons Information Centre
Authors: Annabelle S Chidiac, Nicholas A Buckley, Firouzeh Noghrehchi, Rose Cairns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParacetamol poisoning is the leading cause of paediatric acute liver failure in Australia. This study aimed to describe patterns and characteristics of unintentional paracetamol overdose in children, with a focus on dosing errors, reported to Australia’s largest Poisons Information Centre.MethodsRecords regarding unintentional paracetamol overdose for individuals aged < 12 years old were extracted from the New South Wales Poisons Information Centre database, January 2017-June 2023. Dosing errors during 2021 underwent an in-depth screening of dose, duration, products involved, indications for use, the type of error and need for hospitalisation. Where possible, medical records of hospitalised exposures with dosing errors were screened across all years to obtain outcomes including hospital admission, length of stay, pathology test results, treatment, liver transplantations and deaths. Over-the-counter paracetamol sales data were obtained to examine the number of exposures per 100,000 units or grams sold.ResultsThere were 17,318 unintentional paracetamol exposures including 10,189 dosing errors and 7129 accidental exposures. There was an increase in dosing errors with time, associated with COVID-19 outbreaks in Australia. In-depth analysis of exposures from 2021 was done on 1498 exposures (median age 1 year, 54.0% male) with 1.7% (n = 26) requiring hospitalisation. Liquid dosage forms were the most frequently involved with higher strengths the most implicated. A communication error between caregivers and strength/formulation selection errors were the most frequently cited reasons for error. Of these, 10.6% (n = 15) were treated with the antidote, N-acetylcysteine. No cases of liver transplantation or death were identified and only 3.0% (n = 2) developed hepatotoxicity.ConclusionsParacetamol dosing errors in children are common but are unlikely to cause hepatotoxicity and death. Background

Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is classified as an essential medicine and approved for use as an analgesic and antipyretic in children as young as 1 month old [1, 2]. It is the most widely used medicine in paediatric practice and is available for purchase without a prescription [3]. Paracetamol comes in a variety of over-the-counter formulations including liquids, suppositories, powders, tablets and capsules [4]. Paediatric-specific products are typically suspensions, which come in a variety of strengths and are often supplied with brand specific measuring devices. Other formulations marketed for children include suppositories and chewable tablets. Adult formulations (tablets and capsules) may also be marketed for use in children aged seven years and older [5].

Paracetamol is safe for short-term use in children when administered at the correct dose. Overdose, including unintentional exploratory ingestions and dosing errors, carries a risk of poisoning [6]. The symptoms of paracetamol poisoning are delayed and if left untreated can cause hepatoxicity which may result in liver failure, and on rare occasions, death [7]. Unintentional exploratory ingestions can be easily avoided with appropriate storage, however dosing errors are often complex and require targeted efforts. Most paracetamol dosing is in the community, by caregivers such as parents, grandparents or childcare workers. Caregivers have been found to calculate doses incorrectly or are unable to use measuring devices appropriately [8–12]. Many caregivers are unaware of the risks associated with inappropriate medication use, use medicines unnecessarily, and may be dosing inaccurately [12, 13].

Dosing errors with paracetamol are the leading cause of paediatric acute liver failure in Australia and New Zealand [7]. However, information on the prevalence, characteristics, and underlying factors of these dosing errors in children remain limited. In this study we aimed to describe patterns and characteristics of paracetamol dosing errors in children aged < 12 years old reported to Australia’s largest Poisons Information Centre (PIC).

Methods

Design and setting

This was a retrospective observational study of calls for paracetamol exposures to the New South Wales Poisons Information Centre (NSWPIC). The NSWPIC is Australia’s largest PIC, handling approximately half of Australia’s annual 250,000 PIC calls. As such it may be assumed that national numbers are approximately double what is reported. NSWPIC takes calls primarily from three jurisdictions (New South Wales (NSW), Australian Capital Territory, and Tasmania), but also takes a sample of calls from all Australian states/territories as part of the after-hours call sharing roster of Australian PICs. Australian PICs offer a 24/7 service to healthcare professionals and members of the public. Calls are answered by specialists in poisons information (pharmacists and scientists with additional toxicology training). NSWPIC collects data on each call, including basic demographics, exposure characteristics (substance, preparation, dose, route), recommended management and symptoms present at the time of call. The data is entered at the time of call into a relational database, and each call is peer reviewed for quality assurance purposes (including coding accuracy). Australian PICs do not routinely follow up calls to seek outcome information. Where available, we accessed electronic Medical Records (eMRs) of patients in NSW hospitals to obtain management and outcome information for this study.

We used Australian sales data collected by NielsenIQ during 2021 to allow comparison of products most involved in PIC calls to volumes of use in the community. Data was provided in four-week aggregates by product (dates ranging from 05/01/2021–07/12/2021). NielsenIQ use a proprietary method to estimate sales, including from pharmacies, supermarkets and convenience stores.

Eligibility criteria, data extraction and cleaning

Case selection is summarised in Fig. 1. Exposure calls coded as ‘accidental’, ‘therapeutic error’ or ‘intentional: other’ (all non-deliberate self-poisonings) which mentioned paracetamol were extracted from the NSWPIC database between January 2017 and June 2023. Our focus was on dosing errors, which are typically coded as ‘therapeutic error’, but the ‘intentional: other’ category was also screened for potentially mis-coded calls. For context, we also extracted information on ‘accidental’ exposures (unintentional exploratory exposures where there was no therapeutic intent), but these did not form part of the main analysis. For dosing errors, we included any exposure to paracetamol where there was therapeutic intent, which included errors in dose, timing errors, and person errors (e.g. medication given to the wrong child). Adverse reactions to correct therapeutic doses (based on reported weight), exposures due to breastfeeding and exposures with no therapeutic intent or where documentation errors occurred (therapeutic doses were given but documented otherwise) were excluded. This study only includes individuals aged < 12 years old, as those aged ≥ 12 years have been described previously [14].

Data cleaning and re-coding was used to capture data from free text fields. For the dosing errors the paracetamol-containing products were further categorised based on active ingredients (see Supplementary Methods, Additional File 1).

Exposure calls taken during the year 2021 underwent an in-depth screen to extract additional variables of interest from case notes. Only one author (ASC) screened these calls, however if any details were unclear, a second author (RC) was consulted. The year 2021 was selected as this allows direct comparison with the corresponding data on people ≥ 12 years, published previously [14]. We classified a therapeutic dose as up to 15 mg/kg every 4h with no more than 4 doses (60 mg/kg) in a 24-h period [1, 4]. For a list of exclusion criteria and an in-depth explanation of data cleaning and recoding methods see Supplementary Methods, Additional File 1. Data extracted included basic demographics, paracetamol products involved, their formulation strength, indications for use, the type of error and reason/s it occurred according to the caller, the dose ingested within a 24-h period (in mg/kg), the duration of the error and the need for hospitalisation as identified by the NSWPIC.

NSWPIC hospital referral criteria for paracetamol dosing errors are consistent with national guidelines [15]. Specifically, this includes ingestion of:

≥ 10 g or ≥ 200 mg/kg (whichever is less) over a single 24-h period. or

≥ 10 g or ≥ 200 mg/kg (whichever is less) over a single 24-h period. or

≥ 12 g or ≥ 300 mg/kg (whichever is less) over a single 48-h period, or

≥ 12 g or ≥ 300 mg/kg (whichever is less) over a single 48-h period, or

≥ 4 g or ≥ 60 mg/kg per day for more than 48 h in those who also have abdominal pain, or nausea, or vomiting.

≥ 4 g or ≥ 60 mg/kg per day for more than 48 h in those who also have abdominal pain, or nausea, or vomiting.

We attempted to obtain outcome data for calls originating from hospitals in NSW where the NSWPIC has eMR access. Outcome data could not be obtained for exposures where the call originated outside of NSW (approximately 35% of the NSWPIC calls), for exposures presenting to private hospitals and for some NSW Local Health Districts where access was not granted. For a list of exclusion criteria and an in-depth explanation of data cleaning and recoding methods see Supplementary Methods, Additional File 1. Where available, outcome data extracted included hospital admission, length of stay, time from the last paracetamol dose until hospital presentation (or from presentation until further paracetamol administration, or from an in-hospital dosing error and contact with the NSWPIC), treatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC), symptoms developed, pathology test results (paracetamol concentration, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and international normalised ratio (INR) levels), the need for liver unit referral, liver transplantation and death. Note: in Australia, NAC is an antidote usually given as two intravenous infusions over 20 h (total dose 300 mg/kg), with variations to the dose and duration depending on paracetamol concentrations and/or signs of hepatotoxicity (defined as having an ALT > 1000 U/L) [15]. Paracetamol, ALT and INR levels are used to guide the cessation of NAC and as such patients may not receive a full course if it is not warranted [15].

The NSWPIC often receives multiple calls about one exposure (termed ‘recalls’). Recalls were not counted in the analysis, to avoid double-counting of exposures. However, any additional information reported in recalls were recorded for that exposure.

Sales of over-the-counter paracetamol products during the year 2021 were screened and classified according to their active ingredients and strength in mg/mL or mg/dose unit (for solid preparations).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, averages and ranges (minimum to maximum) for discrete variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Missing data is specified in the tables and was not imputed. Basic demographics and exposure characteristics of dosing errors and accidental exposures were compared.

We used Joinpoint analysis to analyse trends in annual counts (by Australian financial year, July-June), using Joinpoint Regression Program version 5.4.0.0. Log-linear segmented regression models were fit to estimate Annual Percent Change (APC). Autocorrelated errors with constant variance were specified to account for serial dependence in annual data. A grid-search method identified Joinpoint locations, with default spacing constraints (minimum of two observations from any Joinpoint to the series endpoints, and minimum two observations between Joinpoints). Model selection used Weighted Bayesian Information Criterion. For the final selected model, APC and Average APC (AAPC) confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained via the Empirical Quartile method with 5001 resamples using the program’s constant seed option. More granular trends were visually examined using a graph of weekly counts (consisting of 7-day intervals). The average daily rate each month was also calculated.

Using 2021 sales data exposures were also reported as exposures per 100,000 units sold and exposures per 100,000 g sold.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Sydney Children’s Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee (2021/ETH00165).

Results

Characteristics of unintentional exposures, stratified by type

Between January 2017 and June 2023 there was a total of 17,325 unintentional paracetamol exposures identified (Fig. 1). After exclusion criteria were applied there were a total of 10,189 dosing errors and 7129 accidental exposures identified. Calculating precise population rates is challenging because NSWPIC manages approximately half of all Australian poisoning calls, and the jurisdictional origin of calls varies by time of day. Using a crude denominator of 50% of the Australian population aged < 12 years in 2021 [16] yields an estimated rate of 167 paediatric dosing error exposures per 100,000 population per year.

There were an average of 30.1 (range 13–72) exposure calls per week and 4.3 (range 2.3–7.7) exposure calls per day for dosing errors (Fig. 2) with most calls made from the community (94.0%, n = 9888) (Supplementary Table 1, Additional File 1). Accidental exposures had an average of 21 (range 5–41) exposure calls per week and 3 (range 1.8–5.2) exposure calls per day. An increase in exposures from dosing errors was observed over the study period, a trend not seen with accidental exposures (Fig. 2). Joinpoint regression analysis of therapeutic error calls revealed an APC of 5.9% (95% CI 5.0 to 6.7%), July 2018 - June 2021. There was a significant change in trend, with an APC of 16.6% (95% CI 14.6 to 18.0%), July 2021-June 2023. In contrast, accidental exposures to paracetamol decreased over the same time period, with an APC of −12.2%, (95% CI−18.4 to −8.0%) July 2018 - June 2021. The rate of decrease slowed, with a Joinpoint identified in 2021, and an APC of −2.4% (95% CI −9.5 to 3.1%), July 2021 - June 2023 (Supplementary Fig. 1, Additional File 1).

In-depth characterisation of dosing error exposures in 2021

Prior to in-depth screening there were a total of 1625 exposures recorded for the year 2021. After exclusion criteria were applied, this was reduced to 1498 exposures. Patient demographics and exposure characteristics were similar to the entire cohort (Supplementary Table 2, Additional File 1). The median age was 1 year (IQR 0.8–3 years) and 54.0% (n = 809) were male. The majority of exposures occurred in the toddler age group (54.3%, n = 814). Similar to the entire cohort, in 2021 most exposures were advised to stay home (Supplementary Table 2, Additional File 1). However, a smaller proportion of the 2021 exposures were asymptomatic (78.5% vs 84.6% of the entire cohort) and a greater proportion had symptoms unrelated or not known if related to the exposure (19.7% vs 13.9% of the entire cohort) (Supplementary Table 2, Additional File 1).

In 2021 an immediate-release form of paracetamol was used in 99.1% (n = 1484) of exposures and 7.0% (n = 105) used multiple paracetamol-containing products. Liquid dosage forms were the most frequently involved, most commonly the 50 mg/mL and 100 mg/mL preparations (29.5%, n = 442, and 27.8%, n = 416, respectively) (Table 2). Sales data from 2021 showed that liquid preparations had the greatest number of pack sales amongst the paediatric specific dosage forms (93.4%, n = 4,881,294). Of the liquid preparations the 50 mg/mL and 100 mg/mL strengths had the greatest number of exposures per 100,000 units sold at 48.9 and 38.2, respectively (Supplementary Table 3, Additional File 1).

Acute incorrect dose errors and acute interval errors were the most frequently reported (Table 2). Where documented in the call record, the top 3 indications included fever/febrile convulsion, teething/dental/oral pain and respiratory tract infections. Most frequently cited reasons for errors included communication errors between caregivers and strength/formulation selection errors (Table 2). A complete list of indications and caregiver reasons for error can be found in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5, Additional File 1 respectively. The median dose per 24-h period (known for 85.4%, n = 1280, of exposures) was 36 mg/kg (IQR 26.4–51.9 mg/kg), less than the maximum daily dose of 60 mg/kg, and 80.7% (n = 1206) of exposures had a once-off error. A need for hospitalisation attributable to the paracetamol dosing error was identified in only 1.7% (n = 26) of 2021 exposures (Table 2).

Of these exposures, 99 were excluded due to data for that particular admission being not available for review. Therefore, 142 exposures were included that allowed for in-depth analysis of outcome data (Table 3).

The median age of hospitalised cases was 1 year (IQR 0.3–4 years) and 59.2% (n = 84) were male (Table 3). The median time between the last dose of paracetamol and presentation to hospital was 2 h (IQR 1–5.4 h). In 10 (7.0%) exposures further paracetamol was administered after presentation either before the error was identified or before they had been medically cleared. A total of 15 (10.6%) exposures had been given an in-hospital dosing error (iatrogenic errors). Symptoms of toxicity were reported in 38.3% (n = 54) of exposures.

Paracetamol was detectable on presentation in 51.8% (n = 29) of exposures (Table 3). An abnormal ALT (> 50 U/L) was detected in 17.9% (n = 12) and only 2 (3.0%) exposures had an ALT > 1000 U/L. Both of these cases were in the 5–12 years age group. One developed fulminant hepatic failure following repeated supratherapeutic ingestion of liquid paracetamol (48 mg/mL), a combined dose and timing error (excessive dose and 6 doses per 24 h) over a 48-h period, with delayed presentation to hospital. The second case was given multiple paracetamol doses over a 48-h period (strength unknown), only slightly above the recommended dose.

The median length of stay was 3.1 h (IQR 1.3–5.5 h, note that this includes people discharged from the emergency department with no NAC, as well as those admitted/treated). A total of 10.6% (n = 15) received NAC and there were no cases of liver unit referral, liver transplantation or death. Only 1 (0.7%) exposure was transferred to the Intensive Care Unit, although this was to monitor their NAC infusion. Admission to hospital for the paracetamol dosing error was required in 10.6% (n = 15).

Discussion

Paediatric dosing errors involving paracetamol are a common occurrence, being reported to the NSWPIC 30 times per week on average. The majority were in children under 5 years, and over 90% of exposures required no further management and could stay at home. For those that presented to hospital, admission, hepatotoxicity and need for treatment were uncommon. Communication errors between caregivers and strength/formulation selection errors were the most common reasons for error. The 50 mg/mL and 100 mg/mL preparations appeared to be over-represented. There was an increase in exposures over the study period, most likely relating to Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outbreaks and use of paracetamol to treat associated febrile illness. The change in trend detected in 2021–2022 coincides with COVID-19 outbreaks in Australia and the illness becoming more widespread when international borders were opened in late 2021.

Errors by caregivers in calculating and administering doses to children have been previously highlighted [8–13], however little has been done to improve widespread caregiver education. Between 1.7%−3.7% of our cohort cited errors due to measuring devices or misreading dosing instructions however, reasons for error were not documented in the majority of cases, and these numbers are likely an underestimate. Previous studies have indicated that errors may be influenced by the caregivers’ level of education status, health literacy or English proficiency [8, 10]. Since 2013, the Australian government has required packaging to specify age and weight specific doses [5]. Not only can this be confusing, but it requires the caregiver to know the weight of the child, or if unknown, assume that the child fits into the average weight range for their age. However, this does not always occur and can lead to caregivers misreading dosing instructions and choosing the wrong dose. Additionally, many liquid formulations come with measuring devices that are brand-specific, which vary (e.g. gradations on plunger versus the barrel). Due to the variety of brands on the market this can lead to further errors. The introduction of standardised measuring devices and public health campaigns focusing on caregiver education should be explored to allow for appropriate and accurate delivery of medications to children.

High strength liquid formulations were overrepresented with strength or formulation selection errors cited as a common reason for occurrence. In Australia there are 4 available strengths of liquid paracetamol ranging from 24 to 100 mg/ml; up to fourfold errors can occur if the wrong strength is selected. Standardisation of liquid products to a single concentration was implemented in the United States in 2011, and was followed by an almost 20% decrease in exposure calls [17]. There was no difference in the proportion of exposures requiring referral, although this was already relatively small [17]. Standardisation of liquid products in Australia should be explored as this may further reduce the potential for error.

After conducting follow up on hospitalised exposures we found that the occurrence of severe hepatotoxicity was uncommon (3.0%) and death was absent in our study. Although they presented to hospital early, with a median of 2 h between their last dose and presentation, this was only slightly faster than those aged ≥ 12 years with a median of 3 h [14]. Therefore this reduced severity of outcomes is likely due to the fact that children generally have more efficient detoxification pathways for paracetamol than adults [18]. Specifically, sulfation predominates in children, while CYP2E1 is immature and less efficient in children [19, 20]. Children also have larger livers relative to their body size [21], and western children are likely to be glutathione replete. These factors are thought to be protective against paracetamol induced hepatotoxicity in children. Additionally, although medication errors in children can cause great concern to caregivers, the majority of errors were once-off, and the total dose ingested was under the maximum daily limit. It has been reported previously that hepatotoxicity and death within the maximum daily dose is rare [22]. Exposures exceeding 120 mg/kg/day over a period greater than 48 h are often associated with paediatric acute liver failure [7] which did not occur with frequency in our cohort. In our in-depth screening we found that only 1.7% of children required hospitalisation as per the NSWPIC compared to the original 4.0% (based on handling/disposition codes). The majority of this discrepancy is due to self-presentation to hospital without calling PIC. When hospitals called about these cases, PIC advised that the child did not require observation or investigation, and could be sent home. This highlights the role of PICs in reducing unnecessary hospitalisations, reducing logistical burden and cost to the healthcare system. This aligns with findings from a recent RAND evaluation of the United States Poison Center Network, which demonstrated that poison centres generate substantial system‑wide savings (return on investment of $16.77 United States Dollars for every $1 spent), primarily through avoided unnecessary healthcare utilisation [23]. Further promotion of the PIC service could reduce the burden on the healthcare system and families.

We identified an increase in the weekly count of exposure calls beginning in 2020 through to the middle of 2022 which may be attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic. In Australia COVID-19 waves began in March and June 2020 and June and December 2021. In contrast with many other parts of the world, Australia’s COVID peak was in 2022 (with rates ~ 34 times higher in 2022 versus 2021) [24]. The noticeable rise in paracetamol dosing error exposures in 2022 (based on visual inspection of weekly counts) is associated with Australia’s international border reopening in late 2021 which was followed by a surge in COVID-19 cases [24]. This also coincides with the Joinpoint detected after 2021. In addition, COVID-19 vaccination for children was rolled out in January 2022 [25]. This timeline coincides with increases in exposure calls potentially from increased use due to COVID-19 infection, vaccine-related adverse drug reactions or fear among parents. In March 2020, limits were placed on sales of analgesics, with liquid formulations also being placed behind the counter with other Pharmacist only and Prescription only medications [26] due to an increased demand and stockpiling [27]. This may be associated with the short-lived decrease in medication errors in the period following due to a reduced amount of paracetamol in the home. However with paracetamol brought back to front of counter in July 2020 this may have contributed to the following increase [28].

Limitations

The dataset used for this study only contains exposures where a call was made to NSWPIC by health care practitioners or members of the public. It therefore does not capture all paracetamol therapeutic errors that occurred in NSW or Australia. The retrospective nature of this study may result in errors within the recorded information. In addition, some variables had high rates of missing data which may introduce bias if the missingness was not random. The presence of toxicology units within certain hospitals and Australia’s clinical guidelines on paracetamol poisoning management may result in relatively fewer calls to the NSWPIC for advice. The NSWPIC does not do routine follow-up calls, and the majority of exposures lack complete outcome data. The data from 2021 provides the most accurate information due to in-depth screening although it was not feasible to do so for all remaining years. It should be worth noting that despite in-depth screening there remained a substantial amount of missing data, particularly for indications for use and caregiver reasons for error, as this information is not regularly sought during calls. In addition, while the characteristics of 2021 exposures were similar to that of the overall group, some statistically significant differences were found which may limit generalisability of the in-depth analysis of 2021 results (for example, 2021 exposures were less likely to be asymptomatic). Medical record screening was limited to hospitals where the NSWPIC has eMR access. Similar to the 2021 cohort, there remained a substantial amount of missing data, particularly for paracetamol, ALT and INR levels. As this was restricted to a subset of hospitals within NSW, the occurrence of hospital admissions, treatments and outcomes could not be confirmed for a significant proportion of exposures. Lastly, data extraction and screening were completed by a single author, although a second author was consulted for any unclear records.

Conclusions

Paracetamol dosing errors in children are common, although unlikely to cause severe outcomes such as hepatotoxicity and death. The exploration of these events allows for better understanding of the reasons for error and warrants future investigations into approaches that decrease occurrence such as caregiver education or the standardisation of concentrations of liquid products.
