Medium-term clinical outcomes of the all-on-4 system in different bone types: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Basak Topdagi, Muhammet Kurum, Ceren Cakar Guler, Fatih Oktay, Oguz Yuce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to determine the independent influence of bone quality and prosthetic material on medium-term implant survival, peri-implant complications, prosthetic complications, and patient-reported outcomes following All-on-4 rehabilitation.MethodsThis retrospective, observational, multicenter cohort study included 250 edentulous patients (125 maxillary and 125 mandibular cases) who received All-on-4 implant-supported full-arch prostheses between September 2018 and September 2021. Patients were categorized by bone type (Types 1–4) according to the Lekholm and Zarb classification. Clinical parameters, insertion torque, implant stability quotient (ISQ), peri-implant and prosthetic complications, and patient satisfaction (VAS score) were recorded and analyzed. Prosthetic materials used were either metal-acrylic or monolithic zirconia prostheses.ResultsA total of 1,000 implants were evaluated over a mean follow-up period of 3.6 ± 0.4 years. The overall implant survival rate was 94.8%, with significantly lower survival in Type 4 bone (89.1%) compared to Type 1 (98.3%) (p < 0.05). Peri-implant complications, particularly peri-implantitis and screw loosening, were significantly more frequent in lower-density bone types. Prosthetic complications were analyzed at the prosthesis level and were more frequent in the metal-acrylic group (26.9%) than in the zirconia group (8.9%) (p < 0.001). Despite these differences, patient satisfaction scores were high across all bone types (Mean VAS: 8.7 ± 1.2).ConclusionsThe All-on-4 concept demonstrated predictable medium-term clinical outcomes, with high implant survival and favorable patient satisfaction in completely edentulous patients. However, poorer bone quality, particularly Type 4 bone, was independently associated with lower implant survival, reduced primary stability, and a higher frequency of peri-implant complications. In addition, monolithic zirconia prostheses were associated with fewer mechanical complications than metal–acrylic prostheses. These findings highlight the importance of individualized treatment planning that integrates bone quality assessment and prosthetic material selection to optimize medium-term clinical outcomes. Although the findings should be interpreted in light of the retrospective observational design and medium-term follow-up, they provide clinically relevant evidence to support risk-informed clinical decision-making in full-arch implant rehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09299-y.

Background

Complete edentulism remains a major global oral health problem that substantially impairs mastication, speech, facial esthetics, nutrition, and overall quality of life. Implant-supported full-arch rehabilitation has become the preferred treatment modality for edentulous patients because it provides superior functional outcomes and higher patient satisfaction compared with conventional removable prostheses. Among these treatment options, the All-on-4 concept has emerged as a predictable and minimally invasive approach for restoring edentulous jaws by reducing the need for extensive bone augmentation while maintaining high implant survival rates. Recent systematic reviews and long-term clinical studies consistently report implant survival exceeding 94%, supporting the long-term clinical reliability of the All-on-4 concept in both the maxilla and mandible [1–5].

Despite these favorable outcomes, several clinically important questions remain unresolved. Although previous studies have demonstrated high implant survival with the All-on-4 concept, the influence of bone quality on medium-term implant survival and complication profiles has not been consistently established across different patient populations. Furthermore, most published studies have focused primarily on implant survival, whereas the combined impact of bone quality and prosthetic material on peri-implant complications, prosthetic complications, and patient-reported outcomes has received considerably less attention. Consequently, evidence integrating these clinically relevant factors within the same multicenter cohort remains limited, highlighting an important gap in the current literature [6–8].

Therefore, the present multicenter retrospective study aimed to evaluate the medium-term clinical outcomes of the All-on-4 treatment concept in completely edentulous patients, with particular emphasis on the influence of bone quality and prosthetic material on implant survival, peri-implant complications, prosthetic complications, and patient-reported satisfaction. We hypothesized that lower bone quality, particularly Type 4 bone according to the Lekholm and Zarb classification, would be associated with reduced implant survival and increased complication rates, and that monolithic zirconia prostheses would demonstrate fewer mechanical complications than metal-acrylic prostheses. By simultaneously evaluating surgical, prosthetic, and patient-reported outcomes within a large multicenter cohort, This study was designed to provide clinically relevant evidence to support individualized treatment planning for full-arch implant rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective, observational, multicenter cohort study was conducted at the prosthodontics Departments of Tacirler Education Foundation Sultanbeyli Oral and Dental Health Center, Erzincan Binali Yıldırım University, and Bingöl Oral and Dental Health Center. All surgical procedures were performed by three experienced surgeons, each with comparable expertise in the All-on-4 treatment concept. A standardized surgical and prosthetic protocol was applied across all centers. The study was approved by the ethics committees of the respective institutions and carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient selection

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2). The calculation was based on detecting differences in implant survival across bone-type groups.

Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), a significance level of α = 0.05, and statistical power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated as 249 patients for comparisons among bone-type groups using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Although the sample size calculation was based on ANOVA assumptions, the primary outcome of the study was time-to-event (implant survival). Therefore, this calculation may not fully reflect the statistical requirements of survival analysis and should be considered an approximation. In addition, because the calculation was performed at the patient level during the study design phase, clustering effects related to multiple implants per patient were not explicitly incorporated into the power analysis. Because the primary outcome of the study was implant survival, the adequacy of the Cox regression model was also evaluated using an event-based approach.

During follow-up, 52 implant failures were observed. The multivariable Cox model included five covariates: bone type, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, jaw location, and loading protocol. Accordingly, the events-per-variable ratio was approximately 10.4, which was considered acceptable for an exploratory multivariable Cox regression model. Nevertheless, because the original sample size calculation was based on ANOVA assumptions rather than time-to-event modeling, this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

A total of 250 patients (125 maxillary and 125 mandibular) who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. A total of 318 completely edentulous patients who underwent All-on-4 implant-supported full-arch rehabilitation between September 2018 and September 2021 were retrospectively screened. Eligible patients were identified through retrospective review of institutional clinical records and radiographic archives at the participating centers. Clinical and radiographic follow-up data were collected until September 2025, ensuring a minimum follow-up period of three years for all included patients. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had undergone All-on-4 treatment between the specified dates, had a minimum of three years of postoperative follow-up, had their preoperative bone type determined radiographically, and had been treated using a standardized surgical and prosthetic protocol.

Only patients with a minimum postoperative follow-up duration of at least three years were included in the analysis. Patients with controlled systemic diseases, including controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus, were included. Patients were excluded if they had received bone grafts, were treated using alternative implant techniques, had systemic conditions such as uncontrolled diabetes, osteoporosis, or a history of radiotherapy, or if their postoperative follow-up period was less than three years (Fig. 1.).

Data collection and variables

Demographic, surgical, and prosthetic data were retrospectively extracted from patient records and radiographic evaluations. Collected demographic information included age, gender, smoking status, and the presence of systemic diseases. Surgical parameters encompassed implant location (maxilla or mandible), implant dimensions, insertion torque values, implant stability quotient (ISQ) values, and bone quality classification. Prosthetic data included the type of definitive restoration (metal-acrylic or monolithic zirconia), application of immediate loading protocols, and the occurrence of prosthetic complications such as screw loosening or prosthesis fracture.

Because prosthetic material allocation was not randomized, comparisons between metal–acrylic and monolithic zirconia prostheses were considered exploratory and descriptive rather than causal. Material choice was influenced by multiple factors, including patient preference, economic considerations, prosthetic durability requirements, and clinician judgment. In general, monolithic zirconia prosthesis was more frequently selected in patients seeking greater mechanical durability and esthetic performance, whereas metal-acrylic prostheses were often preferred because of their lower cost and easier repairability.

A standardized occlusal scheme was applied in all patients. Bilateral balanced occlusion was established with light centric contacts and minimized posterior cantilever loading. Lateral and protrusive movements were adjusted to avoid non-axial forces, with canine guidance preferred when feasible and group function applied only when anatomical limitations required. Importantly, the same occlusal scheme and adjustment principles were used for both monolithic zirconia prostheses and metal-acrylic prostheses.

Bone quality was assessed retrospectively using preoperative radiographic records and classified according to the Lekholm and Zarb classification. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the structure of available clinical records, bone quality was assessed at the jaw level rather than at the individual implant site level. Therefore, intra-jaw variations in bone density may not have been fully captured. Panoramic radiographs were available for all patients, whereas cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images were available for a substantial proportion of cases but not for the entire cohort, as CBCT imaging was not routinely performed for every patient during the study period. CBCT imaging was available in 64.0% (n = 160) of patients, whereas the remaining 36.0% (n = 90) were assessed using panoramic radiographs in combination with clinical records. When CBCT scans were available, bone quality assessment was primarily based on CBCT findings; in patients without CBCT imaging, classification was performed using panoramic radiographs in conjunction with available clinical records. This heterogeneity in imaging modalities may have introduced variability in bone quality assessment and may have affected the precision of bone classification. Panoramic-based bone quality assessment using the Lekholm and Zarb classification may provide clinically useful information and has been reported to show moderate correlation with intraoperative findings; however, it is more operator-dependent and less precise than CBCT-based evaluation.

All radiographic evaluations were conducted retrospectively by two experienced prosthodontists. Bone quality classification was performed through a consensus-based approach, with both evaluators jointly reviewing the radiographic data and agreeing on the final classification for each case. Due to the retrospective design of the study and the structure of the available clinical documentation, bone type was assigned at the patient/jaw level, reflecting the predominant bone characteristics of the rehabilitated arch rather than individual implant sites. In routine clinical records, bone quality was typically documented for the rehabilitated jaw rather than separately for each implant site; therefore, a jaw-level classification approach was adopted to ensure methodological consistency across all included cases. No formal examiner calibration or inter-observer reliability assessment (e.g., kappa statistics) was performed, which may have introduced observer-related variability.

All implants used in this study belonged to a single implant system (Nobel Biocare, Gothenburg, Sweden). The implants featured a moderately rough surface obtained by sandblasting and acid-etching, along with a tapered macro-design and aggressive thread geometry intended to enhance primary stability, particularly in low-density bone. Implant diameter and length were selected based on individual anatomical conditions and bone availability, following the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Clinical outcomes

The primary objective of the study was to determine whether bone quality was independently associated with medium-term implant survival. Secondary objectives included evaluating the relationships between bone quality, primary stability (insertion torque and ISQ), peri-implant complications, prosthetic complications, prosthetic material, and patient-reported satisfaction. Peri-implant complications, prosthetic complications, insertion torque, ISQ values, and patient satisfaction scores were considered secondary or exploratory outcomes. Implant survival and peri-implant complications were analyzed at the implant level (n = 1000 implants), whereas prosthetic complications were analyzed at the prosthesis level (n = 250 full-arch prostheses).

Patient satisfaction outcomes were evaluated at the patient level (n = 250 patients).

Medium-term clinical outcomes evaluated in this study included implant survival, peri-implant complications, and patient satisfaction. Implant survival was defined as the continued presence of the implant at follow-up without signs of mobility or the need for removal. Peri-implant diseases were diagnosed according to the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions [9]. Peri-implantitis was defined as the presence of bleeding and/or suppuration on gentle probing, probing pocket depth ≥ 6 mm, and radiographic marginal bone loss ≥ 3 mm apical to the most coronal portion of the intraosseous part of the implant. Peri-implant mucositis was defined as bleeding and/or suppuration on probing without radiographic evidence of bone loss beyond initial remodeling.

Patient satisfaction was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS, 0–10). The VAS score was recorded at the most recent follow-up visit, reflecting the patient’s overall perception of function, comfort, and esthetics after completion of treatment and during medium-term follow-up. The VAS score reflected the patient’s overall satisfaction with function, comfort, and esthetics of the implant-supported prostheses.

Peri-implant complications (e.g., peri-implantitis and mucositis) were evaluated at the implant level, since these conditions occur at individual implant sites. In contrast, prosthetic complications (e.g., screw loosening, prosthesis fracture, and chipping) were evaluated at the prosthesis level, as each patient received a single full-arch prosthesis supported by four implants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v27.0 (IBM, USA). Because each patient received four implants, the primary unit of analysis for implant survival and peri-implant complications was the implant level. Patient-level variables (age, sex, smoking status, systemic diseases, and bone type classification) were analyzed at the patient level. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Implant survival was compared across bone types using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was applied to compare survival times between bone types. Insertion torque and implant stability quotient (ISQ) values were compared among bone types using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons.

To evaluate whether bone quality was an independent predictor of implant failure, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed. Bone quality (Lekholm and Zarb classification) was included as the primary independent variable. For regression modeling, Type 1 and Type 2 bone were combined to represent relatively higher-density bone, and Type 4 bone was compared against this reference category. Type 3 bone was not included in the primary comparison to maintain a clear contrast between low- and high-density categories. Because Type 3 bone represented an intermediate bone-quality category, it was not included in the primary high-density versus low-density contrast model.

However, because Type 3 bone represented a substantial proportion of the cohort, an additional Cox proportional hazards regression model including all four bone-type categories was performed as a sensitivity analysis. The results of this full model are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Smoking status (yes/no), diabetes mellitus (yes/no), jaw location (maxilla/mandible), and loading protocol (immediate vs. delayed) were included as covariates. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

To account for the non-independence of implants within the same patient, a sensitivity analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazards model with robust (sandwich) variance estimation clustered at the patient level. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, including both statistical testing and graphical inspection.

Because each patient contributed four implants, implant-level analyses may be affected by intra-patient correlation. Although clustering was addressed in the Cox regression sensitivity analysis, descriptive comparisons of implant-level categorical outcomes, including Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests for implant survival and peri-implant complications, and ANOVA comparisons of insertion torque and ISQ values, were not modeled using multilevel or generalized estimating equation approaches. Therefore, these analyses should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory. The potential influence of within-patient clustering on these implant-level comparisons is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Bone quality was classified at the patient/jaw level because the available clinical records documented the predominant bone characteristics of the rehabilitated arch rather than individual implant sites. Implant survival and peri-implant complications, however, occur at the implant level and were therefore analyzed at the implant level to preserve clinically relevant event information. Consequently, patient-level variables, including bone quality, were assigned to all implants within the corresponding rehabilitated arch. This approach has been used in previous retrospective implant studies but may introduce pseudo-replication because multiple implants from the same patient share identical patient-level characteristics.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 318 completely edentulous patients were initially screened. Of these, 68 patients were excluded for the following reasons: insufficient follow-up duration (< 3 years) (n = 34), history of bone grafting procedures (n = 12), presence of uncontrolled systemic diseases (n = 9), use of alternative implant protocols (n = 7), or incomplete clinical records (n = 6). The remaining 250 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Immediate loading was applied in n = 180 patients (72.0%), while n = 70 patients (28.0%) received delayed loading protocols. The distribution of loading protocols did not differ significantly across bone-type groups (p > 0.05). The primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed according to the predefined study objectives.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. No statistically significant differences in age, sex distribution, smoking status, or prevalence of diabetes mellitus were observed across bone-type groups (p > 0.05). The prevalence of active smoking was 33.3% (n = 10) in Type 1 bone, 36.8% (n = 32) in Type 2 bone, 38.8% (n = 31) in Type 3 bone, and 45.3% (n = 24) in Type 4 bone, with no statistically significant difference observed among groups (p = 0.421). The prevalence of diabetes mellitus was 10.0% (n = 3) in Type 1 bone, 12.6% (n = 11) in Type 2 bone, 15.0% (n = 12) in Type 3 bone, and 18.9% (n = 10) in Type 4 bone, and these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.507).

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients

A total of 250 completely edentulous patients (125 maxillary, 125 mandibular cases) were included in the study. The mean age was 62.4 ± 8.9 years. Of the participants, 54% (n = 135) were female and 46% (n = 115) were male. Active smoking was reported in 38.8% of patients (n = 97). The most common systemic disease was hypertension (21.6%), followed by type 2 diabetes mellitus (14.4%) (Table 1.).

According to the Lekholm and Zarb classification, the distribution of bone types was as follows: Type 1 in 12.0% (n = 30), Type 2 in 34.8% (n = 87), Type 3 in 32.0% (n = 80), and Type 4 in 21.2% (n = 53) (Table 2.). For regression analysis, Type 1 and Type 2 bone (n = 117) were combined to represent higher-density bone and used as the reference category, while Type 4 bone (n = 53) was compared against this group (Table 2.).

A total of 1,000 implants (four implants per patient) were evaluated, and implant survival and complication outcomes were analyzed at the implant level, whereas demographic characteristics were analyzed at the patient level. Implant survival was evaluated over a mean follow-up period of 3.6 ± 0.4 years. Follow-up duration did not differ significantly between bone-type groups or between prosthetic material groups (p > 0.05). During the follow-up period, a total of 52 implant failures were recorded among the 1,000 implants analyzed. The overall implant survival rate was 94.8%. Survival rates by bone type were 98.3% for Type 1, 96.5% for Type 2, 93.7% for Type 3, and 89.1% for Type 4 (Table 3.).

The mean insertion torque and ISQ values showed a progressive decrease from Type 1 to Type 4 bone. One-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences among bone types for both insertion torque and ISQ values (p < 0.05). Post-hoc analysis demonstrated that Type 3 and Type 4 bone had significantly lower insertion torque and ISQ values compared to Type 1 and Type 2 bone (Table 4.). Peri-implant complications were evaluated at the implant level and were observed in 61 implants. Complication categories were not mutually exclusive; therefore, an individual implant could contribute to more than one complication category during follow-up. The analysis was based on the presence of complication types rather than the timing or recurrence of individual events. The most common complication was peri-implantitis (29 cases, 37.2%), followed by screw loosening (22 cases, 28.2%) and mucosal irritation (10 cases, 12.8%). Peri-implantitis was significantly more common in patients with Type 4 bone (48.3% of all cases), and screw loosening also showed a significantly higher frequency in lower density bone types (p < 0.05). While prosthesis fracture and mucosal irritation were more frequent in Type 4 bone, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 5, Fig. 3 ).

Prosthetic complications were evaluated at the prosthesis level in 250 full-arch prostheses. Screw loosening was observed in 18 (11.3%) metal-acrylic prostheses and 4 (4.4%) monolithic zirconia prostheses, representing a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.041). Prosthesis fracture occurred in 14 (8.8%) metal-acrylic prostheses and 3 (3.3%) monolithic zirconia prostheses; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.087). Chipping or surface wear was identified in 11 (6.9%) metal-acrylic prostheses and 1 (1.1%) monolithic zirconia prosthesis, with a significant difference between groups (p = 0.038). Overall, prosthetic complications were recorded in 43 (26.9%) metal-acrylic prostheses and 8 (8.9%) monolithic zirconia prostheses, corresponding to a significantly higher complication frequency in the metal-acrylic group (p < 0.001) (Table 6).

Prosthetic complications by prosthesis type (prosthesis-level analysis)

A total of 51 prosthetic complications were observed among 250 prostheses and were included in an exploratory multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjustment for age, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, jaw location, and bone type, metal–acrylic prostheses remained significantly associated with a higher likelihood of prosthetic complications compared with monolithic zirconia prostheses (adjusted OR: 3.28, 95% CI: 1.46–7.37, p = 0.004). None of the other covariates reached statistical significance in the adjusted model. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated acceptable model fit (p = 0.72) (Table 7).

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to evaluate factors associated with implant failure. In the conventional Cox model, Type 4 bone was significantly associated with implant failure compared with Type 1–2 bone (HR: 2.41, 95% CI: 1.32–4.39, p = 0.004). Smoking was also significantly associated with implant failure (HR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.08–3.31, p = 0.026), whereas diabetes mellitus (HR: 1.34, 95% CI: 0.71–2.54, p = 0.361), jaw location (HR: 1.21, 95% CI: 0.69–2.11, p = 0.503), and loading protocol (HR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.52–1.61, p = 0.744) were not significantly associated with implant failure. In the cluster-robust Cox model adjusted for patient-level clustering, hazard ratio estimates remained unchanged. As expected, robust variance estimation affected confidence intervals and p-values but did not alter the regression coefficients themselves.

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study evaluated the medium-term clinical outcomes of the All-on-4 treatment concept in completely edentulous patients, with particular emphasis on the influence of bone quality and prosthetic material on treatment success. Overall, the findings demonstrated a high implant survival rate (94.8%) and favorable patient satisfaction after a mean follow-up of 3.6 years, supporting the clinical reliability of the All-on-4 concept in routine practice. However, important differences were observed according to bone quality and prosthetic material. Lower bone quality, particularly Type 4 bone, was associated with reduced implant survival, lower insertion torque and ISQ values, and higher rates of peri-implant complications, whereas monolithic zirconia prostheses were associated with fewer mechanical complications than metal–acrylic prostheses. Collectively, these findings suggest that both biological factors and prosthetic material selection contribute to medium-term clinical outcomes and should be considered during individualized treatment planning for full-arch implant rehabilitation.

The All-on-4 treatment concept has become one of the most extensively investigated treatment modalities for the rehabilitation of completely edentulous jaws because it offers a predictable, minimally invasive, and cost-effective alternative to conventional implant-supported full-arch rehabilitation, often eliminating the need for extensive bone augmentation procedures. Systematic reviews and long-term longitudinal studies have consistently demonstrated implant survival rates exceeding 94%, supporting the long-term clinical reliability of this treatment concept across different clinical settings [1–3]. Nevertheless, implant survival alone does not fully reflect treatment success. Current evidence increasingly suggests that long-term outcomes should also be evaluated in terms of biological complications, prosthetic complications, implant stability, and patient-reported outcomes, as these parameters collectively determine the overall effectiveness and durability of full-arch implant rehabilitation [8, 10, 11]. Within this broader context, our findings further support the growing body of evidence demonstrating that successful All-on-4 rehabilitation should be assessed using a comprehensive multidimensional approach rather than implant survival alone.

Primary stability is a fundamental prerequisite for successful osseointegration and has consistently been recognized as one of the strongest determinants of early implant success. It is influenced by multiple biological and mechanical factors, including bone quality and quantity, implant macrogeometry and surface characteristics, surgical technique, and the degree of mechanical engagement achieved at implant placement [4, 12–14]. In clinical practice, insertion torque and the ISQ, obtained by resonance frequency analysis, are the most widely accepted objective indicators of primary implant stability and have been shown to correlate with osseointegration and long-term implant survival [14–16]. Bone quality plays a central role in this process. The Lekholm and Zarb classification remains one of the most commonly used systems for categorizing jawbone quality, and previous experimental, clinical, and biomechanical studies have consistently demonstrated that implants placed in low-density (Type 4) bone generally exhibit lower insertion torque, reduced ISQ values, delayed osseointegration, and a greater susceptibility to early implant failure than those placed in denser bone types [6, 16–19]. These biological and biomechanical principles provide an important framework for interpreting the findings of the present study.

Consistent with these biological principles, implant survival in the present study progressively decreased with deteriorating bone quality, from 98.3% in Type 1 bone to 89.1% in Type 4 bone. Moreover, multivariable Cox regression analysis confirmed that Type 4 bone remained independently associated with implant failure after adjustment for smoking, diabetes mellitus, jaw location, and loading protocol, indicating that bone quality itself constitutes an important determinant of medium-term implant survival. These findings are in agreement with previous clinical studies reporting that reduced bone density compromises primary mechanical stability, delays osseointegration, and ultimately increases the risk of implant failure [16, 17]. Abrahamsson et al. demonstrated that implant stability and early bone healing are strongly influenced by the biological characteristics of the host bone, whereas finite element analyses by Haroun and Ozan further suggested that low-density bone is subjected to greater stress concentrations around the implant–bone interface during functional loading, potentially contributing to mechanical overload and subsequent biological complications [12, 13]. Our findings therefore extend the existing literature by demonstrating that the detrimental influence of poor bone quality remains evident not only during the early healing phase but also throughout medium-term clinical follow-up in a large multicenter cohort of patients treated with the All-on-4 concept.

The progressive reduction in insertion torque and ISQ values observed from Type 1 to Type 4 bone further supports the biological plausibility of our survival findings. Insertion torque reflects the mechanical engagement achieved during implant placement, whereas ISQ provides a non-invasive estimate of implant stability through resonance frequency analysis.

Although these parameters assess different aspects of primary stability, both have been consistently associated with successful osseointegration and long-term implant performance [14, 15]. In the present study, implants placed in lower-density bone demonstrated significantly lower insertion torque and ISQ values, indicating reduced mechanical stability at placement. Moreover, approximately one-fifth of implants placed in Type 4 bone exhibited insertion torque values below the commonly accepted threshold of 35 Ncm, emphasizing the clinical challenges associated with immediate loading in compromised bone. These findings are consistent with those of da Rocha Ferreira et al., who demonstrated a significant relationship between insertion torque, ISQ, and implant stability, as well as with Gill et al. and Ayub et al., who reported that implant macrogeometry and bone density substantially influence primary stability, particularly in low-density bone [14, 15, 17]. Collectively, our results suggest that insertion torque and ISQ should not be regarded merely as intraoperative measurements but rather as clinically useful indicators that may help identify patients at increased risk of implant-related complications and guide individualized loading protocols in All-on-4 rehabilitation.

Another important finding of the present study was the higher frequency of peri-implant complications, particularly peri-implantitis and screw loosening, in patients with poorer bone quality. These findings are biologically plausible because reduced bone density is associated with lower primary stability, greater micromotion during healing, and less favorable stress distribution around the implant–bone interface, all of which may increase susceptibility to biological and mechanical complications [12, 13, 19]. Finite element analyses have demonstrated that low-density bone is exposed to higher peri-implant stress concentrations under functional loading, potentially contributing to marginal bone remodeling and biomechanical overload [12, 19]. Previous clinical studies have suggested that compromised bone quality may predispose implants to reduced osseointegration and subsequent biological complications, particularly when combined with functional loading and patient-related risk factors [16, 17]. In our cohort, peri-implantitis occurred predominantly in Type 4 bone, while screw loosening was also significantly more frequent in lower-density bone. Nevertheless, these associations should be interpreted with caution. Peri-implant diseases are multifactorial conditions influenced not only by bone quality but also by oral hygiene, plaque accumulation, prosthetic design, occlusal loading, smoking, systemic diseases, and maintenance compliance, several of which could not be comprehensively evaluated because of the retrospective design of the study. Therefore, although our findings support an association between poor bone quality and increased peri-implant complications, they should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal relationship.

In addition to biological factors, prosthetic material selection emerged as another important determinant of medium-term clinical outcomes in the present study. Although metal–acrylic prostheses remain widely used because of their lower cost, ease of fabrication, and simpler repair procedures, they are generally considered more susceptible to mechanical complications such as screw loosening, veneer fracture, and occlusal wear during long-term function. In contrast, monolithic zirconia prostheses offer superior fracture resistance, wear characteristics, and structural rigidity, albeit at the expense of greater manufacturing complexity, higher cost, and more challenging clinical repair [8, 20]. Consistent with these characteristics, our study demonstrated significantly fewer prosthetic complications in the monolithic zirconia group than in the metal–acrylic group. Importantly, this association remained significant after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that prosthetic material itself may contribute to mechanical performance independently of other measured clinical factors. Ayna et al. and Barootchi et al. reported lower rates of prosthetic complications in zirconia-based full-arch rehabilitations than in acrylic-based restorations during medium- and long-term follow-up [7, 8]. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution because prosthetic material allocation was not randomized. Material selection was influenced by patient preferences, financial considerations, and clinician judgment, introducing the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding. Therefore, our results support an association rather than a causal relationship between prosthetic material and mechanical complications. Unlike many previous studies that evaluated prosthetic outcomes descriptively, our analysis incorporated multivariable adjustment for age, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, jaw location, and bone quality, thereby strengthening the evidence that prosthetic material may independently influence medium-term mechanical outcomes.

Despite the higher frequency of implant- and prosthesis-related complications observed in patients with poorer bone quality and in those rehabilitated with metal–acrylic prostheses, overall patient satisfaction remained consistently high across all study groups.

This apparent discrepancy between objective clinical outcomes and subjective patient perception has been reported previously in implant dentistry. Improvements in masticatory efficiency, speech, facial esthetics, and overall quality of life often outweigh the impact of minor biological or mechanical complications, particularly when such complications can be managed without loss of implant function [11].

Consequently, patient-reported outcomes should be regarded as complementary rather than stand-alone measures of treatment success. The absence of significant differences in VAS scores among bone-quality groups in our cohort suggests that medium-term patient satisfaction is influenced by multiple factors beyond implant survival or complication rates alone. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because patient satisfaction was assessed using a single VAS, which primarily reflects overall subjective satisfaction and does not comprehensively evaluate the multidimensional aspects of oral health–related quality of life. Future studies incorporating validated patient-reported outcome measures, such as the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP), may provide a more comprehensive assessment of patient experience following full-arch implant rehabilitation.

The findings of the present study have several practical implications for clinicians involved in full-arch implant rehabilitation. First, careful preoperative assessment of bone quality should be considered an integral component of treatment planning, as patients with low-density (Type 4) bone may require individualized surgical and prosthetic strategies to optimize clinical outcomes. Approaches such as under-preparation of the osteotomy, selection of implants with enhanced macrogeometry, the use of wider or longer implants when anatomically feasible, and careful consideration of loading protocols may help improve primary stability in compromised bone conditions [14, 17]. Second, the significantly lower rate of mechanical complications observed with monolithic zirconia prostheses suggests that prosthetic material selection should be guided not only by economic considerations but also by the patient’s biological risk profile, functional demands, and expected long-term maintenance requirements. Finally, our findings emphasize that treatment success should be evaluated using a multidimensional framework that incorporates implant survival, biological and prosthetic complications, and patient-reported outcomes rather than relying on implant survival alone. Such an individualized approach may improve long-term treatment predictability and facilitate more informed shared decision-making between clinicians and patients.

The present study contributes to the existing literature in several important ways.

While previous investigations of the All-on-4 concept have predominantly focused on implant survival or individual clinical outcomes [21–26], our study simultaneously evaluated implant survival, primary stability parameters, peri-implant complications, prosthetic complications, and patient-reported satisfaction within a single multicenter cohort. This comprehensive approach provides a broader understanding of treatment success by integrating biological, mechanical, and patient-centered outcomes rather than relying on implant survival alone. Furthermore, unlike many earlier retrospective studies [27–33], our analyses incorporated multivariable regression models to adjust for important clinical confounders, together with sensitivity analyses accounting for patient-level clustering, thereby increasing the robustness of the observed associations. Finally, by evaluating the combined influence of bone quality and prosthetic material on medium-term clinical outcomes, our findings provide clinically relevant evidence that may contribute to more individualized treatment planning and risk stratification for patients undergoing All-on-4 rehabilitation.

The present study has several notable strengths. It included a relatively large multicenter cohort with standardized surgical and prosthetic protocols, allowing evaluation of medium-term clinical outcomes under real-world conditions. In addition to implant survival, the study comprehensively assessed primary stability parameters, peri-implant complications, prosthetic complications, and patient-reported satisfaction, providing a multidimensional evaluation of treatment success. Furthermore, the use of multivariable regression analyses and sensitivity analyses accounting for patient-level clustering enhanced the robustness of the findings. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective observational design precludes causal inference and may be susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding. Bone quality was assessed at the jaw level using a combination of CBCT and panoramic radiographs rather than standardized quantitative imaging, and several potentially relevant variables, including oral hygiene, parafunctional habits, and occlusal characteristics, were unavailable for analysis. Therefore, although the observed associations are biologically plausible and consistent with previous literature, they should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed by well-designed prospective studies.

Future research should build upon these findings by conducting large-scale prospective multicenter studies with standardized imaging protocols and longer follow-up periods to validate the prognostic value of bone quality in All-on-4 rehabilitation. Quantitative assessment of bone quality using standardized CBCT-based measurements, together with site-specific rather than jaw-level evaluation, may further improve risk stratification and treatment planning. In addition, randomized or well-designed prospective comparative studies are warranted to clarify the independent effect of prosthetic material on long-term biological and mechanical outcomes while minimizing selection bias. Future investigations should also incorporate validated multidimensional patient-reported outcome measures, such as the OHIP, alongside objective clinical and radiographic parameters to provide a more comprehensive assessment of treatment success. Finally, integrating clinical, radiographic, and patient-centered variables into predictive models may facilitate the development of personalized risk assessment tools for patients undergoing full-arch implant rehabilitation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the All-on-4 treatment concept demonstrated predictable medium-term clinical performance, with high implant survival and favorable patient satisfaction in completely edentulous patients. However, treatment outcomes were significantly influenced by both biological and prosthetic factors. Lower bone quality, particularly Type 4 bone, was independently associated with reduced implant survival, lower primary stability, and an increased frequency of peri-implant complications, whereas monolithic zirconia prostheses were associated with fewer mechanical complications than metal–acrylic prostheses. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive preoperative assessment of bone quality and individualized prosthetic material selection to optimize medium-term clinical outcomes.

Rather than relying solely on implant survival, clinicians should adopt a multidimensional approach that incorporates biological, mechanical, and patient-reported outcomes when evaluating treatment success. Although the retrospective design precludes causal inference, this multicenter real-world study provides clinically relevant evidence that may contribute to more personalized treatment planning and risk stratification in full-arch implant rehabilitation.
