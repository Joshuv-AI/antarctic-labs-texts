Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) encompasses a range of conditions affecting the cardiac muscle and the vascular system, which supplies blood to the heart, brain, and other organs. CVD is the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for nearly one-third of all deaths [1]. Five primary risk factors for CVD include body mass index, systolic blood pressure, non-HDL cholesterol, smoking, and diabetes [2, 3]. These factors significantly impact the heart’s ability to contract and pump blood effectively.

Cardiac activity can be measured using various methods. Electrocardiography and echocardiography, which reflect cardiac activity, are commonly used diagnostic tools for establishing clinical CVD diagnoses [4].

An electrocardiogram (ECG) is an electrophysiological signal representing cardiac activity and is measured non-invasively. The electrical conduction pathway, consisting of the sinoatrial node, atrioventricular node, atrioventricular bundle, and Purkinje fibres, governs the depolarization and repolarization of the heart. This electrical activity is recorded as PQRST waveforms in ECG signals [5]. CVD and other heart-related conditions can cause fast, slow, or abnormal heart rhythms, enabling medical professionals to diagnose these conditions using ECG recordings [6, 7]. Although wearable ECG recording devices, such as a smart watch, have been developed recently, they still face challenges in ensuring comfort for continuous monitoring. Additionally, ECG sensors require a minimum size to incorporate electrodes or patches capable of recording electrical signals effectively.

A photoplethysmogram (PPG) is an optically measured signal that measures blood volume changes in the skin through an optical device, typically recorded at the fingertip or wrist, which are common locations for most wearable devices [8]. Blood volume changes are driven by the cardiac cycle, which pumps blood to peripheral regions of the body [9]. While PPG provides insight into cardiac activity, it does not offer as much detailed information as the ECG. PPG is more cost-effective than ECG and can be recorded by various devices. However, PPG primarily reflects cardiac contraction and dilation, as it is measured based on changes in arterial blood volume [10]. Consequently, PPG lacks detailed electrophysiological information, particularly the PQRST waveforms, which provide an understanding of the cardiac conduction system’s activity. Additionally, we can observe time differences between the R peak of the ECG and the systolic peak of the PPG, known as pulse transit time (PTT). PTT is associated with blood pressure and represents the time for a pulse wave to travel from the aortic valve to the PPG sensor location [11].

The cardiovascular relationships between physiological signals motivate the exploration of PPG-to-ECG translation. Several studies [14–17] have proposed computational models for generating ECG from PPG. However, PPG-to-ECG reconstruction presents two primary challenges. First, this translation involves transforming one physiological signal into another, as ECG measures cardiac conduction activity, while PPG reflects blood volume fluctuations. Second, clinical validation is necessary to ensure reconstructed ECG signals are accurate and clinically meaningful. It is hard to use the reconstructed ECG in the medical field without clinical confirmation.

We hypothesize that a deep learning-based model trained on a large dataset incorporating clinical knowledge can accurately reconstruct ECG waveforms from PPG signals, making continuous cardiac monitoring more accessible. We introduce a generative model for reconstructing ECG signals from PPG signals, which may enable continuous cardiac activity monitoring through PPG-to-ECG translation. We evaluated the reconstructed ECG using standard generative model metrics and clinical assessments to ensure quantitative accuracy and clinical validity. Methods

Related work

With recent advancements in machine learning, numerous studies have leveraged deep learning or machine learning models to analyse electrophysiological signals in applications such as arrhythmia detection and signal enhancement. Hannun et al. [20] proposed a deep learning-based arrhythmia classification model using ECG and demonstrated its performance compared to cardiologists. Cho et al. [21] estimated the ventilatory threshold from ECG signals recorded during exercise. Alcaraz and Strodthoff [22] synthesized ECG signals by integrating diffusion models with structured state-space models [23]. Chu et al. [24] suggested a machine learning-based model for pain monitoring with ECG signals.

Several studies have investigated reconstructing ECG signals from PPG using deep learning methods [15–17].

Sarkar et al. [15] employed generative adversarial networks [25] consisting of an attention-based generator and dual discriminators to generate ECG signals from PPG inputs. The common issue of model collapse in generative adversarial networks was addressed using dual discriminators [26, 27]. They demonstrated that heart rate measurements derived from the generated ECG were more reliable than those obtained from PPG.

Lan [16] introduced a Transformer-based model for PPG-to-ECG translation. Transformers [28], widely used in natural language processing, leverage self-attention mechanisms for tokenizing sequences. Lan [16] incorporated a shifted patch-based attention mechanism, enabling the model to capture more nuanced variations in the electrophysiological signal compared to conventional natural language processing applications.

Dataset and preprocessing

The primary objective of this study is to reconstruct the ECG waveforms from the PPG signals, which requires matching the timing between both signals. In other words, we need signals measured simultaneously from a subject. For this reason, we selected the MIMIC-III Waveform Database Matched Subset [29], a publicly available subset from MIMIC-III [30], which can be accessed through PhysioNet [31]. It provides multiple physiological signals, including simultaneously recorded ECG and PPG, and patient health information. This dataset consists of 22,317 records measured from 10,282 patients across different hospitals. Since the signals were collected outside a controlled environment, some records contain missing or incomplete data, which is undesirable for our study. In addition to the MIMIC-III dataset, we used the WESAD [33] and the PPG-DaLiA [34] datasets, which provide synchronized ECG and PPG signals. The WESAD dataset was recorded under various conditions, including amusement, stress, meditation, and recovery within 15 healthy subjects. The PPG-DaLiA contains physiological signals collected from 15 subjects during a wide range of real-life activities, including 8 tasks: sitting, stair-walking, table soccer, cycling, driving a car, lunch, walking, and working.

Physiological signals from the MIMIC-III database were sampled at 125 Hz. For both the WESAD and PPG-DaLiA datasets, ECG and PPG signals were sampled at 700 Hz and 64 Hz, respectively. We resampled them to 128 Hz for consistency. We preprocessed the signals using a 4th-order Butterworth band-pass filter, with a frequency range of 2 to 40 Hz for ECG and 1 to 8 Hz for PPG [15]. The filtered signals were then normalized to a range of [−1, 1] following previous studies [15–17]. Normalization was required to calibrate the values, as ECG signals were recorded in millivolts (mV), whereas PPG signals are unitless. After the train-test split and preprocessing, we segmented both signals using a 4-second non-overlapping window, resulting in samples containing 512 data points each.

Peak-oriented diffusion model

We employ a diffusion model to construct a PPG-to-ECG reconstruction framework. The proposed model is based on a U-Net [35] architecture consisting of an encoder and a decoder . We modified the 2-D convolutional networks into 1-D to process single-channel signals, as the original U-Net was designed for 2-D image input. A critical aspect of reconstructing ECG from PPG is accounting for the peak time differences between the signals. A slight difference in peaks may induce clinical error in the diagnosis. To address this, we introduce a peak-oriented diffusion model , conditioned on the PPG signal c. The proposed model reconstructs the ECG by considering the peak differences through estimating the R peaks from the PPG inputs.

We implemented a peak predictor consisting of a multilayer perceptron, which used a latent space l derived from the encoder to predict R peaks p. An output length from the predictor is 10, filled with dummies whose value is zero if there are fewer than 10 detected peaks corresponding to each sample. These predicted peaks are concatenated and fed into the decoder. During the reverse denoising process for ECG generation, the peak predictor estimates R-peaks using the latent representations conditioned on the PPG signal. This mechanism allows the model to incorporate PTT information. Finally, the decoder maps the denoised outputs to the signal space, utilizing the latent space, PPG signal, and predicted R-peaks as inputs.

The encoder and decoder are composed of four blocks, each containing four layers: two double convolution layers, a self-attention layer, and a sampling layer, in sequential order. In the encoder, the first convolution layer in each block doubles the feature dimension, while in the decoder, it compresses the feature dimension. During decoding, peak embeddings are concatenated with the features after the first convolution layer. The second convolution layer’s output is passed through a self-attention layer with four attention heads. The sampling layer in the encoder performs downsampling using a strided-convolution layer with a stride size of two, and the sampling layer in the decoder upsamples using the nearest-neighbor method. The sigmoid linear unit [36] is used as the activation function for the convolution layers. An additional convolution layer is added as the output layer of the U-Net to match the dimension of the PPG signal, which is one. Table 3 provides details of the hyperparameters used in this study.

Evaluation

Due to the diverse applications of generative models, various evaluation metrics are employed. While accuracy, F-score, and confusion matrix-based metrics are commonly used in classification tasks, generative models require domain-specific evaluation methods. For example, image processing applications often rely on Inception Score [39] and FD, while natural language processing models are assessed using Bilingual Evaluation Understudy Score [40]. We used RMSE and FD as evaluation metrics for generative ECG signals.

However, these conventional metrics do not effectively capture the clinical characteristics of ECG waveforms. Metrics like Inception Score primarily evaluate fidelity and diversity but do not account for peak timing or amplitude variations, which are critical for ECG analysis. Furthermore, since PQRST waveforms are relatively short compared to the overall ECG signal length, small waveform deviations may have minimal impact on conventional evaluation metrics while significantly affecting clinical interpretation. Given that these five waveforms are essential for clinical diagnosis, even minor distortions can lead to misinterpretations in medical assessments.

To address this limitation, we incorporated a clinical evaluation framework by analyzing waveform morphology and measuring the durations of the P-wave, PR interval, QRS complex, QT interval, and T-wave, referred to as the basic components of the ECG. To assess the robustness of the proposed model, we conducted cross-dataset evaluation by testing the model, trained on the MIMIC-III database, against the WESAD and PPG-DaLiA datasets. These datasets were entirely independent of the training data. Notably, the PPG-DaLiA dataset contains signals recorded under daily-life conditions, encompassing diverse noise profiles and motion artifacts. Additionally, we evaluated the model’s performance under synthetic noise using white Gaussian noise across two distinct experimental configurations. The results from the synthetic noises were analysed by statistical methods. The normality was assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and then we investigated the groups’ population through the Kruskal-Wallis H-test. For the post-hoc, we compared each noise group to the group from the original PPG using the Mann-Whitney U test. We used Bonferroni correction to adjust P values during the post-hoc analysis.

In addition to the ECG interpretation, we evaluated the reconstructed ECGs for their effectiveness in detecting AF and for HR estimation. We used ResNet-50 [42] as a classifier for AF detection. It is essential to verify whether the reconstructed ECG is suitable for clinical use.

Since the original ResNet-50 was designed for image data with three-dimensional input (width×height×channels), we modified the architecture to be suitable for the ECG signal with a two-dimensional structure (time×channels). Specifically, we replaced the 2D convolution and pooling layers with 1D layers. The convolution kernel size was set to 7. The number of layers per block was 3, 4, 6, and 3. Each layer contains three convolutional layers. The last classifier layer, consisting of a multilayer perceptron producing class probabilities, had a dimension of 512. ResNet-50 was trained using the Adam optimizer [43] with a learning rate of . A loss function to train the model is the binary cross-entropy loss. The classifier was trained only by the original ECG, even while evaluating the reconstructed ECG, to assess the clinical usefulness of the reconstructed ECG strictly. Classification performance was assessed using accuracy, recall, precision, and F1-score.

For HR estimation, results were visualized using a Bland-Altman plot [44], which provides a statistical method for assessing agreement between the estimated and actual HR values. In addition to quantitative evaluations, qualitative comparisons were conducted by visually inspecting three signals: the original PPG, the original ECG, and the reconstructed ECG.

The values for the P-wave, PR interval, QRS complex, QT interval, and T-wave reflect differences over specific time ranges, while peak values correspond to individual data points. The overall RMSE of the proposed model is 0.220, 0.268, and 0.254 for MIMIC-III, WESAD, and PPG-DaLiA datasets, respectively. It indicates that the model effectively reconstructs ECG waveforms from PPG signals. The QT interval, which includes the QRS complex, S peak, and T peak, exhibits higher RMSE compared to other waveform components and peaks. This increased error may be attributed to F waves in patients with coarse atrial fibrillation, which likely interfere with the ECG morphology, particularly in the segment spanning from the S peak to the T-wave offset.

Discussion

Principal results

To our knowledge, this is the first study to employ a single diffusion model for reconstructing the ECG from the PPG. We evaluated the ECGs in various ways, including clinical interpretation and assessment under the noise conditions. Our results indicated the high fidelity of the reconstructed ECG in terms of generative output and electrophysiological signal.

With the advancement of a deep learning-based generative model, we believe our proposed model can be effectively utilized in mobile healthcare applications, particularly for continuous monitoring of electrophysiological signals using wearable devices. The important thing in healthcare is cardiac activity, which reflects various information, including abnormalities of the cardiac system directly and the balance of the autonomic nervous system indirectly. The previous studies have focused on the PPG signal, which easily measures the cardiac activity, to translate it into the ECG using a generative model. Generative models grow their development in the image domain, and many researchers adjust these models to the electrophysiological signals. The recent generative model, the diffusion model, outperforms the previous models with stable outputs, though the diffusion model requires higher computational complexity than other generative models. Due to the computational cost, it is hard to deploy our work on a portable device directly. However, we hope that cloud or a high computational system can solve this problem, like recent mobile healthcare services.

Our model shows high fidelity, preserving the main components of the ECG, which is demonstrated by the ECG interpretation with evaluation on the large dataset. Compared with the duration of components in Table 1, there are only a few RMSE differences between the original and reconstructed ECG. The R peak predictor trained the model to learn the cycle of ECG, which is represented through the attention map visualization. From a simple task, heart rate estimation, to a complex task, AF detection, the reconstructed ECGs from the PPGs can be used for clinical purposes. We also confirmed that the proposed model has robustness to the noisy signals. Especially, our model maintains the performance against the motion artifact, the most frequent noise occurring in an out-of-laboratory environment, which shows the possibility of usefulness in daily life. Even though our model promises a clinically acceptable ECG, there is a short latency that disturbs mobile healthcare service in real-time. It takes at least 3.25 seconds to generate a reconstructed ECG, even if the diffusion step is reduced to 50 (T = 50). The proposed model is hard to deploy as a real-time system due to inherent latency; however, its performance is sufficient for daily healthcare services.

Limitations

Figure 10 displays the ECG of a patient with AF and hypertension, and the corresponding reconstructed ECG. The translated ECG accurately preserves PQRST waveforms, aligning well with the original ECG. A major feature of AF is the presence of F waves, which occur continuously and irregularly. As highlighted in the zoomed-in region of interest in Fig. 10, the model struggles to reconstruct these irregular waveforms fully, demonstrating the challenge of capturing fine-grained signal variations.

The upper waveform shows an ECG measured from a patient with atrial fibrillation and hypertension, while the lower waveform presents the corresponding reconstructed ECG. The right inset highlights a zoomed-in region of interest on the left side of the figure (subject ID: p095396/2142–11-06–12-12)

A well-known limitation of diffusion models is their difficulty learning subtle features, as they rely on a denoising process to reconstruct data from random noise. Our observations confirmed that this limitation results in reconstructed ECG signals appearing smoother or more filtered compared to the original ECG. AF represents continuous oscillations, called the F wave, in the absence of observable P waves. The proposed model generates a filtered waveform for the F waves, which results in the classifier being challenged to distinguish the AF samples against the normal ECG. We observed that the smoothing effect on ECGs from AF patients may cause the abnormal ECGs to seem normal, as shown in the AF detection performance in Table 8. The classifier to test how the reconstructed ECGs are similar to the original ECG for AF patients presented worse performance in ECGs from non-AF patients, resulting in low specificity.

Several studies [15–17] have explored ECG reconstruction from PPG using deep learning models. We compare our model’s performance in terms of RMSE and FD against previous models, as reported in Table 9. The results reported in Table 9 were derived from the MIMIC-III dataset. We tried to preprocess the signals identically to those in their studies; all studies resampled the signals to 128 Hz and normalized using min-max scaling. Then, the preprocessed signals were segmented with a 4-second moving window.

Our model achieved an RMSE of 0.220 and an FD of 6.456, outperforming the existing methods in overall performance. These results demonstrate that diffusion-based models offer superior performance compared to other generative models for PPG-to-ECG translation. A GAN-based model suffers from model collapse due to unstable adversarial training between the generator and discriminator, which may degrade the quality of the generated outputs. While the Transformer-based model proposed by Lan [16] achieved reasonable performance (0.29 RMSE), it was less effective than diffusion-based approaches such as that of Shome et al. [17] (0.22 RMSE) and our model. This difference may reflect a modality mismatch, as Transformers were originally designed for sequential data in natural language processing. Whereas diffusion models were originally designed for image generation, they are more suited for reconstructing electrophysiological signals, which have structural similarities to images.

Compared to the two-step diffusion model proposed by Shome et al. [17], which separately handled ROI and non-ROI, our model required a higher number of diffusion steps to achieve optimal performance. Specifically, while they reconstructed ECG signals using only 50 diffusion steps, our model achieved its best performance using 300 steps. Fewer diffusion steps accelerate the sampling time since denoising is performed at each step during the reverse process. They reduced the number of steps by introducing two networks to handle different signal segments. In contrast, our approach focuses on end-to-end training with a single unified network, which optimizes the architecture while maintaining high performance. Our model used fewer parameters than the model proposed by Shome et al. [17] (24 M vs. 45 M), trading off between diffusion steps and model parameters. We experimented with 50, 300, and 1000 diffusion steps. We found that 300 steps provided the best quality, yielding the lowest RMSE and FD values, while only a few decreases in performance occurred when reducing the diffusion step. Besides these evaluation metrics, they suggested CardioBench, consisting of HR estimation, AF detection, affect classification, and diabetes detection. These methods can represent the utility of the reconstructed ECG, but it is hard to assess the quality of the reconstructed ECG directly. For this reason, we used the ECG interpretation to enhance the assessment of the generated signals. This allowed for a more detailed examination of metrics such as RMSD or FD. The analysis of attention maps supplements the clinical interpretability of the proposed model, which demonstrates that our model considers the ECG cycle and R peaks during the denoising process.

Conclusions

We introduced a peak-oriented diffusion model for reconstructing ECG waveforms from PPG signals. The core idea of the proposed approach is to train the model to learn physiological relationships between two distinct signal modalities. To facilitate this learning, we incorporated an R peak predictor within the model, enabling it to capture peak differences between PPG and ECG. This design instructed the model to generate high-fidelity ECG signals, preserving clinically relevant features across a large dataset.

Our results demonstrate that the proposed peak-oriented diffusion model not only outperforms previous PPG-to-ECG translation models [15, 16], achieving an RMSE of 0.220, but also shows better performance than the other diffusion-based model [17] with an FD of 6.456. Additionally, we evaluated the reconstructed ECGs through clinical ECG interpretation, comparing the basic components of the waveforms to those of the original ECG, which confirmed the model’s ability to preserve diagnostic information. Since PQRST waveforms and overall ECG morphology are critical for diagnosing cardiac diseases, detailed analysis of generated ECG signals is essential. Furthermore, we experimented with clinical evaluation of the reconstructed ECG, such as the AF detection and estimation of HR, to ensure its potential applicability in medical contexts.

We believe our findings expand the usefulness of the deep learning-based model to mobile healthcare. The reconstructed ECGs demonstrate utility in heart rate estimation and AF detection while maintaining robustness against noise and motion artifacts commonly encountered in daily life. Although the sampling latency prevents real-time processing on mobile devices, the model is sufficiently reliable for daily healthcare services, and integration with cloud or high-performance systems could further enable continuous ECG monitoring with a slight delay in wearable healthcare applications.

Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. First, although the model translates PPG to ECG effectively, as shown by quantitative evaluations, it struggles to fully capture subtle signal features, a known limitation of diffusion-based models. For example, the reconstructed ECG seems to be a filtered signal from the original. Specifically, the model has difficulty reconstructing F waves in atrial fibrillation patients. We plan to overcome this effect by focusing the model trained on various arrhythmia patients. Second, while we used a large dataset for training and evaluation, the MIMIC-III database includes only patient data, highlighting the need for additional validation with datasets from healthy individuals to ensure broader generalizability.

For future work, the model evaluation with other datasets is required, including those measured in an out-of-laboratory environment, to demonstrate reliability with daily healthcare applications. Also, we plan to improve the model by reducing the latency that occurs during the reverse process. This may confirm the continuous ECG monitoring with a wearable device and suggest the feasibility of ongoing analysis in various healthcare services. Furthermore, we see potential for extending diffusion-based models enhanced with physiological knowledge to other electrophysiological signals, which may broaden the application of generative models in diverse medical domains.
