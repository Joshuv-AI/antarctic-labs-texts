Ultrasound synthetic aperture imaging with SpecularNet for enhanced needle visualization
Authors: Che-Chou Shen, Liang-Chun Tung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeUltrasound imaging has been routinely used for needle guidance due to its real-time capability and cost-efficiency. However, conventional ultrasound with fixed-angle transmission often suffers from reduced visualization of oblique needles when the specular echoes are reflected away from the receiving transducer.MethodsSynthetic transmit aperture (STA) beamforming with delay and standard deviation (DASD) relies on the variation of echo magnitude among various incident angles to construct the image. It can be further integrated with the specular information of the needle shaft predicted by convolutional neural networks (CNN) models to improve the image contrast in needle detection. The proposed SpecularNet estimates the orientation and the specular probability of the needle from pixel-wise STA channel data.ResultsThe proposed specular STA–DASD was validated using experiments. Results indicated that, for needle orientations between 10° and 40°, the specular STA–DASD improved the needle-to-speckle ratio (NSR) by 11.0 dB and the needle-to-artifact ratio (NAR) by 18.9 dB compared to the conventional STA–DASD. Compared to standard specular beamforming, the inference to specular information can be efficiently performed in SpecularNet, while both the NSR and NAR of the corresponding specular STA–DASD also improve by another 25–30 dB.ConclusionThe efficacy of the proposed specular STA–DASD beamforming was demonstrated for enhancement of needle visualization. The specular information could be predicted using SpecularNet with high computational efficiency to guide the transmit/receive apodization and to suppress background noise and artifacts using image weighting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10396-026-01623-x.Keywords: Synthetic transmit aperture, Specular reflection, Ultrasound-guided needle biopsy, Delay and standard deviation, Machine learning

Introduction

Ultrasound (US)-guided needle puncture is widely used in clinical applications, including localized anesthesia [1], tissue biopsy [2], central venous cannulation [3], and percutaneous drainage [4]. However, due to the smooth surface and high density of the needle shaft relative to surrounding tissues, it tends to reflect US signals away from the receiving transducer. Specifically, since the US wavelength is much larger than the roughness size of the steel on the surface of the needle, a needle shaft with a non-horizontal orientation behaves like a mirror, reflecting the incident wave according to Snell’s law. The specular reflection from the needle often reduces the magnitude of the echo being received by the transducer and thus compromises the capability of needle detection in US imaging. This phenomenon is exacerbated as the inclination of the needle increases, leading to diminished or even lost needle visualization. Moreover, clinical US guidance for needle puncture is generally performed using standard B-mode image in which the transmit wave is focused on the steering direction of the scanline. In the case of transmit focusing, the needle shaft is only illuminated with a fixed incident angle, and the resultant reflected wave from the needle shaft may severely deviate from the imaging array, so that no receiving elements can capture the specular reflection when the needle is not horizontally aligned with the surface of the imaging array. This is particularly evident for a needle shaft close to the needle tip (i.e., at the distal end of the needle from the insertion site). Consequently, accurate localization of the needle shaft remains challenging during clinical procedures and heavily depends on the experience of the physician to identify the back-and-forth motion of the backscattering signal from the needle tip.

To address the aforementioned challenges, numerous approaches have been proposed to identify the needle in US images and videos. These include the use of external needle tracking systems, enhancing the echogenicity of the needle shaft, image processing for needle enhancement, and adoption of novel beamforming techniques to accurately localize both the shaft and the needle tip. Previous research has introduced electromagnetic trackers [5] for needle localization, as well as optical navigation systems [6] to determine the needle position. However, external tracking systems for needle detection still present certain limitations. For example, electromagnetic trackers are susceptible to interference from surrounding metallic objects, while optical tracking systems require an unobstructed line of sight to function properly. The echogenicity (i.e., the ability to reflect the US signal) of needle shaft can also be increased by sandblasting, dimpling, or with a polymeric coating, which introduce surface roughness on the needle shaft to improve its visibility in US imaging. These echogenic needles have been commercially available but at higher cost [7–10]. Alternatively, the idea of attaching an active acoustic device or a piezoelectric buzzer to the interventional device has also been proposed [11–13]. This would allow the US image system to estimate the position of the needle tip based on the acoustic signals from the active device. Image-processing solutions mainly rely on the segmentation of specular targets in US B-mode images [14, 15]. For example, local phase information from B-mode images can be used to automatically identify the bone surface using 2D log-Gabor filters [15].

To improve needle visualization using beamforming approaches, researchers have proposed a method that combines focused transmissions with calculation of the standard deviation of the magnitude of the delayed echo across the receiving aperture [16], referred to as delay and standard deviation (DASD) beamforming. However, when the specular reflecting surface is not orthogonal to the transmitted beam, the received energy decreases significantly, leading to reduced image contrast. Furthermore, the use of focused transmissions inherently reduces the achievable frame rate, which imposes further limitations on real-time imaging performance. Adaptive apodization of the receiving aperture weighting has also been developed to emphasize the signal from the needle shaft [17, 18]. This approach is based on the distinct physical characteristics between specular reflectors and diffuse scatterers. Specular reflectors reflect signals according to Snell’s law, resulting in strong energy concentrated at a specific direction. In contrast, diffuse scatterers produce echoes in the form of spherical wavefronts, generating dispersed energy on the receiving aperture. These distinctions can serve as a basis for selective imaging of either specular or diffuse echoes using receive apodization. Moreover, the needle shaft can also be better visualized by changing the angle of view in the imaging process. Some researchers have proposed limited-angle measurements and beam steering to differentiate specular reflections from diffuse backscattering but at the cost of frame rate due to the requirement of multiple transmissions for each scanline [19]. Specular beamforming [20] has also been introduced to enhance needle visualization using synthetic transmit focusing (STA) imaging to acquire echoes of each image pixel with a variety of incident angles for estimation of specular probability and angle. The primary limitation of this method lies in its high computational cost as it requires pixel-by-pixel conversion of the STA data into the coordinate of transmit and receive angles. This makes real-time imaging with specular beamforming challenging.

In this study, we proposed a novel imaging method that integrates STA imaging and DASD beamforming with transmit and receive apodization and specular weighting. Note that STA itself offers a broader range of incident angles for the detection of a specular target and boosts the standard deviation of image magnitude for enhanced visualization of the needle shaft. The feasibility of STA–DASD beamforming has been preliminarily verified [21], but it would be further improved by transmit and receive apodization in STA imaging. A deep learning (DL) model that predicts the angle and probability of specular reflection for image pixels was also developed for comparison with standard specular beamforming for higher computational efficiency, achieving real-time and effective enhancement of needle visualization in the proposed specular STA–DASD beamforming. The combination of a DL model with US STA beamforming has been reported to be feasible [22], but the general goal is to produce high-quality images with a reduced amount of data for a low-channel-count portable system. To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first attempt to use a DL model to classify the imaged object by its characteristics in STA Tx/Rx channel data into either specular or scattering pixels.

STA–DASD beamforming

The strength of STA beamforming is not only its ability to improve image quality via synthetic focusing but also to vary the incident angle of the transmit signal toward the needle for better visualization of the needle shaft. STA–DASD beamforming is expected to outperform the STA-DAS counterpart in detecting needle shafts, especially in scenarios where the received echo signals are affected by the needle orientation. However, the drawback of STA–DASD beamforming is its tendency to generate artifacts due to the nature of single-element transmission for LRI reconstruction. In other words, the limited aperture size of single-element transmission introduces a high clutter level in each LRI and thus leads to artifacts in STA–DASD beamforming. Note that the artifacts appear as a gray mask on the image and reduce the contrast between the needle and the background.

SpecularNet

To acquire the aforementioned needle orientation (i.e., Θ in (9)), the STA Tx/Rx channel data have to be converted from the element-index domain to the angle domain as described in [20] for standard specular beamforming. The conversion depends on the exact position of the image pixel and can be computationally demanding. It is therefore beneficial if the specular information can be directly estimated from the STA channel data in the element-index domain using an efficient DL model. Figure 2 demonstrates the normalized magnitude of typical STA channel data from needle pixels with different orientations and also from speckle background for comparison. It is evident that the STA channel data originating from specular reflectors exhibit one bright streak due to the stronger energy for certain combinations of the Tx/Rx elements. Note that, when the STA channel data come from a needle with 0° orientation, the corresponding bright streak is flat in shape and perpendicular to the diagonal of the STA channel data. The bright streak gets more curved with the orientation of the specular reflector. On the contrary, the STA channel data from speckle background lack the directional pattern of a bright streak and are widely distributed across the entire receive aperture. Therefore, one task of the DL model is to recognize the pattern of the bright streak in the STA data to differentiate the specular reflector from the speckle background, while the other task is to predict the orientation of the specular reflector according to the curvature of the bright streak.

Normalized magnitude of STA channel data from specular reflectors and diffuse scatterers in the speckle background.

The proposed SpecularNet comprises two independent models, respectively, trained for predicting the orientation of the specular reflector and for estimating the probability of specular reflection. For both models, the magnitude of STA channel data is used as the input image of the model. SpecularNet is based on the enhanced framework of EfficientNetV2 to efficiently estimate the orientation and the probability of specular reflection for each pixel within the image. EfficientNetV2 is a convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture proposed by Google, designed to improve both computational efficiency and model performance. It incorporates key architectural improvements, most notably the use of Fused-MBConv blocks, which replace the depth-wise separable convolutions in early layers with standard convolutions to enhance training speed. Additionally, the model employs a progressive learning strategy, wherein training begins with lower resolution images and the input resolution is gradually increased over time. This approach reduces computational costs during early training stages while promoting better generalization and convergence. Unlike conventional scaling methods that uniformly adjust model depth, width, and resolution, EfficientNetV2 utilizes a more flexible design to balance efficiency and speed. Its efficient architecture and optimized training pipeline make it particularly well suited for visual tasks with limited computational resources. Note that EfficientNetV2 was selected in this study not only due to its higher computational efficiency and smaller model size but also its high performance in classification tasks for small datasets as compared to other models, such as ResNet and DenseNet. The output of the prediction model for specular orientation Θ(x,z) is a 1-by-72 vector to consider possible specular orientation from − 90° to + 90° at intervals of 2.5°. In other words, the task of classifying the specular orientation is discretized into 72 classes. One-hot encoding scheme is applied to assign the class corresponding to the true angle to be 1 while all others to be 0. Figures S1 and S2 in the supplementary material demonstrate the architectures of SpecularNet for predicting the specular orientation and probability, respectively.

SpecularNet was trained using the TensorFlow framework. All datasets were derived from experimental measurements. The dataset used for angle prediction consisted of 300,000 samples, with 240,000 allocated for training with the loss function of sparse categorical cross entropy and 60,000 for validation. For the probability prediction model, the dataset comprised 161,210 samples, including 129,000 for training with the loss function of binary cross entropy and 32,210 for validation. To improve the generalization ability and to avoid overfitting under the limited amount of data, data augmentation was performed for the probability model. The data augmentation methods in the training dataset included flipping respective to the diagonal, Gaussian blurring, edge sharpening, and contrast enhancement of the STA channel magnitude as the input image. Both models were trained for 32 epochs with a batch size of 32. The Adam optimizer was employed in both cases, with learning rates set to 0.0001 for the angle prediction model and 0.01 for the probability prediction model, respectively. Note that the ground truth of Θ(x,z) in the training of SpecularNet was directly derived from specular beamforming. In the prediction model for the specular probability Λ(x,z), the output was a continuous value between 0 and 1. Nonetheless, to enhance the separation of the specular pixel from the scattering counterpart in the predicted specular probability, the ground truth of Λ(x,z) was modified from the specular beamforming by keeping only the values within the needle region while setting the values outside the needle region (i.e., in the scattering region) to zero.

The flowchart of image processing for specular STA–DASD beamforming is shown in Fig. 3. In block (A), the STA Tx/Rx channel data are first summed in the direction of the Rx channel to construct the LRIs, and then, the STA–DASD image is produced based on the standard deviation of the magnitude among LRIs. Then, Tx apodization of the STA–DASD image is performed to alleviate the image artifact, as shown in block (B). Meanwhile, the STA Tx/Rx channel data are also used as the input to the proposed SpecularNet to predict the specular probability and the angle of each image pixel, as in block (C). Note that the predicted probability appears as a gray-scale map with values ranges from 0 to 1, while the predicted angle is represented using colors for values ranging from + 90° to − 90°. Given the specular angle, Rx apodization in block (D) is performed to highlight the needle shaft from the background scattering. In the final step, the specular probability is further multiplied with the TxRx-apodised STA–DASD image to produce the specular STA–DASD image with high contrast between the needle region and the speckle background.

Experimental setup

A Prodigy imaging system (S-Sharp, New Taipei, Taiwan) with a linear array transducer (L7.5SC) was used to acquire RF signals of STA beamforming in the experiments. The array had 128 channels and was focused at 25 mm in depth in the elevational direction. The imaging and the sampling frequencies were 6.25 MHz and 25 MHz, respectively. The tissue-mimicking phantom was made of agar with glass beads as background scatterers for needle puncture using a 20G spinal needle with an outer diameter of 0.9 mm and a length of 90 mm. Needle insertion was performed using a biopsy needle bracket that can modify the orientation of the needle shaft to range from 10° to 40° in 10° increments, as shown in Fig. S3 in the supplementary material together with the size of the array transducer and the phantom. Five measurements were performed for each orientation of the needle shaft for statistical analysis. Note that STA beamforming is conventionally performed by sequentially using every element in the array transducer to transmit. Therefore, for the 128-channel array used in this study, it took a total of 128 transmissions and reconstruction of 128 corresponding LRIs to acquire the complete STA channel data. This high number of transmissions and LRI reconstruction, however, limits the achievable frame rate in STA beamforming and makes the estimation of specular information susceptible to motion effect. Therefore, the number of transmissions in this study was reduced from 128 to 32 by only choosing the elements whose index was integer multiples of four for transmit. This increased the achievable frame rate in STA beamforming by four times.

Quantitative analysis

To objectively assess the quality of the reconstructed STA images, two quantitative metrics were employed: needle-to-speckle ratio (NSR) and needle-to-artifact ratio (NAR). These indicators were specifically selected to compare the contrast between the two regions of interest (ROI) of the needle shaft and the surrounding background, as well as to evaluate the needle’s visualization in the presence of image artifacts.

Results

The efficacy of introducing specular information into STA–DASD beamforming was evaluated by comparing the quality of B-mode images reconstructed from experimentally measured STA channel data, as shown in Fig. 4, for the needle orientation of 30°. Besides STA-DAS and STA–DASD images, specular beamforming was also demonstrated as the benchmark of B-mode display and to provide the specular probability and the angle estimated for specular weighting and the TxRx apodization of the proposed specular STA–DASD. It can be observed in Fig. 4 that the NSR of STA–DASD was higher than that of STA-DAS; however, the presence of artifacts also led to a reduction in the NAR. By applying both Tx and Rx apodization, artifacts could be more effectively suppressed, while the needle signal was further enhanced. Compared to imaging with conventional STA–DASD, specular STA–DASD with TxRx apodization achieved improvements of 11.0 dB and 18.9 dB in NSR and NAR, respectively. Note that the performance of the specular STA–DASD method also outperformed specular beamforming in terms of both NSR and NAR.

To further enhance both the performnace and the computational efficiency of specular STA–DASD, estimation of the specular probability and angle can be switched from specular beamforming to the proposed SpecularNet as demonstrated in Fig. 5 for needle orientations from 10° to 40°. It was shown that, on the one hand, the estimated specular probability approached one in the region that agreed well with the location of the needle for all needle orientations. Meanwhile, SpecularNet also produced a specular probability close to zero for image pixels in the background region, which is essential to suppress undesired artifacts and the scattering component for enhanced needle visualization. On the other hand, the specular angle estimated using SpecularNet also effectively indicated the needle orientation in the needle region.

The resultant specular STA–DASD images in the upper and lower panels of Fig. 6 were also constructed using specular information provided by SpecularNet and specular beamforming, respectively, for comparison. The results indicated that specular STA–DASD images constructed with SpecularNet information exhibited markedly lower image artifacts and speckle background, leading to better needle visualization than the counterparts constructed with specular beamforming information. The improvement in image contrast in the case of specular STA–DASD images can mainly be attributed to two factors. First, SpecularNet tends to identify the needle region with a higher probability value (i.e., close to one) as compared with standard specular beamforming. This directly leads to a magnitude elevation in the image pixels in the needle region. Second, SpecularNet is also more capable of lowering the probability of the background region (i.e., close to zero), so that both the artifact and speckle components can be effectively suppressed. In other words, SpecularNet tends to predict whether the pixel belongs to the needle region or not in a binary approach, which facilitates the elevation of the image contrast of specular STA–DASD images. Quantitative analysis of image quality also confirmed that SpecularNet consistently led to an increase in both NSR and NAR, compared to standard specular beamforming, for all orientations of the needle in this study. The NSR and the NAR values in both Figs. 4 and 6 are summarized as a function of needle orientation in Fig. S4 in the supplementary material for comparison. It was shown that conventional STA–DASD (i.e., without Tx/Rx apodization and specular weighting) consistently yielded higher NSR valuses but lower NAR values compared to STA-DAS for all needle orientations. The performance of conventional STA–DASD for needle detection can be boosted by adopting Tx/Rx apodization together with the specular probability, so that the resultant specular STA–DASD significantly improves both NSR and NAR. This is particularly evident when the specular probability and angle are predicted by the proposed SpecularNet. Nonetheless, it should be noted that all beamforming methods exhibit a gradual decline in image quality when the needle orientation increases. The accuracy of the specular angle in the needle region predicted using SpecularNet is also provided in Fig. S5 in the supplementary material using the root-mean-square-error (RMSE). This study also showed that the prediction error generally increased with increased needle orientation.

The computation time for estimation of specular information (i.e., probability and angle) was also compared between SpecularNet and standard specular beamforming. All computations were performed on the Jupyter Notebook platform using test datasets comprising five independent measurements for each needle orientation. The results indicated that the proposed SpecularNet can be about 600 times faster than standard specular beamforming, demonstrating that the DL model effectively boosted the computational efficiency as compared to the conventional estimation of specular information.

Discussion

In the present study, a specular STA–DASD beamforming method was developed to enhance needle visualization using the specular information of every image pixel. The aforementioned specular information, including the probability of the image pixel being a specular reflector and the possible angle (i.e., orientation) of the specular reflector, can be estimated using standard specular beamforming, but it comes at the cost of high computational complexity due to its complicated signal processing to convert the STA channel data from the element-index domain to the angle domain. Therefore, SpecularNet has been further proposed to accelerate the computation time by predicting the specular probability and angle directly from the STA channel data using CNN models. Specifically, by taking the magnitude of STA channel data of each image pixel as the input image, SpecularNet recognizes the bright streak pattern in the input image to differentiate the specular reflector from the speckle background and to predict the orientation of the specular reflector according to the curvature of the bright streak.

When the specular information is acquired, the specular reflection from the needle shaft is highlighted by TxRx apodization of the imaging array and also by pixel-based weighting of the image magnitude to further improve needle visualization in the proposed specular STA–DASD image. Our results demonstrated that the specular STA–DASD outperformed the conventional STA–DASD that does not include the specular information in terms of NSR and NAR. Note that the specular information can be estimated with sufficient accuracy even when the number of transmissions in STA is reasonably reduced for higher frame rate and lower motion effect. For the proposed SpecularNet, it tends to binarize the specular probability of the image pixel and leads to higher image quality in the resultant specular STA–DASD image as compared to the benchmark in this study (i.e., standard specular beamforming).

Though SpecularNet can effectively enhance visualization of the needle shaft, it still suffers from several limitations. For example, SpecularNet tends to eliminate the signal from the needle tip, which may compromise the accuracy to pinpoint the target region in tissue biopsy. This is because, due to the presence of an opening at the needle tip (i.e., the bevel), the echoes from the needle tip are not only generated by specular reflection but also partly come from the diffuse scattering in the needle tip. Moreover, while the proposed specular STA–DASD can effectively highlight the needle shaft, anatomical information is lost due to the significant suppression of the scattering tissue background. Note that visualization of the tissue background is also essential for clinicians to ascertain the relative position between the needle and the target in the biopsy process. To overcome this dilemma, the solution may be the combination of both image information in the specular STA–DASD and the STA-DAS to simultaneously provide the needle shaft and the tissue background. In other words, since STA-DAS beamforming provides a high-resolution B-mode image of the anatomy of the imaged object, it can be further overlaid by the specular STA–DASD using color-shading to unambiguously indicate the region of the needle shaft with simultaneous visualization of the tissue background and the potential needle tip.

Another issue associated with the angle prediction of SpecularNet may be the depth-dependent curvature of the bright streak in the STA channel data. Specifically, even for the same needle orientation, the curvature of bright streak in the STA data will increase when the position of the specular pixel gets shallower in the axial direction. This may explain why the angle prediction even in the needle region appears to suffer from higher error when the needle orientation increases, as shown in Fig. S5. This is reasonable because a needle orientation of 40° inevitably includes specular pixels with more diverse depths as compared with an orientation of 10°, leading to more pronounced prediction error of the specular angle in SpecularNet. For future investigations of SpecularNet, its prediction of the specular angle could be improved by considering including the depth information in the model training or using a bank of CNN models individually trained for each zone of depth in the STA image.

Conclusions

In this study, we proposed a novel US imaging method that integrates STA–DASD beamforming with SpecularNet for TxRx apodization and specular weighting to enhance needle visualization with high computational efficiency. Experiments were performed to validate the feasibility and robustness of the approach across different needle orientations, showing consistent improvement in image quality.
