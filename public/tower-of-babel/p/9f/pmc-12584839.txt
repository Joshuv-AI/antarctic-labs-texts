Evidence for early circulation of the M1UK sublineage of Streptococcus pyogenes in Germany, 2015–2023
Authors: Susann Rößler, Sébastien Boutin, Reinhard Berner, Markos K. Tomidis Chatzimanouil, Meriem Belheouane, Jan Rupp, Matthias Merker, the Dresden GAS Study Group, Nicole Töpfner, Dennis Nurjadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSeveral European countries have reported a rise in invasive Group A Streptococcus (GAS) infections, particularly linked to the toxigenic emm1 sublineage M1UK. In Germany, historical molecular data are limited due to the absence of systematic molecular surveillance.MethodsWe performed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on 189 invasive Streptococcus pyogenes isolates collected between January 1, 2015, and May 31, 2023, at University Medical Center Carl Gustav Carus, TU Dresden. Clinical data were extracted from patient records. M1UK sublineage identification was based on 27 characteristic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). A Bayesian coalescent analysis estimated the evolutionary timescales of the M1UK clade in Germany.ResultsThe most common emm type was emm1 (34%, 64/189), followed by emm12, emm4, and emm89. Of the 64 emm1 isolates, 31 (48%) were M1UK. No significant associations were found between clinical outcomes and M1UK or M1global genotypes. Although a post-pandemic shift favouring M1UK was observed, our analysis indicates that M1UK had already been circulating in Germany by 2017. The estimated most recent common ancestor dates to 2012 (95% highest posterior density: 2009–2015), with a stable effective population size over time.ConclusionOur findings confirm the pre-pandemic circulation of M1UK in Germany. While the clinical impact of M1UK remains unclear, integrating clinical data with high-resolution molecular surveillance may improve early detection of emerging high-risk clones.

Introduction

The post-COVID-19 era has seen a troubling trend in several countries: a significant rise in detections and infections caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (group A Streptococcus, GAS). Of particular concern is the increased incidence of scarlet fever and invasive GAS infections (iGAS), notably affecting young children during seasons with high levels of circulating respiratory tract viruses such as influenza and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) [1]. While annual peaks in these infections are typically expected in late winter and early spring [2], the recent surges in iGAS have been alarmingly high, surpassing pre-COVID-19 seasonal peaks and occurring simultaneously in both the Northern and Southern hemispheres [3–5]. Several factors may have contributed to this post-pandemic increase. The diminished mucosal immune response following pandemic containment measures or immune dysregulation following SARS-CoV-2 infections, which leads to higher susceptibility to viral and subsequent bacterial infections [6]. Additionally, changes in the virulence of GAS are also suspected [4,7–9]. In particular, the emergence of a new emm1 genotype sublineage has drawn attention as a potential contributor to increased virulence and spread [7,10].

The emm1 genotype is circulating globally (also referred to as M1global), is considered to be highly virulent and often associated with invasive GAS infections [10,11]. Nevertheless, the emm1 genotype is not exclusively found in iGAS infections but was also found in colonizing strains and non-invasive GAS infections [12]. The emergence of a novel variant of the emm1 lineage in the post COVID-19 pandemic era has recently attracted considerable attention [5]. This specific emm1 genotype (termed M1UK) differs from the archetype M1global by 27 signature SNPs and exhibits an increased expression of streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxin A (speA) [13,14] than the archetype. The term M1UK refers to the first description of this lineage in England in 2010. A recent study by Vieira et al. suggested that this lineage probably emerged around 2008 [15]. Shortly after its initial identification, M1UK was found to account for 84% of emm1 invasive strains in England [14], increasing to 91.5% by 2020 [7]. Meanwhile, this sublineage has been reported to cause invasive GAS infections in various countries across Europe [16–19].

The presence of M1UK in Germany was recently reported by Wolters et al., who reported that 82.4% of emm1 genotypes belong to the M1UK sublineage [20]. To the best of our knowledge, the study by Wolters et al. is the only study to date providing evidence that M1UK strains are circulating in Germany. However, the study only included strains from October 2022 and April 2023, which coincides with the increase in iGAS infections reported in Germany in the last quarter of 2022, and only provides a snapshot of the S. pyogenes population structure after the COVID-19 pandemic. This study aims to determine whether M1UK could be detected pre-COVID-19 pandemic, i.e. before the current post-pandemic spread of iGAS infections in Germany. Further objectives were to analyze whether M1UK correlates specifically with certain clinical phenotypes of iGAS infections and to determine whether M1UK leads to a more severe course of infection than the archetype M1global.

Methods

Study sample and isolate collection strategy

The study was conducted from 1 January 2015, to 31 May 2023, at the University Medical Center Carl Gustav Carus, Technische Universität (TU) Dresden, a 1410-bed tertiary medical care hospital in Saxony, Germany. With regards to COVID-19 pandemic protection measures in Saxony, the study period was subdivided into a pre-pandemic period from quartile 1 (Q1, January to March), 2015 to Q1, 2020. As well as an intra-pandemic period from quartile 2 (Q2, April to June) 2020 to Q1 2022, and a post-pandemic period from Q2, 2022 to Q1, 2023. Since 2015, beta-hemolytic streptococci (BHS) derived from primary-sterile body samples were collected during routine microbiological diagnostics at the Department of Microbiology and Virology, TU Dresden. Laboratory personnel were instructed to systematically take notice of all BHS isolated from primary sterile body sites and deep-seated infections, such as blood, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural, abscess or joint aspirate, intraoperative bone biopsy or swab, as well as intraoperative intraabdominal swabs and to cryopreserve these strains at −80°C until further use.

Microbiological methods

Species identification was performed by Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF, Bruker Daltonics, Germany). Phenotypic resistance, including inducible clindamycin resistance,was determined using VITEK®2 Streptococcus susceptibility card AST-ST03 (Biomérieux, Germany) and was interpreted according to the valid EUCAST clinical breakpoints of the respective year.

Clinical data

A database search of the laboratory information system was performed for the detection of S. pyogenes in any clinical material. Duplicates were removed after the removal of non-invasive materials. Only one strain per patient was included in the genomic analysis. Invasive material was defined as material collected from sterile sites (blood, central nervous system, intraoperative specimens, punctate) and deep respiratory samples, e.g. bronchoalveolar lavage. Clinical data of patients with invasive S. pyogenes isolates were collected from medical records and reported in a study case report form (CRF).

The CRF included age, sex, year and month of S. pyogenes detection, diagnosis, immune status (competent/suppressed), previous local GAS infection, clinical symptoms and organ manifestations, maximum white blood cell count (WBC) (10^9/L), CRP (mg/L) and procalcitonin (PCT) (ng/mL), duration of antibiotic therapy, administration of clindamycin and/or immunoglobulins, admission to intensive care unit (ICU) or paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), length of ICU/PICU stay, invasive and/or non-invasive ventilation, use of catecholamines, clinical diagnosis, length of hospital stay and outcome. Patients were assigned to only one diagnosis in ascending order of severity.

Genomic characterization by WGS

For each isolate, DNA extraction was performed as previously described [21]. DNA-libraries were prepared from extracted genomic DNA with a modified Illumina Nextera XT library kit protocol [22]. Sequencing was performed with 150 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with an estimated genome-wide coverage of at least 50×. Raw sequences were controlled for quality using fastp (v0.23.1 with parameters −q = 30 and −l = 45) and assembled with SPAdes 3.15.5 (with the option – careful and – only-assembler) [23,24]. Draft genomes were curated by removing contigs with a length <500 bp and/or coverage <10×. The quality of the final draft was quality-controlled using Quast (v5.0.2) [25]. The complete draft genomes were processed through available databases using Abricate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) to identify antimicrobial resistance (NCBI [26], CARD [27], ARG-ANNOT [28], ResFinder [29], MEGARES [30] databases) and virulence factor (VFDB [31]). The species identification of each draft genome was done using mash (sub-command screen) [32] by screening each draft genome to a database composed of a representative genome of each species present in the Microbial Genomes resource (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/microbes/) and the emm types and emm clusters were determined using emm-typer (https://github.com/MDU-PHL/emmtyper). MLST was determined using the pipeline mlst (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst). Furthermore, each genome was aligned to the reference genome MGAS5005 (CP000017) from the Microbial Genomes resource using SKA [33]. The alignment was then analyzed with Gubbins 3.1.6 to calculate the SNP distance, account for recombinations and infer phylogenetic tree via raxmlHPC using 10,000 bootstrap replicates [34]. SNPs were extracted from the alignment to the reference using snippy (4.6.0) and classification as M1UK was based on the 27 signature SNPs previously described [14].

Coalescent-based analysis

To reconstruct the evolutionary history and estimate the time to the most recent common ancestor of the M1UK sublineage in Germany, we employed Beast v.2.7.7 [35] and the concatenated SNP alignment. As priors, we chose a general time reversible (GTR) substitution model with invariable sites and a gamma distribution with 8 categories to account for rate heterogeneity (as suggested by jmodeltest2 [36]). Further, we used a tip-dating approach and the coalescent Bayesian skyline model. Using these priors, we compared a strict and a relaxed molecular clock model with strong support (log10 Bayes factor 57) for the relaxed molecular clock model. To assess the robustness of a molecular clock signal in the data, we correlated the root-to-tip distances of a maximum likelihood tree and sampling time of the M1UK clade with TempEst v1.5.3 [37], and performed a permutation test with lmPerm v2.1 in R v4.4.2 (adj. R2 = 0.62, p = 9.2e−08). In addition, we performed date randomization (5 replicates) on our M1UK datasets using the R package TipDatingBeast v1.1 and ran Beast with a chain length of 30 million, 10% burn-in, sampled every 3000 iterations, and using the same priors as mentioned above. Beast runs were analyzed with Tracer v1.6 [38]. There was no overlap between the substitution rate 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals of the real data and all randomized datasets, further suggesting that the data contain sufficient temporal structure. For the real data, all parameters had an estimated sampling size >400.

Statistical analysis

Appropriate measures of the location and spread of the distribution of sociodemographic and clinical variables were tabulated by M1UK and non-M1UK genotype for n = 64 emm1 isolates. The statistical significance in the distribution was determined by chi-squared test for dichotomous variables and by Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variable (median). We used univariable logistic regression to estimate the change in the odds of having an invasive infection with M1UK compared with the non M1UK in the presence of putative risk factors of these outcomes, together with their 95% CIs, and tested against the null hypothesis (H0) with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.00 using an α of .05. A p-value of <0.05 is considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were analyzed using STATA 18 (StataCorp) and GraphPad Prism v10.0.

Ethics

The study was approved by the institutional review board at the TU Dresden (Study proposal: Phänotypisierung invasiver Infektionen durch Gruppe-A-Streptokokken, iGAS-Study Dresden, Study No: BO-EK-134032024), which waived individual informed consent. The study is supported by the clinical directors of all participating departments (GAS Study Group Dresden).

Data availability

The genomic sequences were uploaded to the NCBI public repository under the Bioproject number PRJNA1236010. Sequencing statistics and the accession number of the genomes are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Study population

We performed a query of the laboratory information system (LIS) for the time period between 1 January 2015 to 31 May 2023 for all detections of S. pyogenes. The search delivered 1851 number of detections over this 8-year period. After removing the non-invasive isolates and duplicates, a total of 322 invasive non-duplicate of S. pyogenes isolates were identified, of which 58.7% (189 isolates) were available for WGS (Figure 1(a)). Isolate availability during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic (2019–2022) was reduced compared to the pre- and post-pandemic eras (Figure 1(b)). The distribution by age and sex of all S. pyogenes detections and sequenced isolates is summarized in Figure 1(c). Only invasive isolates were sequenced and included in the analysis. Clinical and molecular characteristics

Of all sequenced invasive S. pyogenes isolates, 34.4% (65/189) were classified as strictly invasive, having been recovered from blood cultures or cerebrospinal fluid. The most prevalent MLST and emm-cluster were ST28/A-C3 (n = 67), ST36/A-C4 (n = 18), ST382/clade Y(n = 15), ST39/E1 (n = 14) and ST101/E4 (n = 14) (Supplementary table 1). The most common emm-type was emm1 with 33.8% (64/189), followed by emm12, emm4, emm89, emm6.4 and emm28 (Figure 2(a)). Further characterization of emm1 isolates revealed the presence of 27 SNPs defining the recently characterized M1UK sublineage in 31/63 (49.2%) of all emm1 isolates. Two emm1 isolates differed from the M1global sublineage by only 26 SNPs, with the absence of the SNP at the M5005_Spy1439 locus (Supplementary Dataset). In our study population, we found evidence that M1UK was already present and circulating in Germany as early as March 2017 (Figure 3). Detection became more frequent in 2019, with this sublineage being detected almost every month, with the exception of January, June, August and December 2019. Furthermore, the M1UK sublineage accounted for a significant proportion (90.1%; 10/11) of all emm1 isolates in 2019. Prior to 2020 (before the COVID-19 pandemic), the clonal structure of the clinical S. pyogenes sequenced was heterogeneous with 29 distinct emm-types. Post COVID-19 pandemic, we observe a predominant of emm1, both M1UK and M1global, as well as emm12 S. pyogenes, suggesting the success of this clone in spreading in our population. All M1UK isolates harbour speA, speB, speJ, speG. Three M1UK strains harbour speC in addition to speA, speB, speJ, speG. Other emm-types, which harboured the speA gene, were emm28 (1 of 11), emm6 (6 of 6) and emm6.4 (12 of 12). Twenty-seven of 189 isolates harbour the streptococcal superantigen gene Ssa, mainly belonging to emm4 (17 of 27), emm22, emm3.1, emm87 (2 of 27 each), emm87 and emm9 (one each). By using a Bayesian skyline model, we estimated that the most recent common ancestor of the German M1UK isolates dates back to 2012 (95% HPD: 2009–2015), however, with a rather constant estimated effective population size over time, with no measurable impact of the COVID-19 lockdowns and subsequent infection control measurements (Supplement Figure S1). The estimated mutation rate was 1.4 × 10−6 substitutions per site per year (95% HPD: 8.6 × 10−7–2.1 × 10−6).

Antibiotic resistance

We did not detect any resistance toward penicillin in our study isolates. The most common phenotypic resistance was toward tetracycline. In total, 9.5% (18/189) of sequenced isolates were phenotypically resistant to tetracycline. Resistance to this antimicrobial drug class was linked with the presence of tet genes (tetM and tetO). Four of 189 isolates (2.1%) were phenotypically resistant to clindamycin, of which two were inducible resistance. All four clindamycin-resistant isolates were also erythromycin-resistant. The presence of ermA gene was associated with inducible clindamycin resistance, and ermB was associated with clindamycin resistance (Figure 2(a)).

Clinical characteristics of M1UK and M1global sublineages

To understand the clinical presentation and potential differentiation between infections caused by the two circulating emm1 clones, we compared the basic patient demographics, clinical presentation and outcomes of patients with M1UK infection (n = 33) and patients with M1global infection (n = 31). The data are summarized in Table 1. Patients infected with M1UK strains were more likely to present with clinical signs of streptococcal toxic shock, which, however, due to the small number of isolates, did not reach a statistical significance (OR 3.0, 95% CI 0.9–9.8, p = 0.07) and pleural effusion (OR 8.1, 95% CI 0.9–70.0, p = 0.06) (Table 1) in a univariate logistic regression analysis. M1UK infected patients also had a higher maximal leukocyte count with 20 × 109 cells/L versus 16 × 109 cells/L, p = 0.001. However, there were no differences in outcome in terms of duration of therapy, length of stay, need for invasive ventilation, or mortality (Table 1). There were no significant differences in basic demographics.

Discussion

This study confirmed for the first time the presence of M1UK in Germany since March 2017, two years before COVID-19 pandemic in Germany. Our data suggest that before the COVID-19 pandemic, the detection of the M1UK was only sporadic. This sublineage became more common after the COVID-19 pandemic and coincided with a shift in the epidemiology of S. pyogenes causing iGAS infections [39]. Although several studies found that the M1UK sublineage of S. pyogenes was present in other European countries before 2019, the only published German studies so far have only included strains collected after 2022 [20,40].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of iGAS infections in Germany and other countries was low [41,42], suggesting that the pre-pandemic presence of the M1UK strain is not solely responsible for the post-pandemic rise in iGAS infections. Although M1UK has been increasingly identified in invasive infections [5], this does not necessarily mean that M1UK causes more invasive disease or leads to a more severe disease course or unfavourable outcomes. In fact, there is currently no conclusive pathophysiological evidence linking the altered M1UK strain to more severe iGAS infections. The most likely explanation for the rise in iGAS infections is a combination of multiple factors. Reduced mucosal immunity due to COVID-19 control measures, increased circulation of viral respiratory pathogens after the pandemic, and potential immune dysregulation following SARS-CoV-2 infections are all possible contributors that may predispose individuals to bacterial superinfections [43,44]. Our skyline analyzes of the M1UK effective population size (genetic diversity) remained flat, which may reflect limited temporal resolution during the years 2020–2022, when sparse sampling could have obscured the very recent increase in M1UK cases observed in 2023. However, given the small sample size per year and the single-centre design of the study, this observation should be reproduced in a larger cohort.

Our study found no statistically significant differences in clinical outcomes between infections caused by the M1global and M1UK sublineages. The primary distinction observed was an association between the M1UK genotype and cases of pleural empyema and streptococcal septic shock. However, given the limited sample size, this observation should be interpreted with caution and warrants confirmation in larger cohorts. Further, pleural empyema can also be caused by other pathogens, such as pneumococci [45], which saw a similar surge alongside M1UK infections after the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, direct causal evidence linking M1UK to pleural empyema remains inconclusive. On the other hand, the M1UK sublineage has been reported to produce higher levels of streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxin A (SpeA), a superantigen that triggers T-cell hyperproliferation, leading to excessive production of inflammatory cytokines [46,47]. This characteristic aligns with the observed association between the M1UK sublineage and an increased incidence of streptococcal toxic shock, as anticipated.

Invasive GAS infections, particularly those that are toxin-mediated, are undeniably severe and life-threatening, requiring immediate antibiotic treatment and intensive medical care [48]. Even before the pandemic, iGAS infections were associated with high mortality rates [49], reaching over 20% in the UK [50] and approximately 8% in Germany, as documented in an ESPED (“Erhebungseinheit für Seltene Pädiatrische Erkrankungen in Deutschland,” Survey unit for rare paediatric diseases in Germany) survey covering the period from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2019.

While we recognize the importance of integrating high-resolution genomic methods to enhance surveillance of key pathogens, such as GAS, and to monitor their emergence and spread, meaningful insights require robust interdisciplinary collaboration, combining clinical, microbiological, molecular genetics, and epidemiological expertise. Such collaboration is essential for fostering balanced discussions and drawing well-informed conclusions about the clinical significance of novel genetic variants. Representative control cohorts of non-invasive iGAS isolates are indispensable for deeper insights into the pathogenic potential of strains like M1UK. Additionally, systematic assessments of primary clinical data are crucial for reliable and detailed clinical phenotyping. Secondary sources, such as health insurance records or physician reports, often lack critical details. For instance, distinguishing between streptococcal toxic shock syndrome and streptococcal sepsis without toxic shock necessitates precise clinical evaluation, as demonstrated in our previous study [41]. Without such meticulous assessments, analyzes and correlations with superantigens – believed to mediate toxic shock in iGAS infections – risk remains inconclusive. Adopting a multidisciplinary approach can substantially strengthen infectious disease surveillance and advance our understanding of iGAS pathogenicity.

In addition to the emm1 lineage, our study identified a rising incidence of emm12 S. pyogenes responsible for invasive iGAS infections within the study population. In Europe, including Germany, emm-type 12 is one of the most common emm-types linked to GAS infections [40]. Outside Europe, emm12 S. pyogenes has also been widely reported in regions such as Taiwan [51], Hong Kong [52], and India [53], often in association with scarlet fever outbreaks [54]. Given the prevalence of this emm-type in our study, enhanced surveillance measures in Germany are recommended to monitor the emergence and spread of emm12 in iGAS infections.

Our study has limitations, we only included isolates and clinical data from a single centre, which may limit the generalizability of our findings for other regions in Germany. However, the characterization of the longitudinal isolates gave new insights into the presence of M1UK S. pyogenes pre-COVID-19 pandemic in Germany. Only 58.7% of invasive isolates from routine diagnostics were retrieved. During the COVID-19 pandemic, laboratory access was limited and routine cryopreservation procedures were frequently suspended, potentially biasing estimates of M1UK prevalence. Following the pandemic, we observed a reduction in population heterogeneity and a predominance of M1UK isolates, consistent with reports from other European countries, which suggests that its true prevalence in Germany may be underestimated. A larger-scale study will therefore be required to comprehensively assess population dynamics and to conduct a Bayesian dating analysis.

Nonetheless, our study included clinical parameters to compare the M1UK and M1global, which are lacking in most molecular epidemiology studies. Integration of molecular characterization with clinical data revealed that the M1UK genotype was primarily associated with pleural empyema and streptococcal septic shock. Nevertheless, given the limited sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger, independent cohorts. To investigate the virulence differences between the M1, functional assays may be useful. To investigate potential differences in virulence and clinical relevance among M1 lineages, functional in vitro assays and in vivo murine models may provide valuable insights. In this study, we also identified other emm-types, such as emm-12, with epidemic potential, which warrant continuous surveillance. Multidisciplinary epidemiological surveillance should be encouraged to increase the clinical value of findings regarding novel genetic variants. A comprehensive understanding of clinical and molecular iGAS virulence could enrich future infectious disease surveillance and accelerate the translation of these findings into diagnostics and clinical care.
