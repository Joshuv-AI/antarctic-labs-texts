Combining high throughput and precision: millifluidic production of magnetic nanoparticles for biomedical applications
Authors: Lennart Göpfert, Max Schoenen, Eva Miriam Buhl, Thomas Weirich, Thomas Schmitz-Rode, Ioana Slabu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnetic nanoparticles (MNP) offer great potential for applications in nanomedicine and regenerative medicine. For successful clinical translation, scalable and standardized manufacturing methods are crucial to enable the production of MNP with tailored properties and high yields. Here, we present a modular and automated millifluidic synthesis system for MNP production. The system comprises five independently controllable interconnected units, allowing precise tuning of synthesis parameters in each unit and continuous production to reach high throughput. Comprehensive characterization was conducted using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy, dynamic light scattering, and magnetometry, while application-relevant performance was evaluated by magnetic hyperthermia, magnetic resonance imaging, and magnetic particle imaging. A production yield of up to 93%, enabling the synthesis of approximately 680 gFe MNP per day was achieved. By varying the reaction temperature, MNP sizes from 17 nm to 27 nm were obtained. Reproducibility experiments consistently demonstrated good agreement. In depth analysis confirms high crystalline structure, narrow size distributions, and excellent magnetic properties. MNP showed remarkable performance as heating agents in magnetic hyperthermia with an intrinsic loss power of 5.4 nHm2 kg−1, outperforming commercially available MNP. They had good contrast agent capabilities in magnetic resonance imaging (r2 = 529 1/mMs) and moderate ones in magnetic particle imaging. The reproducible, scalable and automated synthesis system provides a robust platform for producing functional MNP, supporting their application in biomedical imaging, targeted therapies, and future clinical translation.

Introduction

Magnetic nanoparticles (MNP) are increasingly investigated for their use in magnetic fluid hyperthermia (MFH) and drug delivery, as they offer unique superparamagnetic properties and functionality.1–4 Recently, MNP have been incorporated into scaffolds, enabling magnetically controlled release of growth factors in regenerative medicine or tumor ablation in cancer treatment.5–8 Several MNP-based products, such as Feridex®, NanoTherm®, and Resotran®, are already on the market as contrast agent for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and heating agents for MFH, while MRI-visible hernia implants have entered clinical use.9–11 As an emerging imaging modality, Magnetic Particle Imaging (MPI) enables background-free, real-time imaging of MNP by exploiting their nonlinear magnetization, promising high sensitivity and quantification.12

For new applications, overcoming hurdles to reach clinical translation requires among tailored application specific MNP-characteristics, many regulatory considerations, pre- and clinical studies to demonstrate robust patient safety, efficacy of the therapy and commercial considerations.13–15 Consequently, this requires manufacturing processes that can be carried out reliably, robustly, reproducibly and on a large scale. In most cases, manual handling protocols to synthesize MNP have high technical variability, lack of precision or power and show low throughput production. In addition, manually performed syntheses require expertise and training. Using automated, continuous processes in MNP manufacturing can overcome these limitations, enabling highly scalable, reproducible, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly production of MNP with tailored properties.16

Scaling is an important requirement in the early stages of development that should not be undervalued. For example, the production of magnetic scaffolds can require several hundred grams of dry MNP per batch. Another hurdle is the design of MNP as high-performance hyperthermia and contrast agents.

To meet the growing demand of MNP for diagnostic and therapeutic applications, various synthesis methods have been developed. Biological approaches use magnetotactic bacteria, offering high biocompatibility and high heating performance but low yield.17 Physical methods, such as ball milling or laser ablation, end in broad MNP size distributions.18 Chemical methods, including thermal decomposition and precipitation, enable the production with high yields and tunable MNP properties.19,20 Precipitation synthesis is preferred for scalability reasons, as it is operable with aqueous solutions at lower temperatures.21–23 This method involves mixing an alkaline solution with an iron salt under stirring, introduced by Massart and Khalafalla.24,25 Recently, continuous manufacturing in millifluidic and microfluidic flow regimes has been developed, offering superior MNP properties compared to batch synthesis.26–32 These systems typically use a reactor to mix reactants, a tube coil for residence time adjustment, and pumps for liquid transport. Advanced mixing techniques, such as jet reactors, improve efficiency by creating strong turbulence, reducing mixing time.23 Reaction quenching with additional reactants can be facilitated to prevent clustering.33

This paper presents a modular, continuous synthesis process for MNP production via oxidative precipitation. The process is designed for high yield and application-specific MNP properties. The quality of the resulting MNP was evaluated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to assess particle size and circularity, electron diffractometry to determine the crystalline structure, and dynamic light scattering (DLS) to measure the hydrodynamic diameter. Magnetization and cytotoxicity measurements were performed. Furthermore, their capability to be used as contrast enhancement, tracer and heating agents was investigated.

Experimental

In the following, a production process is introduced to enable high-scale continuous MNP production while tailoring properties for MFH, MRI and magnetic particle imaging (MPI). The system consists of five serially connected units (Fig. 1A–E). The first unit (Fig. 1A) consists of two peristaltic pumps (Boxer Pump 15 KS, Boxer GmbH, Germany) with stepper motors as drive (model DM556T, Stepperonline Inc., USA), which feed iron salts and bases into the system. The tubing is made of medical-grade thermoplastic elastomer (Innovaprene®, Suzhou Innovapure Co., Ltd, China). A custom-designed Arduino® feedback control system uses load-cell signals from the reactant reservoirs to continuously adjust the peristaltic pumps via a fast integral control loop, enabling stable and adjustable flow rates with a dosing accuracy of ± 0.1 mL s−1. Each reactant has a dedicated pumping system, which simplifies process adjustments.

(B) Reactor with a jet mixer. (E) Mixing coil with a tube and product container. Table S2 in the SI summarizes the parameters characterizing the reactor and quench unit.

The second unit (Fig. 1B) mixes the reactants using a jet reactor with a T-piece (IQS-Standard, Sang-A Pneumatic Co., Korea) and an injection needle (Sterican®, B. Braun SE, Germany). Jet reactors provide superior mixing efficiency and scalability compared to simple T-pieces or micromixers due to their strong turbulence generation.23 For a base and iron salt flow rate of 2.6 mL s−1 each, the flow rate ratio is Rr = 18.75, and the Reynolds number Rer = 1664. This results in a mixing time of τmix << 10 ms (calculated according to 23 see Table S2 in the SI). In contrast, a batch reactor can require several seconds to achieve homogeneous mixing.34 Shorter mixing times reduce local pH, and reactant concentration variations, which is crucial for controlling MNP properties.

The third unit (Fig. 1C) consists of a 100 m long PTFE crystallization coil (PTFE Tube Shop BV, Netherlands), where the reaction temperature can be adjusted from 20 to 98 °C in a 10 L water bath. PTFE is beneficial as it prevents MNP adherence due to its strong negative surface charge (<−40 mV) at pH > 10, reducing clogging effects.35 A 2000 W immersion heater (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, Germany) equipped with a two-point controller between the heating coil and a temperature sensor maintained a stable temperature (±1 °C). This unit significantly influences reaction conditions, as reaction temperature (Tr) directly impacts MNP properties. The coil has a total volume of 1.26 L, a flow rate of 5.2 mL s−1, and a retention time of 4 min. The system ensures efficient heat transfer, enabling uniform crystal growth.34

The fourth unit (Fig. 1D) quenches the reaction by injecting citric acid (CA) through another jet mixer (Jet-Quench). CA stabilizes MNP in aqueous solutions and prevents aggregation of MNP.36,37 Using an additional pump system (see above), CA is fed in at a flow rate of 2.6 mL s−1, while the synthesis product flows at 5.2 mL s−1, resulting in a flow rate ratio of Rq = 12.09 and Req = 2496. The mixing time remains below 10 ms.23 CA quenching is used for producing MNP with core sizes < 10 nm.33

The fifth unit (Fig. 1E) transfers the MNP-CA solution through a 10 m tube into the product container. The millifluidic system, with an inner tube diameter of 4 mm, offers major advantages over microfluidic systems (<1 mm)38 as the system enables a high throughput of 600 g MNP per day (∼28.3 L h−1). The system operates at a residence time of approximately 4 min and 16 s, which is substantially shorter than the overall process durations typically reported for thermal decomposition syntheses (e.g., several hours).31 Its robust design minimizes clogging and fouling, thereby enhancing process efficiency and resource utilization. Due to the larger tube diameters, the millifluidic approach offers superior scalability and cost-efficiency, as it is easier and more economical to manufacture than micromixer-based systems.39 Additionally, the system can facilitate real-time monitoring through the seamless integration of sensors, allowing precise control of synthesis parameters.40 Furthermore, the modular system design allows easy integration of additional units, such as filtration or centrifugation modules. The Arduino®-based control system enables flexible tuning of parameters like flow rates and temperature, supporting the production of MNP with tailored properties. These advantages contribute to process intensification, making the system cost-efficient, sustainable and safe.

In the oxidative precipitation synthesis route applied in this study, a reduced metal precursor (Fe2+) is employed, which is oxidized to Fe3+ during the precipitation process. This pathway can promote the formation of larger magnetite or maghemite cores.41 Classical coprecipitation involves a rapid burst of nucleation, leading to a high number of nuclei and consequently limiting particle growth, which results in smaller nanoparticle sizes.36 In contrast, synthesis routes that reduce the nucleation rate can provide more precursor per nucleus and thus promote the formation of larger particles. The oxidative precipitation synthesis route and its underlying reaction mechanisms have been investigated in previous studies, which provide comprehensive insights into intermediate phase formation, oxidation kinetics, and particle growth pathways.42,43 Oxidative precipitation synthesis of MNP is governed by the base/OH− concentration or pH, oxidant concentration, salt counterions, solvent composition, dissolved gas atmosphere, flow configuration, and complexing additives, which collectively affect particle size, morphology, surface charge, and magnetic properties.43–46

Materials and methods

The precursor concentrations for iron salt solution and base were selected based on a previously reported oxidative precipitation protocol.42 To enable continuously driven MNP synthesis, 250 mL of deionized water were initially added for each of the three solutions (iron salt solution, base solution, and citric acid solution) and purged with nitrogen for at least 15 min. For the preparation of MNP, the iron salt solution was prepared by adding 3.6 g ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, Ph. Eur., USP, purity ≥ 99.5%; 51.8 mmol L−1), 0.4 g iron(iii) chloride hexahydrate (Merck KGaA, ACS, Reag. Ph. Eur., for analysis; 5.9 mmol L−1), and 0.2 mL sulfuric acid (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, measured solution, 2.5 mol L−1; 2.0 mmol L−1). The base solution was prepared using 1.31 g potassium nitrate (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, Ph. Eur., BP, purity ≥ 99%; 51.8 mmol L−1) and 1.68 g sodium hydroxide (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, p.a., ISO, purity ≥ 98%; 168 mmol L−1). For the coating solution, 2.4 g citric acid (CA) (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, p.a., ACS, anhydrous, ≥ 99.5%; 50.0 mmol L−1) was added. Ammonia water (Merck KGaA, ISO, Reag. Ph. Eur., 25%, for analysis) and sulfuric acid (Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, measured solution, 2.5 mol L−1, 5 N) were used for pH adjustment.

Synthesis of MNP

All solutions were purged with nitrogen for at least 15 min before usage and have a total volume of 250 mL each (iron salt solution, base solution, and CA solution). Per experiment, the iron salt liquid and base were pumped into the reactor at a rate of 2.6 mL s−1 (cf.Fig. 1). As soon as the first MNP emerge from the crystallization coil, the CA solution was pumped into the system via quenching unit. MNP were produced at different temperatures in the crystallization coil (indicated as reaction temperature) to characterize the process. The reactants contain 803.5 mg of iron in each experiment. The individual samples have the abbreviation 37MNP for a reaction temperature of 37 °C, the abbreviation 47MNP for a reaction temperature of 47 °C, and so forth up to 97MNP for a reaction temperature of 97 °C. The MNP prepared in this way were then stirred for one hour at pH 4. For scalability studies, mixing took place via magnetic stirrer, for all other experiments an electric impeller was used. Afterwards, the synthesis product was centrifuged at 3939G (4500 rpm) for 10 min. The resulting pellet was then dispersed in deionized water.

Characterization of MNP

The prepared MNP were analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS), electrophoretic light scattering (ELS), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID). Iron concentration of the MNP were determined by Fe3+ complexation, using Tiron as an indicator. Application specific properties for the MNP have been investigated for magnetic particle imaging (MPI), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic fluid hyperthermia (MFH).

Cytotoxicity assays

American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) fibroblast cell lines (L929) were cultured in RPMI 1640 (DMEM, Gibco, Invitrogen, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Invitrogen, USA) in humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. CellTiter Glo® assay (Promega, Madison, USA) was used to determine cytotoxicity according to the supplier's instructions. In triplicate, L929 cells were seeded at a concentration of c = 100 000 cells per mL and allowed to incubate with the synthesized MNP resp. Resovist® for 24 h. Magnetic resonance measurements

Magnetic resonance measurements were performed using a clinical MRI scanner (3T Ingenia Elition, Philips Healthcare, The Netherlands) and a body coil. Samples were placed in a phantom containing polyacrylic acid and sodium chloride according to ASTM standards.53 MNP samples were prepared with eight concentrations of iron ([Fe] = [0.025, 0.05, 0.075, 0.1, 0.125, 0.15, 0.175, 0.2] mM). For the study of transverse relaxivities (r2), a Turbo Spin Echo (TSE) sequence (TR = 1500 ms, 32 echoes, TE = [6.05, 12.1, 18.15, … 193.7] ms, FOV = 366 × 366 mm, matrix 512 × 512, slice thickness 3 mm, flip angle 90°) was performed. For the study of longitudinal relaxivities (r1) an inversion recovery spin echo (IRSE) sequence (repetition time TR = 5000 ms, 1 echo, echo time TE = 12 ms, inversion times TI = [50, 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1200, 1400, 1600, 2000, 2500, 2900, 3300] ms, field of view FOV = 320 × 320 mm, matrix 256 × 256, slice thickness 5 mm, flip angle 90°) was performed. Transverse and longitudinal relaxivity were calculated with R1/2 being the transverse relaxation rate of the MNP sample and R1.0/2.0 being the respective relaxation rate of the solvent. Relaxation rates of deionized water were used as reference.

Magnetic particle imaging

The system matrices for magnetic particle imaging were obtained using an MPI system (Bruker BioSpin® MRI GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) equipped with a specialized 1D RX coil insert designed for mice, functioning as the receiver coil in the x-direction. Data were collected for system matrices consisting of 5 × 5 × 5 voxels with a field of view (FOV) of (6 × 6 × 6) mm3. The iron concentration in the samples was 0.316 mgFe mL−1, and all samples were contained in plastic capsules with a sample volume of 15.625 µL, provided by Bruker. In all spatial directions, the drive field amplitude was set at BDrive = 14 mT, and the maximum gradient strength was Gmax = 2.5 T m−1. The system matrices were averaged 1000 times, and background correction was applied using measurements conducted during each individual acquisition of the system matrix. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was calculated and plotted. To assess the decay of signal amplitude at higher harmonics, the ratio A7/A5 of the amplitudes of the 7th to the 5th harmonics was determined.

Results and discussion

To produce MNP with optimized performance for MFH, MRI and MPI (Section 2), a continuous millifluidic synthesis method was developed. For this, the reaction temperature, a key factor influencing MNP properties,29 was varied between 47 °C and 97 °C. Samples are denoted according to the used reaction temperatures (cf.Table 1). MNP size and iron yield were analyzed via DLS, TEM, and photometry to calculate iron concentration (cf. Materials and methods). TEM analysis shows an increasing core size with rising temperature (Fig. 2A–F). The smallest core diameters were obtained with 47MNP (17.5 ± 4.2 nm). 47MNP shows (Fig. 2A), both rod-shaped and spherical MNP, while sample 57MNP (Fig. 2B) shows only spherical MNP. An increase in size of 56% was observed by increasing the temperature (cf. diameters size of 57MNP and 97MNP, Fig. 2B–F). DLS data indicate small hydrodynamic diameters and reduced standard deviations at higher temperatures (Table 2). Circularity analysis confirms high uniformity (e.g., (0.96 ± 0.02) for 77MNP, (0.97 ± 0.015) for 97MNP), which potentially enhances magnetic field response. MNP formation follows oxidation pathways influenced by OH− excess, pH, and Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio.56,57 Rod-like structures likely represent precursors such as akaganeite or goethite.58–61 Rapid base-iron salt mixing favors magnetite formation via iron hydroxides and lepidocrocite.56 A residence time that is long enough promotes the full conversion to magnetite, while the addition of CA selectively removes the unwanted phases.56 Higher reaction temperatures seem to accelerate crystallization, leading to larger core sizes,62 while CA quenching stabilizes MNP and reduces hydrodynamic diameter.33,56,63 The millifluidic closed system prevents oxidation, ensuring phase control. The larger core diameters observed for sample 97MNP may additionally result from the combination of quenching at elevated temperature and the subsequent centrifugation step. Quenching with CA at high temperature can lead to smaller hydrodynamic diameters,33 which may correspond to smaller MNP cores. During the subsequent centrifugation step, these smaller particles may remain in the supernatant and thus be partially removed from the final product. Size-selective modifications induced by centrifugation have previously been reported in the literature.64 Iron yield serves as an indicator of production scalability. At 47–57 °C, yields are low ((11.7 ± 0.2) %). Between 57 °C and 77 °C, the yield increases sharply to (92.3 ± 4.4) % but declines at 87 °C ((48.5 ± 3.4) %) and 97 °C ((42.9 ± 1.6) %) (Fig. 2G). The lower yield at high temperatures can be attributed to smaller hydrodynamic diameters, causing increased loss during centrifugation. A potential daily iron production reaches ∼676 g for 77MNP. The estimated cost per gram (only educts) is < 1.2 €/gFe for 97MNP and < 0.53 €/gFe for 77MNP, with production rates of 12.9 gFe h−1 and 27.8 gFe h−1, respectively.

To evaluate the scalability of the continuous synthesis, a 120 min long-term run was performed to demonstrate sustained reactor operation over extended process time. Here, the influence of process time on the dhyd of the MNP was investigated. Samples were taken from the modular MNP synthesis process every 10 min in a time range between 40 and 120 min and subsequently analyzed via DLS. The corresponding volume-weighted size distributions of the dhyd with a fit to the PDF of the log-normal distribution are displayed in Fig. S6 in the SI. The results show MNP dhyd between 90.22 nm and 114.69 nm. The diameter difference between the average of all recorded values between 40 and 120 min (102.03 nm) and the one at a specific timepoint in the production line was calculated to evaluate the stability of the process. The largest differences occurred at 40 min (12.41 nm) and 80 min (11.58 nm), while the smallest differences were measured at 50 min (1.48 nm) and 110 min (2.17 nm). The average difference over all time points was 7.47 nm. Larger differences between MNP size that occurred at 40 min and 80 min may be due to the manual handling of the downstream process. In comparison to reproducibility (Fig. 2I) and performance tests (Fig. 4) magnetic stirrers were used for scalability studies, which could explain the different dhyd. Larger differences in MNP size observed at 40 min and 80 min may be attributed to manual handling during downstream processing, which currently represents a limitation for fully continuous large-scale operation and may introduce variability during particle separation, washing, and sample collection. In particular centrifugation is presently performed in batch mode and limited to comparatively small processing volumes (∼200 mL per run), which may constrain further scale-up unless continuous separation strategies are implemented. Future work will further focus on automating the downstream process to further minimize manual handling and increase process reliability.

Reproducibility studies confirm the stability of the synthesis process: three independent synthesis under identical process conditions yielded in a dhyd of (75.2 ± 29.0) nm, (79.4 ± 32.8) nm, and (87.3 ± 38.4) nm. The corresponding volume-weighted size distributions of the hydrodynamic diameters with a fit to the PDF of a log-normal distribution the reproducibility study are displayed in Fig. S5 in the SI. The mean value of all approaches was (80.6 ± 33.4) nm, which means that the results are scattered in a narrow range around the mean value (Fig. 2I). Taken together, the findings support the conclusion that the synthesis process offers scalability, cost-efficiency, and operational robustness. The process needs further optimization, especially by automation of downstream operations, to achieve tighter reproducibility.

Application specific MNP analysis

Samples 77MNP and 97MNP were selected for further analysis due to their high yield (77MNP) and tailored core sizes with a narrow size distribution (97MNP) for hyperthermia and magnet-based imaging.65,66 Their crystallographic structure and magnetization properties were investigated using a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM, Fig. 3D–I) and a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID, Fig. 4A). Selected area electron diffraction (SAED, Fig. 3A–C) was performed to verify phase composition and determine the lattice parameters. Due to the limited number of nanocrystallites within the SAED field aperture, the obtained ring patterns were incomplete (Fig. S1 and S2) and unsuitable for precise interplanar spacing analysis. Instead, pseudo-powder diffraction profiles were generated (Fig. 3B and C) based on three SAED patterns per sample. Initial analysis indicates agreement with spinel-type Fe3O4 (a ≈ 8.4 Å, space group Fd3̄m #227). A simulated electron powder diffraction pattern (FULLPROF67) confirmed the close match between experimental and theoretical profiles (Fig. 3A). Lattice parameters were determined via full-pattern LeBail fitting,68 yielding a = 8.405(10) Å for 97MNP and a = 8.402(2) Å for 77MNP. A secondary refinement approach using manually determined peak positions provided a = 8.401(2) Å (97MNP) and a = 8.393(2) Å (77MNP). Experimental details are provided in the SI section (pages S1–S11). HRTEM analysis indicates the high crystallinity and structural quality of both samples. The surface of 77MNP appears rougher than 97MNP (cf.Fig. 3D and H) likely due to higher susceptibility to CA treatment. This could result from a larger surface-to-volume ratio, exposing more surface area to CA. Literature reports indicate op to 8% reduction in MNP size due to surface ablation by CA.56 While thermal decomposition yields defect-free MNP with high saturation magnetization, it requires toxic organic solvents and long synthesis times (up to 40 h), making precipitation-based synthesis an efficient alternative for high scale production. Diffraction methods cannot distinguish magnetite (Fe3O4) from its oxidized counterpart maghemite (γ-Fe2O3).69 Here, magnetite was assumed as the dominant phase. Both samples exhibited uniform, undistorted crystal structures, indicating single-domain particles, consistent with expectations for magnetite MNP below (20–30) nm.70 Saturation magnetization is influenced among others by the iron oxide phase, core size, crystal structure, and surface coating.71 It serves as an indicator of crystallographic changes and synthesis quality. Fig. 4A displays the magnetization curves of sample 77MNP and 97MNP. Sample 77MNP exhibits the highest saturation magnetization, followed by Resovist®32 and 97MNP, cf.Table 2. In comparison, bulk magnetization of magnetite is 127 Am2 kg−1.72 The high magnetization of 77MNP and 97MNP is attributed to their high crystallinity and the absence of structural disorder (Fig. 4A). Magnetite has the highest bulk magnetization among iron oxides, surpassing maghemite and non-magnetic phases such as wustite.73 As the direct coating in the continuous process prevents oxidation, influences on magnetization are expected to come from MNP size.

In general, magnetization was shown to increase with core size in the 1–10 nm range,74 however, for larger MNP, multidomain splitting may occur leading to lower magnetization values. The latter explains the lower magnetization value for 97MNP compared to the one of 77MNP. Continuously operated microfluidic systems produce MNP with similarly high saturation magnetizations.46 Stirred tank reactors in series for ageing/oxidation to magnetite under continuous flow conditions reach slightly lower Ms-values.43 The SLP and ILP of 77MNP, 97MNP and, as reference, of Resovist® (Fig. 4B and Table 2) were determined to estimate their hyperthermia performance (cf. Chapter 3.2.4). The SLP of 97MNP is 4.5 times higher than that of Resovist® and the maximum temperature difference is about 2.8 times higher. High SLP values are advantageous for MFH, particularly for systemic applications of MNP reaching low concentrations at target sites.75 For high SLP values, the interplay between field parameters (amplitude H, frequency f) and MNP properties, including core size, hydrodynamic diameter, agglomeration, and magnetization plays is key.76 Heat generation occurs via Néel and Brownian relaxation. Larger MNP undergo a transition from Brownian-to Néel-dominated relaxation,77 with 77MNP and 97MNP expected to be Brownian dominated.78 Field amplitude and magnetic anisotropy influence relaxation, necessitating combined Néel–Brownian modeling to optimize heating efficiency.79 Interparticle interactions also affect heating performance, influencing the collective magnetic response. For the different applied magnetic fields, the ILP values show a deviation. The ILP formalism is primarily applicable at low magnetic field amplitudes, where the SLP follows the expected quadratic dependence on the applied field strength.80,81 As the field amplitude increases, however, this quadratic behaviour progressively breaks down and the SLP-field relation transitions toward a linear regime. Consequently, ILP values decrease at higher field amplitudes, not because the material becomes intrinsically less efficient, but because the ILP definition itself assumes a quadratic dependence that no longer holds under these conditions. This highlights an important conceptual limitation of ILP: while it is useful for comparing materials under standardized low-field conditions, it should not be interpreted as a universal measure across the full range of experimentally relevant field strengths. In practical hyperthermia applications, where higher fields are often employed, the SLP itself provides a more direct and physically meaningful measure of heating performance.

The role of the above-mentioned parameters and properties on SLP was investigated in many studies. For example, it was shown that MNP with high magnetic anisotropy and non-spherical shapes (e.g., cubes, rods) exhibit enhanced heating efficiency.82,83 The core sizes of 77MNP and 97MNP align with optimal values (∼20 nm) for efficient heat generation.29,84,85 SLP shows a tendency to increase with increasing core diameter, although the effect is less pronounced for larger MNP core sizes from around 25 nm.86 Spherical MNP agglomeration lead to a reduction of heating output.87,88 This might also influence the heating output of 77MNP, as agglomerations are indicated by the difference between core and hydrodynamic size (Table 2).

HRTEM and diffraction data confirm a defect-free magnetite crystal structure in both samples (Fig. 3), contributing to high magnetization and heating performance.89 CA coating induces a magnetically frustrated layer, affecting smaller MNP more strongly 90 which could explain the lower heating output of 77MNP. Also, MNP core sizes at the boundary between single-domain and multi-domain can lead to high heating capacities 57 which is the case for 97MNP. ILP values (97MNP: (5.4 ± 0.26 nHm2 kg−1)) are higher than commercially available MNP.81 97MNP and 77MNP at a concentration of 1 mg mL−1, (f = 95 kHz and H = 19 kA m−1), achieve a substantial therapeutic temperature increase (ΔT > 15 °C, cf. Fig. S9 in the SI) while remaining within the acceptable safety limit of H·f ≤ 5 × 109 A m−1 s−1.91 Considering that a temperature increase of ∼6–8 °C is sufficient to achieve therapeutic efficacy in hyperthermic cancer treatment, this provides a substantial level to reduce either MNP concentration or field parameters to minimize potential adverse effects in patients.92 Magnetic nanoflowers exhibit higher ILP (up to 8.4 nHm2 kg−1),93 but their synthesis is complex and difficult to scale. Alternative strategies, including doping with Zn or Mg and shape tailoring, have been explored to enhance heating efficiency.83,94,95

77MNP and 97MNP were evaluated as contrast agents and compared to FDA-approved contrast agent Resovist®. Higher r2-values enhance negative contrast. The MNP in this work have r2-relaxivity values up to 2.8 times higher than the ones of Resovist® (Fig. 4F and G). In general, r2 is influenced by magnetic susceptibility, core size, and agglomeration.96,97 Measurement settings and MRI field strength also play an important role.98 The high r2-values of 77MNP and 97MNP can be explained by their high magnetic susceptibility and crystallinity (Fig. 3, Section 3).

r 1-Values, determining positive contrast potential, were also measured (Fig. S7). The r2/r1 ratio defines the contrast type, with higher values for negative contrast.99 For 97MNP, r2/r1 = 28.9, and for 77MNP, r2/r1 = 90.8, a strong negative contrast effect is expected, surpassing literature-reported values.100–102 In comparison, hydrothermal-synthesized magnetite nanorods exhibit 13% higher r2, but the synthesis requires the use of toxic solvents, high temperatures (200 °C), and long synthesis durations, limiting industrial applicability.103

MPI performance was assessed by measuring signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) up to the 15th harmonic (Fig. 4C). The amplitude decay ratio A7/A5, a key quality parameter, was evaluated. Although MNP with ∼27 nm core sizes were reported to be optimal for MPI,104 77MNP and 97MNP exhibited A7/A5 ratios ∼50% lower than expected, suggesting suboptimal MPI response compared to Resovist®. As MPI and hyperthermia are both based on the non-linear dynamic magnetic susceptibility, the relaxation dynamics play a crucial role in reaching high performance. Further, particle interactions strongly influence MPI signals, as observed for aggregated MNP.105 Improved understanding of relaxation mechanisms and further optimization of MNP properties are necessary to create high-performance MPI tracers.85

All samples, 77MNP, 97MNP (CA-coated), and Resovist® (dextran-coated), were tested for cytotoxicity (Fig. 4D). Cell viability decreases with increasing iron concentration, with significant differences observed at 31.62 µgFe mL−1. At 1000 µgFe mL−1, viability sharply decreases. There are many possible factors: A major influence on cell viability is the amount of internalized MNP. MNP uptake occurs via phagocytosis and endocytosis, influenced by hydrodynamic diameter, surface charge, aggregation, and stability in biological media.106,107 Internalization kinetics must be assessed for each MNP type.108–110 Negatively charged CA-coated MNP exhibit up to three times higher cellular uptake than dextran-coated MNP, explaining lower cell viability (cf. zeta potential in Table 2).111,112 Faster degradation of CA-coated MNP further contributes to cytotoxicity differences.113 Surfactants influence imaging performance. Biocompatible coatings such as dextran, polyethylene glycol, and CA improve stability and circulation time.73,96 Thus, CA-coated MNP offer advantages for functionalization in biomedical applications, including drug delivery.114 Clinical trials confirm sufficient blood circulation half-lives for CA-coated MNP.96,115,116 Manufacturing processes significantly affect MNP purity, impacting cytotoxicity. Thus, higher purity can be achieved through dialysis, filtration, magnetic separation, and centrifugation, removing excess coating agents, synthesis byproducts, and ions, thereby reducing toxicity.117

Application prospects and current limitations

Clinical translation of MNP therapies depends on the reproducibility, scalability, and efficacy of MNP production. Further, extensive characterization is required to establish MNP as viable diagnostic and therapeutic agents. This study focuses on optimizing MNP production, emphasizing high-yield and cost-effective synthesis (cf. production yield analysis in Section 3). The millifluidic system enables controlled, standardized, and reproducible MNP synthesis, crucial for medical applications. Specifically, CA-coated MNP offer functionalization potential via carboxyl groups on the MNP surface, facilitating covalent binding to biomolecules such as proteins, antibodies, or drugs.63 Further system optimizations are necessary, including refining hydrodynamic size distributions through flow rate adjustments, increasing saturation magnetization via alternative reactant compositions, and enhancing yield by optimizing reactant concentrations. Long-term stability studies are essential to assess storage capabilities under different conditions. Advanced characterization techniques, such as Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, dynamic magnetic susceptibility, thermogravimetric analysis, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and Mössbauer spectroscopy, could provide deeper insights into MNP chemical and magnetic properties.118 For clinical applications, in vitro cell studies must be followed by in vivo animal models and, eventually, human trials.119

The high r2-values make the synthesized MNP promising as negative MRI contrast agents, suitable for early detection of tumors, inflammation, and other pathological changes.120 As an example, MNP have been explored and are of high relevance in diagnosis for neuro-oncology and central nervous system diseases.121 The demand for alternative contrast agents is driven by concerns over gadolinium (Gd)-based agents, which exhibit short circulation times and toxic Gd ion release, particularly in patients with renal impairment.122,123 Additionally, Gd accumulation in brain tissue raises safety concerns,124 reinforcing the need for safer MNP-based MRI agents. There is growing interest in novel agents for advanced diagnostic applications, such as tumor imaging in combination with immune cell tracking.125

The versatility of the millifluidic system could facilitate MNP production for applications such as magnetic cell separation, MPI, and T1-weighted MRI contrast agents. Combining the continuously driven, modular, millifluidic flow system presented here with in-line measurement methods and AI-driven decision-making126,127 could make it possible to implement a self-driving laboratory. Thanks to continuous flow, this lab could conduct more experiments and create more controlled environments than batch approaches.128,129 Integrating advanced analytical methods is crucial for generating training data and ensuring product quality.130 These advancements could enhance synthesis efficiency, reduce errors, and improve reproducibility, paving the way for scalable and clinically viable MNP applications.

Beyond diagnostics, MNP offer various therapeutic applications, including MFH, which selectively destroys tumor cells while minimizing damage to healthy tissue. MFH can also enhance drug delivery by enabling thermally controlled drug release.131 MNP-functionalized biodegradable scaffolds have been investigated for bone tumor therapy,132,133 and MNP-embedded implants allow MRI-based position monitoring and MFH-based tumor treatment in hollow organs.82,134 The combination of imaging and therapy enables image-guided interventions, with MPI-MFH fusion allowing real-time targeted heating. Custom-built MPI-MFH devices have been developed for MNP localization and controlled heating.135,136 MPI has potential for in vivo cancer imaging, cardiovascular imaging, acute stroke detection, and stem cell tracking.137,138

Conclusion

The presented production process is based on a continuous, modular, and scalable millifluidic oxidative precipitation approach, enabling precise control over mixing and reaction conditions, followed by controlled quenching and functionalization. With this system fast mixing times (τmix << 10 ms) at the beginning of the reaction and high production rates (28.3 L h−1) can be achieved. Overall important synthesis parameters (e.g. mixing conditions and reaction temperature) can be adjusted precisely and independently, as all components controlling the synthesis are modularly integrated. Due to the millifluidic production method, clogging is avoided. As the synthesis is performed in an aqueous environment, a low environmental impact and low costs are expected. By varying the reaction temperature, the size of the MNP can be tailored in the range of 17 nm-27 nm. The core size of the MNP is important for a wide range of applications and can be customized with the system for specific applications. It was demonstrated that the MNP are promising for various biomedical applications, such as contrast and hyperthermia agents as well as tracers. The 97MNP reached an ILP value of (5.4 ± 0.26) nHm2 kg−1, which is higher than commercially available stable MNP.81,139 For 77MNP, an r2-value of (528.7 ± 10.2) 1/mMs was achieved, showing favorable properties as contrast agents in MRI. The EC50-value of up to (188.2 ± 25.6) µgFe mL−1 for 97MNP is in accordance with the typical one for CA-coated MNP. The production line presented in this work combines high throughput with precise control of synthesis parameter enabling the production of MNP with high particle performance in hyperthermia and imaging.

Further automatization and optimization of the production system are envisioned by adding a continuous separation of MNP, e.g. by continuous centrifugation, crossflow filtration or field flow fractionation. In situ measurement techniques (magnetic sensors or diffraction techniques) can be used to track MNP properties during the synthesis and possibly develop prediction models for MNP properties.
