Management of Streptococcus pneumoniae mycotic aortic pseudoaneurysm
Authors: Mohit K. Manchella, Sophie X. Wang, Allen D. Hamdan, Lars Stangenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a rare case of a 59-year-old man who developed a progressively enlarging pseudoaneurysm at the level of the left renal artery owing to an infection with Streptococcus pneumoniae, clinically manifesting as worsening pain, new-onset chills, and febrile episodes. This report underscores the complexities associated with the management of mycotic pseudoaneurysm, highlighting the necessity of prompt diagnosis, thorough preoperative evaluation, and individualized surgical intervention to optimize patient outcomes in these challenging cases.

Case report

A 59-year-old man with inflammatory bowel disease presented with acute-on-chronic left lower quadrant abdominal pain after years of intermittent discomfort that recently worsened to 6 to 7 out of 10 severity, accompanied by chills, fever, and headache.

Intake laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (12,500/mm3), creatinine of 0.7 mg/dL, and a C-reactive protein of 59.0 mg/L. Workup initially focused on large vessel vasculitis vs infection. Blood cultures obtained two days after admission were negative. Positron emission tomography (PET) with CT 5 days after admission revealed FDG uptake along the medial proximal aorta with a maximum standardized uptake value of 13 (Supplementary Video 1). Infectious disease was consulted for suspected mycotic aneurysm and recommended serial imaging. After initiation of ceftriaxone and metronidazole 8 days after admission, the patient's symptoms and inflammatory markers improved.

However, interval CTA demonstrated pseudoaneurysm enlargement and persistent perivascular inflammation. The proposed intervention included open pseudoaneurysm repair with a rifampin-soaked Dacron graft and left renal artery bypass for revascularization.

The patient was positioned in right lateral decubitus. A left retroperitoneal incision was made along the tenth rib and extended medially to the lateral edge of the rectus muscle. The retroperitoneum was entered, the peritoneal contents, left kidney, and ureter were mobilized medially. The left diaphragmatic crus was incised to access the supraceliac aorta. The celiac axis and superior mesenteric artery (SMA) were controlled and a clamp site between them was selected.

Left renal bypass was initiated first to minimize renal ischemia. The mid left renal artery was circumferentially dissected, avoiding the pseudoaneurysm at the origin. Owing to dense paravisceral aortic inflammation, the left common iliac artery was identified as the inflow source to avoid a more hostile, infected field. After systemic anticoagulation, a 7-mm ringed polytetrafluoroethylene conduit was selected for left renal bypass as a readily available graft of appropriate caliber for renal revascularization during this complex reconstruction. The graft was anastomosed end-to-side to the left common iliac artery using 4-0 Prolene. Following back-bleeding and flushing, Doppler ultrasound examination confirmed excellent iliac flow. The distal anastomosis was then completed to the left renal artery with running 5-0 Prolene, leaving adequate graft slack to avoid tension. After flushing, Doppler ultrasound examination demonstrated strong distal renal artery signals.

A 14-mm rifampin-soaked Gelweave graft was then prepared. The aorta was clamped below the inferior mesenteric artery and at the intramesenteric level. Dense inflammation without abscess was present around the left renal artery origin. The inflamed left aortic wall segment, including the left renal origin, was excised while preserving the right renal artery and SMA (Fig 2). The proximal, beveled anastomosis at the right renal artery was performed using running 3-0 Prolene with hemostasis ensured via additional pledgeted repair. The proximal clamp was repositioned onto the graft to restore perfusion to the right renal artery and SMA. The distal anastomosis was then completed similarly, with appropriate flushing before unclamping. Postreconstruction Doppler ultrasound examination confirmed strong signals in the celiac axis, SMA, and renal arteries. Protamine was subsequently administered.

Hemostasis was confirmed and retractors were released, ensuring the left renal bypass graft lay without kinking. The abdominal wall was closed in two layers using running PDS sutures. Subcutaneous tissue and skin were closed with absorbable sutures and covered with Dermabond and Tegaderm.

The patient was extubated without complication and transferred to the surgical intensive care unit in stable condition with palpable pedal pulses bilaterally. Intraoperative aortic tissue cultures and pathology were negative, but universal PCR analysis, performed through ARUP Laboratories, identified Streptococcus pneumoniae. Antibiotics were adjusted in accordance with infectious disease recommendations.

At discharge, he was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, tolerating diet, ambulating independently, and had well-controlled pain. He was prescribed an empiric 6-week course of linezolid and ertapenem to provide broad coverage for presumed mycotic aneurysm in the setting of prior antibiotic exposure and culture-negative infection. Left renal bypass from left common iliac artery was patent on 4-month follow-up CTA (Supplementary Video 2). The patient has provided informed consent for the publication of this case report, including the use of its medical information and data.

Discussion

Infected pseudoaneurysms are rare, most commonly occurring in the femoral artery with an incidence of 0.03% to 1.00%.1 Although Salmonella and Staphylococcus aureus account for 75% to 85% of cases, S pneumoniae is a rare causative agent, accounting for only 10% of infections.2,3 The mechanism of infection cannot be established with certainty in our case. However, transient pneumococcal bacteremia with hematogenous seeding of a focally abnormal aortic segment near the left renal artery origin appears most plausible.4 The patient lacked conventional risk factors for S pneumoniae pseudoaneurysm formation, including immunosuppression or recent pneumococcal pneumonia. His only comorbidity was inflammatory bowel disease, in which pneumococcal gastrointestinal infection is atypical.

Infected pseudoaneurysms are diagnostically challenging owing to their insidious onset and nonspecific symptoms. Early signs such as fever, chills, and localized pain mimic other inflammatory or infectious conditions. Given the initial diagnostic uncertainty, we obtained PET/CT in accordance with American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guideline recommendations for investigation of aortic vasculitis.5 However, although it may serve as a useful baseline study, infection clearance is more practically assessed through serial clinical evaluation and trends in leukocyte count and C-reactive protein. Definitive identification of S pneumoniae was achieved through universal polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of intraoperative tissue, underscoring the value of advanced microbiological diagnostics in confirming the etiology of culture-negative infections, particularly when prior antibiotic administration may obscure blood culture sensitivity. This case is notable for the rare combination of paravisceral pneumococcal aortitis at the left renal artery origin and pathogen identification by universal PCR despite negative conventional cultures.

Despite modern surgery and antimicrobial therapy, infected pseudoaneurysms remain lethal, with mortality ranging from 12% to 90%.6 Reoperation is frequent, with freedom from reintervention of only 50% at 1 year and 40% at 3 years.7 Open repair was favored because the patient was young and physiologically robust, the pseudoaneurysm enlarged despite antibiotics, and surgery permitted excision of the inflamed aortic segment, definitive reconstruction, and renal revascularization, whereas endovascular repair would have provided less direct source control. Nevertheless, endovascular aneurysm repair remains a viable option in patients who are poor open surgery candidates for mycotic aortitis, although the risk of recurrent infection may be higher.8

Reconstruction strategies include in situ prosthetic grafts, homografts, or extra-anatomical bypass. Prosthetic and homograft approaches offer favorable long-term patency and lower reinfection rates.6 Although autogenous vein conduits may offer greater resistance to reinfection, their use must be weighed against the added operative time and complexity of harvest. Rifampin-soaked prosthetic grafts are more readily available, can be implanted more expeditiously, and remain an accepted option when combined with appropriate antibiotic therapy.5 In our case, the infected process appeared to be localized without purulence, permitting a targeted repair with preservation of a substantial portion of the native paravisceral aorta. Resection margins were determined intraoperatively by the gross extent of visible aortic inflammation. The diseased segment was excised and reconstruction was performed to adjacent aorta that appeared to be uninvolved, although no definitive intraoperative standard exists to confirm complete clearance beyond grossly abnormal tissue.

Conclusions

Pneumococcal pseudoaneurysms pose distinct diagnostic and therapeutic complexities. Management requires multidisciplinary coordination. PET/CT and PCR may aid in diagnosis. Future research should refine diagnostic biomarkers, graft selection, and antibiotic duration to improve outcomes in these rare, life-threatening infections.
