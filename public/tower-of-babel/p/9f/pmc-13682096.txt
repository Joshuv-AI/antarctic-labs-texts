Antihypertensive treatment non-adherence and spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage: a prospective observational study from Northern India
Authors: Aizul Khursheed, Kashif Qureshi, Farhan Parvaiz, Mohsin Fayaz, Burhaan ul Khursheed, Sarbjit Singh Chhiber, Nayil K Malik, Abrar A Wani, Sajad H Arif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIntracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a leading cause of stroke-related mortality and long-term disability, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries. Hypertension, and particularly non-adherence to antihypertensive therapy, represents the most important modifiable risk factor. This study aimed to evaluate the epidemiological, clinical, and radiological characteristics of hypertensive ICH and to identify preventable risk factors with public health relevance.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary-care referral center in Northern India between March 2023 and February 2024. Adult patients with non-traumatic spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage were enrolled. Data on demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, antihypertensive treatment adherence, clinical severity, and radiological characteristics were collected and analyzed descriptively.ResultsOne hundred patients were included (mean age 61.2 ± 12.4 years; 63% male). Hypertension was present in 78% of patients; among these, 70.5% (95% CI 59.6 to 79.5) reported non-adherence to antihypertensive therapy. Active smoking was observed in 62%. Deep hemorrhages involving the basal ganglia (46%) and thalamus (26%) accounted for 72% of cases. Hydrocephalus occurred in 28%. Higher admission blood pressure was observed in patients with larger hematoma volumes.ConclusionsHypertensive ICH in this Northern Indian cohort disproportionately affects individuals in the sixth decade of life and is strongly associated with uncontrolled blood pressure, antihypertensive treatment non-adherence, and tobacco use. Targeted primary prevention through strengthened antihypertensive care and behavioral risk factor programs is essential to reducing the regional burden of this largely preventable condition.Keywords: Intracerebral hemorrhage, Hypertension, Treatment non-adherence, Smoking, Prospective study, India

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) accounts for 10–15% of all stroke cases worldwide but contributes disproportionately to stroke-related mortality and long-term disability [1, 2]. The 30-day case fatality rate exceeds 40%, and fewer than one-third of survivors achieve long-term functional independence [3, 4]. This burden falls predominantly on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where hemorrhagic stroke contributes substantially to years of life lost and disability-adjusted life years, with incidence in Asian populations nearly double that in other ethnic groups [4–7].

Hypertension is the most consistently identified and potentially preventable risk factor for spontaneous ICH, implicated in up to 80% of cases in high-risk populations [8–10]. Chronic blood pressure elevation promotes hypertensive arteriopathy of small penetrating cerebral vessels, predisposing them to lipohyalinosis, microaneurysm formation, and rupture [4, 11]. In low-income settings, poor hypertension detection, inadequate treatment, and low antihypertensive adherence collectively undermine effective blood pressure control [12, 13]. Adherence follows a dose-response relationship with stroke risk; partial non-adherence meaningfully increases cerebrovascular event rates [14]. Treatment discontinuation is particularly hazardous: a 2025 nested case-control study demonstrated that patients who discontinued antihypertensive therapy for four or more months carried a 3.8-fold higher risk of ICH compared to adherent patients [15].

India bears a disproportionate and growing burden of hemorrhagic cerebrovascular disease [12, 16, 17]. ICH occurs at a younger age in India than in high-income countries, reflecting earlier and more prolonged exposure to uncontrolled vascular risk factors [18–20]. A 2025 global burden of disease analysis confirmed that ICH incidence and mortality among younger adults remain concentrated in low-sociodemographic-index regions [21]. Tobacco use and physical inactivity co-occur with hypertension at high rates, amplifying individual risk [8, 22]. Limited primary care access, poor health literacy, socioeconomic constraints, and fragmented follow-up compound the challenge of sustained blood pressure control [12, 13, 23].

This study was conducted to characterize the demographic, clinical, and radiological profile of patients with hypertensive ICH at a tertiary referral center in Northern India, with particular focus on antihypertensive treatment adherence, modifiable risk factors, and public health implications.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational study was conducted at Sher-i-Kashmir Institute of Medical Sciences (SKIMS), a tertiary-care referral hospital serving as the primary neurological referral center for the Kashmir Valley region in Northern India, between March 2023 and February 2024. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

Adult patients aged 18 years or older admitted with confirmed non-traumatic spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage were eligible. Patients were required to have CT-confirmed evidence of intracerebral hemorrhage with no antecedent head trauma. All hemorrhages were spontaneous (non-traumatic); patients are described as having hypertensive ICH where hypertension was the presumed underlying etiology. Exclusion criteria were traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic stroke, intracranial tumors or vascular malformations, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, and documented anticoagulant or thrombolytic therapy use.

Data collection

After obtaining written informed consent, trained study personnel recorded demographic information, vascular risk factors (hypertension, tobacco use, diabetes mellitus), and antihypertensive medication adherence. Non-adherence was defined as irregular use or complete discontinuation of prescribed antihypertensive therapy, assessed by structured patient self-report corroborated by caregiver interview where available [24, 25]. A formal validated adherence instrument (for example, the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale) was not administered, as such instruments are proprietary, require licensing, and lack locally validated translations for this population; adherence was therefore captured pragmatically through structured self-report with caregiver corroboration. Blood pressure was recorded at the time of presentation, prior to initiation of antihypertensive therapy; Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores were recorded by the admitting physician. Hematoma volume was estimated using the ABC/2 method on non-contrast CT [26]. Radiological data included hemorrhage location, hematoma volume, intraventricular extension (IVE), and hydrocephalus. Prognostic severity was quantified using the ICH score [10].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed descriptively. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD); categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). 95% confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using the Wilson score method. Because this was a descriptive, single-cohort study without a comparison group, analysis was limited to descriptive statistics and interval estimation; formal hypothesis testing and multivariable regression were not appropriate to the design and are identified as priorities for future controlled studies.

Demographic characteristics

The mean age was 61.2 ± 12.4 years (range: 35–90 years), with 78% of patients aged 50 years or older and the highest concentrations in the 50–59 year (32%) and 60–69 year (29%) groups; only 16% were younger than 40 years (Table 1). This profile is consistent with the younger onset of hypertensive cerebrovascular disease in LMICs [18–21]. A male predominance was observed (63% vs. 37%; male-to-female ratio 1.7:1), consistent with global epidemiological data [4]. Higher admissions were noted during periods of extreme heat and cold.

Cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors

Hypertension and treatment adherence

Hypertension was present in 78% (n = 78) of patients, consistent with its established role as a leading risk factor for primary ICH [8–10] (Table 1; Fig. 2). Among these, 70.5% (n = 55; 95% CI 59.6 to 79.5) reported non-adherence to antihypertensive therapy, and 29.5% (n = 23) reported regular medication use. A further 22% (n = 22; 95% CI 15.0 to 31.1) had no prior hypertension diagnosis despite elevated blood pressure at admission, reflecting the burden of undetected disease [12, 13]. Mean SBP was 183 ± 28 mmHg and mean DBP 106 ± 18 mmHg; 46% presented with SBP ≥ 180 mmHg and 74% with DBP ≥ 90 mmHg (Table 2).

Antihypertensive treatment adherence status. Among the 78 patients with hypertension, 55 (70.5%; 95% CI 59.6 to 79.5) reported non-adherence to prescribed therapy. Separately, 22 of the full cohort of 100 patients (22%) had no prior hypertension diagnosis despite elevated admission blood pressure, reflecting undetected disease [12, 13, 15]

Smoking status

Active smoking was reported by 62% (n = 62; 95% CI 52.2 to 70.9) of patients, the second most prevalent modifiable risk factor, with higher prevalence among males and patients younger than 65 years (Table 1) [4, 8, 22].

Diabetes mellitus and body mass index

Type 2 diabetes mellitus was present in 12% of patients and showed no apparent clustering across hematoma size or hemorrhage location categories [4]. Mean BMI was 24.5 ± 4.2 kg/m², with 85% of patients within the normal to overweight range; no clear association between BMI and hemorrhage severity was identified (Table 1) [4].

Clinical presentation and severity

Mean GCS at admission was 11.5 ± 3.6. Severe impairment (GCS ≤ 8) was present in 20%, moderate (GCS 9–12) in 48%, and mild (GCS ≥ 13) in 32% of patients (Table 2; Fig. 3) [10, 26]. The most frequent presenting symptoms were unilateral motor weakness, speech disturbances, and altered sensorium. Patients with infratentorial hemorrhages more commonly presented with ataxia and gait disturbance.

Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) severity distribution at admission (n = 100). One-fifth of patients presented with severe neurological impairment (GCS ≤ 8), a well-recognized predictor of poor functional outcome and in-hospital mortality in ICH [10, 26]

Radiological characteristics

Hematoma volume and blood pressure correlation

Mean hematoma volume was 28.7 ± 19.6 mL (range: 3–110 mL); 31% had volumes > 30 mL (Table 2). Patients with volumes > 30 mL demonstrated higher mean SBP (191 vs. 179 mmHg) and DBP (112 vs. 103 mmHg) at admission, consistent with an association between higher admission blood pressure and larger hematoma size [11, 26] (Fig. 4).

Hemorrhage location

Basal ganglia (46%) and thalamic (26%) hemorrhages accounted for 72% of cases, consistent with the anatomical predilection of hypertensive small-vessel disease [4] (Table 2; Fig. 5). Lobar hemorrhages comprised 13%; cerebellar 12%; brainstem 3%.

Anatomical distribution of hemorrhage locations (n = 100). Basal ganglia and thalamic hemorrhages combined accounted for 72% of cases, consistent with the anatomical predilection of hypertensive small-vessel disease [4]

Intraventricular extension and hydrocephalus

IVE was observed in 54% of patients (95% CI 44.3 to 63.4). Hydrocephalus developed in 28% (95% CI 20.1 to 37.5) and was more frequently associated with thalamic hemorrhage, large hematoma volumes, and intraventricular blood [27] (Table 2). Both are recognized markers of poor prognosis and increased mortality in ICH [10, 27].

Prognostic scoring

The ICH score ranged from 0 to 4 in this cohort (maximum possible score per scoring system: 6; mean: 1.8 ± 1.1). Lower scores clustered among patients with lobar hemorrhage and preserved consciousness; higher scores were associated with large hematomas, IVE, and depressed consciousness [10, 26] (Table 2). Although in-hospital mortality and functional outcomes were not recorded in this cohort, the ICH score is a validated predictor of 30-day mortality; in the original derivation cohort, scores of 1 and 2 corresponded to approximately 13% and 26% 30-day mortality, respectively [28]. The observed score distribution, combined with the high frequency of deep hemorrhage, intraventricular extension, and hydrocephalus, indicates a cohort at substantial risk of poor outcome.

Discussion

This prospective study characterizes the epidemiological, clinical, and radiological profile of hypertensive ICH at a tertiary referral center in Northern India. Preventable determinants, principally uncontrolled hypertension, antihypertensive treatment non-adherence, and tobacco use, emerge as the dominant drivers of disease burden, with direct implications for primary care reform and public health policy [16, 23, 29].

Age and demographic patterns

The mean age of 61 years is lower than in high-income countries, where ICH predominantly affects individuals older than 70 years [3, 4]. This earlier onset reflects prolonged exposure to modifiable vascular risk factors from middle age [19–21]. A 2025 global burden of disease analysis confirmed that ICH incidence and mortality among younger adults remain disproportionately concentrated in low-sociodemographic-index regions, with South Asia bearing substantial burden [21]. Premature cerebrovascular disease carries considerable societal costs, including lost productivity and increased dependency [5–7, 30]. The male predominance (63%) likely reflects higher rates of tobacco use, lower engagement with preventive care, and weaker antihypertensive adherence among men [4, 22]; gender-sensitive prevention strategies remain an underutilized public health lever [16, 23].

Hypertension and treatment non-adherence

Hypertension was present in nearly four-fifths of this cohort, consistent with established evidence that chronic blood pressure elevation underlies primary ICH through hypertensive arteriopathy [4, 8–10]. The non-adherence rate of 70.5% among known hypertensive patients exceeds rates documented in comparable Indian tertiary studies (36–40%) [18] and surpasses rates in high-income country cohorts. The 22% of patients with undiagnosed hypertension signals a persistent population-level detection gap [12, 13].

The mechanistic link between non-adherence and ICH risk is well-established. A 2025 nested case-control study demonstrated that antihypertensive discontinuation for four or more months was independently associated with a 3.8-fold higher ICH risk, with younger age and male sex as significant predictors of discontinuation [15]. A complementary dose-response meta-analysis confirmed that even partial non-adherence meaningfully increases cerebrovascular event rates [14]. The markedly elevated admission blood pressures in this cohort, with a mean SBP of 183 mmHg and 46% presenting with SBP ≥ 180 mmHg, indicate prolonged, untreated hypertension preceding the hemorrhagic event [11]. Simplified drug regimens, subsidized medication access, and structured adherence support are central to closing this gap [12, 13, 23, 25].

Smoking and behavioral risk factors

Active smoking was present in 62% of patients. Tobacco use independently promotes endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and hypertensive vasculopathy [4, 8, 22], and its co-occurrence with uncontrolled hypertension represents a synergistic risk profile [22]. Integrating cessation programs within hypertension clinics and applying population-level tobacco control measures are cost-effective strategies likely to yield meaningful downstream reductions in hemorrhagic stroke burden [16, 23, 29].

Body mass index and metabolic factors

Most patients fell within the normal to overweight BMI range and obesity was uncommon, consistent with evidence that hypertensive small-vessel injury rather than adiposity is the dominant pathogenic mechanism in primary ICH [4]. Diabetes was present in 12% of patients with no apparent association with hemorrhage severity, aligning with the established pattern of weak or conflicting associations between type 2 diabetes and primary ICH compared to ischemic stroke [4].

Clinical severity and neurological presentation

One-fifth of patients presented with severe neurological impairment (GCS ≤ 8), a well-recognized predictor of poor functional outcome and mortality [10, 26]. The severity distribution suggests many patients arrive at tertiary care in an advanced disease state, compounded by pre-hospital delays and limited intermediate care capacity. Improved public recognition of ICH warning signs and strengthened emergency referral pathways could reduce pre-hospital deterioration [16, 29].

Radiological Severity and Blood Pressure Correlation.

The predominance of deep basal ganglia and thalamic hemorrhages (72%) is characteristic of hypertensive small-vessel disease and mirrors findings from comparable Indian cohorts [4, 18]. The positive association between admission blood pressure and hematoma volume is consistent with a contributory role of hypertension in hemorrhage severity [11, 26]. IVE (54%) and hydrocephalus (28%) are associated with raised intracranial pressure, need for surgical intervention, and substantially worse outcomes [27], underscoring why primary prevention offers greater potential benefit than resource-intensive acute neurosurgical intervention in this setting.

Seasonal variation

Higher admissions during extreme climatic periods suggest that temperature-related blood pressure fluctuations, medication access disruption, and dehydration-mediated hemodynamic changes may modulate ICH risk, consistent with seasonal patterns reported in other high-burden Asian populations [31].

Public health implications

Heavy reliance on a single tertiary center for managing largely preventable events reflects missed opportunities at the primary and secondary prevention levels. Community-based hypertension screening, task-shifting of antihypertensive management to trained community health workers, mobile health adherence monitoring, and sustained patient education are evidence-based and scalable strategies [16, 23]. Integration of stroke prevention into national non-communicable disease programs such as the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke, with prioritization of uninterrupted antihypertensive supply, could yield substantial reductions in hemorrhagic stroke incidence, healthcare costs, and DALYs lost [16, 17].

Strengths and limitations

The prospective design, consecutive patient enrollment, and comprehensive assessment of behavioral, clinical, and radiological variables across a population with limited existing data are key strengths. The study also has important limitations. The single-center, tertiary-referral design may limit generalizability to community-level populations and may over-represent more severe cases. Adherence was assessed by structured self-report and caregiver corroboration rather than objective measures such as pharmacy-refill records, pill counts, or electronic monitoring. Self-report is subject to recall and social-desirability bias, and even validated instruments show limited sensitivity against objective monitoring; the reported rate may therefore underestimate true non-adherence [14, 15]. In-hospital and long-term outcome data, including mortality, length of stay, surgical intervention rates, and modified Rankin Scale scores, were not collected; the study therefore characterizes the presenting profile of hypertensive ICH rather than its outcomes. The blood-pressure values for the ≤ 30 mL group in Fig. 4 are derived arithmetically from the overall cohort mean and the > 30 mL group mean and are presented as descriptive group means rather than the product of a stratified statistical comparison. As an observational, single-cohort study without a control group, this analysis cannot establish causation or estimate adjusted risk. Future controlled studies incorporating multivariable analysis that accounts for medication class, hypertension duration, and socioeconomic factors are needed to quantify the independent contribution of non-adherence.

Conclusions

Hypertensive ICH in Northern India is strongly associated with uncontrolled blood pressure, antihypertensive treatment non-adherence, and tobacco use. The profile observed here, younger age of onset, severe hypertension at presentation, and predominantly deep hemorrhages, is consistent with a population carrying years of inadequately treated hypertension [8, 9, 14, 15]. Non-adherence to antihypertensive therapy, present in more than two-thirds of hypertensive patients, is the most clinically actionable risk factor identified and the most tractable intervention target [14, 15]. Health system strengthening, community outreach, and integrated antihypertensive adherence programs represent the most promising strategies for reducing the preventable burden of ICH in this region [16, 23, 29].
