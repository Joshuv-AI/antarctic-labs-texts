Perovskite engineering for efficient oxygen evolution reaction through iron and silver doping
Authors: Elham Mahmoudi, Elham Fattahi, Dina Bakranova, Nagihan Delibas, Elnaz Asghari, Ali Coruh, Aligholi Niaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effects of Fe and Ag doping on the structure, surface properties, and electrochemical performance of a Ba-containing perovskite oxide for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Three catalysts, LBC, Fe-doped LBC (LBCF), and Ag-impregnated LBCF (LBCF-A), were synthesized using sol–gel and wet impregnation methods. Their physicochemical properties were characterized using XRD, FESEM, HRTEM, ICP, FTIR, and XPS. Electrochemical evaluations, including linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), Tafel analysis, and turnover frequency (TOF), were conducted to assess OER performance. Among these, LBCF-A exhibited the best OER activity, with an overpotential of 317 mV at 10 mA.cm−2, a Tafel slope of 101 mV.dec−1, and a significantly higher TOF. The enhanced performance is attributed to Fe-induced modulation of Co oxidation states, formation of oxygen vacancies, and Ag-mediated surface modifications that enriched surface hydroxyls (OH-), O2, and H2O. These synergistic effects improved conductivity and charge transfer, making LBCF-A a promising candidate for efficient and cost-effective OER catalysis.Keywords: Oxygen Evolution Reaction, Perovskite oxides, Ag doping, Overpotential, Lattice oxygenSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Materials science

Introduction

Developing efficient and cost-effective electrocatalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is crucial for advancing electrochemical energy conversion technologies1,2, including water splitting1 and metal- air batterie3. Noble metal oxides such as IrO2 and RuO2 exhibit superior OER activity, but their scarcity and high-cost limit large-scale application3. Therefore, researchers have focused on transition metal-based alternatives4, perovskites5, nitrides, phosphides, and sulfides6. Transition metal-based materials like Fe2O3/Fe3O47, NiO, CuO8, Co3O49, and CoFe2O410 have demonstrated promising OER activity and stability. Among these, spinel Co3O4, with its Co(II)/Co(III) redox couple, is particularly attractive for alkaline OER, exhibiting notable performance11. However, its poor electrical conductivity hinders charge transfer, posing a challenge for practical applications10.

Perovskite oxides (ABO3)13 have emerged as a promising class of materials due to their tunable electronic structure and flexibility in A- and B-site substitution12–15, which can optimize catalytic properties. In particular, cobalt-based perovskites demonstrate significant potential for OER applications, with doping strategies further improving their performance16–19. For instance, incorporating Fe at the B-site enhances charge transfer20, stabilizes the perovskite structure, and optimizes the binding energy of reaction intermediates, leading to enhanced OER activity21. Additionally, A-site modifications, such as alkaline earth metal substitution, have induced oxygen vacancy formation, facilitating improved reaction kinetics22–24.

Studies indicate that the poor electrical conductivity and suboptimal surface characteristics of perovskite oxides significantly limit their OER performance22,25–27. To overcome this limitations, Fe and Ag have been explored as effective dopants due to their high intrinsic conductivity and ability to facilitate a direct four-electron transfer pathway. Fe doping, in particular, enhancees OER activity by improving charge transport and optimizing the binding energy of reaction intermediates, thereby increasing catalytic efficiency28,29. Incorporating Fe into the B-site of perovskite oxides shifts the Co oxidation equilibrium towards higher Co3+ content. It promotes electron delocalization, facilitates oxygen vacancy formation, and strengthens the adsorption of OER intermediates (OH*, O*, and OOH*)30.Although Ag is less commonly used than noble metals such as Pd or Pt, recent studies suggest that Ag incorporation into transition metal oxides can significantly enhance electrocatalytic activity by increasing oxygen vacancies density and improving charge transport31,32. Moreover, Ag promotes the formation of highly active oxy(hydroxide) species during OER, which are critical for stable and efficient water oxidation33. In addition, Ag surface impregnation modulates the surface electronic structure through interactions with Co and Fe, as evidenced by XPS binding energy shifts. This modification enriches active oxygen-containing species (OH-, O2, H2O), improves charge transfer, and lowers the energy barrier for OER19,30,34. Given Ag’s low loading in electrocatalysts and its strong interaction with the perovskite lattice, potential environmental and health concerns are mitigated, making it a viable and cost-effective strategy for boosting OER performance35–39.

This study investigates the electrochemical performance and stability of Fe- and Ag-doped La0.8Ba0.2CoO3 (LBC) perovskites, namely LBC, LBCF, and LBCF-A, to identify potential enhancements in OER activity. A key focus of this work is to examine how the decreasing Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio, increasing Co2+/Co3+ ratio, and the presence of metallic Ag (Ag⁰) modify OER activity. A sol–gel method was employed to fabricate Ag/Fe-doped porous LBC perovskites with abundant oxygen vacancies to enhance catalytic efficiency. All perovskite structures were calcined under identical conditions and characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD), high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and inductively coupled plasma (ICP). Electrochemical evaluations conducted under optimized conditions based on previous study40, revealed that LBCF-A exhibited superior OER performance compared to LBC and LBCF. This improvement is attributed to its higher conductivity and optimized lattice oxygen content, which collectively enhance catalytic activity.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. These included La(NO3)3·6H2O (99.9%, Merck), Ba(NO3)3·3H2O (98.5%, Merck), Co(NO3)2·3H2O (98.5%, Merck), Fe(NO3)2·4H2O (98%, Merck), Ag(NO3)2·2H2O (98%, Merck), Glycine (99.9%, Samchun Pure Chemical), Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) (99.9%, Samchun Pure Chemical Company), Activated Carbon (Merck), N-Methyl Pyrrolidone (NMP) (98%, Tetiran), Potassium hydroxide (KOH) (99%, Merck), and deionized water (DIW).

Catalyst synthesis

The LBC perovskite catalyst was synthesized using a glycine-assisted combustion sol–gel method as described in our previous work40,41. Stoichiometric amounts of metal nitrates were dissolved in deionized water, followed by the addition of Glycine at a 1:1 molar ratio relative to the metal nitrates. The solution was heated to 85 °C under stirring until gel formation occurred, after which self-ignition at approximately 250 °C produced a brown powder. This powder was then calcined at 650 °C for 6 h.

Fe-doped LBC (La0.8Ba0.2Co0.7Fe0.3O3, LBCF) was prepared by partially substituting Co with Fe using the same procedure and conditions. The Fe doping level of 0.3 mol fraction at the B-site was chosen based on literature and preliminary tests optimizing OER activity and stability. Moderate Fe substitution (30%) in Co-based perovskites enhances OER activity by increasing Co3+ active sites and improving Co–O covalency, whereas excessive Fe can reduce conductivity and destabilize the lattice30.

Ag impregnation on LBCF (La0.8Ba0.2Co0.7Fe0.3O3-Ag0.1, LBCF-A) was synthesized via a wet impregnation method adapted from42. Ag was introduced at a surface loading of 0.1 mol fraction to enhance electronic interactions at the catalyst–electrolyte interface without blocking active sites. Small amounts of surface-deposited noble metals such as Ag have been shown to improve oxygenated intermediate adsorption/desorption and surface hydroxylation, thereby enhancing OER kinetics. Higher Ag loadings typically cause agglomeration and reduce catalytic performance43. Briefly, Ag(NO3)2·2H2O was dissolved in deionized water, and the calcined LBCF powder was added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h, followed by evaporation at 65 °C, drying at 80 °C for 12 h, and final calcination at 650 °C for 5 h in air.

Bulk and surface structural characterization

The structural and morphological properties of the synthesized catalysts were analyzed using various techniques. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using a PANalytical X’pert Pro diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in the 2θ range of 20°–80° to determine the phase composition of the as-prepared catalysts. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was conducted on a Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer to identify functional groups present in the samples. Microstructural analysis was carried out using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a TESCAN instrument, while high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) measurements were performed on a JEOL JEM-2100 instrument to provide insights into the nanoscale morphology. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed using a Specs FlexPS system equipped with a Phoibos 150 2D CCD detector and an Al Kα source to examine surface elemental composition and oxidation states. Additionally, elemental composition was assessed using inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis, with ICP measurements conducted on a 7800 ICP-MS.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrocatalytic performance of the synthesized catalysts was evaluated through OER using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in a three-electrode electrochemical setup. The system comprised a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) as the working electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl) electrode as the reference. All electrochemical measurements were conducted using a PGSTAT30 potentiostat–galvanostat.

The ink consisted of as-prepared perovskites and carbon powders in a 2.81 mass ratio (ink concentration:11.5 mg.cc-1), with PVDF as a binder (0.6 mg). The powders were physically mixed, ground, and ultrasonically dispersed in NMP for 2 h. A 3 μL aliquot of the catalyst ink was drop-cast onto the GCE surface (0.0314 cm2), followed by air drying at room temperature for 24 h. Electrochemical measerments were conducted in a 0.6 M KOH aqueous solution.

OER polarization curves were recorded within a potential range of -0.5 to 1.8 V at a scan rate of 5 mV.s⁻1, with all measurements repeated to ensure data reproducibility. Before recording LSV, the electrocatalysts were conditioned by cycling between 1.0 V and 1.5 V (vs. RHE) for 20 cycles at a scan rate of 50 mV.s-1. The double-layer capacitances (Cdl) was determined via cyclic voltammetry (CV) in the potential range of 0.9 to 1.2 V (vs. RHE), a non-Faradaic current region. CV scans were conducted at varying scan rates of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV.s⁻1. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was calculated as Cdl/Cs, where Cs represents the specific capacitance of a perfectly smooth planar surface in the same electrolyte (0.04 mF.cm-2)41, and the values were normalized to the loaded active material on the electrode.

The turnover frequency (TOF) was determined using , where j representing the current density at a given overpotential, S being the geometric electrode area, n is the number of active sites, and F is the Faraday constant44. The mass activity was calculated by dividing the obtained current density (mA.cm-2) at a maximum overpotential of 412 mV by the catalyst loading (g.cm-2). The Tafel slope was derived by transforming the polarization curves using the Tafel equation, η = a + blogj, where η is the overpotential, a is the intercept, and b is the Tafel slope45. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to analyze the catalysts’ charge transfer resistance. Measurements were performed at a polarization voltage corresponding to a current density of 10 mA.cm-2, with an AC signal amplitude of 10 mV across frequencies ranging from 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz at an overpotential of 412 mV.

Results and discussion

Structural analysis of synthesized perovskites

The XRD pattern of LBCF-A is presented in Fig. 1. In contrast, the patterns for LBC and LBCF, along with a magnified view of the sharp peaks, are presented in Fig. S1. Comparison with the reference patterns for LaFeO3 and LaCoO3 confirms that the synthesized perovskites exhibit a composite structure of these phases. The substitution of Ba2+ (1.35 Å) for La3+ (1.06 Å) results in a shift of the central diffraction peaks toward lower 2θ angles, indicating lattice expansion. This observation is consistent with JCPDS #01–084-0848 (LaCoO3) and previous reports46. Further peak shifts40 upon Fe substitution confirm Fe3+ incorporation into the lattice, as Fe3+ has a larger ionic radius than Co3+ (Fig. S1(b)). Minor diffraction peaks corresponding to BaCO3 (JCPDS #71–2394) and BaCoO3 (JCPDS #70–0363) are observed in all samples, likely due to incomplete reactions during synthesis47.

X-ray diffraction patterns for LBCF-A prepared by sol–gel method and standard card.

Following Ag impregnation, metallic Ag0 reflections at 2θ values of 38.1°, 44.3°, and 64.4° confirm Ag deposition on the perovskite surface rather than its incorporation into the lattice, as expected. Since XRD primarily detects long-range structural features, Ag+ substitution cannot be directly confirmed, therefore XPS was employed to investigate Ag’s oxidation state and it’s interaction with the perovskite framework. The presence of metallic Ag0 suggests surface deposition without significantly alteration of the perovskite lattice48,49.

HR-TEM was used to examine the structural characteristics of the optimized LBCF-A sample in greater detail. As shown in Fig. 2(a), HR-TEM reveals distinct crystal planes corresponding to (140) and (202), consistent with the rhombohedral structure of the material. The measured d-spacing values are slightly larger than those of standard LaCoO3, which can be attributed to lattice expansion caused by the incorporating of larger Ba and Fe ions. This finding is in agreement with the XRD results, confirming structural modifications due to elemental substitution.

(a) HR-TEM image, and (b) SAED pattern of LBCF-A.

The SAED pattern, shown in Fig. 2(b), confirms the sample’s polycrystalline nature50. Together, the HR-TEM and SAED analyses confirms the successful formation of the LBCF-A rhombohedral perovskite phase with a well-defined crystalline lattice.

The elemental composition of the synthesized perovskites was determined using ICP spectroscopy. The experimentally measured values were compared with the theoretical stoichiometry, as summarized in Table. S1. The results indicate that the La, Ba, Co, and Fe contents in both samples are close to the expected values, with only minor deviations. These slight variations may be due to partial substitution or redistribution of elements during synthesis. Nevertheless, the overall elemental composition remains consistent with the intended stoichiometry, confirming the successful synthesis of the perovskite structures.

The surface morphology of the calcined perovskite powders was examined using SEM, as shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. S1. The SEM images reveal irregularly shaped particles composed of aggregated nanoparticles of varying sizes, forming a porous network. At higher magnification, the particles display hemisphere-like nanoparticles ranging from 76 to 150 nm, along with agglomerated clusters containing finer particles. The surfaces appear smooth and spongy, with pores distributed throughout the microstructure. Upon partial Fe substitution and subsequent Ag impregnation, the perovskite surface morphology evolves into a more organized porous structure with a slightly reduced average pore size. Such structural refinement refinement is expected to facilitate electron transport, enhance conductivity, and lower charge-transfer resistance, thereby potentially improving OER performance51.

SEM images of representative LBCF (a-1), (a-2), and (a-3), and LBCF-A (b-1), (b-2), and (b-3) perovskite electrocatalysts.

FTIR spectra of the LBC, LBCF, and LBCF-A catalysts (Fig. S1) were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm-1 to analyze their structural characteristics after calcination. Broad absorption bands near 2830 cm-1 and 3680 cm-1 correspond to the stretching and bending vibrations of water molecules (c region)52. A distinct absorption band around 1430 cm-1 is attributed to carbonate ion deformation, indicating possible surface interaction with atmospheric CO2. In the lower frequency range, a band at approximately 1074 cm-1 corresponds to Co–OH bending vibrations, consistent with literature reports where M–OH modes typically appear below 1200 cm⁻1 (b region)53. A strong band near 572 cm-1 in all samples is assigned to the stretching vibration of the transition metal–oxygen (TM–O) bond, a fundamental feature of the perovskite lattice. Notably, Fe substitution in the LBC lattice intensifies all FTIR peaks, suggesting stronger bonding and enhanced interactions due to changes in the oxidation states of Co and Fe at the B-site (a region)54. Aafter Ag impregnation, peak intensities decrease, likely due to surface modification or reduced interaction strength within the lattice. The TM–O bond position remains unchanged, suggesting that Fe and Ag influence bond strength and the electronic environment without significantly altering bond length or type.

XPS analysis was performed to examine the surface composition and oxidation states of Co, Fe, and O in LBCF and Ag-modified LBCF (LBCF-A). The C 1s peak at 284.8 eV was used for charge correction. XPS results for LBC were reported in our previous work40. The survey spectra and Ag peaks are shown in Fig. S1, with Ag exhibiting two peaks assigned to metallic Ag0, consistent with the XRD findings. Figure 4 shows high-resolution XPS spectra of Co 2p, Fe 2p, and O 1s for the LBCF catalyst (panel a) and the LBCF-A catalyst (panel b), highlighting the differences in electronic states and surface oxygen species due to Ag impregnation.

High-resolution XPS spectra of Co 2p, Fe 2p, and O 1 s, for LBCF (Panel a), and LBCF-A (Panel b).

The Co 2p spectra reveal characteristic spin–orbit doublets corresponding to Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2, with associated satellite peaks, in agreement with reported cobalt-based perovskites55. Peak fitting was performed by constraining the expected 2:1 intensity ratio dictated by spin–orbit coupling rules. Upon Ag incorporation, the Co 2p peaks shift slightly toward higher binding energy, accompanied by a reduced Co2+/Co3+ ratio, indicating an increased Co3+ fraction. Such a positive binding energy shift reflects a reduction in electron density at Co sites, attributed to electronic interaction between Ag and the LBCF lattice. Given Ag’s higher electronegativity, it withdraws electron density from adjacent B-site cations, stabilizing Co3+ and modifying the local electronic structure. This effect likely promotes oxygen vacancy formation and alters metal–oxygen bond covalency within the perovskite lattice56,57. The increased Co3+ fraction is pivotal for the OER, as Co3+ species are widely associated with improved catalytic activity via the lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM), where lattice oxygen directly participates in oxygen evolution through reversible oxygen vacancy formation56. The enhanced electron delocalization and vacancy formation promoted by Ag doping improve the adsorption and activation of OER intermediates (OH*, O*, OOH*), thereby lowering the energy barrier for the reaction57. Furthermore, the redox flexibility imparted by the increased Co3+ content accelerates charge transfer processes, which is critical for sustaining high OER kinetics58,59.

The Fe 2p spectra were deconvoluted into Fe3+, Fe2+, and satellite peaks60. In the pristine LBCF, Fe is predominantly Fe3+ with a minor Fe2+ fraction. After Ag incorporation, the Fe 2p peaks shift toward lower binding energy, and the Fe2+/ Fe3+ ratio increases, indicating higher electron density at Fe sites. This opposite shift compared to Co suggests internal charge redistribution between B-site cations due to Ag-lattice electronic interactions. The presence of more Fe2+ in LBCF-A is expected to improve electron availability and intermediate adsorption, thereby enhancing OER activity61–63.

Ag incorporation on the LBCF surface leads to a redistribution of oxygen species, decreasing lattice oxygen while increasing hydroxyl ions (OH⁻), molecular oxygen (O2), and adsorbed water (H2O). Although Ag primarily resides on the surface rather than substituting into the lattice, it can induce near-surface oxygen vacancies via electronic interactions with B-site cations43. These vacancies weaken local lattice oxygen bonds, facilitating oxygen mobility and creating additional adsorption sites for hydroxyl groups and water molecules64. Moreover, Ag increases metal–oxygen bond covalency and strengthens the interaction with oxygenated intermediates. Consequently, even surface-deposited Ag can indirectly modify lattice oxygen behavior and surface chemistry to enhance catalytic performance51,65.

Electrocatalytic performance

The electrochemical OER activity of LBC, LBCF, and LBCF-A was evaluated using a three-electrode setup in a 0.6 M KOH electrolyte. Figure 5(a) compares the LSV curves of the three catalysts (LBC, LBCF, and LBCF-A) and the bare GC electrode. The bare GC electrode exhibited poor OER activity, with a high overpotential at 10 mA.cm−2 (η10). In contrast, coating the GC electrode with perovskite oxides significantly reduced the overpotential. Among the tested materials, LBCF-A demonstrated the best performance, achieving an overpotential of 317 mV at a 10 mA.cm−2, compared to 364 mV for LBCF, and 1140 mV for the bare GC electrode.

(a): Linear sweep voltammograms, (b): onset potential for LBC, LBCF, LBCF-A, and naked GC electrode, (c): Tafel plots for LBC, LBCF, LBCF-A perovskite electrocatalysts.

The onset potential trends, shown in Fig. 5(b), further confirm the improvement in reaction thermodynamics upon surface modification. The onset potential for the bare GC, LBC, LBCF, and LBCF-A electrodes were 1.96 V, 1.59 V, 1.5 V, and 1.46 V, respectively.

The Tafel slope, a crucial kinetic parameter for OER, provides insight into the rate-determining steps (RDS). As shown in Fig. 5(c), LBCF-A exhibited the lowest Tafel slope (101 mV.dec−1) compared to LBC (156 mV.dec−1), LBCF (126 mV.dec−1), and the bare GC electrode (2583 mV.dec−1). The substantial decrease in the Tafel slope suggests a possible shift in the RDS from OOH* formation to proton transfer, thereby facilitating O–O bond coupling66,67.

The intrinsic catalytic activity was quantified by the TOF. For perovskite catalysts containing Co and Fe at the B-site and surface-deposited Ag nanoparticles, counting all metal species as active sites can overestimate TOF68,69. To compute the correct TOF for the OER, it is essential to distinguish between genuinely active sites and those that are inert or inaccessible. The accurate TOF can be expressed as TOF = (Rate of OER)/(active B-site elements + accessible surface Ag atoms)70,71. At an overpotential of 412 mV, LBCF-A exhibited a TOF approximately 6.6 times greater than LBC and 2.7 times greater than LBCF, indicating a higher density of accessible active sites (Fig. 6(a))72.

Mass activity, normalized to catalyst loading at 412 mV, followed a similar trend) Fig. 6(b)). 412 LBCF-A (45.75 A.g-1), outperformed LBC (7.14 A.g-1) and LBCF (17.36 A.g-1), by factors of 6.4 and 2.63, respectively. Detailed electrocatalytic parameters are provided in Table S2, and the performance surpasses that of previously reported Co-, Fe-, and Ag-based oxides (Table S3).

To better understand the observed enhancement, the was estimated via the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) method73. Cdl values were determined from the slope of the linear fit between half-capacitive current density and scan rate74. As shown in Fig. 6(c), LBCF-A had the highest Cdl (7.0 mF.cm-2), compared to LBC (4.6 mF.cm-2) and LBCF (5.4 mF.cm-2), indicating a larger electrochemically active surface.

EIS measurements, performed at 1.6 V over the frequency range 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz, are presented in Fig. 7(a). The Nyquist plots reveal progressively smaller semicircle with Fe substitution and Ag impregnation, indicating reduced charge-transfer resistance (Rct). Equivalent circuit fitting (inset, Fig. 7(a)) modeled the system as a series connection of solution resistance (Rs) and a parallel combination of Rct with a constant-phase element (CPE). Here, Rs accounts for solution resistance, material resistance, and contact resistance between the electrode and current collector. The reduced Rct in LBCF and LBCF-A suggests improved charge transfer, onsistent with LSV and Tafel results. Optimal Ag doping enhanced electrical integration, minimized ohmic losses, and lowered the energy barrier for OER18,74,75.

(a): EIS Nyquist plots of LBC, LBCF, LBCF-A electrodes tested at 1.6 V under open circuit condition, (b): Chronopotentiometric stability test of LBCF-A at a constant current density of 10 mA.cm-2 for 24 h.

Stability tests were performed via chronopotentiometry at 10 mA.cm-2 (Fig. 7(b)). LBCF-A maintained stable operation for 24 h, with only 0.04 V increase in potential, confirming its robust durability under continuous operation.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates the successful design and synthesis of La0.8Ba0.2CoO3-based perovskite electrocatalysts with dual Fe and Ag modification, achieving remarkable improvements in OER performance. Structural and compositional analyses confirmed that Ba2+ and Fe3+ were incorporated into the perovskite lattice, inducing lattice expansion and increasing the concentration of catalytically active Co3+ species and oxygen vacancies. Ag was deposited as metallic Ag0 on the surface, triggering electronic interactions with B-site cations, redistributing surface oxygen species, and enhancing hydroxyl and water adsorption. Electrochemical testing revealed that LBCF-A delivered the lowest overpotential (317 mV at 10 mA.cm-2), the smallest Tafel slope (101 mV.dec-1), the highest TOF, and mass activity over six times greater than the pristine LBC catalyst. These enhancements are attributed to the synergistic effects of Fe-induced lattice oxygen activation and Ag-mediated surface electronic modulation, which collectively improve conductivity, charge transfer, and intermediate adsorption during OER.
