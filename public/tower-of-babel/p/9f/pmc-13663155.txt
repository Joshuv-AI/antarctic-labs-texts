Association Between Life’s Crucial 9 and the prevalence of coronary heart disease in U.S. adults: the potential role of the systemic inflammatory response index
Authors: Ji Ouyang, Jiayuan Song, Yingjie Wu, Zishen Guo, Xiaoqian Liao, Zhixi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis studyaimed to examine the association between Life’s Crucial 9 (LC9) score and the prevalence of coronary heart disease (CHD) among U.S. adults, as well as explore the potential statistical contribution of Systemic Inflammatory Response Index (SIRI) in this association.MethodsData were obtained from 23,508 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2005 to 2018. LC9 score quartiles were used to summarize continuous and categorical variables, which were then reported as weighted means ± standard deviations or weighted frequencies and proportions, respectively. Differences among groups were examined using weighted t-tests and weighted chi-square tests. The relationship between LC9 scores, Ln-SIRI values, and the prevalence of CHD was investigated using weighted logistic regression models following the natural logarithmic transformation of skewed SIRI results. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline regression was utilized to investigate plausible nonlinear connections, subgroup and likelihood ratio tests investigated interaction effects, and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression was incorporated to determine the relative contributions of LC9 components to CHD. An exploratory non-causal mediation analysis using 1,000 bootstrap samples was conducted to assess SIRI as a potential statistical explanatory variable in the association between LC9 and the prevalence of CHD.ResultsIn Model 3, a 10-point increase in the LC9 score correlated with a 24% decrease in the odds of CHD prevalence; participants in the Q4 group had 59% lower odds of CHD compared with those in the Q1 group (P < 0.001 trend). Each Ln-SIRI unit was associated with 56% higher odds of CHD, with Q4 showing 2.02-fold higher odds than Q1 (P < 0.001 trend). Restricted cubic spline regression revealed linear relationships between LC9, Ln-SIRI, and the prevalence of CHD. In particular, LC9 was negatively associated with CHD, whereas Ln-SIRI was positively associated with CHD. Age significantly moderated the association between LC9 and CHD (P for interaction < 0.05), with stronger inverse associations in younger adults. WQS regression identified glucose (weight = 0.34) and tobacco (weight = 0.33) as key CHD-associated factors. Exploratory non-causal mediation analysis revealed that about 6.5% of the LC9-CHD relationship was statistically accounted for by SIRI (P < 0.001), with the majority of factors associated with the link remaining unexplained. This indicates that other factors may be the primary contributors to the LC9-CHD relationship.ConclusionThis study showed that higher LC9 scores were significantly associated with lower odds of prevalent self-reported CHD, while higher SIRI was associated with higher odds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06314-6.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is a predominant type of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and presents as a range of clinical syndromes resulting from atherosclerotic lesions in the coronary arteries. These lesions lead to contractions within the lumen of blood vessels, resulting in myocardial ischemia, hypoxia, and necrosis, which are the major types of ischemic heart disease [1]. American Heart Association’s (AHA’s) Heart Disease and Stroke Statistical Update, 2023, recognized CHD as a progressive cause of CVD fatalities in the United States. Additionally, reports from 2022 demonstrated that this disease was a significant contributor to cardiovascular fatalities, highlighting its ongoing threat to worldwide public health [2]. Consequently, identifying factors associated with CHD is crucial to mitigating these outcomes. Although cardiovascular health scoring systems such as Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) and Life’s Simple 7 (LS7) have been used in assessing the risk of CVD [3, 4], these tools focus primarily on physiological indicators and behavioral factors, thus failing to sufficiently integrate emerging metrics such as mental health.

CHD is closely associated with vascular endothelial damage, lipid deposition, and inflammatory response. The prevalence of CHD increases with age; however, recent global studies show increasing rates in younger demographics [5]. To comprehensively quantify cardiovascular health and assess the risk of CHD-related outcomes, AHA introduced the LE8 scoring system [6]. However, psychological factors like depression and anxiety have been identified as independently linked to CHD prevalence and atherosclerosis progression through activated inflammatory response [7]. The Life’s Crucial 9 (LC9) score was developed in 2020 to incorporate mental health indicators along with LE8, forming a nine-dimensional assessment system covering physical health (diet, exercise, blood pressure, blood glucose, lipids, body weight, sleep quality, and smoking) and mental health [8, 9]. This integration reflects the “biopsychosocial” model of cardiovascular health, mainly focusing on how psychological factors are associated with inflammation, blood circulation, and vascular functions. However, existing research has focused on the relationship between LC9 and conditions like aortic calcification and heart failure [10, 11]. The association with CHD prevalence remains unclear, and potential explanatory factors, like the Systemic Inflammatory Response Index (SIRI), which is calculated from neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts, are an emerging inflammatory marker that has been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in previous studies [12]. Clinical studies have shown that elevated SIRI is linked to poor prognostic outcomes in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) [13, 14]. We hypothesize that the multi-dimensional domains of LC9, particularly behavioral and psychological factors, closely correlate with the systemic inflammatory microenvironment, which may be reflected by composite indices such as SIRI [15]. Furthermore, this composite measure, which integrates lifestyle and physiological characteristics, has been linked to cardiovascular all-cause mortality [16].

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association between the multi-dimensional LC9 score and the prevalence of CHD in U.S. adults, and to demonstrate its added value compared to the traditional LE8 metric. Secondarily, we utilized WQS regression to determine the relative statistical contributions of individual LC9 components. Furthermore, we conducted an exploratory non-causal mediation analysis to assess whether the systemic inflammatory microenvironment (represented by SIRI) could serve as a statistical explanatory link in the LC9-CHD association. By establishing this hierarchy, we aim to provide comprehensive, multidimensional evidence for cardiovascular health assessment.

Methods

Study design and subject

Data accessed from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were utilized, and patients with CHD were recruited from 2005 to 2018 for this research. NHANES is administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which is a core component of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). It uses a complex, multistage, stratified probability sampling design to assess the health and nutritional status of the civilian non-institutionalized U.S. population. Participants were interviewed at their homes by the personnel trained by the NCHS and underwent a thorough physical examination, which involved the collection of blood and urine at a Mobile Examination Center (MEC). Detailed information on research execution can be obtained online at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.html.

A total of 70,190 participants from NHANES 2005–2018 were initially recruited into the study. However, following the exclusion of participants who were younger than 20 years, pregnant, or lacked survey weight data (n = 32,687); had missing data on coronary heart disease (CHD) status (n = 154); had insufficient data for calculating the LC9 score or SIRI (n = 11,752); or had missing covariate data (n = 2,089), the final number was 23,508 (Fig. 1).

Measurement of Life’s Crucial 9

The LC9 score was calculated based on nine components, which included the eight components of the LE8 score and the ninth component to specifically evaluate psychological health. Detailed scoring criteria for the LC9 components are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The eight LE8 components include diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health, body mass index, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure. Diet was assessed using the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015), with the detailed HEI-2015 components and scoring standards detailed in Supplementary Table 2 [17]. Each LE8 component was scored on a 0–100 scale according to the American Heart Association’s LE8 algorithm [18]. Psychological health was assessed by analyzing depressive symptoms using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [19]. To ensure consistency with the 0–100 scoring scale of the LE8 components, the depression component was reverse-coded as follows: PHQ-9 scores of 0–4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, and 20–27 were assigned 100, 75, 50, 25, and 0 points, respectively. Therefore, higher depression component scores indicated better psychological health. The overall LC9 score was calculated as the unweighted average of the nine component scores, with higher LC9 scores indicating better cardiovascular and psychological health.

We additionally used the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) as an independent variable in this study to estimate the 10-year risk of cardiovascular disease [20]. The FRS was calculated using a sex-specific algorithm based on seven traditional risk variables: age, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, total cholesterol (TC), systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive medication use, current smoking status, and diabetes status. Total FRS was then assessed for males and females combined.

Measurement of SIRI

SIRI was calculated using neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts based on the following formula: [12, 21].\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{SIRI} = \text{neutrophil count} \times \text{monocyte count/lymphocyte count}$$\end{document}

Neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts were obtained from complete blood count data measured using an automated hematology analyzer.

Diagnosis of coronary heart disease

Participants who replied “yes” to the NHANES Multiple Choice Questionnaire (MCQ160C) question, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have CHD?” were categorized as having CHD [22]. Therefore, in this cross-sectional research, CHD was defined as self-reported prevalent CHD.

Covariates

Potential covariates were identified in three areas: sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle and behavioral variables, and physiological and biochemical indicators. Detailed classifications and definitions of the covariates are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Sociodemographic traits encompassed age subgroups 20–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years; sex, female or male; race, Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and other races involving multiracial mix; educational attainment, below high school, high school graduate or general equivalency certificate (GEC) or equivalent, and a high school or higher; Poverty Income Ratio (PIR) was used to measure income concerning the federal poverty level, which takes into account factors such as economic inflation and family size. Marital status was grouped into: or cohabiting with a partner, those whose partners have passed away, those who are divorced or have experienced a separation, and those who have never been married before. The lifestyle behavior variable is the smoking status of the individuals. Alcohol consumption status is categorized into three groups: those who have never consumed alcohol, those who currently consume alcohol moderately, and those who currently consume alcohol [23]. Physiological and biochemical indices were categorized as uric acid (UA) and serum creatinine (SCr) (obtained by laboratory tests). Cancer history was determined based on self-reported questionnaire data.

Statistical analysis

Appropriate survey weights were utilized to represent the U.S. population using the complex multi-stage sampling of the NHANES database [24]. When analyses involve multiple data sources, such as laboratory data, dietary data, and questionnaire or examination data, the weight with the broadest coverage should be used, namely the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) examination weight (WTMEC2YR), to ensure the representativeness of the analytic sample. Because this study included seven survey cycles from 2005 to 2018, the combined multi-cycle weight was calculated as WTMEC2YR/7. The primary sampling unit and stratification variables were SDMVPSU and SDMVSTRA, respectively. We compared the distributions of several variables across four groups defined by quartiles of the LC9 score. Continuous variables have been presented as weighted means and standard deviations (SDs) and were analyzed using weighted t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed in the form of numbers as well as weighted proportions, and were also analyzed utilizing weighted chi-square tests [25]. We performed the Ln-transformation to reduce the skewness of SIRI [26].

First, we investigated the associations among LC9 score, aach component of LC9, FRS, LE8 score, SIRI, and self-reported CHD diagnosis using weighted logistic regression models. Both the LC9 score and SIRI were analyzed as continuous and categorical variables. Then three models were proposed: Model 1, which was unadjusted; Model 2, which involved adjustments for demographic traits (age, ethnicity, gender, education level, PIR, and marital status); and Model 3, which was fully adjusted and included adjustments for drinking status, cancer history, UA, and Scr. We also calculated the adjusted predicted probability of coronary heart disease and the marginal difference between the low LC9 group and the high LC9 group.To investigate potential nonlinear associations, we employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models, with the optimal number of knots determined to be 3 based on the minimum Akaike information criterion (AIC) [27]. Subgroup analyses were executed to investigate interaction effects across LC9 scores and covariates on the CHD prevalence, with likelihood ratio tests for interaction assessment [28]. We applied weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression to examine the relative contributions of the nine LC9 components to the prevalence of CHD. The mixture components were divided into four quantiles to characterize the exposure levels. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (40%) and a validation set (60%). The training set was processed through 1,000 bootstrap replications, with a weighting threshold of 0.11 set for the nine components [29]. Repeated hold validation was not used. Furthermore, an exploratory non-causal mediation analysis with 1,000 bootstrap samples was performed to examine the potential explanatory role of SIRI on the relationship between the LC9 score and the prevalence of CHD. This analysis quantified the total effect (TE), direct effect (DE), and indirect effect (IE) to evaluate the proportion of the association explained by SIRI [30]. We employed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate the discriminative ability of the LC9 score for identifying CHD, and further performed decision curve (DC) analysis to assess its clinical net benefit. The performance of LC9 was compared against both the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) and the Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) metric. Finally, we established two sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of the association between LC9 and the prevalence of CHD. In the first section, missing covariates were processed by the “MICE” package using multiple imputation (MI) [31]. The distribution differences of variables between the primitive population and the population with MI were compared. Then, we established a weighted logistic regression in the population with MI. In the second section, we excluded participants with a history of cancer or rheumatoid arthritis in sensitivity analysis. The overall statistical analyses were conducted utilizing R (version 4.4.1), with a two-tailed P < 0.05 regarded as significant.

Associations between LC9 scores and SIRI and CHD

In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), weighted logistic regression (Table 2) revealed a 24% decrease in the odds of CHD prevalence for every 10-point increase in the LC9 score (OR = 0.76, 95% CI:0.70–0.83, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the Q4 group showed a 59% reduction in the odds of CHD prevalence compared to the Q1 group (OR = 0.41, 95% CI:0.28–0.61, P < 0.001), with a significant downward trend observed across the quartiles (P for trend < 0.001). Conversely, every unit increment in Ln-SIRI was associated with 56% higher odds of CHD prevalence (OR = 1.56, 95% CI:1.31–1.87, P < 0.001), with the Q4 group showing 2.02-fold higher odds of CHD prevalence compared with the Q1 group (OR = 2.02, 95% CI:1.50–2.73). Moreover, a considerably increasing trajectory across quartiles was observed (P for trend < 0.001).

Exploratory non-causal mediation analysis of SIRI in the LC9-CHD association

Exploratory non-causal mediation analysis (Fig. 5) suggested that SIRI acted as a statistical explanatory variable in the cross-sectional association between LC9 score and the prevalence of CHD, although its contribution was small (P < 0.001). The indirect effect represented around 6.5% of the total effect, indicating that SIRI statistically accounted for a small proportion of the association. but statistically significant portion of the LC9-CHD association. These findings indicated that systemic inflammation could be, in part, a contributing factor to the inverse relationship observed between LC9 score and the prevalence of CHD.

Sensitivity analysis

In the first section, missing covariates were processed using MI. The results showed that there was no difference in the distribution of variables between the two populations except race, as shown in Supplementary Table 6. This indicates that the population with MI was highly similar to the variable distribution of the primitive population. Then, weighted logistic regressions were established in the population with MI. As shown in Supplementary Table 7, there was still a significant association between higher LC9 scores and lower odds of CHD prevalence, supporting the robustness of the main findings. The mediation analysis suggested that SIRI was statistically significantly involved in the association between LC9 score and the prevalence of CHD, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

In the second section, we excluded participants with a history of cancer or rheumatoid arthritis in the sensitivity analysis. The results still demonstrate the negative correlation between LC9 and CHD, as well as the mediating role of SIRI.

Discussion

This nationally representative cross-sectional study found that higher LC9 scores were significantly related to lower odds of CHD prevalence after adjusting for sociodemographic variables, drinking habits, cancer history, UA, and SCr. Notably, the LC9 score demonstrated superior discriminative ability and clinical net benefit for identifying CHD compared to the traditional LE8 metric. Conversely, a higher Ln-SIRI was linked to a higher likelihood of having CHD. RCS analyses supported linear associations between LC9 and Ln-SIRI. Exploratory non-causal mediation analysis suggested that SIRI served as a statistical explanatory variable in the LC9-CHD association, although only slightly. WQS regression identified blood glucose and tobacco exposure as the major LC9 components associated with the CHD prevalence. Our empirical findings are strictly cross-sectional and statistical in nature, which means that the involvement of SIRI in the LC9–CHD association is not definitive evidence that improving LC9 components via inflammatory mitigation will actively reduce CHD prevalence. Nonetheless, to contextualize these cross-sectional associations within the broader biomedical framework, prior experimental and longitudinal literature have hypothesized several plausible biological pathways linking these factors.

Various components of LC9 are associated with CHD pathophysiology. Our findings indicate that these components, ranging from behavioral factors like tobacco use and physical activity to physiological markers like glucose and blood pressure, collectively reflect an individual’s cardiovascular health profile [32–41]. Recent studies have established the prognostic value of the LC9 score in diseases associated with aortic calcification and heart failure [10, 11]. The findings of this current study corroborate the validity of the LC9 score as a comprehensive assessment tool.

Our findings are consistent with previous evidence, showing associations between CHD and LS7 and LE8 scores [6, 42]. Crucially, our comparative analyses (ROC and decision curves) empirically confirm that LC9 serves as a significant improvement over the LE8 framework by including the assessment of mental health, yielding superior discriminative performance. A recent study has shown that the LC9, which incorporates depression scores, is associated with CVD and all-cause mortality, demonstrating associations that are comparable to or superior to those of LE8 [43]. Recent meta-analyses and comprehensive reviews have consistently shown that depression is significantly associated with increased CVD-related morbidity and mortality [44, 45]. Al-Sutari confirmed that mitigative approaches tailored towards identity, personal and therapeutic controls, and emotional performance are associated with improved mental health in individuals suffering from CHD [46]. Most investigations analyzing NHANES data have shown associations between depressive symptoms and CVD mortality [47]. Psychological traits such as intense anxiety and depression are associated with elevated CRP levels and sympathetic nervous system activation, which are associated with CVD risk [48]. A prospective cohort study showed that positive psychosocial factors are associated with better CVD outcomes [49]. Interventions targeting psychological well-being in individuals suffering from CHD have shown potential for improving outcomes [50]. Notably, people who have good mental health often have socioeconomic advantages, as evidenced by higher social status and healthier lifestyle habits, including frequently engaging in exercise, consumption of a balanced diet, and avoidance of smoking [51]. These imply that lower CVD prevalence is linked to healthy behaviors, such as maintaining mental health.

LC9 and CHD were found to be negatively correlated in a pooled analysis using multivariate logistic regression. Additionally, the linear negative association between LC9 and CHD was verified using RCS regression. Subgroup analyses showed a significant age-related gradient in the association between LC9 and CHD after adjusting for confounders, with stronger negative associations in those aged 20–59 than in those aged 60. Regarding behavioral factors, younger individuals may be more responsive to lifestyle modifications and show higher adherence to smoking cessation, regular exercise, and psychological adjustment [52]. Conversely, this association might be attenuated in older age groups due to cumulative age-related physiological changes and longer durations of exposure to risk factors [53]. Furthermore, age-related differences in systemic inflammatory profiles may partially account for why the statistical association involving SIRI was more noticeable in this demographic [54]. The potential impact of mental health on younger subgroups warrants further investigation, as psychological stress and its related inflammatory responses may be more modifiable through early intervention compared to the chronic, persistent stress patterns often observed in older populations [55]. Younger populations may exhibit higher susceptibility to the deleterious effects of tobacco and glycemic dysregulation due to evolving metabolic profiles, whereas these factors are often compounded by long-term systemic damage in older individuals [56]. These findings suggest that LC9 components, particularly tobacco exposure, glucose status, and psychological health, may be more strongly associated with CHD prevalence in adults aged 20–59 years. Future interventional and longitudinal research is needed to determine whether altering these components can enhance cardiovascular health throughout life.

Exploratory non-causal mediation analysis suggested that SIRI acted as an associated inflammatory marker in the cross-sectional association between LC9 and CHD prevalence, although only slightly. Specifically, the SIRI-related statistical explanatory proportion represented roughly 6.5% of the overall LC9-CHD association, while the rest of the association constituted 93.5%. This pattern suggests that systemic inflammation could be a possible explanatory link; however, it should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship. As an emerging inflammatory marker, SIRI has shown promise in evaluating cardiovascular outcomes. A prospective cohort study by Kong showed a strong association between elevated SIRI and an increased risk of CVD mortality in the obese population [12]. Similar associations were observed in [57], chronic kidney disease [25], chronic heart failure [58], and peritoneal dialysis [59]. It has been determined that elevated SIRI is associated with increased CVD prevalence through multiple mechanisms, including chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, increased thrombotic tendency, and metabolic syndrome [60, 61]. However, the predominant direct effect (93.5%) observed in our study indicates that systemic inflammation, as assessed by SIRI, accounts for only a marginal fraction of the LC9-CHD relationship. This implies that several other biological mechanisms, apart from systemic inflammation, probably influence the negative correlation between LC9 and CHD. These mechanisms may include restoration of endothelial function, optimization of lipid metabolism, improved vascular remodeling, enhanced metabolic reserve, and potentially epigenetic modifications. These alternative mechanisms and factors warrant further investigation to comprehensively understand how LC9 components collectively relate to lower CHD odds.

WQS analysis showed that low blood glucose and non-smoking were the main factors negatively associated with CHD prevalence. Given that blood glucose and tobacco exposure are well-established cardiovascular risk factors, it is biologically plausible that they emerged as the primary components related to the prevalence of CHD in our model. Earlier research has broadly demonstrated that sustained hyperglycemia and smoking can synergistically exacerbate atherosclerosis through macro-pathways involving insulin resistance-mediated lipid metabolism disorders, oxidative stress, and profound endothelial dysfunction [62]. Consistent with these mechanisms, epidemiologic data suggest that smokers have a significantly higher risk of CHD compared with nonsmokers, and this risk decreases after smoking cessation [2]. Mendelian randomization studies have confirmed a causal association between smoking initiation and the risk of coronary artery disease in individuals suffering from diabetes mellitus [63]. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive interventions targeting both glycemic control and smoking cessation in patients with CHD, and provide new strategies for CHD prevention and management. Given the cross-sectional nature of our study, these results represent statistical associations rather than causal evidence that modifying these components directly lowers CHD prevalence.

The advantages of this research include the use of extensive national sample data of 23,508 participants from NHANES and weighted statistics to ensure that the results are representative and reliable, to facilitate extrapolatable conclusions. Additionally, this study employed various statistical methods, such as weighted logistic regression, RCS, WQS regression, and exploratory mediation analysis, to thoroughly assess the relationships between the LC9 scores, SIRI, and the prevalence of CHD. The incorporation of depressive symptoms into the LC9 framework also provides a broader perspective for multidimensional cardiovascular health assessment. This study also has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES data precludes the establishment of temporal or causal relationships among LC9, SIRI, and CHD. Consequently, our mediation analysis should be interpreted as strictly exploratory and non-causal. Reverse causation remains plausible, as CHD prevalence or its corresponding treatments may directly alter systemic inflammatory profiles, lifestyle behaviors, physical activity, psychological status, and medication use. Second, the identification of CHD and several LC9 components, such as smoking status, dietary intake, and depressive symptoms, relied on self-reported questionnaires. This survey-based method may lead to recall bias and possible misclassification. Additionally, it does not provide comprehensive clinical details concerning the timing of disease onset, the severity of the disease, any incidents that occurred, or the specific treatments administered. Third, although we adjusted for multiple relevant covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be completely ruled out. Specifically, we were unable to adjust for medication use (e.g., statin therapy, antihypertensive therapy, anti-inflammatory medication) or the history/duration of CHD treatment, as these factors may be situated on the causal pathway between cardiovascular health (LC9) and CHD; adjusting for them might introduce over-adjustment bias. Additionally, the lack of granular data on acute infections, chronic inflammatory conditions, and hematological disorders—which could potentially influence inflammatory markers like SIRI—remains a limitation of this study. Fourth, excluding participants with incomplete data during the data-cleaning phase may have led to selection bias, potentially impacting the generalizability of our findings. However, sensitivity analyses employing multiple imputation have reinforced the robustness of the primary results. Fifth, while the LC9 score builds upon the established LE8 framework by incorporating psychological health, there is still uncertainty concerning its optimal construction. In this study, the overall LC9 score was calculated as an unweighted average of nine component scores, which assumes equal contribution of all components. The optimal weighting and construct validity of these multidimensional domains require further validation in future prospective studies. Finally, because all variables were measured concurrently, the exploratory mediation findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal pathway. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal sequence and potential causal relationships among LC9, SIRI, and prevalent CHD.

Conclusion

This study showed that higher LC9 scores were significantly associated with lower odds of prevalent self-reported CHD, while higher SIRI was associated with higher odds. SIRI acted as a statistical explanatory variable in the cross-sectional LC9-CHD association, although only slightly, suggesting that systemic inflammation may be a potential explanatory link. However, given the cross-sectional design, the directionality and causality of these relationships cannot be established. Furthermore, reverse causality, where existing CHD affects lifestyle behaviors, psychological well-being, physical activity, medication usage, and inflammatory profiles, continues to be a plausible consideration. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships among LC9, SIRI, and CHD.
