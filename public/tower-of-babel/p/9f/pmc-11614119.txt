Nocturnal wearing of complete dentures and obstructive sleep apnea: A meta-analysis
Authors: Sunit Kumar Jurel, Husbana Altaf Bakrolwala, Pooran Chand, Raghuwar Dayal Singh, Rushikesh Bhau Bhujbal, Balendra Pratap Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Statement of Problem:Improvement in outcomes of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) could answer the question of whether nocturnal wearing of removable complete dentures is beneficial.Purpose:To evaluate the available literature that compares nocturnal wearing of removable complete dentures or overdentures in patients diagnosed with OSA on the Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), quality of sleep (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [PSQI]) and other outcomes.Methods:Registration of the proposed protocol was done in an open-source registry. Databases (Medline, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) were searched until August 2024. All the reports were screened by reading the abstracts and full texts by two independent reviewers and conflicts were resolved by the third reviewer in Covidence. Data extraction and risk of bias assessment as per the Risk of Bias 1.0 tool (RoB 1.0) were done in Covidence. Mean difference and risk ratios were used as effect measures for continuous and dichotomous outcomes. Statistical analysis was performed using a fixed effect model. The certainty of evidence was judged by the GRADE approach.Results:Out of 2428 available reports, three randomized controlled trials were included for statistical analyses involving a total of 123 participants. The pooled estimate for AHI showed no evidence of any difference with and without nocturnal wear of removable complete dentures (MD: 2.88; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.94 to 6.70; 246 participants). No evidence of any difference was found for PSQI (MD: 0.20; 95% CI: -0.97 to 1.37; 138 participants) and for ESS (MD: 0.20; 95% CI: -1.60 to 1.20; 138 participants).Conclusion:The review found insufficient evidence to judge that nocturnal wearing of removable complete dentures was effective in patients with OSA measured by Apnea Hypopnea Index, PSQI, and Epworth sleepiness scale.

INTRODUCTION

As per the World Health Organization (WHO), the loss of teeth can have psychological, social, and functional impacts. The global average prevalence of complete tooth loss is approximately 7% among individuals aged 20 years or older. For those aged 60 years or older, the estimated global prevalence is much higher at 23%.[1] In a survey conducted by Peltzer et al. across various countries including China, Russia, India, South Africa, Mexico, and Ghana, the prevalence of complete tooth loss among the elderly ranged from 3% to 21%.[2] In India specifically, the WHO reports a prevalence of 19% among individuals aged 65–74.[2] In the USA, between 2009 and 2012, the prevalence of edentulism was 4.9%,[3] while in European countries it was 3%.[4] It is observed that countries with higher socioeconomic status and better access to healthcare tend to have more effective measures in place to address the prevalence of edentulism.[5]

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a frequently encountered medical disorder. Its prevalence ranged from 9% to 38% in general young adults and a higher prevalence is found in population above 60 years of age.[6] It was estimated that approximately 936 million population (age 30–69 years) have OSA as per the American Association of Sleep Medicine 2012 criteria and Apnea Hypopnea Index (AHI) of ≥5 events/hr.[7] Furthermore, data from the United States and Europe suggest that 14% to 49% of men have clinically significant OSA.[8]

The characteristic feature of OSA is the partial (hypopnea) or complete (apnea) cessation of breathing which causes desaturation of blood (hypoxemia). The apnea period lasts from 20 to 40 s, when the oxyhemoglobin desaturation exceeds 3% arousal from sleep occurs to reopen the airway.[9] This leads to disturbed sleep and excessive daytime somnolence, headache, disorientation, diabetes, and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.[1011] Based on AHI, OSA is graded as mild (5–15), moderate (15–30), and severe (>30).[12]

Previous research indicates that completely edentulous patients are more prone to developing OSA.[131415] Duration of edentulism increases the incidence of OSA as well as severity of OSA. Various reasons behind the increased prevalence of OSA in the elderly are increased upper airway collapsibility and decreased neuromuscular control.[131516] Furthermore, loss of teeth leads to collapsed vertical dimension along with reduction in muscle tone of pharynx; mandibular movement alterations; altered tongue form and function; and decrease in the size of the upper airway eventually causing a decrease in the retropharyngeal space which increases the susceptibility of OSA.[14] Anatomical changes associated with edentulism and reduced airway space have been reported by a cephalometric analysis done in the edentulous patients on removal of their complete dentures.[17] Bucca et al. reported a higher prevalence of airway obstruction and poorer AHI when participants slept with dentures in comparison to without dentures at night.[18]

Treatment modalities for edentulous OSA patients include lifestyle changes, mandibular advancement devices (MAD), positive airway pressure, and tongue-retaining appliances.[19] Patients have a higher failure rate in regular use of continuous positive airway pressure while sufficient dentition is required by oral appliances for retention.[2021] Noncompliance for positive airway pressure has been 34% in the recent two decades despite various innovative techniques come in the market such as changes in mask design, materials, and delivery of airway pressure.[22] Studies have enumerated various short-term side effects of wearing oral appliances such as xerostomia, hypersalivation, and pain in teeth, muscles, and temporomandibular joints. The major long-term side effect of continued MAD usage is an alteration in occlusion.[2324]

Complete dentures in both maxillary and mandibular arches not only replace all the natural teeth but also alter the position of the tongue, soft palate and cheek, positional relation between the two jaws, and pharyngeal airway space.[17] Few studies have found that complete dentures lead to an increase in retropharyngeal space which helps in reducing the severity of OSA.[17182526]

Many edentulous elderly individuals wear their dentures at night, and while a limited number of studies assessed the role of nocturnal dentures use during sleep, results have been inconsistent.[23] Von Goten et al. reported that edentulous individuals who sleep without dentures showed increased muscle activity in the jaw-closing muscles (elevator muscles), as measured by electromyography.[272829] These muscles lift the lower jaw, and wearing complete dentures disrupts their normal stimulation, indirectly causing the throat passage (pharynx) to open more. This interruption of muscle activity is seen as helpful in OSA because it reduces the occurrences of breathing interruptions during sleep.[3031]

Some studies suggest that wearing dentures at night may be detrimental, causing issues such as pharyngeal musculature problems, open bite, and worsening of OSA, while others contend that nocturnal denture use maintains sleep quality and pharyngeal patency. Removal of dentures at night has been encouraged historically stating that it reduces the stresses to the residual alveolar ridge by providing tissue rest. Failure to remove dentures may also cause soreness and irritation leading to serious oral lesions such as inflammatory papillary hyperplasia and candidiasis.[3233]

In addition, the nocturnal wearing of complete dentures is unlike MAD as there is no advancement of the mandible done, and also the increase in vertical dimension is only up to the vertical dimension of occlusion recorded during jaw relation for fabricating the complete dentures. The stability of bases may be compromised during sleep when evaluating removable complete dentures. All these differences between oral appliances and nocturnal use of complete dentures can be contributing factors to the overall interpretation of findings.

These changes which occur due to edentulism disturb sleep. Proper knowledge of effective intervention and its application would be beneficial to clinicians and health authorities providing services to edentulous OSA patients. Management of such cases is challenging, and optimal treatment should be rendered to ensure improvement and comfort and prevent possible complications.

Hence, evidence is needed to synthesize the effect of the nocturnal use of complete dentures in edentulous patients suffering from mild-to-moderate OSA. This review evaluates the effectiveness of the nocturnal use of removable complete dentures or overdentures[34] in patients suffering from mild-to-moderate OSA with alternative treatment without nocturnal use of removable complete dentures on patient’s centered outcomes.

METHODS

Protocol was preregistered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews before initiating this review.[35] The entire review was reported as per Preferred Reporting Items for Systemic Reviews and Meta-Analysis.[36] We searched three databases: Medline, Embase and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials till August 2024. The following PICO summary was used to prepare unique keywords for database search to find relevant reports for the research question. The research question was “Does wearing of removable complete dentures or overdentures at night in comparison to without wearing at night cause improvement in OSA patients.”

Screening of reports based on title and abstract was done first followed by full text screening by two authors independently [HB & SJ]. Conflicts if any were resolved by the third author [BPS]. Following full-text screening, data extraction was performed in Covidence, including the risk of bias (RoB 1.0 tool of Cochrane), again by the same two authors independently, and then consensus was taken in the same software. Any conflict was resolved by the third author.

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) as well as cross-over trials, adhering to the PICO criteria.

Exclusion criteria were participants with other health conditions, medication use, illicit drug consumption affecting sleep architecture or respiratory muscle activity, posing a risk to sleep. Studies with errors in data, duplicate studies, irrelevant outcomes, case reports, conference proceedings, and letters to the editor were excluded.

Data extraction of each included study was done on five sections as mentioned in Covidence software and included below-mentioned details.

After data extraction in Covidence, statistical analysis and report writing were done in RevMan 5.4 software.[41] We contacted the corresponding author for any clarifications for data if required. RoB 1.0 tool was used for the assessment of RoB as stated in the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews.[42] In this tool, there are seven domains that helped to judge the risk of bias for each study as mentioned in Supplementary Figures 1 and 2. Judgment of each domain was done as low or unclear or high RoB with supporting annotations from the included study.

We used a fixed-effect model to generate a forest plot.[42] We evaluated heterogeneity using I2 statistic: If I2 exceeded 50%, it indicates substantial heterogeneity among studies. The GRADE approach was used to judge the certainty of evidence[4344] using the following factors: Inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, risk of bias, and publication bias. Based on these factors, the outcome was downgraded by one or two levels for the above three outcomes.

RESULTS

A total of 2442 records were found after database searches until August 2024. After screening on the basis of abstract and full text three studies were eligible for meta-analysis.[314546] Study screening flow diagram is presented in Figure 1. Table 1 shows the summary of the characteristics of the included studies. All three studies were randomized cross-over trials with varying first periods before cross-over, i.e., 15 days, 1 day, and 30 days, respectively, for Almeida 2011,[31] Chen 2017,[45] and Emami 2021.[46] Three studies randomized participants into either with nocturnal wear or without nocturnal wear of removable complete dentures. This review included a total of 123 participants with 62 with and 61 without nocturnal wear of removable complete dentures in the first period and vice versa in the second period.[314546] One included study[46] reported 7 participants lost to follow-up whereas there were no dropouts in other included studies.[3145]

Certainty of evidence was assessed and presented in “Summary of finding table” in Table 2 using the GRADE approach. Certainty of evidence for AHI was graded to be low level and for PSQI and ESS, it was very low level. The reasons for downgrading the certainty of evidence were limitations in study design because of high RoB due to the absence of blinding of participant in all studies, and imprecision due to small sample size and confidence interval crossing line of no effect.

DISCUSSION

This review compared the effect of nocturnal wear of removable complete dentures in participants with OSA on AHI, ESS, and PSQI.

All included studies exhibited at least one high risk of bias, primarily attributed to the lack of blinding of participants. In this type of intervention, blinding of participants could not be done which may impact the subjective assessment of outcomes such as ESS and PSQI. In one study, the absence of information regarding trial registry numbers raises concerns about potential selective outcome reporting, particularly for reporting favorable outcomes. This omission negatively impacts the certainty of evidence.

All the studies had variations in the follow-up period before and after cross-over being 15 days, 1 day, and 30 days for Almeida,[31] Chen,[45] and Emami,[46] respectively. No separate first-period data was obtained from any of the included studies. It has been stated that there is no significant period or order effect, hence the various differences in period was justified and the data were combined and analyzed together.

As per the preregistered protocol, only mild and moderate OSA patients were to be included in the assessment, however in all three studies, participants belonging to severe OSA were also included, and combined data was given. Separate data for the first period or according to OSA severity could not be obtained even after contacting the authors.

The recent publication which accessed the efficacy of nocturnal wear of complete dentures on OSA found no effect, also it included retrospective as well as prospective studies and search was done till September 2020.[47]

The AHI serves as an indicator of the severity of OSA, with higher scores denoting more severe OSA. The pooled results from three studies in this review showed no evidence of any difference between participants who wore removable complete dentures at night and those who did not in terms of AHI. While Emami et al.[46] reported no significant difference in mean AHI between denture wearers and nonwearers, Almeida et al.[31] noted a worsening of AHI with nocturnal denture use, and Chen et al.[45] reported significantly higher AHI for denture wearers. In the study by Emami et al.[46] a significant number of participants belonged to the category of severe OSA when compared to the study by Almeida et al.[31] and Chen et al.,[45] this can be a contributing factor leading to differences in the study results.

The PSQI questionnaire, assessing perceived sleep quality over 30 days,[48] showed no evidence of any difference between with and without nocturnal wear of removable complete dentures from a single study. Emami et al.[46] reported negligible differences in daytime sleepiness between those wearing dentures at night and those who did not.

Daytime sleepiness, a common concern in OSA, was assessed utilizing the ESS.[49] The review’s results showed minimal differences in daytime sleepiness between denture wearers and nonwearers.

Only one study, Emami et al.[46] reported both these outcomes. In this study 24 out of 70 participants belonged to the category of severe OSA, making it difficult to interpret the improvement of outcomes in patients with mild-to-moderate OSA. As the grading of severity increases the effect of nocturnal denture wear on OSA will decrease. Furthermore, the other two studies did not report this outcome, so adequate evidence could not be obtained to justify the results.

We did not find any evidence for outcomes such as Stanford sleepiness score and nasal symptoms, patient compliance to nocturnal use of complete dentures, and disease-specific quality of life like SAQLI score or FOSQ in the included randomized controlled trials.

In summary, this review indicates that we are uncertain about the role of wearing of complete dentures at night on AHI, sleep quality (PSQI), or daytime sleepiness (ESS score) in edentulous patients with OSA. As there is no observed reduction in OSA severity or enhancement in quality of life, and considering the detrimental effects of denture use on oral health (e.g., denture stomatitis, mucosal inflammation, residual ridge resorption, and TMJ disorders), it is suggested that patients may benefit from removing their dentures while sleeping.[50515253]

It is recommended that future randomized controlled trials (RCTs) should prioritize robust methodologies in the selection of participants, randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, method of denture fabrication, inclusion of patient’s centered outcomes, adhering to the reporting guidelines such as CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials).[54] In addition, the limited sample size across studies underscores the restricted evidence available on this topic, emphasizing the need for larger and more rigorous investigations in the future. Outcomes such as quality of life and patient compliance should be also considered for the same.

CONCLUSION

According to this review, uncertainty exists about the effect of nocturnal wearing of removable complete dentures on AHI, ESS, quality of sleep (PSQI), and other outcomes on patients having mild-to-moderate grades of OSA. Future trials will most likely change this finding.
