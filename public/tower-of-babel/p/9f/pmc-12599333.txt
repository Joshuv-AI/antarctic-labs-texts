The effect of osseodensification versus conventional drilling technique on bone osteotomy with and without implant placement in dog’s maxilla
Authors: Reham A.H. Ibrahim, Mohamed Adel Ahmed, Ashraf M. Abu-Seida, Mahmoud Hanafy, Sara El Moshy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:This study investigated the bone regenerative potential following immediate implant insertion using osseodensifying Densah drills compared to conventional drills.Materials and methods:Eight adult male mongrel dogs were randomly assigned to three groups using a split-mouth design. Atraumatic extraction of the maxillary second premolar was performed in all dogs, and the sockets of the distal roots served as the control group. Group I was subdivided into two subgroups: in subgroup IA, the mesial socket of the second premolar was drilled using NucleOSS drills, while in subgroup IB, the socket was prepared using osseodensifying Densah drills. The dogs were euthanized immediately after osteotomy preparation. Group II was also subdivided into two subgroups: in subgroup IIA, osteotomy preparation was performed using NucleOSS drills, while in subgroup IIB, the osteotomy was prepared using Densah drills, followed by the insertion of NucleOSS T6 implants in both subgroups. The dogs were euthanized two months after implant insertion. Specimens were stained using Hematoxylin and Eosin as well as Masson Trichrome stain and subjected to histomorphometric analysis. Cone beam computed tomography was used to measure radiographic bone density.Results:Subgroup IA exhibited areas of thermal osteonecrosis around the drilling site. Subgroup IB showed bone chips compressed into the bone marrow spaces of the osteotomy wall. The bone area percentage and bone density around implants in subgroup IIB were significantly higher than those in subgroup IIA (P<0.05).Conclusion:Osseodensification is an effective technique for increasing bone density around implants in areas of poor bone quality, which is considered a promising indicator of long-term clinical success.

Introduction

Bone density is an important factor in assessing implant stability (1). The quality and quantity of the bone’s histological structure at the implant interface are closely related to the bone density at the implant insertion site, and bone strength is directly linked to mineral density and collagen quality (2). Preserving bone mass during osteotomy preparation is essential for ensuring implant stability and long-term clinical success (3).

Conventional drilling procedures for osteotomy preparation involve cutting and removing bone using twisted drills to create a cylindrical hole for implant placement. However, conventional osteotomies have several drawbacks, including the potential for necrosis due to heat generation in the absence of adequate irrigation and inaccurate preparation caused by tool vibration (4, 5). Conventional drills operate in a clockwise direction using a subtractive technique with a positive rake angle, which induces strains in the remaining bone that exceed the threshold for bone microdamage. As a result, osseous tissue requires more than three months to heal (6).

Several studies have been conducted to improve bone density and quality at implant placement sites (7, 8). The bone condensation technique using osteotomes is a surgical method that employs specialized condensers and expanders to compress bone along the osteotomy walls, increasing bone density in low-density areas. However, this approach can lead to trabecular fractures and the formation of debris, which may hinder the osseointegration process (9, 10).

A novel bone-preserving osteotomy preparation technique for implant bed preparation is based on the concept of osseodensification drilling. Osseodensification (OD), a biomechanical, non-subtractive osteotomy preparation technique, was introduced by Salah Huwais in 2013 (11). Densah drills are designed to enhance primary stability by densifying the osteotomy site walls. Unlike conventional drills, osseodensification drills rotate in a counterclockwise (CCW) direction with abundant irrigation, compacting bone apically and laterally against the osteotomy walls. This increases the bone-to-implant contact percentage and improves primary stability (12). Additionally, Densah drills combine the benefits of cutting efficiency with improved tactile control during osteotomy preparation (13). Densah drills are characterized by a tapered geometry with multiple flutes, enabling a faster feed rate and reduced heat production. Furthermore, these drills feature a tapered shank and a cutting chisel edge, allowing them to penetrate deeper into the osteotomy site. Designed with a negative rake angle, densifying drills compact bone at 800–1500 rpm in a non-cutting counterclockwise direction (11). Additionally, osseodensifying Densah drills create smaller osteotomy diameters compared to conventional drills due to the compaction of bone trabeculae. This phenomenon, known as the viscoelastic strain recovery or “spring-back effect,” occurs in cancellous bone following osteotomy preparation (14).

The OD technique forms a layer of condensed autograft around the implant. The autologous bone in direct contact with the implant surface exhibits greater primary stability due to the physical interlocking between the bone and implant, particularly in low-density bone (11, 12). Furthermore, OD promotes the rapid ingrowth of new bone, as osteoblasts grow on the osteotomy bone in immediate contact with the implant, potentially enhancing osseointegration (15, 16). The OD technique has gained attention for its ability to increase primary implant stability; however, limited data are available on bone quality following OD application. Therefore, this study investigated the bone regenerative potential following immediate implant insertion using osseodensifying Densah drills compared to conventional drills in the maxilla of dogs. The null hypothesis is as follows: there is no significant difference in bone osteotomy outcomes, including bone density, histological structure, and osseointegration, between the osseodensification drilling technique and the conventional drilling technique, with or without implant placement, in the maxilla of dogs.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) - Cairo University (Approval number: CU/III/F/69/19). This study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and regulations.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software. Regarding the primary outcome (percentage of new bone area), it was determined that four dogs per time point were appropriate for the study, resulting in a total sample size of eight dogs (zero day and 60 days). The study was designed with a power of 80% and an α error probability of 0.05. Each dog underwent procedures on both sides, representing the two groups at each time point. Therefore, the total sample size was eight dogs (16 sites divided by two) (17).

Animals

Eight healthy adult male mongrel dogs were obtained from the animal house at Cairo University’s Faculty of Veterinary Medicine. Each dog was housed in a cage specifically designed for the facility. The inclusion criteria required the dogs to be in good health, free from dental diseases, weighing between 15–16 kg, and at least 1.5 years old. The exclusion criteria included dogs with heavy plaque, gingivitis, periodontal disease, tooth fractures, systemic diseases such as rabies, or female dogs.

Surgical procedures

The dogs were anesthetized using Atropine sulfate (Atropine sulfate 1%, ADWIA, Egypt), administered subcutaneously, followed by Xylazine HCl (Xylaject 2%, ADWIA, Egypt), given intramuscularly. Ketamine HCl (Ketamine hydrochloride, Rotexmedica Co., Germany) was then administered intravenously at a dose of 5 mg/kg body weight through a cephalic vein cannula to induce anesthesia. To maintain general anesthesia, an intravenous dose of 2.5% Thiopental sodium (Thiopental sodium, EIPICO, Egypt) at 25 mg/kg body weight was administered. Additionally, local anesthesia was provided by infiltration of Lidocaine 2% with Epinephrine 1:100,000 (Octacaine 100 Local Anesthetic, Novocol Pharmaceutical, Canada) into the mucosa at the surgical sites.

The bilateral maxillary second premolars were extracted atraumatically using a tungsten-carbide surgical bur (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). The teeth were sectioned at the bifurcation in a buccolingual direction, allowing each root to be removed individually using an elevator and forceps without damaging the surrounding bone (Figure 1).

In Groups I and II, the apical portion of the mesial socket of the maxillary second premolars on both sides of each dog was prepared using either the conventional drilling protocol with NucleOSS drills (NucleOSS company, Izmir, Turkey) in a clockwise rotation (subgroups IA and IIA) or Densah drills (Versah, LLC, Jackson, MI, USA) in a counterclockwise rotation (subgroups IB and IIB) (Figure 1 g, h). The distal sockets in Group I were left unprepared and served as the control group.

The Densah drill sequence for osteotomy preparation followed sizes 2.0, 2.3, 2.5, and 3.0 mm, with drilling performed in a counterclockwise direction. In contrast, the conventional drill sequence followed sizes 2.0, 2.8, 3.3, and 3.5 mm, with drilling performed in a clockwise direction.

In subgroups IA and IB, the prepared sockets were left empty without implant placement. In subgroups IIA and IIB, after osteotomy preparation, eight cylindrical, threaded, endosseous titanium implants (3.5 mm × 10 mm, NucleOSS T6 dental implants, NucleOSS company, Izmir, Turkey) were placed in each animal, with one side receiving the conventional drilling protocol (subgroup IIA) and the other side receiving the osseodensification protocol (subgroup IIB) (Figure 1 i). The implants were capped with healing screws, and the wounds were sutured using 3/0 vicryl sutures.

Following the completion of the surgical procedures, the animals received intramuscular injections of diclofenac sodium (Voltaren 75 amp, Novartis Co., Egypt) at a dose of 1.1 mg/kg and cefotaxime sodium (Cefotax 250 mg vial, T3A Co., Egypt) at 10 mg/kg, administered once daily for five postoperative days (18). During the first postoperative week, the dogs were maintained on a soft diet consisting of bread, milk, rice, and soup. From the second week onward, they resumed their regular diet.

Histological assessment

In Group I and the control group, the dogs were euthanized immediately, while in Group II, euthanasia was performed two months after implant insertion. According to the study design, 20 mL of 5% Thiopental sodium solution (anesthetic overdose) was rapidly injected intravenously to euthanize the dogs. The maxillary regions of interest were then extracted for analysis.The specimens were fixed in 10% neutral formalin at room temperature for a maximum of five days. Bone specimens were then decalcified by immersion in a solution of equal parts 45% formic acid and 20% sodium citrate for two to three months.

Following decalcification, the samples were dehydrated in gradually increasing alcohol concentrations, cleared with Xylol, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Paraffin cross-sections of 5–6 microns in thickness were mounted on clear glass slides. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate the histopathological characteristics of the bone defect using a Leica Qwin 500 light microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., Switzerland) at 10× and 40× magnification. Masson’s Trichrome stain was also used to illustrate the different stages of collagen maturation, observed under 10× magnification objective lens.

Histomorphometric analysis

The percentage of newly formed bone (%) was measured from Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s Trichrome stained sections in subgroups IIA and IIB. Morphometric analysis was conducted using the Leica Qwin 500 image analyzer computer system (Leica Microsystems, Switzerland). The image analyzer system comprised a colored video camera, a colored monitor, and a Dell personal computer connected to the microscope, all controlled by Leica Qwin 500 software. Before analysis, the system was automatically calibrated to convert measurement units from pixels (as generated by the software) into actual micrometer units for accurate assessment.

Radiography evaluation

Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) Scans were taken with PlanMeca Romexis Promax machine (PlanMeca Romexis, Helsinki, Finland) with identical settings of 14 mA and 84 kV, in control group, immediately after drilling in group I and after two months of implant insertion in group II, using a small field of view (50× 80 mm) and voxel size 0.1 mm (100 micron). Blue sky Bio third party software was used to analyze the scans (Software magnification 4×). Bone density was measured using HU at 3 levels, cervical, middle, and apical 1/3, both on the buccal and palatal aspect for all the designated implant sites. The average density of the various sites in HU was assessed by a radiologist with 8 years experience by measuring the average density of rectangles at the areas of interest. All CBCT units initially provide correlated coronal and sagittal perpendicular multi-planar reformation images. To standardize the measurement, on a reformatted coronal cut with slice thickness 1mm, slice interval 1mm, a rectangle with average dimension 1mm width × 3.3 mm height +/-2 mm was drawn on both buccal and palatal sides of the designated implant or the implant site at the cervical, middle, and apical part and the average HU value was calculated for both the buccal and the palatal sides between the 3 measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS v. 20 for Windows (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro- Wilk tests were applied to assess the normality of the data distribution. To compare two groups, an independent t-test was conducted, while comparisons among three subgroups were performed using ANOVA, followed by the Bonferroni post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. All p values were two-tailed, and values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Histological analysis

In Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) stained samples of the control group, histological examination revealed a wide area of compact bone composed of numerous Haversian systems. At day zero, subgroup IA exhibited microcracks and thermal osteonecrosis, resulting in an area with few empty lacunae around the drilling site (Figure 2 2a-c). In contrast, subgroup IB showed remnants of bone chips enclosing osteocytes, which were compressed into the trabecular bone spaces adjacent to the osteotomy wall (Figure 2 2d-f ). After two months, subgroup IIA revealed areas of granulation tissue and bone spicules in the cervical portion of the bone-implant interface (Figure 3 3a). In the middle portion of the specimen, newly formed osteons were observed within the inter-thread bone (Figure 3 3b,c). In subgroup IIB, all bone chips along the entire implant surface had completely disappeared, indicating bone remodeling. In the cervical and middle portions of the specimens, newly formed osteons were present in close proximity to the implant surface. Additionally, ossification centers were evident in the apical portion, near the implant surface (Figure 3 3d-f ).

In Masson’s Trichrome-stained specimens, the control group, Group I, and Group II exhibited mature osteons stained red, with small immature areas stained blue. In subgroup IIA, woven bone areas were observed, along with a rim of mature bone stained red in the inter-thread area. In contrast, subgroup IIB displayed woven bone areas, with newly formed osteons clearly visible in proximity to the implant surface.

Histomorphometric analysis

In the statistical analysis of the area percentage of newly formed bone in Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) stained sections, a significantly higher mean value was recorded in subgroup IIB (22.26±2.58%) compared to subgroup IIA (11.11±0.82%). The difference between the two subgroups was statistically significant (p<0.001). Similarly, in the statistical analysis of the area percentage of newly formed bone in Masson’s Trichrome-stained sections, a significantly higher mean value was observed in subgroup IIB (14.55±1.13%) compared to subgroup IIA (10.70±1.07%). This difference was also statistically significant (p<0.001).

Radiographic bone density

In the statistical analysis of bone density using CBCT for the control group, subgroup IA, and subgroup IB, the buccal bone density in the control group was 1123±30.36, compared to 1026.75±15.71 in subgroup IA and 1164.75±143.57 in subgroup IB. However, the difference between the groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.066) (Table 1 1a, b). For the palatal bone, the mean density in the control group was 1089.75±85.89, compared to 1070.25±168.43 in subgroup IA and 1187±53.32 in subgroup IB. The difference between these groups was also not statistically significant (p= 0.258) (Table 2). In the statistical analysis of bone density using CBCT for subgroup IIA and subgroup IIB, a significantly higher mean value was recorded in the buccal bone of subgroup IIB (1678.75±73.37) compared to subgroup IIA (919.5±220.84). The difference between the two groups was 759.25±104.07, which was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Similarly, in the palatal bone, a significantly higher mean value was observed in subgroup IIB (1785±213.68) compared to subgroup IIA (1186.5±71.32). The difference between the two groups was 598.5±100.74, which was also statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

Dogs are commonly used as experimental animals in various fields of dentistry due to their similarities to humans in periodontal tissues, saliva, and bone composition. Moreover, canine jaws are widely applied in dental implant studies (19, 20). Accordingly, dogs were utilized in the present study. The premolar teeth of dogs were chosen for their easier accessibility during the surgical procedure, as well as for improved management and manipulation during surgery.

Low-density bone, such as that found in the maxillary posterior region, may hinder bone-implant contact (BIC) and delay osseointegration. The drilling technique and drill design significantly impact osseointegration, offering advantages in overcoming the challenges of implant insertion in low-density bone, as commonly observed in the maxillary posterior area (12). In this study, a split-mouth technique was employed to compare the potential of osseodensifying Densah drills and conventional drills in promoting bone regeneration following immediate implant insertion.

The histological findings in subgroup IA of the present study revealed microcracks and thermal osteonecrosis, resulting in areas with a few empty lacunae around the drilling site. These findings were consistent with those of Agarwal et al. (21), who reported that conventional bone drilling induces microcracks on the drilled bone surface due to the exertion of excessive pressure during the procedure. These microcracks may compromise implant stability and reduce pullout strength. Additionally, they found that conventional bone drilling generates severe thermal osteonecrosis due to the high temperatures produced at the drilling site, leading to osteocyte death within their lacunae.

The histological analysis of group IB revealed the presence of bone chips enclosing osteocytes, which were compressed into the trabecular bone spaces adjacent to the osteotomy wall. In contrast, bone chips were absent in the traditional drilling group. These findings corroborate the results of Lahens et al. (12), who reported that bone debris was found in the osteotomy walls of osseodensification drilling groups but was rarely observed in the regular subtractive drilling group. They concluded that these bone chips contributed to significantly higher BIC compared to regular drilling techniques.

The unique design of Densah drills and their counterclockwise (CCW) rotation provide several clinical advantages. The presence of regular twists with four or more lands ensures accurate guidance of the drills through the osteotomy while reducing the possibility of chatter. The CCW rotation allows the drill to create a cylindrical osteotomy without excavating healthy bone, thereby preserving bone fragments and condensing the osteotomy walls atraumatically. This process minimizes the formation of microfractures (22).

Bone surgeries in dogs typically require 2 to 3 months for complete healing. Therefore, a two-month healing period was selected for the implant groups to evaluate bone quality and the rate of bone regeneration during the healing process (23). The histological findings in subgroup IIA revealed areas of soft tissue in close proximity to the implant, particularly in the cervical region. This finding is attributed to the accumulation of small cracks and thermal trauma induced by the frictional contact between the conventional surgical drill and the osteotomy wall. These factors can lead to osteocyte apoptosis, increased osteoclastic bone resorption, bone necrosis, and ultimately delayed healing (24, 25). In contrast, the histological findings in group IIB showed areas of newly formed bone along the entire implant surface, with newly developed osteons evident near the implant surface and within the inter-thread areas. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections of subgroup IIB revealed a significantly higher bone area percentage compared to subgroup IIA. These findings align with the results of Tian et al. (26), which suggest that the presence of compacted autologous bone fragments along the osteotomy walls in the osseodensification (OD) technique contributes to improved bone regeneration. Moreover, these compacted bone chips help maintain bone mass, preserve existing collagen, and exhibit osteoconductive potential, thereby stimulating new bone development and accelerating the osseointegration process (27). Furthermore, Witek et al. (28) reported that the osseointegration process and bone area fraction occupancy (BAFO%) were more pronounced in OD-counterclockwise (OD-CCW) specimens around highly porous trabecular metal implants in low-density bone. They attributed these findings to the role of autologous compacted bone chips as nucleating sites for bone formation. Although these bone chips enclose osteocytes and possess osteoconductive properties, osteocytes also release biochemical factors such as adenosine triphosphate, nitric oxide, and prostaglandin E2, which further activate osteogenesis (29).

Additionally, the OD technique has been reported to enhance bone-implant contact (BIC) due to the “spring-back effect” of compacted autologous bone fragments. This effect generates a compressive force along the implant surface, increasing the amount of bone covering the implant and improving peri-implant bone density. Consequently, this mechanical interlock between the implant and bone enhances primary stability and stimulates osteogenesis through a mechano-biological healing process (30). Moreover, these compacted bone fragments act as a scaffold for new bone formation, releasing bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), which promote osteoblast differentiation, bone matrix protein synthesis, and play a crucial role in osteogenesis and bone remodeling (31, 32).

In the present study, the histological findings of group IIB revealed an extensive network of large blood vessels near the implant surface. This observation is consistent with the findings of Sultana et al. (27), who attributed it to the presence of collagen-containing compacted bone chips, which promote vascularization and accelerate angiogenesis—both essential processes for new bone formation and remodeling. Additionally, Tabassum et al. (33) reported that autologous bone fragments express and release significant amounts of growth factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and BMP-2, which stimulate angiogenesis and play a crucial role in bone formation and mineralization. Conversely, Mello-Machado et al. (34) found no statistically significant difference between OD and conventional techniques regarding neovascularization.

Masson’s trichrome analysis of subgroup IIB confirmed the presence of mineralized, mature newly formed bone stained red, indicating the presence of collagen type I. In contrast, the blue staining observed in subgroup IIA represented freshly produced immature bone containing collagen type III. These findings are attributed to the autologous bone fragments generated by the OD technique, which release significant amounts of growth factors and osteopontin. These factors stimulate angiogenesis and are critical for bone formation and maturation, ultimately leading to faster osseointegration along the entire implant surface. This, in turn, enhances the long-term stability of dental implants in areas of poor bone quality and reduces the time required for loading after surgery (33).

In the present study, the bone density of the osteotomy walls in subgroup IB was higher than in subgroup IA. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups. This result is likely due to the formation of a condensed bone crust created by Densah drills, which led to increased bone mineral density in the OD group compared to the conventional drilling group (35).

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) results indicated that bone density around dental implants was significantly higher in subgroup IIB compared to subgroup IIA. This can be attributed to the osseodensification technique, which increases bone density around implants as osteotomies expand, preserves bone by avoiding subtraction, and compacts autografted bone fragments containing bone matrix, cells, and biochemicals. These elements self-graft along the implant bed and at the osteotomy apex, thereby enhancing mechanical stability and accelerating the healing process (35, 36).

Based on these findings, we recommend conducting longer follow-up periods and incorporating advanced imaging techniques, such as micro-CT, as well as evaluating osteogenic gene marker expression to confirm the study’s results. Further investigations and additional in vivo trials are necessary to examine the potential impact of compressed bone fragments on the vascular supply of the osteotomy wall.

Conclusion

The osseodensification (OD) technique minimizes bone loss and preserves the bone chips generated during the drilling process, a factor that is unavoidable with conventional techniques. Evidence suggests that the OD technique increases bone area percentage and enhances bone maturation around dental implants. Additionally, OD is highly effective in improving bone mineral density in areas of poor bone quality, facilitating faster osseointegration, and enhancing overall bone quality around dental implants. These improvements are strong indicators of long-term clinical success.
