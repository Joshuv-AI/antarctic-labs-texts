Disparity trends in coexisting renal failure- and pulmonary hypertension-related mortality in the United States (1999–2020): CDC WONDER database study
Authors: Allahdad Khan, Zahid Ullah, Maheen Sheraz, Huzaifa Noor, Syed Ibad Hussain, Umama Alam, Eaman Fatima, Mohammed Saifuddin, Farzana Kousar, Mohamed Antar, Yasar Sattar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renal failure (RF) and pulmonary hypertension (PH) are major contributors to mortality in the United States (U.S.). Globally, RF affects around 11–13% of the population and is the 10th leading cause of death. In the U.S., 15% of adults are estimated to have CKD, with almost 90% undiagnosed. PH affects about 1% of the global population, rising significantly in older adults. RF and PH frequently coexist, especially in end-stage renal disease, amplifying mortality risk. We analyzed annual death certificate data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (1999–2020), identifying deaths with the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems – 10th Revision codes N17–N19 (RF) and I27.x (PH). We calculated crude and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100 000 and assessed temporal trends using Joinpoint regression. Analyses were stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, geographic region, and urban–rural status. Between 1999 and 2020, 63 394 deaths were attributed to combined RF–PH. Overall, AAMR rose from 7.17 to 18.11 per 100 000, with two joinpoints: a sharp rise until ~2012 [annual percentage change (APC) ~+5.8%], a dip through ~2015 (–6.7%), then a rebound (APC ~+5.9%). Increases were observed across sexes, races, regions, and urbanization, but were most pronounced among Black individuals (AAMR 14.07 → 31.29), rural populations, and Western states. From 1999 to 2020, RF- and PH-related mortality in the U.S. nearly tripled, with persistent disparities by race, geography, and urbanization. These findings highlight the need for targeted screening and interventions, especially in high-burden communities, to mitigate risk in vulnerable groups.

Introduction

Renal failure (RF) and pulmonary hypertension (PH) are among the leading causes of mortality worldwide and in the United States (U.S.). Chronic kidney disease (CKD) accounts for 10–13% of the cases worldwide[1]. CKD affects 10–13% of the global population, with 850 million diagnosed cases, and is the 10th leading cause of death globally[2,3]. In the U.S., 15% of the population has CKD, though up to 90% of cases remain undiagnosed[4]. Major factors that have raised RF mortality in the U.S. are the increasing prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and an aging population.

PH has a lower prevalence globally, affecting ~1% of the population, with higher rates among adults over 65, mainly due to left-sided heart and lung diseases[5]. Obesity is a significant health issue in the U.S.; it is associated with risks of heart failure and obstructive sleep apnea, both of which lead to an increase in PH. The following study shows the increase in the PH population, as COVID-19 was highly impacted by heart failures[6]. PH has a high mortality rate compared to the number of diagnosed cases, with higher chances of mortality[7]. The increasing trend in PH mortality in the U.S. is mainly due to the aging population, comorbidity, and differences in healthcare services[8]. PH is a common complication in patients with CKD, with prevalence ranging from 20% to 50% in those with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), particularly among individuals on hemodialysis, representing the burden of cardiovascular and volume-related stressors in this population[9]. However, despite the known burden of each condition, few studies have concurrently examined long-term demographic and regional mortality patterns for RF and PH in the U.S.

Understanding the demographic and regional distribution of RF- and PH-related mortality can help identify populations at greatest risk and guide targeted interventions. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate demographic and regional differences in RF- and PH-related mortality in the U.S. from 1999 to 2020.

Methods

Study setting

In this database study, death certificate data were retrieved from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database–and examined from 1999 to 2020 for PH among the population with RF. The International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems – 10th Revision (ICD-10) was used as follows: N17–N19, I27.0, I27.2, I27.8, and I27.9.

In this descriptive study, we used the data provided by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which was made available through the CDC WONDER database, to analyze annual mortality trends from 1999 to 2020. Data from death certificates of U.S. residents are annually updated on CDC WONDER. We used the final Multiple Cause of Death Public Use record and ICD-10 codes N17–N19 for RF[10] and I27.0 for primary PH; I27.2 for secondary PH; I27.8 for other specified pulmonary heart diseases; and I27.9 for pulmonary heart disease, unspecified[11].

We identified death certificates listing these conditions as underlying or contributing causes of death. An institutional review board was not required for this study, as it used a de-identified, government-issued public use data set. Moreover, this research strictly adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and TITAN guidelines[12,13].

Data extraction

The dataset was thoroughly analyzed for a wide range of demographic variables, including sex, race/ethnicity, age groups, regions, states, place of death, and urban–rural classification. Race/ethnicity groups were delineated as non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, NH Asian or Pacific Islander, NH American Indian or Alaska Native, and Hispanic or Latino. For age stratification, age was divided into the following categories: 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85+ years. The NCHS Urban–Rural Classification Scheme was used to analyze the population by urban [large metropolitan area (population ≥1 million), medium/small metropolitan area (population 50 000–999 999)] and rural (population <50 000) counties, per the 2013 U.S. Census classification. Regions were classified according to the U.S. Census Bureau definitions into Northeast, Midwest, South, and West[14]. Place of death included medical facilities (outpatient, emergency room, inpatient, death on arrival, or status unknown), home, hospice, and nursing home/long-term care facility.

Statistical analysis

Crude mortality rates and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100 000 related to cerebrovascular disease in diabetic patients were obtained. To ensure data comparability, AAMR accounts for the population’s variability in age distribution and was calculated using the direct adjustment strategy based on the 2000 standard population. In order to quantify the national annual trends, the Joinpoint Regression Program (Joinpoint V 4.9.0.0, National Cancer Institute) was employed to analyze the AAMR from 1999 to 2020[15,16]. This technique fits log-linear regression models when temporal variation occurs in order to identify significant changes in AAMR across time. Using two-tailed t-testing, annual percentage changes (APCs) were deemed rising or falling if the slope characterizing the change in mortality differed substantially from zero. P < 0.05 was deemed to be statistically significant.

Results

From 1999 to 2020, a total of 63 394 deaths were reported due to comorbid PH–RF. When stratified by place of death, the majority of these deaths occurred in medical facilities (40 124), followed by the descendants’ homes (10 765), hospices (2930), nursing homes or long-term care facilities (7730), other places (1699), and unknown locations (146) (Supplemental Digital Content Table 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B268, Supplemental Digital Content Central Illustration, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B269).

Overall and gender-stratified trends

The overall AAMR for PH-RF during the study period was 13.3403 per 100 000 (95% CI: 13.236–13.4446). AAMR increased significantly from 7.1653 in 1999 to 17.7544 in 2012 (APC: 5.8009*; CI: 2.7568–8.1315), declined to 14.3456 in 2015 (APC: −6.6614; CI: −10.444–8.8921), and then rose again to 18.1108 in 2020 (APC: 5.8725*; CI: 1.1102–16.3371) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Digital Content Tables 2 and 3, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B268). Race-stratified trends

Among racial and ethnic groups, Black or African American individuals experienced the highest increase in AAMR (14.0731; CI: 12.197–15.9492 to 31.2939; CI: 29.1004–33.4875; APC: 2.9921*; CI: 2.1776–3.9995). Hispanic or Latino individuals also demonstrated a significant increase (4.4198; CI: 3.1277–6.0665 to 11.6406; CI: 10.2912–12.99; APC: 3.51*; CI: 2.3402–5.2912). Among White individuals, AAMR increased from 6.5595 in 1999 to 16.949 in 2011 (APC: 6.4627*; CI: 3.1901–9.6066), declined through 2014, and rose again to 17.404 in 2020 (APC: 4.5768*; CI: 1.1574–13.5136) (Fig. 3, Supplemental Digital Content Tables 2 and 5, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B268). Census region

Among Census regions, the West showed a higher increase in AAMR (7.7719; CI: 6.8502–8.6936 vs. 22.1753; CI: 20.9673–23.3833), trailed by the Midwest (8.5865; CI: 7.7016–9.4715 vs. 20.3809; CI: 19.1952–21.5666), the South (5.969; CI: 5.3635–6.5744 vs. 16.3496; CI: 15.5502–17.1491), and the Northeast (6.8345; CI: 5.9934–7.6755 vs. 14.0369; CI: 12.9807–15.0931).

In the Northeast region, AAMR increased from 6.8345 in 1999 to 14.9788 in 2012 (APC: 4.6441*; CI: 2.3173, 6.8032), followed by a decrease to 11.2664 in 2016 (APC: −6.6523; CI: −12.9449, 7.0797). Finally, it increased to 14.0369 in 2020 (APC: 6.3071; CI: −0.6147, 18.865).

In the Midwest region, mortality rates demonstrated an increase from 8.5865 in 1999 to 20.2299 in 2012 (APC: 5.3473*; CI: 3.8572, 7.5784) and declined to 15.8511 in 2015 (APC: −7.62; CI: −11.6489, 4.0533). The rates finally increased to 20.3809 in 2020 (APC: 6.0337*; CI: 1.376, 16.6877).

In the South region, mortality increased from 5.969 in 1999 to 16.657 in 2012 (APC: 6.0987*; CI: 3.9965, 8.2168), followed by a decline to 13.7126 in 2015 (APC: −6.2555; CI: −9.6416, 7.6626). The rates finally increased to 16.3496 in 2020 (APC: 5.0998*; CI: 1.0922, 13.7316).

In the West region, AAMR showed a significant incline from 7.7719 in 1999 to 19.1343 in 2011 (APC: 7.5655*; CI: 5.7421, 11.2584), then dropped to 13.2707 in 2014 (APC: −5.1874; CI: −8.8224, 5.4444), and later on, the rates increased to 22.1753 in 2020 (APC: 6.6589*; CI: 3.5115, 14.4543) (Fig. 4, Supplemental Digital Content Tables 2 and 6, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B268). Discussion

This national analysis of CDC WONDER data from 1999 to 2020 demonstrates a substantial rise in mortality attributable to PH coexisting with RF. A total of 63 394 deaths were recorded, with most occurring in medical facilities (63.3%). The AAMR increased 2.5-fold, from 7.17 per 100 000 in 1999 to 18.11 in 2020. Trend analysis identified three phases: a sharp rise from 1999 to 2012 (APC: +5.80%), a temporary decline from 2012 to 2015 (APC: −6.66%), and a renewed increase from 2015 to 2020 (APC: +5.87%). These patterns likely reflect evolving disease recognition, treatment practices, and a growing burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities.

Pathophysiological links between PH and RF

PH associated with RF is classified as Group 5 PH, reflecting multifactorial mechanisms[17]. Key drivers include volume overload in CKD and ESRD, leading to elevated left atrial pressures and post-capillary PH[18]. Hemodialysis arteriovenous fistulas can increase cardiac output and pulmonary shear stress, promoting vascular remodeling[19]. Uremia-related endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress reduce nitric oxide bioavailability while increasing vasoconstrictors such as endothelin-1[18,19]. Additional contributors include anemia, sleep-disordered breathing, left ventricular dysfunction, vascular calcification, and arterial stiffness related to mineral metabolism abnormalities, all of which increase pulmonary pressures and right ventricular afterload20–22].

Sex-based disparities

Sex-stratified analysis revealed a steeper and more volatile increase in AAMR among women, rising from 6.73 to 18.30, compared with men, who increased from 7.97 to 17.88. Biological factors, including estrogen-mediated effects on pulmonary vasculature and right ventricular function, may partially explain these differences[23,24]. Women often demonstrate better right ventricular to pulmonary arterial coupling[25], potentially delaying overt failure but possibly leading to worse outcomes once compensatory mechanisms are overwhelmed by renal dysfunction. Systemic and behavioral factors may also contribute. Although women tend to seek care more frequently[26], they often face diagnostic delays, reduced access to advanced therapies, and lower transplantation rates in CKD, compounded by a higher psychosocial burden[27].

Racial and ethnic disparities

Marked racial disparities were observed, with Black individuals experiencing the highest and fastest-rising mortality, with AAMR increasing from 14.07 to 31.29 and an APC of +2.99%, compared with White and Hispanic populations. This disproportionate burden reflects the higher prevalence and severity of hypertension and diabetes among Black Americans[28], conditions that accelerate both CKD and PH progression. Socioeconomic barriers, limited access to specialty care, and delays in diagnosis further exacerbate outcomes[29]. Structural racism and systemic bias contribute to less aggressive treatment, poorer communication, and reduced access to transplantation and specialized cardiopulmonary care[30]. Environmental exposures, such as air pollution, also disproportionately affect Black communities and may worsen cardiopulmonary disease[31]. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating persistent racial disparities in cardiovascular and renal outcomes[32,33].

Geographic and regional variation

Significant regional differences were identified, with the West exhibiting the highest AAMRs by 2020. States such as Colorado, Vermont, and Wyoming reported the highest rates, potentially related to high altitude, chronic hypoxia, wildfire-related air pollution, and limited specialist access[34,35]. Rural and frontier regions face persistent shortages of nephrologists and pulmonologists, contributing to delayed diagnosis and treatment[36]. All Census regions showed a similar temporal pattern of early increase, mid-period decline, and post-2015 resurgence, likely reflecting initial improvements in disease recognition and therapy, followed by rising comorbidity burden and healthcare access challenges[37–39].

Urban and rural disparities

Rural areas consistently experienced higher mortality than urban areas, with AAMR rising to 20.07 by 2020 compared with 17.74 in urban settings. These disparities stem from healthcare workforce shortages, hospital closures, transportation barriers, and a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions in rural populations[36,40]. Socioeconomic disadvantages, lower health literacy, and cultural hesitancy toward care further contribute to late-stage presentation and poorer outcomes[41,42]. The temporary decline in rural mortality from 2012 to 2015 may reflect early impacts of Affordable Care Act–related coverage expansion, but subsequent increases suggest these gains were not sustained amid ongoing infrastructure challenges[43]. Urban populations benefit from higher physician density, greater availability of tertiary care centers, improved transportation options, and broader insurance coverage. Urban health systems are also more likely to implement coordinated chronic disease management programs, which have been shown to improve outcomes in complex conditions such as PH and CKD[42,44].

Implications and future directions

The rising and unequal mortality burden of PH with RF underscores the need for targeted interventions. Future research should prioritize resource-limited and high-risk populations, emphasizing cost-effective screening strategies, community-based care models, telemedicine, and improved access to specialty services. The implementation of evidence-based guidelines in underserved settings is essential to reduce disparities and improve outcomes for this complex and high-risk population.

Limitations

This study, based on CDC mortality data, has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The data rely on the accuracy of death certificates, which may underreport or misclassify PH and RF, especially when they are not listed as the primary cause of death. The dataset lacks clinical details such as disease severity, treatment history, and comorbidities, which limits the ability to fully understand patient outcomes. Additionally, the observational nature of the data prevents causal inferences; associations observed between PH, RF, and mortality cannot establish direct cause-and-effect relationships. Geographic misclassification is possible if the place of death does not reflect the patient’s usual residence or access to care. Confounding by comorbidities such as diabetes, obesity, or heart failure could not be accounted for; while such analyses are possible, they are beyond the scope of the current study and should be addressed in future research. Furthermore, the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10 in 2015 may have introduced inconsistencies in how conditions were coded over time. The dataset also lacks socioeconomic and environmental variables such as income, education, pollution exposure, and insurance status, which are key determinants of health disparities. Lastly, it does not provide information on healthcare utilization, such as access to specialty care or frequency of medical visits, which limits insight into how system-level factors influence mortality trends.

Conclusion

Between 1999 and 2020, mortality from PH associated with RF nearly tripled in the U.S., with persistent disparities across sex, race, geography, and urbanization. Women, Black individuals, rural residents, and those in resource-limited states experienced the highest mortality rates. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions to address high-risk populations and reduce inequities.
