Ergotamine, triptans, and the global challenge of medication-overuse headache: a call to action from Asia–Oceania
Authors: Tissa Wijeratne, Paolo Martelletti
Published: 2025

Introduction

Medication-overuse headache (MOH) is one of the most revealing paradoxes in neurology: medicines designed to relieve pain can, when misused, become the very cause of chronic suffering [1–4]. MOH is now recognised as a leading cause of secondary chronic daily headache worldwide, compounding the burden of primary disorders such as migraine [5–10]. It is common, disabling, costly to health systems, and crucially entirely preventable. Yet, despite this, it remains a neglected global problem [11–14].

Patterns of MOH vary with geography and health system context. In high-income countries, triptans are now the most frequent agents associated with overuse. By contrast, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), ergotamine is still widely prescribed, and in some places sold over the counter. This divergence tells a broader story about inequities in access to medicines, regulation, and education. It also underscores the structural vulnerability of health systems in resource-limited settings, where patients may face few alternatives to older, riskier drugs.

At the same time, there is a largely unrecognised dimension of MOH in LMICs: the unreported harm from excessive use of paracetamol, ibuprofen, and other simple analgesics. These drugs are inexpensive, widely available without prescription, and often used daily for headache relief in communities with limited access to specialist care. Chronic overuse not only fuels MOH prevalence but also contributes to additional complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding, renal impairment, and liver injury. In many parts of Asia–Oceania, the burden of MOH arising from analgesic overuse may be enormous but invisible, rarely captured in registries or national statistics. The Asia–Oceania region shows this paradox clearly. It is home to nearly half the world’s population. Countries like Japan, Singapore, and Australia have advanced neurological services. In these places, ergotamine use is rare, though it still appears from time to time. Australia has also successfully eliminated over-the-counter codeine-containing medications.

By contrast, fragile systems in South and Southeast Asia still face widespread ergotamine and analgesic overuse. Clinicians in Sri Lanka, Indonesia, the Philippines, and India continue to see entrenched ergotamine dependence. They also report high rates of self-medication with over-the-counter painkillers. The Asian Regional Consortium on Headache (ARCH) has repeatedly highlighted this as a major regional priority.

Taken together, these patterns show that MOH is not simply a pharmacological problem but a systemic one, reflecting gaps in education, regulation, and access to safer alternatives.

In this context, the Northern Thai Headache Registry represents a uniquely valuable resource. Carefully maintained, prospectively designed, and embedded in real-world practice, it offers rare comparative data on ergotamine- and triptan-induced MOH in an LMIC setting. Its findings not only confirm what many clinicians across Asia–Oceania have long suspected that ergotamine withdrawal is more severe and relapse more common but also provide evidence strong enough to shape policy and practice far beyond Thailand’s borders.

This editorial builds on the registry’s insights, situating them within the global brain health movement and regional advocacy efforts. We argue that Asia–Oceania, through networks such as ARCH, has both the responsibility and the opportunity to lead the way in phasing out ergotamine, reducing analgesic overuse, scaling access to safer therapies, and ultimately preventing MOH. What emerges is not only a regional challenge but a global story of preventable disability, structural inequity, and the chance to eliminate MOH once and for all.

MOH: a preventable burden

Medication-overuse headache (MOH) should no longer exist. It is one of the few neurological conditions that is almost entirely preventable, yet it continues to burden tens of millions of people worldwide. Epidemiological studies estimate that 1–2% of the global population live with MOH at any moment. Most are in their most productive years, losing quality of life, income, and opportunity.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study consistently ranks migraine as the second leading cause of years lived with disability globally, and the leading cause in people under 50. MOH is harder to capture in population surveys, but it contributes heavily to this ranking by converting episodic migraine into chronic daily suffering. This makes MOH both a magnifier of disability and a marker of health system failure.

The situation is not confined to low- and middle-income countries. MOH is also common in high-income countries, including Australia. Our own work, published in BMJ Open, documented the extent of MOH in the Australian setting, showing that even in a well-resourced health system, patients remain vulnerable when preventive care, education, and access to specialist services are lacking [15]. This evidence underscores a crucial truth: MOH is not simply a problem of poverty or weak systems it is a universal challenge born from fragmented care and misplaced prescribing practices.

In Asia–Oceania, the burden is magnified. Many LMICs lack headache specialists, preventive therapies, and rehabilitation systems. Over-the-counter sales of analgesics and ergotamine remain common. These gaps create ideal conditions for MOH to thrive, often trapping patients in cycles of overuse and relapse.

The tragedy is that MOH is not inevitable. It does not arise from genes or fate. It is a man-made disorder, driven by behaviour, prescribing patterns, and fragile health systems. And for that reason, it is one we can and must eliminate.

MOH across drug classes: lessons from Thailand

The Northern Thai Headache Registry provides compelling evidence of the harm caused by medication overuse. Patients with ergotamine-induced MOH experienced more severe withdrawal, slower recovery, and higher relapse rates compared to those with triptan-induced MOH. Dual overuse of both drugs carried the worst outcomes.

Yet the issue is much broader. Across Asia–Oceania, MOH is driven not only by ergotamine and triptans, but also by paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids, and combination analgesics. These drugs, often taken daily without supervision, can entrench dependence and transform episodic migraine into chronic disability. Over-the-counter access makes the problem even more pervasive, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

The pharmacology explains part of the story. Ergotamine’s long half-life and receptor profile make withdrawal punishing. Triptans, while less severe, still carry risk when overused. NSAIDs and paracetamol seem benign but, when consumed chronically, can lock patients into cycles of daily headache. Opioids, though less common in some LMICs, add further complexity with high risk of dependence and stigma.

For clinicians, the lesson is clear: MOH is not confined to one drug it is the end result of weak systems, poor education, and limited access to preventive care. Every case demands structured withdrawal, support, and long-term strategies to prevent relapse.

For policymakers, the challenge is urgent: why do unsafe practices and unregulated access persist, when safer and more sustainable approaches exist?

ARCH colleagues from Sri Lanka, Nepal, Myanmar, and the Philippines report the same patterns: patients trapped in cycles of daily analgesic or ergotamine use, families struggling with repeated relapses, and non-specialist prescribers unaware of the risks. Thailand’s registry highlights what the region already knows MOH of any cause is preventable, predictable, and unacceptable in 2025.

The response must be bold. The goal is not reduction. The goal must be elimination.

Global inequities and the persistence of MOH

Thailand’s registry has shown what many in Asia–Oceania already witness daily: MOH is not a minor complication but a destructive, recurring disorder. Ergotamine overuse is one example, but the harm goes far beyond it. Across the region, patients develop MOH from triptans, paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids, and combination analgesics. These are drugs intended to heal, yet in the absence of prevention, education, and regulation, they become part of the problem.

In high-income countries, safer prescribing practices, tighter regulation, and access to specialist care have reduced the prevalence of ergotamine- and opioid-related MOH. Yet even here, MOH persists, often driven by triptan or simple analgesic overuse. In low- and middle-income countries, the problem is magnified. Cheap over-the-counter painkillers, weak regulation, and a lack of affordable preventives create a perfect storm for MOH to thrive.

This is more than a pharmacological inequity. It is a health rights issue. A patient with migraine in Melbourne may receive a triptan, preventive therapies, and structured follow-up. A patient in Colombo, Kathmandu, or Manila may self-medicate with daily paracetamol or ibuprofen until both their brain health and their liver or kidneys are damaged. Such inequity is unacceptable in 2025.

Clinical and policy implications

The evidence is clear: MOH is preventable, reversible, and eliminable. Yet it continues to burden millions because of gaps in health systems, policy, and education. The Thai registry reinforces what we already know withdrawal is hard, recovery is slow, and relapse is common but the deeper lesson is that we must never allow patients to fall into MOH in the first place.

For clinicians, this means identifying overuse early and educating patients about the risks of regular medication. For ministries of health, it means regulating unsafe drugs, restricting over-the-counter sales, and embedding MOH prevention into national non-communicable disease frameworks. For global agencies, it means ensuring access to affordable triptans, preventives, and non-drug therapies. Restriction without provision will only worsen suffering.

Regional leadership is essential. ARCH can convene networks, harmonise guidelines, and advocate collectively. But this is not just about ergotamine. It is about changing systems so that no drug however common can push patients into MOH unchecked.

A notable example of education tailored for resource-limited settings is the free online course Headaches in LMICs, developed by Unitelma Sapienza University, with contributions from several international experts, including members of ARCH (link here)*. This course has been very well received across the Global South, providing accessible, evidence-based training on headache disorders, their diagnosis, and management in low- and middle-income contexts. It illustrates how targeted educational initiatives can help close the knowledge and practice gaps that fuel medication-overuse headache (MOH), while empowering local practitioners to deliver safer and more effective care.

MOH as a barrier to brain health

The World Health Organization defines brain health as “the state of brain functioning across cognitive, sensory, social-emotional, behavioural and motor domains, allowing a person to realise their full potential over the life course.” By this definition, MOH is not simply a headache complication. It undermines brain health across every domain. It dulls cognition. It disrupts emotional stability. It reduces participation in family and community life.

This framing resonates strongly in Asia–Oceania. It aligns with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander definitions of health, which see well-being as collective, cultural, and social, not just physical. It also links with the global brain health movement, championed by the World Federation of Neurology through World Brain Day. Since 2014, these campaigns have reached more than a billion people, reminding the world that brain health is central to human health.

From data to advocacy: building a regional movement

Examples across the region show how this reframing can drive action. In Sri Lanka, brain health ambassadors trained at the Kotelawala Defence University now lead awareness campaigns in schools. In Melton, Australia, a city-wide program placed migraine and MOH prevention into school health policy and community services. In Victoria’s Parliament, the launch of the Brain Health Friendship Group brought MOH and migraine into political conversation with bipartisan support.

These experiences prove that MOH is not only a clinical problem but a social and political one. Eliminating MOH requires networks, advocacy, and community empowerment. Looking ahead: eliminating MOH

The future must be bold. Reducing MOH is not enough. Eliminating MOH should be the goal.

That means building national headache registries across LMICs to generate data. It means research on cost-effective withdrawal and prevention strategies. It means WHO regional offices, in partnership with ARCH, setting timelines to phase out unsafe practices and ensure equitable access to modern care [16]. Eliminating medication-overuse headache (MOH) is an achievable global target: by embedding prevention into health systems, aligning with IGAP and SDG 3, and embracing brain health as a unifying framework, we can dismantle inequities, reduce stigma, and deliver sustainable gains in health and productivity worldwide [17–19]. Placing headache and especially the elimination of medication-overuse headache at the heart of SDG 3 offers a low-cost, high-impact strategy to improve health, productivity, and equity for millions worldwide [20].It means embedding MOH prevention into universal health coverage and essential medicines lists. And it means confronting stigma, reframing MOH as a preventable brain health condition rather than a patient failing.

If we act now, MOH can be eliminated within a generation. The cost of inaction will be millions more trapped in cycles of pain, disability, and stigma. The cost of action is modest: prevention, education, and access to safe alternatives. The choice is ours.

Conclusion

The Northern Thai Headache Registry provides compelling evidence: ergotamine-MOH is linked to more severe withdrawal, dual overuse carries the greatest risk of recurrence, and recovery is slower for ergotamine users. These findings mirror what clinicians across Asia–Oceania have long observed and highlight an urgent truth MOH persists because of systemic inequities, not clinical inevitability.

In Bangkok, Colombo, or Manila, patients still endure ergotamine overuse, while those in Melbourne or Tokyo access somewhat safer, modern treatments. This disparity is both a regional inequity and a global injustice. Through the Asian Regional Consortium on Headache (ARCH), Asia–Oceania can lead by example, phasing out ergotamine, ensuring equitable access to safer options, and embedding MOH prevention into national health policy.

But eliminating MOH demands more than drug regulation. It requires embracing the broader brain health framework set out by the WHO in 2021 and aligning with the Intersectoral Global Action Plan (IGAP) on Epilepsy and Other Neurological Disorders and SDG3 (Good Health and Well-Being). Prevention must be holistic, tackling the social and commercial determinants of health alongside clinical care. World Brain Day campaigns since 2014 have shown what is possible: over a billion people reached with messages that reduce stigma, empower patients, and inspire change.

To succeed, communities must be at the centre. Pharmacies, primary care providers, schools, and local leaders are frontline partners in early education, safe prescribing, and stigma reduction. With their support, MOH prevention can move from hospitals into everyday life, empowering individuals to protect their brain health and communities to flourish.

MOH is preventable. It is low-cost to address, and the return on investment is enormous. If we act decisively, we can eliminate MOH once and for all not only in Asia–Oceania but globally. By embracing brain health, building strong partnerships, and placing prevention at the heart of policy, we can create a world where no one suffers unnecessarily from medication-overuse headache.
