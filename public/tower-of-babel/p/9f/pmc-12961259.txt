Recurrent Constellations of Embryonic Malformations (RCEM): Teratogenicity Linked to Transient Hypoxia and Hormone Pregnancy Tests Agrees With RCEM and Suggest a Reactive Oxygen Species Pathogenesis
Authors: Aaron P. Adam, Helen E. Ritchie, Margaret P. Adam, Bengt R. Danielsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNo consistent genetic etiology has been found for a group of six different conditions in humans with multiple malformations called “recurrent constellations of embryonic malformations” (RCEM). Recent studies indicate hypoxia/reoxygenation and generation Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) as an underlying mechanism for RCEM with the specific manifestations related to the timing, severity and duration of the ROS exposure.MethodsMedical literature was evaluated in relation to a hypoxia/ROS related mechanism for RCEM. Special attention was paid to investigate if the reported spectrum of multiple human malformations for Hormone Pregnancy Tests (HPTs), which have been associated with embryonic hypoxia due abnormal uterine contractions in early pregnancy, agrees with the RCEM spectrum.ResultsThe pattern of human multiple and single malformations associated with HPTs in 44 case reports with multiple defects, 86% (38/44) were consistent with RCEM spectrum, the most common was VACTERL. There was also a high consistency with regards to increases in single HPT malformations within the RCEM spectrum in a large case report study (> 225 cases) and in more than 25 human epidemiological studies evaluating HPT teratogenicity. The RCEM spectrum is also consistent with reported malformations when the embryo has been exposed to periods of transient embryonic hypoxia/ROS in animal studies and when exposed to drugs associated with embryonic hypoxia/ROS (e.g., cocaine) and in some genetic diseases with malformations (e.g., NAD+ deficiency) when ROS overwhelms the antioxidant defense.ConclusionsThis review shows that HPTs are associated with human teratogenicity and ROS to be the proximate teratogen underlying RCEM.

Introduction

The genome era has seen a remarkable increase in the understanding of the etiologies of human malformations. However, despite extensive investigations using modern genetic testing techniques, no consistent genetic etiology has been found for a group of six different conditions with multiple malformations called “recurrent constellations of embryonic malformations” (RCEM) (Table 1) (Adam et al. 2020). These conditions occur in both singleton (majority of cases) and twin births. The study focused specifically on twin pairs (Adam et al. 2020), results indicated a primarily non‐genetic etiology for RCEM, including an excess of discordant monozygotic twins compared to dizygotic twins. Supporting data for an embryonic disruption includes only one twin with an RCEM or the twins have different RCEMs, minimal to no familial recurrence risk, lack of a known recurrent genetic cause, and instances of co‐occurrence of two overlapping RCEM phenotypes in one individual (Adam et al. 2020). On the basis of the overlap of the spectrum of malformations in different RCEM entities and the absence of strict diagnostic criteria for each of these constellation conditions, several investigators have concluded that it is not possible to continue to view these conditions as single, unrelated entities (Adam et al. 2020; Barrios et al. 2024; Mark 2022; Park et al. 2021; Stevenson 2021). Rather, they should be viewed as developmental disorders that constitute a spectrum, which is likely to be causally related by a common pathogenesis and could be studied as a group.

The underlying mechanism of the multiple defects in the RCEM spectrum is not established but several studies suggest a relation to decreased vascular perfusion and embryonic hypoxia, with the specific manifestations related to the timing and duration of the disturbance in embryonic development (Adam et al. 2020; Park et al. 2021; Stevenson 2021). Autopsy results suggest unequal blood supply and oxygen distribution in monozygotic twins, which share a single placenta (in contrast to dizygotic twins and singletons) to be a contributing factor to explain only one twin with a RCEM (Stevenson 2021). The shared placenta may also play a role in the higher rate of RCEM in monozygotic compared to dizygotic twins. A large number of animal studies since the 1950s show that transient embryonic hypoxia (Ingalls and Curley 1957) or decreased perfusion are teratogenic. A wide spectrum of both single and multiple birth defects can be induced dependent on the intensity, duration and stage of embryonic development for the hypoxic event, with many defects being preceded by vascular disruption as summarized in review articles (Danielsson et al. 2023; Webster and Abela 2007). Stage specific human malformations resembling those induced by hypoxia in animals suggest the established human teratogens misoprostol (Vargas et al. 2000), cocaine (Woods et al. 1998), phenytoin (Danielson et al. 1992; Danielsson et al. 2005) and the inherited condition homozygous alpha‐thalassemia (Adam et al. 2021, 2005) could also be induced by transient hypoxia.

Mechanistic studies also indicate that the teratogenicity induced by transient hypoxia is related to an excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), overwhelming the antioxidant defense, particularly when oxygen returns (re‐oxygenation) to hypoxic embryonic tissues (Fantel 1996; Woods et al. 1998). Recently formed vessels in the embryo are most susceptible to ROS damage since they are less protected as they consist of only a single layer of endothelial cells and are directly exposed to ROS generated by fluctuations in the oxygen/blood supply (Danielsson et al. 2023). Excessive ROS generation in the endothelial cells results in vascular disruption and the abnormal development of tissues that should have been supplied by the now‐damaged vessel(s). The embryonic heart is also susceptible to ROS and responds with irregular heart rhythm and periods of cardiac arrest, which further aggravate the embryonic hypoxia. Cardiac arrhythmia itself results in alterations in blood pressure and blood flow in cardiogenesis. Such alterations can induce a wide pattern of cardiac malformations (Danielsson et al. 2023; Trinidad et al. 2022).

In support of the hypoxia and ROS mechanism, pretreatment with a potent ROS capturing agent PBN (alpha‐phenyl‐T‐n‐butylnitron) prevented the teratogenic effects of hypoxia induced by embryonic cardiac arrhythmia in rats, including cardiovascular, visceral, and skeletal malformations, among others (Sköld et al. 2001; Wellfelt et al. 1999). Rodent models of the human teratogens phenytoin and cocaine support the hypoxia‐ROS mechanism, as co‐treatment with PBN was effective in markedly decreasing the teratogenic effects (Liu and Wells 1994; Wells et al. 1989; Zimmerman et al. 1994). Also, increased levels of NAD+ can decrease the teratogenicity of hypoxia in mice (Cuny et al. 2020). NAD+ detoxifies ROS during cell metabolism (Burtscher et al. 2025). Genetically or dietary induced NAD+ deficiency in mice results in RCEM spectrum‐type defects, including vertebral anomalies, cardiac anomalies, and renal anomalies (Cuny et al. 2020; Mark 2022). In humans, genetically induced NAD+ deficiency is associated with teratogenicity consistent with the RCEM spectrum (Mark and Dunwoodie 2023; Mark 2022), which is one of the very few known genetic causes of the VACTERL spectrum defects.

In this review special attention will be paid to the Hormone Pregnancy Tests (HPTs). Oral HPTs containing a high dose of progestogen (often norethisterone or ethisterone) and a low dose of a synthetic estrogen were used to diagnose pregnancy from the mid‐1950s to early‐1980s. HPTs were recently shown to be associated with an increase in several specific malformations as the established human teratogen misoprostol (Danielsson and Ritchie 2025). The pattern of defects also resembled the RCEM spectrum. The teratogenicity of the abortive drug misoprostol is attributed to uterine contractions and compression of uteroplacental/embryonic vessels causing transient embryonic hypoxia (Philip et al. 2003; Vargas et al. 2000) and ROS generation (Chernoff and Rogers 2010) if abortion fails.

Oral HPTs (1 tablet for 2 days) were assumed to produce uterine contractions and expelling of the uterine lining if the woman was not pregnant, mimicking monthly withdrawal bleeding (menstruation) caused by a peak followed by rapid decrease in activation of progesterone receptors in the uterus. If the woman was pregnant, no uterine contractions were expected to occur after taking an HPT. It was assumed that the progesterone levels were high enough to prevent contractions in pregnant women. However, today it is known that there are wide variations in progesterone levels in early pregnancy; some pregnant women even have lower progesterone than non‐pregnant women (Takaya et al. 2018). The almost identical increase in a specific pattern of malformations for HPTs and misoprostol indicates a common mechanism of action, that is, abnormal uterine contractions and transient embryonic hypoxia. Pregnant women with low progesterone levels were likely to have had the highest risk (Danielsson and Ritchie 2025).

In this context, it is important to note that there is an ongoing scientific and public debate triggered by alleged victims of HPTs in the United Kingdom and Germany seeking recognition of this association. A report, coordinated by the United Kingdom regulatory agency, concluded that there was no evidence of causality and no identifiable pattern of malformations in the offspring of women who took an HPT while pregnant (Commission on Human Medicines 2017). This conclusion was primarily based on a review of > 25 epidemiological studies (mainly conducted in the 1960s–1980s) and a review of 225 case reports not previously published. In contrast, other studies concluded that there was a causal association between HPTs and human teratogenicity by essentially assessing the same data, including a meta‐analysis (Heneghan et al. 2018). It has further been noted that HPTs cause a recognizable pattern of anomalies as misoprostol consistent with a hypoxia/ROS related mechanism (Danielsson et al. 2023; Danielsson and Ritchie 2025).

In view of the proposed association between embryonic hypoxia/ROS and RCEM, one aim of the current review is to explore if the spectrum of anomalies described in women exposed to HPTs during pregnancy is consistent with the RCEM concept. Special attention is paid to multiple anomalies which are considered to be more sensitive for the detection of teratogenicity than analysis of all or isolated congenital anomalies (Friedman 1992; Garne et al. 2011). However, it is likely that an isolated anomaly (e.g., limb reduction), which is also a component of a multiple malformation association (e.g., VACTERL), has a common etiology (Garne et al. 2011). The pattern of individual HPT defects will therefore also be compared with the RCEM spectrum. The second aim is to compare the pattern of defects included in RCEM with the pattern of defects caused by transient hypoxia in animal studies. The third aim is to present evidence that excessive ROS production may be the final common pathway in the genesis of these types of anomalies. Together, the results can contribute to the elucidation of the role of hypoxia/ROS in the RCEM concept as well as in HPT associated teratogenicity.

Methods

Characterization of the Spectrum of Malformations in Each RCEM Condition

The terminology and types of manifestations included as “core” defects in affected organs in different RCEM conditions is not consistent and varies between published studies and reviews (Adam et al. 2020; Mark 2022). For example, the ocular component included in the Oculo‐Auriculo‐Vertebral spectrum (OAVS) includes eyelid coloboma (most typically upper eyelid), micro or anophthalmia, epicanthal folds, and epibulbar dermoid(s) in different publications (Rooijers et al. 2020), although it is unclear if some of the individuals designated as having OAVS in some of these publications might have had a genetic syndrome that was misinterpreted as representing OAVS (which currently does not have a known, consistent genetic cause). The definition of a “core” defect in certain RCEM conditions is also wide for some affected organs, for example, any type of structural cardiovascular defect fulfills criteria to be included as “core defect” in POC and VACTERL. Any of type of limb defect can be used to fulfill criteria to be included in VACTERL in some publications, although radial ray defects and/or postaxial polydactyly are most commonly accepted to be required for VACTERL‐type limb defects (Adam et al. 2020; Mark 2022).

In contrast a specific gastrointestinal defect (anal atresia), and not any other type of gastrointestinal defect, is required to be included as a “core” manifestation in VACTERL and POC. Furthermore, there is no uniform consensus of clinical diagnostic criteria for each of these RCEM conditions (Mark 2022). All fail to capture cases with partial expression of the phenotype and many overlap with at least one other RCEM condition. The author also suggested widening the spectrum of RCEM‐like defects to include such findings as sirenomelia and urorectal septum malformation sequence, the latter of which encompasses a more generalized finding of “lower intestinal anomalies” and not a specific gastrointestinal defect (Mark 2022).

More recently there has also been renewed interest in conditions that lead to fetal disruption sequence, which may include certain brain malformations, amniotic bands, gastroschisis, and limb reduction defects. These have also been associated with hypoxia and are considered by some as in the same spectrum of RCEM (Adam et al. 2021).

In view of the lack of uniform terminology for malformations included in each RCEM and the lack of consensus of clinical diagnostic criteria for each RCEM condition, all manifestations considered to be “core” defects or “overlapping” defects in the six RCEM in recent reviews have been compiled and are presented in Table 1. The data in Table 1 will be used to evaluate whether the spectrum of malformations reported for HPTs in humans and after transient embryonic hypoxia in animal studies are partly or fully consistent with the RCEM spectrum concept.

Analysis of Spectrum of Multiple and Individual HPT Malformations in Case Report and Epidemiological Studies Compared to the RCEM Spectrum

To investigate whether the spectrum of human multiple and single malformations associated with HPT exposure in humans is consistent with the RCEM spectrum, the results in two comprehensive reports authored by assessors at the UK medicinal regulatory agency (MHRA) have been evaluated in detail. The first study summarized results in relation to HPT associated malformations in more than 25 epidemiological studies (cohort or case–control), conducted between the late 1960 to early 1980s (Seabroke 2017) (with exception of the study by Tümmler et al. (2014)). The studies were selected based on a comprehensive literature search by another regulatory UK assessor (Datta‐Nemdharry 2016) and only included studies with a comparator/control group. Thus, some studies that indicated an increase in specific malformations, for example, limb reduction defects in HPT exposed pregnancies (Jaffe et al. 1975; Papp et al. 1976; Robertson‐Rintoul 1974), were excluded. Summary statistics were expressed as odds ratios for each malformation and for VACTERL association‐type defects (multiple defects). Odds ratios were calculated from proportions data if they were not available from the original study (Seabroke 2017). The quality assessment tool that was developed specifically to assess studies related to HPT exposure during pregnancy also took into account methodological issues, such as confounding by indication, selection of controls, exposure ascertainment/recall bias, sample size, and multiplicity.

The second epidemiological report investigated the evidence of a causal association between malformations and HPTs in a cohort of more than 225 case reports not previously evaluated (Beynon and Seabroke 2017). These case reports originated mainly from regulatory bodies (UK, Australia, New Zealand and WHO) and a patient organization in the United Kingdom. The exposures occurred before HPTs were withdrawn from the market in the United Kingdom in 1979; the withdrawal occurred several years later than in several other European countries (e.g., Sweden and Norway, both of which withdrew HPTs in the early 1970s). Subgroups of major malformations in the case reports were coded according to criteria in the EUROCAT birth register before analysis was performed. This report is the most up‐to‐date study in relation to outlining all specific defects/subgroups associated with HPT use in pregnancy. The report compared changes in proportions of malformations in the HPT cohort (in relation to the expected rate based on data in European birth registries [EUROCAT]), making it possible to compare whether a specific malformation in HPT‐exposed pregnancies was proportionally increased compared to malformations in the general population in the EUROCAT birth registry. This type of methodology is used today for signal detection to identify unusual associations between medication use in pregnancy and congenital anomalies (Dolk 2015).

The EUROCAT system also classifies whether the malformation can be detected at birth or is typically not detected until after at least 1 week after birth (EUROCAT 2013). This is important, as anomalies not obviously detected at birth by visual inspection or due to severe symptoms are likely to be detected at a higher rate today due to better prenatal technologies (e.g., prenatal ultrasound) and better surveillance of pregnancies compared to the 1960s to early 1970s when HPTs were used extensively. For example, limb reduction defects, which according to EUROCAT should be detected at birth, have been stable around 2% of all malformations in the general population over the last 50 years (Beynon and Seabroke 2017). Also complex cardiovascular lesions, which are possible to detect at birth due to severe symptoms (e.g., “blue baby”) have remained stable, while “milder” lesions, such as ventricular and atrial septal defects, have increased dramatically over time, most likely reflecting improved diagnosis (Giang et al. 2023). Similarly, the prevalence of hypospadias has increased dramatically from the 1970s to the 2000s (Dolk 2004; Leunbach et al. 2025). In view of this, only anomalies which are possible to detect at birth are considered reliable when calculating alteration incidences in the case report study versus the EUROCAT registry.

Evaluation of Whether Malformations Associated With HPT Are Consistent With the RCEM Spectrum

Case Report Study: Assessment of Types and Constellations of Multiple Defects in Relation to Defined RCEM Conditions (See Also S1)

In the case report study (Beynon and Seabroke 2017), a total of 44 possible cases of multiple malformation (18% of all HPT‐exposed cases) were not obviously due to a genetic syndrome after review by two different clinical geneticists. Of note, the details of each case were coded using the EUROCAT birth defects coding system; however, detailed clinical information about each case was not presented or available. Furthermore, it remains unclear what, if any, genetic testing was done on this cohort of individuals with multiple malformations, particularly given that the affected individuals were born decades ago. Unlike birth defects registries, some of the affected individuals reported were older at the time of ascertainment, so some coded information, such as kidney neoplasia, was likely not present at birth but instead developed later in life, which sets this data apart from what is most often reported in birth defects registries.

Of the 44 cases with multiple malformations, 10/44 (23%) were consistent with a classic RCEM condition while 31/44 (70%) were consistent with an RCEM‐like constellation and 3/44 (7%) could not be classified as RCEM. In this latter category, one case had insufficient data (coded as hypospadias with multiple congenital anomalies not otherwise specified) and two were coded as situs inversus, which is not a typical RCEM finding. However, as can be seen in Figure 1, the birth defect patterns are consistent with the RCEM concept, demonstrating overlapping features of more than one RCEM phenotype for some. The most common RCEM was VACTERL or VACTERL‐like without overlap with another RCEM (13 cases) and a further six cases had VACTERL/OEIS overlap. There were six cases consistent with OAVS and another four that had features of OAVS/VACTERL overlap. There were three cases of VACTERL/MURCS and one case each of VACTERL/OAVS/MURCS and OAVS/MURCS overlap. There were three cases that could be mild LBWC‐like and 1 case that had LBWC‐like features with OEIS overlap. There was also one case of OEIS and another case of OEIS/OAVS overlap.

There were no cases that were consistent with POC. This is a very rare condition, so it is not surprising that in a total of 44 older individuals, there were no cases consistent with this RCEM phenotype. Similarly, classic LBW features would not be expected in adults, as this constellation of findings frequently leads to a termination of pregnancy or a pregnancy loss. There were three cases that were not completely consistent with the RCEM spectrum hypothesis, including one case of OEIS/MURCS overlap and two cases of OAVS/LBWC‐like features. Therefore, of the total 44 cases, a high percentage (38/44; 86%) were consistent with the RCEM concept.

The occurrence of specific subgroups of individual malformations associated with HPTs in the large case report study is presented in Table 2 (Beynon and Seabroke 2017). In this analysis, “core” single defects of each RCEM were compared to the frequencies in the HPT‐exposed cases to determine if such defects were increased in frequency. Congenital heart defects, a core feature of several RCEM, were included with the caveat that congenital heart defects are one of the most common birth defects, occurring in about 1% of the general population (Bourdon et al. 2024), and are also a common feature in many genetic syndromes. As advanced genetic testing was not done in the single birth defect cohort here, a genetic condition cannot be excluded, although the data was reviewed by two clinical geneticists in the HPT study who did not identify a known or common genetic syndrome in each of the reported individuals based on their review. Therefore, the data on cardiovascular anomalies must be interpreted with caution.

Table 2 shows almost all of the subgroups of individual malformations associated with HPTs are also a “core defect” within one of six RCEM categories (see Table 1). Several prominent defects characterized as “core” defects in one or more RCEM were noted to have a 2‐fold or more increased rate of occurrence compared to the EUROCAT registry, including limb reduction defects, neural tube defects (spina bifida), severe cardiac defects, and bladder exstrophy.

Epidemiological Studies: Assessment of Individual Malformations Reported and Potential Consistency With Individual Malformations in the RCEM Spectrum

Individual major malformations associated with HPTs in epidemiological studies, mainly conducted between late 1960s to 1980s in European countries (United Kingdom, Germany, Spain, France, Sweden, and Hungary) and the United States, were presented in a comprehensive report (Seabroke 2017). Summary statistics expressed as effect estimate (EE) compared to controls, and statistical significance were calculated (marked with *). As presented below, the pattern of malformations showing an increase in these “old” epidemiological studies are consistent with defined “core defects” or “overlapping defects” in the RCEM spectrum listed in Table 1.

Limb defects are characterized as “core defects” in VACTERL and LBWC and have been reported as “overlapping defects” in POC and OEIS. The limb reductions were increased (Effect Estimate in bracket) in all five studies compared to controls: (Lammer and Cordero 1986) (2.16*), (Vajda and Czeizel 1976) (1.32), (Oakley et al. 1973) (1.94), (Janerich et al. 1974) (3.06), and (Hellström et al. 1976) (2.90).

Orofacial clefts are included as “core defects” in the OAVS and LBWC and have been reported as an “overlapping defect” in VACTERL. Cleft palate was increased in 2 out of 2 studies: (Oakley et al. 1973) (1.67) and (Lammer and Cordero 1986) (1.28). Cleft lip+/−cleft palate was increased in 2 of 3 studies (Oakley et al. 1973) (1.4) and (Tümmler et al. 2014) (1.59*).

Cardiovascular (CV) defects of any kind are included as core criteria in POC and VACTERL and have been reported as “overlapping defects” in OAVS and MURCS. CV defects were increased in 9 out 10 studies (Janerich et al. 1977) (5.43*), (Heinonen et al. 1977) (2.1*), (Nora et al. 1978) (3.55*), (Hadjigeorgiou et al. 1980) (3.34), (Rothman et al. 1979) (1.3), (Goujard and Rumeau‐Rouquette 1977) (1.05), (Goujard et al. 1979) (1.49), (Boldt 1977) (1.79). On average, the increase in all of these studies was around 2‐fold (Heneghan et al. 2018).

Central Nervous System Defects

Several studies show an increase in neural tube defects, including spina bifida alone by Gal (1972) (5.63*), spina bifida combined with anencephaly by Sainz et al. (1987) (8.57) and Laurence et al. (1971) (1.26), encephalocele by Lammer and Cordero (1986) (1.49), and all types of neural tube defects by Tümmler et al. (2014) (2.99*), while studies by Oakley et al. (1973) (0.97), and Gaal and Ukvari (1977) (0.83) did not show any increase. Vertebral defects, which are often seen in those with neural tube defects, are defined as core components in MURCS, OAVS, OEIS, and VACTERL and as an overlapping defect in LBWC and POC. However, microcephaly, which is not a core component of any RCEM, was also highly increased in 2 out of 2 studies by Goujard and Rumeau‐Rouquette (1977) (5.62*) and Goujard et al. (1979) (25.6*). Skull defects are “overlapping defect” in OAVS but most individuals with microcephaly who have other features consistent with OAVS have been found to have a genetic syndrome, such as EFTUD2‐related mandibulofacial dysostosis with microcephaly (Lines et al. 2012).

Gastrointestinal Defects

Esophageal atresia/fistula was increased in 2 out of 2 studies by Lammer and Cordero (2.81*) and Oakley et al. (1973) (4.13). This anomaly is a core defect in VACTERL. Intestinal atresia was reported slightly increased in 1 out of 1 study by Oakley et al. (1973) (1.29).

Abdominal Defects

Diaphragmatic hernia was slightly increased in 2 out of 2 studies by Lammer and Cordero (1986) (1.47) and Oakley et al. (1973) (1.22.) Omphalocele was increased in 1 out of 1 study by Oakley et al. (1973) (1.47). Abdominal wall defects are included as core defects in LBWC, POC, and OEIS.

Urogenital Defects

Unilateral absence kidney was increased in 1 out of 1 study by Tümmler et al. (2014) (2.53*), and bilateral renal hypoplasia was highly increased in 1 out of 1 study by Goujard et al. (1979) (25.6*). These types of defects are included as core defects in MURCS and VACTERL and as an overlapping defect in LBWC, OAVS, and OEIS. Urine bladder exstrophy, increased in a study by Tümmler et al. (2014) (37.3*) is a core defect in LBWC and OEIS. Hypospadias was increased in 1 out of 1 study by Mau (1981) (2.19) and various renal defects (e.g., absent kidney, dilation pelvis or ureter, hydrocephalus) have been reported as overlapping in OEIS and VACTERL.

Epidemiological Studies: Assessment of Multiple Malformations Reported and Potential Consistency With the RCEM Spectrum

Only the VACTERL constellation of multiple malformations was assessed in the original epidemiological studies. VACTERL was increased in 2 out of 3 studies. In the study by Nora and Nora (1975), three different analyses were conducted; all showed marked increase in VACTERL (9.74*–13.5*). A later study also showed a marked increase in VACTERL (Nora et al. 1978) (8.42*) however a second study did not show any change (Lammer et al. 1986) (0.93–0.98). In addition, a more recent meta‐analysis based on the results in these studies; showed a significant 7.5 fold increase in VACTERL (Heneghan et al. 2018).

Concluding Remarks on Human Clinical Studies

There was high consistency with regards to malformations within the RCEM spectrum associated with HPTs in the large case report study (Figure 1 and Table 2) and in more than 25 epidemiological studies from various countries mainly published in the late 1960s–1980s (as presented in text in Section 3.2.3). Almost all of the individual malformations that were increased in women using HPTs in pregnancy in both studies are consistent with defects seen in the RCEM spectrum. Additionally, the multiple defects within the 44 cases associated with HPTs are also consistent with the RCEM concept (Figure 1). VACTERL was the most frequent RCEM identified in this cohort. Many (10/44, 23%) were consistent with a classic RCEM condition while most of the remainder (31/44, 70%) were consistent with an RCEM‐like constellation. Out of the total 44 cases, a high percentage (38/44; 86%) were consistent with the RCEM concept. Furthermore, the marked increase in VACTERL (the only constellation of multiple defects analyzed) in the epidemiological studies is consistent with the marked increase in VACTERL in the case report study.

The exposure period when the malformations within the RCEM spectrum can be induced (see Figure 1) is also within the period (weeks 3–4 to weeks 8–9 post‐conception) when HPTs were most often used to diagnose pregnancy (Commission on Human Medicines 2017). Thus, the time period in human pregnancy when these malformations are likely to have been induced correlates well with the time period when HPTs were used.

Malformations Induced by Hypoxia in Animals and the RCEM Spectrum

Numerous studies have investigated teratogenicity of transient hypoxia; the majority were published decades ago (review Danielsson et al. 2023). Older methods used to induce transient hypoxia in animals included clamping of uterine vessels (Franklin and Brent 1964) or decreasing oxygen content in the atmosphere (Ingalls and Curley 1957). More recently, drugs which have been shown to produce a period of transient hypoxia in the embryo (as verified by using a hypoxia probe in the embryo) have also been conducted (Danielsson et al. 2003; Ritchie et al. 2017). Hypoxia resulted in embryonic death or a mixture of embryonic death and malformations and/or normal embryos. The proportions were dependent on the time in organogenesis and the duration and severity of the transient hypoxic event. A large variety of single and multiple defects were induced as described in these animal studies. For example, one study showed that rat embryos could survive up to 3 h of transient interrupted blood and oxygen supply before GD 9 (when the embryonic heart starts beating), but surviving fetuses were severely malformed (Franklin and Brent 1964). In contrast, all embryos died if the embryos were exposed for 3 h on or after GD 9 in the same study. In contrast, several studies show that stage specific malformations could be induced by temporary hypoxia for much shorter periods. A large variety of single (with a much lower extent of multiple defects) have been reported in animal studies (reviews; Danielsson et al. 2023; Ritchie et al. 2017; Webster and Abela 2007) after exposure from GD 9 until the end of organogenesis in rats (GD 17) after exposure to embryonic hypoxia lasting between 30 up to 90 min.

A number of shortcomings must be acknowledged when analyzing “old” studies elucidating teratogenic effects of transient hypoxia compared to modern standards expressed in ICH guidelines for teratogenicity testing in animals (ICH S5 (R3) 2020). For example, the design of the studies is deficient in relation to estimating the incidence of a particular malformation and lack of inclusion of a relevant control group and number of animals needed per exposure group for calculating a statistical increase versus controls or historical control data in the strain used. Furthermore, only selected external visible malformations were examined in the great majority of the studies, and very few studies (if any) examined external, visceral as well as skeletal defects in the same fetus. “Old” hypoxia studies tended to focus on showing the occurrence of specific individual malformations on a specific stage of development rather than presenting multiple malformations in the examined fetuses.

Therefore, the finding of multiple defects in the same animal is only incidentally reported even in relatively recent studies (published after 2000). For example, following a single dose of phenytoin, a drug known to induce embryonic hypoxia in animal studies (Danielsson et al. 2005), induced 56 visceral malformations in 38 fetuses. This clearly implies that some fetuses had more than one visceral malformation, but no details are available (Beekhuijzen et al. 2000).

It should also be noted that some defects within the RCEM spectrum in humans are not possible to identify in rodent teratology studies. For example, eye defects such as epibulbar dermoids or cataracts. Rodent eyes are closed until several days postnatally; however, fetuses are routinely examined a few days before normal delivery. Tracheoesophageal fistula is usually detected shortly after birth in humans due to severe symptoms but is difficult to detect in the rodent fetus examined before birth. For this reason, it is not included in routine teratology studies. Ear and tail defects are also difficult to evaluate in animal teratology studies in relation to humans due to species differences.

Based on the limitations in animal studies mentioned above, Table 3 lists the “core” malformations within the human RCEM spectrum that are possible to detect in rodent and rabbit teratology studies. These are compared to those that have been induced by transient hypoxia (by different means) in published animal studies. Overall, the results show that the defects within the RCEM concept could be induced by transient hypoxia in animals.

Molecular Basis of Hypoxia‐ and ROS‐Mediated Mechanisms Underlying the RCEM Spectrum

The results show that individual malformations within the RCEM spectrum are consistent with malformations induced by transient hypoxia. Oxygen is a vital source of energy in cell metabolism across mammalian species in both the adult and the embryo, but different tissues have their own physiologically important oxygen concentration, physoxia (Carreau et al. 2011; Fathollahipour et al. 2018). For example, the adult thalamus has a much lower oxygen level for physoxia (1%) than other parts of the brain (4%) while the lungs have 14% as physoxia. The oxygen levels at physoxia in the embryo are also low (1%–5%). The oxygen concentration is kept at a low level in the embryo via specialized cell groups within the placenta which store and release oxygen to embryonic blood vessels (Aplin et al. 2015). During cell metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are formed and can serve as cell signaling molecules for biological processes. Excesses of ROS are detoxified by the cellular antioxidant reserve, including the enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase and glutathione peroxidase (Chen et al. 2018) Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) is also an essential regulator of cellular metabolism and redox processes (Burtscher et al. 2025).

Excessive generation of ROS in conditions overwhelming the antioxidant reserve can cause tissue injury in both adults and embryos. Ischemia (resulting in tissue hypoxia) followed by reperfusion and reoxygenation of hypoxic tissues is an established mechanism in humans for tissue damage due to excessive ROS generation as demonstrated in myocardial infarction, intestinal, cerebral, renal, and other ischemic syndromes (Granger and Kvietys 2015). The lack of free placental exchange from maternal blood to the human embryo during organogenesis has been proposed to decrease the risk for ROS induced teratogenicity due to marked fluctuations in oxygen levels. This is important as the embryo has lower antioxidant capacity compared to the adult (Woods et al. 1998). However, if the embryo is exposed to hypoxia (lower oxygen levels than at physoxia), followed by reoxygenation, teratogenicity due to ROS generation can occur (Woods et al. 1998). Some studies indicate that reoxygenation of tissues with low oxygen levels triggers a paradoxical increase in mitochondrial ROS generation (Waypa and Schumacker 2002).

Excess ROS, Vascular Disruption, and Teratogenicity Within the RCEM Spectrum

Vascular endothelial cells in recently formed vessels have increased energy need with subsequent ROS production as compared with mature quiescent cells (Peshavariya et al. 2009). Endothelial cells in recently formed vessels are also directly exposed to ROS as a consequence of transient hypoxia followed by reoxygenation compared to cells outside the cardiovascular system. In addition, recently formed vessels during angiogenesis only consist of a monolayer of vascular endothelial cells, but lack pericytes and muscle cells in contrast to more mature vessels in the embryo (Figure 2A). This permits rapid organ outgrowth of vessels, for example, in developing limb (Figure 2B) but also makes recently formed vessels less protected and more susceptible to damage than more mature vessels (Danielsson et al. 2023). This may explain why transient hypoxia causes developmental stage specific malformations preceded by vascular disruption and hemorrhage, for example, cleft lip on gestational day (GD) 11 and distal limb reduction defects in fore paws on GD 13 and hind paws on GD 14 even though the whole embryo is hypoxic (Danielsson et al. 2023). Only the endothelium on recently formed vessels is disrupted and causes maldevelopment, while other vessels (with less ROS generation and more protection) are more resistant.

Strong evidence suggests that malformations preceded by vascular disruption and transient hypoxia are directly caused by ROS, including superoxide anion radical and other reactive species (Figure 2C) (Fantel et al. 1992; Woods et al. 1998).

Histological changes (oedema, vascular disruption, hemorrhage) appeared in the limb buds shortly after a transient hypoxia (60 min) on GD 14 (Figure 2E), no such changes were observed in control embryos (Figure 2D). At birth, these early changes were manifested as absence, reductions, deformations and fusions of digits (Leist and Grauwiler 1974). Excessive ROS generation was localized to the sites of vascular disruption and digital defects in the limb buds (Figure 2f) (Fantel et al. 1992).

Active angiogenesis varies with developmental stage of the embryo and occurs in different regions of the embryo at the same time. This suggests that multiple malformations within the RCEM spectrum after a single event of transient hypoxia could be caused by a common mechanism. For example, excessive ROS generation and subsequent vascular disruption damage in recently formed vessels can occur simultaneously in different organ systems. Teratogenicity is dependent on which vessels are disrupted, which will vary with the developmental stage and also with the severity (degree and duration) of the transient hypoxic event. Severe hypoxia (even relatively short periods) in combination with reperfusion/reoxygenation, results in massive ROS generation (Burtscher et al. 2025).

Altogether, these data indicate that regions/organs in the embryo under formation, with active angiogenesis and high oxygen metabolism in endothelial cells, are particularly susceptible to ROS‐induced teratogenicity mediated via vascular disruption. Fetal anomalies within a specific RCEM spectrum correlate well with the timeline of their human embryonic development as described in Figure 1 (see Section 3.2.1). However, there is evidence that various types of orofacial clefts, which are components in both LBWC and OAVS, could be induced both by direct hypoxia/ROS damage and vascular disruption in the region where the malformation occurs (e.g., cleft lip on GD 11 in the rat; Danielsson et al. 2023) or secondary to another malformation induced at an earlier stage. One example of an indirect effect is cleft palate in rats exposed to hypoxia on GD 14 where histological examination 72 h after the hypoxic event showed blood coagulation in one region that appeared large enough to push the tongue backward and upward, possibly resulting in mechanical impairment of the closure of the palatal shelves (Leist and Grauwiler 1974). This means that the critical period for individual RCEMs, particularly orofacial clefts, should not be overstated.

Excess of ROS, Embryonic Cardiac Arrhythmia, and Malformations

Transient hypoxia has long been known to induce periods of cardiac irregular rhythm and cardiac arrest in the embryo (Chernoff and Grabowski 1971; Grabowski and Schroeder 1968) in a similar way as in the adult heart (Granger and Kvietys 2015). Mechanistic data indicate that the arrythmia is caused by excessive ROS generation following transient hypoxia: excessive ROS disrupts electric cardiac activity as it alters the structure and function of ion channels essential for normal rhythm regulation, particularly a potassium channel expressed by the hERG gene (Kolbe et al. 2010). The inhibition by ROS of hERG channels prolongs the cardiac action potential duration and afterdepolarizations; the cellular basis of triggered arrhythmias (Trum et al. 2020). The hERG channel is essential for normal rhythm regulation in both the adult human heart as well as in the embryonic heart across species as it starts beating (Danielsson et al. 2012). The human embryonic heart starts beating weeks 3–4 and GD 9.5 in the rat embryo (DeSesso 2017).

There are several pieces of evidence indicating that cardiovascular defects induced by transient hypoxia in the embryo are related to ROS induced cardiac arrythmia per se and not vascular disruption. Embryonic arrhythmia induces severe alterations in blood flow and blood pressure during the formation of the cardiovascular system. Such alterations in cardiogenesis by mechanical methods (e.g., ligation of vessels) or pharmacologically can produce a wide pattern of cardiovascular defects which agree with those induced by transient hypoxia (reviews; Midgett et al. 2017; Sedmera 2005; Trinidad et al. 2022). The cardiovascular defects could be prevented by treatment with a potent ROS scavenger in advance of the hypoxic event (Sköld et al. 2001). In addition, embryonic cardiac arrhythmia causes severe hypoxia followed by reoxygenation and is itself capable of producing ROS and vascular disruption defects in organ systems other than the cardiovascular system such as limb reductions, orofacial clefts (Danielsson et al. 2007; Ritchie et al. 2017).

Excessive ROS, Multiple Defects Within the RCEM Spectrum and Antioxidants

The induction of both single and multiple defects by transient hypoxia and excessive ROS is dependent on the time in organogenesis and the duration and severity of the hypoxic event. A single period of transient severe hypoxia on GD 11 in the rat is able to produce a spectrum of stage specific malformations similar to the malformations included in VACTERL as “core” or “overlapping” defects (Sköld et al. 2001). The spectrum of major malformations included vertebrae defects in the upper thoracic region, anal atresia, cardiovascular malformations and renal defects (including absence renal papilla, increased pelvic cavitation and dilated urethras). Cleft lip, undescended testis and absence of lung lobe, reported to be “overlapping” malformations in VACTERL, were also observed (Table 1). The stage for inducing these defects in rats (GD 11) corresponds to weeks 6–7 of embryonic life in humans when VACTERL is induced in humans (Adam et al. 2020).

Increase in Antioxidant and/or ROS Neutralizing Activity Decreases Teratogenicity

The assumption of ROS as the proximal teratogen is supported by studies that showed an increase of the antioxidant glutathione or an agent which captures ROS can substantially decrease and even prevent all the manifestations associated with VACTERL related to transient hypoxia and ROS (as shown below). Pre‐treatment with OTC (L‐2‐oxothiazolidine‐4‐carboxylate) before induction of a period of severe embryonic hypoxia considerably decreased the incidence of anomalies within the VACTERL spectrum (Sköld et al. 2001; Wellfelt et al. 1999). OTC increases intracellular levels of glutathione (GSH), which is the major antioxidant within cells in both the adult and embryonic tissues. In addition, pretreatment with the potent lipophilic ROS capturing agent, alpha‐phenyl‐T‐n‐butylnitron (PBN) was able to prevent all malformations induced by transient hypoxia on GD 11 (Sköld et al. 2001; Wellfelt et al. 1999). In a similar way, pre‐treatment with PBN on GD 13 or GD 14 totally prevented transient hypoxia from inducing distal digital malformations (Sköld et al. 2001).

Single and multiple malformations in humans associated with cocaine (Hoyme et al. 1990; Plessinger and Woods, Plessinger and Woods Jr. 1998) and phenytoin (Lyon et al. 2003) were related to transient hypoxia and vascular disruption.

The malformations could be recreated in mechanistic animal studies showing that teratogenicity is preceded by embryonic hypoxia, ROS generation, vascular disruption, and hemorrhage for both cocaine (Lipton et al. 2002; Webster and Abela 2007; Zimmerman et al. 1994) and phenytoin (Azarbayjani and Danielsson 2002; Danielson et al. 1992; Danielsson et al. 2007). In support of ROS‐mediated teratogenicity, PBN was effective in decreasing the teratogenicity of both phenytoin (Wells et al. 1989) and cocaine (Zimmerman et al. 1994).

Decrease in Antioxidant and/or ROS Neutralizing Activity Increases Teratogenicity

NAD acts as an antioxidant in all cells (including the developing embryo) and is an essential regulator of cellular metabolism, and redox processes to protect against ROS/oxidative stress. In mammalian species, NAD is synthesized from the essential amino acid tryptophan or vitamin B3. An imbalance between low levels of NAD and high levels of ROS have been associated with a number of ROS related conditions in adult humans and subsequent organ damage, for example, in the diabetic kidney (Xu et al. 2021) and in hypoxia induced vascular dementia in the brain (Zhao et al. 2021). NAD levels vary with the degree of hypoxia and ROS generation (oxidative stress); increasing cellular NAD levels counteract hypoxic injury. (Burtscher et al. 2025) (Figure 3). Mild hypoxia, which is the normal condition in the embryo, increases NAD levels and resilience in healthy, non‐stressed cells. In contrast, severe hypoxia/ischemia, in particular in combination with reperfusion, results in excessive ROS generation and decreased NAD neutralizing function resulting in tissue damage.

Increasing cellular NAD levels to counteract hypoxic injury. (Mild) hypoxia/ischemia (conditioning) increases NAD levels and resilience in healthy, non‐stressed cells, while severe hypoxia/ischemia, in particular in combination with reperfusion, negatively impacts cellular NAD metabolism. NAD‐supplementation and/or improving NAD metabolism by conditioning counteracts hypoxic/ischemic injury (adapted from Burtscher et al. (2025)).

These data indicate that the aggravated teratogenicity in mice reported after combined exposure to NAD deficiency and transient hypoxia compared to hypoxia alone (Cuny et al. 2020) is related to an imbalance between excess ROS and NAD deficiency. While hypoxia alone (8% of oxygen instead of normal 20% in the air for 8 h on GD 9.5) in mice caused a slight increase in vertebral (Sparrow et al. 2012) and/or heart defects (Moreau et al. 2019), combined hypoxia with NAD deficiency resulted in a more severe and broader spectrum of teratogenicity; 52% of the fetuses were malformed, including vertebrae, digit, heart, eye, abdominal wall, and kidney defects (Cuny et al. 2020). It is likely that low levels of NAD negatively impact the defense in neutralizing ROS (oxidative stress) resulting in increased teratogenicity of combined transient hypoxia and deficiency (Figure 3).

The spectrum of defects in the study in NAD‐deficient mice (Cuny et al. 2020) is very similar to the RCEM spectrum in humans (Mark 2022). In normal metabolism, excesses of ROS are neutralized by the antioxidant system. Only pronounced excesses of ROS (e.g., induced by long periods of hypoxia or short periods of hypoxia/reoxygenation) that overwhelm the antioxidant defense are teratogenic.

RCEM and Genetic Conditions When Excessive ROS Overwhelm Antioxidant Defense

Deficiency in the Endogenous Synthesis of NAD

Excesses of ROS, which normally are neutralized by NAD, can cause malformations consistent with the RCEM spectrum if there is a low level of NAD in embryonic tissues due to dietary restriction (Cuny et al. 2020). Similarly, loss of function of enzymes blocking de novo NAD synthesis (HAOO'KNYU) in mice (which result in low NAD embryonic levels) causes multiple congenital defects that fall within the RCEM spectrum in humans, including those found in the VACTERL association (Mark 2022; Shi et al. 2017).

Total or partial loss of function of the genes encoding these enzymes together with a niacin‐free diet resulted in the death of all embryos in mice. However, if the niacin‐free diet was supplemented with a small amount of niacin, embryos with partial loss were normal, while embryos with total loss showed multiple defects (Shi et al. 2017). The malformations were of the same type as in embryos with low NAD levels due to dietary restriction alone. The defects were due to embryonic, not maternal, NAD deficiency, as null mutant dams produced normal heterozygous embryos (Shi et al. 2017).

In humans, biallelic pathogenic variants in HAAO, KYNU, or NADSYN1, respectively, can lead to malformations within the RCEM spectrum similar to those produced by NAD deficiency during embryonic life in mice (Cuny et al. 2020). The results support the hypothesis that ROS is the proximal teratogen behind RCEM. The results imply that the malformations were induced by exposure to excesses of ROS during embryonic life, which in human embryos should normally have been neutralized by NAD.

Two other conditions relating to ROS have also been associated with malformations within the RCEM spectrum, Fanconi anemia and homozygous alpha‐thalassemia.

Fanconi anemia (FA) is a rare genetic condition characterized by a predisposition to teratogenicity and hematological malignancies. A body of evidence shows that ROS generation and oxidative stress (Li et al. 2017), together with impaired ROS detoxifying machinery (Kumari et al. 2014; Pagano et al. 2021; Pagano and Youssoufian 2003), play a major role in FA anemia. Cells in patients with FA also show decreased NAD levels (Berger et al. 1982), which fit well with NAD consumption in conditions with increased ROS generation. Patients with FA show both single or multiple malformations including several types within the RCEM spectrum, such as spina bifida, vertebral defects, cardiovascular defects, esophageal atresia, anal atresia, limb reductions, club foot and renal defects (Auerbach 2009). Together the results suggest that excess ROS, overwhelming the antioxidant defense in the embryo, resulting in malformations within the RCEM spectrum plays a role in FA teratogenicity.

Homozygous alpha‐thalassemia erythrocytes are present in embryonic blood at the onset of circulation around week 4 of embryonic life. Fetuses with homozygous alpha‐thalassemia are unable to make typical fetal hemoglobin, which significantly affects the delivery of oxygen to developing tissues (Adam et al. 2005). Additionally, the defective hemoglobin can lead to increased precipitation and decreased survival of red blood cells (Chui and Waye 1998).

The lack of continuous oxygenation of the human embryo with homozygous alpha‐thalassemia is likely to produce periods of pathological hypoxia, followed by periods of reoxygenation and excessive ROS generation. The excessive ROS generation may also explain why fetuses show an increased risk of birth defects within the RCEM spectrum in humans. In one study, 17.4% showed malformations, including hydrocephalus, microcephaly, cardiac septal defects, and skeletal defects, including digital defects (Liang et al. 1985). Other studies showed severe limb reductions (Adam et al. 2005; Lam et al. 1997). Additionally, neural tube defects (Lam and Tang 1999), hypospadias, and other genital defects are seen with greater frequency than expected (Adam et al. 2005; Guy et al. 1985; Liang et al. 1985; Nakayama et al. 1986; Thornley et al. 2003).

Concluding Remarks and Future Directions of Research

Altogether the results provide evidence that excessive ROS production overwhelming the antioxidant defense is likely to be the final common pathway in the genesis of malformations within the RCEM spectrum (both single and multiple defects). The results show that the human malformations related to HPTs are consistent with the RCEM spectrum and support a causal relation mediated via transient hypoxia and ROS generation during reperfusion/reoxygenation in the same way as in animal studies. This mechanism is also likely to underlie malformations within the RCEM spectrum reported for the established human teratogens misoprostol, phenytoin, and cocaine. The results also suggest teratogenicity mediated via imbalance of ROS formation and antioxidant defense mechanisms in some genetic conditions, such as autosomal recessive congenital NAD deficiency, Fanconi anemia, and homozygous alpha‐thalassemia. Furthermore, since ROS induced RCEMs can be produced both by environmental factors as well as have a genetic basis, future research should also focus on gene–environment interactions.

Recent research, which supports the RCEM concept, indicates specific epigenetic signatures may make it possible to discriminate between genetically mediated malformations caused by discrete genetic syndromes and RCEM conditions, which lack a recurrent known genetic cause (Haghshenas et al. 2024). One of the main drivers for developing epigenetic changes is excessive ROS generation (Chen and Shen 2025; Kietzmann et al. 2017; Shrishrimal et al. 2019).

Furthermore, the results support the further exploration of supplementation of NAD+ (Mark and Dunwoodie 2022) and other antioxidants (Micangeli et al. 2022; Ornoy 2007) during pregnancy to decrease the risk for fetal malformations, particularly in pregnancies predisposed to excessive ROS generation due to disease (e.g., excess ROS related to hyperglycemia in diabetes), exogenous factors, or genetic conditions.
