Clinical Efficacy and Safety of Low‐Energy Extracorporeal Shock Wave Therapy for Various Conditions of Deep Dermal and Subdermal Fibrosis
Authors: Jeongah Ko, Sung Bin Cho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundExtracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) enhances extracellular matrix remodeling and tissue regeneration by promoting growth factor release, regulating blood and lymphatic flows, and reducing fat and fibrotic tissues. Focused shock wave therapy (F‐SWT), radial shock wave therapy (R‐SWT), and combined F‐SWT and R‐SWT have been used to deliver different patterns of shock energy depending on the characteristics of the target lesions.MethodsWe investigated the efficacy and safety of ESWT in patients with dermal and subdermal fibrosis. Fifty‐two patients treated with F‐SWT and/or R‐SWT for dermal and subdermal fibrosis caused due to various reasons were retrospectively analyzed by reviewing their medical records, clinical images, and ultrasound study images.ResultsThe mean number of pulses administered for F‐SWT on the cheek, temple, and chin were 2600.0 ± 1040.8 shocks/session and for R‐SWT were 5080.0 ± 2234.6 pulses/session, and the number of treatment sessions were 8.0 ± 4.4. In patients who were treated with ESWT on the abdomen, the mean number of pulses for F‐SWT were 2600.0 ± 2408.3 shocks/session and for R‐SWT were 8400.0 ± 894.4 pulses/session, and the number of treatment sessions were 3.2 ± 1.6. Most patients were satisfied with the results. Pain during ESWT was well tolerated and post‐ESWT edema was more common in R‐SWT than in F‐SWT.ConclusionOur data demonstrated that ESWT effectively and safely improved the clinical appearance and functional movement of patients with dermal and subdermal fibrosis caused due to various reasons.

Introduction

Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) uses a sequence of shock wave pulses that noninvasively treat a variety of tissues [1]. Shock waves sharply rise to a peak pressure of 100 MPa within a duration of nanoseconds, and the pulses are delivered over a short duration of microseconds [1]. Mechanical shock wave energy is released to tissues depending on the difference in impedance and change into biochemical cellular reactions by activating mechanotransduction pathways [1, 2]. Shock wave energy enhances remodeling of extracellular matrix and enables tissue regeneration by activating the mechanoresponsive cells, particularly fibroblasts [2, 3]. Moreover, wound healing in the initial inflammatory phase can be beneficially modulated by ESWT, which activates anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages and inhibits pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages [3, 4, 5]. ESWT has also been found to regulate inflammation by inducing pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines, including cyclophilin A, interleukin (IL)‐6, and IL‐10, based on the stages of wound healing or scarring development [4, 5]. Additionally, in vivo rat skin study demonstrated that ESWT upregulated fibroblast growth factor (FGF)‐2 expression and induced neocollagenesis and neoangiogenesis [6].

The clinical applications of ESWT have increased over the years and include systemic sclerosis, lymphedema, cellulites, chronic ulcers, hypertrophic scars, and facial wrinkles and enlarged pores [1, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. A previous clinical pilot study found that patients with progressive systemic sclerosis who were treated with shock waves presented increased circulating endothelial cells and endothelial progenitor cells along with clinical improvement in skin textures and vasculatures [7]. Moreover, ESWT was used to treat hypertrophic scars, which improved the overall patient and observer scar assessment scale (POSAS) [12]. Action mechanisms have been suggested to induce scar remodeling by regulating fibroblasts and maintaining normal epidermal integrity by regulating proliferation and differentiation of keratinocytes [12, 13].

Acoustic waves of ESWT can noninvasively and nonthermally penetrate the skin and subcutaneous fat, promoting growth factor release, regulate blood and lymphatic flow, and reduce fat and fibrotic tissues [10]. According to the characteristics of lesions and for therapeutic purposes, focused shock wave therapy (F‐SWT), radial shock wave therapy (R‐SWT), or a combination of F‐SWT and R‐SWT can be used to deliver different patterns of shock energy [10]. F‐SWT electromechanically delivers shock pulses to the deep dermis and subdermis, which break fibrotic tissues and stimulate cell metabolism [10, 14]. Moreover, R‐SWT pneumatically delivers short and radial shock pulses that stimulate blood and lymphatic flow and remove decomposed tissues [10, 14]. However, optimal treatment protocols have not been fully established in patients with deep dermal and subdermal fibrosis due to the heterogeneity in the cause of fibrosis and clinical features. This study aims to investigate the efficacy and safety of ESWT protocols using F‐SWT, R‐SWT, and a combination of F‐SWT and R‐SWT by retrospectively evaluating 52 patients with deep dermal and subdermal fibrosis caused due to a number of reasons.

Materials and Methods

Patients

A total of 52 patients (43 females and 9 males; mean age of 37.0 ± 10.7 years, range of 19 to 66 years) with deep dermal and subdermal fibrosis treated with ESWT between January 2019 and December 2023 were enrolled in this single‐center, retrospective observational study. Patients’ baseline characteristics and causes of deep dermal and subdermal fibrosis are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Patients who underwent concomitant treatments such as skin resurfacing procedures, fractional laser treatments, noninvasive monopolar or bipolar radiofrequency treatments, invasive monopolar or bipolar microneedling radiofrequency treatments, and high‐intensity focused ultrasound within the preceding 3 months were excluded from this study.

ESWT

In this study, we used DUOLITH SD1 Ultra (Storz Medical AG, Tägerwilen, Switzerland) with an SEPIA handpiece (Storz Medical AG) for generating focused and deep electromechanical shock pulses and MASTERPULS MP200 R‐SW module (Storz Medical AG) for generating short and radial acoustic pulses. After obtaining written informed consent, all patients were treated with 6.4 ± 3.9 sessions of ESWT between intervals of 1 and 2 weeks. Neither topical nor systemic anesthesia was required. The treated lesions were gently cleaned using mild soap and 70% alcohol. An ultrasound coupling gel (BNV Biolab, Seoul, South Korea) was used for efficient energy delivery and smooth movement of the applicators perpendicular to the surface of the skin. ESWT was performed after complete contact between the applicators and the skin with gentle compression. Each patient was generally treated with ESWT using a focused handpiece at an energy range of 0.10–0.15 mJ/mm2, a pulse frequency of 3 Hz, and 1000–6000 shocks/session and/or treated using a radial handpiece at a pressure of 1 bar, a pulse frequency of 8 Hz, and 2000–12 000 pulses/session. Neither contact nor noncontact cooling was required because ESWT generated no significant thermal reactions.

No systemic sedatives or analgesics were administered before, during, or after the treatment. Immediately after ESWT, topical solutions of human fibroblast‐culture media (SMPF; BNV Biolab) were applied to the lesions to enhance ESWT‐induced tissue regeneration and reduce the risk of adverse events. In cases of active inflammatory lesions at the time of ESWT, including acne vulgaris, folliculitis, furuncles, and herpes simplex, prophylactic systemic antibiotics or antiviral agents were prescribed for 3 days. Prophylactic topical and systemic antibiotics or steroids were not administered after ESWT.

Objective and Subjective Evaluations

Images were captured using identical camera settings, lighting, and patient positioning at baseline and 3 months after the final treatment. Additionally, 2‐dimensional ultrasound (E‐CUBE12; Alpinion Medical Systems Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) images of the skin with deep dermal and subdermal fibrosis were obtained. An ultrasound study was performed at baseline to evaluate the location and degree of fibrosis, foreign body granulomas, surgical plates, screws, or wires, and 3 months after the final treatment to evaluate the therapeutic outcomes and adverse events of ESWT. An extremely high‐density linear transducer (L3‐12X; Alpinion) was used at a frequency of 3–12 MHz for measuring maximally 3.5 cm deep. Two blinded investigators compared photographs captured before and after the treatment and ultrasonographic images in a non‐chronological order and performed objective clinical assessments of deep dermal and subdermal fibrosis. We used a grading scale for evaluation (Grade 1, <20% improvement; Grade 2, 21%–40% improvement; Grade 3, 41%–60% improvement; Grade 4, 61%–80% improvement; and Grade 5, >80% improvement).

Three months after the final treatment, patients were asked to score their overall satisfaction (score 0: unsatisfied; score 1: <20% satisfied; score 2, 21%–40% satisfied; score 3, 41%–60% satisfied; score 4, 61%–80% satisfied; and score 5: >80% satisfied). The patients were also asked to report the side effects of the treatment, including pain, swelling, blistering, oozing, petechiae, bruising, hematoma, posttherapy dyschromia, scaling or crusting, erythema, and aggravation of inflammatory acne lesions and their duration.

Results

ESWT for Fibrosis on the Face and Neck

The mean number of pulses for F‐SWT on the cheek, temple, and chin was 2600.0 ± 1040.8 shocks/session and for R‐SWT was 5080.0 ± 2234.6 pulses/session, and the number of treatment sessions was 8.0 ± 4.4 (Figures 1 and 2). The mean grade of objective clinical improvement was 3.9 ± 0.8, and mean patients’ satisfaction score was 3.8 ± 0.9. Meanwhile, the mean number of pulses for F‐SWT on the nose was 1500.0 ± 491.6 shocks/session and for R‐SWT was 2142.9 ± 1602.1 pulses/session, and the number of treatment sessions was 6.6 ± 1.8. The mean grade of objective clinical improvement was 3.9 ± 1.3, and mean patients’ satisfaction score was 3.8 ± 1.3. The treatment setting with respect to energy intensity was reduced when treating the forehead or glabellar area with a thin subcutaneous fat layer compared to other facial areas. This was done as patients felt discomfort due to strong and loud vibrations during ESWT. The efficacy of ESWT on facial fibrosis after plastic surgery was also revealed by ultrasonography which showed improved fibrotic tissues with the realignment of dislocated postsurgical bone fragments and wires (Figure 3).

ESWT for Fibrosis on the Trunk and Extremities

In patients who were treated with ESWT on the abdomen, the mean number of pulses for F‐SWT was 2600.0 ± 2408.3 shocks/session and for R‐SWT was 8400.0 ± 894.4 pulses/session, and the number of treatment sessions was 3.2 ± 1.6 (Figure 5). The mean grade of objective clinical improvement was 3.8 ± 0.8, and mean patients’ satisfaction score was 3.6 ± 1.1. The mean number of pulses for F‐SWT on the breast was 2000.0 ± 1414.2 shocks/session and for R‐SWT was 4000.0 ± 2828.4 pulses/session, and the number of treatment sessions was 7.5 ± 3.5. The mean grade of objective clinical improvement was 5.0 ± 0.0, and mean patients’ satisfaction score was 5.0 ± 0.0. Meanwhile, the mean number of pulses for F‐SWT on the arm was 4500.0 ± 2121.3 shocks/session and for R‐SWT was 9000.0 ± 4242.6 pulses/session, and the number of treatment sessions was 3.5 ± 0.7. The mean grade of objective clinical improvement was 3.9 ± 1.3, and mean patients’ satisfaction score was 2.5 ± 0.7. In the patient who was treated with ESWT on the ankle, the number of pulses for F‐SWT was 5000 shocks/session and that for R‐SWT was 10 000 pulses/session. The grade of objective clinical improvement was 5 and the patient satisfaction score was 4.

Side Effects

Pain during ESWT in the cheek, temple, or chin lesions was generally tolerated without any topical or systemic anesthesia. Moreover, the energy level was regulated according to the patient's response to treatment. When ESWT is delivered to the skin on the gingiva, orthodontic braces, or dental prosthetics, few patients experienced sharp pain, however it was mostly tolerable. In patients who had metal plates, screws, or wires on the maxillary and mandibular bones after surgery, the level of energy was set at 10%–20% lower than that in other parts from the beginning and gradually increased according to the patients’ tolerability. Post‐ESWT edema was found more frequently after R‐SWT than after F‐SWT but was transient and self‐limited. Post‐ESWT numbness and tingling sensations were experienced in a limited number of cases and were self‐limiting. Nonetheless, other possible adverse events, including prolonged erythema; edema; post‐therapy vesicle or bullae formation; crusting; scarring; hyper‐ or hypopigmentation; secondary bacterial, viral, or fungal infection; and permanent nerve damage, were not encountered.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the clinical efficacy and safety of ESWT by retrospectively analyzing 52 patients who underwent ESWT for dermal and subdermal fibrosis due to various causes, including surgery, liposuction, fat transplantation, filler injection, thread‐lifting, and trauma. Our patient demonstrated heterogeneous clinical features of dermal and subdermal fibrosis, which involved various tissue layers, ranging from the dermis and subcutaneous fat to the fascia and subfascial muscles. Nonetheless, regardless of the cause and clinical features, patients exhibited satisfactory clinical improvements in pathological fibrosis conditions with functional restoration after repetitive F‐SWT, R‐SWT, or a combination of F‐SWT and R‐SWT. ESWT delivers physical forces that generate biochemically stimulatory effects on treated tissues. Thus, various ESWT‐induced biological effects have been reported to modulate the wound repair process, including angiogenesis, neovascularization, anti‐inflammation, growth factor release, mesenchymal stem cell activation, and tissue‐specific progenitor cell differentiation [7, 15‐18].

Most of our patients were treated with F‐SWT for fibrotic lesions, followed by R‐SWT in every session, and the number of pulses per session for R‐SWT was generally set to double that of shocks per session for F‐SWT. F‐SWT has been shown to electromechanically stimulate cells and fibrotic tissues and enhance cellular metabolism, while R‐SWT pneumatically removes the disintegrated tissue and increases blood and lymphatic microcirculation [10, 19]. A previous study, which comparatively evaluated the functional outcomes of R‐SWT and a combination of F‐SWT and R‐SWT, demonstrated that both methods were similarly effective in the treatment of fasciitis [20]. Meanwhile, the other study revealed that both F‐SWT and R‐SWT were effective for treating tendinopathy without calcification, however long‐term effectiveness was superior in F‐SWT compared to R‐SWT [21]. However, the superiority of F‐SWT, R‐SWT, or a combination of F‐SWT and R‐SWT for treating each type of inflammatory or fibrotic lesions still needs to be elucidated.

Our cases of facial dermal and subdermal fibrosis after surgery or invasive cosmetic procedures were commonly accompanied by unnatural facial expressions, as the fibrotic changes extended over the layers of the fascia and subfascial muscles. Therefore, F‐SWT, which can intensely target deeper tissues, was necessary for patients in combination with R‐SWT to achieve satisfactory results. Immediately after delivering R‐SWT to the lesions, especially on the face and neck, the treated areas were transiently and mildly swollen. Therefore, to deliver F‐SWT accurately to the fibrotic lesions and deeply to the layers of the fascia and subfascial muscles, F‐SWT is generally performed prior to R‐SWT. Meanwhile, for the treatment of dermal and subdermal fibrotic lesions in the periorbital areas, including the eyelids, R‐SWT is preferred. However, some cases with postsurgical or procedural nodulous dermal and subdermal fibrotic lesions on the lower eyelids were effectively treated with F‐SWT without R‐SWT.

Post‐fat‐graft fibrosis is a major complication that presents as accumulation of a disorganized thick extracellular matrix and inhibits the metabolic function of the fat tissues [22, 23]. In the present study, most of the abdominal dermal and subdermal fibrosis lesions were effectively improved only with R‐SWT without combining with F‐SWT, however a greater number of pulses were delivered to abdominal lesions compared to the lesions on the other sites. In our study, the baseline ultrasonographic study showed that the fibrotic lesions were diffusely located in the subcutaneous fat layers of the abdomen without noticeable involvement of the fascia or subfascial muscular layers. Although a combination of F‐SWT and R‐SWT might result in better clinical outcomes, we suggest that the use of high‐dose R‐SWT is more cost‐effective in patients with abdominal dermal and subdermal fibrosis after liposuction. We also suggest that the use of a titanium transmitter (D20‐T; Storz Medical AG) with an intensity of 9/10, rather than a steel transmitter (D20‐S; Storz Medical AG) with an intensity of 7/10, may increase the efficacy of the delivery of short and radial shock pulses into the skin with severe abdominal fibrosis. Nonetheless, for post‐liposuction dermal and subdermal fibrosis in the extremities, which also cause injured nerves, muscles, and/or lymphatic tissues, a combination of F‐SWT and R‐SWT is needed to improve clinical outcomes.

A previous in vivo experimental rat study demonstrated that ESWT improved the axonal regeneration of injured peripheral nerves [24]. The other study found that ESWT resulted in the reinnervation of nerve fibers and endings after inducing the degeneration of pathological sensory nerves [25]. Studies have suggested that ESWT increases the interaction between integrins and extracellular matrix molecules and Schwann cell proliferation [24, 25, 26]. In this study, a female patient had facial dermal and subdermal fibrosis with nerve damage after several plastic surgeries for removing Artecoll‐induced foreign body granuloma. The patient presented with drooping of the right side of the mouth, uneven nodular and fibrotic skin, and subcutaneous facial expressions. After five sessions of combined F‐SWT (5000 shocks/session) and R‐SWT (10 000 pulses/session), the overall uneven fibrotic skin of the patient notably improved without remarkable adverse events. Moreover, the patient's unnatural facial expressions and mouth drooping were also markedly improved, which could have resulted from the effects of ESWT on perineural fibrotic scar tissues. Nonetheless, because our patient reported subjective improvements in pain and dysesthesia, we suggest that ESWT significantly affected the restoration of damaged peripheral sensory and motor nerve fibers.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that F‐SWT and/or R‐SWT effectively improved the clinical appearance and functional movements of 52 patients with dermal and subdermal fibrosis caused due to various reasons. We suggest that ESWT can be used as an alternative treatment for dermal and subdermal fibrosis without additional surgical management. Moreover, ESWT can be performed in both the early and late stages of posttraumatic or surgical pathological fibrosis. However, because the data in our study were obtained by retrospectively analyzing medical records, clinical photos, and ultrasound study images of heterogeneous groups of patients, ESWT indications and parameters for treating dermal and subdermal fibrosis were not conclusive. Therefore, additional prospective controlled studies should be conducted to optimize ESWT parameters for treating pathologically fibrotic conditions in the deep skin and subcutis, with or without the involvement of the fascia and subfascial muscles.
