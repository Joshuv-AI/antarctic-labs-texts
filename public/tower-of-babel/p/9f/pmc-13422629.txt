What is the mechanism of chronic threshold elevation after His-bundle pacing? A histopathological case report suggesting the involvement of fibrin fibrosis
Authors: Kenji Baba, Shogo Sakamoto, Moritoshi Irishio, Toru Kataoka, Daiju Fukuda, Fabian Barbieri, Panteleimon E Papakonstantinou, Ugur Canpolat, Christine Mages, Armen Kocharian, Lorenzo Caratti di Lanzacco, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHis-bundle pacing (HBP) enables physiological ventricular activation and has been associated with superior clinical outcomes compared with conventional pacing. Nevertheless, chronic threshold elevation remains a major limitation, often leading to early battery depletion or lead revision. The pathological mechanisms underlying chronic threshold elevation have not been fully elucidated.Case summaryA 76-year-old man with symptomatic 2:1 atrioventricular block underwent successful HBP implantation, achieving selective His-bundle capture. One week after implantation, both His-bundle and right ventricular pacing thresholds increased markedly. Although the His-bundle capture threshold transiently improved during follow-up, it deteriorated again by 1 year and stabilized. After 5 years and 5 months, early battery depletion prompted extraction of the ventricular lead and re-implantation using left bundle branch pacing. Histopathological examination of the extracted lead tip revealed fibrin-rich tissue with fibroblast proliferation and CD31-positive microvessels. Numerous CD68-positive macrophages were present at the lead–tissue interface. No myocardial or conduction system tissue was identified, which may be consistent with development of a physical barrier between the lead tip and the His-bundle.DiscussionThis case raises the hypothesis that early fibrin deposition followed by organization may contribute to chronic HBP threshold elevation by increasing the separation between the lead tip and the His-bundle. We speculated that fibrotic tissue interposed between the screw and the His-bundle progressively enlarged, leading to micro-dislodgement and subsequent threshold elevation. To our knowledge, this is one of the first reports describing the histopathological findings of a case with chronic threshold deterioration following HBP.Keywords: Case report, Pacemaker, His-bundle pacing, Conduction system pacing, Chronic threshold deterioration, Histopathological examination

Introduction

Conduction system pacing (CSP) provides more physiological intracardiac electrical activation and ventricular contraction than conventional pacing modalities and has therefore been increasingly adopted because of its favourable effects on clinical outcomes and the prevention of left ventricular systolic dysfunction.1 Among CSP strategies, His-bundle pacing (HBP) directly captures the native His-Purkinje system and achieves the most physiological pattern of ventricular activation.2 Previous studies have demonstrated lower rates of mortality and heart failure hospitalization with HBP than with conventional pacing.3 However, chronic pacing threshold elevation remains a major limitation of HBP, occurring in ∼7%–11% of cases and often leading to premature battery depletion or the need for lead revision.3–5 Beer et al.6 identified lead slack configuration as a predictor of chronic threshold elevation, while Sato et al.7 reported an association between the depth of the negative His potential at implantation and subsequent threshold increases. We previously reported that an early increase in right ventricular pacing thresholds predicted subsequent chronic deterioration of HBP thresholds.8 Proposed mechanisms include local inflammation, fibrosis, and micro-dislodgement9; however, pathological evidence supporting these mechanisms remains limited. Here, we present a case of progressive HBP threshold deterioration requiring lead extraction, which allowed histopathological evaluation of the tissue adhered to the lead tip.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old man was admitted with heart failure accompanied by exertional fatigue. Electrocardiography revealed a 2:1 atrioventricular block. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function without evidence of cardiomyopathy, and contrast-enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed no abnormal late gadolinium enhancement. Based on these findings, the atrioventricular block was considered non-structural in origin.

Given the presence of symptomatic 2:1 atrioventricular block, permanent pacemaker implantation was indicated in accordance with the current guidelines.10 A high ventricular pacing burden was anticipated; therefore, HBP was selected and was successfully achieved (Figure 1A). At implantation, the non-selective and selective HBP capture thresholds were 2.25 and 1.25 V/0.4 ms, respectively (Figure 1B).

Chest radiograph and electrocardiogram at pacemaker implantation. (A) Chest radiograph obtained immediately after pacemaker implantation, demonstrating placement of the right ventricular lead at the His-bundle. (B) Two distinct pacing morphologies observed at implantation. The non-selective His-bundle pacing threshold was 2.25 V/0.4 ms, and the selective His-bundle pacing threshold was 1.25 V/0.4 ms.

One week after implantation, both the His-bundle and right ventricular pacing thresholds increased markedly, to 3.75 and 3.0 V/0.4 ms, respectively. Evaluation of the chest radiograph obtained in the standing position revealed a reduction in lead slack (Figure 2). Although lead revision was considered, the patient declined reintervention. Accordingly, the pacing output was increased, and the pulse width was prolonged to 0.8 ms.

Comparison of ventricular lead slack between immediately after implantation and standing chest radiographs obtained 1 week following the procedure. While the ventricular lead demonstrated a U-shaped slack configuration on chest radiography obtained in the supine position immediately after implantation, standing chest radiography performed 1 week after implantation revealed a reduction in lead slack.

Thereafter, the HBP threshold transiently remained at 0.5 V/0.8 ms, but subsequently deteriorated again to ∼3.0 V/0.8 ms by 1 year after implantation and remained stable. In contrast, the right ventricular myocardial pacing threshold stabilized at ∼1.5 V/0.8 ms (Figure 3A). Annual chest radiographs were performed to assess lead position, and no positional changes were observed. Lead impedance gradually decreased during the early post-implantation period and subsequently stabilized (Figure 3B). The ventricular pacing percentage remained at 100%.

Temporal changes in His-bundle and right ventricular myocardial pacing thresholds and lead impedance. (A) Serial changes in His-bundle pacing and right ventricular myocardial pacing thresholds of the ventricular lead from 3 months after implantation to lead extraction, assessed at a pulse width of 0.8 ms. The chronic-phase His-bundle pacing threshold deteriorated by 1 year after implantation. (B) Serial changes in ventricular lead impedance. No increase in impedance was observed throughout the follow-up period.

Despite progressive battery depletion, His-bundle capture was prioritized, and the device output was maintained at 4.0 V/0.8 ms. At 5 years and 5 months after implantation, early battery depletion occurred. The patient declined lead addition, and lead extraction was performed. As the lumen-less lead (SelectSecure 3830, 69 cm, Medtronic Inc.) could not be removed by traction or screw-out, the connector was cut, and the lead extended using a Bulldog lead extender (Cook Medical Inc.). A 12-Fr laser sheath was required to dissect adhesions extending to the level of the tricuspid valve, after which the ventricular lead was successfully extracted using simple traction (Figure 4). A mass of tissue was found adhered to the lead tip. The lead was successfully extracted without the laser sheath advancing over the lead tip; therefore, it was considered unlikely that tissue from the superior vena cava or right atrium had migrated to the lead tip. Left bundle branch pacing was subsequently performed, with stable pacing thresholds.

Fluoroscopic images during lead extraction. Adhesions in the left subclavian vein, superior vena cava, and right atrium were dissected using a 12-Fr laser sheath, followed by extraction of the ventricular lead using simple traction. The lead was successfully extracted without the lead tip entering the laser sheath. Based on this finding, the tissue adherent to the screw tip was considered to have originated from tissue adjacent to the His-bundle.

Histopathological evaluation of the adherent tissue was undertaken (Figure 5A). Haematoxylin–eosin staining demonstrated fibrin-rich tissue (Figure 5B). CD31-positive microvascular structures suggested angiogenesis (Figure 5C). Numerous CD68-positive macrophages were observed at the tissue–lead interface (Figure 5D). No myocardial or conduction system tissue was identified.

Tissue adherent to the extracted lead tip and histopathological evaluation. (A) Tissue adhesion was incidentally observed on the screw tip at the time of ventricular lead extraction. (B) Haematoxylin–eosin staining (4× magnification) demonstrated abundant fibrin-rich tissue without evidence of myocardial or conduction system tissue. At the interface between the lead and the adherent tissue, accumulation of inflammatory cells suggestive of chronic inflammation, including macrophages and monocytes, was observed. Granulation tissue was also identified in the outer layer. (C) CD31 immunohistochemical staining (10× magnification) demonstrated CD31-positive microvascular structures within the fibrin layer, suggesting angiogenesis. (D) CD68 immunohistochemical staining (10× magnification) demonstrated scattered to clustered infiltration of numerous CD68-positive macrophages within the fibrin layer and granulation tissue, suggesting ongoing tissue reactions related to chronic inflammation and fibrin organization and degradation.

Discussion

Chronic pacing threshold elevation remains a clinically important limitation of HBP, as it may result in premature battery depletion and the need for reintervention. Although inflammation, fibrosis, and micro-dislodgement have been proposed as potential mechanisms, histopathological confirmation was lacking. To our knowledge, this case represents one of the first histological evaluations of tissue associated with chronic HBP threshold deterioration. The adherent tissue consisted predominantly of fibrin, with evidence of ongoing organization and macrophage-mediated chronic inflammation. Importantly, no myocardial or conduction system tissue was identified.

The initial threshold elevation observed 1 week after implantation is a commonly encountered phenomenon following conventional pacemaker lead implantation. Crossley et al.11 demonstrated that the introduction of steroid-eluting leads attenuated transient acute-phase threshold elevation, supporting the concept that early threshold increases are primarily related to acute inflammatory responses. Accordingly, the early threshold rise observed in our case was most likely attributable to acute inflammation. Furthermore, Kołodzińska et al.12 reported that inflammatory fibrin deposition can develop at the lead tip as early as 1 week after cardiac implantable electronic device implantation. In the setting of HBP, the allowable lead implantation area is extremely limited, and the target tissue is firm, often making deep screw fixation challenging. Consequently, even minimal fibrin deposition around the lead tip may substantially affect the HBP threshold.

The transient improvement in pacing thresholds during mid-term follow-up suggests partial resolution of the acute inflammatory response. However, we speculate that the subsequent late threshold elevation beginning ∼1 year after implantation may have resulted from progressive organization and proliferation of residual fibrin tissue. Previous studies have shown that fibrin surrounding pacemaker leads can undergo organization driven by chronic macrophage-mediated inflammation, often without significant changes in lead impedance.13,14 Consistent with these observations, lead impedance remained stable throughout long-term follow-up in the present case.

In a previously reported autopsy case with preserved HBP thresholds, peri-lead organization was limited, with minimal fibrin tissue and a lead tip–His-bundle distance of ∼0.5 mm, indicating close proximity.15 In contrast, the present case demonstrated abundant fibrin tissue formation (≥1.5 mm) without identifiable conduction system tissue, raising the hypothesis that physical separation between the His-bundle and the screw electrode may have contributed to chronic pacing threshold deterioration.

Previous reports have suggested that insufficient lead slack may contribute to chronic HBP threshold deterioration.6 In the present case, insufficient slack was also observed, raising the possibility that lead orientation may have contributed to increased separation from the His-bundle.

This report is limited by its single-case nature. In addition, as histological evaluation was restricted to tissue obtained from the extracted lead, increased distance between the His-bundle and lead tip could not be directly confirmed. Alternative mechanisms, including lead dislodgement or lead damage, therefore cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, our findings may support the hypothesis that the use of steroid-eluting leads to suppress local inflammation and appropriate lead fixation with optimal orientation towards the His-bundle could potentially help prevent chronic threshold deterioration.

Conclusion

Histopathological analysis of tissue adherent to an extracted HBP lead demonstrated fibrin deposition with subsequent organization and chronic inflammation. These findings raise the hypothesis that the formation of organized fibrin tissue may have contributed to chronic HBP threshold deterioration.
