Efficacy and safety of HRS-7535, an oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist, in patients with diabetic kidney disease (SOLID-DKD): a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe efficacy of non-peptide small-molecule glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists in diabetic kidney disease remains uncertain, particularly as add-on therapy to contemporary high-intensity treatments. We assessed HRS-7535, a novel oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist, in this population.MethodsSOLID-DKD was a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial conducted at 72 clinical sites in China. Adults with diabetic kidney disease (urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio [UACR] 300–<3000 mg/g; estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] ≥30 mL/min per 1.73 m2) were randomly assigned (1:1:1) via a central interactive web-response system, stratified by baseline UACR, concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor use, and finerenone use to once-daily oral HRS-7535 (30 mg or 90 mg) or placebo for 16 weeks. The primary endpoint was the relative change in UACR from baseline to week 16. This trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06415214) and is completed.FindingsBetween June 21, 2024, and March 30, 2025, 281 participants were randomised; 280 received at least one dose (30 mg: n = 93; 90 mg: n = 94; placebo: n = 93). Baseline median UACR was 763 mg/g; 181 (64.6%) participants were receiving SGLT2 inhibitors and 68 (24.3%) were receiving finerenone. At week 16, the placebo-corrected reduction in UACR with 90 mg was −32% (95% CI −43 to −18; treatment-policy estimand [intention-to-treat]) and −38% (−49 to −24; efficacy estimand [on-treatment analysis]); with 30 mg, −14% (−29 to 4) and −19% (−34 to −2), respectively. Compared with placebo, the mean difference in glycated haemoglobin with HRS-7535 90 mg was −1.09% (−1.32 to −0.86) and in body weight was −2.95% (−3.94 to −1.95). Adverse events were primarily mild-to-moderate gastrointestinal events during dose escalation. Serious adverse events occurred in 5 (5.4%), 4 (4.3%), and 2 (2.2%) participants in the 30 mg, 90 mg, and placebo groups. Discontinuation due to adverse events occurred in 3 (3.2%), 1 (1.1%), and 0 participants, respectively. No deaths occurred.InterpretationHRS-7535 dose-dependently reduced albuminuria in patients with diabetic kidney disease receiving intensive contemporary therapy, with a short-term safety profile consistent with the GLP-1 receptor agonist class, supporting its evaluation in phase 3 renal outcome trials.FundingJiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for randomised controlled trials published in English between Jan 1, 2000, and Feb 1, 2026, with the terms “GLP-1 receptor agonist”, “small molecule”, “non-peptide”, and “diabetic kidney disease”. While injectable semaglutide has established renal benefits, and oral small molecules like orforglipron show metabolic efficacy, proof-of-concept for the specific renal efficacy of oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonists remains absent. Furthermore, as the standard of care for diabetic kidney disease rapidly evolves, the additive renoprotective potential of this class on top of contemporary high-intensity care, particularly in combination with sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, represents a critical knowledge gap.

Added value of this study

This study provides the first clinical evidence for the effects of HRS-7535, a novel oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist, in patients with diabetic kidney disease. Distinct from historical trials, this substantial albuminuria-lowering effect was achieved against a background of high-intensity contemporary therapy, with 65% of participants on SGLT2 inhibitors and 24% on finerenone at baseline. In addition to reducing albuminuria, HRS-7535 treatment reduced body weight and glycated haemoglobin, and was associated with trends towards improvements in blood pressure, lipid profile, and liver enzymes. The short-term safety profile was consistent with the GLP-1 receptor agonist class.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings suggest that oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonists have the potential to emerge as a potent and accessible therapeutic alternative to peptide-based injectables for managing diabetic kidney disease. The reduction in albuminuria demonstrated by HRS-7535 indicates therapeutic promise and provides a rationale to advance this agent into large-scale confirmatory phase 3 trials.

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease is the leading cause of end-stage kidney disease worldwide, affecting approximately 40% of the 589 million adults living with diabetes.1,2 In recent years, the standard of care has evolved from renin–angiotensin system (RAS) inhibition alone to a multitargeted approach, incorporating sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and finerenone to mitigate disease progression.2,3 Despite these therapies, a substantial residual risk of kidney failure and cardiovascular death persists, as demonstrated in multiple pivotal outcome trials.4, 5, 6

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are emerging as a foundational class of therapeutics for cardiorenal protection, a role validated by the FLOW trial with injectable semaglutide.7,8 However, widespread adoption of peptide-based therapies is hindered by significant limitations. Injectable formulations impose administration burdens and cold-chain requirements, while current oral peptides have low bioavailability and high manufacturing costs, limiting their accessibility in resource-limited settings.9,10 Moreover, oral peptides rely on absorption enhancers that necessitate strict fasting and staggered administration with other therapies, which impedes adherence in patients with diabetic kidney disease who manage complex polypharmacy.9,11 Although novel oral small molecules offer a convenient alternative and have demonstrated efficacy in glycaemic control and weight management,12, 13, 14 proof-of-concept for specific renal efficacy is lacking.

Furthermore, the additive renoprotective benefit of GLP-1 receptor agonism on top of contemporary high-intensity guideline-directed medical therapy has not been adequately established. Existing data rely primarily on subgroup analyses of landmark trials, which were not specifically powered to evaluate combination outcomes and are constrained by limited event rates and historical treatment standards.15, 16, 17 This evidence gap hinders the definition of optimal combination and rapid-sequence initiation strategies for comprehensive guideline-directed medical therapy in clinical practice.16

HRS-7535 is a novel oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist with high oral bioavailability that permits administration without food or timing restrictions, potentially offering superior flexibility and scalability.18 In this proof-of-concept and dose-finding phase 2 trial, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of HRS-7535 in patients with diabetic kidney disease and severely increased albuminuria who were receiving optimised guideline-directed medical therapy.

Methods

Trial design and oversight

The SOLID-DKD (Small-molecule Oral GLP-1 receptor agonist Lowering Intensive-treatment-resistant albuminuria in type 2 Diabetes with Diabetic Kidney Disease) trial was a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial conducted at 72 sites in China (Appendix pp 2–4). The study protocol and statistical analysis plan are available online. The SOLID-DKD trial is prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06415214) and the trial is completed.

Ethics

The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The protocol was approved by the Clinical Trial Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University College of Medicine ([2024] Ethics Approval No. 165 and No. 498), and by the institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each participating site. All participants provided written informed consent before any study-related procedures.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18–75 years with a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes and diabetic kidney disease. Key inclusion criteria included an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 30 mL/min per 1.73 m2 or higher and a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) between 300 mg/g and 3000 mg/g, which was confirmed by at least two samples collected on separate days during the screening period. Participants were required to have a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) level between 6.5% and 10.0% and a body-mass index (BMI) between 20.0 kg/m2 and 40.0 kg/m2.

All participants were required to have received a stable dose of a RAS inhibitor for at least 4 weeks before screening, except in cases of documented medical contraindications or clinical intolerance (e.g., history of hyperkalaemia or hypotension). Concomitant use of SGLT2 inhibitors and/or finerenone was permitted if treatment had been initiated at least 3 months before screening. Key exclusion criteria included type 1 diabetes, acute kidney injury or dialysis within 6 months, acute diabetic complications or severe hypoglycaemia within 6 months, significant gastrointestinal disease, a history of pancreatitis, or a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer. Participants who had received any GLP-1 receptor agonist within 4 weeks prior to screening were also excluded. Participants were recruited from outpatient nephrology and endocrinology clinics at the 72 participating sites.

Randomisation and masking

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio via a central interactive web-response system to receive oral HRS-7535 at a dose of 30 mg or 90 mg, or matching placebo once daily. To maintain masking, all participants took three tablets daily, comprising active HRS-7535 30 mg tablets or matching placebo tablets in different combinations. Participants, investigators, and all study personnel remained masked to treatment assignment throughout the trial. Randomisation was stratified by screening UACR (≤1000 mg/g vs. >1000 mg/g), baseline use of SGLT2 inhibitors (yes vs. no), and baseline use of finerenone (yes vs. no).

Procedures

The study included a screening period of up to 3 weeks, a 16-week double-blind treatment period, and a 2-week safety follow-up period (Appendix p 5). Following randomisation, participants attended in-person visits at weeks 4, 8, 12, 16, and 18. Participants in the 30 mg group received a constant dose for 16 weeks. The 90 mg group underwent a dose-escalation regimen (30 mg, 60 mg, and 90 mg at 4-week intervals) to the target dose; down-titration was permitted to manage individual tolerability. Background renoprotective therapies were to be maintained at stable doses until week 16, whereas other glucose-lowering agents were adjusted as needed for safety.

Visit procedures included recording vital signs, blood and urine sampling, and collecting information on adverse events, concomitant therapies, and treatment adherence. Race was self-reported by the participants according to prespecified categories. To minimise intra-individual variability, the UACR values at baseline and week 16 were derived from three separate measurements: two morning spot urine samples and one 24-h urine collection collected on different days. The UACR value was defined as the geometric mean of these three measurements if the coefficient of variation (CV) was 25% or less, or as the median if the CV exceeded 25%. For all other visits, UACR was assessed using a single morning spot urine sample. All UACR assessments were performed by a central laboratory. The only exceptions were the initial screening and post-treatment safety follow-up visits, where testing at local laboratories was allowable.

Outcomes

The primary objective was to assess the superiority of at least one dose of HRS-7535 compared with placebo in reducing albuminuria. The primary efficacy outcome was the relative change from baseline in UACR at week 16. Secondary efficacy outcomes included the proportion of participants achieving a UACR reduction of ≥30% and specific HbA1c targets (<7.0%, ≤6.5%, and <5.7%), as well as changes in 24-h urinary albumin and protein indices, HbA1c, body weight and eGFR over time. Exploratory outcomes included the change from baseline in daily insulin dose at week 16, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) assessments in a prespecified sub-cohort, specifically evaluating global kidney oxygenation via apparent transverse relaxation rate (R2∗) using blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) MRI, and perfusion via renal blood flow using arterial spin labelling (ASL) MRI. Safety outcomes included investigator-reported adverse events, episodes of hypoglycaemia, vital signs, 12-lead electrocardiograms, and laboratory assessments. Prespecified adverse events of special interest included severe hepatotoxicity, clinically significant calcitonin abnormalities, acute pancreatitis, and severe hypoglycaemia.

Statistical analysis

A sample size of 267 participants (89 per treatment group) provided 75% power to detect an estimated 23.6% reduction in the geometric mean of UACR for HRS-7535 compared with placebo. The calculation was based on an assumed standard deviation of 0.8 for the change in log-transformed UACR and accounted for a 15% incidence of intercurrent events. A one-sided alpha level of 0.10 was applied, consistent with a phase 2 proof-of-concept design. Efficacy analyses were performed in the full analysis set, defined as all randomised participants who received at least one dose of study treatment.

In accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation E9(R1) addendum, two estimands were defined. The primary analysis used an efficacy estimand (on-treatment analysis) to assess the pharmacological effect of the drug as intended. Data collected after the following intercurrent events were excluded: premature discontinuation of study treatment, initiation of prohibited medications with a potential impact on efficacy, and discontinuation of background renoprotective therapies. Additionally, data were excluded from visits with insufficient treatment exposure, defined as an actual cumulative dose <40% of the protocol-defined target dose for that interval or plasma concentrations below the limit of quantification at the corresponding visit. A supplementary analysis using a treatment-policy estimand (intention-to-treat) was performed to evaluate the treatment effect in a real-world setting. In this analysis, all available data were included without any exclusion or censoring following intercurrent events. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation; specifically, a Copy Reference approach based on a missing not at random (MNAR) assumption was applied to participants in the HRS-7535 groups experiencing intercurrent events, while a missing at random (MAR) assumption was used for other missingness. To interrogate the robustness of the primary analysis, prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed, including scenarios where data during periods of insufficient treatment exposure were retained and scenarios applying a stricter definition of insufficient exposure (actual cumulative dose <80% of target).

The primary endpoint was analysed using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) on log-transformed UACR. The model included log-transformed baseline UACR as a covariate, with treatment group, visit, treatment-by-visit interaction, and stratification factors as fixed effects. Results were back-transformed to geometric least-squares mean ratios. Treatment differences are reported as the percentage difference relative to placebo. Secondary continuous endpoints were analysed using MMRM or analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model, while binary endpoints were analysed using logistic regression adjusting for treatment group, baseline value, and stratification factors. Prespecified subgroup analyses for the primary endpoint were evaluated using the same MMRM approach, augmented with the relevant subgroup variable and its corresponding interaction terms (treatment-by-subgroup and treatment-by-visit-by-subgroup) as fixed effects to estimate treatment differences and exploratory interactions within each category. Due to the phase 2 proof-of-concept design, analyses of primary, secondary, and exploratory endpoints were not adjusted for multiplicity; nominal p-values and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported for estimation purposes rather than definitive hypothesis testing. All analyses were performed with SAS software, version 9.4.

Role of the funding source

The funder was involved in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, and writing of the report. Corresponding authors had full data access and final responsibility for the decision to submit for publication.

Results

Between June 21, 2024, and March 30, 2025, 426 participants were screened across 72 clinical sites in China, of whom 281 were randomised (Fig. 1). Overall, 280 participants received at least one dose of the study drug and were included in the full analysis set and the safety set (n = 93 in the 30 mg group, n = 94 in the 90 mg group, and n = 93 in the placebo group). At week 16, treatment with HRS-7535 was associated with dose-dependent reductions in albuminuria (Fig. 2A). Based on the treatment-policy estimand (intention-to-treat), the geometric least-squares mean percentage change in UACR from baseline was −44% (95% CI –51 to −35) for the 90 mg group, −29% (−39 to −19) for the 30 mg group, and −18% (−29 to −5) for the placebo group. The corresponding placebo-corrected reduction was −32% (95% CI –43 to −18; p < 0.0001) for the 90 mg dose and −14% (−29 to 4; p = 0.11) for the 30 mg group (Fig. 2A). These findings were highly consistent when evaluating the intrinsic pharmacological effect using the efficacy estimand (on-treatment analysis), where the geometric least-squares mean percentage change in UACR from baseline was −46% (95% CI –54 to −38) in the 90 mg group and −30% (−40 to −20) in the 30 mg group, compared with −14% (−25 to −1) in the placebo group. The placebo-corrected reduction under this estimand was −38% (95% CI –49 to −24; p < 0.0001) for the 90 mg dose and −19% (−34 to −2; p = 0.031) for the 30 mg dose (Fig. 2B and Appendix p 6). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the albuminuria-lowering efficacy of HRS-7535 was robust across the majority of prespecified clinical categories (Fig. 3). Regarding concomitant medications, the renoprotective effect appeared consistent irrespective of baseline finerenone use. However, a nominal interaction was observed regarding the baseline use of SGLT2 inhibitors (p-interaction = 0.03); the placebo-corrected UACR reduction with the 90 mg dose was −60% (95% CI –71 to −44) in non-users versus −22% (−39 to −1) in users (baseline characteristics of these subgroups are provided in Appendix p 20). A post hoc analysis stratified by the number of background renoprotective agents showed a graded attenuation of the treatment effect. Reductions in 24-h urinary albumin and protein indices showed a similar pattern compared to UACR (Appendix p 13). Treatment with HRS-7535 also resulted in improvements in metabolic control. At week 16, the mean placebo-corrected change in HbA1c was −0.71% (95% CI –0.94 to −0.48) with the 30 mg dose and −1.09% (95% CI –1.32 to −0.86) with the 90-mg dose (Fig. 4A); reductions were also observed in fasting plasma glucose (Appendix p 14). More participants achieved HbA1c targets of <7.0%, ≤6.5%, and <5.7% with HRS-7535 than with placebo (Fig. 4B). Regarding body weight, the mean placebo-corrected change was −0.61% (95% CI –1.60 to 0.37) with the 30 mg dose, while the 90 mg dose resulted in a mean placebo-corrected change of −2.95% (95% CI –3.94 to −1.95), with no evidence of a plateau by week 16 (Fig. 4C). The placebo-corrected differences in fasting serum insulin were −1.51 μU/mL (95% CI –7.22 to 4.19) for the 30 mg group and −0.10 μU/mL (−5.80 to 5.60) for the 90 mg group. For fasting serum C-peptide, the placebo-corrected differences were 0.20 ng/mL (−0.24 to 0.64) and 0.38 ng/mL (−0.08 to 0.84) for the 30 mg and 90 mg groups, respectively (Appendix p 14).

In the HRS-7535 groups, 24-h urinary creatinine levels remained stable from baseline to week 16 (geometric mean change: +0.5% [95% CI –7.7 to 9.3] with 30 mg and −0.5% [95% CI –8.0 to 7.6] with 90 mg). In contrast, a mean reduction of −9.3% (95% CI –15.8 to −2.2) was observed in the placebo group (Appendix p 7). In exploratory post-hoc correlation analyses involving pooled HRS-7535 participants, no correlation was observed between the reduction in UACR and changes in 24-h urinary creatinine (r = 0.01, p = 0.88). Regarding metabolic and haemodynamic parameters (Appendix p 8), UACR reduction exhibited moderate-to-weak correlations with weight loss (r = 0.34, p < 0.001), HbA1c improvements (r = 0.16, p = 0.041), and the eGFR decline (r = 0.15, p = 0.048). No significant correlation was found with changes in fasting plasma glucose (r = 0.13, p = 0.090). In the placebo group, body weight was the only parameter significantly associated with UACR reduction (r = 0.25, p = 0.023).

A mild, transient decline in the eGFR was observed during dose escalation, followed by a recovery (Fig. 4D). Consequently, no apparent difference from placebo was observed with the 30 mg dose (−1.57 ml/min per 1.73 m2; 95% CI –4.24 to 1.11) or the 90-mg dose (1.14 ml/min per 1.73 m2; 95% CI –1.59 to 3.88) at week 16. Furthermore, in the prespecified fMRI sub-cohort, exploratory assessments indicated no statistically significant macroscopic shifts in global kidney oxygenation or perfusion at week 16 (Appendix pp 14–15).

Discussion

In this phase 2 trial involving participants with diabetic kidney disease who had severely increased albuminuria, the novel oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist HRS-7535 elicited dose-dependent reductions in UACR. Using the treatment-policy estimand (intention-to-treat), which evaluates the treatment effect in a real-world setting regardless of intercurrent events, the 90 mg dose achieved a placebo-corrected reduction of 32%, a magnitude commensurate with the efficacy of injectable semaglutide reported in the FLOW trial.8 When evaluating the pharmacological effect using the efficacy estimand (on-treatment analysis), the 90 mg dose achieved a 38% placebo-corrected reduction. Given that a 20%–30% reduction in albuminuria is recognised as a validated surrogate endpoint that reliably predicts a delay in chronic kidney disease progression,19 these data extend the therapeutic promise of the GLP-1 receptor agonist class from peptide-based therapies to oral small molecules. Crucially, this incremental albuminuria-lowering effect was demonstrated against a background of contemporary intensive standard-of-care, involving significantly higher baseline usage of SGLT2 inhibitors (64.6% vs 15.6% in FLOW) and finerenone (24.3% vs 0% in FLOW).8 By addressing the residual risk that persists despite optimised guideline-directed therapy, our findings provide a rationale for further exploring the role of oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonists within the evolving contemporary treatment landscape for diabetic kidney disease.

A modest UACR reduction was also observed in the placebo group, likely attributable to natural biological variation, regression to the mean in a cohort selected for elevated UACR, and optimised adherence under trial monitoring (Hawthorne effect).20 The placebo-corrected trajectory therefore provides a more accurate representation of the intrinsic pharmacological effect of HRS-7535 (Appendix p 6). Notably, our trial design facilitates the differentiation between time-dependent and dose-dependent effects. In the 30 mg constant-dose group, the anti-albuminuric effect rapidly reached a plateau within 4 weeks. In contrast, the 90 mg group exhibited a progressive deepening of UACR reduction that closely tracked its stepwise dose-escalation regimen, suggesting that the maximal efficacy of HRS-7535 might not have fully saturated at 90 mg. This dose-response trajectory is consistent with observations in the SMART trial, where higher obesity-targeted doses of semaglutide yielded greater albuminuria reductions than standard diabetes-specific doses.21 Given that doses of HRS-7535 up to 180 mg have already demonstrated manageable tolerability in obesity trials,12 these distinct dose-response dynamics provide a rationale for evaluating higher doses in future outcome trials to potentially achieve additional renoprotective benefits.

Complementing this renal efficacy, the 90 mg dose of HRS-7535 elicited substantial metabolic benefits, including robust glycaemic control and progressive weight loss. The glycaemic improvement is particularly notable given the high baseline treatment intensity in this cohort, with 60.4% of participants receiving insulin. Accordingly, a concomitant reduction in daily insulin dose was observed, suggesting the potential for simplifying insulin regimens in this population. The weight reduction with the 90 mg dose was gradual and sustained, with no evidence of a plateau by week 16. Nevertheless, 24-h urinary creatinine excretion remained stable in the HRS-7535 groups, whereas a decline was observed in the placebo group (−9.3%). Although this divergence might imply that the weight loss induced by HRS-7535 was primarily driven by fat mass reduction with relative preservation of lean tissue, the lack of formal body composition imaging limits definitive conclusions on muscle mass. From a methodological perspective, the stability of urinary creatinine ensures the accuracy of the UACR assessment, confirming that the observed reductions were not confounded by fluctuations in the creatinine denominator. This robust measurement validity is corroborated by the complete lack of correlation between changes in these two parameters (r = 0.01).

Furthermore, the renoprotective effect appeared partially uncoupled from systemic metabolic improvements. Consistent with mediation analyses of the SURPASS, LEADER, and SUSTAIN-6 trials in type 2 diabetes,22,23 and clinical observations from the SMART trial in non-diabetic cohorts,21 UACR reductions in our study correlated only modestly with HbA1c improvement (r = 0.16) and weight loss (r = 0.34), with no correlation observed for fasting plasma glucose. Weight loss was also associated with UACR reduction in the placebo group (r = 0.25). This contrast suggests that while metabolic improvements contribute to albuminuria lowering, they do not fully explain the potent efficacy of HRS-7535. Moreover, haemodynamic factors appear to explain only a fraction of this metabolism-independent renal benefit, as UACR reduction was only weakly associated with the change in eGFR (r = 0.15). Further evidence for a non-haemodynamic mechanism stems from our follow-up data after treatment cessation. During the 2-week washout period, UACR levels exhibited a partial rebound but remained numerically below baseline values. This prolonged effect, also noted in the SMART trial,21 is unlikely to be explained by reversible haemodynamic modulation given the rapid pharmacokinetic clearance of the short-acting HRS-7535 molecule compared with once-weekly peptide agonists. Nonetheless, these exploratory correlations cannot establish causal pathways. Formal mediation analyses are warranted in confirmatory trials.

To investigate potential intra-renal functional shifts, we evaluated global kidney perfusion and oxygenation via exploratory MRI. While BOLD-MRI and ASL-MRI did not detect statistically significant macroscopic changes in oxygenation or perfusion at week 16, this exploratory analysis is inherently underpowered due to the relatively small sample size in this imaging sub-cohort, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the presence or absence of intra-renal haemodynamic shifts. Nevertheless, these exploratory findings align with the REMODEL trial.24 In that study, 52 weeks of semaglutide treatment similarly did not induce significant alterations in these specific parameters despite a larger cohort of 106 participants, but substantially downregulated inflammatory and fibrotic transcriptomic pathways in kidney biopsies.24 Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis of a sustained direct tissue-level renal effect of GLP-1 receptor agonism, likely including the attenuation of intra-renal inflammation and oxidative stress, alongside the preservation of endothelial integrity.25 To definitively elucidate these pathways and overcome the mechanistic limitations of the current trial, the incorporation of exploratory inflammatory and fibrotic biomarkers is warranted in future confirmatory trials.

We observed a graded attenuation in UACR reduction related to background treatment intensity, primarily driven by concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor use (60% reduction in non-users versus 22% in users). This trend is similar to the numerical pattern reported in the FLOW trial (34% versus 24%).15 This pattern likely reflects overlapping therapeutic mechanisms, including shared metabolic benefits and haemodynamic modulation via tubuloglomerular feedback.26 The 22% UACR reduction achieved on top of SGLT2 inhibition is highly consistent with the observation in both the FLOW trial (24%) and a trial evaluating semaglutide added to empagliflozin (22%),15,27 suggesting a reproducible and incremental anti-albuminuric benefit, aligning with recent meta-analyses demonstrating additive kidney benefits.28 Conversely, the pronounced 60% reduction in non-users suggests HRS-7535 may offer a therapeutic alternative for patients intolerant to SGLT2 inhibitors. However, this subgroup analysis lacked adjustment for multiplicity, rendering the nominal interaction susceptible to type I error. Furthermore, the magnitude of reduction in non-users may be confounded by the small sample size and baseline phenotypic differences compared with SGLT2 inhibitor users (Appendix p 20). Future prospective outcome trials utilising formal factorial designs, analogous to the CONFIDENCE study,29 are warranted to definitively dissect this pharmacological interaction while controlling for baseline variances.

In contrast to the partial attenuation observed with SGLT2 inhibitors, the efficacy of HRS-7535 appeared consistent in combination with finerenone, indicating that their mechanisms of action are likely independent and complementary. Theoretically, whereas finerenone primarily targets mineralocorticoid receptor-mediated inflammation and fibrosis alongside haemodynamic modulation, GLP-1 receptor agonists address metabolic drivers while exerting distinct effects on vascular function.25,30 However, as with the SGLT2 inhibitor analysis, this subgroup comparison was not formally powered to detect interactions, and these hypothesis-generating findings warrant confirmation in adequately powered phase 3 trials.

The short-term safety profile of HRS-7535 was consistent with the known class effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists. Gastrointestinal adverse events were frequent but mostly mild-to-moderate and transient, occurring primarily during dose escalation with prevalence diminishing over time. With individualised dose adjustments, most participants were able to maintain treatment at their maximum tolerated dose. Hepatotoxicity led to the discontinuation of lotiglipron and danuglipron, and has been a potential concern with small-molecule oral GLP-1 receptor agonists. In the current trial, no hepatotoxicity signal was observed; aminotransferase levels decreased with the 90 mg dose, and no participant receiving HRS-7535 had elevations exceeding three times the upper limit of normal. Furthermore, no adverse psychiatric effects were observed, as evidenced by stable PHQ-9 depression scores across all treatment groups.

Regarding renal safety, an initial dip in eGFR was observed during dose escalation, followed by recovery by week 16. This pattern is consistent with the transient haemodynamic effects characteristic of the GLP-1 receptor agonist class, though the early decline appeared somewhat more pronounced than that reported with semaglutide in the FLOW trial,8,31 potentially reflecting the amelioration of glomerular hyperfiltration in the 38% of participants with baseline eGFR ≥90 mL/min per 1.73 m2. Interpretation of these eGFR changes is further limited by the reliance on creatinine-based estimation without cystatin C confirmation or directly measured GFR. This finding warrants further evaluation in adequately powered studies. No evidence of increased risk for acute kidney injury or hyperkalaemia was observed, the latter standing in contrast to RAS inhibitors and finerenone, for which hyperkalaemia remains a dose-limiting concern.

Distinct from oral peptide GLP-1 receptor agonists, which require absorption enhancers and strict administration protocols,9 the small-molecule HRS-7535 exhibits a conventional absorption profile.18 This characteristic enables fixed-dose combinations to reduce pill burden and offers superior scalability. Such attributes are vital for expanding treatment access in resource-limited settings where the global burden of diabetic kidney disease is most pronounced, potentially overcoming the implementation barriers associated with the complexity and cost of peptide-based therapies.

Our trial has certain limitations. First, the study was conducted exclusively in a Chinese population, limiting immediate generalisability across diverse racial and ethnic populations and warranting future multi-regional trials to address potential unmeasured sociocultural confounders. Nevertheless, our participants' clinical characteristics and high-intensity background therapies align closely with contemporary clinical practice for diabetic kidney disease (Appendix pp 10–11). Second, the relatively short duration of 16 weeks limited the assessment of clinical hard endpoints or eGFR slopes. Additionally, the statistical power for subgroup and sub-cohort analyses was limited by sample size. Thus, the long-term impact on kidney disease progression will be evaluated in the forthcoming phase 3 RENEW-CKD trial (NCT07625774). Key strengths include the rigorous standardisation of high-intensity background therapy, representing contemporary standard-of-care. Furthermore, the collection of 24-h urine samples significantly enhanced the accuracy and reliability of the primary endpoint assessment.

In conclusion, among patients with diabetic kidney disease receiving contemporary intensive background therapy, HRS-7535 demonstrated substantial, dose-dependent reductions in albuminuria alongside comprehensive metabolic benefits. The renoprotective efficacy operates at least partially independent of systemic metabolic improvements, coupled with a manageable short-term safety profile and the convenience of an oral formulation. These findings provide a compelling rationale to advance HRS-7535 into larger confirmatory outcome trials.
