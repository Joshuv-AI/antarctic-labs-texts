Contralateral language network integration predicts and protects against naming decline after temporal lobe resection
Authors: Karl‐Heinz Nenning, Karin Trimmel, Lisa Bartha‐Doering, Marc Berger, Matthias J Koepp, Georg Langs, Gregor Kasprian, John S Duncan, Silvia B Bonelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAnterior temporal lobe resection (ATLR) is an effective treatment for drug‐resistant temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) but carries a substantial risk of language impairment, particularly in naming. Understanding and predicting the impact of ATLR on language functions remains a major clinical challenge.MethodsIn this study, we used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to investigate the short‐term effects of ATLR on the organization of the functional language connectome with a focus on the role of the nondominant hemisphere. We studied 44 patients with TLE due to unilateral hippocampal sclerosis (24 left, 20 right) who underwent language fMRI and neuropsychological testing preoperatively and 4 months after ATLR. We examined functional connectivity changes pre‐ and postsurgery and their relationship with neuropsychological performance.ResultsATLR induced widespread alterations in functional connectivity, with distinct ipsilateral disruptions and contralateral compensatory changes. Left ATLR reduced mainly interhemispheric temporal connectivity, whereas right ATLR primarily affected bilateral frontal connections. Postoperatively, left ATLR showed increased intrahemispheric frontotemporal connectivity, and right ATLR exhibited more widespread intra‐ and interhemispheric increases. In left TLE, better preoperative naming was associated with stronger connectivity of the right (nondominant) temporal lobe and between the left inferior frontal cortex and bilateral posterior hippocampi. Postoperatively, reduced right frontotemporal integration was linked to greater naming decline, underscoring a compensatory role of the nondominant hemisphere. Finally, a machine learning model using preoperative functional connectivity fingerprints outperformed demographic and clinical variables in predicting clinically significant naming decline following ATLR.SignificanceOur findings highlight the critical role of the right temporal lobe in supporting naming function after left ATLR and suggest that preoperative assessment of its connectivity may improve prediction of postoperative language outcomes.Keywords: ATLR, functional connectivity, naming, outcome prediction, temporal lobe epilepsy

INTRODUCTION

For drug‐resistant focal epilepsy, surgical resection is an effective treatment alternative, potentially offering seizure control, prevention of disease progression, and protection against further neuropsychological decline. 1 In particular in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), anterior temporal lobe resection (ATLR) has consistently been associated with favorable seizure outcomes. 2 , 3 However, ATLR carries a substantial risk of disrupting critical cognitive functions, particularly language and memory abilities. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Epilepsy is increasingly recognized as a network disorder that extends beyond the focal seizure onset zone and involves distributed brain regions. 8 , 9 Surgical removal of epileptogenic tissue therefore may induce widespread network changes, with potential behavioral and cognitive consequences. 10 Evidence suggests that contralateral networks contribute to cognitive outcomes following surgery, 11 , 12 but their role in supporting language functions remains poorly understood. This is particularly relevant as language is typically left‐lateralized yet relies on distributed and dynamic networks across the brain that change across the lifespan. In focal epilepsy, atypical language lateralization is more frequent, 13 , 14 suggesting language reorganization or compensatory recruitment as also observed in children with early onset epilepsy in the left hemisphere. 15

In this study, we employed functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to investigate the following: (1) how the functional language connectome is altered in TLE before and after unilateral ATLR, (2) the specific role of contralateral networks in maintaining language functions before and after surgery, and (3) whether individual functional connectivity fingerprints can predict naming decline following ATLR. Given that confrontation naming is the most frequent and severe cognitive deficit following dominant ATLR, we defined naming decline as our primary clinical endpoint.

The study cohort was part of a previously published study, 16 comprising 44 individuals with medically refractory TLE due to unilateral hippocampal sclerosis (24 left, 20 right), who underwent ATLR at the National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery, London. All patients had qualitative assessment by expert neuroradiologists, including the evaluation of hippocampal volumes and T2 relaxation times confirming unilateral hippocampal sclerosis and normal contralateral medial temporal lobe structures. All patients were treated with antiseizure medication at the time of their assessment, and medication remained unchanged in 34 patients, whereas in 10 patients the doses were slightly reduced by the time of the postoperative assessment. The standard neurosurgical procedure was removal of the temporal pole and opening of the temporal horn, followed by en bloc resection of the hippocampus with a posterior resection margin at the mid brainstem level. One year following epilepsy surgery, 32 patients (19 with left TLE) achieved complete seizure freedom or experienced only auralike seizures (International League Against Epilepsy [ILAE] class 1–2), whereas 11 patients (5 with left TLE) experienced seizures with or without worthwhile improvement (ILAE class 3–4).

MRI data acquisition

MRI data acquisition was performed on a 3‐T General Electric Excite HDx scanner, equipped with an eight‐channel head coil. The functional data were acquired with a gradient‐echo planar T2*‐weighted sequence. Each volume comprised 58 contiguous 2.5‐mm oblique axial slices positioned to maximize coverage of the temporal and frontal lobes, with a 24‐cm field of view, 96 × 96 matrix reconstructed to 128 × 128 for an in‐plane resolution of 1.88 × 1.88 mm. Echo time was 30 ms, and repetition time was 4.5 s.

In‐scanner language task

A clinically well‐established and frequently used verbal fluency (VF) paradigm was performed by all individuals during fMRI. 16 The paradigm consisted of a block design setting with 30‐s activation blocks alternating with 30 s of crosshair fixation over a duration of 5.5 min. During the VF task blocks, subjects were asked to covertly generate different words beginning with a visually presented letter (A, D, E, S, and W).

Preoperative lateralization indices (LIs) were established with the SPM LI‐Toolbox, 17 which leverages a bootstrapping approach to obtain robust and specific lateralization indices. For each subject, the LI was calculated based on a combined mask of the inferior frontal gyrus and middle frontal gyrus and ranged from −1 (fully left lateralized) to 1 (fully right lateralized). To ensure clear left language dominance, left language dominance was defined by a preoperative LI of ≤−.4. 16

Out‐of‐scanner language tests

All individuals in the study cohort had out‐of‐scanner neuropsychological language testing, including phonemic VF and the McKenna graded naming test. 18 Neuropsychological assessment was performed before and 4 months after ATLR. The change in testing scores (postoperative − preoperative scores) was used to indicate postoperative language deficits. Clinically, a postoperative decline in naming was defined as a change of >3 points, which represented a decline of at least 15 centiles. A clinically significant VF decline was defined as change of >1 SD.

Additionally, verbal intelligence quotient (IQ) was measured prior to surgery using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, 3rd edition.

Data preprocessing

Standard data preprocessing was performed with SPM8. 19 In brief, preoperative images were realigned using the mean image as a reference, slice‐timing corrected, and spatially normalized into standard Montreal Neurological Institute space using a scanner‐specific Echo Planar Imaging (EPI) template generated from the cohort with 2‐mm isotropic voxel size. 20 Postoperative acquisitions were aligned to the preoperative mean image and spatially normalized by applying the subject‐specific preoperative transformation. To ensure the validity of the EPI‐based normalization, particularly in the presence of postoperative anatomical deformations and resection cavities, spatial alignment was verified through visual inspection for all subjects. No spatial smoothing was applied, because analysis was based on regions of interest (ROIs), which implicitly acts as smoothing, and not to add any confounding connectivity between spatially neighboring ROIs.

The fMRI data were denoised via nuisance regression including six motion parameters and their first derivatives, the average white matter and the average cerebrospinal fluid signals, and their first derivatives, and a high pass filtering with .01 Hz was applied. To remove global effects of task onset and to account for underlying neural activity only, we additionally added the task paradigm to the nuisance regression model. 21 This procedure minimizes the contribution of stimulus‐locked coactivation, allowing for the assessment of intrinsic background connectivity that sustains task performance.

Manual segmentation of resection zones, as well as the anterior and posterior hippocampus, was performed. To quantify ATLR‐related effects on hippocampal connectivity, the anterior hippocampus was defined as the part of the hippocampus that was removed during ATLR, and the posterior hippocampus was defined as the part that remained after surgery.

Network construction

First, 200 ROIs were derived from Schaefer's functional cortical parcellation. 22 We then used Neurosynth to pinpoint language‐relevant ROIs by generating a language‐specific activation map based on the meta‐analysis of 1101 fMRI studies. 23 The map was thresholded at z > 3.1 and, due to the left lateralization, mirrored to incorporate homologous regions in the right hemisphere. A parcel was considered to be language relevant when more than 75% of the delineated voxels were found to be active in the meta‐analysis map. This conservative threshold was selected to prioritize network specificity, ensuring that only regions with a high probability of involvement in core language processing were included in the analysis. This resulted in 50 ROIs (Figure S1), comprising language‐related regions in the inferior frontal cortices (IFCs), temporal lobes, supplementary motor areas, inferior parietal lobes, fusiform gyrus, and parahippocampal gyrus, as well as the anterior and posterior hippocampus (grouped together as hippocampal formation).

For each ROI, the average time series of the corresponding voxels was calculated. For the postoperative data, voxels segmented as part of the resection zone were omitted, and ROIs with more than 50% of voxels labeled as resection zone were excluded from further analysis. This threshold was established to balance topological preservation with signal stability. Retaining ROIs with varying degrees of resection allows for network continuity, whereas regions with >50% volume loss were considered too compromised to yield a representative mean time series. Because the used functional parcellation is asymmetric, 22 three ROIs were excluded for patients with left TLE and two for right TLE in the postoperative data.

Finally, the functional connectivity between all language‐relevant ROIs was defined as the Fisher z‐transformed Pearson correlation coefficient between their respective time series.

Connectome analysis

All statistical tests were based on a general linear model (GLM) within the network‐based statistics (NBS) framework. 24 In brief, NBS finds connected network components based on a predefined suprathreshold (keeping only connections with p < .01 uncorrected) and calculates component‐based test statistics characterized by its extent. This initial cluster‐forming threshold was chosen to remain sensitive to the subtle, spatially distributed network attenuations characteristic of TLE. Although stricter thresholds are often used to identify focal effects, they can fragment the broad, distributed components central to global network integration. The component‐based test statistics are then recalculated multiple times with randomly permuted group memberships and subsequently compared to the result of the true grouping. We generated an empirical null distribution using 10 000 permutations. The p‐value of each observed component was calculated as the proportion of permutations in which the maximum randomized test statistic was greater than or equal to the observed statistic. This ascribes a p‐value controlled for the familywise error rate to each connected component. We denote the p‐values obtained with this procedure as p NBS.

Within‐group analysis (preoperative vs. postoperative) was performed based on a paired t‐test and between group analysis (decliners vs. nondecliners) with a two‐sample t‐test. Similarly, we performed regression analysis to find network components that are associated with naming performance pre‐ and postoperatively. For all tests, age, sex, and verbal IQ were entered as confounders in the GLM.

Prediction analysis

We evaluated whether seed‐based functional connectivity could classify patients into two groups: decliners (clinically significant naming decline) and nondecliners. We used logistic regression implemented in GLMNet 25 with nested cross‐validation to assess predictive performance while mitigating the risk of overfitting inherent in modest sample sizes. For each of the 50 ROI‐specific models, the input features consisted of the connectivity strengths between the seed ROI and the 49 other regions. In the outer loop, we used a leave‐one‐patient‐out scheme to estimate model generalization, and an inner fivefold cross‐validation loop was utilized to optimize the regularization parameter lambda for the L1‐penalized logistic regression (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) on the training data. Model performance was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and permutation testing (1000 label shuffles) was performed to establish a null distribution. For comparison, we trained a parallel model predicting decline based on standardized demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, disease duration, age at onset, hippocampal volumes, language lateralization, preoperative naming, phonemic fluency, and verbal IQ).

RESULTS

Neuropsychological language test results

Out‐of‐scanner neuropsychological language test results can be found in Table 1.

Preoperative language test results in left and right TLE

Compared to healthy controls, we observed significantly lower preoperative naming scores for left (t = 6.50, p < .001) and right (t = 3.63, p = .001) TLE. Left TLE exhibited notably lower naming scores than right TLE before ATLR (t = −2.39, p = .021) and an even stronger difference after ATLR (t = −4.54, p < .001).

In contrast, preoperative VF scores were lower than controls in left (t = 3.22, p = .003) but not in right (t = 1.10, p = .280) TLE. VF scores between left and right TLE were not different preoperatively (t = −1.74, p = .089) nor postoperatively (t = −1.32, p = .193).

Postoperative language change in left TLE

Left TLE patients showed significantly reduced naming scores 4 months after surgery (t = 3.55, p = .002). Postoperatively, naming declined in 14 of 24 patients, and in 12 of those 14 this decline was classified as clinically significant. Five patients showed a postoperative improvement in naming, classified as clinically significant in one, and five patients remained unchanged.

For VF, no significant postoperative change was observed (t = −.77, p = .450). Single subject analysis revealed a postoperative decline in VF in nine of 24 left TLE patients; of those, three were classified as clinically significant. Thirteen left TLE patients showed a postoperative improvement, with five classified as clinically significant, and two patients remained unchanged.

Postoperative language change in right TLE

Right TLE patients showed no significant change postoperatively for naming (t = −.42, p = .678) nor for VF (t = −.46, p = .652). For naming, seven of 20 right TLE patients exhibited a non‐clinically significant decline after ATLR, five patients showed a postoperative improvement in naming, which was classified as clinically significant in two, and eight right TLE patients had unchanged test scores.

For VF, 10 of 20 right TLE patients showed reduced scores postoperatively, with two classified as clinically significant. Eight patients showed a postoperative improvement, with two classified as clinically significant, and one patient remained unchanged. One patient did not complete the VF test postoperatively.

FUNCTIONAL IMAGING RESULTS

We applied network‐based statistics to investigate group differences in functional connectivity, identifying components of interconnected edges with significantly altered connectivity strength. We focused our primary brain–behavior analysis on naming performance in the left TLE cohort, as this represents the most vulnerable clinical function in dominant TLE and was the only domain to exhibit a significant postoperative decline in our cohort. Associations with VF were examined as a secondary endpoint.

Increased connectivity in left and right TLE after ATLR

After ATLR, increased connectivity was observed in both left (p NBS = .035) and right (p NBS = .002) TLE (Figure 1B).

In contrast to postsurgical decreases, right TLE exhibited a more extensive increase in connectivity (156 edges in the observed network component; Cohen d: component = 2.99, edges = .45–.85) than left TLE (78 edges; Cohen d: component = 1.94, edges = .45–.91). Left TLE notably showed increased frontotemporal intrahemispheric connectivity within both the ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres, as well as increased connectivity within the right IFC.

In right TLE, more widespread increases in connectivity were observed, affecting both intra‐ and interhemispheric connections. This was especially prominent for connections from left and right IFC to both temporal lobes.

Connectivity fingerprinting predicts individual post‐ATLR naming decline in left TLE

Seed‐based connectivity of individual ROIs showed the utility of functional connectivity patterns to preoperatively predict significant cognitive decline after ATLR (Figure 4). Using logistic regression, we found 10 of 50 ROIs that had a predictive performance better than chance (permutation test p < .05 uncorrected), for which the AUC ranged from .72 (95% confidence interval [CI] = .52–.93) to .90 (95% CI = .77–1.00). Those ROIs were primarily located in the left hemisphere, but also in the right IFC. The highest predictive performance (AUC = .90, 95% CI = .77–1.00) was achieved with an ROI in the left temporal lobe (L‐Temp‐4). Notably, this was the only region that remained significant after correcting for multiple comparisons across all tested ROI‐specific models (false discovery rate [FDR]‐corrected p < .05). For this ROI, the prediction model revealed connections to the posterior part of the ipsilateral hippocampus as well as the right temporal lobe to be discriminative between patients with and without naming decline after ALTR. Overall, the predictive connectivity fingerprints of seven of those 10 ROIs showed an involvement of the right temporal lobe. Despite the spatial proximity of these seeds, an analysis of their predictive features revealed a mean Dice score of only .12 (SD = .19), indicating that these ROI‐specific fingerprints retain a substantial degree of independence and capture nonredundant predictive information. DISCUSSION

In this longitudinal study of individuals with unilateral mesial TLE, we investigated the early postoperative effects of ATLR on the organization of the functional language connectome and specifically the role of the nondominant hemisphere 4 months after surgery. We found a brain‐wide impact of ATLR with ipsi‐ and contralateral changes in functional connectivity and observed associations between functional connectivity and out‐of‐scanner naming, driven by the functional integration of the nondominant temporal lobe. Moreover, we leveraged a machine learning model to identify preoperative functional connectivity fingerprints that could reliably predict clinically significant naming decline after ATLR, outperforming conventional clinical and demographic measures.

Early postoperative effects of ATLR on the functional language connectome

In both left and right TLE, anterior temporal lobe resection had a widespread impact on the functional language connectome, with distinct group‐specific differences. In left TLE, ATLR led to reduced interhemispheric connectivity, primarily between left and right IFC and between both temporal lobes. In contrast, right TLE showed less extensive disruption, mainly affecting connectivity of the left and right IFC.

Given the interconnected nature of the brain, it is not surprising that neurosurgical resection results in an altered functional or structural connectivity. 10 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Prior work showed that more regional alterations were primarily linked to seizure outcome, 30 whereas more global network disruptions were associated with worse neuropsychological performance. 31 As resected brain areas were excluded from the analysis, the widespread effects of left ATLR likely reflect downstream reorganization of the language network, underscoring the central role of the left temporal lobe in language processing. 32 , 33 , 34 Consistent with previous studies, 35 , 36 , 37 clinically significant naming decline was observed mainly in patients with left TLE, further emphasizing the critical role of the dominant temporal lobe within the intact language network.

Notably, ATLR did not only result in reduced connectivity. Distinct patterns of increased connectivity were observed in both groups, left and right TLE, postoperatively. A widespread increase in connectivity was found in patients with right TLE, involving intra‐ and interhemispheric frontotemporal networks. Intriguingly, left TLE exhibited primarily intrahemispheric increases in frontotemporal connectivity within both hemispheres. Impaired frontotemporal connectivity is a commonly observed characteristic of left TLE, 38 , 39 , 40 and reduced connectivity has been associated with poorer naming performance. 41 , 42 Previous studies suggest the brains' potential for short‐term plasticity within the language network, 43 and cortical reorganization has been reported as early as 6 months after ATLR during a verb‐generation task. 44 However, the relatively short follow‐up period of 4 months in our cohort suggests that the observed increases likely reflect functional normalization of previously impaired networks within the ipsilateral hemisphere, alongside possible compensatory mechanisms contralaterally. 12 , 45

Role of the contralateral hemisphere in left TLE patients

Patients with TLE often show atypical language network organization, with increased right hemisphere involvement. 13 , 15 , 46 , 47 , 48 Consistent with this, a significant contribution of the right hemisphere during a language task was also revealed in our study, in particular when comparing left TLE patients with and without clinically significant decline in naming. Specifically, patients without significant decline showed stronger right temporal lobe involvement with stronger intralobular connectivity but also stronger contralateral frontotemporal connectivity with the left IFC. These findings suggest that integration of the right hemisphere—particularly the right temporal lobe—into language networks supports the preservation of naming performance after surgery.

There is converging evidence that semantic–lexical aspects of language rely, at least in part, on right hemispheric regions. 49 , 50 , 51 In this context, involvement of the right hemisphere may benefit naming, a complex language function requiring both retrieval and integration of phonological and semantic information. 52 , 53 Although most of the previous studies demonstrating this link included only healthy individuals, atypical language organization has been demonstrated more frequently in patients with TLE. 54 , 55 It is important to note that our study consisted only of patients with typical left‐dominant language lateralization, suggesting that here, right hemisphere involvement indicates increased cognitive flexibility that is associated with a better naming performance rather than atypical language representation. Our findings align with and extend limited prior evidence that stronger integration of the right temporal lobe into language networks can compensate for impaired left hemispheric connectivity. 14 , 50 , 56 This could explain why reduced preoperative right temporal connectivity predicted a significant naming decline following left ATLR. The determinants of such contralateral integration remain unclear but may include age at epilepsy onset, baseline language lateralization, genetic factors, severity of hippocampal sclerosis, and underlying pathology.

The potential importance of the right, nondominant, hemisphere was further supported by our findings of the regression analysis. Before left ATLR, better naming performance was associated with stronger bilateral frontotemporal connectivity, including both posterior hippocampi, and stronger right temporal–left IFC connectivity. Notably, in patients with significant naming decline, greater disintegration of the right temporal lobe was associated with poorer naming outcomes after ATLR. In our previous activation‐based study, we identified the ipsilateral posterior hippocampal remnant as critical for preserving language function. 16 Our present findings extend this work by highlighting the additional role not only of the posterior hippocampal connectivity but also of the right temporal lobe and its connectivity profile, further underscoring the importance of nondominant hemisphere contributions to language resilience.

Our findings demonstrate a robust association between stronger right temporal connectivity and better postoperative naming outcomes. Although we interpret this as evidence of compensatory recruitment, consistent with previous models of interhemispheric plasticity in aphasia and epilepsy, we acknowledge that correlation does not imply causation, and alternative mechanisms may contribute to this relationship. Although we included only patients with strictly left language lateralization to account for premorbid differences in language organization due to underlying disease, variability in disease burden (i.e., seizure frequency etc.) could play a role. Given the 4‐month postoperative window, it remains unclear whether these connectivity profiles represent a permanent reorganization or a transient stage in the recovery trajectory. Future longitudinal studies with extended postoperative follow‐ups are essential to disentangle transient surgical effects from stable functional compensation.

Preoperative functional connectivity fingerprinting as predictor of individual postoperative outcome

At the group level, our findings revealed a robust correlation between preoperative functional connectivity and clinically significant naming decline following ATLR. Using machine learning, we demonstrated that the preoperative functional connectivity profiles of even a single seed region were predictive of naming decline, with 10 of 50 seed regions showing strong predictive value. Interestingly, although most predictive seed regions were located in the language dominant hemisphere (8/10), the model consistently assigned greater weight to interhemispheric connections. In particular, pathways involving the right temporal lobe, especially frontotemporal connections linking left IFC to the right temporal regions, emerged as significant. These results align with previous studies indicating that structural connectivity to contralateral regions plays a key role in mitigating post‐ATLR naming decline. 57 Consistent with prior functional imaging studies, our results indicate that connectivity patterns bypassing the medial temporal lobes, specifically those anchored in the left inferior frontal gyrus and extratemporal networks, are key determinants of postoperative language 58 and memory 59 , 60 outcomes. Although only the left temporal lobe (L‐Temp‐4) remained significant after stringent FDR correction, the identification of several additional predictive candidates highlights the potential of distributed language networks to inform outcome prediction. However, these exploratory findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and require independent validation in larger datasets to confirm the clinical utility of the regions that did not survive multiple comparison correction. Importantly, predictions based solely on demographic and clinical variables performed no better than chance, highlighting the added predictive value of individual functional connectivity profiles. Taken together, these findings underscore the clinical potential of preoperative fMRI‐based connectivity fingerprinting as a tool for outcome prediction. Such approaches could complement existing structural connectivity markers 61 , 62 , 63 and ultimately inform individualized treatment planning in epilepsy surgery.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, the VF task was performed covertly, and although all participants showed typical activation patterns, this approach did not allow real‐time monitoring of task performance or compliance. Furthermore, the VF task might be less sensitive than other paradigms for detecting associations with naming performance. Although the longitudinal study design and homogeneity of the patient group are significant strengths, replication in larger and more diverse cohorts is essential to fully assess the predictive potential of preoperative functional connectivity. In this context, we acknowledge that our seed‐based prediction models were exploratory in nature. Larger sample sizes will be required to validate the clinical utility of such biomarkers and to disentangle the effects of ongoing seizure activity from postsurgical connectivity changes. Although independent ROI‐based models do not explicitly account for shared variance among collinear networks, this approach was prioritized over high‐dimensional multivariate modeling to preserve the anatomical specificity required for translating functional connectivity patterns into clinical practice. We also acknowledge that specific analytic choices, including EPI‐based normalization, ROI definition, postoperative exclusion thresholds, and nuisance regression strategies, represent methodological constraints. Although these choices were intended to maximize specificity and signal fidelity, they may influence the observed extent of network reorganization. Establishing the optimal framework for handling postoperative structural and functional heterogeneity remains an important objective for future methodological research. Finally, our study focused on short‐term postoperative effects at 4 months, and longer follow‐up will be essential to clarify mechanisms of long‐term reorganization and plasticity.

CONCLUSIONS

Taken together, our findings highlight the added clinical value of functional connectivity analyses in the preoperative evaluation of language function in TLE. In left TLE, a stronger integration of the right nondominant temporal lobe into the language network supported naming performance. Ultimately, individualized preoperative connectivity fingerprinting may provide a powerful tool for predicting surgical outcomes and guiding treatment decisions.
