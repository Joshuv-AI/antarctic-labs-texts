Short-term effects of ambient air pollution on musculoskeletal diseases in Yangzhou during 2019–2022
Authors: Hu Zhang, Bing Wang, Wen Xiong, Junpeng Xia, Chao Zhao, Ruijun Dong, Lihong Yin, Qianqian Wang, Kaiyue Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the limited research on the association between air pollutants and musculoskeletal diseases (MDs), our study aimed to examine how exposure to air pollutants influenced the risk of developing musculoskeletal disorders.MethodsWe extracted daily air pollutants and musculoskeletal diseases data from 2019 to 2022 in Yangzhou. The combined effects of air pollutants particulate matte 2.5 (PM2.5), PM10, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulphur dioxide (SO2), ozone (O3) and nitric oxide (NO) were comprehensively assessed. Logistic regression modelling was firstly applied to evaluate the association between air pollutants and the incidence of MDs. The joint effect of multiple air pollutants was further explored using Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) modelling. In order to deeply investigate the risk effects of heavy metals in PM2.5 on MDs, we also explored the association between heavy metals in PM2.5 and MDs.ResultsAmong the individual air pollutants, SO2 (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.08–4.24) and O3 (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.13–2.62) had the most significant effects on the elevated risk of MDs. It was found that SO2, O3 and PM10 had a positive joint effect on the risk of developing MDs when the levels of other air pollutants were higher than the 55th percentile. Furthermore, the logistic regression and BKMR model analyses showed that the heavy metals Selenium (Se, OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.22–0.68), Cadmium (Cd, OR = 0.62, 95% CI. 95% CI: 0.40–0.97), Antimony (Sb, OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.29–0.88), and Thallium (Tl, OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.35–0.85) exerted a negative joint effect on the risk of developing MDs.ConclusionOur findings indicated that short-term exposure to ambient air pollutants may elevate the risk of MDs, with particular attention warranted to the health impacts of SO2 and O3 emissions in urban areas. In addition, the heavy metals in PM2.5 exerted negative relationship with the risks of MDs, which provided the new sights of the protective role of heavy metals against bone disease.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23600-8.Keywords: Air pollution, Musculoskeletal diseases, Mixed exposure, BKMR analysis

Introduction

At present, air pollution has become a public health issue of global concern [1]. Air pollution exposure has contributed to a substantial increase in the incidence and death rates of most diseases [2]. Air pollution has emerged as a major contributor to non-communicable diseases worldwide. In line with the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, ambient fine PM2.5 pollution was identified as a significant factor which accounted for 1.42 million premature deaths in China alone and contributed to 34.3% of the global disease burden [3]. Extensive research has demonstrated that both prolonged and acute exposure to air pollution elevated the risk of emergency room visits, hospital admissions, and premature mortality [4]. In accordance with the World Health Organisation (WHO), ambient air pollution posed a significant global health challenge. Recent research suggested that the detrimental effects of ambient air pollution on skeletal disorders were substantial and appeared to be growing over time [5]. The GBD study estimated the burden of diseases associated with ambient air pollution using PM2.5 and ozone as quantitative indicators of population exposure, and previous studies have confirmed that these two indicators were reliable and accurate predictors of adverse disease outcomes [6].

Since the 1980 s, researchers have suggested a link between air pollution and bone health. Current evidence has highlighted the strong association between long-term air pollution exposure and skeletal disorders, including bone mineral density (BMD), osteoporotic fractures, and the development of osteoporosis [7]. The human musculoskeletal system consisted of bone, cartilage, tendons, muscles and joints, etc. This system served crucial protective and supportive functions for the body, yet musculoskeletal disorders could significantly impair its integrity, presenting a major global health burden [8]. Musculoskeletal disorders had a complex etiology, ranging from genetic predisposition to environmental exposures [9]. Numerous studies have examined the connection between air pollution and musculoskeletal disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis and osteoporosis. While the findings have been mixed, they collectively indicated that air pollution may have harmful effects on musculoskeletal health [10]. Research indicated that various air pollutants had diverse effects on musculoskeletal conditions, with a greater impact of particulate matters (PMs), which consisted of harmful particles that altered epigenetic age and disrupted metabolic function [11]. The modest association observed between NOx exposure and musculoskeletal disorders could potentially reflect the composite nature of NOx as a pollutant category, encompassing multiple nitrogen oxide species that may collectively exert protective or neutralizing effects on musculoskeletal health [10].

Analysis of longitudinal data from over 40,000 participants in a nationwide Korean health insurance database revealed a significant dose-dependent association between elevated PM2.5 exposure and higher hospitalization rates for osteoporotic fractures among elderly populations [11]. These findings provided compelling epidemiological evidence supporting the adverse impact of particulate matter on bone mineral density and fracture risk [12]. Meanwhile, the data analysis from UK biobank of 341,000 participants showed that higher levels of air pollution exposure were linked to the increased risk of osteoporosis [13]. However, the current evidence on the outcomes associated with outdoor air pollution and musculoskeletal disorders was incomplete and not clearly conclusive. In our present study, we conducted a prospective study in Yangzhou, a city straddling China’s north-south geographical divide, from January 2019 to December 2022, to investigate the relationships between short-term exposure to respirable particulate matter (specifically PM10 and PM2.5), gaseous pollutants (NO2, SO2, CO, and O3), and heavy metals present in PM2.5 (including Antimony, Arsenic, Beryllium, Cadmium, Aluminium, Chromium, Mercury, Lead, Manganese, Nickel, Selenium, and Thallium) and hospital admissions of MDs. This study will display new perspectives on how air pollution influences the incidence of musculoskeletal diseases and depicts comprehensive estimates of skeletal diseases burdens caused by air pollution.

Materials and methods

Data source and study participants

Study area

Yangzhou, the centre of China’s Yangtze River Delta and located in Jiangsu Province, sits at the confluence of the Yangtze River and the Beijing-Hangzhou Grand Canal, with a total area of 6,591 square kilometres and a resident population of 4.58 million. In accordance with the Yangzhou Ecological Environment Status Bulletin (2023), the number of days of effective monitoring of ambient air in Yangzhou city was 365, with a total of 275 good days, or 75.3%. There were 90 days of light, medium and heavy pollution throughout the year, O3, PM2.5 and PM10 as the main pollutants on 56, 23 and 10 days respectively, of which the proportion of O3 polluted days reached 62.2%. From 2019 to 2022, the annual average concentration of PM2.5, PM10, O3 and NO2 over four consecutive years significantly surpassed the annual target values set by the WHO 2021 Air Quality Guidelines (AQGs) (5 µg/m3 average annual target of PM2.5, 15 µg/m3 average annual target of PM10, and 10 µg/m3 average annual target of NO2, 60 µg/m3 averaged over 6 consecutive months of the year with a daily maximum 8-hour average).

Hospital admissions data

The study utilized daily hospital admission records from Yangzhou University Affiliated Hospital spanning January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2022, encompassing a total of 917,924 admissions. The participant selection methodology was illustrated in Figure S2. Each admission record comprised 100 data fields, from which we extracted key variables including the healthcare facility, admission date, patient demographics (sex, age, home address), diagnostic information (ICD code and discharge diagnosis), and financial details (total hospitalization cost and out-of-pocket expenses). Daily hospital admissions corresponding to 12 of the 22 ICD-10 chapters (B902–Z971) were analyzed as health outcomes to evaluate the association with air pollution. Chapters without established biological or clinical evidence of such links were excluded. The International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) was employed to assess musculoskeletal disorder prevalence. This study defined musculoskeletal disease cases as including degenerative joint disease, back pain, inflammatory joint disease, osteoporosis, and total musculoskeletal disorders (detailed ICD-10 codes provided in Table S1). Participants with preexisting musculoskeletal conditions, incomplete air quality data, or missing key covariates were excluded. All data were anonymised and de-identified before analysis. Therefore, this study has been approved by the Ethical Review Committee (ERC) of Yangzhou University Hospital (Project ID: 2024YKL03G013).

Air pollution exposure estimation

Ambient air pollution data during the study period were obtained from the Yangzhou Environmental Protection Bureau (YEPB). PM2.5 (µg/m3), NO2 (µg/m3), PM10 (µg/m3), SO2 (µg/m3), and CO (mg/m3) were the daily 24-hour average concentration data, and O3(µg/m3) was the maximum 8-hour moving average concentration data. The monitoring data were obtained from the monitoring station of Hanjiang District, Yangzhou City in the study area (Fig. 1).

Heavy metals in PM2.5 exposure assessment

PM2.5 sample collection

The sampling site was located on the roof of a non-teaching building of about 20 m in a primary school in Hanjiang District, Yangzhou City, without high-rise buildings and factories and other sources of pollution in the surrounding area. Samples were collected from 10 to 16 days from January 2019 to December 2022 (February 2020 due to the impact of the new coronavirus epidemic (COVID-19) did not go out to collect samples), with a sampling time of not less than 20 h per day and flow rate of ≥ 100 L/min. Prior to and following sampling, the filter membrane was equilibrated in a constant temperature/humidity chamber for 24 h before weighing, then stored at 4 °C in light-protected conditions until analysis. A total of 329 samples of atmospheric fine particulate matter were collected.

PM2.5 sample measurement

PM2.5 mass concentration. A sampler equipped with specific cutting characteristics was used to extract a quantitative volume of air at a constant speed, effectively capturing PM2.5 particles onto a filter membrane of known mass. The PM2.5 mass concentration was calculated by determining the difference between the pre- and post-sampling weights of the filter membrane and the sampled air volume. Prior to weighing, the static electricity of the filter membrane should be neutralized. The difference between the two weighing masses of the same filter membrane must not exceed 0.04 mg, ensuring compliance with the constant weight requirement. The average of these two weighing results was then used as the final weighing value for the filter membrane.

Concentration of metals and metalloids. We took one-eighth of the sampled filter membrane in a 15 mL acid-resistant centrifuge tube, added 10 mL of 5% HNO3 solution (v/v), and ultrasonicated the membrane in a 70 °C water bath for 3 h. After cooling to room temperature, the sample was centrifuged (5000 rpm, 5 min) and filtered through a 0.45 μm aqueous membrane. We used inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry to detect the concentrations of antimony (Sb), aluminium (A1), arsenic (As), beryllium (Be), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), mercury (Hg), lead (Pb), manganese (Mn), nickel (Ni), selenium (Se), and thallium (Tl) in the filtrate under the conditions and with the parameters shown in Table S2, and the corresponding metals and metalloid contents in the atmosphere were obtained by converting according to the sampling volume. The detection limits obtained for the respective heavy metal elements were 0.22, 12.0, 0.62, 0.06, 0.05, 1.90, 0.15, 1.40, 0.39, 0.95, 2.00, and 0.03 ng/m3.

Quality control. Each time the laboratory blank, field blank, samples and samples in parallel (10%) were processed and measured together. The correlation coefficient of the standard curve was more than 0.999. The relative deviation of sample parallel determination was less than 20%. Every 20 samples introduced into the standard solution must have measured values within 10% of the standard value. The recovery of spiking was controlled at 80–120%.

Covariate assessment

The covariates examined in this study comprised age, gender, total hospitalization costs, residence place, and out-of-pocket expenditures. To assess the association between air pollutants and heavy metals in PM2.5 across various musculoskeletal disease groups, Spearman’s correlation analysis was utilized.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were compared between control and musculoskeletal disease using Student’s t-test and χ² tests, respectively. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using logistic regression to examine the association between air pollution exposure and musculoskeletal diseases. The analysis employed sequential adjustment: Model 1 (crude), Model 2 (age- and sex-adjusted), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for additional confounders). To correct false positives caused by multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was performed statistically significant. Additionally, we employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression to characterize potential non-linear exposure-response relationships between ambient air pollutants and musculoskeletal disorder risk. We applied BKMR modeling to assess the combined effects of all air pollutant exposure levels on musculoskeletal disease risk, as well as the effects of individual air pollutants within mixtures and potential interactions among different air pollutants. The probit link function of BKMR was employed to analyze the current binary outcomes, specifically control and musculoskeletal disease. Pearson correlation analysis were utilized to categorize the levels of air pollutants into distinct groups. Due to the relatively high correlations observed among different air pollutants, we implemented a hierarchical variable selection method utilizing a Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm over 5000 iterations. A posterior inclusion probability (PIP) threshold of 0.5 was used to determine the relative importance of each air pollutant exposure for the occurrence of MDs.

To enhance the robustness of our BKMR findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded individuals with air pollutant levels exceeding three times the interquartile range to evaluate whether extreme values influenced the observed association between air pollutants and musculoskeletal diseases. Second, we adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, admission route, residence place, total inpatient costs, and out-of-pocket payments, and reran the BKMR analyses to ensure the stability of our results. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS and R software. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 presented the baseline characteristics of the study population (N = 818,531), including 30,697 incident cases of musculoskeletal diseases (MDs). The MDs group exhibited significantly younger age distribution compared to non-cases (53.47 ± 21.06 vs. 58.84 ± 16.72 years; P < 0.001). Independent relationship of air pollutants with musculoskeletal diseases

Logistic regression models were employed to assess the independent associations between air pollutants and MDs. Using sequential modeling approaches, three analytical models were constructed. As presented in Table 2, Model 1 (unadjusted) revealed a significant positive association between O3 exposure and MDs risk (OR = 1.60, 95%CI: 1.07–2.39). The heavy metals in PM2.5 Antimony (0.59(95%CI: 0.36,0.97)), Selenium (0.45(95%CI: 0.26,0.74), Thallium (0.61(95%CI: 0.40,0.91), Arsenic (0.54(95%CI: 0.31,0.93) and Lead (0.51(95%CI: 0.29,0.89) revealed the negative association with the risk of MDs. After adjusting for age and sex (Model 2), both SO₂ and O3 demonstrated significant associations with elevated musculoskeletal disease risk, and the effect of SO2 (95%CI: 2.16(1.10,4.31)) was stronger than that of O3 (95%CI: 1.71(1.13,2.62)). The heavy metals in PM2.5 Antimony (0.50(95%CI: 0.28,0.86)), Selenium (0.39(95%CI: 0.22,0.68)), Thallium (0.54(95%CI: 0.34,0.84)), Arsenic (0.41(95%CI: 0.22,0.75)), Cadmium (0.62(95%CI: 0.39,0.97)), Chromium (0.45(95%CI: 0.20,0.92)) and Lead (0.41(95%CI: 0.22,0.74)) revealed the negative association with the risk of MDs. In the model adjusted for all confounding factors (Model 3), the association between SO2, O3, and the risk of MDs weakened but remained statistically significant (OR ranged from 2.16, 1.71 to 2.13, 1.65, respectively, PFDR < 0.05). As for heavy metals in PM2.5, the significant metals exerted the more negative relationship with the risk of MDs (PFDR < 0.05, Fig. S4). In brief, the air pollutants SO2 and O3 showed the positive-going effects on the risk of the MDs. Nevertheless, most of the heavy metals in PM2.5 revealed the attenuated effects on the risk of MDs (Fig. S5).

The joint effect of air pollutants on musculoskeletal diseases

Firstly, we used the Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) model analysis to flexibly simulate and visualize the predicted association of air pollutants with musculoskeletal disorders (Fig. 2, Fig.S6). Total air pollutants were associated with MDs risk with an overall inverse ‘J’ curve. Restricted cubic spline analyses with multivariate adjustment showed that air pollutant metrics were associated with musculoskeletal disease risk in a ‘J’ shape. We have found the evidence of a non-linear association between SO2 and MDs (Pnonlinearity< 0.05). A sharp change in SO2 occurred at 2.25 µg/m3, after which it began to plateau relatively well. The analyses also revealed that other air pollutant concentrations were significantly linearly associated with MDs (P < 0.001).

Exposure-response relationship of air pollution and musculoskeletal diseases. The restricted cubic spline regression model adjusted for age, sex, residence place, hospitalisation costs and deductible

To comprehensively evaluate the joint effects of multipollutant exposure on MD pathogenesis, we employed Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) modeling analysis. As presented in Table S6, the conditional PIPs revealed SO₂ as the most influential pollutant (PIP = 0.806) within the mixture, followed by PM10 (0.652), O3 (0.704), NO2 (0.625), PM2.5 (0.612), and CO (0.492). Figure 3A showed the links between the air pollutants and musculoskeletal disorders when the concentrations of the other air pollutants were fixed at the median level. The results revealed that SO2, PM10, and O3 were positively associated with MDs, while NO2 was nonlinearly associated with MDs. CO and PM2.5 were negatively associated with MDs. Figure 3B displayed the association between combined air pollutants exposure and MDs (with reference to the median concentration of each air pollutant), and the findings indicated a linear rise in the risk of MDs as air pollutant concentrations increased. Figure 3C displayed the individual exposure effects of air pollutants and their potential interactions. When all other pollutants were fixed at their 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, the single-pollutant effect estimation exhibited minimal variation, suggesting no significant interactions among pollutants. To further explore air pollutant interactions, we also plotted binary exposure-response functions, and the results in Fig. 3D showed no significant change in slope among the six air pollutants, suggesting no interaction among the six pollutants.

To gain further insight into the influence of the composition of PM2.5 on the risk of developing MDs, we further explored the BKMR model analysis of the relationship between heavy metals in PM2.5 and MDs. The conditional PIPs analysis of the BKMR model for the MDs group in Table S7 showed that Se had the highest PIP value of 0.543, followed by Cr (0.534), Tl (0.468), Al (0.429), As (0.428), Pb (0.443), Sb (0.345), Mn (0.367), Cd (0.296), Hg (0.236) and Ni (0.230). Figure 4A showed the results of the association of the heavy metals with MDs when the concentrations of the other heavy metals in PM2.5 were fixed at the median, showing negative associations of Sb, Cd, Se, and Tl with MDs, and nonlinear associations of the other heavy metals with MDs. Figure 4B demonstrated the joint effect of heavy metal mixtures on MD risk, revealing a significant negative association when all metals were at the 55th percentile compared to median levels. There were no significant interactions between heavy metals and one (or more) other pollutants in PM2.5 (Fig. 4C). The parallel slopes of exposure-response curves across concentration ranges (with co-pollutants fixed at median levels) suggested potential interactive effects among heavy metals (Fig. 4D). After adding all confounders as new variables in the sensitivity analysis of the BKMR model, the results showed trend effects consistent with the above findings (Fig. S7-S8).

Discussion

The prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders and osteoporotic fractures was anticipated to rise significantly in the coming decades as the global population continued to age. This increasing trend highlighted the importance of identifying risk factors for musculoskeletal diseases in designing preventive measures [14]. In addition to established risk factors for skeletal disorders (e.g., age, sex, and urbanization), growing evidence has highlighted the emerging role of environmental exposures, particularly air pollution. Prospective findings based on the UKB database revealed that long-term exposure to air pollutants promoted increased risk of musculoskeletal disorders [15]. Our analysis revealed significant sex-specific disparities in air pollution-related musculoskeletal disorders. This observation aligned with the established epidemiological patterns showing males typically experience higher incidence rates of conditions such as osteoarthritis and traumatic joint injuries. There were physiological and social differences between women and men [16]. Endocrine factors may mediate sex-specific susceptibility to air pollution’s musculoskeletal effects. Experimental evidence suggested that testosterone modulated bone metabolism and may interact with environmental toxicants to influence skeletal health outcomes [17]. Moreover, the differences in behavioural and lifestyle factors between the genders, such as levels of physical activity or exposure patterns, may also lead to the detected susceptibility. In addition, the genetic and epigenetic factors may also play key roles in determining how males and females differentially respond to air pollutant exposure [18]. The precise mechanisms underlying gender-based disparities in air pollution-associated musculoskeletal disorders required further investigation, particularly the interplay between biological factors and sociocultural determinants.

While prior research has identified older populations as particularly vulnerable to air pollution effects, our findings specifically demonstrated significantly greater pollutant-associated bone strength reduction in individuals aged ≥ 53 years. The findings from the quantile regression analysis contributed to elucidate the age-related difference. Specifically, quantile regression demonstrated that individuals with lower bone strength exhibited less sensitivity to air pollution, which aligned with the observation that bone strength tended to be weaker in older individuals. In addition, the main reason for the decrease in bone strength in elderly patients was the ageing of bone tissue, so the effects of air pollution may be attenuated accordingly [19].

Conventional risk factors for musculoskeletal conditions encompassed a range of elements, including socioeconomic status, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, seasonal variations, and microbiota. For instance, socioeconomic disparities have been identified as contributors to the incidence of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and osteoarthritis (OA) [20]. Furthermore, lifestyle choices such as smoking and alcohol consumption have been implicated in the onset of RA, with an interesting observation that smoking may even offer a protective effect against hip and knee OA [21]. Research has also highlighted that individuals with limited understanding of osteoporosis and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, such as singles and lower annual incomes, were less likely to receive proper evaluation and treatment for this condition [22]. The results of this study suggested that people suffering from musculoskeletal disorders spent higher amounts on hospitalisation and lower out-of-pocket expenses. Current evidence remained limited regarding socioeconomic influences on air pollution-related musculoskeletal disorders, underscoring a critical knowledge gap requiring further investigation. Additionally, obesity exerted profound effects on musculoskeletal health through inflammatory and degenerative pathways [23]. The complex etiology of these disorders highlighted environmental exposures as potentially modifiable risk factors. Chronic exposure to air pollution has been proved to be strongly associated with skeletal related diseases [24]. Exposure to air pollution have caused the increased systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which may result in disturbances in bone metabolism. Numerous studies have examined the impact of chronic air pollution exposure on osteoporosis or bone strength metrics (e.g., bone mineral content and bone mineral density). In a cross-sectional study involving 3,717 participants from peri-urban regions of South India, the findings showed a correlation between reduced bone mineral content and increased exposure to PM2.5 [25].

Current levels of particulate air pollution in China were much higher than in high-income countries [26]. As observed in the current study in Yangzhou, Jiangsu Province, a city in the centre of China where southern and northern parts of the country met, the exposure-response relationship typically exhibited a steeper slope at lower concentrations compared to higher ones. This phenomenon might be partially explained by the greater measurement error in exposure levels during periods of heavy pollution, coinciding with intensified public health interventions and heightened self-protective behaviors among individuals. For instance, a UK cohort study revealed a direct correlation between air quality indicators and the risk of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [27]. Additionally, studies have shown that individuals exposed to particulate matter exhibited lower osteocalcin levels compared to unexposed individuals [28]. Our findings aligned with the prior studies, reinforcing the positive association between air pollution and musculoskeletal health issues. The observed exposure-response gradients for SO2 and O3 in our regression models strengthened the epidemiological evidence for air pollution as an emerging risk factor for musculoskeletal pathology (OR > 1, PFDR < 0.05). The effects of different air pollutants on musculoskeletal disorders varied significantly, with particulate matter (PM) exhibiting a more pronounced impact compared to nitrogen oxides. PM, composed of harmful particles, has been shown to influence epigenetic aging and interfere with metabolic processes [29].

Extensive research has demonstrated a significant dose-dependent association between prolonged exposure to particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, and PM10) and increased osteoporosis risk, with Odds Ratios (ORs) progressively escalating with longer exposure periods. Notably, analysis using a 5-year moving average (lag04) indicated that each 1 µg/m3 increment in PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations was associated with 9.5% and 5.4% elevated risks of musculoskeletal disorders, respectively [30]. The biological mechanisms through which PM2.5 affected musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders may still be linked to the roles in promoting oxidative stress. Oxidative stress has been identified to induce inflammation and subsequently activate innate immune cells, thereby promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [31]. Short-term exposure to PM2.5 has been demonstrated to elevate oxidative stress, as well as markers like serum myeloperoxidase and 8-epi-PGF2α, suggesting increased risks of inflammatory-related underlying diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and ankylosing spondylitis [32]. The link between particulate matters and musculoskeletal disorders may be due to the ability to infiltrate the lower respiratory tract, causing both direct and indirect damage to multiple systems. The detrimental health impacts of PM arose from the capacity to penetrate respiratory barrier and enter systemic circulation. The direct pathway involved translocation of PM components across the pulmonary membrane into the bloodstream, while indirect effects occurred through systemic consequences of localized airway reactions. Current literature identified four primary mechanistic pathways underlying these effects: inflammatory responses, disruption of vitamin D metabolism, oxidative stress-mediated damage, and interference by environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals [31, 32]. Long-term exposure to ambient air pollutants (PM1, PM2.5, PM10 and NO2) demonstrated a significant negative association with bone strength, as quantified by ultrasound-based assessment, even after adjusting for multiple potential confounders. Notably, quantitative regression modeling indicated that the inverse relationship was more evident among individuals with greater baseline bone mineral density [33, 34].

To date, the health effects of single air pollutants (e.g., NO2 and PM2.5) have been widely demonstrated. However, given that humans were exposed to mixtures of multiple air pollutants, it was important to use mixed pollutant exposure estimation methods [35]. In this study we applied the Bayesian kernel function regression modelling-based approach to investigate the combined effects of six typical air pollutants on the risk of MDs, and the results revealed significant contributions of SO2, PM10 and O3 to musculoskeletal disease risk. The correlation between the two air pollutants and increased risk of MDs was firstly revealed by linear regression results, and further BKMR modelling revealed that combined exposure to SO₂ and O₃ increased the risk of musculoskeletal disorders.

Since the pollution was a complex of different chemical components including particulate matter, gases, heavy metals and metalloids [36]. Our further study explored the effect of heavy metals and metalloids in the air pollutant PM2.5 on the risk of musculoskeletal disorders. Gases and metal compounds in air pollution, such as NO2, O3 and heavy metals, could contribute to the formation of free radicals, which in turn cause the inflammatory response and even directly induce bone ageing due to the ability to cross cell membranes [37]. In this study, we found that heavy metals and metalloids Se, Sb, Tl, As, Pb, Cr and Cd in PM2.5 were negatively associated with the risk of musculoskeletal disorders based on linear regression analysis (PFDR< 0.05). A study published in The Lancet Planetary Health found that individuals residing in areas with high levels of PM2.5 and carbon black emissions from vehicle exhaust exhibited lower levels of parathyroid hormone, which regulated calcium metabolism as well as reduced bone mineral density, thereby increasing the susceptibility to osteoporosis [38]. For the elderly, osteoporosis was prone to fractures, which increased the risk of death by up to 20%. PM significantly impacted health outcomes related to pollution, the most severe effects of PM resulted from smaller particle sizes, as they could more easily penetrate the lower respiratory tract and enter the bloodstream [39].

Metals were recognized as common components of particulate matter. While the use of unleaded gasoline has significantly reduced Pb concentrations in the air, the atmospheric levels of anthropogenic-derived heavy metals such as Cd, Cr, Zn, and Hg have been increasing [4, 40]. Studies have shown that Pb have displaced divalent cations such as calcium, magnesium, and iron, and therefore accumulated in bone [41]. Research indicated that Pb could displace essential cations like calcium, magnesium and iron, leading to the accumulation in bone tissue. Human studies have found that lead exposure was associated with reduced bone mineral density (BMD). Higher concentrations of metals such as Pb, Cd, and cobalt were observed in femoral head biopsy tissues from individuals with osteoporosis compared to controls, particularly in trabecular bone tissue. Strikingly, lead was detectable in 92% of osteoporosis cases versus only 20% of controls, with multi-metal co-accumulation showing particularly pronounced disparities [42]. Mechanistic studies further indicated that lead promoted osteocyte cytotoxicity through oxidative stress pathways [43]. The combined effect of heavy metal mixture in PM2.5 on the risk of developing MDs was further analysed in this study based on the BKMR model, which showed that the combined effect of Sb, Cd, Se and Tl on musculoskeletal lesions was negative and statistically significant when all the heavy metals and metalloids in PM2.5 were above the 55th percentile. Relevant studies have shown that heavy metal exposure levels were positively correlated with the risk of osteoarthritic diseases, and the results of this study may be due to measurement error, residual confounding factors, or unique local sources. Recent epidemiological reports have shown the close association between low levels of Cd exposure and lower BMD values and higher fracture incidence. The effects of Cd on bone appeared to be mediated through the induction of oxidative stress, increased bone resorption and impaired calcium absorption, as has been proposed for the effects of lead on bone [44]. In brief, various metals in PMs have been demonstrated to influence bone health. Although it was difficult to determine their true effects, metal concentrations in the bone matrix and indirect effects may contribute to bone damage. Moreover, ambient levels of anthropogenic heavy metals continued to rise globally, particularly in developing regions, while the potential health risks posed by the synergistic interactions remained poorly characterized [45]. The association between heavy metal exposure in PM2.5 and MDs needs further validation.

This study revealed a significant link between exposure to ambient air pollutants and MDs risk. However, our research had several limitations. First, the study was conducted only in Yangzhou, which may limit the generalizability to regions with different climatic conditions and pollution profiles. Multicentre, large-scale, and prospective studies were warranted for systematic validation of definitive conclusions. Second, our investigation focused on the short-term effects of air pollution, whereas the long-term impacts and cumulative effects on bone and joint health may also be significant. Third, air pollution exposure levels were dependent on fixed-site monitoring stations, which may not accurately capture individual exposures and do not take indoor air pollution into account. Although this research controlled for several confounding factors, such as age, sex, place of residence and hospitalization costs, other variables like lifestyle, diet, socioeconomic status and physical activity could also influence the results. Additionally, while we identified the associations between air pollution and MDs, the precise biological mechanisms underlying the association have not been fully elucidated. The relationship between heavy metals in PM2.5 particles and musculoskeletal disorders needed to be verified in large populations and in vitro and in vivo experiments in the future.

Conclusions

Collectively, this study revealed a significant association between short-term air pollution exposure and increased risk of musculoskeletal disorders (MDs), with SO₂, PM10, and O3 exhibiting notable associations. Additionally, the heavy metals Sb, Cd, Se and Tl present in PM2.5 particles exhibited an inverse relationship with the likelihood of developing MDs. These findings underscored the critical need for strategies to mitigate the impacts of air pollution on musculoskeletal health and call for further research to validate these associations across diverse populations.
