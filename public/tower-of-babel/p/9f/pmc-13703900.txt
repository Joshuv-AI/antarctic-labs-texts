Contemporary patellofemoral arthroplasty is associated with lower revision rates compared with the previous decade: A meta‐analysis using revisions per 100 component‐years
Authors: Clemens Clar, Maximilian Budin, Laura Rasic, Andreas Leithner, Julia von der Linden, Patrick Sadoghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to assess global data on patellofemoral arthroplasty (PFA) survival a decade after a prior investigation by Reihs et al. using the same endpoints and data sources, aiming to quantify potential changes in implant survival over this period. The hypothesis was that the pooled revision rate of contemporary PFA, quantified as revisions per 100 component‐years (CY), would be lower than prior decade estimates derived from identical methodology and registry sources.MethodsA systematic search in Embase, MEDLINE and CENTRAL through October 2025 using a predefined search strategy was conducted using Ovid. Two reviewers screened and extracted data. The primary effect measure was revisions per 100 CY. Pooled estimates were obtained with fixed‐ and DerSimonian–Laird random‐effects. Risk of bias was assessed with ROBINS‐I. National arthroplasty registries meeting EAR/EUPHORIC Type A.1.1.1.1. criteria were analyzed.ResultsFifteen primary studies were included, with most series using contemporary onlay designs. Per‐study rates ranged from 0.355 to 3.400/100 CY. The pooled revision rate was 1.59/100 CY (95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.24–2.04) with random effects, corresponding to a 10‐year revision rate of 15.9%, representing an absolute improvement of 6.3% in survivorship compared to the benchmark analysis by Reihs et al. heterogeneity was moderate (I 2 = 52.7%). ROBINS‐I indicated predominantly critical risk of bias. Registry synthesis (NJR, NZJR, AOANJRR, 117.524 CY) yielded 1.85/100 CY (95% CI = 1.775–1.931), corresponding to a 10‐year revision rate of 18.5% (95% CI = 17.75–19.3) and an improvement of 0.4%–8.5% in the last decade if compared to Reihs et al.ConclusionsThis meta‐analysis demonstrates that modern PFA has a more favourable survivorship profile over the past decade, particularly with onlay designs. Proper patient selection and registry‐based surveillance remain key to further optimization.Level of EvidenceLevel IV.

INTRODUCTION

Contemporary studies suggest that 11%–24% of patients over the age of 55 presenting with knee pain suffer exclusively from isolated osteoarthritis of the patellofemoral joint (PFJ), for which treatment options include total knee arthroplasty (TKA) as well as patellofemoral arthroplasty (PFA) [7, 11, 15, 37]. The advantages of PFA entail preservation of natural knee kinematics, shorter hospital stays, reduced invasiveness and faster postoperative recovery [21, 40]. Since its introduction in 1955, PFA has evolved significantly. Early studies indicate revision rates of up to 35% prompted critical reassessment [5], whereas subsequent improvements in implant design and patient selection have been associated with reported survivorship of 92% at 5 years, 83% at 10 years, 75% at 15 years and 67% at 20 years [20, 45].

National arthroplasty registries provide population‐level outcome data; however, their interpretation remains limited by relatively low procedural volumes for PFA [8]. To date, no contemporary analysis has reassessed global PFA revision rates using the same endpoint definition and registry sources as the benchmark study by Reihs et al., thereby enabling direct comparison across decades. Such a methodologically consistent reassessment provides surgeons and healthcare systems with an updated benchmark for contemporary implant durability and supports evidence‐based decision‐making when considering PFA. Accordingly, a systematic review approach was employed, integrating registry data with recent clinical studies for comprehensive analysis [1, 4, 25, 30].

The aim of this study was to assess global data on PFA survival a decade after a prior investigation by Reihs et al. applying identical endpoints and registry sources to enable temporal comparison. The hypothesis was that the pooled revision rate for contemporary PFA, quantified as revisions per 100 component‐years (CY), would be lower than prior decade estimates derived from identical methodology and registry sources.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The online databases Embase, Medline and Central were systematically screened using Ovid. To minimize bias, study headings, medical subheadings and keywords were refined using Boolean operators. Subsequently, the search terms were adapted according to the specific characteristics of each database. The applied search terms were: ((knee joint OR patellofemoral joint OR osteoarthritis, knee OR patella OR femur) AND (arthroplasty, replacement, knee OR prosthesis failure OR prosthesis‐related infections) AND (patellofemoral OR femoropatellar)) NOT (‘TKA’ OR ‘total knee’ OR ‘unicondylar’).

This meta‐analysis was preregistered on PROSPERO and conducted in compliance with PRISMA guidelines [29].

The databases were screened and assessed for eligibility by two independent individuals (L.R. and C.C.). Potential overlap of patient cohorts was evaluated by comparing study characteristics, including institution, recruitment period, sample size and author groups. When overlapping cohorts were identified, solely the most recent study or the dataset with the longest follow‐up period was incorporated in the meta‐analysis to avoid double‐counting of patients. To be included in the study, publications had to meet the following criteria: a mean follow‐up period of at least 24 months, a clearly stated endpoint of revision for any reason, defined as the explantation or exchange of at least one prosthetic component, and a specified implant type, with unicondylar knee arthroplasty (UKA) and TKA being excluded. Studies were excluded if they were case reports or re‐revision studies [40]. Publications from January 2015 onward were eligible for inclusion, and the final database search was conducted in October 2025.

The following parameters were analyzed: title, authors, published journal, number of primary surgeries, type of prosthesis, patient follow‐up duration (in months), number of patients lost to follow‐up and deceased patients, number of revisions for any reason and revision rate.

Data from national arthroplasty registries were accessed through the official EFORT (European Federation of National Associations of Orthopedics and Traumatology) website for European arthroplasty registries and through official national websites for the rest of the world [14]. A total of 24 national registries were identified, of which only 11 provided data on knee arthroplasty. These databases were subsequently screened for available data on PFA, with inclusion criteria requiring information on the number and model of PFAs used, the number or rate of revisions and the follow‐up period. Ultimately, only three registries, the National Joint Registry (NJR) of the United Kingdom, the New Zealand Joint Registry (NZJR) and the Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry (AOANJRR), met all the requirements and were included in the study. These registries were classified as Type A.1.1.1.1., corresponding to the highest level of registry reporting according to the EAR/EUPHORIC classification criteria [23, 33, 40].

Parameters compared in the study

In this study, no outcome analysis of a single implant was performed. Instead, inventor studies versus independent studies and onlay versus inlay designs were compared, and a comparative analysis of the total survival rates was conducted. Furthermore, mid‐term (≤7 years) versus long‐term studies (>7 years) were analyzed and compared. Publications that indicated the centre of origin of an implant or named its developers as authors or co‐authors were categorized as ‘developer studies’ [40].

Assessment of revision for any reason

The primary outcome was defined as ‘revision for any reason’. This parameter serves as an objective measure, encompassing a wide range of complications following PFA that ultimately necessitate reoperation of the prosthesis [38]. It indicates the risk of arthroplasty revision over specific time periods, thereby enhancing the comparability of the included studies [33, 40].

Revisions per 100 observed CY

The data from the three annual reports of the prosthesis registers and the included studies were pooled, and the revision rate was calculated as ‘revisions per 100 observed CY’. This is a widely used classification of this outcome in the field of orthopaedics [33, 34, 45]. The revision rate per 100 CY was calculated as the number of revisions divided by the total observed CY, multiplied by 100. Mathematically, a value of 1 revision per 100 CY correlates with a revision rate of 10% within 10 years [16, 33, 40]. In this study, the value of revisions per 100 CY is used as an indicator to compare outcomes between the individual studies and groups. This enables a standardized statement about the outcomes, despite the varying follow‐up periods of the individual studies. By calculating the revision rate in CY, it is possible to establish a constant hazard rate over the entire investigation period and present the results in a statistically significant manner. The calculation of revision rates using the Kaplan–Meier method was not feasible, as most included studies did not provide extractable time‐to‐event data. Only Akhbari et al. [2] reported both a Kaplan–Meier curve and life‐table information, while a few others mentioned Kaplan‐Meier analyses but lacked sufficiently standardized or complete reporting to allow consistent reconstruction of time‐to‐event data required for pooled Kaplan–Meier analysis [8, 40].

Assessment of quality

The ROBINS‐I tool was used for the assessment of the quality of the included studies. Among the 15 included studies, the following seven domains were categorized as ‘low risk’, ‘moderate risk’, ‘serious risk’ or ‘critical’: bias due to confounding, bias in the selection of participants into the study, bias in the classification of the intervention, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of the outcome and bias in the selection of the reported result [44].

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 21 (SPSS Inc.) was used for statistical analyses. The investigated parameters were evaluated either as absolute numbers or as medians. The included parameters were analyzed descriptively, as no p values could be calculated because the registry data and clinical studies report real‐world outcomes rather than probabilistic estimates. Differences between groups were therefore interpreted in a descriptive manner, acknowledging that revision rates after PFA are influenced by multiple factors independent of the implant used. Patient selection, the healthcare system of the respective country, and the surgical technique were identified as key influencing factors [40].

RESULTS

A total of 15 primary studies on PFA were included in this meta‐analysis, comprising 1.477 implanted PFAs of 1248 participants. The studies incorporated 967 (77.5%) female patients and 281 (22.5%) male patients [1, 2, 6, 9, 13, 17, 19, 22, 26, 27, 28, 32, 39, 41, 47]. Fourteen studies were originally published in English, while one study was available in both Spanish (original) and English. The fixed‐effects model yielded a pooled revision rate of 1.95/100 CY (95% CI = 1.73–2.19), correlating to a 19.5% 10‐year revision rate. Using a DerSimonian–Laird random‐effects model, the pooled rate was 1.59/100 CY (95% CI = 1.24–2.04), which correlates with a 15.9% revision rate at 10 years.

In subgroup analyses, ‘developer’ studies demonstrated a pooled revision rate of 1.28 revisions per 100 CY (95% CI = 0.43–3.21), whereas independent studies showed a higher pooled rate of 1.75 revisions per 100 CY (95% CI = 1.45–2.10). Stratification by implant design revealed lower revision rates for onlay implants (13 out of 15 studies), with a pooled random‐effects estimate of 1.43 revisions per 100 CY (95% CI = 1.09–1.88), compared with inlay designs (2 out of 15 studies), which demonstrated a higher pooled rate of 2.97 revisions per 100 CY (95% CI = 1.80–4.91). When analyzed by follow‐up duration, the studies showed comparable revision rates between mid‐term studies (≤7 years; 8 studies), with a pooled rate of 1.52 revisions per 100 CY (95% CI = 1.18–1.98), and long‐term studies (>7 years; 7 studies), which yielded a pooled rate of 1.78 revisions per 100 CY (95% CI = 1.39–2.29) (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

The most important finding of this study is that contemporary data suggest a more favourable survivorship profile of PFA compared with earlier reports. Using the same endpoints and methodology as Reihs et al., the pooled random‐effects revision rate was 1.59 revisions per 100 CY in clinical studies and 1.85 revisions per 100 CY in registry data. Although no formal statistical comparison was performed, these values are numerically lower than those previously reported, indicating a potential temporal trend towards improved implant survival.

The primary outcome of this study, revisions per 100 CY, indicates a more favourable survivorship profile of PFA compared with the preceding decade, with a literature‐derived rate of 1.59/100 CY versus 2.22/100 CY reported by Reihs et al. and a pooled registry rate of 1.85/100 CY versus 1.89–2.70/100 CY in the prior decade. These findings are supported by contemporary reports, which attribute the observed gains chiefly to optimized implant designs and refinements in surgical techniques, while the progression of osteoarthritis remains the predominant indication for revision [18, 42, 46]. This prompts consideration in everyday clinical practice of whether PFA, when appropriately indicated, is superior to TKA, given the lower 10‐year revision risk of TKA [10, 24, 31]. However, DeFrance et al. [12] reported a 10% patient dissatisfaction rate in a systematic review, which precludes a definitive answer to this debate. Furthermore, it is crucial to consider that CY‐based comparisons provide an average estimate of revision rates and cannot account for time‐varying failure mechanisms or differences in follow‐up distributions across studies.

The overall revision rate across studies was highly variable despite moderate heterogeneity, ranging from 0.355/100 CY to 3.400/100 CY. Notably, the Avon implant yielded the most favourable outcome, with 96.1% survivorship at 11 years. Given that this study was independent without developer involvement, certain biases can be ruled out. Nevertheless, the survival curve is directly affected by surgical experience, hospital setting, and other factors, which attenuates the strength of the conclusions and indicates that any truly superior implant should be identified through future multicenter studies [40].

When comparing clinical studies to national registry data, the revision rates are 1.59/100 CY versus 1.85/100 CY, corresponding to 10‐year revision rates of 15.9% and 18.5%, respectively. It is striking that registry‐derived revision rates are higher, a finding that aligns with current literature [40, 46]. This is readily explained by the fact that national registries reflect the entire population, including older, multimorbid patients, anatomically complex PFAs and less stringent indications, factors that directly increase the likelihood of revision. Registries also capture the full reality of care, encompassing low surgeon volumes and learning curves, variability in surgical technique and heterogeneous rehabilitation pathways. By contrast, the analyzed studies largely represent high‐volume specialist centres with substantial expertise that employ contemporary PFA implants, which in turn lower the probability of revision [15, 38, 46]. Accordingly, comparisons between registry data and clinical studies should be interpreted with caution, and conclusions regarding PFA survivorship must remain descriptive rather than definitive.

Given the observed increase in PFA survival over the last decade, recent studies have examined the role of robotic assistance in enhancing surgical precision. Early evidence suggests that robot‐assisted techniques reduce variability in component placement compared with conventional methods, potentially minimizing maltracking, edge loading and early wear, which are crucial contributors to early failure. Improved accuracy may also preserve native bone and soft‐tissue balance, facilitating more predictable kinematics and reducing revision rates. While long‐term data are still emerging, the integration of robotics appears to be a promising adjunct that could contribute to the trend towards enhanced implant longevity observed in recent registries and cohort studies [21, 35, 48]. Beyond technical advancements, changes in indication practice may also have influenced contemporary outcomes. An increasing emphasis on confirmed isolated patellofemoral osteoarthritis, the absence of relevant tibiofemoral degeneration and appropriate limb alignment reflects a more restrictive selection strategy. Given that progression of tibiofemoral osteoarthritis remains a leading cause of revision, stricter adherence to these criteria may partially contribute to the observed numerical reduction in revision rates [3, 36, 42, 43].

According to the ROBINS‐I assessment, 12 of the included studies were rated as having a critical risk of bias and three as having a serious risk of bias. These ratings were predominantly driven by potential bias due to confounding, reflecting the non‐randomized design of the available evidence and limited control for relevant patient‐, implant‐ and surgeon‐related factors. While such studies remain highly informative for capturing real‐world PFA performance, particularly in the context of evolving implant designs and surgical techniques, the presence of confounding limits the precision with which causal inferences can be drawn from the pooled estimates. Consequently, the reported revision rates should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of observed revision rates rather than definitive effect estimates, and their clinical interpretation should be considered alongside registry data and future comparative studies with improved methodological control.

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting these findings. First, all included studies were non‐randomized cohort series and were predominantly rated at critical risk of bias according to ROBINS‐I. Confounding by indication, differences in implant selection, surgeon experience and institutional case volume may have influenced revision rates and cannot be fully adjusted for within the available data structure. Consequently, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of observed revision rates rather than causal effect estimates.

Second, the use of revisions per 100 CY assumes a constant hazard rate over time and does not account for time‐dependent failure mechanisms. Early technical failures and late progression of tibiofemoral osteoarthritis may follow different hazard patterns that are not fully captured by CY‐based aggregation. Furthermore, heterogeneous follow‐up durations across studies may influence the stability of pooled estimates despite random‐effects modelling.

Third, although potential cohort overlap was carefully assessed, complete exclusion of overlapping patient populations cannot be guaranteed due to incomplete reporting of recruitment periods and loss‐to‐follow‐up accounting in some series. Informative censoring and inconsistent reporting of deaths or secondary procedures may therefore have affected the calculated revision rates.

Fourth, no formal statistical comparison with the prior decade analysis was performed because individual patient‐level data were unavailable. The observed differences should therefore be interpreted as numerical trends rather than statistically confirmed improvements.

CONCLUSION

This meta‐analysis demonstrates that modern PFA has achieved a more favourable survivorship profile over the past decade, particularly with onlay designs. Proper patient selection and registry‐based surveillance remain key to further optimization.
