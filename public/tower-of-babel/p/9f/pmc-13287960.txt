A Practice Framework for Genetic Testing in Asymptomatic Relatives of Patients With Creutzfeldt‐Jakob Disease: Experience and Insights From Israel
Authors: Dror Shir, Noa Bregman, Aya Bar David, Talya Nathan, Orly Goldstein, Tanya Gurevich, Avner Thaler, Anat Mirelman, Tamara Shiner, Mali Gana Weisz, Shiri Shkedi‐Rafid, Gael Ganz, Hagit Shani, Elon Pras, Odelia Chorin, Gia Sorkin, Keren Markus‐Bustani, Shlomzion Kahana Merhavi, Mor Shechory‐Stahl, Miri Yanoov Sharav, Michal Dicastro, Dorin Trigubov, Dalit Barel, Esther Kahana, Avi Fellner, Amos Korczyn, Hagit Baris Feldman, Lior Greenbaum, Nurit Omer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGenetic Creutzfeldt‐Jakob Disease (gCJD) is an autosomal dominant prion disease caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the PRNP gene. It is relatively prevalent in Israel due to a large cluster of individuals from Libyan Jewish origin harboring the p.Glu200Lys variant. In our experience, increasing awareness, expanded reproductive options, and the emergence of research initiatives have led more asymptomatic relatives to seek presymptomatic testing. Since gCJD is a highly penetrant and fatal condition, the decision to pursue testing involves complex medical, psychological, ethical, and familial considerations.MethodsAn Israeli multidisciplinary expert panel, familiar with gCJD, collaborated to formulate a structured approach to the genetic testing process of at‐risk individuals.ResultsWe describe the core components of the recommended presymptomatic testing process, in a real‐world setting. We focus on referral considerations, eligibility and timing of testing, psychological assessment, a stepwise approach for the testing process, results disclosure, post‐result support, and implications for family planning.ConclusionsBased on the valuable experience of professionals who address these issues in clinical practice, the proposed framework aims to provide a comprehensive structured methodology for clinicians and care teams supporting individuals at risk for gCJD. It may serve as a model for the testing process of other late‐onset monogenic neurodegenerative diseases, in Israel and worldwide.

Introduction

Creutzfeldt‐Jakob Disease (CJD) is a rare, invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorder [1]. It is a human prion disease, and presents as a rapid progression of dementia, neuropsychiatric features, ataxia, myoclonus, and other neurological deficits, resulting in death within months [2]. In addition to the fast progression of neurological symptoms, the diagnosis is supported by typical MRI findings, elevated total tau protein levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and prion protein aggregation assays [3].

While approximately 85%–90% of CJD cases worldwide are sporadic, and rarely acquired, the genetic form of CJD (gCJD), caused by pathogenic variants in the prion protein gene (PRNP), is found in about 10%–15% of patients [4]. gCJD is an autosomal dominant disease, and the pathogenetic variants are transmitted to 50% of the offspring. It presents unique challenges for at‐risk individuals, particularly when facing the option of genetic testing prior to disease onset, known as presymptomatic testing [5, 6]. Clinical practices regarding the testing process vary worldwide, and are influenced by local guidelines, cultural differences, and ethical considerations.

The PRNP p.Glu200Lys founder variant (NM_000311.5, chr20‐4,680,464 G>A, c.598G>A, p.Glu200Lys), commonly known as E200K, is relatively prevalent among individuals of Libyan and Tunisian Jewish descent [7]. This variant has also been identified in Jews from other countries, but also among individuals of other ethnicities [8, 9]. Historically, the high incidence of CJD among this population was erroneously attributed to environmental factors, such as the consumption of scrapie‐infected meat. However, in the early 1990s, the monogenic etiology of the disease was discovered [10, 11]. Estimates from genetic and epidemiological studies suggest that the number of asymptomatic E200K heterozygotes in Israel is likely in the scale of hundreds to thousands [12].

The p.Glu200Lys variant has an age‐dependent penetrance [13] and is considered to have a near‐complete penetrance by the ninth decade of life [7]. Although the mean age of onset is early 60s [14], there is substantial variation in onset age, with cases occurring in both younger and older individuals. The factors that trigger the onset of clinical manifestations remain unknown [15].

With the incorporation of genetic testing into routine clinical practice, interest in testing for the variant has increased among asymptomatic family members. This is particularly driven by family planning options, including prenatal diagnosis and preimplantation genetic testing (unpublished data, Shir D and Omer N, Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center). Although the proportion of Libyan Jews within the Israeli population has remained relatively stable over time [16], we observed an increase in the number of individuals seeking guidance from the genetics institute and the cognitive unit specializing in gCJD at Tel‐Aviv Medical Center (Figure 1). During the years 2014 to 2017, the number of tests for asymptomatic individuals remained low (ranging from 2 to 3 per year). A notable rise began in 2018, with a continued upward trajectory in subsequent years. By 2024, the number of tests per year reached its highest recorded level (29 individuals). The recent diagnosis of first‐ or second‐degree relatives with gCJD, reproductive planning options for carriers of gCJD‐causing variants, advances in research, and the potential for future development of treatment options are some of the reasons that drive individuals at risk to confront the decision of whether to undergo genetic testing [17].

However, the devastating nature of gCJD, with its poor prognosis, presents ethical dilemmas, particularly with regard to whether the potential benefits outweigh the disadvantages of presymptomatic testing. Given the significantly variable age of onset, confirmed asymptomatic carriers need to carry on with their lives with a ‘ticking clock’ sensation, until first symptoms arise, without currently available therapeutic interventions. Thus, gCJD presents significant challenges for both the family members (potentially harboring the disease‐causing variant), as well as for the medical professionals who treat the patients.

The first recommendations for presymptomatic genetic testing for gCJD [5] were published in 1994, based on those established for Huntington disease (HD) [18]. Although these guidelines have remained largely unchanged for the past 30 years, a recent article by Goldman and Vallabh (Goldman et al., 2022 [19]) highlights both the growing need and the complexity of counseling for individuals at risk of developing genetic prion diseases. These authors recommend that testing will be managed by a multidisciplinary team, including a neurologist, a genetic counselor, and a social worker. A psychiatric evaluation is suggested for individuals with either identified risk factors or uncertainties about their readiness for testing. In such cases, a one‐month interval between genetic counseling and the genetic testing is recommended to allow time for reflection and the option to reconsider.

In an effort to formulate an updated framework for gCJD, we present practice recommendations for CJD presymptomatic genetic testing, developed by an expert panel in Israel. These recommendations provide a structured approach for pre‐test evaluation, testing procedures, and post‐test follow‐up, which may be applied globally.

Materials and Methods

A multidisciplinary panel of 23 professionals from six medical centers and public healthcare systems across Israel convened (January 2025) to discuss and formulate best practice recommendations for presymptomatic testing for gCJD. Participants were invited based on their clinical and research expertise in prion diseases, neurogenetics, genetic counseling, and psychological support for individuals at risk of neurodegenerative disorders. The multidisciplinary group included eight neurologists with expertise in neurodegenerative diseases, five medical geneticists, five genetic counselors, 3 genetics researchers, one cognitive neurology nurse and a medical psychologist experienced in counseling individuals undergoing predictive genetic testing.

During the meeting, a primary questionnaire was presented to all participants, outlining key discussion topics. Initial discussions took place in small working groups, where preliminary recommendations were recorded. Notably, participants were familiar with the established guidelines for predictive testing in asymptomatic family members of patients with Huntington disease [1]. These guidelines serve as a conceptual framework for the current recommendations, including key elements of structured pre‐ and post‐test counseling and psychological evaluation prior to testing. When differing opinions emerged, these were addressed through moderated discussion among panel members. In cases where consensus was not immediately achieved, a vote was conducted among participants to determine the preferred recommendation. Recommendations were informed by available literature where empirical evidence in similar conditions existed (e.g., predictive testing frameworks for HD) and by multidisciplinary expert consensus in areas where evidence is limited, reflecting current clinical practice in neurogenetics and counseling of individuals at risk for neurodegenerative disorders. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes each recommendation alongside its evidence basis.

Following the initial meeting, a draft document summarizing the proposed recommendations was developed via repeated email correspondence. To incorporate the perspective of individuals at risk, a family representative also revised the draft to reflect the carriers' point of view. The draft recommendations were circulated to additional experts in neurology and medical genetics who were interested to join this initiative, including senior clinicians with expertise in prion diseases, who contributed feedback and participated in the final approval of the document. In total, 29 professionals contributed to the development and review of the final framework.

Draft recommendations were subsequently refined through iterative manuscript circulation and feedback among participants until final agreement was reached during a concluding online meeting (July 2025). This interactive process ensured that the final recommendations incorporated diverse expertise and real‐world clinical perspectives. The finalized document was reviewed and approved by all co‐authors.

Results

Pre‐Testing

Referral Considerations

As part of the counseling of an affected family member, genetic counseling for unaffected at‐risk family members should be recommended. The expert panel agreed that all relatives of individuals with genetic or apparently sporadic CJD who express interest in genetic testing should have access to counseling. Proactively advocating for testing based solely on family origin was considered impractical and unnecessary, as it may stigmatize certain communities and cause unnecessary psychological distress in a broad population with a relatively low absolute risk. In addition, special emphasis should be placed on raising awareness among community medicine practitioners, who rarely encounter CJD.

All participants agreed that genetic counseling can be effectively delivered by a genetic health professional (a physician who is a medical geneticist or a genetic counselor) or by a neurologist familiar with the disease. Provision of genetic counseling should be in accordance with local medical regulations. In Israel, both are authorized to deliver counseling. Generally, the pretesting session includes an explanation of gCJD disease course and prognosis, information about the familial variant, autosomal dominant inheritance, penetrance, recurrence risk in offspring, and options for prenatal diagnosis. These key points are summarized in Text box 1. The option of referral for further counseling by a genetic health professional should also be raised by the practicing neurologist.

Eligibility and Timing for Presymptomatic Genetic Testing

In line with the principles of genetic testing, individuals have the right to access information about their genetic status if they choose to do so [20, 21]. However, during the consensus meeting, specific circumstances and recommendations were outlined to ensure that ethical and psychological considerations are appropriately addressed.

The panel strongly advised against genetic testing in individuals under 18 years of age, in accordance with the Israeli Genetic Information Law [22] and established ethical guidelines discouraging non‐actionable genetic testing in minors [23].

Panel members noted that young adults (e.g., aged 18–25 years) may be at a critical life stage where gCJD testing results could influence career choices, relationships, and family planning. Concerns were raised about young adults' readiness to undergo such testing for an incurable disease. An additional consideration is that gCJD caused by the p.Glu200Lys variant in the Israeli population rarely manifests before the age of 40, with the youngest known patient developing symptoms at 34 years old (unpublished data, Omer N, Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center).

Hence, genetic counseling and testing of young adults can be largely considered as non‐urgent. For this young age group, a multidisciplinary team, including a mental health professional (see below), should carefully review the motivations for testing, assess emotional resilience, and rule out impulsivity that could lead to regret following receipt of a positive result. An important distinction is made in cases of young adults seeking genetic testing for prenatal counseling (including during an ongoing pregnancy). This should be recognized as an exception and testing should be considered in accordance with other recommendations elaborated here.

Working group discussions revealed broad agreement regarding individuals who seek genetic counseling and testing during emotionally intense periods, such as shortly after a close relative has been diagnosed with gCJD or died due to this disease. It was agreed that in such circumstances, individuals should be encouraged to consider postponing their decision by 6 months, as bereavement and grief may temporarily impair decision‐making capacity and reduce emotional capacity in coping with potentially distressing results [24].

Recommendation 3: Genetic testing should not be offered to individuals under age 18 years and should be carefully considered in young adults [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25].

Recommendation 4: Individuals experiencing acute emotional distress should be encouraged to delay testing by at least 6 months.

Prenatal Considerations

In Israel, prenatal genetic screening and testing are widely utilized, as they are supported by the socialized healthcare system, a national program for birth defect prevention, and by broad access to medical genetics units nationwide [26].

Since gCJD is an autosomal dominant, adult‐onset disorder, it is not included in the routine carrier screening programs offered to all prospective parents in Israel. This program primarily targets severe and early‐onset autosomal recessive and X‐linked conditions. As mentioned, the panel unanimously agreed that actively offering genetic testing for gCJD to all individuals of Libyan or Tunisian descent could lead to unnecessary distress, stigmatization of a large population, and an undue burden on both prospective parents and the healthcare system.

Preimplantation Genetic Testing (PGT) is a technique that can be used during in vitro fertilization (IVF) to test embryos for genetic conditions before implantation of healthy embryos [27]. PGT is widely available across Israel and is publicly funded for up to two healthy offspring. Direct PGT refers to testing the embryo for a known familial variant without requiring additional linkage analysis [28]. Exclusion PGT involves selecting embryos without prior knowledge of their parents' carrier status, ensuring that the embryo did not inherit the at‐risk allele from the affected grandparent, while the at‐risk parent's genetic status remains unknown. The latter option is available in Israel for first‐ and second‐degree relatives of individuals with gCJD at tertiary medical centers.

Prenatal diagnosis for gCJD is also possible and can be performed through chorionic villus sampling as early as 11–12 weeks of gestation, or later by amniocentesis at weeks 17–23 of gestation. However, the decision to terminate a pregnancy following a positive result presents significant ethical challenges. Approaches to this issue vary considerably across different countries and subpopulations, reflecting diverse cultural, legal, and ethical frameworks, which are beyond the scope of this work.

During prenatal genetic counseling due to any indication, a family history suggestive of gCJD may be reported, particularly among individuals and couples from Libyan or Tunisian Jewish ancestry. Although the genetic cause of CJD has been known since the 1990s [29, 30], some families either concealed the diagnosis or were unaware of its precise nature. Therefore, any neurologic illness in adulthood that followed a rapid and aggressive course leading to death in the Libyan or Tunisian Jewish populations could be considered potentially relevant to gCJD symptoms. This is pertinent when assessing familial risk in any genetic counseling session and should be considered on a case‐by‐case basis.

Recommendation 5: PGT and prenatal genetic testing for gCJD should be considered on a case‐by‐case basis, emphasizing individualized decision‐making.

Workup Process

Psychological Appraisal

The original work on predictive genetic testing for prion diseases acknowledges the importance of psychological support, emphasizing that individuals should be capable of coping with a positive result. However, it does not specify the need for prior psychological assessment as a prerequisite for genetic testing, nor does it address the provision of post‐result psychological support [31].

The need for pre‐testing psychological assessment was one of the most debated issues among the panel members. Most agreed that a preliminary psychological evaluation is essential to uphold the principle of beneficence, which entails a moral obligation to act in the best interests of others by promoting well‐being and preventing harm. This perspective was grounded in both clinical experience and previous research, which highlight the potential of severe psychological consequences following positive presymptomatic genetic testing for HD [32]. In contrast, some panelists emphasized the principles of autonomy, arguing that individuals have the right to make informed, independent decisions about their health without coercion, and that access to testing should remain equitable [33, 34]. Consistent with these considerations and previous reports, some authors have suggested that psychological evaluation should serve as a standard safeguard rather than an absolute prerequisite, reflecting the need to balance respect for patient autonomy with clinical responsibility. Those opposing a mandatory psychological assessment also referenced a systematic review evaluating the psychological impact of a positive predictive test result for HD, which found no significant long‐term increases in depression, anxiety, or other negative effects in tested individuals. Instead, it reported a temporary increase in distress immediately after receiving the test result, which later returned to baseline levels [35].

Overall, there was a broad agreement among panel members on the importance of psychological assessment prior to genetic testing in asymptomatic individuals. This can also provide individuals with insights into additional possibly overlooked perspectives, including cascade implications on additional family members, possibly raising issues of guilt and blame.

The recommended psychological assessment process consists of an in‐person meeting with an experienced mental health professional, such as a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, preferably one who is familiar with gCJD and has experience in counseling individuals at risk for adult‐onset neurodegenerative diseases. The session should include a comprehensive evaluation of the individual's mental health history, understanding of the testing process and potential implications of the results, and their coping strategies. The assessor should provide written confirmation regarding the individual’s psychological readiness to proceed with testing. Follow‐up sessions may be offered before and after the test but are not mandatory. When psychological assessment raises concerns regarding an individual's readiness for testing or capacity to cope with a positive result, postponement of testing with additional support and repeat evaluation is generally recommended. In more complex cases, multidisciplinary discussion may help guide decision‐making. If an individual wishes to proceed despite these concerns, a shared decision‐making process involving the patient and the treating physician and/or geneticist is advised.

Consensus was not reached regarding the circumstances in which psychological assessment may be deferred. Concerns were raised about the potential long‐term implications and psychological impact on individuals who refuse to undergo such an assessment, an issue that warrants further discussion. One proposal was to provide medical professionals with a structured questionnaire or a screening tool to be used specifically in cases where individuals decline psychological assessment, to assist in determining whether their refusal may be acceptable and the testing process can proceed. Nevertheless, an agreement was not reached regarding this. Exceptional cases, particularly where genetic testing is imperative for medical decisions, may be considered on an individual basis for review by a multidisciplinary team or an ethics committee.

Recommendation 6: Pre‐test psychological appraisal is principally mandated to evaluate individuals' preparedness for a positive result. This should include a structured assessment by a mental health professional.

Timeline

The optimal genetic testing process should involve multiple steps to ensure proper preparation, evaluation, and support. To streamline and optimize this process, the panel outlined an ideal approach consisting of four key steps: an initial genetic counseling session, a psychological assessment, a testing appointment to obtain DNA for genetic analysis (usually through blood sampling), and a final session to disclose the results (discussed below). Overall, the process from pre‐test counseling to blood sampling should take no less than 1 month, allowing individuals time to reconsider their decision, reflect on possible consequences, and consult with family members, friends, or professionals. Special circumstances, such as an ongoing pregnancy, may require an expedited timeline while preserving all four key steps. In this context, positive testing results in one of the parents may have implications for pregnancy management.

Genetic Testing Procedure

Genetic testing for gCJD should be performed in clinically certified genetic laboratories with relevant experience. For specific variants (e.g., p.Glu200Lys in Israel), targeted Sanger sequencing is typically used, with a local positive control sample. In select cases, sequencing of the entire coding region of PRNP may be conducted as part of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) panels or exome sequencing. Further technical details are beyond the scope of this manuscript and are not addressed here.

Patient identity must be carefully verified prior to sample collection. Test results should be reported exclusively to the referring medical team who conducted the pre‐test counseling and not be disclosed directly to patients, relatives, or other healthcare providers. The genetic laboratory is responsible for maintaining patient confidentiality and ensuring secure transmission of results to the referring provider.

It is recommended that the costs of counseling (both pre‐ and post‐test) and genetic testing itself be publicly funded, for example by health maintenance organizations (HMOs) to ensure equitable access for all family members.

Post‐Testing

Reporting of Results

The panel members agreed that all test results, whether positive or negative, should be disclosed in person to the tested individual. Israel's relatively short travel distances make an in‐person disclosure feasible, and telehealth sessions are not usually recommended.

Ideally, test results should be delivered by the same clinician who provided the initial genetic counseling to maintain continuity in the therapeutic relationship and preserve the trust established in the first meeting. The atmosphere of the post‐testing meeting should be informative and supportive, ensuring that individuals feel empowered to process the results at their own pace.

In cases of positive results, the healthcare professional should provide clear explanations regarding the implications of the findings, including age‐related risk of disease penetrance, recommendation regarding testing of at‐risk family testing, reproductive considerations, family planning strategies, and available support networks. If relevant, participating in a research initiative should be offered. It is also important to highlight that genetic information may be crucial for additional family members, particularly those who are currently pregnant or have reproductive plans in the near future. A written summary of the consultation should be provided, encompassing all relevant information along with contact details for further follow‐up and support.

Recommendation 8: Test results should be delivered in person by the same trained clinician or genetic counselor who provided initial counseling.

Post‐Result Support Program

Based on the panel members' experience, individuals receiving positive genetic results for gCJD often describe feeling as though they have been “thrown into the deep end”. As the principle of “primum non nocere” (first, do no harm) remains a mainstay of medical practice, the possible implications of a positive result underscore the need for a more structured and comprehensive support system following result disclosure.

This sense of emotional overwhelm is often magnified in Israeli families, which tend to be extended. This means that multiple family members may carry the disease‐causing variant. As a result, the implications of harboring such variants are deeply intertwined with complex familial adverse experiences, potentially triggering a wide range of emotional responses, from guilt to anger, and leading to secrecy or concealment within the family. Additionally, a positive result for one individual may inadvertently reveal the genetic status of other family members who may not be interested in undergoing testing. Due to limited public resources, a structured program for ongoing support of gCJD asymptomatic carriers is currently not available in Israel. In some cases, support and follow‐up are provided through the HMOs psychological services or through participation in research platforms [36]. The latter serve as a valuable resource by providing a consistent point of contact to help address the challenges and long‐term implications of receiving genetic results.

Establishing a designated, structured program for carriers of gCJD causing variant remains an important goal. All panel participants agreed that such a program should incorporate regular meetings, support groups, and updates on research advancements to provide ongoing guidance for carriers and their families. Close collaboration with patient organizations was considered essential in this context.

Recommendation 9: Given the critical importance of post‐test counseling and support, healthcare systems and policymakers should promote the development of a structured and easily accessible support program for gCJD asymptomatic carriers and their family members.

Recommendation 10: Opportunities to participate in research initiatives should be offered, recognizing both their central role in advancing scientific knowledge and potential to provide participants with valuable support and follow‐up.

Conclusions

Presymptomatic genetic testing for gCJD involves an interplay of personal, psychological, and ethical considerations. While the decision to undergo testing is made by healthy individuals and does not lead to immediate medical intervention or treatment, the process is guided by healthcare professionals [37]. The distinctive epidemiology of gCJD in Israel offers valuable insights and practical experience with these challenges.

This manuscript aims to establish a standardized approach to genetic testing, counseling, follow‐up, and management for individuals at risk. Our framework is based on the infrastructure of the Israeli healthcare system, where genetic services are widely accessible and reproductive options such as preimplantation genetic testing are publicly funded. As the availability and structure of such services differ significantly across countries, local adaptation is required to establish a standardized approach, and may potentially serve as a model for other genetic neurodegenerative diseases.

Given the devastating nature of gCJD, fundamental principles of presymptomatic genetic testing—autonomy, beneficence, and non‐maleficence—should be carefully balanced. While individuals have the right to know their genetic status, the potential emotional impact might be significant, particularly in light of the current lack of available medical interventions to alter disease course [38]. This highlights the importance of appropriate timing and psychological preparedness, as emphasized in the proposed framework.

Despite the challenges of presymptomatic testing in gCJD, raising awareness of reproductive options for individuals at risk remains essential. This enables future parents to make informed choices that align with their values and preferences.

In the context of research, presymptomatic testing may contribute to identification and study of at‐risk individuals, a critical step in advancing disease‐modifying therapies and preventive strategies. As with other neurodegenerative diseases, emerging clinical trials are expected to increase interest in presymptomatic testing for gCJD [39]. In our experience, this shift has already begun, evidenced by a growing number of at‐risk relatives seeking genetic counseling and testing.

By implementing the recommendations outlined in this framework, healthcare providers may be better equipped to offer meaningful guidance and support in the testing process for asymptomatic individuals at risk for gCJD.
