Threat context impairs cognitive control of neutral words processing in social anxiety individuals: evidence from ERP and theta oscillations
Authors: Haiyan Zhao, Feng Si, Hui Meng, Haibo Yang, Dawei Sun, Longyan Peng, Jianqin Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial anxiety (SA) is characterized by cognitive control impairments, particularly in the processing of threat, yet how threat-laden environments modulate cognitive control over neutral stimuli remains unclear. This study examined whether threat context impairs the processing of neutral words in individuals with high social anxiety (HSA) through electrophysiological and oscillatory dynamics.MethodsA total of 151 participants (HSA vs. low social anxiety, LSA) completed a Stroop task in two conditions: (1) threat context (neutral words intermixed with social threat words), and (2) neutral-only (neutral words only). Behavioral responses (RTs), ERP (N2, N450, SP), and frontal-midline theta oscillations were analyzed.ResultsThreat context significantly prolonged RTs in HSA compared to LSA individuals. ERP data revealed that LSA individuals exhibited larger N450 and reduced SP under threat than under neutral-only conditions, indicating efficient conflict resolution. However, LSA individuals showed blunted N450 but amplified SP, suggesting prolonged attentional engagement with neutral stimuli in threat contexts. Time-frequency analyses further demonstrated that LSA participants increased frontal theta power in the threat context, whereas HSA individuals displayed suppressed theta activity, reflecting impaired top-down cognitive control. Threat contexts contaminate the processing of neutral stimuli in HSA individuals, marked by attenuated conflict detection (N450), increased attention engagement (SP), and deficient theta-mediated control.ConclusionThese findings indicate that threat context impairs cognitive control of neutral word processing in HSA individuals when threat is possible, bridging cognitive and clinical models of attentional dysregulation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12868-025-00976-2.Keywords: Social anxiety, Cognitive control, Event-related potential, Time-Frequency representation

Introduction

Social anxiety (SA) is a psychological condition characterized by an intense fear of being judged, criticized, or negatively evaluated in social or performance situations. Individuals with social anxiety often experience extreme discomfort in social interactions and may avoid situations that could lead to perceived embarrassment or scrutiny [1]. This anxiety can interfere with daily life, making it difficult to engage in regular social, work, or academic activities. It often includes physical symptoms such as sweating, trembling, rapid heartbeat, and a feeling of being “on edge” in social settings. Social anxiety may vary in severity, and in extreme cases, it can lead to significant distress and impairment in functioning. Cognitive models of social anxiety posit that individual with HSA exhibit hypervigilance to potential threats, followed by avoidance or difficulty disengaging from threat stimuli [2, 3]. Extensive research has primarily focused on how explicitly threatening stimuli (e.g., angry faces, critical words) capture attention in individuals with HSA [4, 5]. However, an important yet underexplored question concerns how the presence of threat stimuli influences cognitive control of neutral stimuli. In this study, cognitive control refers to the ability to ignore distractions (such as anxiety or threat monitoring) in a threatening context and maintain focus on neutral stimuli. This ability is crucial in understanding how individuals with HSA may struggle to disengage from threat-related information and focus on neutral stimuli.

Empirical studies provide substantial evidence supporting atypical processing of neutral stimuli in HSA individuals. Lange et al. (2012) found that patients with Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD) rated neutral symbols more negatively when these were preceded by neutral faces, suggesting that individuals with SAD may perceive neutral cues as threatening [6]. In line with these findings, studies consistently show that HSA individuals exhibit altered processing of neutral stimuli compared to non-anxious individuals [7]. For instance, slower response times in neutral-word Stroop tasks are interpreted as indicative of cognitive control deficits in HSA individuals [8]. These individuals also tend to show impairments in categorical perception during tasks like Stroop, forced choice, and lexical decision tests [9]. Electrophysiological data further highlight these differences. ERP components such as N2 and N450 reflect cognitive control deficits, particularly when processing neutral stimuli in the presence of threat-related information. These components are associated with conflict detection and cognitive regulation, suggesting that anxiety impairs the ability to process neutral stimuli [8, 10]. fMRI findings support these results, showing heightened activation in the medial prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex during emotional interference tasks, while regulatory regions like the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex exhibit decreased activation [11].

Studies exploring attentional biases also provide insight into how HSA individuals process neutral stimuli. Mansell et al. (2002) used a modified dot-probe task and found that HSA individuals did not show a significant attentional bias toward or away from social-evaluative words compared to neutral words, indicating subtle atypical processing patterns [12]. However, Roberts et al. (2010) found that HSA individuals exhibited greater attentional bias toward social threat words, supporting the hypothesis that social anxiety increases sensitivity to social threats, even though no absolute biases in response times were observed [13]. Further studies, such as Boehme et al. (2015) found that only HSA participants showed interference effects in an emotional Stroop task with both threatening and neutral words, indicating cognitive control deficits [11]. Cloitre et al. (1992) similarly observed slower responses to threat words in SAD individuals, supporting the theory of inhibited motor responses under threat conditions [14]. Wabnitz et al. (2016) found that SAD patients showed early hypervigilance (EPN) and later cognitive avoidance (N400) to both threatening and neutral words, with attentional bias shifting from general to specific over time [15]. Collectively, these studies emphasize significant impairments in cognitive control and attentional biases in individuals with elevated social anxiety. Specifically, they highlight atypical processing of neutral stimuli, marked by early hypervigilance followed by later-stage cognitive avoidance. However, the exact role of contextual threat in modulating cognitive control over neutral stimuli remains unclear, and this is the central focus of the present study.

Theoretical models such as attentional control theory (ACT) and the cognitive-motivational framework propose that anxiety compromises goal-directed attention, especially under threat context, by impairing top-down control mechanisms [16–21]. Electrophysiological evidence supports this view, identifying ERP markers like N2, N450, and SP, and frontal-midline theta oscillations as indices of conflict detection and cognitive regulation [8, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Most studies have focused on comparisons between threat and neutral stimuli or on the differences in how individuals with high and low social anxiety process threatening stimuli. Previous studies investigating the processing of neutral and threat-related words in social anxiety have primarily employed trial-based or block-based designs, in which neutral and threatening stimuli are presented either randomly intermixed or in separated, balanced blocks [2, 7, 26, 27]. Although such designs aim to minimize systematic bias, they may not adequately account for the heightened anticipatory arousal and vigilance commonly observed in individuals with HSA, particularly when participants expect the appearance of threatening stimuli. This heightened anticipation may affect the baseline processing of neutral words, potentially confounding the interpretation of group differences [28]. Moreover, not all previous studies have systematically controlled for rest intervals or participant expectations between blocks, which may further contribute to carryover or anticipatory effects [2, 7]. These uncontrolled expectancy effects may partially explain the inconsistent findings in prior research, where some studies reported impairments for neutral stimuli in HSA individuals while others did not. Therefore, explicitly controlling for expectancy effects is critical in order to isolate the specific cognitive mechanisms underlying neutral word processing. As a result, it remains unclear to what extent observed differences reflect specific responses to stimulus type or are instead influenced by general arousal and expectation. To address this issue, the present study incorporated a pure neutral-only condition in addition to the threat-context condition. This design allowed us to determine whether differences in neutral word processing among individuals with HSA reflect genuine cognitive biases or are merely artifacts of anticipatory threat, thereby providing a clearer explanation for inconsistencies in previous research.

In this study, all stimuli were centrally presented, which does not elicit spatial hypervigilance as seen in paradigms with unpredictable or peripheral threat cues. Rather, we interpret hypervigilance in this context as a state of increased anticipatory attention and heightened readiness for potential threat stimuli, consistent with the cognitive–affective model of anxiety. Similarly, cognitive control here refers to the participant’s ability to maintain focus on task-relevant features (color naming) and suppress distraction from threat-related word meanings, even in the absence of direct competition between stimulus features. The unpredictable alternation of neutral and threat-related words may increase demands for anticipatory monitoring and ongoing cognitive control, particularly in individuals with high social anxiety.

Emerging evidence suggests that even neutral information can become contaminated by the anticipation of threat cues in anxious individuals [29, 30]. For HSA individuals, who are often hypervigilant, threat words may significantly interfere with cognitive control over neutral stimuli. Given that real-world environments often contain ambiguous or mixed emotional cues, exploring how contextual threat affects responses to neutral stimuli is crucial for refining cognitive models of social anxiety. This understanding can help explain how anxious individuals process information beyond explicitly negative stimuli.

To address this question, the present study aims to investigate how environmental threat contexts influence cognitive control mechanisms when processing neutral stimuli in individuals with HSA. Specifically, we use a modified emotional Stroop task to assess both behavioral (reaction times) and electrophysiological (ERPs and theta oscillations) responses under two conditions: (1) threat context, where neutral words are intermixed randomly with social threat words, and (2) neutral-only context, where only neutral words are presented. When neutral words are randomly mixed with social threat words, heightened worry and vigilance in HSA individuals can cause their processing of neutral words to be influenced by threat cues. We adopted a fully randomized experimental design to more accurately assess how HSA individuals process neutral words under different conditions. The pure neutral-only condition was included, where participants assigned to this condition only completed the Stroop task with neutral words, in order to avoid the potential confounding effect of threat words on the processing of neutral words.

The emotional Stroop task, where participants name the color of threat-related versus neutral words, is frequently used to investigate attentional biases and cognitive control [29, 31]. Slowed response times (RTs) to threat words relative to neutral words suggest difficulties in disengaging from negative content [32, 33].

Event-related potentials (ERPs) and time-frequency representations (TFRs) offer valuable insights into cognitive control mechanisms among individuals with anxiety in the emotional Stroop task. Based on prior research, we selected N2, N450, sustained potential (SP), and frontal-midline theta oscillations as key electrophysiological markers for cognitive control and conflict monitoring in the emotional Stroop paradigm. The N2 component, occurring between 200 and 350 ms post-stimulus, is widely recognized as an indicator of early conflict detection and initial attentional control processes [34, 35]. Typically localized to frontal-central sites, the N2 component is sensitive to early-stage cognitive conflicts elicited by task demands. The N450 component, appearing around 350–500 ms, is primarily associated with conflict monitoring, interference resolution, and threat-related information processing in emotional Stroop tasks [25, 36–39]. This negative waveform is most prominently observed in frontal regions, particularly when participants process threat-related stimuli. Sustained potential (SP), generally emerging around 500–800 ms post-stimulus, is a late ERP component associated with prolonged attentional engagement, emotional processing, and post-conflict cognitive control in emotionally significant or salient tasks [40–42]. SP reflects sustained allocation of cognitive resources and is typically distributed over the central-parietal scalp region [25, 31]. Previous research indicates that individuals with HSA exhibit smaller N450 amplitudes and larger SP amplitudes compared to individuals with LSA, suggesting altered conflict monitoring and sustained processing [8].

Additionally, frontal-midline theta oscillations (4–7 Hz), as analyzed in Time-Frequency Representation (TFR) studies, are closely linked to cognitive control and conflict monitoring, becoming particularly pronounced during tasks that involve conflict or uncertainty [43–45]. High anxiety individuals often show diminished or disrupted frontal theta responses when confronted with threats, indicating reduced efficiency in top-down regulatory mechanisms [8, 46]. Thus, this study specifically focuses on the N2, N450, SP, and frontal-midline theta oscillations to comprehensively capture the neural correlates of cognitive control in social anxiety.

Based on previous research, the current study examined whether the processing of neutral words would differ when neutral words are randomly mixed with threat words (“threat context”) compared to a purely neutral-only presentation, and how this effect might vary between HSA and LSA participants. We recorded RTs, ERPs (focusing on N2, N450, and SP), and frontal-midline theta power to capture the temporal and oscillatory underpinnings of this potential contamination effect. We hypothesized that (1) HSA participants would exhibit slower RTs under threat context than LSA participants, (2) group differences would manifest across the N2, N450, and SP components, reflecting conflict detection, conflict resolution, and later-stage attentional engagement, respectively, and (3) frontal‐midline theta would be reduced in HSA participants under threat contexts, compared to LSA. Understanding these processes can deepen our insight into how anxiety not only affects responses to explicit threats but also alters the processing of neutral information in situations where the presence of a threat is potential.

Method

Participants

After recruiting participants through WeChat Moments, they completed the Chinese version of the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS) online [47, 48]. According to the established scoring criteria [49, 50], individuals with LSAS scores above 60 were defined as HSA (high social anxiety), and those with scores below 35 were defined as LSA (low social anxiety). Subsequently, 82 LSA participants and 84 HSA participants were invited to the laboratory for the experiment. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. A total of 8 participants were excluded due to excessive EEG artifacts (> 25%), and 7 participants were excluded due to low accuracy (< 75%) or extremely deviant reaction times (exceeding the 3SD criterion). Ultimately, 151 participants (mean age: 21.3 ± 2.2 years; 120 females, 31 males) were included in the final analysis, consisting of 76 HSA and 75 LSA participants. The LSAS scores for HSA and LSA were 74.56 ± 14.33 and 21.09 ± 9.52, respectively; t (149) = 27.040, p < .001. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University, Daqing Campus (Approval No.: HMUDQ20240420103). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Material

In this study, we drew upon the same pool of stimuli used in our previous work [7, 25]. Specifically, we selected 30 social threat words and 30 neutral words from a social threat word database developed by our team. These words were evaluated by 36 native Chinese speakers on four dimensions: valence, arousal, familiarity, and social relevance. For a detailed description of the stimulus set and the rating procedures, please refer to Cao et al. (2023) [7].

Procedure and design

The emotional Stroop task followed the same design and procedures reported in Cao et al. (2023). In brief, participants sat approximately 70 cm from the computer screen, which displayed stimuli at a visual angle of 5.2° × 2.3°. Each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 500- 700ms, followed by an emotional word for 800ms, and then a 700ms blank screen. Altogether, 240 trials were administered: half featured neutral words and half featured social threat words. Participants responded by identifying the color of each word using designated keyboard keys. For more details, see Cao et al. (2023). Cognitive control, in this context, refers to the ability to ignore distractions in a threatening context and maintain focus on neutral stimuli. This ability was measured by analyzing reaction times and electrophysiological responses to neutral stimuli. See Fig. 1.

Sample stimuli and procedures for the stroop task

In this study, we employed two distinct Stroop task presentation methods to examine how HSA individuals process neutral words in the presence of social threat words. The first method randomly presents both neutral words and social threat words together (including 120 neutral and 120 threat trials). In this threat context condition, neutral words may be “contaminated” by the negative emotional influence of threat words; thus, during data analysis, only the data from these contaminated neutral words (120 neutral trials) are extracted. The second method presents only neutral words in a neutral-only condition (120 neutral trials), where participants are exposed exclusively to neutral stimuli, ensuring that these words remain pure and unaffected by any emotional content.

We adopted a 2 (Group: high vs. low) × 2 (Task Type: threat context vs. neutral-only) completely randomized, between‐subjects design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: (1) HSA + threat context (n = 38), (2) HSA + neutral‐only (n = 37), (3) LSA + threat context (n = 39), or (4) LSA + neutral‐only (n = 37). Each participant completed only the assigned version of the Stroop task—either the mixed presentation (threat context) or the purely neutral presentation (neutral‐only).

EEG recording and preprocessing

Electroencephalogram (EEG) was continuously collected using a Neuroscan ESI 64-channel recording device employing an Ag/AgCl electrode cap and the international 10–20 extended electrode sites, with a bandpass filter of 0.05–100 Hz and a sampling rate of 500 Hz. A left mastoid was utilized as reference electrode online, and the reference was changed offline into the average of the left and right mastoids Electrooculogram (EOG) signals were recorded using electrodes placed above and below the eyes as well as at the outer canthi, and impedances were maintained below 5 kΩ throughout the experiment. To exclude high-frequency noise interference, the raw EEG data were filtered using a digital zero-phase 30 Hz low-pass filter (–6 dB half-amplitude cut-off, 24 dB/octave Butterworth).

For event-related potential (ERP) analyses, EEG data were segmented into 1000 ms epochs, beginning 200 ms prior to stimulus onset. Baseline correction was applied using the 200 ms pre-stimulus period. Trials with amplitudes exceeding ± 75 µV were excluded. EOG artifacts were corrected using linear regression [51]. The average ERP waveforms were computed from correct-response trials. Data preprocessing was performed using Neuroscan Scan 4.5 software, and statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS 29.0.

For the time-frequency representations (TFR) analyses, EEG data were segmented into 2000 ms epochs, starting 500 ms before stimulus onset. Waveforms were baseline corrected relative to the 500 ms pre-stimulus interval. Trials with amplitudes exceeding ± 75µV were removed prior to averaging, and EOG blink artifacts were corrected using a linear regression estimate.

Statistics and analyses

Behavioral data analyses

The accuracy and reaction times (RTs) for neutral words were analyzed in response to neutral words under both the threat context and neutral-only conditions. Trials with erroneous responses or RTs greater than 3 SD from the mean were excluded from the analysis. A 2 (Task Type: threat context vs. neutral-only) × 2 (Group: HSA vs. LSA) between-subjects ANOVA was performed for both accuracy and RTs.

ERP analyses

Based on inspection of the grand average ERP waveforms (Fig. 3) and previous studies [8, 31, 32], the mean amplitudes were computed at time windows of 250–320 ms (for N2) [34, 35], 380–480 ms (for N450) [38, 39], and 480–800 ms (for SP) [25, 40]. Drawing on previous literature, the present analysis utilized data from five selected electrode sites: Fz, FCz, Cz, CPz, and Pz. A three-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted for the measurements of N2, N450, and SP with the following factors and levels: 2 (Group: HSA, LSA) × 2 (Task Type: threat context vs. neutral-only) × 5 (Site: Fz, FCz, Cz, CPz, and Pz).

(A) Grand-average ERP waveforms averaged across the five midline electrodes (Fz, FCz, Cz, CPz, and Pz) for the HSA and LSA groups under the threat context and neutral‐only conditions. (B) Topographic maps of the difference amplitudes obtained by subtracting ERPs to neutral words in the neutral‐only condition from ERPs to neutral words in the threat context condition, shown at three-time windows (250–320 ms, 380–480 ms, and 480–800 ms post‐stimulus). (C) Topographic maps of the difference amplitudes comparing HSA vs. LSA for neutral words under both the threat context and neutral‐only conditions, again displayed across the same three-time windows (250–320 ms, 380–480 ms, and 480–800 ms)

TFR analyses

TFRs of theta-band activity (4–7 Hz) were obtained using a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) within the Fieldtrip toolbox [52, 53]. To minimize spectral leakage, a single Hanning taper was applied to the data before the FFT. A 500-ms sliding window was then moved across the time series with a 90% overlap between adjacent segments. This approach balances between enhancing temporal resolution and reducing signal attenuation due to windowing. Baseline correction was carried out using the interval from − 400 ms to -100 ms relative to stimulus onset as a reference period. Power values for 0-1500ms post-stimulus were normalized by converting to decibels [2]. Following normalization, the amplitudes were extracted to produce the time-frequency plots.

Guided by visual inspection of the resulting spectrograms and previous findings [43, 44], the analysis focused on the mean theta power observed between 350 and 500 ms at frontal and fronto-central electrodes (F1, Fz, F2, FC1, FCz, FC2). These values were then submitted to a three-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with Task type (threat context vs. neutral-only) and Site (F1, Fz, F2, FC1, FCz, FC2) as within-subject factors, and Group (HSA vs. LSA) as a between-subject factor.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 29.0. Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated, and statistical significance was assessed at an alpha level of 0.05. Simple effects analyses were performed for any significant interaction effects (p < .05).

ERP data

N2 (250-320ms)

A three-way ANOVA for the N2 component revealed a significant main effect of Site, F (4,588) = 25.323, p < .001, = 0.147, with the most negative N2 at the Fz site (2.3 ± 0.3 µV). No other main effects or interactions reached significance (Fs < 3.155, ps > 0.05). See Fig. 3.

N450 (380-480ms)

For the N450 component, there was no significant main effect of Group, F (1, 147) = 0.239, p = .625 (HSA mean = 5.98 ± 0.4 µV; LSA mean = 5.70 ± 0.4 µV). However, a Group × Task Type interaction emerged, F (1,147) = 4.228, p = .042, = 0.028. Follow-up comparisons showed that, under the threat context condition, the LSA group’s amplitude (4.4 ± 0.6 µV) was more negative than the HSA group’s amplitude (5.9 ± 0.6 µV; p = .048), whereas there was no group difference in the neutral-only condition (6.1± 0.6 µV vs. 6.9 ± 0.6 µV; p = .274). Furthermore, for the LSA group, N450 amplitudes were more negative under the threat context condition (4.4 ± 0.6 µV) than under the neutral-only condition (7.0 ± 0.6µV; p = .002). In contrast, the HSA group did not differ significantly between threat context (5.9 ± 0.6 µV) and neutral-only condition (6.0 ± 0.6µV) (p = .801). There was also a significant main effect of Site, F (4,588) = 62.295, p < .001, = 0.298, indicating a frontal distribution for N450 with the most negative value at Fz (4.2 ± 0.3 µV). See Fig. 3.

SP (480- 800ms)

Analysis of the SP revealed an interaction of Group × Task Type, F (1,147) = 4.745, p = .031, = 0.031. Simple sample analysis found that under threat context conditions, the HSA group showed a larger SP amplitude (4.7 µV) than the LSA group (3.1 ± 0.5µV; p = .033), whereas under neutral-only conditions, there was no significant difference (4.4 ± 0.6 µV vs. 5.1 ± 0.6 µV; p = .350). For the LSA group, the SP amplitude was significantly larger under the neutral-only condition (5.1 ± 0.6µV) than under the threat context condition (3.1 ± 0.5µV; p = .011), while the HSA group exhibited no difference (4.7 ± 0.5µV vs. 4.4 ± 0.6 µV; p = .627). A significant main effect of Site also emerged, F (4,588) = 67.072, p < .001, = 0.313, with the largest SP amplitude at Cz (5.4 ± 0.5 µV), indicative of a central scalp distribution. See Fig. 3.

To further validate the differentiation between ERP components, we additionally performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA; see Supplementary Materials). The PCA results were consistent with our original findings and confirmed the separation of N2, N450, and SP.

TFR analysis

A three-way ANOVA of theta-band power revealed a significant Group × Task type interaction, F (1,147) = 4.185, p = .043, = 0.028. Under threat context conditions, the LSA group exhibited stronger theta power (2.4 ± 0.2 dB) than the HSA group (1.8 ± 0.2 dB; p = .013), whereas under neutral-only conditions, there was no significant group difference (2.3 ± 0.2 dB vs. 2.2 ± 0.2 dB; p = .701). For the HSA group, theta power was significantly stronger in the neutral-only condition (2.3 ± 0.2 dB) than under threat context conditions (1.8 ± 0.2 dB; p = .023). In contrast, the LSA group showed no difference between threat context (2.3 ± 0.2 dB) and neutral-only conditions (2.2 ± 0.2 dB; p = .557). Additionally, the main effect of Site was significant, F (4, 588) = 20.249, p < .001, = 0.123, revealing a frontal-central distribution with the highest theta power at the FCz electrode (2.4 ± 0.1 dB). Discussion

This study aims to investigate whether cognitive control over neutral word processing is impaired in individuals with HSA when threat words may be presented. The combined analysis of behavioral, ERP, and TFR data revealed significant differences between HSA and LSA individuals under the threat context. Behaviorally, HSA individuals exhibited longer RTs compared to their LSA counterparts. Regarding the ERP results, under the threat context, HSA individuals exhibited a reduced N450 amplitude and an enhanced SP amplitude, indicating altered cognitive processing of neutral words relative to LSA individuals. Additionally, TFR analysis indicated that HSA individuals had weaker theta power than LSA individuals, further highlighting altered neural dynamics during neutral word processing. These findings suggested that threat contexts impair cognitive control over neutral stimuli in HSA individuals. Our findings further suggest that it is not neutral stimuli themselves but rather the anticipation of threat that critically impairs cognitive control in HSA individuals. The inclusion of a pure neutral-only condition provided a necessary baseline to clarify inconsistencies in prior literature and underscores the unique methodological contribution of the present study.

Behaviorally, HSA individuals exhibited longer RTs than LSA individuals in the threat context, as expected. Additionally, HSA individuals showed delayed RTs to neutral words in the threat context compared to the neutral-only condition, while LSA individuals did not show such a difference. These results suggested that the contextual presence of threat primes a hypervigilant state in HSA individuals, diverting resources away from task-relevant processing. This aligns with theories positing that anxiety broadens attentional scope to prioritize threat detection at the cost of goal-directed control [3, 17], suggesting that HSA individuals have difficulty allocating cognitive resources effectively when threat cues are present. For the LSA individuals, the absence of a threat-related delay highlights their relatively more efficient ability to ignore task-irrelevant emotional information [30]. The result in the present study is consistent with the attentional bias theory [2], which indicates that HSA individuals experience worry in the presence of threat stimuli or situations where threat is possible, making it difficult for them to disengage attention from threat-related stimuli, ultimately affecting their processing efficiency [31, 54]. Notably, in neutral-only conditions, HSA individuals displayed faster RTs compared to the LSA individuals. This may be because HSA individuals are more likely to comply with or adhere to the experimenter’s instructions and may also be more concerned about making mistakes. As a result, in the neutral-only condition, HSA individuals may exert extra effort to complete the task, leading to faster RTs.

N2, although a prominent frontal peak was observed, was not significantly modulated by either task type or group. It is possible that the environmental or experimental context (e.g., task type, emotional salience of the stimuli) did not activate the same cognitive processes associated with N2 in previous studies, particularly if threat-related stimuli were not sufficiently prominent to engage conflict monitoring processes.

The N450—a marker of conflict detection—was selectively modulated by threat context in LSA individuals, who showed more negative amplitudes under threat context versus neutral-only conditions. In contrast, HSA participants exhibited no significant N450 differences between conditions, indicating a failure to upregulate conflict monitoring when threat is possible adaptively. This blunted response parallels findings in anxious attachment and test anxiety [55, 56], where impaired conflict detection predicts maladaptive avoidance. Furthermore, LSA individuals exhibited larger N450 amplitudes under the threat context than HSA individuals. The N450 component is typically associated with emotional conflict detection and resolution (e.g., processing of emotional information) [8, 42]. Therefore, LSA individuals may recognize and process threat-related information or emotional conflicts more effectively. In contrast, HSA individuals may be more affected by interference or emotional responses, reducing conflict monitoring ability. This finding may indicate that cognitive control is impaired in HSA individuals under threat contexts, resulting in a weaker response to threat-related information. In contrast, LSA individuals can better engage in conflict monitoring and avoid emotional interference.

The SP component, which indexes sustained attentional engagement, showed that HSA individuals exhibited amplified SP amplitudes under the threat context compared to LSA individuals. This suggests that HSA individuals allocate excessive resources to post-conflict processing of neutral stimuli when threat is contextually salient, potentially reflecting more sustained or elaborate processing at later stages, in line with theories suggesting that anxiety prolongs the processing of emotionally salient information [30]. Such sustained engagement may perpetuate anxiety by reinforcing the association between neutral stimuli and threat contexts. For the LSA group, the SP amplitudes were significantly larger under the neutral-only condition than under the threat context condition, while the HSA group exhibited no condition difference. In the threat context, threat-related cues facilitated the processing of neutral words in LSA individuals, resulting in a decreased SP amplitude. In the neutral-only condition, because the arousal level of neutral stimuli is lower, LSA individuals need to allocate more cognitive resources to process these neutral stimuli. In contrast, worrying about making mistakes and negative assessment is one of the core features of social anxiety. Additionally, as the task itself emphasizes quick and accurate responses, HSA individuals tend to invest excessive cognitive resources in tasks, particularly when the task involves potential social evaluation, regardless of the presence of threat-related stimuli.

TFR analyses further dissociated group differences in the threat contexts. LSA individuals increased frontal theta power under threat conditions, consistent with adaptive recruitment of cognitive control networks [57]. In contrast, HSA individuals displayed suppressed theta activity, indicating a deficit of top-down regulation when threat stimuli appeared. This theta suppression aligns with models of anxiety as a state of “cortical inefficiency”, where excessive bottom-up threat signaling overwhelms prefrontal regulatory mechanisms [58, 59]. No group differences were found under the neutral-only condition, which the absence of threat-related stimuli or the anticipation of threat may explain. Without emotional interference or facilitation, neither HSA nor LSA individuals needed to recruit comparative cognitive control resources, resulting in comparable levels of theta activity across groups. Moreover, for HSA individuals, theta power was significantly higher in the neutral-only condition than under threat conditions. In contrast, LSA participants showed no difference between the two conditions. This pattern suggests that HSA individuals are capable of engaging top-down cognitive control, as reflected by increased theta power, when the environment is emotionally neutral and free of threat-related interference. However, under threat contexts, their cognitive control system appears to be disrupted or suppressed, leading to reduced frontal theta activity. This may reflect a state of cognitive overload or inefficiency, where excessive bottom-up processing of anticipated threat impairs the recruitment of prefrontal control mechanisms. Conversely, LSA participants maintained relatively stable theta activity across both conditions, indicating more consistent and resilient cognitive control that is not significantly disrupted by the presence of threat-related cues.

In addition to impaired cognitive control, our results may also be influenced by negative expectancy bias in HSA individuals. Previous research suggests that individuals with HSA often experience heightened anticipatory anxiety, which leads them to develop negative expectations about neutral or ambiguous stimuli.

[60]. In the present study, HSA participants might have experienced prediction errors when neutral words appeared in the mixed-threat context. These prediction errors, associated with their negative expectancy bias, could have contributed to the observed ERP findings, particularly in the N450 and SP components. In contrast, prediction errors may not have occurred in the neutral-only condition, where expectations would not be shaped by threat-related cues.

Thus, while cognitive control deficits under threat are likely driving some of the observed differences, we also acknowledge that negative expectancy bias may play a significant role in modulating HSA individuals’ responses. Future research should aim to investigate how expectancy biases and prediction errors interact with cognitive control processes in HSA individuals, using both behavioral and neural measures, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of these underlying mechanisms.

Theoretical and clinical implications

Our findings bridge cognitive neuroscience and clinical models of social anxiety. The triad of blunted N450, amplified SP, and theta suppression under threat contexts provides a mechanistic explanation for real-world attentional dysregulation in HSA individuals, such as distractibility in social settings or misinterpretation of neutral cues as threatening. Clinically, these neural markers could serve as targets for neuromodulation (e.g., theta-enhancing neurofeedback) or cognitive training aimed at strengthening conflict monitoring and attentional disengagement.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study into cognitive control in individuals with high social anxiety (HSA), several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not include direct measures of cognitive control, such as independent executive function or task-switching assessments. Thus, our interpretation that the observed differences in reaction times and ERP components reflect impaired cognitive control in HSA individuals remains speculative. Future studies should incorporate explicit cognitive control tasks to validate this hypothesis.

Second, we cannot be certain that the mixed-stimulus context truly induced a threat-related state, as no subjective measures of state anxiety or perceived threat were collected. It is possible that the observed group differences reflect increased cognitive flexibility demands or overall processing load, rather than anxiety-specific effects. Including subjective and physiological measures in future research would help clarify the mechanisms at play. Third, as recent literature suggests, neural and behavioral differences in HSA participants may also be explained by negative expectancy bias or prediction error, rather than by cognitive control deficits alone. Assessing expectancy and prediction error directly would help disentangle these possible mechanisms.

Fourth, the use of only word stimuli may constrain ecological validity, as real-life social threat often arises from facial expressions or interpersonal interactions. Furthermore, we focused on the impact of mixed versus neutral-only word presentation, but did not systematically vary the ratio of threat to neutral stimuli. Future work could manipulate stimulus ratios to investigate potential dose-dependent effects. Finally, our sample consisted primarily of young adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or more diverse populations. Replication with broader demographic groups and varied stimulus modalities would enhance our understanding of social anxiety effects in real-world contexts.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide new evidence on how social anxiety modulates cognitive and neural processing under conditions of uncertainty, and we hope this work will inspire further research in this field.

Conclusion

The results showed that HSA individuals responded more slowly than LSA individuals in the threat context and more slowly than HSA individuals in the neutral-only condition. ERP data showed that HSA individuals displayed decreased N450, amplified SP, and theta suppression during processing neutral words under threat contexts compared to LSA individuals. These findings indicate that threat context impairs cognitive control of neutral word processing in HSA individuals, contrasting with the more flexible engagement of conflict monitoring observed in LSA individuals. This pattern aligns with models suggesting that anxious individuals engage in sustained worry and hypervigilance yet may lack efficient top-down mechanisms to regulate this prolonged engagement. Future research targeting these mechanisms may yield more effective interventions designed to mitigate anxiety-related interference in real-world contexts.
