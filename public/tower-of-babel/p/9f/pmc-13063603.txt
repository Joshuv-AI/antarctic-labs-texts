Prognostic stratification of cardiovascular risk and cardiac remodeling in prediabetes: a multimodal analysis comparing ADA and WHO/IEC diagnostic criteria
Authors: Zhihao Zheng, Yanjun Song, Kongyong Cui, Jining He, Xiaohui Bian, Chenxi Song, Qiuting Dong, Chen Zhu, Rui Fu, Kefei Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrediabetes, an intermediate metabolic state preceding diabetes, independently accelerates cardiovascular pathology through dysglycemia-driven mechanisms. This study evaluates the heterogeneous cardiovascular risk stratification by directly comparing two major diagnostic criteria (ADA vs. WHO/IEC) and assesses the causal cardiovascular consequences of prediabetes, an area requiring further elucidation.MethodsAfter excluding participants with baseline cardiovascular disease, the remaining cohort with complete glycemic and relevant assessment data (n = 278,697) was stratified into normoglycemia, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Prediabetes was subsequently classified according to both ADA (fasting plasma glucose, FPG 5.6–6.9 mmol/L and/or glycosylated hemoglobin A1c, HbA1c 5.7–6.4%) and WHO/IEC (FPG 6.1–6.9 mmol/L and/or HbA1c 6.0–6.4%) criteria. Associations with incident cardiovascular disease (CVD), mortality, and cardiac remodeling (via cardiac magnetic resonance, CMR) were assessed using multivariable-adjusted models. Mendelian randomization (MR) tested causality of prediabetes on outcomes. All observational analyses were adjusted for key demographic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates.ResultsOver 13.5 years, prediabetes—irrespective of criteria—elevated CVD risk (ADA: HR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.12–1.16; WHO/IEC: HR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.19–1.27), with stronger mortality associations in WHO/IEC-defined individuals. MR analyses confirmed that prediabetes was causally associated with increased CVD (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02), coronary heart disease (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.17), myocardial infarction (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.19), stroke (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.10), and primary hypertension (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02) risks. In an exploratory CMR substudy (n = 2512), early concentric left ventricular remodeling was suggested, particularly under WHO/IEC criteria. Risks were consistently observed across genetic susceptibility strata, though the lack of significant interaction warrants cautious interpretation and further investigation into potential effect modifications.ConclusionThese findings highlight the differential prognostic utility of ADA and WHO/IEC criteria for cardiovascular risk stratification in prediabetes.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03123-1.

Introduction

Prediabetes is a condition characterized by chronically elevated levels of glucose in the blood that exceed the normal range but remain below the diagnostic threshold for diabetes mellitus. Affecting an estimated 350 million adults worldwide, this intermediate metabolic state is not merely a precursor to overt diabetes but an independent risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and all-cause mortality [1, 2]. The condition accelerates atherosclerosis through hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and low-grade inflammation, establishing a pathophysiological continuum with cardiovascular disease (CVD) that operates even in the absence of progression to diabetes [3–5]. Notably, even in the absence of progression to diabetes, prediabetes is associated with a 13–16% increased risk of coronary artery disease and stroke, underscoring its clinical significance [6].

Clinical adoption of prediabetes diagnostic criteria exhibits substantial geographic variation, with profound implications for risk stratification. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria—which employ lower glycaemic thresholds (fasting plasma glucose, FPG 5.6–6.9 mmol/L or glycosylated hemoglobin A1c, HbA1c 5.7–6.4%) are widely applied in primary care in the United States and many Asian countries [7]. In China, recent nationally representative survey (2015–2017) adopted the ADA definition, reporting a prediabetes prevalence of 35.2% (95% CI 33.5–37.0%) among adults aged ≥ 18 years [8]. Conversely, WHO/IEC criteria (fasting glucose ≥ 6.1 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.0%, 67% vs. 34%) remain more commonly used in several European and public health-oriented settings, where higher specificity is prioritized to identify individuals at more unequivocally elevated risk [9]. Critically, this diagnostic divergence translates directly into differential prognostic performance. In the same Asian coronary cohort, individuals meeting the stricter WHO/IEC criteria experienced a significantly higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events than those with normoglycaemia (adjusted HR 1.50, 95% CI 1.10–2.06), whereas this association was not statistically significant for ADA-defined prediabetes (adjusted HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.81–1.68) [10]. These findings are corroborated by the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study, a large U.S. cohort with over two decades of follow-up. In ARIC, HbA1c-based definitions (ADA HbA1c 5.7–6.4% and IEC HbA1c 6.0–6.4%) were more specific and yielded higher hazard ratios for chronic kidney disease, atherosclerotic CVD, peripheral arterial disease and all-cause mortality than did fasting glucose-based definitions, whereas the ADA fasting glucose criterion (5.6–6.9 mmol/L) was the most sensitive for identifying individuals at risk of these long-term outcomes [11].

Observational studies consistently link prediabetes to subclinical atherosclerosis, as measured by carotid intima-media thickness and coronary calcium scoring, even after adjusting for traditional risk factors [12–14]. However, these associations are susceptible to residual confounding (e.g., undiagnosed metabolic syndrome) and reverse causation (e.g., pre-existing vascular pathology influencing glycemic measures). Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses have attempted to address these limitations, with recent studies suggesting a causal relationship between genetically elevated hemoglobin A1c and coronary artery disease [15–17]. Nevertheless, the causal effects of prediabetes for cardiovascular risk and mortality remain unexplored. Furthermore, whether the observed associations are causal and how these diagnostic discrepancies translate into early subclinical cardiac remodeling remain unclear.

This study addresses two critical gaps: First, it provides a direct comparative assessment of the prognostic utility of ADA versus. WHO/IEC prediabetes criteria for cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in a large, contemporary cohort with advanced phenotyping, including cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. Second, it employs Mendelian randomization to test the causal relationship between prediabetes and cardiovascular outcomes, moving beyond observational associations.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study is based on data from the UK Biobank (UKB), which stands as a comprehensive biomedical database encompassing population health and genetic research resources, recruited more than 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years from 22 assessment centers across the UK between 2006 and 2010 (mean follow-up duration: 13.5 years). The complete UKB study protocol is accessible at UKB Study Rationale. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee (REC reference for UK Biobank 11/NW/0382), and all participants provided written informed consent (Supplementary Appendix S1).

In this study, a total of 502,367 UK adults underwent initial screening. Within the UKB cohort, participants were excluded for the following reasons: missing data on blood glucose or HbA1c at baseline (n = 99,582), baseline diagnosis of involved diseases, including cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, stroke and primary hypertension (n = 123,343) and lack of polygenic risk score assessments or and lost to follow-up (n = 745). This resulted in a final analytical cohort of 278,697 participants (Fig. 1). Follow-up for each participant was censored at the date of the first incident CVD event, date of death, date of loss to follow-up, or at the administrative censor date of the linked health records, whichever occurred first.Fig. 1Study flow chart. The entire study adheres to the principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and ethical approval for the project was granted by the National Research Ethics Service.

Assessment of exposure

Glycemic Status Classification in the UK Biobank Cohort.

For the purpose of this study, to establish a comparator with stricter glycemic thresholds than the ADA criteria, we also defined a ‘WHO/IEC’ criterion. This label refers to the concurrent application of two independent standards: the impaired fasting glucose (IFG) threshold as historically defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) (FPG 6.1–6.9 mmol/L) and the HbA1c range for high risk of diabetes as suggested by the International Expert Committee (IEC) in 2009 (HbA1c 6.0–6.4%). It is important to note that while the WHO’s diagnostic guidelines for dysglycemia are primarily based on glucose measures, and the IEC provided recommendations specifically on HbA1c use, the term ‘WHO/IEC’ here is used operationally to denote this specific combination of higher cut-offs for comparative analysis. Blood samples from UKB participants were collected at baseline recruitment (2006–2010). Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) Definition.

T2DM was defined by any of the following: Self-reported physician diagnosis via touchscreen questionnaire; Use of glucose-lowering agents (including insulin or oral medications, verified by medication codes); Baseline HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol).

Participants not meeting prediabetes or T2DM criteria were classified as normoglycemic. To enhance diagnostic specificity, individuals with type 1 diabetes or secondary diabetes were excluded based on self-reported medical history and medication profiles.

Assessment of outcome

The primary outcomes were incident major cardiovascular disease (CVD), CVD mortality, and all-cause mortality. Incident CVD was a composite endpoint defined by the first occurrence of coronary heart disease (CHD; ICD-10 I20-I25), myocardial infarction (MI; I21-I22), stroke (I60-I64), or primary hypertension (I10). Data on hospital admissions for these events were obtained via linkage to national records: Health Episode Statistics, the Patient Episode Database, and the Scottish Morbidity Records. Mortality data were sourced from National Health Service Digital (England and Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register within the National Records of Scotland (Scotland). Death from CVD was identified using codes I00-I99.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates were selected based on their association with diabetes and cardiovascular risk, as well as previous publications [10, 21–24]. Within the UKB cohort, these covariates encompassed a range of factors such as age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), sun exposure time, educational level, physical activity, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, healthy diet score, healthy sleep pattern, history of cancer, dyslipidemia, heart failure and polygenic risk score for corresponding outcome. Specific information on covariates is presented in Supplementary Table S3 and the field ID of the questions above in the UKB is presented in Supplementary Appendix S1. For individuals who did not provide information about their alcohol drinking, an ‘‘Unknown’’ status was assigned. Grades of genetic risk, categorized as low, moderate, and high genetic risk, were defined based on the tertiles of PRS for their outcomes. The PRS for hypertension utilized in this study was derived from the UKB database and has been previously detailed [25].

GWAS (genome-wide association study) of prediabetes and mendelian randomization

We performed GWAS using the machine-learning-based method REGENIE of the prediabetes trait across 50,048 cases and 148,630 controls of European descent from the UKB data [26]. REGENIE is an efficient two-step whole-genome regression method for genetic association tests that accounts for both sample relatedness and population structure. Firth logistic regression test was used for the binary trait of prediabetes. The REGENIE method involves two steps. In step 1, we included directly genotyped variants (a total of 588,699) with a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 1%, missingness lower than 10%, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium test p-value greater than 1 × 10–15. In step 2, we performed the single-variant association testing using the imputed dataset (a total of 7,677,418 autosomal variants) with covariates of age, sex, and the first ten ancestral principal components. Independent significant loci were identified as those with p < 5 × 10–8, r2 < 0.1, and distance > 250 kb.

We then employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization approach, utilizing summary-level data from genome-wide association studies conducted on individuals of European ancestry. We utilized the obtained GWAS summary results of prediabetes as the primary exposure for our MR analysis. We also included results from other GWAS for CVD (GWAS IDs: ebi-a-GCST90029019), coronary heart disease (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST000998), myocardial infarction (GWAS ID: finn-b-I9_MI_STRICT_EXNONE), stroke (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST005838) and primary hypertension (GWAS ID: ukb-b-12493). Instrumental variables were selected based on stringent criteria: (1) genome-wide significance with a p-value threshold of less than 5 × 10–8, (2) absence of linkage disequilibrium (R2 < 0.01 within a 10,000 kilobase window), and (3) no evidence of potential pleiotropic effects. The number of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) used as instruments varied from 54 to 78 across different MR analyses, depending on the exposure and outcome data sources (Supplementary Table S4).

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging assessment

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging was performed in a subset of UK Biobank participants during a dedicated imaging visit using a 1.5 Tesla scanner (MAGNETOM Aera, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) [27]. The acquisition protocol followed the standardised UK Biobank CMR protocol described in detail by Petersen et al [28]. A complete description of the sequences, image analysis, quality control procedures, and parameter definitions is provided in Supplementary Appendix S2.

Importantly, glycemic status was determined from blood samples collected at the baseline assessment (2006–2010), whereas CMR imaging was performed during a separate imaging visit conducted several years later. This temporal discordance may introduce non-differential misclassification of exposure, biasing effect estimates toward the null; therefore, all CMR findings should be regarded as conservative and hypothesis-generating [29].

The following CMR parameters were analysed: left ventricular (LV) and right ventricular (RV) volumes, mass, ejection fraction; LV mass index (LVMI); LV mass-to-volume ratio (MVR); LV mean myocardial wall thickness (global average); and LV global longitudinal strain (GLS). LVMI was calculated as LV mass divided by body surface area (BSA) using the Du Bois formula (BSA = 0.007184 × height(cm)^0.725 × weight(kg)^0.425) [30].

A complete list of all parameters with their corresponding actual variable names in the analytical dataset is provided in Supplementary Table 34. Associations between glycemic status (normoglycemia, prediabetes, type 2 diabetes) and each continuous CMR metric were assessed using multivariable linear regression models adjusted for the same covariate set as in the primary Cox analyses. Owing to the modest sample size (n = 2512), these analyses are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables representing baseline characteristics were reported as count (frequency) and compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared across groups utilizing the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. For instances of missing data, we employed imputation by substituting the median for variables with a messiness rate < 5%. For variables with a messiness rate ≥ 5% (Supplementary Table S5), missing data were treated as a distinct category labeled ‘‘unknown.’’

Normality of continuous baseline variables was assessed by skewness, kurtosis and the Shapiro–Wilk test (with random subsampling due to large sample size); variables with |skewness|> 1 or |kurtosis|> 3 were considered non-normally distributed. Depending on normality and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test), we applied one-way ANOVA (or Welch ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normal variables. For omnibus tests with P < 0.05, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey HSD, Games–Howell, or Dunn–Bonferroni methods as appropriate. Full results of normality assessment, omnibus tests, and all post-hoc comparisons are provided in Supplementary Tables 35 and 36.

The association between glucose status and cardiovascular outcomes was evaluated using a fully adjusted multi-variable Cox proportional hazards model. Covariates included in the fully adjusted model were: age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index, sun exposure time, Townsend Deprivation Index, educational level, physical activity (MET), smoking status, alcohol consumption status, healthy diet score, healthy sleep score, history of cancer, heart failure, dyslipidemia, and the polygenic risk score for the corresponding outcome. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported as effect estimates. To explore potential non-linear relationships, restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 3 knots at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles were applied to flexibly model the potential non-linear associations between glycemic parameters and clinical outcomes. The likelihood ratio test comparing the spline model to a linear model was used to evaluate statistical evidence of nonlinearity (P for non-linearity < 0.001).

Next, we explored the associations between different criteria of prediabetes and cardiovascular risk in participants with varying levels of genetic susceptibility to corresponding outcome (categorized as low, moderate, and high genetic risk by tertiles).

In addition to the analyses presented earlier, we conducted several supplementary analyses to enhance the comprehensiveness of our study within the UKB cohort. Cumulative risk of cardiovascular risk among different criteria of glucose status is illustrated using Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves, and statistical significance is determined through a log-rank test. Moreover, we performed subgroup analyses to explore the associations of glucose status with the risk of cardiovascular events. Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying participants based on various factors, including demographic data and comorbidities.

Several supplementary and sensitivity analyses were performed, including subgroup analyses and the exclusion of early incident cases or participants with a history of cancer. Detailed descriptions and full results of these analyses are presented in the Supplementary Material.

In MR analysis, we used odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI to estimate the disease risks caused by prediabetes. For the primary MR analysis, we utilized the random-effects inverse-variance weighted (RE-IVW) method to estimate the causal effect. Several other MR analyses, including IVW, fixed effects IVW, MR-Egger regression, weighted median (WM), and penalized WM, were performed to assess the robustness of the results. Heterogeneity among the SNP specific causal estimates was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, with a significant P value indicating variability beyond that expected by chance alone. The MR Egger intercept test was used to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy. To confirm the causal effect of any single SNP, we conducted a leave-one-out (LOO) analysis by discarding each exposure-associated SNP and repeatedly performing IVW analysis.

We performed analyses with the R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance in 2-sided statistical testing.

In addition, we examined the distribution of prediabetes cases according to the specific glycemic parameter(s) that met the diagnostic thresholds. Among the 50,147 participants with ADA-defined prediabetes, 18,696 (37.3%) were identified by elevated FPG only, 25,955 (51.8%) by elevated HbA1c only, and 5425 (10.8%) met both criteria. Among the 12,514 participants meeting the stricter WHO/IEC criteria, the corresponding proportions were 6750 (53.9%) for FPG only, 5048 (40.3%) for HbA1c only, and 645 (5.2%) for both (see Supplementary Table 31). Furthermore, all participants meeting the WHO/IEC prediabetes criteria also met the ADA criteria, resulting in a complete overlap of 12,514 individuals, while 37,633 participants were classified as prediabetes exclusively by the ADA criteria (Supplementary Table 37).

Cardiovascular disease incidence

ADA-defined prediabetes

As detailed in Table 2 and Fig. 2, multivariable-adjusted Cox models demonstrated that ADA-defined prediabetes was associated with a 14% increased risk of incident CVD (HR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.12–1.16). Significant risk elevations were consistently observed across major cardiovascular endpoints, including coronary heart disease (CHD, HR = 1.17, 95% CI 1.14–1.20), myocardial infarction (MI, HR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.17–1.25), stroke (HR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.19–1.27), and primary hypertension (HR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.14–1.18). Furthermore, RCS analysis of log-transformed FPG (FPG-logtrans) revealed significant non-linear positive associations with all these endpoints (P for non-linearity < 0.001), showing a monotonic WHO/IEC-defined prediabetes

In comparison, prediabetes defined by the stricter WHO/IEC criteria was associated with a stronger CVD risk, corresponding to a 23% higher hazard (HR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.19–1.27). The risks for individual cardiovascular endpoints were also substantially elevated, with HRs of 1.28 (95% CI 1.23–1.35) for CHD, 1.33 (95% CI 1.26–1.41) for MI, 1.33 (95% CI 1.25–1.40) for stroke, and 1.25 (95% CI 1.20–1.29) for primary hypertension. Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed consistently higher cumulative event rates in prediabetes versus normoglycemia under both criteria, with significantly greater risk observed for WHO/IEC-defined compared to ADA-defined prediabetes (log-rank P < 0.001). RCS analysis for log-transformed HbA1c (HbA1c-logtrans) highlighted differential patterns: while risks for all endpoints increased monotonically above a reference point of 1.65 (~ 5.7%), the associations below this threshold varied. For MI, CHD, and stroke, risks remained stable near the null, whereas for CVD and primary hypertension, a downward trend in risk estimates was noted in the lower HbA1c range; this pattern may reflect modeling variability or residual confounding and should not be interpreted as a protective effect.

Mortality outcomes

Cardiovascular mortality

Prediabetes was associated with a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular mortality, with the magnitude of risk differing by diagnostic criteria. The HR was 1.47 (95% CI 1.34–1.62) for ADA-defined and 1.96 (95% CI 1.70–2.25) for WHO/IEC-defined prediabetes, indicating a stronger association under the stricter criteria. Within the ADA-defined groups, T2DM showed the highest risk (HR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.78–2.37). RCS analysis indicated monotonic increasing relationships of both FPG-logtrans and HbA1c-logtrans with cardiovascular mortality risk above their respective reference points.

All-cause mortality

Both prediabetes criteria were associated with elevated all-cause mortality, with HRs of 1.24 (95% CI 1.19–1.28) for ADA and 1.39 (95% CI 1.30–1.47) for WHO/IEC definitions. Notably, under WHO/IEC criteria, the all-cause mortality risk in prediabetes was not statistically different from that in T2DM (HR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.95–1.13; P = 0.437). RCS analysis for HbA1c and all-cause mortality showed risk ratios remaining near unity below the reference point but accelerating markedly above it.

Kaplan-Meier analyses corroborated these findings, showing elevated cumulative mortality rates for prediabetes compared to normoglycemia. Under WHO/IEC criteria, dynamic temporal patterns emerged: for cardiovascular mortality, prediabetes incidence initially exceeded T2DM until approximately year 8, after which T2DM risk escalated more steeply. All-cause mortality trajectories showed a similar near-parallel course in the first 8 years, followed by a gradual divergence toward higher risk in T2DM, although confidence intervals overlapped considerably throughout follow-up.

Impact of prediabetes on CMR parameters

Among the 2512 participants included in the CMR substudy, the classification according to ADA criteria yielded 2152 normoglycemic individuals, 301 individuals with prediabetes, and 59 individuals with type 2 diabetes. Under the WHO/IEC criteria, 2406 participants were normoglycemic, 47 had prediabetes, and 59 had T2DM (Table 3). Given the smaller sample size available for CMR phenotyping (n = 2512), the following analyses should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted within the context of limited statistical power. Specifically, under the ADA criteria, patients with prediabetes showed less pronounced changes in LV mass index (β = 0.14, 95% CI 0.05–1.63) and LV end-diastolic volume (LV EDV) (β = 3.20, 95% CI 1.12–6.89). However, under the WHO/IEC criteria, prediabetes patients exhibited a more significant increase in LV mass index (β = 1.95, 95% CI 0.24–5.40) and elevation in LV mass-to-volume ratio (LV MVR) (β = 0.19, 95% CI 0.11–0.37), indicating early signs of concentric remodeling. In contrast, patients with T2DM also showed a significant increase in LV EDV (β = 10.22, 95% CI 5.09–18.35) and a further increase in LV MVR (β = 0.35, 95% CI 0.22–0.51), reflecting a decrease in late ventricular compliance. Regarding RV parameters, changes during the prediabetes stage were more subtle. Under the WHO/IEC criteria, prediabetes patients showed a marginally reduced RV stroke volume (RV SV) (β = − 4.06 mL, 95% CI − 9.09–0.97), while T2DM patients exhibited significant reductions in RV EDV (β = − 6.81 mL, 95% CI − 12.51–1.10), suggesting impaired RV volumetric compensatory function. However, there were no significant differences in RV ejection fraction (RV EF) among the subgroups, indicating that early structural changes precede functional impairments. Additionally, no significant abnormalities were observed in LA and right atrium RA volumes in prediabetes under the ADA criteria. However, there was a trend of increased LA maximal volume (β = 4.54 mL, 95% CI 0.85–9.77) in prediabetes patients under the WHO/IEC criteria.

Mendelian randomization analyses

The results of the two-sample MR analyses indicate that prediabetes was causally associated with increased CVD (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02), coronary heart disease (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.17), myocardial infarction (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.19), stroke (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.10), and primary hypertension (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02) risks (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis confirms the robustness of the results. Genetic risk stratification

To assess the interaction between prediabetes definitions and genetic predisposition, participants were stratified into low, moderate, and high genetic risk groups based on PRS tertiles for cardiovascular outcomes. No significant interactions were observed between prediabetes definitions and polygenic risk score tertiles for any cardiovascular outcome (all P for interaction > 0.05). For example, in the case of incident CVD, the hazard ratios for ADA-defined prediabetes across low, moderate, and high genetic risk tertiles were 1.15, 1.14, and 1.13, respectively (P for interaction = 0.117). Complete results for all endpoints are available in Supplementary Tables S6–S11.

It should be noted that the absence of significant interaction may be influenced by limited power to detect effect modification or by the specific genetic variants captured in the polygenic risk scores, which may not fully represent pathways that interact with dysglycemia.

Sensitivity analyses

Excluding participants occurring outcomes within the first-year (n = 276,079, Supplementary Table 27) or with a history of cancer (n = 259,174, Supplementary Table 28) did not materially alter the results. After excluding participants who had an endpoint event within the first year, the significant association with CVD events was maintained for WHO/IEC-defined prediabetes (HR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.17–1.25), whereas ADA-defined prediabetes showed a consistent association (HR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.10–1.14, Supplementary Table 27). Adjustment for waist circumference, either as a replacement for or in addition to BMI, produced hazard ratios for prediabetes-associated cardiovascular outcomes that were virtually identical to those from the primary BMI-adjusted models (Supplementary Table 32). Consistent with the results of the primary analyses, there was a significant difference in the risk of events in subjects with prediabetes compared to normal subjects and no difference in the vast majority of endpoint events with subjects with T2DM, and the results did not differ significantly by exclusion factors.

Sensitivity analyses revealed a gradient of cardiovascular risk across prediabetes subgroups defined by different glycemic parameters. Compared to normoglycemic individuals within each diagnostic framework, the adjusted HR for incident CVD among ADA-defined prediabetes was 1.09 (95% CI 1.06–1.13) for the FPG-only subgroup, 1.13 (1.10–1.16) for the HbA1c-only subgroup, and 1.24 (1.19–1.30) for those meeting both criteria. Under the WHO/IEC criteria, the HRs were 1.13 (1.08–1.19), 1.21 (1.15–1.27), and 1.77 (1.58–1.97), respectively (Supplementary Table 31).

Discussion

Our study advances the understanding of prediabetes as a heterogeneous metabolic state by providing a head-to-head comparison of the cardiovascular implications dictated by the two predominant diagnostic criteria, an area of ongoing debate and clinical uncertainty. The WHO/IEC-defined prediabetes group (4.5% prevalence) exhibited markedly elevated risks for incident CVD (HR 1.23), cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.96), and all-cause mortality (HR 1.39), surpassing the risks associated with the broader ADA-defined cohort (18.0% prevalence; CVD HR 1.14). These findings align with large-scale epidemiological studies demonstrating that stricter glycemic thresholds better capture individuals with advanced β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance—key drivers of endothelial dysfunction and subclinical atherosclerosis [3, 31]. In contrast, the ADA criteria encompass a heterogeneous population where transient hyperglycemia may reflect compensatory mechanisms or early metabolic dysregulation, as evidenced by longitudinal data reported that 54% of patients with ADA-defined prediabetes returned to normoglycemia at least once during the 3-year follow-up period [32]. Our supplementary analyses further illuminate the heterogeneous nature of prediabetes. The differential CVD risk observed between subgroups defined solely by FPG versus HbA1c suggests these biomarkers may capture distinct, if overlapping, pathophysiological processes—such as acute postprandial dysregulation versus chronic glycemic exposure. This heterogeneity underscores that the choice of diagnostic criterion not only affects the size of the identified at-risk population but may also select for groups with varying cardiovascular risk profiles. Consequently, the generalizability of studies on ‘prediabetes’ depends critically on the specific glycemic parameters used to define it.

Our findings demonstrate a clear gradient in cardiovascular risk stratification between the two predominant diagnostic frameworks. The ADA criteria, by virtue of their lower glycemic thresholds, cast a wider net that supports early identification and population-level screening—a strategy aligned with preventive cardiology principles that emphasize broad risk factor interception. In contrast, the stricter WHO/IEC criteria identified a subgroup with markedly higher cardiovascular and mortality risks, underscoring their utility in contexts where specificity and intensive risk management are prioritized, such as in patients with additional cardiometabolic risk factors or established subclinical atherosclerosis. Rather than advocating for a universal replacement of one criterion by the other, our data support a context-adapted, sequential approach: initial screening with ADA criteria to capture a broader at-risk population, followed by risk refinement using WHO/IEC thresholds—particularly when HbA1c ≥ 6.0% or when clinical decisions regarding pharmacotherapy or intensive lifestyle intervention are required. This stepwise strategy resonates with recent guideline updates that emphasize individualized risk assessment and aligns with our observation that WHO/IEC-defined prediabetes carries a risk profile sometimes approximating that of established diabetes.

The results of MR analysis and observational studies complement each other significantly. Although the effect sizes derived from genetic prediction (OR = 1.01–1.12) are lower than those estimated by traditional epidemiological studies (HR = 1.14–1.33), the directions of the associations are entirely consistent. This provides a genetic basis for establishing differentiated thresholds for blood glucose control, corroborating findings from prior genetic studies [33]. Notably, while elevated cardiovascular risks were observed across all genetic susceptibility strata—for example, WHO/IEC-defined prediabetes in the high genetic risk tertile was associated with a hazard ratio of 1.78 (95% CI 1.42–2.22, Supplementary Table 11) for CVD mortality—no statistically significant multiplicative interaction was detected between glycemic status and polygenic risk score strata for any outcome (all P for interaction > 0.05). The observed risk pattern, wherein higher genetic susceptibility appeared to coincide with more pronounced dysglycemia-related risk, may tentatively reflect mechanisms suggested in experimental models, where genetic variants that amplify oxidative stress are known to exacerbate hyperglycemia-induced vascular injury [34–36]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the lack of significant interaction may reflect methodological limitations, including the moderate statistical power for interaction tests and the possibility that the PRS did not encompass all relevant pathways that might modify the effect of dysglycemia on cardiovascular outcomes.

The distinct shapes of the nonlinear risk trajectories for HbA1c and FPG suggest they may capture different aspects of dysglycemia-related pathophysiology. HbA1c demonstrated a J-shaped association with CVD, where levels below approximately 5.7% were associated with a paradoxically lower risk. This pattern may reflect, in part, that low HbA1c in some population-based studies can be associated with nutritional status, anemia, or other comorbidities [37, 38]. In contrast, FPG exhibited a predominantly linear association with CVD risk across its physiological range. This more direct, dose–response relationship aligns with studies suggesting that fasting hyperglycemia more immediately disrupts endothelial homeostasis and insulin signaling [39]. The inflection point of the HbA1c curve around 6.0%, beyond which CVD risk rises more steeply, corresponds to thresholds linked to accelerated subclinical atherosclerosis (e.g., coronary artery calcification) in imaging studies, reinforcing its clinical relevance as a marker of sustained glycemic exposure [40, 41].

CMR analyses provide novel insights into the heterogeneous structural remodeling associated with prediabetes, critically modulated by diagnostic criteria. Participants classified as prediabetic under WHO/IEC criteria exhibited increases in LV mass index and significant concentric remodeling, as evidenced by elevated LV MVR, whereas ADA-defined prediabetes showed no statistically significant alterations in these parameters. These findings align with prior studies linking dysglycemia to subclinical cardiac pathology—both in established diabetes and across the glycaemic spectrum. Jensen et al. demonstrated that diabetes mellitus is associated with concentric remodelling, reduced ventricular volumes, and impaired myocardial strain in the UK Biobank population, while Bertrand et al. recently reported that type 2 diabetes independently predicts lower stroke volume and increased LV wall thickness in individuals without prior cardiovascular disease [42, 43]. Our results extend these observations by showing that stricter glycaemic thresholds (WHO/IEC) identify a prediabetic subgroup with already detectable concentric remodelling, a phenotype strongly associated with incident cardiovascular disease. Exploratory CMR analyses provided preliminary insights into cardiac structural changes across glycemic categories, though these observations are constrained by the modest subsample size and confidence intervals that often included the null. Therefore, they should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The observed LV MVR elevation in WHO/IEC-defined prediabetes mirrors findings from population-based cohorts, where concentric hypertrophy independently predicted CVD mortality [44]. Mechanistically, this remodeling likely reflects insulin resistance-driven myocardial fibrosis and vascular stiffness, exacerbated by chronic hyperglycemia—a pathophysiological continuum previously described in experimental models [45]. In contrast, ADA-defined prediabetes may encompass individuals with transient or compensatory hyperglycemia, insufficient to induce overt structural changes, thereby diluting prognostic utility. Together, these findings position prediabetes as a dynamic transitional phase where timely intervention may decouple metabolic dysfunction from end-organ damage.

Although this study is based on a large-scale prospective cohort and MR analysis, it still has certain limitations. First, UK Biobank participants are predominantly of European ancestry and were aged 40–69 years at recruitment; therefore, our findings may not be directly generalisable to other ethnicities or to younger populations. Although similar trends have been reported in Asian cohorts, dedicated studies in more diverse ethnic and age groups—particularly among adolescents and young adults, where the global epidemics of prediabetes and obesity are escalating—are urgently needed to confirm and extend our conclusions. Secondly, glycemic status in this study was defined using a single baseline measurement of FPG and/or HbA1c. This approach does not capture within-individual variability over time, regression to the mean, or the dynamic progression or regression of prediabetes. Such non-differential misclassification likely attenuates the observed effect sizes toward the null; therefore, the hazard ratios reported here may be conservative estimates of the true associations between prediabetes and cardiovascular outcomes. Future studies incorporating repeated glycemic measures or continuous glucose monitoring would help refine phenotypic classification and provide more precise risk estimates. Next, important covariates—including lifestyle factors, diet, and medication use—were self-reported and thus susceptible to measurement error and recall bias. Moreover, the exclusion of approximately 20% of the initial cohort due to missing baseline glycemic data may introduce selection bias if these individuals differed systematically from those included in the analysis. In addition, linkage to primary care records in UK Biobank is currently incomplete (approximately 45% of the cohort). Consequently, chronic conditions that are typically diagnosed and managed exclusively in primary care settings—such as certain subtypes of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, or early-stage diabetes complications—may be under-ascertained in our study [46]. This non-differential outcome misclassification would tend to bias hazard ratios toward the null, further supporting the conservative nature of our estimates. Future studies with complete primary care linkage will be valuable to confirm and extend our findings. Furthermore, we conducted supplementary analyses by incorporating additional covariates—including detailed lipid profiles, inflammatory markers, renal function, and cardiovascular medication use—as presented in Supplementary Table 29. Nevertheless, despite these extended adjustments, residual confounding from clinically relevant but unavailable measures in our cohort (such as insulin resistance markers [e.g., HOMA-IR] or direct indicators of subclinical atherosclerosis [e.g., carotid intima-media thickness]) remains possible. Future studies with more granular phenotyping are warranted to further clarify the underlying mechanistic pathways. Finally, the MR analysis is limited by its reliance on genetic variants associated with a broad prediabetes phenotype; these instruments may reflect pathways related to generalized dysglycemia. Further studies using genetic instruments more specific to prediabetes are needed to clarify its distinct clinical implications.

Conclusion

In summary, prediabetes represents a pivotal stage for mitigating cardiometabolic decline. The differential risk stratification afforded by ADA and WHO/IEC criteria should not be viewed as a discrepancy to be resolved, but as complementary tools that can be integrated into a holistic, staged management framework. We recommend that clinical practice consider both the scope of screening (where ADA criteria may be advantageous) and the intensity of intervention (where WHO/IEC criteria may better identify candidates for aggressive management). Future guideline development could benefit from explicitly incorporating such a dual-threshold approach, accompanied by standardized protocols for risk communication and subsequent monitoring. Ultimately, through early detection, stratified risk assessment, and tailored intervention—ranging from lifestyle modification to targeted pharmacotherapy—healthcare systems can more effectively attenuate progression toward irreversible cardiovascular complications.
