A detailed analysis of the effects of body parameters on motor function and fine motor skills measured at one-year intervals in patients with spinal muscular atrophy
Authors: Ewa Gajewska, Magdalena Sobieska, Aleksandra Bieniaszewska, Anna Sowińska, Joanna Michalska, Barbara Steinborn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal muscular atrophy mainly affects motor neurons in the spinal cord, leading to progressive muscular atrophy and weakness. This study aims to carefully analyse the effect of body parameters on motor function and fine motor skills, measured at one-year intervals, in patients with spinal muscular atrophy.MethodsThe study included 38 participants diagnosed with SMA. Patients were divided into three groups based on their level of motor function: non-sitters (n = 9), sitters (n = 23), and walkers (n = 6). Anthropometric measurements (weight, height/length) were taken in all participants, and BMI and BMI-z-score were calculated. The motor function was assessed using validated scales: CHOP-INTEND for the non-sitters group and HFMSE scale for the sitters and walkers group. The RULM scale assessed fine motor skills for the sitters and walkers group.ResultsAt the 12-month follow-up, the CHOP-INTEND scale showed improvement in most non-sitter patients (6/9). The sitter’s group was more heterogeneous on the HFMSE scale, with 10 out of 23 patients showing deterioration in function, 8 showing improvement, while the walker’s group showed the least variability. In the non-sitters group, the dominant category of BMI z-score was underweight, while half of the sitters in the group were underweight, although there were just as many healthy weights. The strongest group, the walkers, was mostly healthy. We have observed a certain tendency that as BMI increases, motor function increases in all groups, while fine motor skill decreases in the most varied sitters group.ConclusionsAs BMI increases in SMA patients, motor function improves. Fine motor skill deteriorates as BMI increases in the sitter’s group. The assessment of individual components of motor function and fine motor skills reveals the actual changes that occur over time in patients with SMA.Keywords: SMA, Motor function, BMI, BMI-z-score, Upper limb

Background

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), linked to chromosome 5q (SMA), is an autosomal recessive disorder caused primarily by biallelic (homozygous) variants in the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene, affecting approximately 1 in 15,000 live births [1]. Spinal muscular atrophy primarily affects motor neurons in the spinal cord, resulting in progressive muscular atrophy and weakness.

Historically, SMA was classified into types based on the natural history of the disease. In the new therapeutic era, phenotypes are shifting [1]. SMA spans a wide range of phenotypes, including children who cannot sit (non-sitters- SMA type 1), those who can sit but cannot walk (sitters- SMA type 2), and those who can walk with difficulty (walkers- SMA type 3) [2, 3]. Functional scores, such as the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Infant Test (CHOP INTEND), Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale Expanded (HFMSE), and Revised Upper Limb Module (RULM), are used to assess disease severity, clinical progression, and treatment effects in SMA.

According to the recommendations published in 2018, all SMA patients should undergo neurologic examinations every 6 months. Such examinations should include the assessment of motor functions using an appropriate functional scale, conducted by a physiotherapist. The choice of a scale should be based on the patient’s age, SMA type, and current neurological status [4]. Functional status and, therefore, the ability to use an appropriate measuring tool was determined by the ability of the patient to sit by himself for at least three seconds (sitters) or not (non-sitters) [5, 6]. Thus, the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Infant Test (CHOP INTEND) scale is used to analyse the motor function of “non-sitters” patients. In contrast, the Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale – Expanded (HFMSE) is used for patients who sit and walk (sitters and walkers). The Revised Upper Limb Module (RULM) is used to assess fine motor skills for “sitters” and “walkers” patients.

One of the alarming symptoms in patients with spinal muscular atrophy is their body mass. Analysis of Body Mass Index (BMI) reference values based on the general pediatric population is not a reliable indicator of the nutritional status of children with SMA, as they exhibit a distinct growth pattern associated with the disease [7]. Previous publications compared World Health Organisation (WHO) percentiles of the most vulnerable children with SMA1 and showed that body weight was significantly lower than that of healthy peers of both sexes and at all ages. It has also been demonstrated that SMA2 patients have a lower weight than the general pediatric population since the first years of life [7]. In contrast, limited research analyses the effect of nutrition on functional levels in SMA patients. Published studies have shown that body composition mildly to moderately correlates with motor function, depending on the clinical phenotype. According to Barnello et al., this could be explained by the higher prevalence of severely underweight SMA1 children, whereas those with SMA2 had nutritional status that varied from under- to overnutrition [8]. The risk of inadequate nutrition in children with SMA results in adverse effects on their body composition and nutritional status, ultimately affecting their functional abilities [7]. In SMA patients, the BMI z-score parameter is used as a relative measure of BMI, taking into account the patient’s age and sex. Patients with a low BMI/age z-score were at a greater risk of developing further reduction in those parameters. According to Ferramtini et al., low BMI/age z-score is a common pattern/symptom/feature in patients with SMA type 2 [9]. The modified z-score is a standardisation assessment that measures the strength of the outlier, that is, how much a particular score differs from the typical score. Regarding standard deviation units, it approximates the difference in score relative to the median.

Our study aims to carefully analyse the effect of body parameters on motor function and fine motor skills, as measured at one-year intervals, in patients with spinal muscular atrophy.

Methods

The prospective study was conducted twice over one year. Participants were recruited between 2022 and 2024 at a leading Clinic for SMA patients. The final diagnosis of spinal muscular atrophy was made by a neurologist (J.M.) based on genetic testing and clinical symptoms. The inclusion criteria were: genetic confirmation of 5q-autosomal recessive SMA (due to homozygous deletions or compound heterozygous mutations in the SMN1 gene [10], clinically confirmed diagnosis of SMA1, SMA2 or SMA3, no participation in any experimental pharmacological studies at the time of evaluation, age between 2 and 16, consent of parent/guardian for the study. Exclusion criteria: children diagnosed with SMA during newborn screening, lack of parental/guardian consent for the study, and other genetic diseases.

The SMA patients described in this paper, in whom the disease initially progressed naturally (before universal screening of all newborns was introduced in our country), were treated with generally available treatment as soon as it was introduced. The study included 38 participants diagnosed with SMA, including 19 females and 19 males. SMA1 type was diagnosed in 8 children, SMA2 in 20, and SMA3 in 10 children. Patients were divided into three groups based on their level of motor function: non-sitters (n = 9), sitters (n = 23), and walkers (n = 6).

The non-sitters group of patients are children who cannot maintain a sitting position on their own. Patients’ enrollment as a sitters was related to maintaining a stable sitting position for at least 5 s without upper limb suport. Walkers are children who can walk 10 m independently.

SMN2 copy number is definable, and generally, a higher SMN2 gene copy number correlates with less severe disease, although there are exceptions [1]. In our subjects, two copies of the SMN2 gene were present in 5 patients from the non-sitters group only, three copies in 2 non-sitters, 21 sitters, and four walkers, while four copies of the SMN2 gene were present in two patients from all three groups. We do not analyse the treatment used (Nusinersen, Risdiplam) or the intensity of physical therapy because, given the small size of the group, further divisions would make statistical analysis impossible.

Anthropometric measurements

All participants underwent anthropometric measurements (weight, height/length) according to standardised procedures as part of routine clinical practice. According to WHO standards, children’s height and weight were measured to the precision of 0.1 kg. In SMA 1- non-sitters patients, body weight was measured using an electronic wheelchair scale. The caregiver stood alone on the scale while the examiner clicked the tare button, which reset the scale reading to zero. The child was then handed to an adult on a scale. In this way, the scale recorded only the child’s weight [11]. In sitters, the subject’s weight was obtained using a chair scale, as they could not be assessed in the standing position, while walkers could be evaluated in the standing position. Supine Length (SL) was measured using an inflexible tape with an accuracy of 0.1 cm on the child’s right side. Nurses or caregivers placed the child supine on a suitable examination table, with the shoulders and buttocks resting on the table, the arms along the torso, palms facing up, and the legs as straight as possible, in contact with the table. In cases of scoliosis and contractures, segmental lengths were measured three times from the apex of the head to the greater trochanter of the femur, successively from the greater trochanter of the femur to the lateral epicondyle of the femur (knee) and from the knee to the distal point of the heel bone. Then, the total average measurement was summed and recorded [12]. All people involved in the measurements were instructed and trained in advance. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the following equation: BW (kg)/SL2 (m2). The nutritional status of the patients was determined by calculating BMI/age Z-scores [13, 14]. The BMI z-score value < -1 was considered a below-normal value (underweight), a value between − 1 and + 1 was a normal value (healthy weight), a value between + 1 and + 2 was overweight, and a value > + 2 was obese [13].

Motor function assessment

Patients with neuromuscular disease present unique challenges in motor assessment. The researchers used validated scales designed explicitly for SMA patients to assess motor function, including the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Infant Test (CHOP-INTEND) for the non-sitters group. In contrast, the Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale – Expanded (HFMSE) was used for patients who sit or walk (sitters and walkers). Additionally, a functional analysis of fine motor skills was conducted for the sitters and walkers group, using the Revised Upper Limb Module scale (RULM) [15]. Motor function assessment was conducted by two physiotherapists trained to perform functional evaluations who have been caring for SMA patients for 8 years (E.G. and A.B.).

The CHOP INTEND is a reliable, easily administered, well-tolerated motor test measure for SMA1 and similarly weak infants with neuromuscular disease [16]. The CHOP-INTEND scale assessed 16 activities, including head control, spontaneous movement of the upper and lower limbs, shoulder, elbow, and hip flexion, as well as Landau and Galant reflexes. Every task is assigned a score between 0 and 4 points, with a maximum of 64 possible points (Fig. 1).

Analysis of changes in motor function during 12-month follow-up in children of non-sitters, sitters, and walkers

HFMSE is particularly useful for evaluating the motor capabilities and limitations in patients with different types of SMA [17]. The scale comprised the 33 motor elements. The tasks assessed are sitting, rolling, propping on forearms and extended arms, changing positions from sitting to standing, jumping, squatting, climbing stairs, and others. Every question has a possible score range of 0 to 2, where 2 indicates that the task was completed correctly, 1 indicates that the task was performed with some errors, and zero indicates that the task was not completed. The maximum score is 66 (Fig. 1) [18, 19]. The RULM is an assessment scale developed to evaluate upper limb function in patients with SMA, particularly those who can maintain a sitting position but have difficulty walking or experience progressive muscle weakness in their upper limbs [3, 20]. The RULM is assessed in a sitting position, using a specific test set (coins, a Ziploc container, a button light, and weights of different masses). The test consists of 20 tasks, with the entry item (A) serving as functional class identification, which is not included in the total score. Eighteen elements were scored from 0 to 2 points (2 points means the task was completed correctly, 1 point - the task was completed with compensation/or less effective, and 0- the task was not completed). One question with a Ziploc container was scored from 0 to 1 (0 means a failure to perform; 1 is the correct task performance). The maximum number of points achievable was 37 (Fig. 1) [18].

For each scale, the number of children who achieved the maximum number of points and therefore could not improve in the second test (ceiling effect) is indicated.

Statistical analysis

Interval variables were described by the mean value with standard deviation, and ordinal variables were described by median, quartile range, and min-max values. Only min-max values are given for the underrepresented group of walkers. Binary variables are shown as counts. The t-test for related variables or the Wilcoxon test was used to test for differences over time. Pearson’s test was used to test the correlation of the interval variables, reporting the r-value. The Spearman test was used to assess the correlation between ordinal or interval variables, and the rho value was reported. To examine the relationships between the dependent variable and multiple independent variables, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted. All predictors were entered into the regression model simultaneously using the enter method.

A significance level of p < 0.05 was assumed for all calculations. The Statistica.pl version 12.1 was used in all calculations.

Results

First analysed were changes in motor function during a 12-month follow-up in patients with SMA, categorised into non-sitters, sitters, and walkers. It was verified how many patients experienced improvement, no change or worsening of motor functions, with clinically significant changes taken into account. According to the literature, a clinically significant improvement in the CHOP-INTEND, HFMSE, and RULM scales is considered to be an improvement of 4 points or more, 3 points or more, and 2 points or more, respectively [21, 22]. For the group of non-sitters assessed with the CHOP-INTEND scale, improvement was achieved in most patients (6/9), with clinically significant improvement demonstrated in one child. Two patients worsened, with one significant clinical deterioration. For the group of sitters assessed with the HFMSE scale, 10 of 23 patients showed deterioration of function, of which 3 cases were clinically significant. Improvement occurred in 8 patients, with 3 cases being clinically significant. For the walkers’ group, two patients showed improvement, while the others exhibited no change or worsening of function. In one child, the improvement was clinically significant. In the RULM scale group of the sitter, improvement was observed in 11 of 23 patients (in 5 cases, the improvement was clinically significant).

In comparison, significant worsening occurred in 8 patients, of whom 7 experienced clinically significant worsening. In the case of the walker group, the majority of patients showed no change, which was due to their maximum score at the first follow-up. One patient showed significant improvement, achieving the maximum scale score at the second follow-up.

The number of scores obtained from motor function testing measured by the CHOP-INTEND scale in the non-sitters group increased after 12 months from 35 to 40 points. In the sitters and walkers group, changes measured by the HFMSE scale increased sequentially from 27 to 29. Due to the insufficient size of the walker’s group, only the minimum and maximum values are included in the table (Table 2). Notably, the level of fine motor development, as measured by the RULM scale, remained unchanged in the sitter’s group (27 points) during the 12-month follow-up (Table 2).

The CHOP-INTEND (non-sitters), HFMSE (sitters and walkers), and RULM (sitters and walkers) functional scales were then used to assess the number of patients who had improved/decreased or experienced no change in each motor function component tested, along with the extent of the improvement (Fig. 1). Ranges of improvement and deterioration were determined based on the scale scores used. Thus, for the HFMSE and RULM scales, the ranges for improvement were defined as + 1 and + 2 and for deterioration as -1 and − 2. For the CHOP-INTEND scale, additional values of + 3 and + 4 and − 3 and − 4 were considered due to the different range of scores in the scale itself. The MAX parameter was used to differentiate between patients who showed no improvement due to no change in function (no change-0) and those who had already achieved the maximum score at the first examination (MAX-0).

For the CHOP-INTEND scale, lack of improvement dominated in most items. The MAX parameter was dominant in grip function, hip adductors, and elbow flexion. Shoulder flexion and elbow flexion improved in three patients. Three patients exhibited deterioration in the function of spontaneous movement of the lower limb, head alignment with the centre, and hip flexion and dorsiflexion of the foot (Fig. 1). The HFMSE and RULM scales were assessed separately for the sitters and walkers groups. For the HFMSE scale, the primary difference between the groups was the dominance of the MAX parameter in the walkers’ group. In the case of the sitters’ group, the dominance of the MAX parameter was evident in the first part of the scale.

In contrast, in the second part, which contained the more complex movement tasks, the dominance of the parameter remained unchanged. Considering the sitter’s group, the scale elements showing the most significant improvement were the four-point kneeling position and the transition from high kneeling to one-legged kneeling. In contrast, the deterioration elements were the transition from lying to sitting or standing without assistance. In the case of the walker’s group, improvement was achieved in key elements, including the transition from high kneeling to one-legged kneeling, stair climbing, and forward jumping. Deterioration occurred in the functions of squatting, jumping, climbing, and descending stairs, both with and without handrail support, in the same child (Fig. 1).

In the RULM scale, the MAX parameter completely dominated the walkers’ group. Notably, 5 out of 6 patients reached the maximum score on the scale at the first examination, and one of the children, who was improving the function of paper tearing and arm flexion with a 1 kg weight, reached this score at the second observation. When the sitter’s group was analysed, the MAX parameter dominated 10 of the 19 items—no change dominated in 6 parameters. The parameters in which patients showed significant improvement were paper tearing and arm inversion, both without weight and with a 500 g weight. The worsened functions were pressing the light switch and tearing paper (Fig. 1).

During the 12-month follow-up, the BMI of most patients remained unchanged. In the non-sitters group, one patient experienced a decrease in BMI, while another had an increase. The group also had a preponderance of underweight patients. In the sitter’s group, we observed a reduction in BMI values for the most part. Also, there is a preponderance of patients in the underweight category, but concerning the non-sitter group, it can be seen that a large group are healthy-weight children. In contrast, the fittest SMA patients or walkers are predominantly at the same BMI level and are in the healthy weight category (Fig. 2).

The BMI category on the first assessment and BMI change after 12 months

To analyse whether there was a functional change over time, we considered the change in BMI rather than the change in z-score, a parameter that describes a specific child rather than their place in the SMA-free population. Analysis of the effect of BMI on functional assessment in non-sitters, sitters, and walkers revealed that gross motor skills improve as BMI increases in both non-sitters, as measured with the CHOP-INTEND scale, and in sitters and walkers, as measured with the HFMSE. These correlations were demonstrated to exist but were found to be insignificant (Fig. 3). Analysis of the effect of BMI on the development of fine motor skills as measured by the RULM scale in sitters showed an inverse relationship. As BMI increases, fine motor skills deteriorate. These relationships are not statistically significant, while in patients, walkers remained at the same level (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our study analysed a special group of patients known as transients, who experienced the natural course of the disease and received treatment after the first symptoms appeared. They were focused on evaluating body parameters and the impact on motor development during the 12-month follow-up. In addition, they are distinguished by a detailed analysis of motor function using the appropriate scales for SMA (CHOP-INTEND and HFMSE), as well as an analysis of fine motor skills using the RULM scale.

The two groups (Nusinersen and Risdiplam) had different timing of treatment initiation since entering our study. This was particularly evident in the Risdiplam group, whose recruitment began at the end of the clinical trial, which may have flattened the therapeutic effect. Therefore, we refrained from comparing the two groups and focused on presenting the changing functional parameters over time. Due to the nature of the drugs used, the start of the study did not completely coincide with the start of the drug and differed between the two groups.

The nutritional status of children with SMA varies significantly depending on the type of SMA. SMA 1 patients tend to be underweight [7, 23], while SMA 2 patients may be overweight or even obese, although quite a few underweight patients are also reported [7]. SMA 3 patients, that is, patients who are strong and walk frequently, have a normal nutritional status. We observed similar results in our study with a group of non-sitters, including children with weak motor skills, all of whom were classified as SMA1 type. In this group, most were underweight, and there was no obese child.

In contrast, in the walker’s group (all patients diagnosed as SMA3), there were no underweight children; most were of healthy weight. A more diversified group of child sitters (19 SMA 2 and 4 SMA3) had a preponderance of patients with the categories underweight and healthy weight; far fewer were overweight and obese. It is also worth noting that the z-score analysis helps to show children in relation to a healthy population. In contrast, BMI describes the body parameters of a particular child. Therefore, the change in BMI rather than the change in z-score was considered to analyse whether there was a functional change over time.

The motor function analysis was based on validated scales for SMA, specifically the CHOP-INTEND scale for patients with weak motor function (non-sitters) and the HFMSE for patients who are sitters or walkers. The RULM assesses fine motor function for sitters and walkers. Regardless of motor category, most of our study patients improved in function. During the 12-month follow-up, the median total CHOP-INTEND score increased from 35 to 40. However, very few children reach the maximum score for a given item, and the predominant observation is that there is no change. Clinically, when analysing motor function, an improvement or worsening is seen in one patient. At the same time, a slight increase in BMI was associated with an improvement in the CHOP-INTEND scale; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Since most underweight children were observed in this group, improved nutritional status may lead to improved function, as suggested by Pinar et al., who noted that nutritional support can have a positive effect on motor function [23].

In the sitter’s group of children, the median for the total HFMSE scale score increased from 27 to 29 during the 12-month follow-up. This sitter’s group of children was the most heterogeneous group from the standpoint of both motor function and BMI-z-score or BMI. Clinically significant changes regarding motor function were observed in three children, who improved and those who worsened. The change in individual scale items was evident in the first part. The most significant improvement was observed in the four-kneeling position and during the transition from a high to one-legged kneeling position. At the same time, the deterioration items were the transition from lying down to sitting or standing without assistance. Additionally, in these patients, an increase in BMI was associated with improved motor function, as measured by the HFMSE scale, although the difference was not statistically significant. Our observations are consistent with those of others, indicating that there are more changes in Type II than in Type III, with the latter showing more stable results over a 12-month follow-up period [24, 25].

In the walker’s group (n = 6), a clinically significant change in motor function was observed in one patient. We also observed improvements in motor function in tasks from the second part of the HFMSE scale, such as transitioning from straight kneeling to one-legged kneeling, stair climbing, and forward jumping. Despite the small study group, gross motor function improves with increasing BMI. In contrast, data from Baranello et al. show that motor skills were weakly correlated with BMI in non-sitters, sitters, and walkers. According to his article, this is not surprising. Given that the degree of muscle atrophy and residual muscle mass, secondary to the underlying pathophysiology, likely play a significant role in determining motor function in SMA patients [8].

Considering fine motor skills using the RULM scale in the sitter’s group, it is noteworthy that they decrease with increasing BMI. When considering the gain of one or more fine motor skills activities during the 12-month follow-up, it was observed that 11 out of 23 patients showed improvement, while 8 showed deterioration. At the same time, in the walkers group, most children remained at the same (maximum) level. The first study to analyse individual scale items in both motor function (CHOP-INTEND, HFMSE) and fine motor skill (RULM) assessment was performed by Bieniaszewska et al. [18], showing the importance of qualitative analysis and not just quantitative, due to the indication of the exact goal of therapy and monitoring of changes over time. A similar study was conducted by Coratti et al., analysing patients with SMA types 2 and 3 using the RULM scale during a 12-month follow-up. They indicated that loss of one or more activities was found in 54% of those with type 2 and 29% with type 3. One or more activities were gained in 42% of those with type II and 22% with type III. Simultaneous gains and losses occurred in 27% of those with SMA 2 and 9% with SMA 3. They pointed out that by measuring the number of lost or acquired abilities, we gain an additional method for detecting change that can be used to interpret longitudinal data collected in individuals with SMA who have been treated [24].

In the group of walkers where the loss of muscle mass is the smallest, underweight children are not observed. Lost muscle mass cannot be regained with age. It can be assumed that the increase in weight in sitters and non-sitters is due to an increase in connective tissue and adipose tissue. Concluding from the observation of children with Duchenne dystrophy, where the sign of significant muscle strength loss is the growth of connective and adipose tissue, it can be expected that in patients with SMA, a similar process is occurring [26].

It is necessary to be very careful about bringing their body weight up to the typical characteristic of their equals, as this can result in reduced function. In other studies we observed that as body weight increased, the risk of trunk asymmetry increased [27]. Other studies have shown that body composition correlates mildly or moderately with motor function, depending on the clinical phenotype [28].

Their weight increase is not associated with an increase in muscle mass. Measurements taken in SMA patients should include body composition data, not just a simple measurement of height and weight, as described in an earlier study by Baranello et al. This study suggests that body composition may be linked to higher motor abilities in SMA patients, and body composition shows promise as a potential biomarker [8].
