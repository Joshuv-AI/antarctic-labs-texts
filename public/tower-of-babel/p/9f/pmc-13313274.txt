RSV prophylactics for pediatric populations: A product review and meta-analysis of real-world effectiveness
Authors: Isabela O Oliva, Carlos R Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of acute lower respiratory tract infection and hospitalization in infants and young children worldwide. Clinical management in infants remains primarily supportive, and natural infection provides incomplete and transient immunity. For decades, prophylaxis was limited to monthly palivizumab for selected high-risk infants. Recent advances in prefusion F structural biology, antibody engineering, and maternal immunization have transformed RSV prevention. This review summarizes the biologic and clinical rationale for preventing RSV disease in pediatric populations, with emphasis on currently licensed long-acting monoclonal antibodies and maternal RSVpreF vaccination. We integrate a meta-analysis of real-world effectiveness data and discuss implementation of these novel immunoprophylactic strategies in clinical practice. Across 43 observational studies, pooled effectiveness against RSV-associated hospitalization was 80.9% for nirsevimab and 79.1% for maternal RSVpreF vaccination, demonstrating substantial real-world protection during early infancy. Success now depends on optimizing implementation and achieving equitable access.KEYWORDS: Respiratory syncytial virus, monoclonal antibodies, maternal vaccination, prefusion F glycoprotein, pediatric prophylaxis, nirsevimab, clesrovimab

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of acute lower respiratory tract infection in infants and young children and a major driver of pediatric morbidity and hospitalization worldwide.1,2 Despite decades of research, clinical management remains primarily supportive, as no specific antiviral therapy is routinely recommended for children.2,3 Natural infection induces only partial and transient immunity, resulting in recurrent infections throughout childhood and adolescence.4 Given the substantial burden of severe early-life RSV disease and the limitations of natural immunity, establishing safe, effective, and dependable preventive strategies has long been a public health priority.5–7

Until recently, prophylaxis relied exclusively on palivizumab, a monoclonal antibody restricted to high-risk infants due to high cost and monthly dosing requirements.8 For more than two decades after its approval in 1998, no additional infant RSV prophylactics were available. This changed with the approval of nirsevimab (European Union 2022, United States 2023), the maternal RSV (RSVpreF) vaccine (2023), and clesrovimab (United States, June 2025).9 These advances have made it possible to extend RSV prevention beyond narrowly defined high-risk infants.10

In this article, we summarize the biologic and clinical rationale for preventing RSV disease in pediatric populations, with emphasis on currently licensed long-acting monoclonal antibodies and maternal RSV vaccines. We integrate this narrative synthesis with a systematic review and meta-analysis of real-world effectiveness data and discuss the implementation of these novel immunoprophylactic strategies in clinical practice and public health programs.

Methods

Narrative synthesis

We conducted a structured narrative review to summarize current evidence on pediatric RSV prevention, including monoclonal antibodies, maternal vaccination, and pediatric vaccine candidates. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched for English-language articles published from January 2015 through May 2026, with additional studies identified through reference review. Sources were prioritized when they provided efficacy, safety, or regulatory data relevant to currently licensed or late-stage RSV prevention products. The full search strategy is provided in the Supplementary material.

Systematic review and meta-analysis of real-world effectiveness

To synthesize post-licensure effectiveness data, we conducted a systematic review and product-specific meta-analyses of observational studies evaluating nirsevimab and maternal RSVpreF vaccination for prevention of RSV-associated hospitalization in infants. Clesrovimab was not included in the meta-analysis because no post-licensure effectiveness studies were available at the time of the search. Detailed eligibility criteria, study selection, and risk-of-bias assessments are provided in the Supplementary Material.

We used random-effects models to estimate pooled product-specific effectiveness. Extracted relative effect estimates and their 95% confidence intervals were transformed to the log scale for analysis. Pooled relative effects were then back-transformed and expressed as effectiveness percentages using 100 × (1 − relative effect). Heterogeneity was assessed using I2, τ2, and Cochran’s Q test. Small-study effects were assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test when at least 10 studies were available. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I for non-randomized studies of interventions.11 Certainty of evidence for each pooled estimate was assessed using GRADE, accounting for risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias. These analyses followed PRISMA guidance and were registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261382759). Additional analytic details are provided in the Supplementary Material and Supplementary Tables S1-S5.

Results

The study selection process is summarized in Figure 3. After duplicate removal, 822 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. A total of 148 articles were assessed for eligibility, and 43 studies directly evaluated real-world effectiveness were identified that contributed to quantitative synthesis. A total of 74 did not meet criteria for quantitative synthesis but provided relevant biologic, clinical, safety, regulatory, or implementation context and were synthesized narratively.

RSV disease and the rationale for prevention

RSV was first discovered in 1955 from chimpanzees and subsequently identified as a major human pathogen in infants with bronchiolitis and pneumonia in 1956 (Figure 1). Clinical manifestations range from mild upper respiratory symptoms to severe bronchiolitis or pneumonia with hypoxemia and wheezing.12,13 Acute otitis media develops in approximately one-quarter of pediatric RSV cases, while neurologic complications such as apnea, seizures, or encephalopathy occur infrequently.14 Management of RSV bronchiolitis is primarily supportive. Although ribavirin is FDA-approved for treatment of RSV lower respiratory tract infection, it is generally reserved for selected severely immunocompromised patients and is not routinely recommended for otherwise healthy infants.1,15–17

Primary RSV infection typically occurs within the first year of life, with severe disease concentrated among infants younger than 6 months and those with risk factors such as prematurity, congenital heart or lung disease, immunodeficiency, or neuromuscular disorders.1,16,18 Transmission occurs through respiratory droplets and direct contact following an incubation period of 2–8 d.1,16,18 RSV exhibits strong seasonality, typically peaking in the winter in temperate climates and during rainy periods in tropical regions.19 Although the COVID-19 pandemic temporarily disrupted RSV circulation, surveillance data indicate that seasonal patterns had largely reestablished by 2024–2025, with some regional variability.19–23

Globally, RSV causes an estimated 3.4–3.6 million hospitalizations and around 101,000 deaths each year among children under five, with the vast majority occurring in resource-limited settings.16,24 In the US, RSV accounts for approximately 60,000–80,000 hospitalizations annually in children under five years.1,25,26 Mortality in high-income countries is low, generally less than 0.5%, but the healthcare burden and societal costs are substantial.27,28 Severe early-life RSV infection is also associated with recurrent wheezing and possible later asthma development, further increasing its potential long-term burden.1,16,29,30 Together, the concentration of severe disease in early infancy, limited therapeutic options, and seasonal circulation provide the clinical rationale for preventive strategies that protect infants before or during their first RSV season.

RSV biology

RSV belongs to the genus Orthopneumovirus within the Pneumoviridae family and is an enveloped, negative-sense, single-stranded RNA virus with an approximately 15-kb genome comprising 10 genes encoding 11 proteins. Two major antigenic subgroups, RSV-A and RSV-B, co-circulate globally and undergo periodic genotype replacement with moderate antigenic drift.31,32 Viral entry is mediated by the fusion (F) and attachment (G) glycoproteins (Figure 2).33,34 RSV primarily infects ciliated epithelial cells of the lower airways, where viral replication disrupts epithelial integrity, promotes mucus production, and contributes to airway obstruction.13 Initial control depends on innate immune recognition and interferon-mediated antiviral responses, although RSV NS1 and NS2 proteins can antagonize interferon signaling. Viral clearance ultimately requires adaptive immunity, including neutralizing antibodies and cellular immune responses.35,36

Structure-based design of current pediatric RSV prophylactics. Crystal structures of the RSV fusion (F) glycoprotein in prefusion (left) and postfusion (right) conformations are shown; these structures do not include the transmembrane or intracellular portions of F. Major antigenic sites are indicated: site Ø (green) and site V (red) are prefusion-exclusive and contain the most potent neutralizing epitopes; site II (pink) and site IV (orange) are present in both conformations but are substantially more immunogenic when displayed on prefusion F. All three interventions currently used to prevent severe RSV disease in infants target prefusion-preferred or prefusion-specific epitopes: nirsevimab binds site Ø, clesrovimab binds site IV, and the maternal bivalent prefusion-stabilized F subunit vaccine (RSVpreF; RSV-A + RSV-B) elicits high-titer polyclonal neutralizing antibodies across all prefusion domains and directed predominantly against sites Ø and V. in contrast, palivizumab, which targeted only the lower-potency site II, has been largely superseded by these next-generation interventions.

The RSV F glycoprotein undergoes a conformational transition from a prefusion to a postfusion state during viral entry. The modern era of RSV immunoprophylaxis was catalyzed by the characterization of the prefusion F glycoprotein structure (Figure 2), which revealed that the prefusion conformation exposes potent neutralization-sensitive epitopes (site Ø, site II, and site IV) that are absent or less accessible in the postfusion form (Figure 2).37 Because prefusion F is structurally unstable and naturally refolds into the postfusion conformation, structure-guided stabilization was required to preserve these protective epitopes. This effort led to the DS-Cav1 mutation set, which locks F in its prefusion configuration.38–41 These structure-based innovations laid the foundation for the current prefusion F vaccines and monoclonal antibodies.37,39,41,42

Immunologic considerations for RSV prevention

Active immunization refers to vaccination strategies that stimulate the adaptive immune system to generate protective responses. In contrast, passive immunization provides preformed antibodies that confer immediate protection without requiring de novo antibody generation.43–46 Although active immunization is feasible in early infancy for some pathogens, such as hepatitis B, vaccine performance depends on the immune response required for protection, antigen characteristics, and the extent of maternal antibody interference.45 For RSV, active immunization in early infancy, the period of greatest disease vulnerability, has been difficult to achieve. This is partly due to developmental immaturity of the infant B-cell compartment, which tends to generate low-affinity and short-lived antibody responses to RSV antigens. It is also due to interference from maternally derived IgG, which can diminish vaccine responsiveness by masking key RSV epitopes and delivering inhibitory signals to naïve B cells.43–47

A second challenge is the risk of maladaptive immune priming. This concern stems from the formalin-inactivated RSV vaccine experience in the 1960s, in which vaccination predisposed RSV-seronegative children to more severe respiratory disease after subsequent RSV infection, resulting in >80% hospitalization rates and two deaths among vaccinees.4,38,48,49 This phenomenon is now known as vaccine-associated enhanced respiratory disease (VAERD) and was associated with Th2-skewed inflammation and suboptimal neutralizing antibody responses. Although FI-RSV induced abundant antibodies against RSV G and F proteins, the F-specific responses were biased toward postfusion F (site I) and deficient in prefusion F antibodies, helping explain why the vaccine was poorly neutralizing.37 This history informed current regulatory standards, which now require candidate vaccines intended for RSV-seronegative infants to demonstrate absence of VAERD and induction of Th1-biased immunity with appropriate CD8+ T-cell responses in early-phase trials.

Early RSV prevention strategies

The formalin-inactivated RSV vaccine experience in the 1960s effectively halted pediatric RSV vaccine development for decades and shifted prevention efforts toward passive antibody-based strategies (Figure 1).50 Early passive immunization relied on monthly intravenous RSV-enriched hyperimmune globulin (RSV-IGIV, RespiGam), which reduced hospitalizations in preterm infants but was ultimately discontinued due to safety concerns, fluid overload risks, and logistical burdens.8,51–53

Palivizumab, approved in 1998, was the first-humanized monoclonal antibody licensed for RSV prophylaxis after pivotal trials demonstrated a 55% reduction in RSV-related hospitalizations among high-risk infants.3 It targets the conserved site II of the RSV fusion glycoprotein, enabling broad neutralization of both RSV-A and RSV-B strains (Figure 2). However, the need for up to five monthly doses and the substantial financial burden constrained its use to narrowly defined high-risk populations. Palivizumab transitioned to a second-line role starting in 2023 with the introduction of long-acting monoclonal antibodies. Production has already ceased in the United States and is scheduled for global phase-out by December 2025 (Table 1).54

Current RSV prevention strategies

RSV-IGIV and palivizumab established that passive antibody-mediated prophylaxis could prevent severe RSV disease, but their logistical and cost limitations restricted population-level use. Long-acting monoclonal antibodies were developed to overcome these limitations by providing season-long protection with a single intramuscular dose, enabled by Fc modifications that extend antibody half-life.54 Nirsevimab was the first long-acting antibody to be licensed. It targets site Ø on prefusion F and is given as a single, weight-banded intramuscular dose (50 mg for <5 kg; 100 mg for ≥5 kg). In the phase 3 MELODY trial,16 nirsevimab reduced medically attended RSV lower respiratory tract infection through 150 d by 74.5% (95% CI 49.6–87.1%).9,55–58 Clesrovimab, approved by the US FDA in June 2025, targets site IV on prefusion F (Figure 2) and is given as a single, weight-independent 105 mg intramuscular dose. In the phase 3 CLEVER trial, it reduced medically attended RSV lower respiratory tract infection through 5 months by 60.4% (95% CI 44.1–71.9%) and RSV hospitalizations by 84.2% (95% CI 66.6–92.6%).37,54,59,60

Protection during early infancy can also be achieved through maternal immunization and transplacental transfer of RSV-neutralizing antibodies. The bivalent recombinant RSV prefusion F (RSVpreF) vaccine is currently the only maternal RSV vaccine to achieve regulatory approval (Table 1).40,42 It contains stabilized prefusion F proteins from both RSV-A and RSV-B and is approved in the United States for use at 32 through 36 weeks of gestation to prevent RSV-associated lower respiratory tract disease in infants. In the phase 3 MATISSE trial, efficacy against severe medically attended RSV-associated lower respiratory tract illness in infants was 82.4% within 90 d after birth and 70.0% within 180 d.37,42,61,62

Discontinued and investigational RSV prevention approaches

Several RSV monoclonal antibody candidates did not progress to implementation despite promising clinical activity. Motavizumab, a high-affinity monoclonal targeting the RSV F glycoprotein, reduced RSV hospitalizations in high-risk infants but was not approved because of increased adverse events.63 Suptavumab, a monoclonal antibody that targeted antigenic site V on prefusion F, received FDA fast-track designation in 2015. However, its development was discontinued after it failed to meet its phase 3 efficacy endpoint. The failure was attributed to a natural mutation in the predominant circulating RSV subgroup B strain that resulted in loss of antibody binding and neutralization.62

Maternal vaccine development has also had setbacks. GSK’s adjuvanted prefusion F maternal vaccine candidate (RSVpreF3) was discontinued despite evidence of infant protection (efficacy ~70%), after the phase 3 GRACE trial reported a higher rate of preterm birth in the vaccine group than in the placebo group (6.8% vs 4.9%; RR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.08–1.74) and an imbalance in neonatal deaths. Novavax’s maternal ResVax program, which used a post-fusion F antigen, was halted after the Prepare trial showed only modest efficacy (39–44%) and did not meet its primary endpoint.64

Direct active immunization of infants has also been challenging. No active RSV vaccine is currently licensed for children under 24 months, and regulatory approval for RSV-seronegative infants (<12 months) has not been achieved.65,66 Non-replicating platforms, including subunit and mRNA vaccines, have shown favorable immunogenicity in seropositive children and adults but have faced unacceptable reactogenicity or unresolved theoretical VAERD concerns in seronegative infants.36,37,67 As a result, pediatric development has largely focused on intranasal live-attenuated and replication-restricted vectored approaches that better mimic natural infection and are viewed as lower theoretical VAERD risk in this age group.38–40,49,50,68 However, translating these concepts into a licensed product for infants has been challenging because of suboptimal immunogenicity. For example, the Sanofi/NIAID RSVt intranasal program was discontinued after failing efficacy endpoints. Moreover, live vaccines may not be suitable for all infants, particularly those who are severely immunocompromised and at risk for prolonged replication or dissemination of vaccine-type virus.69

Real-world effectiveness

Post-licensure effectiveness of immunoprophylactic products may differ from pre-licensure efficacy observed in clinical trials. To synthesize post-implementation evidence, we extracted data from 43 observational studies evaluating real-world effectiveness against RSV-associated hospitalization in infants. The meta-analyses included 39 studies that evaluated Nirsevimab,34,70–109 and 5 that evaluated maternal RSVpreF vaccination (Figure 3).97,101,110–112 One study contributed data to both product-specific comparisons,55 and one additional post-licensure study provided a direct head-to-head comparison of nirsevimab with maternal RSVpreF vaccination but was not pooled with the product-specific analyses because it lacked a no-prophylaxis comparator.86

The pooled random-effects meta-analysis of 39 studies showed high effectiveness of nirsevimab against RSV-associated hospitalization, with a pooled effectiveness estimate of 80.9% (95% CI: 77.3%–83.9%). Individual study estimates ranged from 53.5% to 98.0%, with low between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 0.9%; τ2 = 0.155; Cochran Q p < .001) although all estimates favored nirsevimab (Figure 4). Maternal RSVpreF vaccination also demonstrated significant real-world effectiveness, with a pooled estimate of 79.1% (95% CI, 73.6%–83.5%) across five studies. Individual study estimates ranged from 70.0% to 82.2%, with no statistical evidence of between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0.017; Cochran Q p = .444) (Figure 5). Direct comparative evidence was limited to one post-licensure head-to-head study, in which nirsevimab was associated with a lower risk of RSV-associated hospitalization than maternal RSVpreF vaccination (adjusted HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.61–0.88).

Risk-of-bias assessments showed that most included studies had low or moderate risk of bias, with the most frequent concerns related to confounding and participant selection (Figure S1). Funnel plot assessment and formal asymmetry testing did not suggest evidence of small-study effects or publication bias among nirsevimab studies (Figure S2). Certainty of evidence using GRADE was moderate for both Nirsevimab and RSVpreF, reflecting a large and precise pooled effect but limitations from observational designs, risk of bias, and heterogeneity. Certainty for comparison between nirsevimab and maternal RSVpreF vaccination was more limited because the evidence base was smaller (Table S5).

Discussion

In this review, we synthesized the biologic rationale, clinical development, and real-world effectiveness of current RSV prevention strategies for infants and young children. The narrative synthesis highlights how advances in prefusion F structural biology, antibody engineering, and maternal immunization have shifted RSV prevention from prophylaxis for selected high-risk infants toward broader protection during early infancy. From an effectiveness standpoint, our meta-analysis found broadly similar pooled protection against RSV-associated hospitalization for nirsevimab and maternal RSVpreF vaccination, although the evidence base was stronger for nirsevimab. These findings support both strategies as effective approaches to early-life RSV prevention, but they do not establish equivalence or interchangeability.

Maternal RSVpreF vaccination is dependent on transplacental transfer of maternal antibodies and may be well suited to settings where antenatal care delivery is robust and timely vaccination is feasible.42 None of the included real-world studies stratified vaccine effectiveness by specific gestational age at immunization. This reflects national programmatic constraints; the US and Argentine cohorts restricted administration to a narrow window of 32–36 weeks’ gestation to mitigate theoretical preterm birth risks,55,101 whereas UK and European cohorts permitted vaccination from 28 weeks onward.113

In contrast, monoclonal antibodies provide direct passive immunity to the infant and can be administered at first healthcare contact, independent of maternal factors, offering greater flexibility. Direct comparative evidence remains limited, and additional real-world studies are needed to define relative effectiveness and optimal programmatic use across healthcare settings.

Implementation in public health programs

In the United States, current Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommendations use maternal RSVpreF vaccination and infant long-acting monoclonal antibodies as alternative pathways to protect infants during their first RSV season. When maternal vaccination was not received, was administered too close to delivery, or when additional protection is indicated for high-risk infants, nirsevimab or clesrovimab is recommended. Having more than one option with distinct prefusion F targets is advantageous because it creates a higher genetic barrier to resistance, as mutations that affect one product would not necessarily compromise the other. Although concurrent shortages could still occur if products depend on shared materials or supply chains, having more than one product may also enhance supply resilience by reducing dependence on a single manufacturer.42,60,114,115

International implementation has followed the same broad shift toward population-level infant RSV prevention,116 but program design varies according to feasibility and health system capacity. Several countries, including Spain, France, Portugal, and Luxembourg, rapidly incorporated nirsevimab into routine immunization schedules for all or nearly all infants,116 whereas other settings, such as the United Kingdom and Argentina, have emphasized maternal RSVpreF vaccination as the main population-level strategy, with monoclonal antibody prophylaxis reserved for selected high-risk infants or used where maternal vaccination is absent or insufficiently timed. Recommended gestational age windows for maternal immunization also vary by country. In the United States and Argentina, administration is recommended between 32–36 or 32–36.6 weeks of gestation, reflecting efforts to allow adequate antibody generation and transplacental transfer while avoiding earlier use in pregnancy given unresolved concerns about possible preterm birth signals.37,42,61 In contrast, the United Kingdom recommends vaccination from 28 weeks of gestation, which may improve programmatic flexibility and help provide protection when delivery occurs before later gestational windows.113

Cost, access, and public health impact

Beyond clinical effectiveness, the population-level impact of RSV prevention will depend on affordability and high coverage. Several high-income countries have implemented infant RSV prevention programs with full or partial public reimbursement.116 For the United States, 2025–2026 CDC contract prices per dose are approximately US$560 for nirsevimab and US$445–480 for clesrovimab. Both are provided at no cost to eligible children through the Vaccines for Children program. Multiple jurisdictions have concluded that universal deployment of both long-acting monoclonal antibodies and maternal vaccination is cost-effective at current prices. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratios are US$15,000 to US$45,000 per quality-adjusted life-year gained.117,118

Global implementation will require affordable procurement and sustainable financing. A US$450 million Advance Market Commitment announced by Gavi, UNICEF, and the Gates Foundation in June 2025 is intended to support tiered pricing of US$15–22 per dose for monoclonal antibodies and US$12–18 per dose for maternal RSVpreF vaccine through 2030.118 Initial Gavi-supported introductions of nirsevimab are scheduled for 2026–2027 and clesrovimab scale-up is anticipated thereafter.119 Modeling studies incorporating 2025 real-world effectiveness estimates have evaluated universal implementation in Gavi-eligible countries. These analyses estimate that 450,000 to 600,000 hospitalizations and 25,000 to 40,000 deaths annually could be averted by 2030.117,120

Limitations

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. For the meta-analysis of real-world effectiveness data, included studies were observational and therefore subject to residual confounding and differences in healthcare-seeking behavior. Heterogeneity was observed among studies, likely reflecting between-study differences in populations, RSV seasons, and confounder adjustment strategies. Direct comparative evidence between maternal vaccination and infant monoclonal antibodies was limited, and no post-licensure effectiveness studies of clesrovimab were available at the time of the search. Therefore, the optimal use of maternal vaccination versus infant monoclonal antibodies remains uncertain, including whether selected infants would benefit from a dual approach. For the narrative review, the synthesis of biologic, clinical, regulatory, and implementation evidence was structured but not fully systematic and may reflect author judgment in source selection.

Future directions

Despite major advances, several unresolved questions remain. Additional data are needed on the durability of protection and the role of repeat dosing in settings without clearly defined RSV seasonality or among high-risk children who remain vulnerable beyond their first RSV season. Whether prevention of early-life RSV disease modifies later recurrent wheeze or asthma-related outcomes also requires continued study. Finally, although prefusion F is highly conserved across RSV-A and RSV-B and widespread immune escape has not been observed, genomic surveillance has identified variants with reduced susceptibility to current monoclonal antibodies.37,121,122 These variants remain relatively infrequent, and their clinical impact is not yet fully established. However, increasing population-level use of monoclonal antibodies could alter viral evolutionary dynamics, underscoring the importance of continued genomic surveillance and post-implementation effectiveness monitoring.123

Overall, these findings reinforce the clinical utility of both passive infant immunization with nirsevimab and maternal immunization with RSVpreF vaccine as effective strategies to prevent RSV-associated hospitalization during early infancy. As the evidence base evolves, additional real-world studies, especially direct comparative analyses and studies evaluating implementation across different health-care systems, will be important to clarify relative effectiveness, durability of protection, equity of access, and optimal programmatic use.

In addition, implementation challenges persist, including ensuring equitable access in low- and middle-income countries, optimizing timing of maternal vaccination, ensuring timely infant administration of monoclonal antibodies, and integrating these interventions into existing health-care systems.124 Addressing these gaps will be critical to maximizing the global impact of RSV prevention strategies.

Conclusion

For the first time, large-scale prevention of severe infant RSV disease is feasible. Real-world evidence has shown that maternal vaccines and long-acting monoclonal antibodies targeting prefusion F epitopes are highly effective at preventing RSV-related hospitalization in early life. The remaining barriers are largely operational, and success will depend on identifying optimal implementation strategies, ensuring timely delivery, and achieving equitable access.
