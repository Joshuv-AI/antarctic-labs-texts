ATG7-driven mitophagy in BMSC@CS hydrogel reprograms metabolism to boost bone regeneration
Authors: Qiumei Lan, Mengtian Fan, Fengmei Zhang, Qian Gong, Jiayan Zhong, Miao Yi, Qiqi Zeng, Jianqiang Chen, Yiming Pan, Nana Geng, Biao Kuang, Yu Du, Chuanrong Zhao, Deping Zeng, Chunliang Zhao, Zhen Li, Guixue Wang, Fengjin Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bone regeneration remains a formidable challenging due to impaired energy metabolism and the limited osteogenic potential of transplanted stem cells. Mitochondrial homeostasis orchestrates osteogenesis, with mitophagy maintaining the metabolic rhythm essential for bone formation. However, the role of autophagy related gene 7(ATG7), a pivotal regulator of mitochondrial function, in bone regeneration remains elusive. In this study, we developed a ATG7-overexpressing bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (ATG7-BMSCs) encapsulated within a thermoresponsive chitosan (CS) hydrogel, creating a biocompatible, living platform capable of adapting to physiological conditions. The hydrogel exhibited excellent injectability, physiological gelation at 37 °C, and prolonged biocompatibility, providing a favorable microenvironment for stem cell survival and proliferation. The system was evaluated through in vitro assays for mitochondrial function and osteogenic differentiation, as well as in vivo using rat cranial and femoral defect models. Mechanistically, ATG7 activation enhanced mitophagy, preserved mitochondrial integrity, reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and reactivated oxidative phosphorylation via the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, thereby reprogramming osteogenic metabolism and promoting differentiation. Conversely, ATG7 deficiency led to mitochondrial dysfunction, glycolytic dependence, and impaired bone formation. In vivo, the ATG7-BMSC@CS hydrogel markedly accelerated new bone formation and mineral density recovery while maintaining excellent injectability, biocompatibility, and systemic safety. Collectively, this study identifies ATG7-driven mitophagy as a critical regulator of osteogenic metabolism and bone formation, and establishes a smart, living hydrogel platform that seamlessly integrates metabolic reprogramming with stem cell delivery. This strategy provides a conceptual blueprint for next-generation, metabolism-oriented bone repair at the intersection of cell biology, biomaterials, and precision medicine.

Introduction

Bone defects from trauma, tumor resection, infection, or degenerative disease remain difficult to repair [1], as conventional grafts face challenges of immune rejection, poor integration, and infection risk [2,3]. Tissue engineering strategies that combine scaffolds, bioactive factors and bone marrow derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) offer a promising alternative [4,5], yet their therapeutic efficacy is frequently constrained by poor cell survival and impaired osteogenic differentiation within the hostile defect microenvironment [6]. Increasing evidence implicates disrupted mitochondrial quality control and consequent bioenergetic failure as central contributors to stem-cell dysfunction and failed bone repair [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Addressing these energy metabolic limitations is therefore critical for improving stem cell based bone regeneration and motivates strategies that restore mitochondrial homeostasis to enhance BMSC survival and osteogenic performance.

Mitochondria are central to cellular energy metabolism and redox balance [11], and their dysfunction severely compromises osteogenic differentiation [12,13]. Mitophagy—the selective autophagic elimination of damaged mitochondria—plays an essential role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and metabolic flexibility during osteogenesis [14,15]. Recent studies have demonstrated that moderate activation of mitophagy promotes osteoblast differentiation by preserving mitochondrial integrity and preventing excessive ROS accumulation, whereas impaired mitophagy leads to metabolic stress, reduced ATP synthesis, and diminished bone forming capacity [16,17]. Among key autophagy regulators, Autophagy Related Gene 7 (ATG7) serves as an indispensable E1-like activating enzyme in the autophagosome formation cascade [18] and has been identified as a pivotal node linking mitochondrial quality control to osteogenic metabolism [19,20]. However, the direct mechanistic contribution of ATG7 mediated mitophagy to bone regeneration and its integration into a biomaterial-based therapeutic platform remain largely unexplored.

Given its central role in mitochondrial quality control and metabolic homeostasis, the genetic modification of ATG7 in MSCs holds promise for enhancing their therapeutic potential in bone tissue engineering. To further improve cell survival and functionality, hydrogels provide biomimetic microenvironments that closely mimic the natural extracellular matrix (ECM) and support controlled cellular behavior [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Among natural polymers, chitosan/β-glycerophosphate (CS) hydrogels exhibit excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and thermosensitivity, enabling injectable and site-specific delivery for bone regeneration [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Therefore, CS hydrogels serve as an ideal carrier for delivering ATG7-modified BMSCs to bone defect sites. This approach represents a new generation of biologically inspired biomaterials, where the modulation of cellular autophagy and scaffold responsiveness act in concert to drive functional tissue regeneration [[29], [30], [31]].

In this study, we evaluate the therapeutic potential of the ATG7-BMSC@CS system for bone regeneration and elucidate the mechanisms underlying the osteogenic differentiation of ATG7-modified BMSCs. This study represents the successful integration of ATG7 gene modification with a CS based hydrogel delivery system, achieving a tri-modal therapeutic strategy that synergistically combines gene, cell, and biomaterial-based therapy. Importantly, we uncover the mechanistic role of ATG7 in orchestrating metabolic reprogramming through the mitophagy–PI3K–AKT axis, bridging a critical knowledge gap between autophagy and energy metabolism during osteogenesis. Materials and methods

Hydrogel preparation

100 mg of CS (degree of deacetylation: 95 %, Aladdin, C105799) was dissolved in 4 ml of 0.1 M HCl and stirred at room temperature until completely dissolved. Various concentrations (6 %, 8 %, or 10 %) of β-GP (Sigma, 50020) were dissolved in 1 ml of ultrapure water, and this solution was slowly added dropwise to the CS solution while it was kept on ice. The mixture was stirred on ice for 6–8 h. After thorough mixing, the solution was transferred to a 37 °C incubator to form the hydrogel.

Characterization of the injectable CS thermosensitive hydrogel

The structures of the hydrogels and element mapping of hydrogels could be examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi, SU8100), and their rheological properties were evaluated via an arheometer (TA, HR 10). The injectability of the hydrogel was tested using a 1 mL syringe. Swelling behavior was determined by immersing freeze-dried samples of known dry weight in PBS at room temperature, removing them at predetermined intervals, blotting surface water, and weighing; the swelling ratio was defined as (Ws–Wd)/Wd, where Ws and Wd are the swollen and dry weights, respectively. Biodegradability was assessed by incubating freeze-dried samples in PBS for up to 28 days, followed by freeze-drying and weighing at days 7, 14, 21, and 28 days; the mass loss rate was calculated as (W0–Wt)/W0, where W0 and Wt represent the initial and remaining dry weights, respectively. For in vivo degradation analysis, sterile CS hydrogel (0.4 mL per site) was injected intradermally into the dorsal skin of adult Sprague-Dawley rats under isoflurane anesthesia. At 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-injection, the remaining hydrogel and surrounding tissues were carefully excised, photographed, and analyzed to assess residual mass and morphological changes.

Biocompatibility and cytotoxicity evaluation of CS hydrogel

The CS hydrogel was sterilized by pasteurization and used for BMSC encapsulation to assess its biocompatibility and cytotoxicity. For biocompatibility analysis, BMSCs were embedded within the hydrogel and cultured in standard medium. Cytoskeletal organization and cell morphology were visualized by staining filamentous actin (F-actin) with TRITC-phalloidin (Beyotime, C2207S) and nuclei with DAPI (Beyotime, C1005). Live/dead staining (Beyotime, C2015M) was performed on days 1, 4, and 7, and samples were imaged by confocal microscopy to evaluate cell viability and distribution within the hydrogel. For cytotoxicity assessment, as previously described [32], hydrogel extracts were prepared by incubating samples in α-MEM (hydrogel/medium = 1:5, v/v) at 37 °C for 24 h to obtain 100 % extracts, which were serially diluted to 25 %, 50 %, and 75 % with fresh α-MEM (Gibco, C12571500BT). BMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates and exposed to the extracts for 7, 14, and 21 days. At each time point, 10 % (v/v) CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, C0038) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite e Plex, Tecan) to quantify cell viability.

Transgenic mice

ATG7flox/flox mice and DMP1-Cre mice were kind gifts from Prof. Lin Chen (Daping Hospital, Army Medical University; Chongqing, China). ATG7flox/flox, DMP1-Cre mice were used to construct the ATG7fl/flCKO model, and ATG7flox/flox (ATG7fl/fl) mice were used to construct the ATG7 control model. All the mouse strains were placed on a full C3H/HeJ background. The body weights of the mice were monitored on a weekly basis, and X-ray and micro-CT (NEMO, NMC-200) examinations were conducted to assess their growth and bone development. All procedures involving animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University.

Histological staining and TRAP staining

HE, Safranin–Fast Green, Masson and TRAP were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions for the Hematoxylin‒Eosin/HE Staining Kit (Solarbio, G1120), Modified Safranine O‒Fast Green Cartilage Stain Kit (Solarbio, G1371), Modified Masson Trichrome Stain Kit (Solarbio, G1346) and Tartrate‒Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) Stain Kit (Solarbio, G1492).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

IHC and IF staining were performed to evaluate the expression of target proteins. Samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking with 5 % BSA, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by HRP- or fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, PK10009). For IHC, signals were developed using DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin (Proteintech, PK10009). For IF, nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1005). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany) and analyzed with ImageJ software.The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Isolation and culture of primary osteoblasts and BMSCs

Cranial tissues from 3-day-old ATG7fl/flCKO and ATG7fl/fl mice were aseptically harvested, minced into ∼1 mm3 fragments, and digested with 1 mg/mL type I collagenase (Beyotime, ST2294) at 37 °C for 1 h. The digested tissues were transferred to DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT) and cultured for 4–5 days to obtain primary osteoblasts. BMSCs were isolated from femurs and tibias of 3-day-old rats (Animal Experiment Center, Chongqing Medical University) by flushing with α-MEM (Gibco, C12571500BT) containing 10 % FBS (Azaood, AD00004–500) and 1 % PS. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in 5 % CO2 and subsequently induced toward osteogenic differentiation in α-MEM (Gibco, C12571500BT) supplemented with 10 % FBS (Azaood, AD00004–500), 1 % PS, 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid (Macklin, A800295), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Macklin, G806967), and 100 nM dexamethasone (Macklin, D137736).

ATG7 overexpression plasmid transfection

Cells were transfected with pCDNA3.1-ATG7 plasmids via Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, Cat. 11668030) for 48 h.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells via an RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, R711). RT‒PCR was performed via a CFX-connect fast real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). The results were analyzed via the comparative 2-△△Ct method. The sample mixture was composed of 5 μl of SYBR (Vazyme, Q711-03), 1 μl of primers, 1 μl of cDNA, and 3 μl of ddH2O. The touchdown cycling conditions were as follows: 1) 95 °C for 3 min; 2) 95 °C for 10 s; 3) 58 °C for 30 s; 4) steps 2–3 were repeated for 39 cycles; 5) 65 °C for 5 s; and 6) 95 °C for 5 s. The primers used for PCR and qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with protease (Beyotime, ST505) and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, P1081), followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. The protein concentration was quantified via a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0012). Western blotting was performed according to standard protocols. Briefly, samples were separated on 4 %–20 % gradient SDS‒PAGE gels and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were then sequentially incubated with 5 % BSA, specific primary antibodies, and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. After reacting with the ECL reagent, signals were captured via a membrane imaging system (BIO-OI, China). The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining

ALP and ARS staining were performed to evaluate early and late osteogenic differentiation, respectively. ALP staining was conducted 7 days post-treatment, and ARS staining after 21 days, unless otherwise indicated. For ALP assays, cells were washed and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A), then stained using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime, C3206) following the manufacturer's instructions. For mineralization, cells were fixed, rinsed with ddH2O, and stained with 1 % Alizarin Red S (Oricell, ALIR-10001). Stained samples were imaged under a microscope, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Transcriptome analysis

Primary osteoblasts from 3-day-old ATG7fl/flCKO and ATG7fl/fl mice were collected. Total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer's instructions for the TRIzol reagent (Ambion,15596026CN). The RNA-seq libraries were prepared on the basis of the manufacturer's protocol for the NEBNext® Ultra™ Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA), and the libraries were sequenced via an Illumina sequencing platform. The fragments per kilobase of transcript sequence per million base pairs sequenced (FPKM) was used to measure the expression level of each transcript. Differential expression gene analysis was performed via edgeR. Differentially expressed genes were screened and subjected to hierarchical cluster analysis. The GO (Gene Ontology) database (Bioconductor, USA) was used to analyze gene functions, and the KEGG database (Kanehisa Laboratories, Japan) was used to analyze pathway enrichment.

ROS and mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

Cells were treated for 48 h prior to all assays. Intracellular ROS levels were assessed by incubating cells with 5 μM H2DCFDA (MCE, HY-D0940) in PBS for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by mounting with DAPI-containing medium (Solarbio, S2110) or staining with 1 mM mitochondrial tracker (MCE, HY-D1783) for 30 min ΔΨm was evaluated using 10 μM TMRM (MCE, HY-D0984A) under the same conditions. Fluorescence signals were observed by microscopy and quantified by flow cytometry (FACS) (Guva, Guava® easyCyte™️).

Lactic acid detection

The lactate concentration in the cell culture supernatant was quantified via a colorimetric assay kit (Sorebábo, BC2230). According to the manufacturer's protocol, 100 μL of the clarified supernatant was sequentially added to Extraction Buffer I and Extraction Buffer II to obtain the test solution. Following the sample loading table provided in the instructions, the corresponding reagent components were added to the reaction mixture. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 1000 rpm to remove the supernatant, and the resulting pellet was retained. An appropriate volume of ethanol was added to the pellet, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm via a microplate reader (infinite e plex, Tecan).

ATP detection

The intracellular ATP content was measured via the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0027) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed with ATP lysis buffer, and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was collected, and 20 μL of each sample was transferred to a 96-well plate. Subsequently, 100 μL of ATP detection working solution was added to each well, and the luminescence intensity was immediately measured via a microplate reader (infinite e plex, Tecan). A standard curve was generated using known concentrations of ATP, and the ATP levels in the samples were quantified by comparing the luminescence values to the standard curve. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and accuracy. The protein concentration of each sample was determined via a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0012) to normalize the ATP levels.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of mitochondrial ultrastructure

Primary osteoblasts from ATG7fl/flCKO and ATG7fl/fl mice were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde and 1 % osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in an ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (50–70 nm) were cut via an ultramicrotome and mounted on copper grids. The sections were stained with 2 % uranyl acetate and lead citrate to enhance contrast. The samples were examined via transmission electron microscopy (JEOL, JEM-1400) at 80 kV.

Establishment of a bone defect model

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University. Sixty male Sprague–Dawley rats (6–8 weeks, 180–200 g) were housed under SPF conditions (20–26 °C, 50–60 % humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard chow (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd., 1010085) and water. Cranial and femoral defect models were established, with rats randomly assigned to control, BMSC@CS, and ATG7-BMSC@CS groups. Hydrogel stock solutions were sterilized by pasteurization (80 °C, 30 min, followed by immersion in ice water for 5 min, repeated twice).

For the cranial defect model, rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1 % sodium pentobarbital. A 3 cm longitudinal incision exposed the cranial plate, and two 5 mm cylindrical defects were created on either side of the suture using a high-speed drill. Defects were filled with the respective hydrogels, and wounds were sutured. Rats were euthanized at 6 and 12 weeks (n = 5 per group), and cranial bones with periosteum were harvested and fixed.

For the femoral defect model, rats were anesthetized similarly, and a 1 cm oblique incision exposed the lateral femoral epicondyle and shaft. A circular defect (3 mm diameter, 2.5 mm depth) penetrating the medullary cavity was created with a low speed drill without breaching the contralateral cortex. Hydrogels were applied to fill the defects, and the site was closed layer by layer. Five rats per group were used.

In vivo bone defect repair assessment

At the conclusion of the treatment period, the animals were euthanized, and the cranial and femoral bones from the defect sites were collected. The collected tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and then decalcified, embedded, and sectioned. The repair of the bone defects was evaluated through micro-CT (NEMO, NMC-200), Masson staining (Servicebio, G1006), HE staining (Solarbio, G1120), and immunohistochemistry.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the means ± SDs. Student's t-test was used to compare two groups. Differences between more than two groups were analyzed via one-way ANOVA, and Tukey's test was used for post hoc multiple comparisons. Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 9. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical and biological characterization of an injectable thermosensitive hydrogel encapsulating ATG7-Modified BMSCs

We successfully developed an injectable thermosensitive ATG7-BMSC@CS hydrogel (Fig. 1A), which has a well-defined gelation phase transition temperature, determined at the crossover point where G′ equals G″. Below this critical temperature (G′< G″), the hydrogel remains in a liquid state. Our study revealed that the phase transition temperature of the hydrogel gradually decreased with increasing β-GP content. At β-GP concentrations of 6 %, 8 %, and 10 %, the hydrogel successfully formed a stable gel at physiological temperature (Fig. 1B). To further assess the effects of different β-GP concentrations on the gelation time of the hydrogel, we found that increasing the β-GP concentration did not lead to significant differences in the gelation time (Fig. 1C). SEM revealed that the hydrogel possesses a porous, three-dimensional network structure, which facilitates cell encapsulation and growth (Fig. 1D). Considering the experimental requirements, we selected the CS hydrogel with 8 % β-GP for further studies, as its gelation time closely aligns with physiological conditions. The gelation temperature of the optimized 8 % β-GP hydrogel was determined to be 36.8 °C closely matching physiological temperature and confirming its suitability for injectable in situ formation (Fig. S1A). The injectability of the hydrogel was evaluated using a 1 mL syringe (26G), and hydrogel could be smoothly extruded through the syringe without clogging, indicating good injectability (Fig. S1B). Furthermore, swelling tests demonstrated that the CS hydrogel has excellent water absorption capacity, leading to a high swelling ratio, which ensures an adequate hydration environment for encapsulated cells (Fig. 1E). Degradation studies revealed that the hydrogel underwent controlled biodegradation over time under physiological conditions, making it suitable for tissue engineering applications (Fig. 1F). Phalloidin/DAPI staining showed uniform distribution of BMSCs within the hydrogel and well-spread cytoskeletal morphology, indicating excellent cytocompatibility of the thermosensitive CS hydrogel (Fig. S1D). Cell viability assessments, including live/dead cell staining, confirmed that the hydrogel maintained high cell viability post encapsulation. Moreover, time-course observations revealed continuous cell proliferation within the hydrogel matrix, indicating that it provides a suitable microenvironment for sustained cell growth and metabolic activity (Fig. 1G). To assess the long-term safety of the hydrogel, we conducted a cytotoxicity assay using hydrogel extracts over 21 days. The results demonstrated that the hydrogel did not induce any significant cytotoxic effects on BMSC, as cell viability remained high at all tested time points (Fig. S1E). In vivo degradation studies revealed that the CS hydrogel, after gelation at physiological temperature, maintained its morphology and mechanical integrity, providing continuous structural support for bone formation and tissue remodeling (Fig. S1F).). These findings demonstrate that the thermosensitive CS hydrogel combines good injectability, physiological gelation, high porosity, favorable swelling behavior, controlled biodegradability, and excellent cytocompatibility, making it a promising platform for cell delivery and tissue engineering applications.

Osteoblast-specific deletion of ATG7 impairs postnatal bone growth and mineralization in mice

To investigate the role of ATG7 in osteoblasts, ATG7fl/fl mice were crossed with DMP1-Cre mice to generate conditional knockout mice (ATG7fl/flCKO). Comparative analysis between ATG7fl/fl and ATG7fl/flCKO mice revealed that at 2, 3, and 4 weeks postnatally (Fig. 2A), compared to their littermate controls, ATG7fl/flCKO mice presented significantly reduced body length and weight (Fig. 2B–C), and a reduction in the average lengths of the femur and tibia at all examined time points (Fig. 2D–G). X-ray and micro-CT analysis further demonstrated a substantial reduction in bone density, cortical thickness, and bone caliber in the tibia of ATG7fl/flCKO mice (Fig. 2H–J, S2A-B). Histological assessments via HE and Safranin O-Fast Green staining revealed a notable decrease in trabecular number and increase in trabecular spacing in the femurs of ATG7fl/flCKO mice. In contrast, TRAP staining revealed no significant differences between ATG7fl/flCKO mice and controls, indicating that osteoclastic activity was unaffected (Fig. 2K). In comparison with the control group, immunofluorescence staining for OPN and OCN demonstrated a significant downregulation of osteogenic markers in the ATG7fl/flCKO group (Fig. S2C). These findings indicate that ATG7 plays a critical role in maintaining bone homeostasis and promoting bone formation [31,39]. Western blot and RT-qPCR analyses confirmed optimal overexpression at a plasmid dose of 4 μg (Fig. S3A–C). Under these conditions, ALP staining, ARS staining, RT-qPCR analysis, and immunofluorescence staining of osteogenic markers (OPN, OCN) collectively demonstrated that ATG7 significantly enhanced the osteogenic differentiation (Fig. S4A–D). This effect was further validated in primary rat BMSCs (Fig. 3A). This validation was accompanied by successful ATG7 overexpression, as confirmed by Western blot and RT-qPCR (Fig. 3B–C, S5A), and a significant upregulation of the osteogenic markers ALP, OPN, OCN, and DMP-1 (Fig. 3D). ATG7-modified BMSC-encapsulated hydrogel accelerates bone regeneration in rat models of calvarial and femoral defects

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of the ATG7-modified BMSC-encapsulated hydrogel system in vivo, we established two rat models of bone defects, namely, calvarial and femoral defects. The hydrogel systems from different groups were injected into the bone defect sites, with a blank group serving as the control. For the calvarial defects, micro-CT and histological analyses were performed at 6 and 12 weeks postimplantation (Fig. 4A). In the calvarial defect model, micro-CT imaging and quantitative analysis revealed that, compared with the other groups, the ATG7-BMSC@CS group presented the greatest degree of bone regeneration at both 6 and 12 weeks, as evidenced by significantly greater bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) and bone surface/tissue volume (BS/TV) (Fig. 4B–C). Loss and gain of ATG7 function disrupts and restores mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetics during osteoblast differentiation

To elucidate the downstream pathways and underlying mechanisms by which ATG7 regulates osteogenic differentiation, we performed whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing on primary osteoblasts isolated from the bones of ATG7fl/flCKO mice and wild-type controls (ATG7fl/fl). Venn diagram analysis identified 11,150 coexpressed genes between the two groups (Fig. 6A). Differential expression analysis revealed 856 significantly altered genes in ATG7-deficient osteoblasts compared to controls, including 383 upregulated and 473 downregulated genes (p < 0.05; Fig. 6B). To further explore their biological relevance, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. The DEGs were significantly enriched in GO terms across biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories, particularly those related to mitochondrial energy metabolism, glucose metabolism, autophagy, glycosaminoglycan binding, and extracellular matrix interactions (Fig. 6D). Specifically, terms such as metallopeptidase activity, electron transfer, mitochondrial protein complexes, autophagy, ATP synthesis, bone mineralization, and osteoblast differentiation were prominently represented. Importantly, many of these enriched terms are functionally linked to the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 6E), a central regulator of cell survival, metabolism, and autophagy [[33], [34], [35]]. These findings suggest that ATG7 may modulate osteogenic differentiation through PI3K–AKT-mediated metabolic and autophagic processes.

TEM showed an increased number of mitochondria with disrupted cristae in ATG7-deficient cells, indicative of ultrastructural abnormalities (Fig. S7). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that ATG7-deficient osteoblasts exhibited significantly reduced mRNA expression of key genes involved in both energy metabolism (GLUT3, GLUT4, CYTB, CYC1, Ndufs1, Ndufs5, Ndufs6, and Uqcrfs1) (Fig. S8A) and osteogenic differentiation (ALP, OPN, OCN, and DMP-1) (Fig. S8B). To further evaluate mitochondrial function, we assessed ROS production and ΔΨm using fluorescence staining and flow cytometry. These analyses revealed elevated oxidative stress and a marked reduction in ΔΨm in ATG7-deficient osteoblasts (Fig. S9A–D). Collectively, these findings indicate that ATG7 deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by impaired energy metabolism, disrupted mitochondrial ultrastructure, and compromised membrane potential. The presence of abnormal cristae suggests defects in mitochondrial biogenesis or fission–fusion dynamics, potentially contributing to the observed morphological and functional impairments.

Further analysis of lactate levels in the culture supernatant and intracellular ATP content revealed that ATG7-deficient osteoblasts exhibited increased lactate accumulation and decreased ATP production compared to controls (Fig. S10A–B). This shift—characterized by reduced oxidative phosphorylation and elevated glycolysis—is indicative of a Warburg-like metabolic reprogramming [36]. The observed mitochondrial dysfunction in ATG7-deficient cells likely impairs oxidative phosphorylation, compelling a greater reliance on less efficient anaerobic glycolysis for energy production. The elevated lactate levels further support this metabolic shift, highlighting the essential role of ATG7 in preserving mitochondrial function and cellular energy homeostasis. The next phase of research focused on evaluating the impact of ATG7 overexpression in MSCs on mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. ATG7 overexpression enhanced mitochondrial quality by promoting autophagy, thereby improving mitochondrial structure and function (Fig. 7A). This optimization of mitochondrial integrity supports more efficient energy metabolism, which is essential for stem cell differentiation and tissue repair [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. ATG7-overexpressing MSCs revealed significant upregulation of key energy metabolism-related genes, including GLUT-3, GLUT-4, CYTB, CYC1, Ndufs-1, Ndufs-5, Ndufs-6, and Uqcrfs1 (Fig. 7B), and elevated ΔΨm as well as reduced oxidative stress in ATG7-overexpressing BMSCs (Fig. 7C–F). ATG7-mediated mitophagy drives osteogenic function in MSCs

ATG7 plays a crucial role in sustaining mitochondrial homeostasis and ensuring efficient energy production [44,45]. It promotes mitophagy by facilitating the clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby preserving mitochondrial integrity and optimizing energy metabolism in MSCs [46]. This process enhances energy supply, reduces oxidative stress, and ultimately promotes MSCs differentiation into osteoblasts, contributing to bone repair and regeneration [47,48]. Western blot analysis revealed that ATG7 overexpression in MSCs significantly upregulated key mitophagy-related markers, including Pink-1, Parkin, LC3, and downregulated P62 (Fig. 8A, S11A). During mitophagy, lysosomes maintain a close functional interaction with mitochondria. Fluorescence staining revealed colocalization between mitochondria (green) and lysosomes (red). As shown in Fig. 8B, ATG7 overexpression enhanced lysosomal fluorescence and mitochondria-lysosome interaction, indicating activation of mitophagy and improved mitochondrial quality control during osteogenic differentiation. In vivo, immunohistochemical staining of cranial bone defects confirmed that mitophagy was activated in the ATG7-BMSC@CS group, as indicated by increased LC3 and Pink-1 expression, which correlated with enhanced bone regeneration (Fig. S11B). Enhanced mitophagy led to the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria and reduced intracellular ROS levels. This mitochondrial quality control mechanism also alleviated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [49], as evidenced by the downregulation of ER stress markers ERN1, PERK, BIP, and ATF6 in the ATG7-overexpression group (Fig. S12). These findings suggest that ATG7 promotes osteogenic differentiation by enhancing mitophagy-mediated mitochondrial quality control, and alleviating ER stress. To further verify whether the pro-osteogenic effect of ATG7 is dependent on mitophagy, the mitophagy inhibitor Mdivi-1 was subsequently employed. However, treatment with Mdivi-1, significantly suppressed ATG7-induced mitophagy (Fig. 8C), leading to a notable reduction in osteogenic marker gene expression, including ALP, OPN, OCN, and DMP-1 (Fig. 8D) and diminished osteogenic differentiation as shown by ALP staining (Fig. 8E). In terms of mechanism, GO analysis and KEGG pathway analysis identified ATG7 as a crucial regulator of mitochondrial energy metabolism and cellular homeostasis. Notably, KEGG enrichment highlighted the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway plays a major downstream effector. Western blot analysis confirmed that ATG7 overexpression activated the PI3K-AKT pathway in MSCs (Fig. S13A), whereas Mdivi-1 treatment effectively suppressed this activation (Fig. 8F–G), suggesting that mitophagy mediates PI3K–AKT pathway engagement. Further, inhibition of PI3K–AKT using LY294002 blocked ATG7-induced pathway activation (Fig. S13B). Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining for Pink-1 revealed that LY294002-mediated inhibition of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway attenuated the ATG7 overexpression-induced enhancement of mitophagy (Fig. S14A–D). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining for ALP, ALP staining, and RT-qPCR analysis further demonstrated that LY294002 suppressed the osteogenic differentiation capacity of MSCs (Fig. S15A–D). Collectively, these findings indicate that ATG7 promotes both mitophagy and osteogenic differentiation of MSCs via activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway.

Taken together, ATG7 enhances osteogenesis by promoting mitophagy, which in turn sustains mitochondrial function, alleviates ER stress, and activates the PI3K-AKT signaling cascade-highlighting a mechanistic axis linking mitochondrial quality control to stem cell differentiation and bone regeneration.

Regulation of mitophagy and metabolic reprogramming by ATG7 facilitates osteogenesis in ATG7-Deficient osteoblasts

ATG7 is indispensable for mitophagy, and its deficiency disrupts mitochondrial quality control, impairing osteoblast function and bone formation. ATG7-deficient osteoblasts display swollen mitochondria with disorganized cristae (Fig. S7), a collapse of membrane potential, heightened oxidative stress, and a metabolic drift from oxidative phosphorylation to anaerobic glycolysis, resulting in diminished ATP output and weakened osteogenic capacity (Figs. S7–10). Restoring mitophagy reverses these defects, highlighting the essential role of ATG7 in mitochondrial integrity and metabolic homeostasis (Fig. 7).

To probe this further, we applied FCCP—a classical mitochondrial uncoupler that dissipates ΔΨm, stabilizes Pink-1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane, and recruits Parkin—to ATG7-deficient osteoblasts. In ATG7-deficient osteoblasts, Western blotting confirmed that ATG7 loss markedly suppressed Pink-1, Parkin, LC3, and elevated P62, whereas FCCP treatment reinstated their expression (Fig. 9A, Fig. S16), indicating that FCCP partially rescued mitophagy. Additionally, FCCP also upregulated a panel of energy-metabolism-related genes, including GLUT-3, GLUT-4, CYC1, Ndufs-1, Ndufs-5, Ndufs-6 and Uqcrfs-1 (Fig. 9B), and curtailed ROS accumulation (Fig. 9C-D). Studies have demonstrated that ATG7-deficient osteoblasts exhibit functional abnormalities and reduced osteogenic capacity [50]. RT‒qPCR results revealed markedly decreased mRNA expression of key osteogenic markers, including ALP, OPN, OCN, DMP-1, and RUNX2 (Fig. S8B). Notably, treatment with FCCP significantly upregulated these markers (Fig. 9E), and ALP staining further confirmed that FCCP improved the osteogenic function of ATG7-deficient osteoblasts (Fig. 9F). These findings highlight ATG7 as a central regulator of mitochondrial quality control and metabolic homeostasis during osteogenesis. ATG7 deficiency disrupted mitophagy, leading to mitochondrial damage, ROS accumulation, metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis, and impaired osteogenic differentiation. Pharmacological activation of mitophagy with FCCP partially reversed these defects by restoring mitophagy-related proteins, improving mitochondrial function, and rebalancing energy metabolism. Consequently, FCCP treatment enhanced the expression of osteogenic markers and matrix mineralization in ATG7-deficient osteoblasts.

Together, these results demonstrate that mitophagy is indispensable for maintaining mitochondrial integrity and osteoblast function, and that restoring mitochondrial quality can partially compensate for ATG7 loss. Targeting mitophagy thus represents a promising therapeutic approach for enhancing bone regeneration under metabolic or genetic stress.

Conclusion

This study elucidates ATG7 as a pivotal upstream regulator of osteogenic energy metabolism through the coordination of mitochondrial turnover and metabolic homeostasis. ATG7-driven mitophagy safeguards mitochondrial integrity, augments cellular bioenergetics, and fulfills the energetic demands of osteogenic differentiation via activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling cascade. By incorporating ATG7-overexpressing BMSCs into a thermosensitive CS hydrogel, we established a dynamic, cell-responsive microenvironment that recapitulates physiological metabolic cues and significantly accelerates bone regeneration both in vitro and in vivo. This bioinspired design exemplifies a new generation of mechanism-guided biomaterials, where cellular autophagy modulation and scaffold responsiveness synergistically drive functional tissue regeneration [[51], [52], [53]]. Although pharmacological modulators such as Mdivi-1, LY294002, and FCCP validated the proposed mechanistic pathway, their off-target effects highlight the necessity for more specific genetic or molecular strategies in future studies. Collectively, these findings position ATG7 as a master regulator linking mitophagy, energy metabolism, and osteogenesis, offering a promising paradigm for metabolism-driven and autophagy-inspired bone tissue engineering.
