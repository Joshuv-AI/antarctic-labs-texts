The evolution of gravity waves as they propagate into shallower water: a field experiment
Authors: George Spiliotopoulos, Vanessa Katsardi, Vincenzo Fiamma, Carlo Ruzzo, Alessandra Romolo, Felice Arena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a small-scale field investigation into the evolution of surface gravity waves propagating above relatively steep bed slopes () from deep waters to the shallowest threshold of intermediate water. The measurements were conducted at the Natural Ocean Engineering Laboratory (NOEL). The study analyzes diverse sea states to determine the influence of sea-state steepness and effective water depth () on wave profiles and statistics. The results show a clear transition in wavefield properties: spectra narrow and peak in intermediate depths () before significant energy dissipates in shallower water due to breaking (). While extreme waves in deeper water () exhibit shapes consistent with quasi-determinism theory, wave profiles in shallow water (), evolve toward solitary wave trains, indicating a transition where frequency dispersion does not dominate the regime anymore. The analysis of wave height distributions shows that modern formulations perform robustly in intermediate depths, but all models struggle with the steepest, shallowest conditions where nonlinearity and breaking compete intensely.Keywords: Field Experiment, Random Waves, Probability of Exceedence, Finite Water Depths, Extreme WavesSubject terms: Natural hazards, Ocean sciences, Physics

Introduction

This paper presents field observations of surface gravity waves, documented at the Natural Ocean Engineering Laboratory (NOEL) of the Mediterranea University of Reggio Calabria, Italy. The experiment emphasizes the evolution of a sea state as it transits from deep to shallow waters. Previous relevant 2D and 3D laboratory investigations1–3 have demonstrated that the characteristics and formation of limiting waves are critically dependent on the effective water depth (, where is the wave number corresponding to the spectral peak and d the water depth) and the wave steepness (, where is the significant wave height). However, these studies are in some respect constrained by the laboratory environment, either by the simplified 2D wave generation, or by the finite flume/basin length (restricting both fetch and wave development), by the absence of natural wind forcing, the scale effects related to viscous decay that can artificially influence the wave propagation.

Consequently, laboratory studies cannot fully replicate the combined effects of natural wind forcing, wave development, and broad-spectrum nonlinearity encountered in the field. Furthermore, while field experiments offer realism, the vast majority have been conducted on mild or nearly flat bathymetries4,5. This has created a significant gap in understanding wave transformation on the steeper slopes (e.g., 1/10–1/5) that are prevalent in many rocky, tectonically active coastlines, such as those in the Mediterranean region. In contrast, the field conditions at NOEL can mitigate these limitations, allowing the observation of wave evolution under realistic wind forcing and over natural bathymetry.

This study is designed to bridge three key gaps in the existing literature on nearshore wave evolution: (i) the limitations of laboratory environments in reproducing realistic sea states; (ii) the inability of typical field campaigns, which rely on isolated probes, to track the continuous spatial evolution of a specific wavefield; and (iii) the scarcity of field measurements on steep seabed slopes where nonlinear and breaking dynamics are most intense. While previous laboratory investigations have used wave gauges to track evolution in controlled settings, and field campaigns have typically relied on isolated probes at disparate locations, our experiment employs a series of co-located probes to continuously monitor the evolution of the same wavefield as it propagates from deep to the shallow threshold of intermediate waters.

This setup provides a rare, direct field measurement of the wave transformation process. The primary objective of this work is to identify the physics underlying the evolution of the wavefield including the corresponding wave height statistics under realistic, yet scalable, field conditions.

The objective of this experiment is to measure numerous sea states evolving from deep to shallow water, capturing the role of various wavefield characteristics. These include the effective water depth and wave steepness, as well as directionality, spectral bandwidth, and the uni-/multimodal nature of the frequency spectrum. While the full dataset encompasses these diverse parameters, the present work focuses on a selected series of sea states characterized by a unimodal spectrum, covering a range from linear to breaking wave conditions.

Background

The NOEL has been a site for a multitude of field experiments6–12 while the theory for the wave energy converter device, the so-called REWEC, was validated there as a scale model13–15.

Regarding previous work relative to our research and concerning wavefields propagating from deep to shallower water depths, relevant experiments have been previously conducted at NOEL 15 years ago. In these experiments, proof was derived for the quasi-determinism theory (QD) of Boccotti6,16 both in terms of the wave form17 as well as in predicting the probability of large wave heights10. However, these experiments used pressure transducers submerged on a horizontal beam almost aligned with the dominant wave direction, which captured pressure head waves rather than direct surface elevation. While these studies provided foundational insights, the experimental setup was not optimized to capture the high-fidelity, continuous spatial evolution of the surface elevation; the latter being essential for analyzing detailed wave transformation processes. Our new experiment directly addresses this by utilizing a strategically aligned array of ultrasonic probes to obtain high-fidelity measurements of the surface elevation, thereby enabling a detailed investigation of wave evolution from deep to shallow water.

For instance, Whittaker et al.18 analysed field data of surface water waves propagating on relatively shallow waters (effective water depth ) using buoy measurements at individual, isolated locations rather than through simultaneous measurements across a range of water depths, precluding the tracking of continuous spatial evolution. Studies in this range are of particular interest due to the scarcity of field data at such small effective depths. They have also shown that the quasi-determinism16 (QD) description of large waves may have some relevance even in this shallow water range. Nevertheless, the comparisons were made with the average linearised profile of the largest non-breaking measured waves. In the shallowest regions, the dispersive properties are limited, and the waves exhibit solitary-type behavior. Evidence for the latter is provided by field data19 that involve severe sea states with large waves, including breaking waves, propagating at effective water depths between . Strong relevant evidence of the large waves’ behaviour in the shallow end of intermediate waters is also shown in other flume laboratory1,20,21 and numerical22 experiments.

The formation of large waves is known to directly influence the statistical properties of a wavefield, including wave crest and height distributions23–25. While nonlinearity is very significant, its effects are moderated by wave breaking, which imposes a limit on wave height and consequently alters these statistical properties24,26. Furthermore, the relative importance of breaking increases as the effective water depth decreases. This has been demonstrated by field measurements at remote offshore locations with mild slopes ()4, and across 10 sites in the North Sea with nearly flat bathymetry (slopes )5. Earlier field campaigns in shallow water have also documented changes in wave statistics with depth. For example, the international expedition “Lubiatowo-74”27 measured wave-profile transformations across the surf zone, reporting changes in crest-height distributions, wavelength, and period. A more extensive field study including a spatial array of eleven synchronized measurements across the Black Sea coastal zone28 focused on non-Gaussian theories. While that study provided valuable field evidence of depth-induced statistical changes, it was conducted on a relatively mild slope (1/40–1/167).

Simultaneously, the competitive influence of wave steepness and breaking has also been highlighted in laboratory studies, including those in flumes with mild slopes (flat-bed, 1/100 and 1/250)29, flat-bed wave basin laboratory measurements in intermediate water depths ()30 and long directional simulations of both breaking and non-breaking waves covering a broad range of flat-bed depths ()31.

Despite the fact that these studies cited above have significantly advanced the understanding of wave transformation and statistics, their findings remain largely confined to mild or nearly flat bathymetries. This focus leaves a critical knowledge gap, because many natural coastlines–especially in active tectonic and rocky coastal settings–exhibit substantially steeper profiles. For example, the mean continental shelf slope of the Mediterranean Sea is approximately 1/82, but extensive stretches of coastline display much steeper gradients, such as the Greek coast (1/72–1/14)32, the Strait of Messina (1:6)33, and parts of the French Mediterranean (e.g., Nice, slopes of 1/14–1/10) and Atlantic Brittany (slopes up to 1/2.5)34,35. On such steep slopes, wave shoaling, nonlinearity, and breaking are compressed spatially and interact more intensely, processes that remain poorly quantified by direct field measurement. The present field experiment addresses this gap by investigating wave transformation on a seabed slope of 1/10–1/5, representative of many understudied yet geomorphologically common coastal environments.

The small-scale field measurements

The NOEL facility’s specific wind (including fetch and speed) and bathymetric conditions enable small-scale field experiments; in particular at a length scale of 1:30 to 1:50 relative to the open sea, corresponding to a time scale 1:5 to 1:7. For example, typical peak periods, range from s to s36. Due to the uncontrolled nature of field experiments, data were collected over a two-month period between May and July 2023, a timeframe ideally suited by the local wind climate, to establish a database with an optimal variety of sea states.

The wind and fetch characteristics of the NOEL site favor the generation of locally forced, fetch-limited wind waves. The spectral properties of the observed sea states–peak frequencies, significant wave heights, and spectral shapes–align with classical JONSWAP-type growth relationships for pure wind seas under steady forcing over limited fetch. Consequently, the wavefields analysed here represent coherent, locally generated wind waves. The sensor array was aligned with the dominant wave direction, ensuring consistent wind-wave alignment. A comprehensive description of the wind climate, effective fetch, and bathymetric configuration of the NOEL site, together with their relevance for the generation of locally forced JONSWAP-type wind waves, can be found in earlier site-specific studies6,9,37–40.

A sampling rate of 10 Hz was selected to ensure accurate measurements. This rate is substantially higher than the 1Hz-4Hz typical open-ocean measurements for two primary reasons. First, the smaller time scale of the NOEL sea states demands a proportionally higher sampling rate to resolve individual waves. Second, and more critically, lower sampling rates (e.g. Hz) are known to underestimate wave crests and can lead to at least a 5% underestimate of the probability of exceedance of the maximum wave height while deviations of up to 11% must be expected for the measurements of extreme wave heights5,41,42. While a Hz rate may be sufficient for full-scale open-sea conditions, the 10 Hz rate used here ensures that sampling errors are negligible for the scaled, large waves under consideration.

Measurements were conducted using an array of 7 ultrasonic probes aligned with the dominant wave direction, positioned above the average water surface and mounted on 4 distinct steel columns. There are measurements at three distinct water depth levels (Fig. 1). The current arrangement allows minimum disturbance of the wavefield, with the probes offering highly accurate measurements, while pressure transducers are also being used for validation and further processes. The water depth ranged from m at the seaward end of the experiment to m at the landward end. Variations in the water depth due to the tide were within m. Processing the field data

Surface elevation was measured continuously throughout the experiment. For practical handling and storage, the continuous time series was segmented into sequential 5-minute files. From this full dataset, stable and representative 1-hour periods were selected for detailed analysis, ensuring they covered a range of significant wave height () occurrences, thereby capturing sea-states with varying wave steepness. The 1-h time window was chosen as representative of a storm corresponding to a full scale of approximately 5-7h. In addition to the attention paid to the sampling rate discussed earlier, the fixed instruments used for the surface elevation measurements provide the most reliable information, especially when considering extreme wave events45. Nevertheless, errors and false measurements can occur during adverse conditions, where precision is most critical. To mitigate this, the chosen sea-states underwent through a quality control prior to analysis. The proposed process adopts slightly modified established approaches from the literature5,46,47, resulting in very few sea-state discards while leading to datasets largely free from spurious measurements:

Step 1: Outliers. Outliers are removed by applying a Hampel filter48 and selecting a 13-sample moving window (with six neighbors on either side). Any sample exceeding two standard deviations from the local median is replaced by a shape-preserving piecewise cubic spline interpolation (Fig. 2(a)).

Step 2: Instrument lock-In. In some cases, instrument lock-in occurs, recording more than ten5 equal or close to equal values. These are replaced with estimates extrapolated from forward and reverse autoregressive fits of the remaining samples. The window used for extrapolation is one minute of preceding and following samples from the errors. These errors are infrequent, but may cause the appearance of large periods during zero-crossing analysis. By replacing them with values derived from the adjacent sea-state we assure that the statistics are not altered and that there are no spurious outliers in the time series (Fig. 2(b)).

For small, linear waves, the surface profile is symmetrical and the MWL coincides with the SWL. Therefore, the SWL may be identified from the Signal Mean only for small waves. The 1-hour deep-water signals were finally tested for stationarity using three standard statistical tests for unit root and serial correlation49, the Augmented Dickey–Fuller test50, the Phillips–Perron test51, and the Ljung–Box test52. Having completed Steps 1–6 for all the chosen 1-hour sea-states, zero up-crossing and down-crossing analyses were performed. The differences between these two zero-crossing methods were found to be either insignificant or small with the largest down-crossing wave heights found to be larger than the up-crossing wave heights as noted in other works involving field measurements in a similar depth range5,53. Therefore, all the data that follow are based upon a down-crossing analysis and the underlying spectra were extracted by using Welch’s54 power spectral density estimate with DFT (Discrete Fourier Transform) points and a rectangular window of the same length (roughly 1/36th of the total 1 h samples), to attain high resolution and have a good approximation of the realistic spectrum.

The sea states selected for detailed analysis exhibited a narrow directional spread aligned with the probe array. Previous directional measurements at NOEL indicate that wind-generated waves at the site are characteristically long-crested, with a standard deviation of the directional distribution at the spectral peak of 9.This value corresponds to a fairly narrow directional spread, smaller than typical spreads measured for wavefields at the open-ocean waves55. Indeed, waves at NOEL come from a limited range of principal directions but also the effects of refraction decreases the directional spread near the shore. The long-crested nature of the incident wavefield ensures that the observed statistical evolution is primarily driven by depth-induced processes rather than directional wave-wave interactions. Broader directional distributions may modulate nonlinear energy transfers and breaking thresholds. Future work with the full dataset will examine how different directional spreads influence wave-height statistics.

The surface elevation for the weakly nonlinear case, , in deep water (Fig. 4(a)) exhibits a nearly focused wave trough, consistent with a wave group approaching a focused crest at a close follow-up position. Indeed, the large wave form characterised by both a large crest and trough is observed in all three depth locations. The deep trough in the shallowest location is associated with the small steepness of this case. All other cases shown in Table 1 are more nonlinear and are therefore characterised by shallower troughs in shallower waters. Similarly to Fig. 4(a), the surface elevation for the case, in deep water (Fig. 5(a)) shows a wave group towards focusing of the underlying components. Both these wave forms (Fig. 4(a) and Fig. 5(a)) are consistent with the quasi-determinism theory16,64,65 (QD), which predicts the most probable shape of a large linear wave. This finding hardly comes as a surprise, as other field47,66,67 and laboratory data24, as well as extensive numerical simulations23,68 have verified the theory in deep water. The dispersive properties of the waves measured in the deeper sensor, SD, remain for the two largest cases as well (Figs. 6(a) and 7(a)) despite the fact that the wavefield has entered the intermediate water region (). The same stands for the large waves measured at the intermediate sensor, SI, with the local effective water depths ranging from (Figs. 4(d) and 7(d)). Indeed, it has also been previously found, from the analysis of field18,26,69, laboratory26,70 and numerical data71 that the QD theory is still valid for intermediate water depths.

To provide a quantitative comparison with QD theory, the surface elevation profiles around the largest wave for case (Fig. 6(a)-(c)), based on the corresponding expected large-wave predicted by QD theory using the locally measured spectra, have also been plotted scaled to the local largest wave height. At the deep-water sensor (SD, Fig. 6(a)), the measured wave is compared with a QD profile at a phase slightly before full focusing (, from the focal point, where is the wavelength corresponding to the peak wavenumber, ), reflecting the fact that in a random record a large wave is unlikely to be exactly at the theoretical focal instant. This phase-shifted QD profile closely matches the observed wave group. At the intermediate sensor (SI, Fig. 6(b)), the measured wave aligns well with the fully focused linear QD shape, although the crests either side of the large event are somewhat shorter. The largest crest is larger due to the effects of nonlinearity. In the shallowest record (SS, Fig. 6(c)), however, the observed wave deviates markedly from the linear QD prediction: the central crest is steeper, the adjacent crests are much closer in height to the central crest, and the zero-crossing periods adjacent to the large wave are longer. These features are consistent with earlier laboratory findings in intermediate water1 and with numerical studies that highlight the role of nonlinearity in modifying extreme-wave shapes in finite depth29,72,73. The similar depth- and nonlinearity-dependent similarities or departures from the QD shape with the extreme-wave profiles of the other cases (Figs. 4,5,7), described previously, can be addressed similarly.

In all (b) and (e) plots in Figs. 4-7, a constant trend of spectra becoming narrower and peakier is identified. This is as the waves propagate from the SD to the SI wave-gauge position, in effect from deeper to the intermediate depth range. Narrower energy spectra are expected in finite water depths especially for nonlinear wavefields, due to the reduction in their dispersive properties. This effect has been previously shown numerically72,74 when comparing the nonlinearly evolved wave spectrum with the corresponding linear theory. With this experiment, this trend is verified in real sea-states as an evolution spectrum characteristic between infinite and finite water depths.

Also, it is evident that as steepness increases, the more the total energy dissipates as the waves move towards shallower water. The reduction of energy in shallower water depths, shown gradually in the Figs. 4-7(h) plots, can be attributed in part to the shoaling effect as waves move towards the shore, but in steeper conditions, it is a clear indication of wave breaking.

Special attention is paid in the wave profile in the shallower gauge, in effect all the Figs. 4-7(g) plots. The wave profiles undergo a clear transformation, gradually losing their characteristic dispersive form observed in deeper waters, evolving toward a more uniform, solitary-like appearance. Across the range of effective water depths , he wave profile evolves through distinct forms. In particular, in Fig. 5(g) where the wave propagates over the largest effective water depth (of the SS sensor), the waves resemble a regular wave train. Similar results have been found numerically for flat beds ()74, experimentally but also within field observations over mild bed slopes1. Interestingly, it is shown here, that this quasi-regular wave-form is also valid over steeper bed slopes for similar effective water depths (). In contrast, in Fig. 6(g) where the wave propagates over the smallest effective water depth (of the SS sensor, ), the wave train resembles a series of isolated, solitary-like waves, suggesting that frequency dispersion no longer dominates the wave evolution over these scales; the data appearing as individual waves that propagate as isolated events without undergoing significant change. This is again consistent with earlier laboratory findings1. In effect, the data in the cases presented in the remaining cases (Figs. 4(g),7(g)) present a median between the above two wave forms; the appears to mark a threshold where wave profiles begin to deviate noticeably from their deeper-water, dispersive shape toward more solitary-like forms. In the third column of Figs. 4-7((c),(f),(i)) the probability of exceedance, Q, of the crest heights, normalised with the local significant have height (), is plotted along with the Rayleigh crest height distribution for reference. As expected, the Rayleigh distribution generally underestimates the measured crest heights because the presented wavefields range from weakly to highly nonlinear. This systematic underestimation indicates that wave crests are statistically higher relative to their associated troughs compared to a linear Gaussian sea; a direct statistical signature of crest-trough asymmetry, visually evident in the corresponding surface elevation profiles (Figs. 4-7(a),(d),(g)). For the least steep cases ( and ), this trend is more evident in deeper water, especially for (Figs. 4(c),5(c)). The deviation of the crest height distribution from the Rayleigh one, indicative of crest-trough asymmetry, for the same two wave groups, as they propagate in intermediate waters, is only marginally smaller (Figs. 4(f),5(f)). In shallow water, the steeper case agrees better with the Rayleigh distribution (especially in Fig. 5(i)), suggesting a reduction in crest-trough asymmetry, likely due to increased breaking dissipation that limits crest growth. On the other hand, for the steepest cases ( and ) the trend observed in the crest height distributions suggests that crest-trough asymmetry does not diminish but rather intensifies as the wave groups propagate from deeper to shallower water depths. Also, particular interest lies in the wave height distributions from sea states where rogue waves (satisfying both criteria) occur (Figs. 4(c), 6(f)). In these distributions, the largest crest elevations are markedly separated from the distribution of the rest of the wavefield; a defining feature leading to their classification as rogue waves.

Concurrently, a progressive increase in normalised crest-height () was often observed from deep to shallow water, serving as a direct signature of growing nonlinearity and the changing shape characteristics of individual waves towards more peaked crests and flatter troughs. These combined influences are increasingly present as nonlinearity increases and are associated with at least second-order effects82.

It is important to emphasize that the wave-height distributions examined here derive from locally generated, fetch-limited wind waves, whose spectral properties follow classical growth laws (see Section “The Small-Scale Field Measurements”). This consistent wind-forcing context ensures the wavefields are coherent, stationary, and free of swell contamination, providing a well-controlled baseline. Consequently, the observed evolution in distribution shape (the elevated body and truncated upper tail in shallow water) can be attributed primarily to shoaling and breaking rather than to variations in wind forcing or mixed sea-state conditions.

Furthermore, it is also important to note that this evolution in distribution shape occurs over a very short spatial scale due to the steep local slope (1/5 to 1/10). The vertical drop is approximately 2.2m over 10 m horizontally from SD to SI sensors, and 1.3m over 12 m from SI to SS sensors. Over such a steep, rapidly varying bathymetry, the wave statistics do not reach a local equilibrium at each sensor location; instead, the measurements capture a continuous spatial transformation of the wavefield. A clear change in the shape of the distribution is already evident between the intermediate (SI) and shallow (SS) sensors (Fig. 9(b)), which is particularly pronounced for cases with larger Ursell numbers (cases III, IV and VI). This indicates that the stronger nonlinearity associated with higher accelerates the statistical adjustment. The observed evolution therefore reflects a persistent non-equilibrium state, consistent with the concept of finite relaxation lengths for statistical adjustment over variable bathymetry83. On such steep slopes, the competing processes of nonlinear shoaling and depth-limited breaking are compressed into a short fetch, making the evolution of statistical shape particularly sensitive to the degree of nonlinearity.

The H/d ratio and the wave steepness parameter are plotted at the last two rows of Fig. 9. A clear trend of increasing wave steepness () as water depth decreases is evident, with the most pronounced increases occurring in the shallowest record. The small changes in steepness between deep and intermediate records is likely due to shoaling, while in shallow water, steepness appears to approach a limiting value, constrained by wave breaking. More definitively, the ratio in shallow water reached or surpassed the classic McCowan75 breaking limit for solitary waves (e.g., values of 0.871 and 0.791 in Table 2 for cases and , respectively), indicating intense breaking events and establishing the upper bound of wave stability in such environments.

Wavefields characterised by a smaller peak period (s, II. and V. ) show a distinct behaviour when it comes to wave steepness distribution. Wave steepness is smaller in intermediate water compared to deep water, only to increase again in shallower water. However, these examples are also characterised by small Ursell numbers, , hence they are less nonlinear; while based on the H/d plots, not a lot of wave breaking is present during the evolution of their wavefield.

Assessment of wave height models

The performance of the established wave height distributions (Rayleigh56, Forristall57, Boccotti10, Klopman59, and Karmpadakis et al.2) is evaluated against the measured wave height data from the six selected sea states, as detailed in Table 1. The comparison is presented in terms of the normalised wave height, , across the three depth levels: deep (SD), intermediate (SI), and shallow (SS).

For cases and (Figs. 10 and 12, respectively), the modified distribution by Karmpadakis et al.2 demonstrates the most consistent agreement with the measured data across all water depths. Although these are not the least steep cases in the dataset, they correspond to the smallest Ursell number () in deep water; a parameter more directly linked to nonlinear effects. This model, which incorporates an exponential shape parameter K dependent on , effectively captures the subtle changes in the wave height distribution as waves propagate into shallower water. The distributions by Forristall and Boccotti also perform reasonably well, particularly in the deeper (SD) and intermediate (SI) sensors. Boccotti’s distribution, which accounts for finite spectral bandwidth effects, shows a notable ability to predict the upper tail of the distribution in intermediate depths, underscoring the continued relevance of spectral characteristics even in transitional depth regimes. The classical Rayleigh distribution, as expected, consistently underestimates the measured wave heights due to its underlying linear assumptions, with the discrepancy becoming more pronounced in shallower waters where nonlinear effects intensify.

A central observation from these comparisons is the changing shape of the wave height distribution as waves shoal. In most cases, the distribution in shallow water exhibits a characteristic pattern relative to its deep-water counterpart: for more common waves (higher probabilities of exceedance, Q), the ratio is higher, but for the most extreme waves (lower Q), the ratio becomes lower. This pattern is a direct signature of the competing processes of nonlinear shoaling, which generally increases wave heights, and selective depth-induced breaking, which truncates the upper tail of the distribution by limiting the maximum possible wave height. Large waves that grow within wavefields of high Ursell numbers, e.g. and 0.141 (Figs. 11-13), are losing part of their energy through breaking, as discussed in Fig. 8, and hence their wave height, lowering the upper tail of the distribution. Conversely, the large waves within low Ursell numbers, e.g. and 0.130 (Figs. 10-12) continue to steepen through shoaling and nonlinear wave-wave interactions, elevating the body of the distributions ( increases similarly for both cases beyond ). It should be emphasized that this transformation occurs over a very short horizontal distance on the steep slope studied here, meaning the statistics are in a pronounced non-equilibrium state throughout the measurement array. The models that explicitly incorporate a depth-limiting factor, such as Klopman and Karmpadakis et al., are designed to capture this truncation, though the precise calibration for the steep seabed slope encountered in this experiment remains a challenge.

In summary, while modern formulations like that of Karmpadakis et al.2 offer robust predictions across a wide range of intermediate depths and lower steepness conditions, accurately modeling the statistics of the steepest waves in very shallow water, where nonlinearity and breaking compete intensely, remains an open challenge. The results highlight the necessity for further refinement of existing models, potentially by incorporating a more direct dependence on the local bed slope or improved parameterizations of breaking dissipation, particularly for steeply sloping bottoms such as the one investigated here (1/10-1/5).

Conclusions and discussion

This study has presented a detailed analysis of a comprehensive field dataset that captures the evolution of surface gravity waves as they propagate from deep water to the shallow threshold of intermediate waters. The investigation, conducted at the NOEL facility, focused on the interplay between spectral parameters, local water depth, wave profile characteristics, and statistical properties across a wide range of wave steepness. This research presents several key novelties that advance the understanding of nearshore wave dynamics. Firstly, it provides a unique and comprehensive field dataset that directly tracks the evolution of the same wavefield from deep to shallow water over a significantly steeper seabed slope (1/10–1/5) than previously studied in most field experiments. This setup overcomes limitations of laboratory studies and isolated field probes, offering a rare continuous view of the transformation process under realistic conditions. Secondly, the study clearly documents and quantifies the progression from a dispersive wavefield, where extreme waves conform to quasi-determinism theory, to a regime in very shallow water () where waves resemble solitary wave trains where frequency dispersion does not dominate the regime anymore. This morphological transition initiates for , providing direct field evidence for this fundamental shift in wave behaviour.

The analysis of frequency spectra reveals a clear transition in the wavefield’s energy distribution that is strongly dependent on both steepness and depth. As waves propagate into shallower water, a shoaling-induced narrowing and peaking of the spectrum is observed in intermediate depths, followed by significant dissipation of total energy in the shallowest waters. This energy loss is markedly more pronounced in steeper sea states, unequivocally attributed to wave breaking. The spectral evolution patterns observed align with previous studies indicating that the relative importance of breaking increases substantially as the effective water depth decreases1,2,4, though the field measurements herein provide more direct evidence of this transition under natural conditions on a steep slope.

The wave profiles in the vicinity of the maximum wave height provide compelling evidence of the changing physical processes governing wave evolution. In deeper (SD) and intermediate (SI) waters, the form of large waves is consistent with the predictions of quasi-determinism theory16, often appearing as focused wave groups. This is further supported by the occurrence of rogue waves; as shown in Table 2, local maxima in various cases exceed the threshold. The fact that these extreme events occur across a range of wavefield steepness underscores that they are often a result of dispersive focusing rather than being solely dependent on local nonlinearity47. However, as steepness and shallowness increase, the waves increasingly “feel” the bed, leading to profound changes in wave characteristics. In the shallowest records (SS), particularly at very low effective water depths (), the wave profiles closely resemble a train of solitary waves, suggesting that frequency dispersion no longer dominates wave evolution in shallow water. This finding confirms earlier laboratory work1 and field observations19, demonstrating the above transitions. Table 2 further quantifies this transition, showing how local steepness metrics like and generally increase as waves shoal, highlighting the growing nonlinearity.

The analysis of probability distributions for key parameters further elucidated the wave transformation process. The crest height distributions consistently deviated from the linear Rayleigh reference, underscoring the prevalence of nonlinearity. This crest-trough asymmetry generally intensified from deep to shallow water, a signature of growing second-order effects, but this trend could be reversed in the shallowest depths by intense breaking dissipation. The competing influences of nonlinearity and breaking were vividly captured in the wave height exceedance plots, which exhibited a characteristic shape in shallow water: the bulk of the distribution was elevated due to shoaling-induced nonlinearity, while the extreme tail was truncated by the selective breaking of the largest waves. This competition culminates in a depth-limited state, where metrics such as approached or surpassed the theoretical breaking limit, and the highest waves in shallow water were constrained by the deep-water significant wave height. Furthermore, wavefields with smaller peak periods exhibited a unique evolution of wave steepness, influenced by a complex interplay of shoaling effects on amplitude and wavelength, highlighting the nuanced role of spectral characteristics in the transformation process.

The comparison of measured wave height distributions with established theoretical models underscores the considerable challenge of predicting statistics in steep, shallow water conditions. For higher frequency cases with lower nonlinearity, the slightly modified distribution by Karmpadakis et al.2 showed the best agreement across all depth levels, demonstrating the value of their improved parameterization of the shape factor K and the use of . However, for the steeper and shallower cases, the predictive capability of all models diminished significantly in the shallowest waters, reflecting the complex and competing physical processes at play. In these extreme conditions, Klopman’s59 distribution, with its more gradual depth dependence, sometimes performed better at the SS sensor, but at the cost of overestimation in deeper waters. The modifications introduced by Karmpadakis et al.2 provided substantial improvement over the original Klopman distribution in deep and intermediate depths, but accurately modeling the statistics of the steepest waves in very shallow water remains a formidable challenge.

In summary, this field experiment has successfully documented the continuous transformation of a wavefield from a deep-water, dispersive system to a shallow-water, nonlinear and depth-limited system. The transition is governed by the competing influences of wave steepness and effective water depth, which modulate both nonlinear energy transfer and dissipative breaking processes. The findings demonstrate that while established wave height distributions10,57,59 can offer adequate predictions in deeper water and more recent2 models can improve these predictions particularly in intermediate depths. Difficulties remain for modeling the statistics of the steepest waves in very shallow water. This is due to the intense and localized nature of wave breaking, particularly on steeper bed slopes such as the one in this experiment, which are not yet fully captured by existing statistical models. Future work should focus on incorporating the effects of seabed slope directly into these models to enhance their predictive capability in such critical coastal environments.
