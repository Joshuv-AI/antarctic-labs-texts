Prediction model for depression risk in middle-aged and elderly patients with metabolic syndrome: a nomogram and interpretable machine learning approach based on CHARLS
Authors: Jiahao Chen, Yisi Lin, Rui Hu, Chuanchen Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with metabolic syndrome (MetS) are more prone to depression, which is a significant complication impacting quality of life. This research seeks to create and validate predictive models for assessing depression risk in patients with MetS.MethodsData from the 2011 (baseline) and 2015 waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were employed in this study. By excluding variables with more than 20% missing values, 38 features, such as demographic information, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, health status indicators, and blood test information, were included. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) identified 11 key contributors, and 6 machine learning (ML) models were employed to determine the best depression risk in patients with MetS. Furthermore, the 2015 CHARLS data were included as a temporal validation cohort.ResultsIn the 2011 CHARLS data, 5204 patients with MetS were analyzed, of whom 2543 (48.6%) exhibiting depression as indicated by a CESD-10 score of 10 or higher. 11 factors were selected to develop six ML models. The logistic regression (LR) (AUC: 0.749, 95% CI: 0.725–0.773) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) (AUC: 0.749, 95% CI: 0.725–0.773) models showed the same predictive ability in the test set. Utilizing grid search optimization, the XGBoost model attained an AUC of 0.737 (95% CI: 0.714–0.760) on the validation set.ConclusionThe nomogram and SHAP visualization provide reliable tools for predicting depression in patients with MetS. The clinical utility of models applying LR and XGBoost is noteworthy, offering crucial insights for earlier detection and preventative actions for community staff and doctors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07434-7.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Depression, CHARLS, Machine learning, Prediction model

Introduction

Depression, acknowledged as a common mental health concern, exerts a substantial influence on the global health landscape. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study underscored its significant impact on individuals and communities [1]. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is characterized by a cluster of metabolic disturbances that escalate the probability of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [2], hypertension [3], type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [4], and mortality [5, 6], and is strongly associated with depression [7, 8]. Previous research revealed that individuals with MetS have a higher likelihood of developing depression than those without MetS, and that the risk increases with the number of components [9, 10]. Furthermore, numerous studies have demonstrated that among patients with metabolic syndrome (including hypertension, diabetes and CVD), the presence of depression is associated with a significant increase in mortality rates [11–13]. Consequently, the development of a predictive model for depression is of particular importance for individuals with MetS.

Previous research has identified several key factors associated with the onset and persistence of depression in individuals with metabolic-related disorders, such as T2DM, hypertension, and CVD. These factors include older age, female gender, low educational attainment, sleep duration, and chronic pain [14–18]. However, the underlying mechanism between metabolic disorders and depression remains unclear. In recent years, impairment of the glymphatic system has been found to be the bridge between metabolic dysfunction and depression, and several studies have linked energy metabolism, mitochondrial hypotheses, and depression [19, 20]. Due to the intricate relationship between Mets and depression development, it’s crucial to pinpoint essential characteristics and create a dependable predictive tool for early detection and personalized treatment.

Machine learning (ML) algorithms can provide a revolutionary method for tackling these issues by leveraging four main technical benefits. Firstly, ML algorithms inherently identify complex patterns within high-dimensional datasets without the need for predefined configuration constants or imperative rule encoding. Secondly, ML algorithms effectively generalize learned patterns from training sets to diverse validation datasets, ensuring consistent predictive accuracy across varied populations. Thirdly, contemporary ML frameworks incorporate distributed processing architectures alongside automated optimization protocols, enabling the efficient analysis of terabyte-scale healthcare data while minimizing resource overhead. Lastly, ML models dynamically refine decision boundaries through continuous feedback mechanisms in response to evolving data streams, facilitating real-time clinical decision support in dynamic environments [21–23].

ML algorithms are extensively used in constructing depression risk prediction models. Hong et al. utilized ML to create a depression risk prediction model for disabled elderly adults in China [24]. A study by Zheng et al. developed an ML prediction model for 2-year depression in Chinese elderly individuals with chronic diseases, utilizing data from a large national cohort [25]. These studies indicate that ML algorithms enhance predictive accuracy, minimize errors, and handle large data sets, demonstrating significant potential. An extensive literature review revealed that existing research predominantly focuses on the association between MetS and depression, lacking studies that utilize ML techniques to develop predictive models for depression risk in MetS patients. This study leverages a comprehensive dataset from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to identify factors linked to depression in individuals with MetS, aiming to develop an ML-based risk prediction model for depression in these patients.

Method

Participants

The study utilized data obtained from CHARLS [26]. CHARLS is a longitudinal survey designed to represent Chinese adults aged 45 and older. It began with a national baseline survey in 2011, followed by nationwide surveys in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. CHARLS participants were selected by a strategy that involved multistage probability sampling and a size-proportionate method. CHARLS encompassed 150 counties and districts across 28 provinces, as well as 450 villages, in China. The CHARLS data received approval from the Peking University Ethics Review Committee (IRB00001052-11015), and informed consent was secured from all participants. The participants were interviewed face-to-face in their homes using computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) technology.

We used the data from wave 1 (baseline) of CHARLS to develop a series of predictive models for depression in participants with MetS. Additionally, we used data from wave 3 (2015) as a temporal cohort to validate the risk prediction model. Participants were excluded if they were under 45 years old or had missing data on MetS and depression. Ultimately, 5205 individuals were included from wave 1, and 1943 participants were included from wave 3 for model validation (Fig. 1).

Data collection

MetS

MetS was defined based on the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Panel III (ATP III) criteria [27]. Participants were classified as having MetS if they exhibited three or more of the following components: (1) elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG ≥ 100 mg/dL) or were on medication for high glucose; (2) dyslipidemia, indicated by total cholesterol (TC ≥ 150 mg/dL) or treatment for high triglycerides; (3) low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women); (4) elevated blood pressure (systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg) or were on antihypertensive medication; (5) central obesity, defined by a waist circumference (WC) ≥ 90 cm for men or ≥ 85 cm for women.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), a 10-item scale known for its strong validity and reliability in detecting depression in Chinese adults [27, 28]. The CESD-10 comprises 10 items, each rated on a scale of 0 to 3, indicating the frequency of event occurrences from ‘not at al’ to ‘nearly every day’. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, where higher scores suggest more severe depressive symptoms; a score of 10 or above is a reflection of clinically significant depression [29]. Participants were divided into two groups based on scoring outcomes: those with MetS without depression and those with MetS with depression.

Predictive variables

In the process of selecting potential predictor variables, an initial literature review was conducted to identify variables previously demonstrated to be significant in predicting depression [9, 30–36]. We identified five domains of potential predictors, including: (1) Demographic information: age, gender, marital status (Married/Not married), education level (Primary or lower/Junior high school or above), and residence (Urban/Rural); (2) Lifestyle factors: smoking status, drinking status, self-assessment of life satisfaction (Satisfied/Not Satisfied), self-assessment of health (Good/Not Good), Nap (0 min, 1–30 min and >30 min), nocturnal sleep time (hours); (3) Comorbidity (showed as the total numbers of coexisting comorbidities): hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychological disorders, arthritis, dyslipidemia, liver and kidney diseases, digestive disorders, asthma, and memory issues; (4) Health status indicators: body mass index (BMI), BP (average systolic and diastolic), average pulse, brain damage, pain, self-reported vision and hearing conditions (Good/Not Good), basic and instrumental activities of daily living (BADL and IADL); (5) Blood test information: blood urea nitrogen (BUN), FPG, creatinine, TC, HDL-C, C-reactive protein, glycated hemoglobin, uric acid, white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume, cystatin, and platelet count.

Data processing

Before training a model, feature selection is important for preventing overfitting by discarding features that are both irrelevant and redundant [37]. In this study, predictors with more than 20% missing were excluded, leaving 38 predictors in the analysis (Table S1). For missing data, we used.

multiple imputation by chained equations with the “mice” package of R software. Specifically, iterations of 10 times were used to estimate the missing values and five imputed datasets were generated. Among the 5 imputed datasets generated, the best set of data was selected for subsequent analysis based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [38]. This method reduces potential selection bias by including all participants, even those with incomplete records, whereas direct deletion could jeopardize the representativeness of the sample. Subsequently, the Z-score algorithm was employed to normalize the continuous variables, thereby facilitating a consistent evaluation of attributes and mitigating the risk of model overfitting. For model development and initial test, we adopted a stratified random splitting protocol whereby each group was partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets [39]. Feature engineering and algorithm training were performed on the training set, whereas the test and validation sets were used to assess model generalizability.

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis identified 11 variables, which were subsequently validated using a 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 2). The selected predictors included: gender, education level, self-assessment of health, self-assessment of life satisfaction, number of concomitant diseases, BADLs, IADLs, Self-reported vision, night sleep time, pain, and creatinine. After LASSO, potential multicollinearity among predictors was thoroughly assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) analysis. A VIF threshold greater than 5 was employed to identify significant collinearity among the features. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression (LR) analysis was used to assess selected predictors of depression for patients with MetS (Table S2). Six ML algorithms, including LR, Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), were employed to predict depression risk in participants with MetS.

Model evaluation

Three criteria were used to assess the clinical significance of the predictive models: discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical benefit. The discriminative ability of models was evaluated using the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve, while calibration and the discrepancy between predicted and actual outcomes were assessed through the calibration curve and Brier score. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was applied to evaluate clinical utility by calculating net benefit over various probability thresholds. The model’s effectiveness was evaluated using a confusion matrix, enabling the calculation of accuracy, sensitivity, precision, specificity, and F1-score. Furthermore, Shapley Additive explanations (SHAP) method based on a game theory is used to interpret results of any ML model [40]. In this study, SHAP values and metrics of variable importance were utilized to evaluate feature contributions.

Statistical analysis

The presentation of continuous variables is in the form of mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are represented by frequencies or percentages, and comparisons are performed using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, version 4.4.2).

Results

Patient characteristics

Out of 17,705 participants identified in the 2011 CHARLS wave, 5,668 diagnosed with MetS were included. Following the application of exclusion criteria, 463 patients were eliminated, leaving a total of 5204 participants in the final sample. Among the participants, 2543 individuals (48.3%) had depression (with a CESD-10 score ≥ 10), and 2661 individuals (51.7%) did not. Among the total cohort, 60.1% were women, 86.7% were married, 79.2% lived in rural regions, and 70.8% had an education level of primary school or less. The dataset was split 7:3, with the training set consisting of 3643 individuals and the test set comprising 1561 participants. Baseline characteristics of the training set were presented in Table 1. Significant differences were shown in demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, comorbidity, health status indicators, and blood test biomarkers between individuals with and without depression. In addition, data from the 2015 CHARLS wave were incorporated as a validation set. A total of 1,943 patients with MetS were included after excluding cases with missing information. Optimal ML model selection

Before modeling, the VIF analysis was conducted. As indicated in Table S3, the VIF values span from 1.019 (self-assessment life) to 1.338 (gender), which are considerably below the threshold of 5. This confirms the presence of weak collinearity among these variables, thereby affirming their appropriateness for further analysis.

The prediction of depression among MetS patients was carried out using six ML models, and their performance is illustrated in Fig. 3 via the ROC curve, calibration curve, and DCA. The RF model demonstrated strong discriminative performance (Fig. 3-A), achieving an AUC of 0.896 (95% CI: 0.886–0.906), an accuracy of 0.820, a sensitivity of 0.728, a specificity of 0.900, a precision of 0.884, and an F1 score of 0.798. However, the RF model demonstrated moderate robustness on the test set with an AUC of 0.740 (95% CI: 0.716–0.766) (Fig. 3-B). This overfitting feature of RF models has been found in several studies of predictive models for depression based on the CHARLS database [31, 41, 42]. Furthermore, although the AUC of RF in the test set (0.740) was comparable to that of LR and XGBoost (0.749), its brier score substantially (Fig. 3-D) worse than that of LR: 0.203 (95% CI: 0.189–0.216) and XGBoosrt: 0.206 (95% CI: 0.193–0.219). The DCA curves also showed a worse performance of RF than that of LR and XGBoost (Fig. 3-F). Therefore, taking all the above three points into consideration, we decided not to consider it the best model. While the LR model did not achieve the highest predictive performance in the training set, with an AUC of 0.755 (95% CI: 0.740–0.771), it demonstrated consistently stable performance in the test set, achieving an AUC of 0.749 (95% CI: 0.725–0.773), an accuracy of 0.675, a sensitivity of 0.631, a specificity of 0.717, a precision of 0.678, and an F1 score of 0.654. XGBoost demonstrated robust predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.797 (95% CI: 0.783–0.812) on the training set, second only to the RF model. It also attained an AUC of 0.749 (95% CI: 0.725–0.773) on the test set, matching the highest rank with the LR model. While SVM, KNN, and LightGBM showed strong predictive capabilities, LR and XGBoost were deemed more stable across various datasets, thus identifying them as the optimal models. The calibration curve was used to evaluate model calibration, with LR achieving a Brier score of 0.200 (95% CI: 0.187–0.213) in the training set and 0.203 (95% CI: 0.189–0.216) in the test set; XGBoost achieving a Brier score of 0.191 (95% CI: 0.178–0.204) in the training set and 0.206 (95% CI 0.193–0.219) in the test set, demonstrating strong calibration and predictive accuracy (Fig. 3C-D). Furthermore, the DCA curves of LR and XGBoost are depicted in Fig. 3 (E-F). Within the figure, gray lines indicate scenarios where the model predicted no depression in individuals with MetS under extreme thresholds, yielding a net clinical benefit of 0. In contrast, black lines with negative slopes denote negative net clinical benefit, reflecting the model’s prediction of depression in all MetS patients under maximal intervention intensity. The colored lines (blue for LR and green for XGBoost) represent the DCA curve specific these two models. As demonstrated, when threshold probabilities fall below 90% in the test set (or < 92% in the training set) for LR and threshold probabilities fall below 86% in the test set (or < 77% in the training set) for XGBoost, screening strategies based on our models generate superior net benefit.

Hyperparameter tuning and model assessment on the validation dataset

We ultimately selected the XGBoost model for hyperparameter optimization using grid search. The optimization process identified the following optimal parameters: learning_rate of 0.1, max_depth: 3, subsample of 0.8. After optimization, the model achieved an AUC of 0.769 (95% CI: 0.753–0.784) on the training data and 0.755 (95% CI: 0.731–0.779) on the testing data (Figure S1). Furthermore, in the validation set (Fig. 4), the LR model and XGBoost model both exhibited good predictive performance, LR showed an AUC of 0.735 (95% CI: 0.712–0.758), an accuracy of 0.691, a sensitivity of 0.600, a specificity of 0.757, a precision of 0.636, and an F1 score of 0.617; XGBoost model showed an AUC of 0.737 (95% CI: 0.714–0.760), with an accuracy of 0.692, a sensitivity of 0.619, a specificity of 0.744, a precision of 0.631, and an F1 score of 0.625. The calibration curve showed that the LR achieved a Brier score of 0.203 (95% CI: 0.189–0.216) and the XGBoost achieved a Brier score of 0.204 (95% CI: 0.186–0.222). Moreover, the DCA of the validation dataset, interventions guided by both prediction models yielded outstanding results, except for a narrow range of low preferences. Furthermore, SHAP values were displayed to understand the model’s predictions, identifying pain, sleep time, and self-assessment of health as the main contributors, with other variables playing a minor role (Fig. 5-B).

Construction of a nomogram

Since LR was ultimately selected as one of the optimal predictive models, we developed a nomogram incorporating the 11 previously identified independent risk factors. As shown in Fig. 5-A, each variable is represented on a separate axis with calibrated values. Summing these individual scores yields a total predictive score, where higher values correspond to increased probabilities of depression in individuals with MetS.

Discussion

This study developed and validated two prediction models for assessing depression in middle-aged and older adults in China using the CHARLS database. Individuals with MetS face a significantly higher risk of depression compared to those without the condition [10, 43].

The mechanism linking MetS and depression remain incompletely elucidated, reflecting a multifactorial biological interplay. Current evidence suggests that shared underlying processes may contribute to this comorbidity, including dysregulated neuroinflammatory cascades, such as elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction that impairs cellular energetics [20, 44]. Moreover, emerging research further implicates impaired glymphatic clearance in the pathophysiology of both conditions, where disrupted toxin removal exacerbates neuroinflammation and leads to the accumulation of metabolic waste [19]. These interconnected pathways—encompassing neuroendocrine, metabolic, and clearance systems—underscore the complex, bidirectional nature of the association between MetS and depression.

While many studies have investigated the link between MetS and depression, no prior research has developed a reliable predictive model for assessing depression risk using a large national database. Herein, this study bridges the gap in existing literature regarding ML’s connection between Mets and depression. Regarding predictive factors, the selection of variables plays a key role in predicting prediction models. The LASSO algorithm was applied to identify 11 significant variables from the initial set of 38 features. As presented in Fig. 5, when individuals with MetS, a higher assessment of health and life, fewer comorbidities, and more nighttime sleep time, collectively decreased depression risk. Conversely, being female, experiencing pain, with disability, bad vision, and lower creatinine significantly gave rise to the higher likelihood of depressive symptoms. Previous studies have thoroughly investigated the connection between these factors and depression.

Regarding gender and educational attainment, which served as significant predictors of depression [45–47]. Gender disparities in depression prevalence are well-documented, with women consistently exhibiting higher rates of depression than men, potentially due to biological factors such as hormonal fluctuations, combined with sociocultural stressors like gender-based discrimination and caregiving burdens (e.g., caring for family members). Lower educational levels, conversely, are associated with limited access to higher economic levels and medical resources and worse cognitive function, amplifying vulnerability to chronic stressors and diseases, which may precipitate the occurrence of depression.

Similarly, pain, chronic diseases, and ADL limitation (BADL and IADL) are linked to depression [48–50]. A meta-analysis has shown that the risk of depression is three to four times higher in older adults with chronic illnesses, whether single or multiple, than in those without these conditions [51]. As a recent study based on the CHARLS reported, pain and commodities are two important features in predicting disabilities in older adults in China [52]. People with chronic pain or comorbidities often develop functional limitations that impair occupational ability, daily living, and recreational activities, ultimately reducing quality of life and preventing positive life experiences. Individuals with chronic diseases often experience disabilities and persistent pain, leading to substantial psychological stress that may result in depression [53].

Self-assessments of health and life satisfaction are key factors affecting depression risk in middle-aged and older adults with MetS. Negative assessments of self-health and life satisfaction were linked to a higher risk of depression. These findings align with a study published in The Lancet [54]. According to the study, depression was connected to lower health conditions, regardless of whether it appeared alone or with other chronic comorbidities. Besides, Spence et al. similarly showed that better physical health is inversely related to the likelihood of depression [55]. Individuals in poor health often experience chronic diseases and pain, impacting their emotional and mental health. Life satisfaction directly represents both subjective well-being and an individual’s comprehensive outlook on life. Patients with MetS are more prone to developing chronic diseases such as CVD, chronic kidney disease, and osteoarthritis [56–58], which are all strong risk factors for depression. These conditions can result in disability and significantly decrease satisfaction with life, thereby increasing the risk of depression [59].

Research focusing on the relationship between nocturnal sleep and the incidence of depression has increased significantly in recent years. A large-scale study by Dong et al. indicates that, after adjusting for confounding variables, sleeping more than 8 h is significantly associated with a reduced risk of depression (OR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.84–0.91) [36]. Further analysis using CHARLS data also supports this inverse relationship, showing that longer night sleep duration predicts a lower risk of depression [60]. Underlying mechanisms between sleep deficiency and depression involve overactivation of the cerebral nuclei, neurotransmitter system disorder, and reduced neuroplasticity [61, 62]. These disturbances can disrupt both sleep regulation and mood stability and are linked to dysregulation of hormonal systems critical for mood regulation.

Among people aged 50 and over, screening detected bilateral presenting distance VI or blindness in nearly 80% of cases, and near VI in two-thirds of cases [63]. Research indicated that self-reported VI (including distance VI and near VI) is associated with the increased risk of depression [64]. Patients with MetS are more susceptible to the acceleration of retinal aging and have a higher risk of suffering from glaucomatous optic neuropathy or macular degeneration [65].

Emerging evidence highlights serum creatinine as a potential predictor of depression both in animals and humans. Liu et al. [66] uncovered an association between reduced creatinine levels and an increased risk of depression. The researchers also examined the link between creatinine levels and Hamilton Depression Scale scores, discovering a significant negative correlation in both plasma and urine samples. Creatinine is a product of muscle metabolism, and its concentration is positively correlated with the muscle mass [67]. Research indicates that sarcopenia and frailty, both age-associated conditions marked by reduced muscle mass, are significantly linked to depression [68, 69]. Moreover, recent investigations suggest that individuals with MetS have an increased propensity to develop sarcopenia or frailty [70, 71], thereby highlighting the importance of creatinine as a key predictor of depression.

To develop our primary predictive model, we leveraged a hybrid feature selection strategy integrating LASSO regression with multiple LR models. This approach was benchmarked against alternative models using RF, XGBoost, SVM, KNN, and LightGBM algorithms. Our integrated model-constructing framework improves model interpretability and maintains strong predictive capabilities, unlike many previous studies that relied solely on a single traditional statistical model. A comprehensive set of metrics, including AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score, was used to assess model efficacy. The assessment was enhanced using calibration plots and DCA. Our multifaceted evaluation framework thus strengthens the reliability of the findings relative to studies employing a more limited set of performance indicators.

Strengths and limitations

The depression risk prediction model from this study shows considerable promise for clinical application. Firstly, as previously discussed, the study ensures the model’s clinical interpretability, thereby facilitating its comprehension and application by clinicians, which contributes to the early screening of depression risk. Secondly, the study involves the construction and external validation of the model, ensuring the sample’s universality and representativeness. This enhances risk assessment for high-risk individuals with MetS. The model is expected to be used in primary and specialized healthcare settings to evaluate depression risk in individuals with MetS, aiding in the creation of tailored intervention and treatment plans. The nomogram, being intuitive and user-friendly, can be seamlessly incorporated into electronic health record systems for automated risk assessment.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, our model was developed retrospectively using data form a single wave of CHARLS and the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes the causality between predictors and depression onset. Secondly, the assessment of depressive symptoms using the CHARLS self-reported data could introduce reporting bias. Thirdly, due to the constraints of the database, crucial diagnostic data, such as metabolic, proteomic, and medical imaging data, were unavailable, which could impact the model’s accuracy [72–74]. Fourthly, as the data was obtained from CHARLS, primarily representing the Chinese population. While temporal validation supports model stability, geographic and ethnic external validation is necessary before broad clinical implementation. Finally, due to limitations in the CHARLS database, some important variables, such as social support, was not included. Hence, future investigations should aim to substantiate models constructed in this study in diverse demographic cohorts and explore adding more variables to increase predictive accuracy.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated two depression risk prediction models for middle-aged and elderly adults in China. The validation performance and interpretability of the LR and XGBoost models suggest their potential as effective tools for early detection and intervention. Future research should investigate the models’ effectiveness across various prospective clinical cohorts.
