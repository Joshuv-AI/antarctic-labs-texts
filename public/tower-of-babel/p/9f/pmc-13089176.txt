Nanozymes for ferroptosis-based cancer theranostics
Authors: Rizwan Ullah Khan, Linghui Qian, Junxia Min, Hongshang Peng, Fudi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanozymes, engineered nanomaterials with enzyme-mimetic activities, have emerged as versatile platforms for ferroptosis-based cancer theranostics. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome resistance to conventional cancer therapies. By catalyzing redox reactions, nanozymes can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and promote ferroptotic lipid peroxidation, thereby triggering cell death in tumor cells that evade apoptosis-based treatments. In parallel, non-redox activities of nanozymes, including hydrolase- and phosphatase-like functions, enable them to remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME), modulate biomolecular signaling, and support targeted therapy. This review provides a systematic and design-oriented overview of nanozymes that interface with ferroptosis. We summarize how redox and non-redox nanozyme activities converge on key ferroptosis-related processes, such as ROS production, glutathione depletion, iron metabolism disruption, and TME regulation. We then highlight rational engineering strategies, including single-atom and multimetallic catalytic centers, biodegradable coordination frameworks, stimuli-responsive architectures, and protein corona engineering, that enhance catalytic specificity, tumor targeting, and biosafety. Theranostic implementations are discussed with emphasis on multimodal imaging-guided platforms and combination regimens that integrate chemotherapy, radiotherapy, phototherapy, and immunotherapy. Finally, we outline major translational challenges and future opportunities, including AI and computation-guided nanozyme design and adaptive, corona-informed systems tailored for personalized cancer therapy. This review aims to serve as a roadmap for developing clinically translatable nanozymes that unify diagnosis and treatment through ferroptosis-oriented precision oncology.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, with millions of new cases and deaths reported annually, underscoring the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies [1]. Conventional treatments, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, have improved survival rates but are often limited by drug resistance, metastasis, and off-target toxicity, highlighting the need for approaches that balance efficacy with precision [[2], [3], [4]].

In this context, ferroptosis has emerged as a pivotal therapeutic target. Ferroptosis is a regulated, iron-dependent form of cell death driven by lipid peroxidation that can bypass apoptosis resistance in aggressive tumors, providing a complementary mechanism to eliminate malignant cells [5,6]. Unlike apoptosis, which many cancers evade through genetic mutations and signaling adaptations, ferroptosis exploits the vulnerability of tumor cells to iron-mediated oxidative damage, a weakness that can be selectively targeted. Its tight coupling to iron metabolism and redox balance creates unique opportunities for selective intervention. Nanozymes, engineered nanomaterials with enzyme-mimetic properties, can precisely modulate these pathways, bridging catalytic activity with spatiotemporal control over ferroptosis induction [[7], [8], [9]].

Ferroptosis is therefore increasingly recognized as a key mechanism for redefining cancer therapy. By targeting lipid peroxidation and iron homeostasis, processes often hyperactive in tumors, it enables selective eradication of malignant cells while sparing healthy tissues, addressing a long-standing challenge in oncology. Nanozymes, with their tunable catalytic properties, are uniquely positioned to harness this potential. They can amplify ferroptosis in tumors through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, deplete antioxidant defenses, and directly regulate iron trafficking, making them versatile tools for engaging this pathway [6,10].

Nanozymes can also balance dual functional roles. Redox-active systems catalyze ROS generation via Fenton and Fenton-like reactions, amplifying lipid peroxidation to trigger ferroptosis. Non-redox counterparts regulate iron homeostasis (e.g., promoting ferritin degradation or inhibiting ferroportin) or activate prodrugs through hydrolytic or bioorthogonal reactions, thereby expanding their therapeutic reach beyond ROS production [11,12]. This versatility allows nanozymes to remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME), deplete antioxidants such as glutathione, and enhance immune infiltration, while simultaneously integrating diagnostic capabilities (e.g., MRI, PET, PAI) for real-time monitoring of treatment response, as depicted in Fig. 1 [[13], [14], [15]]. Theranostics involves the combination of diagnostic imaging with targeted therapy to more precisely and safely treat disease; notably, cancer. Small molecules such as erastin (System Xc− inhibition) and RSL3 (GPX4 inhibition) are useful ferroptosis probes, but their in vivo use can be limited by systemic exposure, off-target toxicity, rapid clearance, and limited control over where ferroptosis is triggered. In contrast, nanozymes provide (i) catalytic amplification from endogenous substrates, (ii) improved pharmacokinetics and local tumor retention, (iii) tumor-conditional activation (pH/H2O2/GSH or external stimuli) for higher selectivity, and (iv) multifunctionality, including co-delivery compatibility and intrinsic/co-engineered imaging for response monitoring. Together, nanozymes offer a designable route to induce and track ferroptosis with better spatiotemporal control than single-mechanism small molecules [9,16,17].

Despite recent advances, existing reviews on nanozymes and ferroptosis have been imbalanced in their focus. Many primarily emphasize redox mechanisms over non-redox pathways, overlook the interplay between nanozyme design and ferroptosis regulation, and understate the role of the protein corona, a dynamic biomolecular layer that shapes in vivo performance, targeting, and safety [[18], [19], [20]]. This gap limits our understanding of how nanozymes can be systematically engineered to balance catalytic efficiency with biocompatibility, a key barrier to clinical translation.

Compared with previous reviews on nanozymes or ferroptosis that primarily emphasize redox catalysis or individual therapeutic modalities, this article provides three distinctive contributions. Firstly, it offers a balanced framework that integrates both redox and non-redox nanozyme activities, linking ROS generation, iron metabolism, prodrug activation, and TME remodeling within a unified ferroptosis-centered perspective. Secondly, it focuses on rational nanozyme engineering, including single-atom and multimetallic catalysts, polyphosphazene-based coordination scaffolds, and protein corona regulation, to clarify how atomic-level design and bio–nano interactions can be exploited to optimize efficacy and biosafety. Thirdly, it connects these mechanistic and design insights with theranostic implementation, highlighting multimodal imaging-guided platforms and outlining AI- and computation-guided strategies for next-generation, patient-tailored nanozyme systems. Together, these aspects position this review as a forward-looking roadmap rather than a descriptive summary of existing nanozyme applications.

Nanozymes and ferroptosis: emerging players in oncology

Key Definitions

Nanozyme: A nanomaterial that converts enzyme substrates to products with enzyme-like kinetics.

Chemodynamic therapy (CDT) vs ferroptosis (scope/overlap). CDT denotes in situ catalytic ROS production in tumors (typically Fenton/Fenton-like chemistry). Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent RCD defined by phospholipid lipid peroxidation (LPO) overwhelming repair (often the System Xc−–GSH–GPX4 axis). They overlap only when CDT-induced ROS is validated to cause ferroptosis, supported by (i) Fer-1/Lip-1 and/or DFO/DFP rescue, (ii) LPO readouts (C11-BODIPY, MDA/4-HNE), and (iii) core markers (GPX4/SLC7A11/ACSL4 ± iron-axis markers). Thus, CDT ≠ ferroptosis, and ferroptosis can also arise via non-CDT routes (e.g., GPX4/System Xc− inhibition).

Bioorthogonal reaction: A chemical transformation that proceeds in living systems without perturbing native biology, marked by high chemoselectivity, fast kinetics, and physiological compatibility, enabling live-cell imaging, targeted delivery/prodrug activation, and antibody–drug conjugation.

Theranostics: An integrated strategy (often a single construct) that combines diagnostic imaging (e.g., magnetic resonance, fluorescence) with therapy (e.g., chemotherapy, ferroptosis induction) for image-guided treatment and response monitoring.

Single-atom nanozyme (SAN): A nanozyme whose active centers are individual, atomically dispersed metal atoms anchored on a solid support, providing uniform, enzyme-like catalytic sites.

Photothermoelectric catalysis (PTEC): A catalytic process in which light-induced heating creates a temperature gradient in a thermoelectric material, generating a Seebeck potential that separates charge carriers and thereby drives redox reactions (e.g., ROS production).

Protein corona: The dynamic protein layer on nanoparticles that sets their biological identity (circulation, uptake, immune recognition). It has soft and hard layers, shaped by surface chemistry, and can be engineered (e.g., PEG, zwitterions, membrane coats) to tune behavior.

Regulated cell death (RCD): Mechanistic cell-death endpoints (e.g., ferroptosis, cuproptosis, apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, PANoptosis), not therapeutic modalities. In this review, RCD terms are used only when pathway-specific evidence is provided, whereas RT/SDT/PTT/PDT/CDT denote treatment modalities.

Redox enzyme-like activities of nanozymes (ferroptosis execution modules)

Redox-active nanozymes play a pivotal role in cancer therapy by modulating oxidative stress and inducing ferroptosis through ROS modulation (Fig. 3b). For example, peroxidase (POD)-mimicking nanozymes, such as Fe3O4 nanoparticles, catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals, initiating lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death [23,24]. Oxidase (OXD)-like nanozymes, including gold and MnO2-based systems, enhance ROS production by activating molecular oxygen, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to oxidative damage [17,25,26]. Catalase (CAT)-mimicking nanozymes, like CeO2, decompose excess H2O2 to reduce oxidative burden or relieve tumor hypoxia [27]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like nanozymes neutralize superoxide radicals, converting them into less harmful species while preserving therapeutic ROS levels [28]. Glucose oxidase (GOD), like Au@COF@MnO2, modified graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4: GCN), and noble metal nanozymes facilitate glucose oxidation, generating H2O2 in situ [28,29]. Glutathione peroxidase (GPx)-mimicking nanozymes deplete intracellular glutathione (GSH), weakening the antioxidant defenses of cancer cells and amplifying ferroptosis [30,31]. Non-redox enzyme-like activities of nanozymes

Recent advances in nanozyme engineering have enabled non-redox (electron-transfer–independent) enzyme-mimicking functions, expanding applications beyond ROS modulation (Fig. 3c). Hydrolase-like nanozymes, particularly MOFs with esterase-/phosphoesterase-like activity-catalyze bond cleavage to enable site-selective drug release or prodrug activation within tumors [10,32]. Phosphatase-like systems (e.g., nanoceria, CeO2-based nanoparticles) can also support dephosphorylation-driven prodrug activation and/or modulation of phosphorylation-dependent signaling [32]. In addition, nuclease-like constructs (e.g., AuNP–Zn(II) nanonucleases) catalyze phosphodiester hydrolysis for nucleic-acid processing in diagnostics or gene-regulatory applications [33]. Bioorthogonal nanocatalysts, including Cu- and Pd-based systems, enable in situ click/decaging reactions to activate masked therapeutics or imaging probes under physiological conditions [11,[34], [35], [36], [37]]. For example, Pd nanoparticles can deprotect caged compounds in living systems [35,36], and mobile magnetic nanocatalysts (FePd nanowires) have been used to activate prodrugs (e.g., Pro-5-FU) locally for bioorthogonal cancer therapy (Box 1) [37]. Overall, these non-redox mechanisms complement redox catalysis by improving tumor selectivity, spatiotemporal control, and therapeutic index (Fig. 3c).

Importantly, non-redox activities should not be generalized as autonomous ferroptosis “executors.” Ferroptosis commitment is defined by membrane lipid peroxidation and supported by ferroptosis-specific rescue/markers. In most ferroptosis-centered platforms, non-redox nanozymes function as auxiliary, threshold-lowering modules that: (i) enable localized prodrug activation (including agents that subsequently perturb System Xc−/GSH–GPX4 defenses), (ii) remodel the TME to raise the effective throughput of redox catalysis (e.g., pH-gated activation, hypoxia relief, ECM modulation), and/or (iii) rewire nutrient/antioxidant availability so that a lower ROS flux is sufficient to trigger runaway lipid peroxidation. Accordingly, throughout this review we classify a platform as “ferroptosis-inducing” only when ferroptosis-specific evidence is provided (see the minimum evidence set checklist in Fig. 2c). Several multifunctional nanozymes integrate both redox and non-redox activities, enabling synergistic therapeutic effects (Table 1). Nanozymes in TME modulation

Nanozymes play a transformative role in modulating the TME, a complex and dynamic system characterized by hypoxia, acidity, elevated antioxidant levels (e.g., GSH), and immune suppressive conditions that collectively support tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [38,39]. Nanozymes reprogram the TME through both redox and non-redox mechanisms, making them highly adaptable agents for precision cancer therapy (see Fig. 4). Their functions are often activated under TME-specific stimuli, such as acidic pH or elevated hydrogen peroxide, enabling selective therapeutic action [11,35,38]. From a ferroptosis standpoint, the TME acts as a “reaction microreactor” that gates catalytic flux: acidic pH accelerates metal-center redox cycling, high H2O2 supplies substrate for POD/CAT cascades, and high intracellular GSH creates a selective sink that can be exploited by GSH-consuming designs.Fig. 4Modulating of TME with nanozyme-based ferroptosis combination therapy. A key avenue of TME modulation is immune regulation [31]. Nanozyme platforms such as GL-EF@siPD-L1 lipid nanoparticles and Fe-SAzymes inhibit immune checkpoints and activate dendritic cells, while others, like gCM@MnAu nanozymes, stimulate the cGAS–STING pathway, enhance immunogenic cell death (ICD), and establish immune memory [30,40,41]. These systems help overcome tumor immune evasion by improving T-cell infiltration and immune responsiveness. Similarly, nanozymes disrupt tumor cellular energetics by generating ROS, targeting glycolytic pathways, impairing mitochondrial function, and depleting GSH, all of which sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis and other cell death pathways [30,42,43].

ROS modulation remains a cornerstone of redox-active nanozymes. For example, manganese oxide-based nanozymes, cerium oxide-doped particles, and carbon quantum dots serve as stimuli-responsive agents, quenching ROS in normal tissues but generating ROS in the reductive, acidic TME. This duality enhances cancer selectivity while reducing systemic toxicity [24,25,44,45]. To maintain objectivity, the pro-vs anti-oxidant behavior of CeO2-family materials should be interpreted as context-dependent (pH, H2O2, GSH level, and dominant surface redox cycle), and ferroptosis induction should be claimed only when lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis-rescue assays are demonstrated. In another example, Fe-based MOFs with catalase-like activity, co-loaded with GOx, decompose H2O2 into O2, thereby relieving tumor hypoxia and sustaining Fenton-type ferroptosis while simultaneously depleting glucose to couple photodynamic therapy (PDT) with starvation therapy [46].

Notably, nanozymes also exhibit non-redox catalytic activities that are critical for TME remodeling and therapeutic precision [11,47]. These include hydrolytic functions that cleave ester, amide, or phosphate bonds, facilitating controlled prodrug activation, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and stimuli-triggered drug release without generating oxidative stress. For instance, Zr-based MOFs have demonstrated phosphoesterase-like activity, while other systems catalyze bioorthogonal decaging or addition reactions, enabling spatially and temporally resolved drug activation or release via bond cleavage rather than redox disruption. [11,[34], [35], [36]]. These mechanisms are particularly valuable in redox-sensitive tissues and offer an expanded therapeutic toolkit beyond ROS generation.

In parallel, pH-responsive nanozymes in acidic tumor regions promotes drug release or activation through non-redox pathways, enhancing tumor specificity and minimizing off-target toxicity [48,49]. This tunable behavior allows nanozymes to act as logic-based nanodevices that respond only in pathological microenvironments. Nanozymes can also counteract hypoxia, another TME hallmark. Oxygen-generating nanozymes such as Fe-MOF-RP and OxgeMCC-r decompose endogenous H2O2 to alleviate hypoxia, enhancing therapies like PDT and ferroptosis, while concurrently providing imaging capabilities for treatment monitoring [50,51]. In this manner, these nanozymes can act as theranostic compounds.

On the biophysical front, nanozymes degrade ECM components to facilitate drug and immune cell penetration. Systems like PEGylated MCSP nanozymes and ZIF-90 nanomotors disrupt the ECM, improving treatment delivery and response [42,52]. Concurrently, nanozyme-mediated GSH depletion promotes oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis, while triggering immunogenic signaling [53,54].

Angiogenesis inhibition is another critical function. By generating ROS or disrupting vascular endothelial signaling, nanozymes impair blood vessel formation, starving tumors of nutrients and oxygen [51,55]. These effects are enhanced when nanozymes are combined with imaging modalities such as CT, PET, MRI, or fluorescence-based probes [56]. Radiolabeled or magnetic nanozymes offer real-time monitoring of biodistribution, ROS activity, and treatment efficacy [4,45,57].

Finally, combination therapies involving nanozymes with chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, sonodynamic therapy (SDT), photothermal therapy (PTT), or chemodynamic therapy (CDT) have shown robust synergistic effects, overcome resistance mechanisms and amplifying anticancer outcomes [30,40,43,58,59]. This detailed discussion shows that nanozymes provide a multifunctional and programmable platform for TME modulation by leveraging both redox and non-redox catalytic pathways, establishing them as a powerful class of agents for next-generation cancer therapy. Here we note again that “CDT” is a broader catalytic ROS concept, whereas “ferroptosis-based therapy” requires ferroptosis-specific evidence and mechanistic anchoring in lipid peroxidation and GPX4/System Xc− collapse.

Mainstream ferroptosis-relevant nanozyme material families: comparative analysis

Although many nanozyme platforms converge on ferroptosis endpoints (ROS/lipid peroxidation, GSH–GPX4 collapse, and iron dysregulation), their material class strongly determines (i) the dominant catalytic route (Fenton/Fenton-like vs non-Fenton vs non-redox activation), (ii) whether activity is mainly surface-catalysis or metal release–assisted, (iii) intrinsic imaging readouts (e.g., Fe/Mn for MRI, Au/Bi for CT, NIR absorbers for PAI/PTT), and (iv) translational attributes such as stability, biodegradability/clearance, and batch reproducibility.

Mechanistic induction and monitoring of ferroptosis

Unlike apoptosis or necroptosis, ferroptosis is driven by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation that exceeds cellular antioxidant repair, typically involving labile iron accumulation, ROS/LPO amplification, glutathione (GSH) depletion, and functional collapse of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [60,61]. Nanozymes, with tunable redox cycling, substrate accessibility, and stimulus responsiveness, provide a versatile platform to both induce and report ferroptosis with spatiotemporal control.

At the catalytic level, atomically engineered nanozymes have clarified how active-site structure governs ferroptotic outputs. For example, graphitic N-doped Fe nanozymes integrating single-atom Fe–N4 sites with Fe clusters show coupled oxidase/peroxidase/catalase-like activities that ignite ROS cascades, deplete GSH, and support photothermal imaging (PTI), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and photoacoustic imaging (PAI) readouts of ferroptotic progression [62]. Likewise, Fe3O4/CaP composites loaded with atovaquone amplify ferroptosis via dual Fe2+ supply (Fe3O4 degradation plus disruption of iron-rich mitochondria) and suppression of the dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) axis, triggering mitochondrial redox collapse (“ferroptosis cascade storm”) [63].

ROS initiation and lipid peroxidation. Many nanozyme-driven ferroptosis pathways begin with ROS overproduction. In Fenton/Fenton-like routes, transition-metal centers (e.g., Fe2+, Cu+, Mn2+) catalyze H2O2 conversion into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH), initiating lipid peroxidation chain reactions. Iron-based nanozymes (e.g., Fe3O4, Prussian blue) are frequently exploited because acidic pH and elevated H2O2 in the tumor microenvironment (TME) favor redox activation [13,24,64,65]. Complementarily, non-Fenton ROS pathways arise from oxidase-, peroxidase-, or glucose oxidase-like activity: MnO2, CeO2, and noble-metal platforms (e.g., Au-based) can promote oxygen activation or H2O2 formation, while metal-free nanozymes (e.g., N-doped carbon dots, graphitic carbon nitride) operate via photoactivation/electron transfer, offering options for iron-limited or redox-sensitive contexts [25,66,67].

CeO2-family nanozymes: redox “duality” and the context window for ferroptosis claims.

Ceria (CeO2) and related Ce-based oxides are widely reported as ROS-scavenging platforms because Ce3+/Ce4+ surface cycling can remove superoxide and hydrogen peroxide under near-neutral physiological conditions, yielding an overall anti-oxidant phenotype [68]. However, CeO2 behavior is not fixed; it depends on the microenvironment and the dominant surface redox cycle [68]. In tumor-like contexts, typically acidic pH, higher local H2O2, and high thiol flux (GSH turnover), ceria surfaces may favor GSH oxidation and altered redox cycling that can increase oxidative pressure and lower the threshold for lipid peroxidation [68]. Therefore, in this review we treat CeO2 platforms as context-dependent redox modulators, and we only classify a CeO2-based system as ferroptosis-inducing when ferroptosis-specific evidence is provided, including: (i) rescue by Fer-1/Lip-1 and/or iron chelators (DFO/DFP), (ii) direct lipid peroxidation assays (C11-BODIPY, MDA/4-HNE, lipid-ROS), and (iii) core markers (GPX4/SLC7A11/ACSL4; ± iron-axis markers), with TEM morphology as supportive. These approaches are especially advantageous in iron-limited or redox-sensitive settings.

Antioxidant collapse: GSH–GPX4 axis. Nanozymes also sensitize ferroptosis by weakening the antioxidant repair network. Catalytic oxidation of GSH reduces the capacity to detoxify lipid hydroperoxides, indirectly disabling GPX4 function; in parallel, some systems suppress cystine uptake through system Xc−, further limiting GSH synthesis [69,70]. In addition, bioorthogonal nanozymes (Pd- or Cu-based catalysts) can enable site-specific activation of pathway inhibitors (including GPX4-directed strategies) within the tumor microenvironment, improving selectivity while minimizing systemic exposure [11,37].

Iron metabolism disruption and redox amplification. Control over iron trafficking is a central lever. Fe-based nanozymes, especially MOF-embedded systems such as MIL-101(Fe), can release Fe2+ under acidic TME conditions, expanding the labile iron pool to fuel Fenton chemistry and amplify •OH generation [54]. Multimetallic architectures (e.g., Cu–Co–Fe atomically dispersed catalysts) can further intensify ROS flux through synergistic redox cycling and improved charge transfer, thereby strengthening ferroptotic commitment [54].

Downstream ferroptosis wiring. Beyond ROS and GSH depletion, nanozymes can reshape lipid and iron metabolic circuitry. Upregulation of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) increases PUFA incorporation into phospholipids, raising the membrane's susceptibility to peroxidation [5,71]. Nanozyme-triggered ferritinophagy (ferritin degradation) releases additional Fe2+, while iron homeostasis shifts (e.g., altered IRP2 signaling or increased transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1)) further promote ferroptotic sensitivity [72].

Theranostic monitoring of ferroptosis. Nanozymes are well positioned for real-time readouts because many contain intrinsically imageable elements or can be paired with responsive probes. Iron- and manganese-based nanozymes provide MRI contrast (often T2/T2∗ for iron) for non-invasive tracking of tumor accumulation and catalytic activation [56,57]. Ratiometric PAI platforms (e.g., signal ratio changes reflecting ROS/LPO dynamics) allow spatiotemporal mapping of oxidative stress for optimized intervention timing [31,73]. Fluorescent LPO indicators such as BODIPY 581/591 C11 enable high-resolution reporting of membrane oxidation, capturing terminal ferroptotic events [74]. Dual-modal constructs (e.g., Fe–Au hybrids) can unify MRI/CT imaging with catalytic/photothermal functions, enabling synchronized therapy and imaging feedback to support adaptive dosing [49,64,65].

Collectively, Section 2 defines the actionable ferroptosis checkpoints that nanozymes can modulate, (i) iron availability and redox cycling, (ii) lipid peroxidation susceptibility (PUFA–phospholipids), and (iii) antioxidant repair capacity (System Xc−/GSH/GPX4 and bypass routes such as FSP1 and DHODH). In Section 3, we discuss nanozyme material engineering and protein corona effects only insofar as they tune these checkpoints and their theranostic readouts (e.g., ROS/lipid-ROS reporting, Fe/Mn-enabled MRI, high-Z CT contrast, PA/FL signals, and radionuclide PET/SPECT), thereby directly linking design choices to ferroptosis induction and monitoring.

Design strategies and protein corona in nanozyme-based cancer theranostics

Nanozyme engineering has advanced beyond simple mimicry of natural enzymes toward rationally designed, functionally optimized platforms tailored for cancer therapy. Their catalytic efficiency, responsiveness to tumor-specific stimuli, and ability to evade immune clearance are all dictated by deliberate structural innovations, functional modifications, and interactions with biological systems, such as the protein corona.

Structural and functional innovations in rational design

This subsection maps atomic-site and defect engineering to ferroptosis checkpoints (H2O2 activation/ROS flux, GSH/GPX4 disruption, iron release) and the imaging signals used to verify on-target activation. The therapeutic efficacy of nanozymes hinges on the rational design of their atomic configuration, redox properties, surface chemistry, and morphology. Recent advances in single-atom nanozymes (SANs), MOFs, and nanozymes based on Fe, Au, Ce, and Pt have significantly improved catalytic activity and tumor specificity [31,66,75]. Active site engineering, such as incorporating Fe–N4 motifs, mimics natural peroxidase centers, enhancing H2O2 activation and selectivity [76]. Amorphous nanostructures, like Gd(OH)3 nanocages, provide flexible atomic arrangements, boosting multi-ROS generation [77]. Morphological tuning, including hollow architectures, nanosheets, and nanoclusters, increases surface area and substrate accessibility for deeper tumor penetration [16,30,42,75]. Surface charge modulation through cationic coatings, like polyethyleneimine (PEI) and polylysine, promotes cellular uptake via electrostatic interactions [41,65,78]. Additionally, targeted ligand conjugation with folic acid, peptides, or antibodies enhances receptor-mediated internalization and tumor-selective delivery, enabling efficient catalytic activity in complex TMEs [54,79].

Among these innovations, polyorganophosphazene (POP) has emerged as a programmable coordination layer to stabilize single and multiatomic nanozymes, such as Cu–Co–Fe single-atom catalysts within MIL-101(Fe), forming trimetallic atom catalysts (TACs) with atomic precision [54,80]. Integration of N, S, and P dopants from POP (termed “PZS” in the original study) fine-tunes the local electronic environment, inhibits metal aggregation, and drives the formation of defect-rich hollow nanostructures, thereby enhancing POD-, CAT-, and GPx-like activities and boosting ROS generation and tumor-specific activation [54]. Defect engineering at the cation/vacancy level further optimizes charge transfer, redox cycling, and ferroptosis efficiency [54,81,82]. Self-doped Cu2+xZn1-xSnSe4 nanosheets (n-CZTSe@PEG NSs) with Cu/Zn antisite defects act as thermoelectric nanozymes that, under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, use their defect-rich structure to enhance charge transport, peroxidase-like activity, and ROS-driven ferroptotic therapy [82]. Likewise, sulfur-vacancy-rich Cu2MnS3-x–PEG/GOx (MCPG) nanosheets exploit SV-mediated redox disruption and photothermoelectric catalysis (PTEC) to couple photothermal and thermoelectric effects, promoting Cu+/Cu2+ cycling, ROS amplification, and coordinated cuproptosis–ferroptosis–apoptosis under NIR-II irradiation [81].

Stimuli-responsive properties and TME adaptability

Stimuli-responsive nanozymes enable precise, tumor-specific activation, improving therapeutic selectivity while minimizing off-target toxicity [38] [83]. Designed to respond to acidic pH, elevated H2O2, or high GSH levels, these systems facilitate spatiotemporally-controlled drug release and catalytic activity. For instance, pH- and ROS-responsive nanozymes are activated in acidic or oxidative TMEs, while GSH-sensitive linkers trigger degradation or drug liberation under reductive conditions [84,85]. For instance, Cu3BiS3 sonopiezoelectric nanospheres combine ultrasound-induced Ca2+ influx with NIR-mediated collagen denaturation to remodel the ECM, thereby facilitating nanoparticle penetration and enhancing ROS-driven cell death [86]. Similarly, Fe3+-doped dendritic mesoporous organosilica (Fe-DMOS@CaO2–HA) self-generates H2O2 and releases Ca2+ in situ, triggering Fenton reactions and inducing ferroptosis-mediated immunogenic cell death [87]. Moreover, non-redox nanozymes, such as hydrolase-mimicking or bioorthogonal catalysts, function effectively in ROS-sensitive settings, expanding their clinical versatility [[35], [36], [37]]. Additionally, surface hydrophilicity, colloidal stability, and charge are engineered to enhance blood compatibility and reduce nonspecific protein adsorption, as elaborated below.

Strategies to enhance stability and biocompatibility

Enhancing physiological stability and biocompatibility is essential for the systemic performance of nanozymes. Surface modifications, such as PEGylation and zwitterionic coatings, prolong circulation and reduce immune clearance [37,79]. Biomimetic strategies, including red blood cell membrane coating, offer stealth properties to evade macrophages [26,88]. Additionally, controlling nanoparticle size and morphology optimizes renal or hepatic clearance while minimizing reticuloendothelial system uptake [38,89]. Mechanisms of tumor targeting and accumulation

To enhance tumor accumulation beyond passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR), nanozymes are commonly engineered with active targeting, biomimetic cloaking, TME-responsive retention, and (where applicable) physical guidance strategies. First, ligand-mediated targeting (e.g., folic acid, transferrin/TFRC ligands, aptamers, antibodies, and peptides such as RGD) promotes receptor-mediated endocytosis in tumor cells and tumor endothelium, improving cellular uptake and intracellular delivery compared with non-targeted controls [78,79,90]. Second, biomimetic nanozymes, including red blood cell (RBC) membrane, platelet, leukocyte, or tumor cell membrane camouflage, as well as exosome-/nanovesicle-inspired coatings, can reduce opsonization and macrophage clearance, prolong circulation, and enable homologous targeting to tumor tissue; these approaches also intersect with protein-corona engineering by biasing adsorption toward “stealth/targeting” coronas rather than complement-rich coronas [12,18,26,88,91]. Third, TME-responsive accumulation/retention strategies exploit tumor acidity, high GSH/ROS, or protease activity to trigger charge reversal, ligand exposure, size switching, or in situ aggregation, thereby increasing intratumoral retention and reducing off-target distribution (e.g., “mask–unmask” designs that shield ligands/catalytic sites in blood but expose them after tumor entry) [87,92]. Fourth, matrix and transport barriers are addressed by engineering nanozymes that degrade or remodel the extracellular matrix (ECM) and stroma, improving penetration and intratumoral distribution; for example, catalytic or enzyme-assisted ECM modulation can facilitate access to hypoxic or stromal-protected niches where ferroptosis sensitivity is often heterogeneous [42,52]. Finally, physical targeting can provide additional spatial control in selected systems: magnetic guidance can enhance local deposition of iron oxide–containing nanozymes, while externally triggered activation (e.g., NIR/ultrasound) can confine catalytic “on-state” to the tumor region, improving therapeutic index when systemic exposure is unavoidable [90,93,94]. Collectively, these strategies tune circulation, deposition, penetration, and cellular/subcellular localization, thereby increasing effective tumor exposure and strengthening ferroptosis-selective nanozyme activity while mitigating RES sequestration.

Overcoming delivery barriers and enhancing cellular uptake

Nanozymes encounter several delivery challenges, including limited tumor accumulation, poor tissue penetration, and inefficient intracellular trafficking. To address these, subcellular targeting moieties, such as mitochondrial or lysosomal tags, direct catalytic activity to precise organelles [90,93]. Enzyme-responsive platforms enable drug release upon encountering TME-specific stimuli like GSH or H2O2 [84]. Multifunctional nanozymes co-loaded with chemotherapeutics, immune agents, or metal ions enhance synergistic efficacy [59,95]. Moreover, magnetic (F3O4) or gold-based constructs provide theranostic potential by integrating catalytic therapy with imaging modalities such as MRI or CT [15,62,93]. Protein corona formation: from barrier to functional tool

Here we focus on how corona composition and dynamics influence ferroptosis efficacy by modulating tumor delivery, intracellular access to catalytic sites, and the balance between targeting versus catalytic availability. Once nanozymes enter biological fluids, a protein corona rapidly forms on their surface, redefining bio-identity and influencing circulation time, biodistribution, cellular uptake, and catalytic efficiency [18,38,96]. Hydrophilic surfaces and PEGylation typically yield thinner, stealth-like coronas, whereas hydrophobic or highly charged surfaces recruit denser protein layers that can hinder targeting or activity [18]. Rather than avoiding the corona entirely, recent strategies leverage it functionally; for example, adsorption of specific proteins such as clusterin can contribute to “stealth” behavior and reduce non-specific uptake [18,91]. Corona composition can also modulate activity; notably, albumin coronas can preserve or even enhance peroxidase-like performance compared with fibrinogen, consistent with reports that protein identity/shape affects nanozyme catalysis (Fig. 6b) [97].

Corona–activity trade-off (active-site accessibility). A key trade-off is that coronas may shield atomic active centers, particularly in single-atom nanozymes (SANs) and multimetallic motifs where active sites are small and surface-exposed, thereby reducing substrate diffusion, lowering ROS productivity, and weakening ferroptosis induction. Balancing corona-mediated targeting with catalytic accessibility can be achieved by: (i) sparse PEG/zwitterionic brushes that limit nonspecific adsorption while retaining permeability (ii) pre-adsorbed “functional hard coronas” (e.g., albumin/apolipoproteins); that provide stealth/targeting yet remain permeable to small substrates; (iii) sheddable coronas that detach under TME cues (pH/GSH/enzymes) to re-expose active sites; (iv) active-site-exposed architectures (mesoporous shells, nanoframes, hollow/defect channels) that preserve access even when coated; and (v) spatial separation of targeting and catalysis (targeting ligands on an outer layer while catalytic sites reside in accessible pores) [18,38,96,97]. Practically, corona effects can be quantified by comparing enzyme-like kinetics (Vmax/Km) before and after serum incubation and correlating corona composition with catalytic retention.

Recent studies have revealed that systemic metabolic states, such as hypercholesterolemia, can reshape protein corona composition by enriching apolipoproteins and depleting complement proteins, ultimately altering immune responses and biodistribution patterns of nanoparticles, offering a path toward personalized nanomedicine, and precision oncology for improved targeting and therapeutic outcomes [98].

Future directions in nanozyme design and protein corona engineering

Advancing nanozyme design requires the integration of structural innovation with a deeper understanding of biological interactions, particularly the dynamics of protein corona formation. Instead of treating the corona as a passive byproduct, it should be regarded as a modifiable, strategic interface that can be pre-engineered to function as a tunable biological shell, governing circulation half-life, targeting specificity, immune evasion, and catalytic activity. Emerging computational tools, including machine learning and multiscale simulations, are proving essential in predicting corona composition and designing nanozyme surfaces for optimal biological responses [18,31,96,97,99].

Importantly, analyzing protein corona profiles in patient-derived plasma opens up new possibilities for personalized nanozyme fabrication, where nanozymes are tailored to the metabolic and immunological context of individual patients. This could enable dynamic alignment of catalytic function with tumor-specific microenvironment conditions, improving selectivity, minimizing toxicity, and enhancing clinical outcomes. From a precision oncology perspective, such data-driven, corona-informed designs could stratify patients based on metabolic status or disease stage, ensuring that nanozymes reach their intended targets with higher fidelity [98]. Collectively, these insights underscore a paradigm shift toward corona-engineered nanozymes that are not only biologically informed but also adaptable to individual therapeutic needs.

Theranostics applications of nanozymes

This section will highlight the multifaceted applications of nanozymes in cancer therapy, spanning from synergistic combination strategies to imaging-guided platforms, specialized delivery systems, and the induction of diverse cell death pathways.

Rationale for combining nanozymes with therapeutic modalities

Nanozymes serve as robust catalytic agents capable of enhancing the efficacy of conventional therapies such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, PDT, photothermal therapy (PTT), and immunotherapy, as shown in Fig. 7a. Their redox and non-redox activities amplify therapeutic outcomes through precise modulation of the TME [4,100]. For example, Fe-based nanozymes catalyze Fenton reactions that generate hydroxyl radicals, enhancing chemotherapeutic sensitivity [49,64,65]. High-Z (high atomic number) element–containing nanozymes (e.g., Au, Pt, Bi) potentiate radiotherapy by promoting radiolytic ROS generation, leading to ferroptosis even in resistant tumors [6,48,75]. In immunotherapy, nanozymes such as MnO2-based platforms relieve tumor hypoxia and stimulate ICD, enhancing checkpoint blockade efficacy [30,42]. Moreover, nanozymes designed to induce lipid peroxidation and inhibit antioxidant pathways, like GPX4, synergize with other treatments to overcome resistance and drive robust tumor eradication. For example, self-doped semiconductor (n-CZTSe@PEG NSs) nanozymes enable photothermal-accelerated ferroptosis and relieve tumor hypoxia, establishing a model for energy-assisted ROS therapy [82]. Likewise, Ce6@HGMOF nanozyme systems that couple oxygen generation with metabolic starvation unify energy disruption and oxidative stress for enhanced tumor ablation [46]. Functional integration: ROS modulation, prodrug activation, and immune remodeling

Beyond their catalytic core, nanozymes exhibit programmable functional integration for precise TME modulation (as discussed in detail in the subsection “2.3 Nanozymes in TME modulation” above). One of the most critical functions of nanozymes is the controlled generation of ROS through a spectrum of redox catalytic mechanisms, including OXD-, GOx-, and POD-like activities. These encompass classical Fenton and Fenton-like reactions catalyzed by transition metals (e.g., Fe2+, Cu+), as well as non-Fenton electron transfer pathways observed in metal-free systems such as nitrogen-doped carbon dots or graphitic carbon nitride [26,49,64,101]. Collectively, these diverse mechanisms enable nanozymes to precisely modulate oxidative stress within the TME, forming the biochemical basis for ferroptosis initiation and other redox-dependent therapeutic effects. Simultaneously, nanozymes can activate prodrugs through hydrolytic or bioorthogonal reactions, providing a non-redox mechanism for site-specific drug release. Palladium-, copper-, and peptide–zinc-based nanozymes exemplify this non-redox functionality by enabling bioorthogonal cleavage of protective groups on prodrugs, thereby improving treatment selectivity [[35], [36], [37]]. In parallel, nanozymes modulate immune responses by remodeling the ECM, relieving hypoxia, enhancing immune infiltration, and promoting ICD through ROS-mediated or ferroptotic pathways. These integrated properties make nanozymes ideal candidates for multitargeted interventions with reduced systemic toxicity.

Responsive nanozyme-based imaging modalities

One of the most valuable theranostic attributes of nanozymes is their ability to facilitate real-time imaging of treatment responses. Responsive nanozymes have been engineered to enhance various imaging modalities, including MRI, computed tomography (CT), PAI, and fluorescence imaging, as shown in Fig. 7b. For instance, manganese- and iron-based nanozymes serve as MRI contrast agents that respond to TME conditions, such as pH or GSH levels [44,45,56]. These systems enable visualization of ROS generation, drug release, and tumor remodeling in situ. Gold- and cerium-based nanozymes can also provide CT or PAI signal enhancement, offering multimodal imaging functionality [73,102]. Such capabilities allow dynamic monitoring of therapeutic progression and nanozyme biodistribution, improving clinical precision and guiding personalized treatment protocols.

Multi-modal diagnostic–therapeutic platforms

To further augment clinical utility, nanozymes are increasingly incorporated into multi-modal platforms that combine therapeutic and diagnostic elements. These systems integrate catalytic functionalities with imaging agents and targeting ligands, enabling simultaneous tumor detection, treatment, and monitoring [57,103]. For example, a multi-responsive cascade catalytic nanoassembly (Au@COF@MnO2) exhibits multi-responsive release, offering triple enzymatic functions to amplify ROS and hence, ferroptosis therapy with real-time trimodal (PT, PA, and MR) imaging in vivo for tumor treatment visualization, synchronizing therapy with diagnostic feedback [25]. In another example, a vacancy-engineered MCPG nanozyme serves as a PA/MR/thermal triple-modality imaging probe under NIR irradiation, enabling imaging-guided energy–defect therapy [81]. By bridging therapy and diagnostics into a single platform, nanozyme-based systems overcome temporal and spatial limitations, facilitating early response evaluation, minimizing over-treatment, and enabling dose modulation tailored to patient-specific responses.

Heterogeneity-aware ferroptosis targeting: biomarkers → nanozyme design

Tumor heterogeneity creates subpopulation-dependent ferroptosis sensitivity, driven by differences in iron handling, lipid remodeling, and antioxidant defenses. Ferroptosis-prone subclones are often enriched for ACSL4/high-PUFA phospholipids and TFRC (transferrin receptor)/labile iron signaling, whereas resistant subpopulations may upregulate defense axes such as SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4, FSP1–CoQ10, or mitochondrial DHODH, or reprogram membranes toward ferroptosis-resistant lipid compositions. Accordingly, nanozyme design should be matched to the dominant resistance node within the tumor (Table 4): (i) boosting iron/ROS flux when iron availability is limiting, (ii) collapsing antioxidant defenses (GSH/GPX4 or FSP1/DHODH) in resistant cells, and (iii) improving spatial delivery to hypoxic, stromal-protected niches via ECM remodeling, biomimetic camouflage, or triggerable activation. Practically, heterogeneity-aware strategies combine biomarker-guided targeting ligands (e.g., integrin, folate receptor, TFRC-related ligands where applicable), tumor-matching biomimetic coatings (cell membrane/exosomes), and local activation triggers (pH/H2O2/GSH, NIR/US) to concentrate catalytic activity in therapy-relevant tumor subpopulations while limiting off-target oxidative injury [12,26,71,88].

Nanozymes to overcome ferroptosis resistance

Ferroptosis resistance frequently emerges via upregulation of lipid peroxide repair (GPX4/SLC7A11), reinforcement of CoQ-dependent buffering (FSP1, DHODH), iron sequestration/export (FTH1, ferroportin), and lipid remodeling that reduces PUFA-PL vulnerability [21,59]. Under these conditions, nanozymes offer three resistance-breaking advantages: (i) orthogonal oxidative pressure, catalytic amplification of lipid-ROS that can exceed GPX4 repair capacity; (ii) multinode collapse, simultaneous GSH depletion plus iron pool expansion (e.g., ferritinophagy activation) [22,54,72] to block compensatory rewiring; and (iii) microenvironmental leverage, TME-acidic activation, H2O2-dependent catalysis, or hypoxia-relief that selectively raises catalytic flux in tumors. Rational designs that co-deliver GPX4/SLC7A11 inhibitors or attenuate FSP1/DHODH defenses, while enriching PUFA substrates (or promoting ACSL4-driven PUFA-PL formation), may be particularly effective in resistant subtypes. For example, Fe3+-doped hexagonal manganite YMnO3:Fe provides a representative mechanics-triggered (US) piezocatalytic ferroptosis platform [105]. Polarization engineering and chemical-bond reconstruction enhance carrier separation and ROS output under ultrasound, resulting in apoptosis plus ferroptosis, with explicit canonical ferroptosis readouts (GPX4 downregulation and ACSL4 upregulation) and in vivo tumor inhibition, alongside MRI-enabled monitoring [105].

Delivery and spatiotemporal control of ferroptosis execution

Delivery carriers are treated here as a control layer that determines where/when nanozyme catalysis is activated and therefore whether ferroptosis remains tumor-selective. The therapeutic efficacy of nanozyme-based systems relies heavily on the choice of delivery carriers that enhance circulation, biodistribution, and tumor-specific accumulation. Common nanocarriers, including liposomes, micelles, dendrimers, polymeric nanoparticles, and mesoporous silica, are widely used to encapsulate or support nanozymes, ensuring catalytic protection and stimuli-responsive release (Fig. 8b). These platforms often incorporate surface modifications, such as PEGylation, tumor-targeting ligands (e.g., folic acid, Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD)) and biomimetic coatings, to promote active targeting and immune evasion [79,85,90]. Hybrid carriers employing TME-sensitive polymers further enhance precision and minimize off-target effects [86]. Detailed strategies regarding structural tuning, surface engineering, and functionalization for delivery optimization have been extensively discussed above in the section entitled “Design strategies and protein corona in nanozyme-based cancer theranostics”.

RCD crosstalk as mechanistic outcomes of ferroptosis-centered nanozyme therapy

Beyond modality choice (Section 4.1) and delivery control (Section 4.7), nanozyme-driven lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial stress can co-engage other RCD pathways as downstream mechanistic outcomes that influence efficacy and immunogenicity (Fig. 8c). Nanozymes induce a spectrum of regulated cell death (RCD) mechanisms beyond classical apoptosis, contributing to their therapeutic versatility (Fig. 8c). Ferroptosis, characterized by lipid peroxidation and iron dependency, is a central mode of action for many Fe-based nanozymes. Additionally, nanozymes can trigger pyroptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy-dependent death, either directly through ROS or indirectly via immune signaling pathways [19,40]. Some multifunctional nanozymes are engineered to switch between or simultaneously induce different modes of RCD depending on environmental cues, enhancing cytotoxicity and overcoming resistance. For instance, Pd@CeO2-Fe-Co-WZB117-DSPE-PEG-FA (PCDWD) integrates Fe-Co dual atom nanozymes with photothermal and metabolic modulators to simultaneously trigger ferroptosis and apoptosis under TME conditions [102,106]. A self-assembling photoactive nanoplatform synchronously initiates ferroptosis and pyroptosis via ROS generation and caspase-1/GSDMD activation, thereby strengthening antitumor immunity [107]. Similarly, a defect-engineered Cu2-xS nanozyme system induces cuproptosis and apoptosis, highlighting multi-pathway orchestration through defect engineering [81].

Challenges and future perspectives

Despite substantial progress, the clinical translation of nanozyme-based cancer theranostics remains hindered by several key barriers. Addressing these limitations is essential to unlocking the full potential of nanozymes in oncology. Encouragingly, recent innovations offer emerging opportunities to overcome these challenges, which are discussed in detail in the following subsections.

Translational barriers and biosafety considerations

Key limitations still constrain the clinical applicability of nanozyme-based cancer theranostics. First, many nanozymes exhibit broad substrate reactivity relative to natural enzymes, raising risks of off-target redox reactions and systemic toxicity [26,108,109]. Second, protein corona formation can unpredictably alter active-site accessibility, biodistribution, immune recognition, and targeting, complicating reproducibility across patients [12,18,26,88,91]. Third, the long-term fate of inorganic components (metal ions/clusters) and potential accumulation in reticuloendothelial system (RES) organs remain major biosafety concerns. Finally, translation requires alignment with clinical-grade manufacturing, including batch reproducibility, sterilization, purity specifications, and stability standards [108,109]. Translational landscape and clinical precedent

Clinical penetration of “nanozyme-as-ferroptosis-therapy” remains limited; however, there is substantial clinical precedent for component materials and adjacent use-cases (e.g., iron oxide–based agents, Prussian blue, clinically used photosensitizers, and nanomaterial radiosensitizers) [3,109]. This precedent supports feasibility discussions regarding biodistribution, safety monitoring, and manufacturing pathways without over-claiming approved ferroptosis nanozyme therapies [3,20,109]. Importantly, most nanozyme studies still report outcomes at the preclinical level (tumor inhibition, oxidative stress modulation, and short-term safety in rodents), underscoring the need for standardized PK/PD linking, long-term toxicology, and patient-stratified designs [3,108,109]. Clinical trial design gaps and optimization directions

Translation of ferroptosis-oriented nanozymes is frequently limited by four correctable trial-design issues. (i) Tumor heterogeneity: ferroptosis sensitivity varies across subclones and niches (hypoxic cores, stroma-protected regions) [2,69,111]. Trials should stratify patients using ferroptosis-relevant biomarkers (e.g., GPX4/SLC7A11, ACSL4, TFRC) and compensatory axes (FSP1, DHODH) to enable interpretable responder analyses [2,54,69,100,111,112]. (ii) Personalization and corona variability: inter-patient plasma composition can reshape the corona and alter both biodistribution and catalytic accessibility; baseline (and ideally longitudinal) plasma sampling can link corona signatures to PK/PD and outcomes, while standardized surface chemistries (PEG/zwitterions/albumin pre-coats) reduce variance [12,18,26,88,91]. (iii) Dose optimization: dosing should be guided by activity exposure, not injected mass, link dose to tumor accumulation (imaging-confirmed), lipid-peroxidation PD markers, and RES organ monitoring [3,69,109]. (iv) Administration frequency: schedules should match accumulation–activation–clearance kinetics; imaging-guided timing (treat at peak tumor activation) can reduce overtreatment and improve reproducibility [3,15,45,109].

Biodegradable nanozymes: degradation mechanisms, metabolic fate, and safety rationale

A key translational solution is the development of bioresorbable or transformable nanozymes that minimize long-term retention while preserving catalytic efficacy [9,17,38,108,109]. Biodegradation can occur via (i) dissolution/ion release (acidic/lysosomal acceleration; chelator-assisted), (ii) framework disassembly (bioresorbable MOFs/coordination polymers), (iii) oxidative/enzymatic erosion of polymeric or carbonaceous scaffolds, or (iv) “transformable” conversion into excretable species (e.g., ultrasmall fragments) [9,17,38,108,109]. Importantly, safety depends on the post-degradation fate of metal species: ions may bind proteins, be sequestered in reticuloendothelial system (RES) organs, or be cleared via hepatobiliary or renal routes depending on speciation and size. Accordingly, biodegradable designs often incorporate controlled chelation, size minimization for renal clearance, or transient catalytic states that deactivate after tumor exposure [9,17,108,109]. Long-term safety assessment should quantify: (a) time-resolved metal speciation (ion vs lattice), (b) organ retention (liver/spleen/kidney), (c) catalytic persistence, and (d) late inflammation/fibrosis signals, alongside ferroptosis-specific PD biomarkers to confirm tumor-selective activity [9,17,38,109].

Long-term fate and clearance of inorganic nanozyme components

After systemic administration, inorganic nanozymes typically follow two limiting behaviors: (i) dissolution-dominant systems that release metal ions (often accelerated in lysosomes), and (ii) persistence-dominant systems where a stable lattice/metal core remains particulate over extended periods [9,17,38,108,109]. For Fe-based nanozymes (e.g., Fe3O4, Fe-MOFs), dissolution yields Fe2+/Fe3+ that enters endogenous handling pathways (transferrin binding, ferritin storage, heme/enzymes); therefore, cumulative dosing/frequency must avoid chronic iron loading and excessive RES retention [17,34,46,109]. Mn-containing nanozymes often release Mn2+ and are primarily cleared through hepatobiliary routes, motivating rapid-transforming formulations, low cumulative exposure, and long-term organ monitoring [17,34,46,109]. In contrast, Au/Pt components are frequently slow to dissolve and may exhibit RES sequestration (liver/spleen) with prolonged persistence, making accumulation a key translational concern [17,34,46,109]. Practical mitigations include ultrasmall (typically <∼5–6 nm) renal-clearable constructs, bioresorbable frameworks/coordination networks, transformable or chelation-assisted designs to reduce long-lived residues, and ICP-MS/OES mass-balance studies (organs + urine/feces) at extended time points to quantify true clearance kinetics and define safe dosing schedules [17,34,46,109].

From bench synthesis to GMP manufacturing: CMC requirements

Clinical translation requires nanozymes to meet CMC (chemistry, manufacturing, and controls) standards comparable to biologics and complex nanomedicines [3]. Key priorities include: batch-to-batch consistency of size/distribution, surface chemistry, and (for SAN/DAC/TAC) active-site density/coordination; sterility assurance compatible with nanozyme stability (filtration, aseptic processing, or validated irradiation where feasible); and purity specifications covering residual solvents/ligands, endotoxin, free metal ions, and aggregation [3,109]. Because catalytic function is central, release testing should include standardized activity assays under clinically relevant conditions (pH, H2O2, GSH) plus stability-indicating methods (storage, serum exposure, freeze–thaw) [108,109]. Finally, stability testing should be harmonized (real-time/accelerated) with predefined acceptance criteria for potency, dispersion, and metal leakage [3,109]. Off-target ferroptosis risk management in RES organs (liver/spleen)

Because systemically administered nanoparticles commonly accumulate in the reticuloendothelial system (RES), unintended ferroptosis in hepatocytes/Kupffer cells and splenic macrophages is a key translational concern for pro-oxidant nanozymes [3,12,38,109]. In practice, tumor selectivity is achieved by restricting the catalytic “on-state” and/or metal redox availability to tumor-like microenvironments. First, many platforms are engineered for tumor-activated catalysis, where acidic pH, elevated H2O2, and high thiol turnover increase Fenton/Fenton-like flux and/or GSH depletion, while activity is attenuated under neutral physiological conditions [99,100]. Second, stimuli-responsive shielding (pH/GSH/ROS/enzyme-cleavable shells) can keep active sites partially masked during circulation and in RES-rich organs, then expose catalytic centers after tumor entry, endocytosis, or subcellular trafficking [12,18,26,88,91]. Third, bioresorbable/transformable designs (degradable frameworks, polymer–metal coordination networks, ultrasmall fragments) reduce long-term RES residency and chronic lipid-peroxidation risk by favoring clearance over persistence [3,12,38,109]. Fourth, PK engineering (size control, reduced opsonization, biomimetic cloaks, PEG/zwitterions) can lower Kupffer sequestration and splenic capture [3,12,38,81,82,91,109]. Finally, imaging-guided dosing/scheduling (MRI/PAI/CT confirmation of tumor accumulation/activation) enables adaptive dose and frequency selection to maximize tumor exposure while limiting RES burden [3,12,38,109].

Emerging trends and future directions

Collectively, the emerging directions converge on a practical roadmap from computationally guided design and catalytic diversification to closed-loop theranostics, supported by standardized assays, patient stratification, and GMP/CMC-ready manufacturing [11,34,37,41,44,54,94,99,[113], [114], [115], [116]]. Conclusion

Nanozymes have emerged as a versatile class of catalytic nanomaterials for precision cancer theranostics, offering controllable redox and non-redox activities that can be tailored to the biochemical features of tumors. By catalyzing ROS generation, perturbing iron homeostasis, and remodeling the tumor microenvironment, nanozymes provide a powerful platform to initiate and amplify ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death that can overcome resistance to conventional, apoptosis-focused cancer therapies.

This review summarizes how diverse nanozyme activities converge on ferroptosis-related pathways, including lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion, iron metabolism, and TME regulation. It highlights key structural and functional design principles, such as single-atom and multimetallic catalytic centers, polyphosphazene-based coordination frameworks, defect engineering, and programmable protein corona regulation, that enhance catalytic efficiency, tumor targeting, and biosafety. It further outlines theranostic implementations in mono- and combination therapies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, phototherapy, immunotherapy, and chemodynamic or sonodynamic approaches, with particular emphasis on multimodal imaging-guided platforms (e.g., MRI, CT, PET, and PAI) that enable real-time visualization of ferroptotic progression and adaptive treatment strategies.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain for clinical translation. These include improving catalytic selectivity and substrate specificity, understanding and controlling protein corona evolution in vivo, ensuring long-term biosafety and biodegradability, and establishing standardized assays and manufacturing protocols for nanozyme-based formulations. Tumor heterogeneity and inter-patient variability further underscore the need for rational, adaptable design strategies. Integrating AI, quantum chemical calculations, and multiscale simulations with high-throughput experimental screening is expected to accelerate the development of nanozymes tailored to specific tumor contexts or even individual patients.

Overall, ferroptosis-oriented nanozymes represent a promising route toward precision oncology, capable of unifying diagnosis and therapy within a single platform by combining catalytic intervention with sophisticated imaging readouts. Continued interdisciplinary efforts spanning materials science, cancer biology, immunology, imaging, and computational modeling will be essential to translate these multifunctional catalytic systems from experimental proof-of-concept to safe and effective clinical interventions.
