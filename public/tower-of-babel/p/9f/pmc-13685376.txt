Emergency medicine physician–led care at the patient’s residence: a cohort study of a mobile emergency unit
Authors: Masoud Moradi, Lise Waagø Christiansen, Trygve Bjørnevik, Mette Rahbek Kristensen, Johanne Overgaard Wessels, Anders Brøns Møllekær, Line Emilie Lilholm Laugesen, Peter Biesenbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency departments (EDs) are often overcrowded. Older adults, who are frequently multimorbid and resource-intensive, represent a growing share of acute care demand. In Denmark, mobile emergency units (MEUs) provide emergency physician-led assessment and initial treatment in the patient’s residence to potentially reduce avoidable hospital transfers. However, data on real-world service delivery and medium-term outcomes after MEU contacts remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-centre cohort study of MEU contacts within the Esbjerg Hospital catchment area (Region of Southern Denmark) from 1 September 2023 to 31 December 2024. Using the hospital’s electronic health record, we extracted demographics, comorbidities, on-scene diagnostics and treatments, and patient disposition, and descriptively summarised immediate disposition at the index encounter, service delivery, and 180-day all-cause mortality without risk adjustment.ResultsThe cohort comprised 1,987 MEU contacts corresponding to 1,336 unique patients, with a median (interquartile range) age of 80.9 (71.1–87.9) years. Nearly one-third had ≥ 3 comorbidities. Point-of-care testing and ultrasound were used frequently (60.5% and 24.8%, respectively), and on-scene therapies were commonly administered (antibiotics 31.6%, intravenous fluids 18.0%, and analgesia 9.0%). 82.7% of contacts were managed at home without hospital conveyance at the index encounter, while the remaining contacts were directly admitted to hospital from the scene. 443 (33.2%) of the 1,336 unique patients died within 180 days.ConclusionsMEU home visits involved bedside diagnostics and on-scene treatments, and most contacts were managed at home without conveyance at the index encounter. Mortality over 180 days was substantial and should be interpreted cautiously in this older, multimorbid cohort. Background

Emergency departments (EDs) face rising demand and crowding. This situation presents challenges to the timely triage and delivery of healthcare, as healthcare staff and physical capacity are stretched, and there is an increased risk of treatment delays and adverse outcomes [1–5]. The population of older adults living with multimorbidity is a major contributor to this care burden. As the global population ages and the management of chronic diseases improves, the number of patients with these conditions is expected to rise. Moreover, these patients often present with features that lengthen the assessment time and increase resource use [6–9]. Within this context, emergency medicine (EM) has increasingly prioritised alternative pathways for acute evaluation, particularly those delivered outside the hospital setting [10]. In Denmark, mobile emergency units (MEUs) enable emergency medicine physicians to provide home-based assessments, initial treatment, and disposition decisions at the patient’s residence. This approach aims to reduce avoidable ED transfers and hospital admissions, to shorten the time to clinician assessment, and to support continuity of care [11–13]. Importantly, beyond potential system-level benefits, MEU home visits may also be valuable from a patient perspective by avoiding burdensome transfers and supporting comfort and continuity of care. In our setting, we recently reported factors associated with 30-day hospital admission among older adults initially managed at home after an MEU assessment [14]. In Denmark, acute care is primarily organised around general practice, with out-of-hours services provided through regional GP on-call systems, while potentially life-threatening conditions are handled through the 1–1-2 emergency system. In the Region of Southern Denmark, MEUs have been implemented as ED-operated outreach services (Esbjerg and Odense) for clinically stable patients in whom home-based emergency assessment and initial treatment are deemed appropriate. The MEU functions as an integrated part of the hospital emergency department and the regional acute-care pathway.

Although the use of home-based emergency visits is increasing, there is limited data on medium-term outcomes. Moreover, most reports are based on selected populations and provide limited detail on patient characteristics, on-scene diagnostics and treatments, and subsequent patient outcomes. In a Cochrane review on admission-avoidance interventions, Edgar et al. [15] synthesised data from randomised controlled trials conducted in selected populations (e.g., those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, those recovering from a stroke, and older adults with acute medical conditions). In their meta-analysis, Arsenault-Lapierre [10] also focused on a specific population, namely adults with chronic disease who receive hospital at home service. In Denmark, Udesen et al. [11] described a mobile emergency service for nursing-home residents – a service highly similar to MEUs – but they only considered institutionalised older adults and 90-day mortality.

Therefore, we aimed to provide a descriptive, real-world account of patient characteristics, on-scene diagnostics and treatments, immediate disposition (non-conveyance vs direct hospital admission), and medium-term survival following MEU contacts during 2023–2024.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective, single-centre cohort study including all consecutive contacts handled by the MEU from 1 September 2023 to 31 December 2024 within the catchment area of Esbjerg Hospital in the Region of Southern Denmark. The service covers approximately 3,400 km2 and a resident population of about 240,000 people. For context, the Esbjerg Hospital emergency department recorded 52,218 patient attendances across 2023–2024 (all dispositions), based on local administrative data. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

The MEU was implemented in September 2023 as part of the regional prehospital system and is operated by the hospital’s ED. It is staffed daily (7 days per week) from 08:00 to 18:00 by an emergency department nurse and a senior physician from the Department of Emergency Medicine. In our setting, MEU shifts are most often covered by board-certified emergency medicine consultants, while the remaining shifts are covered by senior physicians (final-year EM residents or physicians from other specialties undergoing merit-based transition to emergency medicine), all with several years of acute-care experience. The nurse is an emergency department nurse (rotating function) with routine experience in acute assessment and treatment.

The MEU can be activated either by the Emergency Medical Dispatch Centre (EMDC) following 1–1-2 calls assessed as suitable for MEU response, or via the ED on-call physician/admission coordinator following referral from general practitioners or municipal/nursing-home staff. In brief, MEU activation is intended for clinically stable patients without suspicion of immediately life-threatening conditions requiring urgent ambulance transport or advanced prehospital resuscitation. In our region, patients with suspected time-critical or high-acuity cardiovascular presentations (e.g., chest pain suggestive of acute coronary syndrome, unstable arrhythmias) and other immediately life-threatening emergencies are generally managed through standard emergency ambulance pathways and conveyed directly to hospital; therefore, such presentations are under-represented in the MEU cohort. This selection mechanism influences the case mix and on-scene diagnostic profile. Accordingly, ECG is not performed routinely but only when clinically indicated during the on-scene assessment. On scene, the MEU team performs a bedside emergency assessment, may initiate treatment (e.g., intravenous fluids/antibiotics), and formulates a management plan. Collaboration with municipal acute-care nurses enables continuation of prescribed therapy and monitoring at the patient’s residence, with escalation to the ED if the patient’s condition deteriorates. If immediate hospital conveyance/admission was required, transport was arranged through the regional EMS/ambulance service according to standard protocols. The MEU retains medical responsibility for up to 96 hours after the last contact. Further details of the MEU service model have been described previously [14] (Fig. 1).

We did not systematically extract activation route, dispatch/triage indices, prior healthcare utilisation, or complete on-scene vital signs because these variables were inconsistently documented in routine MEU records across heterogeneous contact types.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was immediate disposition at the index MEU encounter, operationalised as (1) managed at home without hospital conveyance and (2) direct hospital admission from the scene. Direct hospital admission was defined as transfer/transport to hospital (e.g., emergency department or inpatient admission) initiated immediately following on-scene assessment by the MEU team. The secondary outcome was all-cause mortality at 180 days after the index visit. Mortality at 30 and 90 days was assessed descriptively as tertiary outcomes using the same Kaplan-Meier estimates. Time zero was the date of each individual’s first qualifying MEU contact within the study period (index visit). When a patient had multiple MEU contacts, we considered only the index visit for survival analysis. However, we calculated descriptive statistics for the on-scene clinical information for all MEU contacts.

Statistical methods

We used IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for all analyses. The categorical variables were summarised as n (%), and the continuous variables were summarised as the mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate. We used the Kaplan–Meier method to analyse survival from the index visit to 30, 90, and 180 days.

Ethics and governance

This project was approved as a quality-improvement project by Esbjerg Hospital administration and registered in the internal registry of the Region of Southern Denmark. In accordance with Danish legislation and guidance from the National Committee on Health Research Ethics, this retrospective, non-interventional study did not require formal ethics committee approval. Nevertheless, we adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the General Data Protection Regulation.

Results

Screening of MEU contacts

A total of 2,146 MEU contacts were recorded in the administrative MEU list during the study period. We excluded 159 records that did not represent an actual MEU home visit: (i) dispatch requests/telephone triage contacts where the MEU was requested but the team did not attend the patient; and (ii) administrative/registration errors where a contact appeared in the MEU list but no MEU visit occurred and no corresponding EHR documentation existed.

We included a total of 1,987 MEU contacts, which corresponded to 1,336 unique patients, median follow-up was 279 days (IQR = 57–445). Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 1,336 unique patients, stratified by immediate disposition at the index MEU encounter (non-conveyed vs direct hospital admission). Overall, the median (IQR) age was 80.9 (71.1–87.9) years, and 52.2% (n = 697) of patients were female. The most common reasons for MEU activation were respiratory complaints (n = 240 (18%)), pain without trauma (n = 193 (14.4%)) and decline in general condition (n = 186 (13.9%)). On average, patients were visited 1.4 times (range 1 to 7) by the MEU team. Nearly one-third of MEU patients had ≥ 3 comorbidities, and nearly 90% of patients had at least one chronic condition.

The MEU team frequently employed point-of-care diagnostics on scene. POCUS was used in 492 (24.8%) of contacts, of which 299 were unremarkable and 193 were documented as abnormal in the MEU record. Similarly, POCT blood tests were performed in 1203 (60.5%) of contacts, with a venous blood gas being taken in 61 cases, CRP tests in 201 of cases, and both venous blood gas and CRP together in 941 of cases. Because POCT results were frequently documented as qualitative interpretations (normal/abnormal) and more than one test could be performed per contact, we report POCT utilisation and test-type frequencies rather than aggregated normal/abnormal counts. The most administered medications were antibiotics (32% of contacts), intravenous fluids (18%), and analgesics (9%). Table 2 summarises the on-scene assessments and interventions in all contacts.

Discussion

We conducted a retrospective cohort study to describe a physician-led Mobile Emergency Unit outreach service embedded within the regional prehospital system and operated by the emergency department. By including consecutive MEU contacts across both community-dwelling patients and nursing-home residents, we provide a real-world description of the case mix encountered, the bedside diagnostics and initial treatments delivered on scene, and medium-term survival following MEU contact. These findings help illustrate how an ED-based outreach model may support home-based acute assessment with selective escalation to hospital care when clinically indicated. Importantly, this study is descriptive and unadjusted and was not designed to evaluate comparative safety or effectiveness versus ED-based care.

As shown in the Results, most patients assessed by the emergency physician-led MEU were managed at their residence, with on-scene assessment and treatment delivered without the need for immediate hospital conveyance. The service model provides access to bedside diagnostics (e.g., POCT and POCUS) and allows initiation of treatments when indicated, alongside selective escalation to hospital care when deemed necessary. In addition, home-based management can be continued over consecutive days when needed through collaboration with municipal acute-care nurses, who support ongoing intravenous therapy and monitoring at the patient’s residence. For the older and multimorbid population served by the MEU, this approach may help avoid potentially unnecessary admissions and limit exposure to hospital-associated complications [16–20]. However, this potential benefit cannot be evaluated in our dataset because we did not capture downstream utilisation after the index encounter (e.g., delayed admissions, ED revisits); therefore, we cannot infer safety outcomes from this descriptive dataset.

Among all of our contacts, 17.3% resulted in direct hospital admission, higher than what Udesen et al. [11] reported among nursing-home residents (12.5%). However, the findings from the two studies cannot be directly compared for three reasons. First, the studies had a different case mix: we included all contacts (adults living in the community and nursing homes), whereas Udesen et al. [11] only considered nursing-home residents, where on-site management and advance care planning often reduce hospital transfers. Second, we reported a per-encounter rate at the scene, whereas Udesen et al. [11] calculated per-patient admissions (i.e., the numerator and denominator differ). Third, Udesen et al. [11] examined the period from November 2020 to December 2021, at the height of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, when strict capacity constraints and infection-control policies were in place. On the other hand, we considered the period 2023–2024, after the end of COVID-19 pandemic-related restrictions. Consequently, the rate of direct hospital admissions should be interpreted as context specific rather than directly comparable. In our setting, MEU activation is intended for clinically stable patients without suspicion of immediately life-threatening conditions; time-critical presentations (e.g., suspected acute coronary syndrome or unstable arrhythmia) are typically conveyed directly to hospital via standard emergency pathways and are therefore under-represented in the MEU cohort. This preselection mechanism likely influences both diagnostic patterns (e.g., low ECG utilisation) and observed outcomes.

Mortality in our cohort was substantial and likely reflects a clinically vulnerable and prognostically heterogeneous population encountered by the MEU. However, these estimates should be interpreted as descriptive outcomes rather than evidence of avoidability or safety. We lacked standardised measures of functional status/physiological vulnerability or acute illness severity, palliative care status, and cause-of-death information, and we did not perform risk-adjusted analyses. Moreover, we did not capture subsequent diagnostic work-up or escalation after the index encounter. Therefore, we cannot assess whether outcomes might have differed with additional in-hospital testing such as broader laboratory panels or radiology. Accordingly, the present data cannot determine whether any deaths were preventable or whether a higher level of in-hospital testing would have altered outcomes. Future prospective studies with predefined safety endpoints (e.g., adverse events and delayed admissions after non-conveyance) and linkage to subsequent healthcare utilisation are required to assess safety more directly.

It is informative to contextualise our survival estimates against mortality reported after ED-based care, although direct comparisons are limited by substantial heterogeneity in case mix and eligibility criteria. Across international studies of older adults, short- and medium-term mortality varies widely, with generally lower rates in more selected cohorts and higher rates in cohorts enriched for physiological vulnerability or specific high-risk presentations [21–26]. For example, reported 30-day mortality ranges from ~5–16% in several ED cohorts, and studies consistently show higher mortality among patients with markers of vulnerability such as nursing-home residence or when vulnerability measures are available [22, 24–26]. Similarly, studies incorporating vulnerability-focused pathways or comprehensive geriatric assessment report higher longer-term mortality, approaching the levels observed in our cohort at 180 days [21, 23]. Danish ED-related cohorts also show lower mortality, likely reflecting the exclusion of terminal/palliative patients, survivorship at discharge, and differences in index-episode definitions [27, 28]. Evidence specific to MEU-type services remains limited. In a relatively comparable Danish study restricted to nursing-home residents, Udesen et al. reported a 90-day mortality of 36.4%, plausibly reflecting the higher baseline risk typical of institutionalised populations [11]. In contrast, we included all consecutive MEU contacts irrespective of residence, admission status, or terminal/palliative care, improving external validity but introducing heterogeneity and limiting benchmarking. Our findings should therefore primarily be viewed as real-world descriptive estimates that can generate hypotheses for future prospective studies with prespecified stratification (e.g., residence, vulnerability/comorbidity burden, and admission status) and risk-adjusted analyses (e.g., adjusted hazard ratios) to enable clearer comparisons.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Our study has several strengths. First, we captured all MEU contacts within a defined catchment area and under a single service model. We estimated survival at specific times (30, 90, and 180 days after the index visit) using the Kaplan–Meier method with explicit administrative censoring. The survival analysis was unadjusted and descriptive; comorbidity burden was captured as an unweighted count rather than a weighted index. Thus, the Kaplan–Meier estimates reflect average survival in a prognostically heterogeneous cohort and cannot identify drivers of mortality or provide risk-standardised benchmarking against other populations. Moreover, we examined the use of POCT, POCUS, and on-scene treatments during MEU contacts, data that have rarely been reported. Finally, we included patients living in the community and in nursing homes, enhancing the external validity of our findings.

Despite these strengths, there are some limitations. First, the retrospective, single-centre design precludes causal inference, is susceptible to residual confounding/misclassification, and may limit generalisability. Second, we did not perform adjusted analyses. We analysed survival at the patient level using the first MEU visit, but some patients had multiple MEU contacts within the 2-year period; this phenomenon may reflect unmeasured vulnerability. Third, standardised measures of functional status/physiological vulnerability and cause-of-death data were unavailable, and we did not conduct competing-risk analyses or quantify downstream utilisation (e.g., delayed admissions, ED revisits, and repeat MEU/ED use and readmissions after non-conveyance). Finally, combining encounter-level descriptive variables with patient-level survival data and including patients receiving palliative/terminal care may have inflated crude mortality estimates and complicated comparison with previous studies. We did not systematically capture clinical decision rationales or quantify how POCT/POCUS findings influenced treatment escalation or conveyance decisions; therefore, the clinical impact of these diagnostics cannot be inferred from this descriptive study.

Conclusion

Assessment and treatment of patients at their residence by emergency physicians in this MEU service model was described, and most contacts were managed at home without hospital conveyance at the index encounter (82.7%). Supplemental test capacities in the form of POCT and POCUS were frequently used and may plausibly support bedside assessment and on-scene triage decisions. A mortality rate of 33.2% at 180 days should be interpreted with caution given an older, multimorbid and prognostically heterogeneous case mix and the likely inclusion of patients with advanced illness and palliative care needs. As this study was descriptive and unadjusted and did not capture downstream utilisation (e.g., delayed admissions/ED revisits), it cannot establish causal or comparative safety or effectiveness. Prospective, stratified studies are warranted to evaluate the safety, cost-effectiveness, and system-level impact of the MEU.
