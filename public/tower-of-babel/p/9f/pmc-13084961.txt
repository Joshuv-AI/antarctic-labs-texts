Assessing patient perspectives on enrollment in lymphoma clinical trials
Authors: Neha Akkad, Minxing Chen, Sophia Nguyen, Alexis Romero, Lynne Nguyen, Lorna H. McNeill, Loretta J. Nastoupil, Jason Westin, Paolo Strati, Sairah Ahmed, Liliana Vallejo, Rebeca Yzquierdo, Amy Ayers, Christopher R. Flowers, Chijioke Nze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical trials drive advances in lymphoma care, yet certain patient groups including socioeconomically disadvantaged, racial/ethnic groups, and women remain underrepresented. Given limited/inconsistent data regarding solutions to improve clinical trial enrollment of these groups, the authors performed a study to assess patient perspectives on barriers to enrollment and define priorities for future interventions.MethodsPatients completed surveys developed to assess patient‐level factors, barriers to opportunity, attitudes toward participation, and perspectives on interventions.ResultsAmong 301 participants, non‐Hispanic White (NHW) and patients with an income >$100 k/year reported better understanding of clinical trials compared to Hispanic (H) patients and patients with an income <$50 k/year (NHW: 58% vs. H: 40%, p = .015; >$100 k/year: 59.8% vs. <$50 k/year: 38.8%, p = .012). Compared to NHW, H and African American (AA) patients reported more concern regarding visit frequency (NHW: 10%; H: 27%; AA: 23%, p = .005), travel distance (NHW: 14%; H: 34%, p = .010; AA: 31%, p = .013), taking time off work (NHW: 9%; H: 23% p = <.001; AA: 18%, p = .006) and study complexity (NHW: 6%; H: 20%, p = .008; AA: 22%, p = .001). Compared to male patients, female patients more commonly noted that having childcare services (male: 11%, female: 23%, p = .004), flexible appointment times (male: 43%, female: 62%, p = .015), and housing closer to the clinic/hospital (male: 44%, female: 55%, p = .016) as helpful interventions to improve trial participation. Regardless of group, 71% of patients noted that insurance/financial support would be the most helpful facilitator of clinical trial enrollment, and 88% reported their physician’s advice would most influence their decision to participate.ConclusionData from this study suggest which specific strategies should be prioritized in future implementation studies to improve enrollment in lymphoma clinical trials and achieve disease population representation.

INTRODUCTION

Clinical trials are critical for advancing our understanding of cancer biology, evaluating the safety and efficacy of novel treatments, and developing treatment guidelines. Despite their importance, clinical trials often lack representation from patients who are female, Hispanic ethnicity (H), African American (AA), >65 years of age, socioeconomically disadvantaged, or living in rural geographies. 1 Prior studies have shown that AA and H patients were underrepresented in clinical trials compared with their expected proportion based on cancer incidence in the United States. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Moreover, a recent regimen for patients with lymphoma presented at the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Oncology Drug Advisory Committee met the primary end point in a phase 3 trial, but due to inconsistency in treatment effect in subgroups defined by race and region, the panel determined the results were not applicable to the US population, thus highlighting the importance of representation of all groups of patients in clinical trials. 7 , 8

Barriers to enrollment exist at multiple levels including system/societal, institutional, and patient. 9 , 10 On the system/societal level, studies have shown that AA and H patients are significantly less likely to receive care at academic medical centers where participation rates in clinical trials are typically higher and are less likely to enroll in cooperative group cancer trials than their NHW counterparts. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Patient‐related factors such as reluctance and medical mistrust are often cited as impacting clinical trial enrollment. However, recent large studies have demonstrated that willingness to participate did not differ significantly between demographic groups; rather, structural barriers, such as study design and limited accessibility accounted for the largest barriers to participation. 11 , 15 , 16 Individual‐ and neighborhood‐level socioeconomic status (SES) has also been identified as drivers of disparities in cancer treatment. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

Given the paucity of data and lack of consensus in the literature regarding barriers to clinical trial enrollment among AA and H patients with lymphoma, and to further elucidate the impact of sex, education level, and annual income, we conducted a prospective survey study of patients with lymphoma receiving care at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC) to better define their perspectives on clinical trial participation and identify specific barriers to clinical trial enrollment. These data can inform how to design and prioritize targeted interventions in future studies to improve representation in clinical trials and ensure optimal treatment strategies for patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient enrollment

Patients with lymphoma receiving care at MDACC were screened and included if they were >18 years of age, self‐identified as NHW, AA, or H, had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, provided consent, and could answer questionnaires in English or Spanish. We aimed to accrue 100 patients per group among AA, H, and NHW patients. This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of MDACC.

Survey content and development

Patients who provided written informed consent completed a REDCap survey including questions regarding demographic factors (e.g., age and race) and comorbidities (see Supporting Information for survey English version). Surveys were developed in collaboration with subject matter experts from MDACC Center for Community‐Engaged Translational Research and included items drawn from well‐established instruments including items published in Ulrich et al. 24 Questions related to clinical trial knowledge were adapted from the Center for Information and Study on Clinical Research Participation 2021 Perceptions & Insights Study 25 and National Cancer Institute Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS) 5 Cycle 4. Specifically, questions from HINTS were regarding patient knowledge/experience and advisors. (Supporting Information Patient Surveys, page 3 and 5, respectively) 26 Several trust‐related items were adapted from a foundational study describing now widely recognized dimensions of medical mistrust. 27 Questions assessed key patient‐level factors, including awareness (e.g., health literacy, knowledge, and understanding/awareness of clinical trials), potential barriers to opportunity (e.g., transportation, housing, child care, and financial concerns), attitudes toward participation (e.g., trust and willingness), and perspectives on interventions aimed at improving clinical trial enrollment. Survey questions were formatted on a five‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” and “no influence” to “significant influence.”

Statistical analysis

Participant baseline demographic and clinical data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical measures. Two‐sample t‐test p values (for continuous measures) or χ2 tests (Fisher exact test if any cell value is smaller than 5) were also used. Incomplete surveys were excluded. A multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate for predictors of clinical trial enrollment. Reference groups used were follicular lymphoma for histology, NHW for race, and female for sex. To evaluate if the association between gender and clinical trial participation differed by histology, we also specified an interaction term between histology and sex. National and state area deprivation index (ADI) was assessed for association with distance and income using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analyses with robust standard errors. We assessed the association between ADI and income level using OLS regression, followed by logistic regression models to evaluate the association between ADI and trial participation. Logistic regression analyses were stratified by race/ethnicity. All statistical analysis was performed using Stata/SE v18.0 (College Station, Texas).

RESULTS

Patient cohort characteristics

Of 346 patients who provided consent, 301 completed all surveys (87% response rate). A total of 103 (34%) were NHW, 98 (32%) were AA, and 100 (33%) were H. Both H and AA patients were younger than their NHW counterparts (median age NHW: 66 vs. AA: 61, p = .045; H: 60, p < .001). Patients most commonly reported a bachelor’s degree as their highest level of education (22%), except H patients who most often reported some college credit and no degree (20%). NHW patients had the largest proportion with a household income >$100,000 (41%) compared to 19% for AA patients and 32% for H patients (Table 1). Lower income level was associated with greater ADI (coefficient = –0.0236, p < .001), showing a correlation between individual‐level SES and neighborhood‐level SES. Patient characteristics.

Association between patient demographics and prior clinical trial participation

A total of 37% of patients reported prior participation in any clinical trial. Male patients reported higher participation rates in clinical trials than female patients (44% vs. 29%, p = .010). NHW reported higher clinical trial participation (53%), compared to AA (29%), and H patients (29%) (p < .001). Regarding histology, patients with mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) had the highest participation rate of 76%, patients with marginal zone lymphoma had the lowest participation rate of 18%. (Table S2). In multivariable analysis, trial participation was significantly associated with MCL histology (odds ratio [OR], 5.28, p < .001), H race (OR, 0.49, p = .030), and female sex showed a trend (OR, 0.58, p = .051). When MCL cases were excluded, H race was no longer significantly associated (OR, 0.57, p = .108), but female sex became significant (OR, 0.52, p = .030), suggesting that the observed difference may be driven by the MCL subgroup (Tables S3A and S3B). After adding a histology × sex interaction term, female sex was no longer significant (OR, 0.55, p = .23), whereas MCL (OR, 4.12, p = .008) and H race (OR, 0.48, p = .032) remained associated, indicating that H patients were less likely to participate independent of histology and sex interactions.

There was no difference in age, level of education, yearly household income, general health status, activity level, national or state ADI, or comorbidities (except diabetes) for those who reported prior participation in a clinical trial versus nonparticipants. (results not shown) Clinical trial participants most often reported their commute time to be 2–5 hours (31%) and nonparticipants most often reported their commute to be <1 hour (44%; p = .041).

Patient reported experiences in, understanding of, and willingness to participate in clinical trials

Among patients who participated in a clinical trial, their experience was uniformly positive (85%) across groups. Most patients reported understanding the term “clinical research study” very well or somewhat well (89%). NHW patients reported understanding very well more often than H patients (58% vs. 40%, p = .015) but similar to AA patients. Patients with a higher income level reported better understanding with 60% of patients with an income >$100 k/year reporting understanding very well compared to 39% of patients with an income of <$50 k/year (p = .012). There was no difference in understanding based on sex or education level (results not shown).

NHW patients more often reported that a physician/member of their medical team discussed clinical trials with them (73%) compared to H (51%; p = .002) or AA (54%; p = .007) patients. Most patients thought clinical trials were somewhat safe or very safe (98%) across demographics. Overall, half of patients thought that participants in clinical trials receive the same care as nonparticipants, however NHW patients thought that participants receive better care than nonparticipants compared to H patients (NHW: 60% vs. H: 34%; p < .001).

Patients most commonly indicated that they were very willing to participate in clinical research studies in the frontline setting (42%), second/relapsed setting (63%), or in addition to standard of care treatment (49%). Additionally, most patients described that they were very willing to participate in clinical research studies if they knew standard treatments were not very effective (59%), if there were no other standard options (76%), or if the trial offered an easier to receive treatment option (50%). Patient concerns when considering clinical trial enrollment

H and AA patients noted significantly greater concerns than NHW patients regarding frequency of visits/testing, travel distance to their appointments, taking time off from work, and study complexity (Table 3). AA patients were also more concerned about pain/discomfort, out‐of‐pocket costs, and type of testing (e.g., labs, imaging, and physical examination) compared to NHW patients. Taking time off from work was a bigger concern for female patients (21%) than male patients (13%; p = .024).

Patients with an education level less than or equal to a high school diploma (<HS) more commonly had concerns about out of pocket costs, travel distance to appointments, there being someone in the research team to contact, and type of testing required compared to patients with a higher level of education (Table S4). A similar pattern was observed in patients who had an income level of <$50 k/year compared to patients with a higher income level (Table S4).

When asked about whose advice would most influence their decision to participate in clinical trials, 88% reported that their doctor’s advice would have the most influence across groups. Faith/community/religious leader and disease‐specific patient support groups were ranked higher by AA patients in influencing their decision to participate in trials than NHW patients (Figure 1). There was no significant difference in rankings between NHW and H patients. Patients with an education level <HS and patients with an income <$50 k/year were more likely to rank family and friends, faith/community/religious leader, and nonprofit health organizations as more influential than patients who had an education level >HS or income >$50 k/year (Table S5).

Patient concerns about past medical mistrust

Regardless of demographics, most patients reported believing that their physician would fully explain research/clinical trials and would not ask them to participate in harmful medical research. Compared to their NHW counterparts, H and AA patients were significantly more likely to agree with statements about past medical mistrust and participating in research without permission (Table S6A). There was no difference in agreement with these statements in male versus female patients (Table S6B).

Patient opinions on interventions to improve informed decision‐making regarding clinical trial participation

When asked how helpful suggested interventions would be to improve informed decision‐making regarding participation in clinical trials, the intervention reported to be most helpful was insurance/financial support across groups (71%) (Figure 2). Additionally, 64% of all patients noted that written materials with explanations of drugs/toxicities/trial logistics would be extremely helpful (Figure 2). H (26%) and AA (18%) patients found childcare services more helpful compared to NHW (4%) patients (p < .001 and p = .019, respectively).

Compared to male patients, female patients more commonly rated having childcare services (F: 22.7%, M: 11.0%, p = .004), flexible appointment times (F: 62.0%, M: 43.3%, p = .015), and housing options closer to the clinic/hospital (F: 55.1%, M: 43.9%, p = .016) as helpful interventions to improve trial participation. Patients with an education level <HS ranked childcare services, having someone who speaks their language to explain the study to them, and transportation services higher compared to patients with a higher level of education (results not shown). Patients with an income <$50 k/year ranked transportation services as more helpful compared to patients with an income >$50 k/year (<$50 k/year: 54% vs. $50 k–100 k/year:37% vs. >$100 k/year: 33%; p = .050).

DISCUSSION

The underrepresentation of specific patient groups in clinical trials hinders their equal access to novel therapies and negatively impacts the validity of new approaches in populations that do not accurately reflect those for whom the interventions are intended. Prior studies and reviews of the literature have suggested possible interventions to increase clinical trial enrollment of specific patient groups; however, few of these strategies have been interrogated and proven to be efficacious in real‐world studies. 28 , 29 , 30 Data from this study suggest which of these potential strategies should be prioritized in future implementation studies based on patient perspectives, these include: universal screening, active prescreening, trial simplification, local laboratory testing, virtual visits, patient navigators, community advisory boards, involvement of lay health care workers/patient advocates, training in patient‐centered communication, promoting health insurance coverage for clinical trials, and implementing support for childcare, transportation, and other reimbursement for participants into trial budgets.

NHW patients more often reported that clinical trials were discussed by their medical team than AA or H patients, consistent with prior studies showing that provider perceptions may influence trial discussions. 31 , 32 , 33 Strategies to overcome this provider level barrier include universal screening, defined as identifying potentially eligible patients using limited eligibility criteria, 28 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 and active pre‐screening, 29 , 38 methods that can reduce possible unintended exclusion when approaching trial candidates. Automated screening approaches using artificial intelligence algorithms 39 may also address this barrier with attention given to avoid building in analogous exclusions.

Concerns about clinical trials were found to differ by race. H and AA patients had more concerns about study complexity. Clinical trial simplification is the aim of Project Pragmatica, an initiative led by the FDA to allow for efficient trial conduct through reducing eligibility criteria, implementing flexible trial delivery, and assessing patient‐centric outcomes. 40 , 41 It aims to accomplish this this by incorporating various design elements that are more reflective of routine clinical practice to reduce the burden of trial participation. Our data reinforce the value of these approaches. AA and H patients had greater concerns about frequency of testing, frequency of visits, travel distance, and needing to take time off from work. These concerns underscore the importance of implementing decentralized trial strategies, such as remote consenting, virtual visits, laboratory testing closer to home, and mobile phlebotomy, to reduce logistical burden and address structural barriers. 28 , 42 Patients with low education level or income also noted significant concerns about having a contact person in the research team. Incorporating research patient navigators 28 , 43 , 44 as liaisons is therefore recommended to identify barriers to participation, uncover resources, and enhance trust.

Faith/community/religious leaders were cited as valuable sources of advice for AA and low‐income patients, suggesting that engagement with these leaders should be prioritized as a strategy to increase clinical trial enrollment. This is supported by the findings of Project CHURCH, an ongoing initiative between MDACC and Houston area AA churches that has demonstrated the effectiveness of community‐based participatory research and the critical role of community figures in addressing historical mistrust and improving recruitment for research studies. 45 Additional strategies to engage with faith/community/religious leaders include establishing community advisory boards early in the clinical trial design process, and in the creation of patient facing materials, as well as sharing the results of clinical trials with the community. 28 , 46 Involving lay health workers/health advocates, defined as members of the local community with training in complex disease care‐coordination, has also been shown to improve enrollment in clinical trials. 29 , 47 Medical mistrust remains a high concern, particularly among AA and H patients. Evidence‐based strategies to improve culturally sensitive discussion of the impact of social determinants of health on clinical trial enrollment are needed, 28 but efficacious strategies are scarce at this time. 29 , 48 In our study, patients had a high degree of trust in their identified provider, highlighting the value of a trusting relationship in overcoming reluctance and the importance of providers as gate‐keepers for trial offerings.

All patient groups noted that the most important facilitator of clinical trial participation was insurance/financial assistance. This highlights that national, state, and local policies and insurers should consider mandating coverage for clinical research studies to improve access. Additional facilitators highlighted by AA, H, female, and patients with an education level <HS were childcare services, payment for participation, and transportation services. It is therefore important to consider coverage or reimbursement for these services when designing clinical trial budgets. Interestingly, clinical trial participants self‐reported further travel distance than nonparticipants, highlighting that patients are likely willing to travel further distances to participate in clinical trials, however, certain groups, such as those with a lower education or income level, may need assistance to accomplish that. Partnerships with disease‐specific foundations such as the Blood Cancer United and the Lymphoma Research Foundation can also provide resources to help overcome these financial barriers. 28 , 49 , 50

Limitations of this study include performance at a large academic medical center with an extensive clinical trial portfolio. All patients with lymphoma in this setting were more likely to have insurance than the general population. Barriers to clinical trial enrollment identified here are likely to be amplified in other populations outside of an academic medical center, such as patient populations receiving treatment in community practices. Additionally, this study was confined to NHW, H, and AA patients who spoke English or Spanish to examine perspectives of the most populous groups of patients and those previously identified to be underrepresented in studies. The findings may not be as relevant to other demographic groups with different cultural contexts or languages however our analysis of education, SES status, and distance likely extend to other groups with similar adverse nonmedical drivers of decreased participation.

To our knowledge this study represents the largest sample size of patients with lymphoma ever surveyed on barriers and facilitators of clinical trials enrollment. The NIH’s recent mission and strategy includes “shifting to solution‐oriented approached in health disparities” research. 51 This study can serve as a source and priority list of evidence‐based interventions that can be used in future implementation studies and provides simple, tested questionnaire tools for assessing barriers and identifying solutions in other clinical settings. Future work will focus on evaluating lymphoma patient perspectives in community‐based practices.
