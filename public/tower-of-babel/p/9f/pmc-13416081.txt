The circadian clock controls hepatic stellate cell activation via a BMAL1/CK1ε/REV-ERBα/transgelin signaling pathway
Authors: Manuel Johanns, Alexandre Berthier, Jimmy Vandel, Sandra Courquet, Marie Lapage, Julie Dubois-Chevalier, Manjula Vinod, Ninon Very, Francesco Paolo Zummo, Marie Bobowski-Gérard, Georgiana Toma, Céline Gheeraert, Didier Vertommen, Gaëtan Herinckx, Violeta Raverdy, François Pattou, Bart Staels, Jérôme Eeckhoute, Philippe Lefebvre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceLiver fibrosis is a progressive and life-threatening condition that lacks effective targeted therapies. Circadian rhythms and fibrogenesis are increasingly recognized as interconnected, but the molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we show that although the liver clock is generally maintained during human disease progression from a fatty liver to fibrosis, changes in circadian gene amplitude occur in people with obesity. We identify a clock-controlled signaling pathway that regulates hepatic stellate cell activation, a key initiator of fibrosis. Manipulating core components of the circadian clock identified transgelin, a component of the cytoskeleton, as a downstream effector of clock-regulated HSC contractility. These findings reveal a circadian mechanism linking the molecular clock to primary fibrogenic events and suggest innovative chronotherapeutic strategies.

Organ fibrosis results from an excessive wound-healing response to ongoing viral, mechanical, nutritional, or other insults that remodel tissue architecture, increase stiffness, and impair organ functions. It underpins life-threatening conditions such as liver cirrhosis, pulmonary and renal fibrosis, and systemic sclerosis, for which transplantation is often the only therapy. Chronic liver diseases (CLDs) have various causes, including viral infections, excessive alcohol intake [alcohol-related liver disease (ALD)], poor diet [metabolic (dysfunction)-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)], or both [metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-related liver disease (MetALD)] (1). Liver fibrosis is a key pathological outcome of chronic liver injury and a major contributor to mortality from CLDs worldwide. It is also the most significant predictor of patient mortality from liver- or cardiovascular-related adverse events (2–4), highlighting the importance of early detection and intervention.

Fibrosis is now recognized as a dynamic, multistage process triggered by tissue injury and inflammation. When this response is not resolved, it shifts from a reparative phase to a pathological one, characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) buildup. This progression occurs through a “hot fibrosis” stage, marked by active inflammation and ECM accumulation, leading to “cold fibrosis,” where ECM production dominates (5). In the liver, excessive ECM buildup is driven by proliferative, migratory, and contractile myofibroblasts that gather in fibrotic regions (6). The main source of these myofibroblasts is hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), whose transition from a quiescent to an activated state is notably accompanied by significant changes in cellular metabolism (7–10).

Cellular metabolism is controlled by exogenous cues and internal mechanisms, among which the circadian clock has emerged as a critical regulator, orchestrating the expression of hundreds of circadian output genes (11, 12). The core of the circadian clock relies on a series of interlocking transcriptional–translational feedback loops (TTFLs) that generate rhythmic gene expression. At its core is a cell-autonomous TTFL where the proteins BMAL1 (encoded by the Basic Helix-Loop-Helix ARNT Like 1 [Arntl] gene) and CLOCK (encoded by the Circadian Locomotor Output Cycles Protein Kaput [Clock] gene) form a heterodimer that activates the transcription of the period (Per1-3) and cryptochrome (Cry1-2) genes. PER-CRY dimers are the negative arm of the TTFL as they repress BMAL1 and/or CLOCK transcriptional activity (13). BMAL1 is considered the only essential component of this central loop (14). To enhance and sustain the BMAL1 oscillation, a secondary TTFL operates in parallel in which RORα/β/γ nuclear receptors (encoded by Rora, Rorb, or Rorc genes) act as activators and REV-ERBα and REV-ERBβ (encoded by Nr1d1 and Nr1d2 genes) act as repressors of Bmal1 expression. BMAL1 itself drives the expression of both RORs and REV-ERBs, creating a “stabilizing loop” that fine-tunes the primary BMAL1:CLOCK TTFL and maintains the precision of the circadian rhythm with a period of approximately 24 h (15). Additional layers of regulation occur through posttranslational modifications of core clock proteins (16).

Considering this, the potential link between the circadian clock, HSC activation, and fibrosis has been examined through pharmacological and genetic approaches. Whole body Bmal1 gene inactivation in ob/ob mice protects from diet-induced fibrosis (17), while its AAV8-mediated overexpression blunts carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced fibrosis (18). Disruption of the BMAL1:CLOCK positive arm of the clock in ClockΔ19 mice leads to arrhythmicity and increased sensitivity to CCl4-induced fibrosis (19). In contrast, Per2-null mice, characterized by disruption of the negative arm of the clock and subsequent increased CLOCK expression also show sensitivity to CCl4-induced fibrosis (20, 21). Inconsistencies among these results may stem from systemic effects and/or altered intercellular communication (22, 23). Using REV-ERBα/β agonists to pharmacologically target the clock-stabilizing loop provides protection against fibrosis, although the proposed mechanisms vary significantly (23–26). Investigation of REV-ERB cell-autonomous effects by agonist treatment also supports the identification of REV-ERBs as factors promoting HSC quiescence, a conclusion further reinforced by loss-of-function studies (23, 27, 28).

Reciprocally, whether profibrotic conditions alter core clock gene (CCG) expression has been investigated primarily in murine model systems. A methionine and choline-deficient diet or CCl4 treatment, both potent inducers of liver fibrosis, disrupted the phase and amplitude of multiple CCGs (28–30). Although this causal relationship cannot be directly tested in humans, correlations between disruptions of rhythmic processes, such as the sleep–wake cycle, and cirrhosis have been observed (31). Mathematical extrapolation of gene expression patterns in human livers suggests that core clock components are resilient in liver cancer (32), consistent with our findings in human liver biopsies with known collection times (33). Additionally, biopsies from patients with different liver disease etiologies revealed a decrease in BMAL1 expression in fibrosis, along with decreased expression of circadian clock repressors (17).

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive examination of CCGs expression in livers of a time-of-day-controlled human metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and fibrosis cohort. While time-of-day-dependent CCG expression remained largely resilient, we identified significant alterations in its amplitude among patients with MASH and fibrosis. In mice, profiling of CCG expression in liver parenchymal and nonparenchymal cells demonstrated the exquisite sensitivity of the molecular clock in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and cholangiocytes (CHs) to both acute and prolonged CCl4 administration. Mechanistically, the CLOCK:BMAL1/REV-ERB axis was implicated in mediating the profibrotic response in primary human HSCs and the immortalized mouse HSC line, EMS404 which exhibited robust circadian rhythmicity and responded to transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) stimulation. In this context, REV-ERB agonism and BMAL1 inhibition effectively abrogated TGFβ-induced HSC activation and reduced cell contractility without disrupting the canonical TGFβ signaling pathway. These effects were corroborated at the transcriptional level by a pronounced suppression of the profibrotic program in HSCs, as evidenced by the downregulation of ECM- and focal adhesion-related gene expression. Finally, a combined transcriptomic and proteomic approach identified transgelin as a molecular relay for CCG-mediated regulation of HSC activation.

Materials and Methods

Detailed descriptions of reagents, constructs, and experimental procedures are provided in SI Appendix.

The HUL/ABOS Cohort.

The Hôpital Universitaire de Lille (HUL) cohort, also known as the Biological Atlas of Severe Obesity (ABOS; ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01129297), was established in 2006 after approval by the Lille Hospital Ethics committee (no CP06/49) to recruit severely obese patients undergoing bariatric surgery. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients fasted overnight, and liver wedge biopsies were collected during surgery, immediately snap-frozen, and time-stamped. The cohort includes >2,000 patients, with >900 biopsies histologically and transcriptionally characterized (34). A propensity-matched subcohort (n = 213) was selected from 319 patients, balanced for age, BMI, insulin resistance, fibrosis, sex ratio, disease stage, and sampling time (AM/PM), comprising 71 normal liver (NL), 71 steatosis, and 71 MASH cases. Clinical and molecular features are summarized in SI Appendix, Fig. S1A. The “F0 vs. >F2” subcohort has been described previously (35).

Human Transcriptomic Data Analysis: Daytime Differential Analysis.

Transcriptomic data collection and initial processing were described in ref. 34. Briefly, gene counts (RSEM) were filtered and analyzed in R using DESeq2 (v1.43.0) to assess differential expression between morning and afternoon samples (sex as covariate). Time-dependent genes were identified by combining differential expression, Kolmogorov–Smirnov testing, and partial Spearman correlation analyses, with p-values aggregated using Fisher’s method and FDR correction. Genes with FDR < 0.01 (±fold-change > 1.2) were considered time-dependent (33).

Animal Experimentation.

All animal procedures were approved by Comité d’Ethique en Expérimentation Animale no. 075 (Lille, France). Eight- to twelve-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were from Charles River; Per2::Luc (IMSR_JAX:006852) from The Jackson Laboratory; and whole-body REV-ERBα knockout mice were generated as described (see SI Appendix). Mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle (22 to 24 °C) with ad libitum access to chow (A04, Safe) and water. Liver fibrosis was induced by intraperitoneal CCl4 (#319961, Sigma-Aldrich; 0.5 mL/kg in olive oil), administered once or three times weekly for 2 wk, with tissues collected 24 h after the final injection.

Liver Explant Preparation and Ex Vivo Luciferase Activity Monitoring.

Per2::Luc mice were euthanized at ZT7 according to the approved procedure by the Comité d’Ethique en Expérimentation Animale n°075 (Lille, France). Livers were rapidly excised, sectioned (2 mm3), and cultured (15 explants/insert) on Millicell membranes (#PICMORG50, Millipore) in bicarbonate-free DMEM (#D5030, Sigma) containing 200 µM luciferin. Explants were maintained at 36 °C without CO2, and bioluminescence was recorded (1 min every 10 min) using a Kronos Dio system (ATTO) and analyzed with Kronos software (v2.30.243).

Liver Cell Type Isolation.

Liver parenchymal and nonparenchymal cells were isolated from male C57BL/6J mice by collagenase IV perfusion and enzymatic dissociation (pronase/DNase I), followed by hepatocyte sedimentation and red blood cell lysis. Cells were sorted on a BD Influx v7 (BD Biosciences) as previously described (36).

Primary Human HSCs.

Cryopreserved primary hepatic stellate cells from a healthy donor (donor A, male) were from Innoprot (P10653). Donor B HSCs were isolated from a cirrhotic male donor at HUL (35). Both primary HSCs were grown in DMEM supplemented with 15% FCS. Cells were treated at 70 to 80% confluency (passage 8 to 10) with DMSO (control) or 5 µM SR9011 or 5 µM CLK8 for 24 h, then with 5 ng/mL TGFb or vehicle in the same media for an additional 24 h.

Cell Lines, Plasmids, Small Interfering RNAs, and Lentiviruses.

Cell lines, plasmids, siRNAs, lentiviruses, and related procedures are described in SI Appendix.

Collagen Gel Contraction Assay.

Collagen gels were prepared from rat tail collagen (11179179001, Roche) neutralized in DMEM and mixed with EMS404 cells (final density 1.25 × 106 cells/mL), then cast in 6-well plates. After polymerization, medium and treatments were added, gels were released on day 1, and contraction was monitored by measuring gel area daily up to day 5 using ImageJ.

Cell Adhesion Assay.

EMS404 cells were treated for 24 h (DMSO, CLK8, SR9011, or TGFβ), replated onto collagen-coated 24-well plates (2.5 × 105 cells/well), and allowed to adhere for 45 min. Nonadherent cells were removed, adherent cells were fixed, stained with Hoechst, and nuclei were quantified by ImageJ following automated thresholding and segmentation.

Seahorse Assays.

Glycolytic rate was measured in EMS404 cells using an XF96 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent). Cells (7.5 × 104/well) were treated with TGFβ (1 to 5 ng/mL), equilibrated in XF assay medium, and analyzed using the Glycolytic Rate Assay with rotenone/antimycin A and 2-deoxyglucose injections. Glycolytic activity was quantified as extracellular acidification rate (ECAR).

RNA Sequencing (RNA-seq), Data Processing and Analysis.

RNA-seq libraries were prepared (polyA selection, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation) and sequenced (2 × 100 bp) on a DNBSEQ platform (BGI). Reads were quality-filtered (SOAPnuke), aligned to the mouse genome (mm10; HISAT2), and quantified (RSEM); differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (q < 0.05).

ChIP-seq.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) data were reprocessed as described in ref. 37 from GSE39977 [BMAL1 cistrome (38)] and GSE26345 [REV-ERBα cistrome (39)]. Tracks were visualized using IGV [v2.17.2.02 (40)].

Proteomic Analysis–Tandem Mass Tag Isobaric Labeling.

EMS404 cells were lysed in detergent buffer, and proteins were extracted, acetone-precipitated, and digested with trypsin. Peptides were quantified, TMTpro-labeled, pooled, fractionated by high-pH reverse phase, and analyzed by LC–MS/MS on a Fusion Lumos Orbitrap. Data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (v2.5) with standard filtering, yielding >5,000 proteins.

Statistical Analysis.

Except for omics analyses, statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.4.1). Data are presented as mean ± SEM from ≥2 independent biological replicates. Comparisons used unpaired two-tailed t tests (Welch’s correction) or one- or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test as appropriate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Time-of-Day Differential Expression of CCG Is Altered in Steatotic and MASH Livers.

From an initial cohort of 620 patients living with obesity and with complete clinical, biometric, histological data as well as recorded time-of-day of biopsy collection and liver transcriptomic data (34), we constructed 3 equally sized groups (n = 71 each; 213 patients in total) with a consistent male-to-female ratio. These groups were optimally matched for age, BMI, insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and fibrosis stage to minimize potential bias and control for known confounders. Each group represented one liver condition: healthy (NL, normal liver), steatotic, or MASH (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). A multivariate ANOVA indicated, as previously reported (33), that sex, histology, and time of day were significant explanatory variables, as supported by the corresponding F ratios (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Biopsy collection times were similarly distributed across groups, with most samples (60 to 65%) collected in the morning (AM) with a peak collection time around 9:30 AM. Afternoon (PM) samples, with a collection peak at 1:30 PM, accounted for approximately 35 to 40% of the total (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). We used our combinatorial statistical method (33) combining 3 different analyses of gene expression distribution (differential expression, partial Spearman correlation, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) aggregated by a Fisher test to identify significantly time-dependent genes (TDG) for each group. As previously reported, many TDGs were specific to each group (44 to 64%), with a core conserved set of genes (12%, 66 out of 552 total) whose functions are expectedly associated with circadian rhythmicity and glucose and lipid homeostasis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Among these genes were CCGs (Dataset S1), thus confirming our initial analysis carried out on a broader cohort with less stringent matching (33). We calculated the mean expression value for each CCG and compared AM vs. PM values across groups (Fig. 1 A and B). Fold change variation analysis across groups revealed that globally AM to PM amplitudes (ΔFC) mildly increased in steatosis and significantly decreased in MASH livers (Fig. 1 C, Inset), with NR1D1 and NR2D2 showing increased amplitudes while BMAL1 amplitude was reduced (Fig. 1C).

A similar approach was followed to compare nonfibrotic (Kleiner score F = 0, n = 53) to matched fibrotic (Kleiner score F > 2, n = 53) livers (Fig. 1D). Common TDGs included again CCGs (NR1D1, NR1D2, NPAS2, CIART, PER3, CLOCK, BMAL1, CRY1, NFIL3) (Fig. 1E), yet for some of them, the AM vs. PM differential expression was altered in fibrosis (Fig. 1F). Fold change variation analysis across groups revealed that AM to PM amplitudes globally increased in fibrotic livers (Fig. 1 F, Inset). Individually, NR1D1, NR2D2, and BMAL1 transcripts showed increased AM vs. PM amplitudes, whereas the BMAL1 dimerization partner CLOCK displayed a lower amplitude in fibrosis.

Taken together, our data from human livers indicate that although CCGs remain resilient in the context of MASH and fibrosis, they exhibit detectable alterations in their time-of-day-dependent amplitude across conditions. However, in the absence of complete circadian expression profiles, and given interindividual variability and the spread in biopsy collection times, we cannot determine whether these changes reflect alterations in overall expression levels, amplitude, period, or a combination thereof. These observations prompted us to further investigate the behavior and role of CCGs in mouse models of fibrosis.

Fibrosis Progression in Mouse Liver Perturbs CCG Expression in Several Cell Types.

The hepatotoxicant CCl4 was used in C57Bl6/J male mice to induce a pericentral injury and a profibrotic response in the liver (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Twenty-four hours after the first injection, hepatocytes (HC), stellate cells (HSCs), Kupffer cells (KCs), sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), and cholangiocytes (CHs) were isolated (around ZT3, i.e., 3 h after lights-on) and mRNA levels of selected fibrosis markers (Acta2, Col3a1, Col1a1, Dpt…) were evaluated (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Acta2 and Ankrd1 were strongly induced in HSCs, while Tgfb1 expression increased in KCs. After a 2-wk CCl4 treatment, Acta2, Col1a1, and Dpt expression were significantly increased in HSCs while Tgfb1 expression returned to normal in KCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Liver histology and serum markers of liver injury clearly indicated established fibrosis under this condition (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Since this suggested that the profibrotic milieu might evolve, we monitored the expression level of the most representative CCGs in each condition (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). Interestingly, CCG expressions were differentially affected by both treatment duration and cell type. The most pronounced changes were observed in HSCs, while in KCs CCGs remained largely unaffected. Most CCGs showed a decreased expression in response to CCl4, a trend that was also confirmed by transcript analysis from bulk liver tissue following long-term CCl4 exposure (8 wk, SI Appendix, Fig. S2E).

Taken together, these data suggest that despite an evolving fibrotic milieu, CCG expression is mostly downregulated in HSCs and to a lesser extent in CHs.

Chemical Interference with the Molecular Clock Attenuates the Ex Vivo Profibrotic Response.

Since Cry2, Nr1d1, Nr1d2, and Bmal1 expression was altered in CCl4-activated HSCs, the ability of pharmacological clock modulators targeting CRYs, REV-ERBs, or BMAL1 to interfere with the liver fibrotic response was interrogated in an ex vivo model of fibrosis (Fig. 2A). Liver explants from Per2::Luc mice (41) exhibit a bioluminescent rhythm with a ≈ 24-h period (Fig. 2B) and develop a spontaneous fibrotic response that peaks after 6 d in culture (Fig. 2C, compare D0 vs. D6 DMSO) which is mostly driven by TGFβ (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). The REV-ERBα/β agonist SR9011 (EC50 ≈ 790 nM for REV-ERBα) (42), the BMAL1:CLOCK disruptor CLK8 (effective range: 10 to 40 µM) (43) and the CRY stabilizer KL001 (effective range: 0.5 to 25 µM) (44) effectively perturbed the circadian activity of the Per2-Luc fusion protein, substantiating their efficacy in this model system (Fig. 2B). When monitoring the expression of archetypical fibrotic players (Acta2, Col1a1, Col3a1, Timp1) in the presence of increasing doses of these compounds, both CLK8 and SR9011 efficiently blunted the induction of these genes in cultured liver explants at Day 6, whereas KL001 was less effective (Fig. 2C) and was therefore not investigated further. Importantly, the inhibitory effect of SR9011 was not observed in liver explants prepared from Nr1d1 KO mouse livers (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and C) providing genetic proof for specificity of this agonist and for a potentially key role for REV-ERBα.

This approach was replicated in human HSCs isolated from 3 different donors and challenged with TGFβ (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5). Of note, primary human HSCs also undergo spontaneous activation when cultured on standard cell culture plates. While TGFβ had little to no effect on BMAL1 and NR1D1 mRNA expression, the TGFβ-mediated overexpression of ACTA2 and COL1A1 was clearly decreased upon CLOCK inhibition or REV-ERB agonism at both the transcript (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) and protein levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–C). To strengthen these data obtained in a human HSC background, we performed similar western-blot analysis in the SV40 large T-immortalized human HSC cell line LX2 (45). The inhibitory activity of both SR9011 and CLK8 on TGFβ-mediated expression of the prototypical profibrotic protein COL1A1 was again observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). A collagen gel contraction assay demonstrated the ability of these 2 compounds to oppose the TGFβ-induced contraction of LX2 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E), thereby confirming results obtained in a mouse background.

Taken together, these pharmacological interference experiments established the involvement of the REV-ERB/BMAL1:CLOCK axis in regulating the HSC-driven profibrotic response in both human and mouse models.

Interaction of the REV-ERB/BMAL1:CLOCK Axis with the TGFβ Pathway.

To further investigate the cellular mechanisms underlying the functional interplay between the TGFβ and the REV-ERB/BMAL1-CLOCK axis, we first characterized the murine HSC cell line EMS404 as a suitable model system. EMS 404 cells are SV40 large T antigen-immortalized HSCs derived from a C57Bl6 mouse (46) since in our hands, we found primary HSC isolation to be labor-intensive, to show very high intrinsic variability, and are poorly amenable to genetic manipulation (e.g., lentiviral transduction or siRNA transfection). Upon stimulation with various profibrotic cytokines [TGFβ, angiotensin II (ATII), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)], the transcriptional responses of CCGs and profibrotic genes to these treatments varied (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). TGFβ emerged as the most consistent and potent inducer of Serpine1/Pai1, a canonical TGFβ target gene (47), along with other profibrotic genes.

HSC activation is also characterized by a metabolic switch toward aerobic glycolysis to support the energy and biosynthetic demands of activated HSCs during fibrogenesis. EMS404 cells were thus treated with TGFβ and the ECAR was measured using a Seahorse extracellular flux analyzer to monitor proton (H+) efflux as a readout of glycolytic activity. In the presence of rotenone and antimycin A (Rot/AA), which respectively inhibit complexes I and III of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (thereby blocking mitochondrial respiration), TGFβ pretreatment led to a dose-dependent increase in ECAR (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). The addition of 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) markedly reduced ECAR, confirming that the acidification was primarily due to glycolytic proton and lactate export. These findings indicate a robust increase in glycolytic capacity of TGFβ-activated EMS404 cells. In summary, EMS404 cells recapitulate the main metabolic and transcriptomic features of HSC transition toward an activated state.

Synchronized EMS404 cells exhibit cyclic expression of REV-ERBα, BMAL1 and CLOCK after synchronization by a serum shock (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). This prompted us to develop EMS404 cells bearing a stably integrated luciferase reporter gene driven by the mouse Bmal1 promoter (−924; +43 bp). Live recording of the bioluminescence emitted by EMS404-Bmal1Luc cells after synchronization (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E) yielded a cyclic pattern with a period of ≈22 h in control conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and F). Interfering pharmacologically with the clock using CLK8 or SR9011 dose-dependently affected as expected this cyclic pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and F). Interestingly, neither SR9011 nor CLK8 interfered with the activation of the canonical TGFβ signaling pathway in these cells as evidenced by SMAD2&3 phosphorylation levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G), suggesting a direct effect of the clock further downstream of the TGFβ canonical response.

In this cellular context, TGFβ treatment shortened the Bmal1Luc period by 3.8 ± 1.1 h without affecting the amplitude of the bioluminescent signal (Fig. 3A). While increasing REV-ERBα protein level with a moderate effect on cyclic Nr1d1 mRNA levels, TGFβ increased both Bmal1 mRNA and BMAL1 protein expression (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Both CLK8 and SR9011 blunted the TGFβ-induced expression of COL1A1 at the gene and protein levels, as well as the nonrhythmic expression of Timp1 and Serpine1 (Fig. 3 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). This effect was comparable to that of SB431542 (Fig. 3C), a potent blocker of the canonical TGFβ signaling pathway through TGFβ type 1 receptor (TGFBR1) kinase inhibition.

Since individual gene and protein levels do not fully capture the integrated and functional cellular response to profibrotic agents, EMS404 cells were embedded in a collagen lattice to assess their contractile activity in response to TGFβ, a hallmark of hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation. TGFβ increased the contraction of collagen disks over time, which was evaluated in the presence or absence of the test compounds SB431542, SR9011, or CLK8 (Fig. 3E). Although less potent than the reference compound SB431242, both SR9011 and CLK8 significantly reduced TGFβ-stimulated collagen disk contraction. By the end of the linear phase (day 1), comparison of collagen disk areas showed that a TGFβ-induced 82% disk shrinkage, compared to a 32% shrinkage observed with vehicle-exposed cells, indicating both the strong procontractile effect of TGFβ and the baseline activation of EMS404 cells in standard 2D plastic culture. SB431542 effectively suppressed TGFβ-induced contraction, preserving 88% of the disk area, while SR9011 and CLK8 also demonstrated significant anticontractile activity, maintaining 50% and 42% of the original disk surface, respectively.

Thus, BMAL1:CLOCK and REV-ERBα pharmacological modulation controls the fibrotic response in a representative mouse HSC cell line at both the gene expression and functional levels. These conclusions were corroborated by genetic manipulation of BMAL1 and of REV-ERBα levels either by RNA interference (BMAL1) or overexpression (REV-ERBα), which confirmed the favorable, antifibrotic impact of both BMAL1 depletion and REV-ERB induction (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

The REV-ERB/BMAL1:CLOCK Axis Essentially Controls ECM-Related Gene Expression.

To identify the cellular processes triggered by TGFβ and modulated by REV-ERBs or BMAL1:CLOCK, we conducted a comprehensive RNA-seq analysis in EMS404 cells. This analysis compared untreated cells to those treated with various compound combinations (Fig. 4A). From a quantitative perspective, TGFβ induced 1,994 transcripts (FC > 1.2, q < 0.05). SB431542 almost completely suppressed TGFβ-induced gene expression, repressing the expression of 1,992 (99.8%) TGFβ-induced transcripts (Fig. 4B). Both SR9011 and CLK8 treatments exerted a less pronounced yet significant inhibitory effect on gene induction by TGFβ: 614 (31%) transcripts were fully repressed (FC < 1.2) by CLK8 and 752 (37%) by SR9011 (Dataset S1). A biological term enrichment analysis revealed that TGFβ-activated ECM-related genes were significantly suppressed by both CLK8 and SR9011 (Fig. 4C). This highlighted the potent antagonistic effects of these clock-targeting compounds on TGFβ-induced ECM remodeling and suggested additional, TGFβ-independent effects.

Transgelin Is a REV-ERBα Target and Regulates HSC Activation In Vitro.

The results suggested that REV-ERB and BMAL1 modulators may share a common mechanism of action in regulating ECM-related events. We hypothesized that this effect could be driven by a shared set of gene(s) modulated by SR9011, CLK8, or the Bmal1 siRNA. Therefore, we compared genes that were significantly upregulated or downregulated (log2FC > |1|, q-value < 0.05) across all 3 conditions before TGFβ treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Using this stringent threshold, only 28 upregulated genes and 8 downregulated genes met this criterion, thus pointing to potential key mediators underlying the convergent effects of these clock modulators on ECM-related pathways. Predicting that REV-ERBα depletion should induce an opposite effect on the expression of these key players, we performed Tandem Mass Tag (TMT)-based proteomics by LC-MS/MS to identify differentially expressed proteins in naïve or REV-ERBα-depleted EMS404 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). This additional layer of filtering finally identified transgelin (Tagln) as a protein whose expression pattern aligned with responses to all mentioned treatments, thus hinting at a potential role as a downstream effector.

Tagln is an actin-binding protein that regulates cytoskeletal dynamics and controls cell migration and contraction. Although Tagln is co-upregulated with other ECM-related genes such as Col1a1, Acta2, and Dpt, its precise functional role in HSC activation remains poorly defined. We observed that transgelin expression is constant throughout the day (AM vs. PM) in healthy liver but is upregulated in human fibrotic livers (fibrosis stage F > 2; Fig. 5 A and B). Tagln expression was strongly induced in HSCs during the early and later phases of CCl4-induced liver injury in mice (Fig. 5C). Its expression fluctuates over time with very low amplitude in the liver of ad libitum-fed C57Bl6/J male mice (10-fold lower vs. Rev-Erbα and Bmal1) (Fig. 5D) and was not considered as circadian, in agreement with publicly available datasets [CircaDB, (48)] and our human data (Fig. 5A). Tagln expression is induced by TGFβ and repressed by SR9011 and CLK8 in EMS404 cells (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Analysis of published ChIP-Seq data for REV-ERBα, REV-ERBβ, BMAL1 and RNA pol2 in mouse liver (38, 49, 50) detected REV-ERBα binding sites in the vicinity of the Tagln promoter, while no BMAL1 binding sites were detected at this locus (Fig. 5F).

The role of transgelin was investigated in EMS404 cells through small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown (Fig. 6 A–D). This approach efficiently abolished transgelin (Tagln) expression at the mRNA and protein levels and prevented its upregulation in response to TGFβ (Fig. 6B), known to result from phospho-SMAD2/3 binding to its cognate response element (SBE). Interestingly, Tagln silencing also significantly impaired TGFβ-induced Acta2 expression under these conditions (Fig. 6C). In contrast, the expression of Col1a1 and Col3a1, both mechanistic effectors of ECM deposition, was left unchanged, pointing toward an implication in the contractile rather than the fibrogenic phenotype of activated HSCs. To evaluate whether Tagln and Acta2 downregulation might affect cellular contractility, we performed a collagen gel contraction assay as above (Fig. 6D). Tagln knockdown led to a ≈ 50% reduction in TGFβ-induced contractile activity, indicating that TAGLN significantly contributes to the generation of TGFβ-induced tension in stellate cells. A single-cell atlas of mouse fibrotic livers placed Tagln in a so-called “contractility/migration module” together with Acta2 (αSMA) as HSCs transition to myofibroblasts (51). Since the cell attachment assay reflects the early cytoskeletal reorganization of cells which acquire a spread adhesive morphology, thus priming cells for traction-force generation, we used EMS404 cells in such an assay (Fig. 6E). This analysis confirmed the potent action of TGFβ as a priming event of early cellular HSC responses and showed the inhibitory effect of the REV-ERBα agonist SR9011 and of the CLOCK:BMAL1 blocker CLK8 on the TGFβ-induced cellular adhesion.

The comparable transcriptional effect of BMAL1(:CLOCK) inactivation and REV-ERBα agonism, combined with the lack of BMAL1 chromatin binding sites at the Tagln genomic locus, suggested that BMAL1 might regulate Tagln expression indirectly via REV-ERBα. Protein kinases are key regulators of the circadian clock by altering the stability and/or nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling of core clock proteins (16). Glycogen synthase kinase 3-b (GSK3β) and casein kinase 1 epsilon (CK1ε) are well-known modulators of REV-ERBα, influencing its stability and subcellular localization (52, 53). While phosphorylated GSK3β levels remained unchanged following BMAL1 depletion, CK1ε was markedly reduced under these conditions and paralleled the downregulation of TAGLN (Fig. 6F). Based on the hypothesis that CK1ε may limit REV-ERBα nuclear localization, hence its transrepressive activity, we treated EMS404 cells with the dual CK1δ/ε inhibitor PF670462 (54) and assessed Tagln expression (Fig. 6G). PF670462 significantly decreased expression in presence of TGFβ, mimicking the effects of BMAL1 inhibition.

Taken together, these observations identify TAGLN is a key regulator of stellate cell contractility via a BMAL1-CK1ε-REV-ERBα axis (Fig. 6H).

Discussion

The molecular clock plays a central role in liver physiology and pathology. Its function as a key regulator of lipid and glucose metabolism, as well as redox pathways, has been extensively studied. The consequences of clock disruption on these processes have been characterized in both mouse models and human studies (55). Given this regulatory capacity, the causative relationship between circadian disruption and the progression of MASLD toward MASH and hepatic fibrosis has fostered intense scrutiny. Notably, it was suggested that components of the molecular clock may represent promising pharmacological targets for chronic liver diseases (23, 56). However, the extent to which the circadian clock is disrupted in human liver disease, whether this disruption affects nonparenchymal liver cell types and contributes to disease progression remains unclear. Our previous findings demonstrated that CCGs retain differential expression between morning and afternoon liver biopsies in human MASH and fibrosis (33). Using multiparametric propensity score matching, accounting for sex, BMI, diabetes status, and statin use, we show here that CCG mRNAs daily oscillations are altered in human MASH and fibrotic livers (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Interestingly, these alterations are also detected in steatotic livers, indicating that metabolic dysregulations impinge on the molecular clock. This aligns well with reported dysregulations of circadian rhythmicity in livers from high fat-fed mice (57–59). While informative about human pathology, the use of liver biopsies does not allow for distinguishing between different liver cell types, as the transcriptomic signal is likely dominated by hepatocytes.

This question was investigated using the pericentral hepatotoxicant CCl4 in mice from which major liver cell types were separated by cell sorting. This showed that CCG expression is sensitive to chemical liver injury, mostly in HCs and HSCs. Conversely, altering circadian rhythmicity through pharmacological manipulation of CLOCK:BMAL1 or REV-ERBα coincided with decreased expression of TGFβ-induced HSC activation markers in mouse liver explants and human primary HSCs (Figs. 2 and 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This is in line with studies in pulmonary and liver fibrosis showing beneficial effects of REV-ERBα agonism (23, 60–62).

Whole body knockout of Bmal1 reduced liver fibrosis markers in surviving, leptin-deficient mice; however, it is unclear whether this effect is due to beneficial pleiotropic metabolic changes or results from cell-autonomous mechanisms (17). Along this line, BMAL1 overexpression in TGFβ-stimulated human hepatic stellate LX2 cells inhibited glycolysis, proliferation, and phenotypic activation (18). In our study, REV-ERBα activation and BMAL1:CLOCK inactivation limited the transition from a quiescent to an activated phenotype, these processes were molecularly characterized in the well-defined mouse hepatic stellate cell line EMS404. Both REV-ERBα agonism and BMAL1:CLOCK blockade significantly blunted TGFβ-induced profibrotic gene expression and cellular contractility, a hallmark of HSC activation (Fig. 3). Transcriptional profiling of SR9011 or CLK8-treated EMS404 cells revealed their clear TGFβ-antagonizing effect, characterized by a downregulation of regulons associated with ECM remodeling and cellular migration. This pattern mirrored the effects observed with Bmal1 siRNA treatment, ruling out nonspecific effects of CLK8. Interestingly, primary HSCs from mice expressing the dominant-negative mutant of CLOCK ClockΔ19 showed accelerated activation in vitro (19), a finding consistent with our observations. However, the CLOCK-controlled regulon showed only very minor overlap with our REV-ERBα- and Bmal1 siRNA/CLK8 regulon. The data rather pointed to Rho GDP-dissociation inhibitors as potential molecular relays of CLOCKΔ19-mediated defects. This highlights the reciprocal relationship between ECM architecture, the cytoskeleton, and the clock machinery which remains to be fully investigated (63). It also suggests that core clock components probably act through distinct signaling pathways to affect the same biological processes.

The mechanistic links between circadian dysregulation and HSC activation remain poorly defined. Through integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses following treatment with various clock perturbagens, transgelin was identified as a key relay of the BMAL1-REV-ERBα and TGFβ signaling axis in HSCs. Tagln expression is SMAD-dependent in TGFβ-activated HSCs (64, 65) and single cell data show it is induced in CCl4- and bile duct ligation-activated HSCs (51, 66). Its expression correlates with ECM-related genes in multiple cell types (67) and its inhibition has been proposed as a valuable strategy to maintain physiological functions in lung and kidney fibrosis (65, 68). REV-ERBα-mediated repression of Tagln expression is dependent on BMAL1-driven induction of CK1ε, a kinase known to phosphorylate phosphodegrons in several clock proteins including REV-ERBα (69). Tagln knockdown blunted Acta2 induction by TGFβ in our model (Fig. 6C). Although limited, this observation suggests that the roles of transgelin extend beyond being a mere effector of cellular contractility via its interaction with actin filaments. Indeed, emerging evidence suggests it also plays regulatory roles by engaging with signaling pathways such as the PARP–Rho axis, potentially relaying cytoskeletal cues to the nucleus and influencing gene expression (70).

In conclusion, our human data demonstrate that the circadian clock is altered in fibrotic human livers. Our mechanistic studies further show that HSC contractility is controlled by the BMAL1/REV-ERBα signaling axis. We extend the current knowledge by demonstrating that pharmacological interventions targeting REV-ERBα or its upstream regulator BMAL1 interfere with a critical arm of HSC activation, namely the acquisition of increased cellular contractility. Although these interventions also affect other components of HSC activation, such as ECM biosynthesis (Figs. 2C and 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and D), our results also point toward the modulation of mechanotransduction pathways through transgelin, a known promoter of actin filament bundling, cytoskeleton remodeling (71), and cellular contractility (72) and as illustrated in our synthetic model (Fig. 6H). Acquisition of a contractile phenotype is a cardinal feature of the initiation phase of HSC activation, preceding the perpetuation phase that encompasses fibrogenesis per se (73). Strong evidence supports a causal role for cellular contractility in promoting fibrosis, including the contraction-dependent activation of latent TGF-β (74) and the antifibrotic effects of YAP inhibition, a central effector of the cellular contractile program (75). Together, our data identify circadian regulators of the early steps of HSC activation and define targetable nodes that can be exploited to block this pathway, as REV-ERBα pharmacological or genetic manipulation affect fibrosis progression in the CCl4 model (25, 28, 76), although the significance of this pathway in human pathology remains to be fully demonstrated. It also paves the way for exploring alternative treatment options for early fibrosis in liver and could have implications beyond this, as REV-ERB agonists have demonstrated antifibrotic activity in experimental models of cardiac, pulmonary, and renal fibrosis (77–81).
