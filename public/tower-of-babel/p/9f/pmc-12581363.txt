First genome sequences of the dinoflagellate Effrenium voratum strain isolated from the coral Hydnophora exesa in temperate Japanese region
Authors: Yuki Yoshioka, Masanobu Kawachi, Mayumi Kawamitsu, Albert Murzabaev, Noriyuki Satoh, Chuya Shinzato, Eiichi Shoguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDinoflagellates are important unicellular eukaryotes found in freshwater and marine ecosystems. The Family Symbiodiniaceae is well-known as symbiotic algae in various marine organisms, including many endosymbiotic species living in cnidarian cells. In contrast, the genus Effrenium is non-symbiotic, exhibiting traits such as resting cyst formation and heterotrophic feeding. Effrenium strains have been used as controls in symbiosis-related comparative studies. The only described species, E. voratum, is cosmopolitan, and draft genomes of three strains have been reported. However, genomic data from strains originating in temperate Japanese regions are lacking. A high-quality draft genome of an additional E. voratum strain will provide valuable resources for molecular biology of symbiosis and comparative genomics on local adaptation in Symbiodiniaceae.Data descriptionWe describe the first draft genome (version 1) of E. voratum strain NIES-2908, which was isolated from the coral Hydnophora exesa in the temperate Japanese coastal water of Goto islands, Japan. Using PacBio HiFi sequencing, the genome was assembled to a total size of 1,006 Mbp, comprising 6,051 contigs. The GC content was 50.5%, and repetitive sequences accounted for 37.9% of the genome. Gene prediction identified 54,346 protein coding genes, with 68.4% completeness based on the BUSCO alveolate dataset.

Objective

Alveolate dinoflagellates in aquatic environments have diverse species that have survived in free-living, facultative, symbiotic, or parasitic lifestyles as autotrophs, mixotrophs, or heterotrophs since around 650 Ma [1]. Perhaps due to reports of mass coral bleaching events, dinoflagellate Symbiodiniaceae has been well known for the importance of the endosymbiotic-algae abundance in stony corals [2] and many of them live in facultative lifestyles [3]. On the other hand, Effrenium voratum is free-living and has been described as an exceptional species. For examples, the formation of resting cysts [4] and heterotrophic feeding [5] have not been observed in other Symbiodiniaceae, except E. voratum. The free-living strains have been used for comparisons in some symbiotic experiments in cnidarian-Symbiodiniaceae relationship [6]. In addition, the high production of long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids such as docosahexaenoic acid has been reported from the species as a candidate for commercial application [7].

Currently, draft genomes from about 30 dinoflagellate species have been published [8]. Of these, half are from the family Symbiodiniaceae that have been isolated from coral reef invertebrates. Recently, genomes of three strains in Effrenium voratum have been reported [9]. These strains, RCC1521, CCMP3420, and CCMP421, have been isolated from the Mediterranean Sea off Blane (Spain), Eastern North Pacific off Santa Barbara (USA), and Cooks Strait (New Zealand), respectively. This species is cosmopolitan and is also found in western North Pacific [10, 11], but genomic information of the local population is not available. Draft genomes of dinoflagellate Symbiodiniaceae strains from subtropical Japanese region have been reported [12, 13]. While the presence of reef-building corals in temperate Japanese regions has also been observed [14–16], no genomic sequences of Symbiodiniacea from such regions are available. Here, the aim of this study is to generate the draft genome of the E. voratum strain from western North Pacific (temperate Japanese region).

Data description

The strain NIES-2908 of E. voratum was originally isolated from the coral Hydnophora exesa in the temperate coastal waters of Goto islands, Japan. Genome and Iso-Seq sequencing were conducted using the PacBio Sequel IIe system, and illumina RNA-Seq data were also obtained (Table 1—Data set 1).

A schematic overview of the genome assembly and gene annotation workflow is shown in Fig. S1 (Table 1—Data file 1). HiFi reads potentially derived from non-eukaryotic organisms were identified using KRAKEN v2.1.3 [20] with its standard database and removed as in Yoshioka et al. [21]. The genome size was estimated to be ~ 905 Mb using GenomeScope v2 [22] (Table 1—Data file 2). Cleaned HiFi reads were assembled with Hifiasm v0.19.8 [23], and error-correction was carried out using Inspector v1.0.1 [24]. Circular and organelle-like contigs were excluded. Repetitive elements were identified de novo with RepeatModeler v2.0.4 [25] with the “-LTRStruct” option. Both de novo and known repeats from the RepeatMasker database were combined and were soft-masked using RepeatMasker v4.1.5 [26]. Additional simple tandem repeats were detected using TANTAN v49 [27].

Gene prediction was performed using BRAKER pipeline v3.0.8 [28–30] on a soft-masked genome, incorporating transcriptomes (Iso-Seq and illumina sequences), and proteomes obtained from OrthoDB v11 (Alveolata and Stramenopiles), as well as several dinoflagellate species (Table 1—Data set 1). BRAKER was run independently for each transcriptome data type, and the resulting gene models were merged using TSEBRA [31]. Polishing of gene models were performed using PASA v2.5.3 [32], with Iso-Seq data and transcriptome assembly generated by Trinity v2.15.1 [33]. Non-canonical splice sites were permitted during gene prediction and polishing. Predicted genes larger than 100 bp were retained. Each gene was named using the format ‘g’ plus a number from one end of each contig, e.g., c0001.g1 is the first gene of contig c0001. Gene model completeness was assessed with BUSCO v5.7.0 [34] using the Alveolata odb10 dataset.

To ensure taxonomic identification, we conducted a molecular phylogenetic analysis based on 18S rRNA. Dinoflagellate 18S rRNA were retrieved from LaJeunesse et al. [35], and the corresponding region of NIES-2908 was retrieved via BLAST search [36]. Nucleotide sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.520 [37], and poorly aligned regions were trimmed with ClipKIT v2.2.4 [38]. Maximum likelihood analyses were performed using IQ-TREE v2.2.2.6 [39] with ModelFinder [40] employed to determine the best-fit substitution model (-M MF; -B 1000). Pelagodinium spp. were used as the outgroup. The resulting phylogeny showed that NIES-2908 clustered with other E. voratum strains (Table 1—Data file 3).

The total assembly size of the strain NIES-2908 was 1,006 Mb, with contig N50 of 357.9 kb (Table 1—Data set 1). Genomic statistics were compared with three previously reported E. voratum genomes, showing comparable assembly size, GC content, and repeat abundance (Table 1—Data set 1). Gene model completeness assessment revealed that 68.4% of the alveolate gene set was complete. (Table 1—Data set 1). These genomic data will serve as a valuable resource for investigating adaptation to temperate environment and exploring the genomic basis of unique traits among E. voratum strains.

Limitations

In this study, the genome assembly of the NIES-2908 strain was predicted to contain 54,362 protein-coding genes (Table 1—Data file 4 and Data file 5). While the completeness score of the gene models is comparable to those of gene models in three reported genomes from different strains (Table 1—Data set 1), the total gene numbers are notably higher. This discrepancy may be due to the inclusion of potential pseudogenes, which were computationally filtered out in the previous work [9]. Pseudogenes with important biological functions have been identified by molecular experiments in mammalians [41]. In consideration of limitations of genome annotations, we did not perform such analysis in this study.
