Centromeric Sequences in Ogataea polymorpha Genome Enable Development of Stable Multigene Expression Plasmid Tools
Authors: Yanfei Cheng, Yuanyuan Shi, Ning Sun, Xin Zhang, Mengwei Sun, Xiuping He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCentromeres are the chromosomal sites at which the kinetochore forms and attaches to spindle microtubules, directing chromosome segregation. Plasmids based on centromeres can maintain stability and distribute accurately during cell division, supporting them as effective genetic tools for research and biotechnological applications. Here, the centromeric regions on seven chromosomes of Ogataea polymorpha, a methylotrophic non‐conventional yeast with great potential in biotechnology, were located by ChIP‐seq with the native Cse4. The actual centromeric sequences of chromosomes 1, 2 and 5 were characterised, which are very different from those of other eukaryotes and each unique. Although long terminal repeats (LTR) were found in these centromeres, they are ‘solo LTR elements’ and not important for the function of centromeres. Hence, the O. polymorpha centromeres were categorised into small regional centromeres. O. polymorpha centromeric plasmids were constructed for the first time, which exhibit high genetic stability and compatibility. Application potential for multigene or multicopy expression was validated by the production of uricase and triterpene squalene. This research elucidates the structural features of O. polymorpha centromeres and constructs new, stable centromeric plasmids, expanding the phenomenal diversity of centromeres and providing a powerful genetic toolbox for multi‐node and multiple‐layered engineering of cell metabolism and physiological functions.

Introduction

Methanol, the simplest saturated monohydric alcohol, can be massively produced from byproducts of the coal chemical industry, natural gas and CO2 (Zhou et al. 2018). Methanol can provide more driving force for the production of high‐value and complex molecules due to its higher reducing power than the sugar‐based carbon sources, which makes it a widely available and cost‐effective chemical feedstock (Whitaker et al. 2015; Price et al. 2016). In nature, there are a class of natural methylotrophic microorganisms, which can utilise methanol as carbon and energy sources. Among them, Ogataea polymorpha (also known as Hansenula polymorpha) is a methylotrophic non‐conventional yeast. The specific properties of O. polymorpha, such as thermotolerance, wide substrate spectrums, as well as fitness to harsh industrial conditions, make it a great potential cell factory for the advanced biomanufacturing using various feedstocks including methanol (Gellissen 2002; Xie et al. 2024). Efficient production of β‐lactam antibiotics (Gidijala et al. 2007), glutathione (Ubiyvovk et al. 2011), fuel ethanol (Dmytruk et al. 2017; Vasylyshyn et al. 2024) and terpenoids (Ye et al. 2023, 2024) has been achieved in this non‐conventional yeast. Moreover, O. polymorpha is also a highly competitive candidate to produce heterologous preventive or therapeutic proteins (Manfrão‐Netto et al. 2019; Tsuda and Nonaka 2024; Xie et al. 2024). Currently, various recombinant proteins have been expressed in O. polymorpha, and some of them such as Hepatitis B vaccines, hirudin, insulin and IFNa‐2a Reiferon are commercially available (http://www.dynavax.com/about‐us/dynavax‐gmbh/; Manfrão‐Netto et al. 2019). Additionally, the characteristics of O. polymorpha in methanol metabolism, peroxisome biogenesis and nitrate assimilation make it an ideal model organism for revealing the underlying mechanisms of these biological processes (Gellissen 2002; Siverio 2002; van der Klei and Veenhuis 2006; Nagotu et al. 2008; Martin et al. 2011). However, although the genome sequences are available, functional annotation of many genes and the regulatory mechanism of native metabolism in O. polymorpha are severely required to be revealed further (Saraya et al. 2012; Manfrão‐Netto et al. 2019; Manfrao‐Netto et al. 2021).

No matter for the fundamental research or the biotechnological applications, various degrees of genetic engineering are essential. Several genetic tools, such as the construction of integration vectors for the expression or deletion of target genes, CRISPR‐Cas9 genome editing technology and selective marker rescue, have been developed for O. polymorpha in past years (Saraya et al. 2012; Manfrão‐Netto et al. 2019; Tsuda and Nonaka 2024; Jia et al. 2025; Zou et al. 2025). Shuttle vectors, being able to replicate in a cloning host and the host of interest, are very useful tools for genetic manipulation in microbiology. Episomal plasmids have been constructed for O. polymorpha; however, they are unable to stably persist (Sohn et al. 1996, 1999; Kim et al. 2003). Due to the lack of stable extrachromosomally replicating plasmids for O. polymorpha, expression vectors are generally integrated into the genome of the host cells (Saraya et al. 2012). O. polymorpha is a typical yeast strain with non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ) as the major DNA repair mechanism, which results in a high frequency of random integration within the host genome, leading to undetectable alterations in genetic background and hindering the accurate characterisation of the function and effect of the genetic manipulation. Moreover, large homologous arms (about 1000 kb) are required for double crossover recombination, which is also an inevitable challenge for rapidly and efficiently engineering O. polymorpha.

Centromeric plasmids were developed to provide stable, low‐copy‐number vector systems in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Mereshchuk et al. 2001). These plasmids contain the centromeric sequences from S. cerevisiae , which enable the plasmids to attach to the spindle apparatus during mitosis, supporting the segregational and structural stability (Gnügge and Rudolf 2017). In S. cerevisiae , the centromeres of all the chromosomes have been characterised, which are highly conserved, typically spanning about 125 base pairs and containing three conserved elements (CDEs) including CDEI (an approximately 8‐base pair sequence), CDEII (an AT‐rich region of about 78–86 base pairs), and CDEIII (an approximately 26‐base pair sequence critical for centromere function) (Mehta et al. 2010). The centromeres of Candida albicans , Schizosaccharomyces pombe and Pichia pastoris (Komagataella phaffii) have also been identified, which are much larger and more complex than those in S. cerevisiae (Sanyal et al. 2004; Allshire and Karpen 2008; Coughlan et al. 2016; Nakamura et al. 2018). The central core regions essential for centromere function in these yeast species span approximately 1–7 kb and are all different and unique without obvious CDEs. All of the eukaryotic kinetochores assemble at centromeres by interacting with a variant histone H3 known as centromere protein A (CENP‐A) in vertebrates or as Cse4 in S. cerevisiae and other point‐centromere yeast (Wisniewski et al. 2014; Deng et al. 2023). Here, we identified the centromeric regions of O. polymorpha for the first time by immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP‐seq) with the native Cse4 protein, and obtained the exact centromeric sequences by the gradual truncation. Furthermore, extrachromosomal centromeric plasmids were constructed and characterised for their genetic stability and compatibility. Finally, the application potentials of the plasmids for recombinant protein production and terpene biosynthesis were further investigated.

Results

Location of Putative Centromeric Regions in Genome of O. Polymorpha

ChIP‐seq is an effective technique for the investigation of the interaction of protein with DNA, which has been widely used to track the target sequences on the genome (Sanyal et al. 2004; Hanson et al. 2014; Kobayashi et al. 2015; Chatterjee et al. 2016; Tu et al. 2020). To locate the centromeric regions of O. polymorpha, yeast strain CSE4‐3HA was constructed, in which the native CSE4 was replaced by the strong constitutive promoter P GAP1 and the coding sequence of CSE4 with a 3 × HA tag (Figure 1A). ChIP‐seq of strain CSE4‐3HA was performed to identify sequences associated with centromeric nucleosomes containing histone Cse4. The Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) visualisation tool was used to analyse the binding peak map of Cse4 and ChIP‐seq sequencing readings in the genome combined with genome annotation information of O. polymorpha. A total of 55 peaks were revealed in the IP samples, with an average length of 6256.04 bp. Several peak signals with various intensities were distributed across each chromosome, but there was only a single strongest signal with the longest sequence on each of the seven chromosomes. All of these regions range from about 7.4 kb to 14 kb in size, located in regions with high AT content (more than 60% compared to the 52.2% of the genome), sparse gene distribution, and are non‐transcribed, suggesting the putative centromeric regions (Figure 1B). Interestingly, all of the putative centromeric regions of six chromosomes are located in the intergenic spacer region and continuous except chromosome 2, which is interrupted by a 4 kb DNA sequence lacking Cse4 binding signals (Figure 1C).

(B) The binding peak map of Cse4 in the genome. (C) The schematic illustrations of the location of the putative centromere regions (CEN) of seven chromosomes.

Identification of the Sequence and Function of Centromeric Regions in O. Polymorpha Chromosomes

According to the result of ChIP‐seq, DNA fragments corresponding to the putative centromeric regions of chromosome 1, 2 and 5 were amplified from the genome of O. polymorpha HP1. For chromosome 1, the full‐length sequence of the putative centromeric region (CEN1) is about 9.6 kb, which was systematically dissected into five segments and designated as CEN1‐1, CEN1‐2, CEN1‐3, CEN1‐4 and CEN1‐5, respectively (Figure 2A). These segments were cloned into the plasmid pGG, resulting in five CEN1 serial plasmids ranged from pGG‐CEN1‐1 to pGG‐CEN1‐5 (Table S1 and Figure S1). The five plasmids were transformed into O. polymorpha Hu11. However, transformants were generated only from plasmid pGG‐CEN1‐1 and pGG‐CEN1‐3 with transformation efficiencies of 4.60 × 102 and 1.59 × 103 CFU/μg respectively (Figure 2B), suggesting the location of the core sequence of CEN1 in the region of CEN1‐3. To determine the core centromeric sequence in chromosome 1, six additional plasmids were constructed and transformed (Figure 2A, Table S1 and Figure S1). Among these plasmids, only pGG‐CEN1‐11 and pGG‐CEN1‐111 successfully yielded transformants, with transformation efficiencies of 2.00 × 102 and 5.27 × 102 CFU/μg respectively (Figure 2B). These results confirmed the 1010 bp of core sequence of CEN1, which supports the replication and segregation of plasmids during cell division.

The centromeric regions of chromosome 2 (CEN2) and chromosome 5 (CEN5) were identified using the same strategy as for CEN1. In the about 14 kb putative centromeric region of chromosome 2, the 5512 bp CEN2‐1 served the function of the centromere due to the successful transformation with an efficiency of 1.95 × 102 CFU/μg (Figure 2C,D). Subsequently, a series of deletions was performed on the CEN2‐1 sequence, resulting in the construction of seven additional plasmids for transformation (Figure 2C and Figure S1). DNA fragments covering the 1304 bp of CEN2‐11 played the role of the centromere (Figure 2D), indicating the location of the core sequence of CEN2 in CEN2‐11. In the about 8.2 kb putative centromeric region of chromosome 5, the 2615 bp of CEN5‐1 supported the efficient transformation of plasmid pGG‐CEN5‐1 with an efficiency of 1.89 × 103 CFU/μg comparable to that of the full length CEN5 (Figure 2E,F). After a series of truncations of CEN5‐1, the core sequence of CEN5 was identified as the 440 bp of CEN5‐1111, which endowed the plasmid pGG‐CEN5‐1111 with 5.20 × 102 CFU/μg of transformation efficiency (Figure 2E,F).

Plasmids were extracted from the corresponding yeast transformants and recovered in Escherichia coli , supporting their extrachromosomal existence in yeast cells. Furthermore, the copy number of the above plasmids in yeast cells was analysed by measuring the ratio of promoter P GAP1 and MOX coding sequence using the genome of strain HP1 as the control. Each of these plasmids carrying the centromeric regions of chromosome 1, 2 and 5 maintained a single copy in yeast cells of O. polymorpha, consistent with the characteristics of centromeric plasmids.

Function of the Long Terminal Repeats (LTR) in Centromeres

Motif analysis of the centromeric sequences CEN1‐3, CEN2‐14 and CEN5‐1 on the MEME website (http://meme‐suite.org/index.html) revealed the highly similar long terminal repeats (LTR) with sizes from 241 to 294 bp (Figure S2). Four copies of LTR, two copies of LTR, and three copies of LTR were found in the full length of CEN1, CEN2 and CEN5 respectively (Figure 3A). Sequence alignment showed that the LTR of different centromeres had high identity with the highest of 90.54% (Figure 3B and Figure S2). These direct or inverted repeats were ‘solo LTR elements’ belonging to the Tpa5 family (Neuvéglise et al. 2002; Lynch et al. 2010). To clarify the role of LTR in centromeres, plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐LTR and pGG‐CEN5‐LTR containing only the LTR sequences of CEN1 or CEN5 were constructed (Figure S1). Only a few transformants could be acquired from the LTR plasmids with very low transformation efficiency of approximately 10 ± 5 CFU/μg (Figure 3C), which might be due to the 76% sequence similarity of LTR with an HARS sequence of shuttle vector pYT3 (Accession No. AF347016.1). Further, centromeric plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3‐ltr, pGG‐CEN2‐14‐ltr and pGG‐CEN5‐1‐ltr with deletion of the LTR sequences of CEN1‐3, CEN2‐14 and CEN5‐1 were constructed (Figure S1). The transformation efficiencies of these plasmids were found to be comparable to the plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3, pGG‐CEN2‐14 and pGG‐CEN5‐1 (Figure 3C). The affinity of CEN1‐3 and CEN1‐3‐ltr with the histone Cse4 was further analysed using microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay, which resulted in a Kd value of 1975.38 ± 53.23 nM for CEN1‐3 and a Kd value of 415.09 ± 154.71 nM for CEN1‐3‐ltr (Figure S3). The results suggest that the deletion of the LTR sequences might increase the binding efficiency of Cse4 to the centromere, but had no obvious effect on the function of centromeres.

Highly Genetic Stability of Centromeric Plasmids in Extrachromosomal Form

Yeast strains containing plasmids carrying various DNA fragments of centromeres were cultivated in 2 mL of SD medium and SD‐Ura medium respectively for ten continuous passages as described in Section 4. Auxanogram of single colonies on SD and SD‐Ura plates indicated that the centromeric plasmids maintained high genetic stability under both the selective and non‐selective conditions. Among the truncated centromeres, CEN1‐3, CEN2‐14 and CEN5‐1 endowed the plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3, pGG‐CEN2‐14 and pGG‐CEN5‐1 with the highest stability (Figure S4). Three single colonies of each yeast strain were randomly selected for extraction of yeast plasmid. Recovery of yeast plasmid in E. coli and sequence analysis confirmed that the plasmids remained in extrachromosomal form in yeast cells. Further characterisation of the third generation of yeast cells by flow cytometry confirmed the contribution of different centromeres to the genetic stability of plasmids (Figure 4 and Figure S5). The analysis of GFP fluorescence in 3 × 104 single cells for each sample showed that 96.4%, 97.9% and 99.9% of cells of strains HP1‐CEN1‐3, HP1‐CEN2‐14 and HP1‐CEN5‐1 were GFP‐positive under non‐selective conditions, equivalent to 96.9%, 98.4% and 100% of that of the positive control HP1‐GG with integration of the GFP expression cassette in the genome. Under the selective condition, the percentages of GFP‐positive cells of strains HP1‐CEN1‐3, HP1‐CEN2‐14 and HP1‐CEN5‐1 were equivalent to 98.1%, 98.5% and 100% of that of HP1‐GG (Figure 4A,B). For yeast strains containing plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3‐ltr, pGG‐CEN2‐14‐ltr and pGG‐CEN5‐1‐ltr, the deletion of LTR in CEN1‐3 had no effect on the genetic stability of yeast plasmids, while the deletion of LTR in CEN2‐14 and CEN5‐1 resulted in a very minor reduction in genetic stability compared to the intact plasmids pGG‐CEN2‐14 and pGG‐CEN5‐1 (Figure 4B,C). These results show that CEN1‐3, CEN2‐14 and CEN5‐1 can be used as the key centromeric elements to maintain the stable replication and inheritance of plasmids, which might support the plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3, pGG‐CEN2‐14 and pGG‐CEN5‐1 as potentially effective genetic tools for fundamental research and engineering yeast cell factories for advanced biomanufacturing.

High Compatibility of the Centromeric Plasmids in Yeast Cells

To evaluate the potential application of the centromeric plasmids in genetic manipulation of multiple genes, the compatibility of different centromeric plasmids was investigated. Plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐3‐RFP, pZeo‐CEN2‐14‐BFP and pGG‐CEN5‐1 (Table S1 and Figure S6) were co‐transformed in pairs or in triplet into yeast strain Hu11‐leu2 to generate strains C1R‐C2B, C1R‐C5G, C2B‐C5G and C1R‐C2B‐C5G, respectively. After ten passages of growth under both selective and non‐selective conditions, the growth of single colonies on SD, SD‐Ura, SD‐Leu and SD‐Ura‐Leu media supplemented with or without zeocin was compared and analysed statistically, which demonstrated that the different plasmids coexisted in yeast cells under non‐selective conditions with the genetic stabilities ranging from 92.0% to 100.0% (Figure 5A). Fluorescence analyses of yeast cells from the first generation to the third generation were further performed by flow cytometry (Figure 5B–E). After three generations of culture under non‐selective conditions, 99.1% of RFP‐positive cells, 92.9% of BFP‐positive cells or 85.5% of both RFP‐positive and BFP‐positive cells were detected in the 3 × 104 single cells of strain C1R‐C2B (Figure 5B). 98.9% of the single cells of strain C1R‐C5G were both RFP‐positive and GFP‐positive (Figure 5C). 86.7% of the third generation cells of strain C2B‐C5G were both BFP‐positive and GFP‐positive (Figure 5D), while 78.9% of the third generation cells of strain C1R‐C2B‐C5G were simultaneously RFP‐positive, BFP‐positive and GFP‐positive (Figure 5E). Under the selective conditions, 84.5%, 99.6%, 90.2% and 96.5% of the third generation single cells of C1R‐C2B, C1R‐C5G, C2B‐C5G and C1R‐C2B‐C5G were positive in fluorescence analyses. The copy number of each plasmid in the third generation of culture was conformable to the fluorescence analyses (Table 1). These results indicate the stable persistence of different plasmids with various centromeres in the same yeast cells, suggesting the high compatibility of the different centromeric plasmids.

The Centromeric Plasmids for Recombinant Protein Production

The high genetic stability and compatibility of the different centromeric plasmids might provide effective tools for the production of proteins and chemicals. To assess the application in protein production, the plasmids were used to express uricase, an important medical enzyme used in clinical analysis for tests of urate level and as therapeutic drugs for gout, hyperuricemia, and tumour lysis syndrome (Oldfield and Perry 2006; Chen et al. 2008). Plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐UOX, pZeo‐CEN2‐UOX and pURA3‐CEN5‐UOX were constructed by replacing the expression cassettes of RFP, BFP and GFP in plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐3‐RFP, pZeo‐CEN2‐14‐BFP and pGG‐CEN5‐1 with the expression cassette P GAP1 ‐UOX‐T MOX respectively (Figure S7). After the introduction of these plasmids alone or collectively into strain Hu11‐leu2, recombinant strain HP‐C5‐UOX containing pURA3‐CEN5‐UOX, strain HP‐C15‐UOX containing both pLEU2‐CEN1‐UOX and pURA3‐CEN5‐UOX, strain HP‐C125‐UOX containing pLEU2‐CEN1‐UOX, pZeo‐CEN2‐UOX and pURA3‐CEN5‐UOX simultaneously, were constructed. After cultivation in 5 mL of YPD medium for 48 h, 17.0 U/L, 33.6 U/L and 43.1 U/L of uricase were produced by the single copy, dual copies, and three copies of the UOX gene (Figure 6A), indicating the proportional relationship between uricase activity and the doses of the UOX expression cassettes. No obvious difference in cell growth was observed among these yeast strains (Figure 6A), suggesting no physiological burden resulted from multiple plasmids. These results demonstrate the potential of these centromeric plasmids for the expression of multiple copies of target genes.

The Centromeric Plasmids for Terpene Synthesis

For efficient synthesis of chemicals by microbial cell factories, the expression of multiple genes is generally required to optimise the native metabolic pathways or construct novel pathways. Terpenes are a class of value‐added compounds with multiple biological activities, which involve long and complex synthetic pathways (Frey et al. 2024). The biosynthesis of polyunsaturated triterpenoid squalene was used to evaluate the application of centromeric plasmids in chemical synthesis. The heterologous genes encoding 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGR), the key rate‐limiting enzyme of the terpene biosynthetic pathway (Zhou et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2022), were expressed in strain Hu11‐leu2 under the control of promoter P GAP1 in centromeric plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐tHMG1, pLEU2‐CEN1‐EfHMG1 and pURA3‐CEN5‐EfHMG1 (Figure S8). Separate expression of a single copy of tHMG1 encoding the truncated form of HMGR of S. cerevisiae and EfHMG1 encoding the HMGR of Enterococcus faecalis in strain HP‐C1‐tH and HP‐C1‐EfH led to 39.7% and 2.9 times increases in squalene production when compared to the host strain Hu11‐leu2 (Figure 6B). When the tHMG1 and EfHMG1 were coexpressed in the form of plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐tHMG1 and pURA3‐CEN5‐EfHMG1, strain HP‐C15‐tHEfH yielded 68.9 mg/L squalene, indicating 4.6 times the squalene production of that of strain Hu11‐leu2 (Figure 6B). Furthermore, strain HP‐C15‐2EfH carrying dual copies of EfHMG1 through plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐EfHMG1 and pURA3‐CEN5‐EfHMG1 produced 115.4 mg/L squalene after 24 h of cultivation, which was 6.7, 1.0 and 0.7 times higher than those of strains Hu11‐leu2, HP‐C1‐EfH and HP‐C15‐tHEfH respectively, while no obvious influence on cell growth was observed (Figure 6B). The significant enhancement of squalene biosynthesis in strains HP‐C15‐tHEfH and HP‐C15‐2EfH confirmed further the validity of these centromeric plasmids for engineering the expression of multiple genes.

Discussion

Genetically manipulation and genomic editing are enabling technologies for biological fundamental research and biologics manufacturing. Apart from efficient control of the expression of multiple genes by chromosomal integration approaches, the extra‐chromosomal plasmids are also powerful genetic tools in the research of biological sciences and metabolic engineering for the renewable production of bulk and fine chemicals (Fang et al. 2011; Galanie et al. 2015; Gnügge et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2020; Li et al. 2025; Liang et al. 2025). For the model yeast S. cerevisiae , various plasmid systems, such as yeast episomal plasmids (YEps), yeast centromeric plasmids (YCps) and yeast integrative plasmids (YIps), can be used for genetic manipulation (Gnügge and Rudolf 2017). However, there are lacking plasmid‐based genetic tools for the non‐conventional yeast O. polymorpha (Saraya et al. 2012). Autonomously replicating sequences (ARS) and centromeric sequences (CEN) are the characteristic elements of centromeric plasmids, which guarantee the stable maintenance and correct distribution of plasmids during cell division, supporting the persistence in yeast cells like mini‐chromosomes (Westermann et al. 2007; Gnügge and Rudolf 2017). Hence, the centromeric plasmids seem to have the most general applicability.

Centromeres are the eukaryotic chromosomal sites at which the kinetochore forms and attaches to spindle microtubules, directing the chromosome segregation along the mitotic spindle apparatus in mitosis and meiosis (Westermann et al. 2007; Talbert and Henikoff 2020). Clark and Carbon isolated the first functional eukaryotic centromere DNA from the budding yeast S. cerevisiae , which could be limited to 125 bp and enables the plasmid to function as a chromosome both mitotically and meiotically (Clarke and Carbon 1980). Up to now, a variety of eukaryotic centromeres have been revealed that vary largely in size (from approximately 100 bp to megabases) and organisation. Although unique sequences, transposons or highly repeated sequences generally exist in centromeric regions, no conservative or consistent elements have been found in the centromeres of different species (Sanyal et al. 2004; Allshire and Karpen 2008; Mehta et al. 2010; Coughlan et al. 2016; Talbert and Henikoff 2020). Ty5‐like retrotransposon clusters were found in the genome of O. polymorpha, but centromeric DNA sequences remained unknown (Ravin et al. 2013; Riley et al. 2016; Chang et al. 2022). In‐depth data analysis facilitates the prediction of centromeric regions, while the exact localization of centromeres requires further determination through various approaches (Sanyal et al. 2004; Varoquaux et al. 2015; Coughlan et al. 2016; Nakamura et al. 2018). In this study, the native histone H3 variant Cse4 was used to locate the centromeric regions in different chromosomes of O. polymorpha by immunoprecipitation sequencing. Sequences ranging from 7.4 to 14 kb in length were identified from each of the seven chromosomes, which are AT‐rich, almost gene‐free and non‐transcribed except that of chromosome 2, consistent with the characteristic features of centromeric regions (Talbert and Henikoff 2020). After a series of truncations, the centromeric function of these regions from chromosomes 1, 2 and 5 was confirmed, and the core sequences of centromeres were determined as 1010 bp of CEN1‐111, 1304 bp of CEN2‐11 and 440 bp of CEN5‐1111 with AT contents of 62.4%, 64.5% and 63.9% respectively (Figure 2), which are sufficient for plasmid replication and segregation during mitosis. All of the O. polymorpha centromeres are very different from other eukaryotes and each unique. LTRs ranged in size from 241 to 294 bp with high sequence similarity (up to 90.54%) were found both in the full length and the truncated forms of CEN1, CEN2 and CEN5 (Figure S2 and Figure 3), conforming to the genome analysis (Ravin et al. 2013). However, these LTRs are ‘solo LTR elements’ belonging to the Tpa5 family, an element homologous to Ty5 (Ty5‐like) (Neuvéglise et al. 2002; Lynch et al. 2010), and have no significant effect on the function of centromeres. Hence, the O. polymorpha centromeres might be categorised into small regional centromeres rather than point centromeres or transposon‐rich centromeres due to the structural features and the loss of di‐ or tri‐methylation of lysine 9 of histone Cse4 (Coughlan et al. 2016; Talbert and Henikoff 2020).

The E. coli and O. polymorpha shuttle plasmids based on the identified centromeric sequences were constructed, which demonstrate high genetic stability whether under selective or non‐selective conditions when compared to the positive control strain HP1‐GG carrying a chromosomally integrated GFP expression cassette. Among these centromeric plasmids, pGG‐CEN1‐3, pGG‐CEN2‐14 and pGG‐CEN5‐1 exhibited excellent traits in both transformation efficiency and genetic stability (more than 95% under the nonselective condition) (Figure 2, Figure S4 and Figure 4). In the methylotrophs clade, the centromeres of P. pastoris were identified and used to construct plasmids. P. pastoris strain harbouring plasmid pUC19‐Cen2‐EGFP with the full‐length Cen2 (6655 bp) exhibited a genetic stability of 84.14% after 24 h culture under selective pressure, and 14.97% after 24 h culture under non‐selective condition (Nakamura et al. 2018). The P. pastoris centromeres are structurally defined, containing a nonrepetitive central core region (1 kb) flanked by two long inverted repeats (2‐kb of IR) (Coughlan et al. 2016; Nakamura et al. 2018). Thus, the huge disparity in genetic stability between centromeric plasmids of P. pastoris and O. polymorpha might result from the very different structural features of their centromeres.

With the rapid development of metabolic engineering and synthetic biology, engineering long and complex regulation networks or metabolic pathways involving multiple genes is increasingly common (Srinivasan and Smolke 2020; Frey et al. 2024). The high transformation efficiency and genetic stability suggest that plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3, pGG‐CEN2‐14 and pGG‐CEN5‐1 (Appendix S1–S3) are the effective toolboxes for efficient and controllable expression of multiple genes. Apart from multigene constructs on a single plasmid, it may be advantageous to split the genetic load by various plasmids, which allows the separate and fine control of the expression of different genes and avoids the unusually large plasmids (Behrendt et al. 2024). To maintain two or more plasmids simultaneously in one strain, the plasmids must be compatible with each other. In this study, the compatibility of the three plasmids was examined by flow cytometry through the combined expression of fluorescent protein BFP, RFP and GFP, and by spot assay of single colonies on different media plates due to the selective markers on the plasmids. High compatibility was observed for these plasmids in pairs or in triplets even under the nonselective conditions for more than 72 generations (Figure 5). Among these combinations, groups of plasmids carrying CEN1‐3 and CEN5‐1 exhibited the highest compatibility (98.9% under nonselective conditions and 99.6% under selective conditions), while some reductions in compatibility were observed in groups containing the CEN2‐14 plasmid. The exact reasons for this reduction are not yet clear. The centromere sizes of CEN1‐3, CEN2‐14 and CEN5‐1 are 3121, 2553 and 2615 bp respectively. Moreover, the genetic stability of plasmid pGG‐CEN2‐14 is almost the same as the plasmid pGG‐CEN2‐4 containing 3538 bp of CEN2‐4. Hence, we speculate that the lower compatibility of CEN2‐14 with CEN1‐3 and CEN5‐1 might be mainly related to the sequence characteristics rather than the centromere sizes. The stable coexistence of plasmids makes the genetic engineering of O. polymorpha even more flexible, providing more options in adjusting copy numbers and expression levels of various genes. These potentials were confirmed by the production of protein of uricase and chemical of squalene (Figure 6). Furthermore, the lesser compatibility of the CEN2‐14 plasmid might be applied in scenarios where the specific genes are desired only under specific conditions or timeframes.

In conclusion, the centromeres of O. polymorpha on seven chromosomes were identified by ChIP‐seq with the native histone H3 variant Cse4, and the full‐length sequences and the core sequences of CEN1, CEN2 and CEN5 were characterised, which are very different from each other and other eukaryotes. Although LTRs belonging to the Tpa5 family were found in these centromeric sequences, they are ‘solo LTR elements’ and may not be important for the function of centromeres. Hence, the O. polymorpha centromeres may be speculated to be small regional centromeres. A series of new O. polymorpha centromeric plasmids were constructed based on CEN1, CEN2 and CEN5, among which plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3, pGG‐CEN2‐14 and pGG‐CEN5‐1 exhibit high genetic stability and compatibility, facilitating the genetic manipulation of O. polymorpha for basic research and advanced biomanufacturing. Furthermore, the combination of the plasmid toolboxes with genome engineering tools will offer more powerful and flexible options for the synthetic biology and metabolic engineering‐driven construction of microbial cell factories, which will be explored systematically and widely in further research.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Culture Conditions

O. polymorpha and E. coli strains used in this work are listed in Table 2. Yeast cells were generally grown at 37°C in the standard YPD medium containing 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone and 20 g/L glucose or the synthetic minimal SD medium consisting of 6.7 g/L yeast nitrogen base without amino acids (YNB), 20 g/L glucose. To screen or analyse yeast transformants, SD media containing 300 μg/mL of zeocin (SD‐Zeo), 20 mg/L uracil (SD‐Ura), 40 mg/L leucine (SD‐Leu) or 20 mg/L uracil and 40 mg/L leucine (SD‐Ura‐Leu) were also used. E. coli cells were grown at 37°C in Luria‐Bertani (LB) medium containing 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L peptone, 10 g/L NaCl and 100 μg/mL of ampicillin, which was supplemented when necessary.

Plasmid and Strain Construction

All plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. For ChIP‐seq with Cse4, the expression cassette P GAP1 ‐CSE4‐3HA‐T ADH1 covering the GAP1 promoter P GAP1 , the coding sequence of O. polymorpha CSE4 with 3 × HA tag and S. cerevisiae ADH1 terminator T ADH1 was synthesised (Nanjing Genscript Biotechnology Co. Ltd). Zeocin‐resistant cassette zeoR was obtained by PCR from plasmid pMOXZα‐A (Chen et al. 2008). DNA fragment carrying P GAP1 ‐CSE4‐3HA‐T ADH1 ‐zeoR flanked by homologous arms was constructed by fusion PCR and used to replace the native CSE4 locus of O. polymorpha HP1 by double crossover homologous recombination to generate strain CSE4‐3HA.

For construction of O. polymorpha centromeric plasmids, E. coli vector pUC19 was used as the basic backbone. The URA3 gene encoding orotidine‐5′‐phosphate decarboxylase and promoter P GAP1 were amplified from the genome of the strain HP1. The DNA fragment covering green fluorescent protein (GFP) coding sequence gfp5 and terminator T SUP4 were amplified from plasmid pYC‐AGA (Zhou et al. 2021). The DNA fragments were ligated with the pUC19 using the Uniclone One Step Seamless Cloning Kit (Beijing Genesand Biotech Co. Ltd.) to generate plasmid pGG (Table S1 and Figure S1). The plasmid pGG was linearized by restriction endonuclease EcoRI at the URA3 promoter and transformed into O. polymorpha Hu11 (Song et al. 2015) by electroporation using a Bio‐Rad Gene‐Pulser Apparatus (1.5 kV, 50 μF, 200 Ω, 4–5 ms) to generate the strain HP1‐GG with integration of the linearized pGG in the genome. Different sizes of putative centromeric DNA fragments identified by ChIP‐seq were amplified from the genome of the strain HP1 with the corresponding primers (Table S2), and ligated with the pGG backbone to generate a series of centromeric plasmids (Table S1 and Figure S1). To analyse the influence of long terminal repeats (LTR) on the centromeric function, plasmids with only the LTR sequence or without the LTR sequence in the centromeric sequences were also constructed. All the constructed plasmids were introduced into O. polymorpha Hu11 to result in the corresponding yeast strains through the complementation of uracil nutritional deficiency due to the gene URA3 in the plasmids. The transformation efficiency was calculated as the colony‐forming unit (CFU) per microgram of plasmid DNA.

For fusion expression of Cse4 with GFP, the coding sequence of the CSE4 gene was amplified from the genomic DNA of the wild‐type strain HP1 using primers GAPCSE‐F and CSE‐R. The plasmid backbone was amplified from the plasmid pGG‐CEN1‐3 using primers CSEGFP‐F and CSEGAP‐R. The coding sequence of the CSE4 and the linearized pGG‐CEN1‐3 backbone were then ligated using the Uniclone One Step Seamless Cloning Kit to generate plasmid pGG‐CSE4‐GFP, which was then transformed into the O. polymorpha Hu11 to result in yeast strain HP1‐CSE4‐GFP.

To investigate the compatibility of different centromeric plasmids, the coding sequences of red fluorescent protein (RFP, mCherry) and blue fluorescent protein (BFP) were synthesised by Nanjing Genscript Biotechnology Co. Ltd. The URA3 and gfp5 of the centromeric plasmids were replaced by the 3‐isopropylmalate dehydrogenase gene LEU2 of O. polymorpha and RFP coding sequence, the zeocin‐resistant cassette zeoR and BFP coding sequence, generating plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐3‐RFP and pZeo‐CEN2‐14‐BFP respectively (Table S1 and Figure S5). All the constructed plasmids were introduced into O. polymorpha Hu11‐leu2 (Song et al. 2015) by electroporation, resulting in the corresponding yeast strains.

For expression of uricase using the centromeric plasmids, the coding sequence of uricase (Uox) without the peroxisomal targeting signal (Ala‐Phe‐Lys), and methanol oxidase gene MOX terminator were amplified from the genome of the strain HP1. The two DNA fragments were used to replace the coding sequences of RFP, BFP, GFP and terminator T SUP4 in plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐3‐RFP, pZeo‐CEN2‐14‐BFP and pGG‐CEN5‐1, resulting in plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐UOX, pZeo‐CEN2‐UOX and pURA3‐CEN5‐UOX (Table S1 and Figure S6), which were introduced into O. polymorpha Hu11‐leu2 separately or simultaneously by electroporation, resulting in the corresponding yeast strains.

To assess the application of centromeric plasmids in the biosynthesis of compounds, the coding sequence of uricase in plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐UOX and pURA3‐CEN5‐UOX was replaced by the coding sequence of 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGR) from S. cerevisiae (tHMG1) or from E. faecalis (EfHMG1) (Lu et al. 2022), generating plasmids pLEU2‐CEN1‐tHMG1, pLEU2‐CEN1‐EfHMG1 and pURA3‐CEN5‐EfHMG1 respectively (Table S1 and Figure S7).

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (ChIP‐Seq) With Cse4

ChIP‐seq was conducted as previously described with some modifications (Volpe and Demaio 2011; Du et al. 2022). Log‐phase cells of strain CSE4‐3HA (about 1 OD 600) were cross‐linked using 1% formaldehyde for 20 min followed by the addition of 125 mM glycine to stop the reaction. Cells were harvested and ground with liquid nitrogen, and then chromatin fragmentation (250–500 bp) was conducted by sonication with a Bioruptor Plus (Diagenode, Belgium). Dynabeads Protein A (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) was added to the chromatin solution and incubated at 4°C for 2–3 h for pre‐washing, separated and discarded the magnetic beads. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube labelled ‘input’ as the input group. Monoclonal anti‐HA (Abcam, USA) was added to the pre‐washed chromatin and incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation. After centrifugation at 12,000 g at 4°C for 10 min, the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. The washed magnetic beads were added to the supernatant and incubated at 4°C with rotation for 2–3 h; then the magnetic beads were separated and the supernatant was discarded. After being washed, the magnetic beads were incubated with elution buffer at 65°C for 10 min to elute the protein complexes. After twice of elution, the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube, labelled ‘ChIP’. 5 M NaCl was added to ‘ChIP’ and ‘input’ tubes to a final concentration of 0.2 M and incubated overnight at 65°C to decrosslink. High‐quality DNA was purified and concentrated by DNA Clean and Concentrator (Zymo Research Corporation, USA). Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina sequencing platform for high‐throughput sequencing. ChIP peaks were visualised with Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) version 2.7.2, a genomic data set viewer for visualisation of genomic features (Robinson et al. 2011).

Quantitative PCR for Analysis of the Copy Number of Centromeric Plasmids

The copy number of centromeric plasmid in yeast cells was determined by the ΔΔCT method using genomic DNA of strain HP1 as a control. Yeast strains carrying centromeric plasmids were cultured in 5 mL SD media with or without the addition of uracil, leucine or zeocin at 37°C with agitation at 200 rpm for 20 h. Yeast cells were collected by centrifugation at 5000 g for 5 min, washed twice with TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and resuspended in 200 μL TE. The cell suspension was mixed with 50 mg acid‐washed glass beads (0.3–0.5 mm diameter) and 100 μL phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25: 24: 1, v/v/v) to extract the total DNA including both chromosomal DNA and extrachromosomal plasmid DNA. The extracted DNA was quantified spectrophotometrically and stored at −20°C. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed with the Quant One‐Step qRT‐PCR Kit (SYBR Green, Tiangen, Beijing, China) and LightCycler 96 System (Roche, Switzerland) with the appropriate primers corresponding to the promoter P GAP1 , MOX coding sequence and coding sequences of RFP, BFP, gfp5. Data were collected and processed by the second‐derivative maximum method of the LightCycler 96 software SW1.1. The copy number of centromeric plasmid was determined as the ratio of ΔΔCT for promoter P GAP1 versus MOX gene or RFP, BFP and gfp5 versus MOX, respectively. The ratio of P GAP1 and MOX gene in strain HP1 was set as 1.

Fluorescence Assay of GFP, RFP and BFP

Yeast cells were cultivated in 2 mL of SD series media with the addition of uracil, leucine or zeocin as needed at 37°C and 200 rpm for 20 h, and then diluted to an OD 600 of 0.5 in the same SD series media and grown at 37°C and 200 rpm for 24 h. Yeast cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 g for 5 min, washed twice with 0.2 M PBS buffer and resuspended in the PBS buffer. After gradient dilution to an OD 600 of about 0.4, the OD 600 and fluorescence intensity were measured using a TECAN high‐throughput multiplate reader (INFINITE 200 PRO). The wavelengths of excitation and emission for GFP are 488 and 509 nm, which are 587 and 610 nm for RFP, 402 and 457 nm for BFP, respectively. Relative fluorescence intensity (RFU) was calculated and presented as the ratio of fluorescence intensity versus OD 600.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Yeast cells were pre‐cultured at 37°C and 200 rpm for 24 h in SD media with or without the addition of uracil, leucine or zeocin (300 μg/mL). The seed cells were inoculated into 3 mL of the same SD media at an initial OD 600 of 0.5 and grown at 37°C and 200 rpm for 24 h. Such cultivation was repeated three or ten times for the evaluation of plasmid retention through continuous passage of culture (approximately 1 h of cultivation was regarded as 1 generation). Yeast cells were sampled, washed twice with PBS and resuspended in the PBS prior to flow cytometry. Fluorescent cells were detected using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer equipped with a 488‐nm blue laser for GFP, a 561‐nm yellow‐green laser for RFP or a 405‐nm violet laser for BFP (BD Biosciences, USA). The GFP fluorescence signal was collected through a 530/30‐nm band pass filter, while the RFP fluorescence signal was collected through a 610/20‐nm band pass filter, and the BFP fluorescence signal was collected through a 450/50‐nm band pass filter. The mean signal of 30,000 events was defined as fluorescence intensity for each sample. The data were analysed using the BD FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences, USA).

Genetic Stability Analysis

For analysis of the stability of plasmid in yeast cells during the culture process, yeast strains were cultivated under both selective and non‐selective conditions at 37°C and 200 rpm, and transferred to new medium every 24 h for a total of ten passages. The culture samples were withdrawn every passage respectively. The culture was serially diluted by 10‐fold in sterile distilled water, and then was spread on both selective and non‐selective media plates. After cultivation at 37°C for 48 h, 100 single colonies were randomly selected and inoculated into 1 mL of sterile water. The cell suspension was spotted correspondingly onto both selective and non‐selective media plates. After cultivation at 37°C for 48 h, the growth of colonies was compared between selective and non‐selective media. Growth on selective media plates indicates the stable existence of plasmid due to the selective marker (URA3, LEU2 or zeoR) on the plasmids. Yeast plasmid was extracted by using HiPure Yeast Plasmid Mini Kit (Guangzhou Magen Biotechnology Co. Ltd.), and was transformed into E. coli for recovery of yeast plasmid.

Microscale Thermophoresis (MST) Assay With Fusion Protein Cse4‐GFP

For analysis of the effect of LTR on the binding efficiency of Cse4 protein to the centromeric fragments, microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay was conducted using a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH). Yeast strains HP1‐CSE4‐GFP and HP1‐CEN1‐3 were cultured in SD liquid medium at 37°C with agitation of 200 rpm for 24 h. Yeast cells were harvested and lysed in PBS buffer (pH 7.4, 8 g/L NaCl, 0.2 g/L KCl, 1.44 g/L Na2HPO4, 0.24 g/L KH2PO4) with the addition of the 100‐fold dilution of protein inhibitor cocktail (M5 Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Mei5 Biotechnology Co. Ltd). The crude lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C and used directly for binding assays to preserve the native state of the protein. The full‐length CEN1‐3 fragment (3121 bp) and the truncated CEN1‐3‐ltr fragment with deletion of the LTR sequence of CEN1‐3 (2854 bp) were amplified from the plasmids pGG‐CEN1‐3 and pGG‐CEN1‐3‐ltr respectively using the primers CEN1‐3‐SF and CEN1‐3‐SR. The resulting PCR products were purified by agarose gel electrophoresis followed by gel extraction, and then concentrated via ethanol precipitation. The final DNA pellets were resuspended in PBS buffer, and the concentrations were adjusted to 200 μM for the MST binding assays.

The two DNA ligands CEN1‐3 and CEN1‐3‐ltr were serially diluted in the PBS buffer to generate 16 two‐fold dilution series, with concentrations ranging from 3.05 nM to 100 μM. For the binding reaction, 10 μL protein lysate was mixed with an equal volume of each DNA dilution, resulting in a final sample volume of 20 μL. MST measurements were performed at 25°C using an instrumental setting of 20% LED power and 80% MST power. Crude lysate containing Cse4‐GFP was used to mimic a physiologically relevant context and the control lysate from cells expressing GFP alone was used to account for any non‐specific DNA binding. Data from three independent measurements were collected. The thermophoresis traces were analysed using the MO. Affinity Analysis software (NanoTemper Technologies, v2.3). The change in normalised fluorescence (Fnorm) was plotted against the logarithm of the DNA concentration. The dissociation constant (Kd) was determined by fitting the data to a law of mass action model for a 1:1 binding interaction.

Assay of the Uricase Activity

Uricase activity was assayed as previously described with some modifications (Tork et al. 2020). Yeast cells were initially inoculated in 2 mL of selective media (SD, SD‐Leu or SD‐Zeo respectively) and cultured at 37°C with agitation of 200 rpm for 20 h. The host strain Hu11‐leu2 was cultured in SD‐Ura‐Leu medium under the same conditions. Subsequently, the cultures were transferred to 5 mL of fresh YPD medium and incubated at 30°C and 200 rpm. After 48 h of cultivation, cell growth was determined by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 600 nm (OD 600). Yeast cells were harvested from 5 mL of culture by centrifugation at 5000 g for 5 min, washed twice with 0.05 M borate buffer (pH 8.5) and resuspended in 2 mL buffer. Cell lysis was performed using a Scientz‐48 High‐throughput Tissue Grinder (Ningbo Xinzhi, China). The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant of the cell‐free extract was immediately assayed for uricase activity. The enzymatic reaction was conducted at 30°C for 5 min in a 3.0 mL reaction system (pH 8.5) containing 0.05 mL of crude enzyme extract, 2.65 mL of borate buffer and 0.3 mL of substrate solution (100 μg/mL uric acid in borate buffer). The reaction was terminated by adding 0.2 mL of 0.1 M potassium hydroxide solution. As the control, potassium hydroxide solution was added into the reaction system prior to the crude enzyme. The absorbance of samples at 292 nm was spectrophotometrically measured. The difference in absorbance at 292 between the sample and the control was used to quantify the reduction of uric acid in the reaction system. One unit (U) of uricase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to catalyse the conversion of 1 μmol uric acid to allantoin per minute under the standard conditions (30°C, pH 8.5).

Assay of Cell Growth and Squalene Production

Yeast cells were pre‐cultured in 5 mL of selective media (SD, SD‐Leu or SD‐Ura respectively) at 37°C with agitation of 200 rpm for 20 h. Subsequently, the cultures were transferred to 45 mL of YPD medium containing 20 mg/L terbinafine and incubated at 30°C and 200 rpm for 24 h. Cell growth was monitored by OD 600 measurement. For quantification of squalene, yeast cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 g for 5 min and washed twice with sterile water. Squalene accumulation in yeast cells was analysed using the previously described method (Zhou et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2022). Squalene titre was designated as the milligram of squalene per litre culture (mg/L).
