Deconstructing bilingualism and its sociocultural determinants in cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias
Authors: Miguel Arce Rentería, Iris M. Strangmann, Ladan Ghazi Saidi, Melany M. Subina, Avanthi Paplikar, Tanya Dash, Ana Sofia Costa, Durjoy Lahiri, Zara A. Page, Arpita Bose, Samrah Ahmed, Shana D. Stites, Aminette D'Souza, Ratnavalli Ellajosyula, Sanne Franzen, Suvarna Alladi, Jessica de Leon, Marco Calabria, Boon Lead Tee, Ganesh M. Babulal, Stephanie M. Grasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite bilingualism being highly prevalent worldwide, the role of bilingualism in cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) remains inconclusive. Gaps include whether bilingual adults show reduced ADRD risk, how cognitive decline manifests across both languages, and what considerations are needed for accurate clinical assessment and intervention. The Bilingualism, Languages and Literacy Special Interest Group, part of the Diversity and Disparities Professional Interest Area, gathered an international, interdisciplinary group of researchers to identify key methodological factors impacting the study of bilingualism in cognitive aging and ADRD. Factors identified ranged from the operationalization and measurement of bilingualism, heterogeneity of bilingual populations, inconsistent accounting of sociocultural influences, scarcity of best practices in the clinical assessment and interventions of/for bilingual adults, and limited inclusion of conceptual frameworks. Findings generated reporting recommendations designed to increase rigor, comparability, and reproducibility across studies, and provide guidance for strengthening future research involving bilingual adults.

BACKGROUND

The global number of individuals living with dementia will increase to around 153 million by 2050, 1 with around two‐thirds of these cases disproportionately residing in low‐and‐middle income countries (LMICs). 2 As the global population ages, communities, families, and healthcare systems will increasingly face the social, emotional, and economic burden of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). In parallel with the global increase in dementia, the size of the aging population who speak two or multiple languages (bilingual/multilingual speakers, hereafter referred to as bilinguals for brevity) will increase well. For instance, in the United States, the number of people speaking a language other than English tripled from 23.1 million in 1980 to 67.8 million in 2019. 3 In Europe, around three quarters of adults reported knowing at least one foreign language. 4 In India, an LMIC which is the most populated country in the world, 122 languages are spoken across five different language families. As of the 2011 census, 26% of the population of India, which amounts to over 300 million people, reported speaking at least two languages. 5 In other LMICs, such as Mexico, we also see great linguistic diversity. Mexico has over seven million speakers of approximately 68 indigenous languages and around six million Mexicans who also speak English, representing a similar pattern across other Latin American countries. 6 Overall, current estimates indicate that more than half of the world's population knows two or more languages. 7

Despite bilingualism being the global norm, monolingual practices remain the standard in clinics worldwide, creating persistent challenges in the assessment and treatment of bilingual individuals due to a lack of language‐specific tools, standardized assessments, and clinician training. 8 Besides being a source of clinical complexity, bilingualism has unique potential as a protective factor. Like other cognitively enriching experiences—formal education, higher literacy, stimulating leisure activities—bilingualism is conceptualized as a cognitively complex activity involving attentional and executive control mechanisms. 9 A “bilingual advantage” in cognition has been reported in children and in young and older adults 9 —particularly within the domain of executive function—though results are mixed. 10 Similarly, bilingualism may contribute to cognitive reserve (CR) and reduce dementia risk, where bilingual people retain similar levels of cognition in the face of greater AD‐related neurodegeneration compared to monolinguals, 11 , 12 yet various studies investigating bilingualism as a protective factor in cognitive aging have found no evidence of such protection. 13 Moreover, while cross‐sectional and retrospective studies indicate that bilingualism is associated with an older age of dementia symptom onset, 14 longitudinal studies do not find an effect of bilingualism on risk of incident dementia or on rate of cognitive decline. 10 , 15 As such, bilingualism presents itself both as a potential resource for cognitive resilience, in addition to a pressing clinical challenge for assessment, diagnosis, and care. As bilingual populations age, it is critical to understand the influence of bilingualism on cognitive aging and ADRD, address the gaps in current knowledge and opportunities for future research, as well as consider its potential application in ADRD intervention.

Key methodological differences across studies on the role of bilingualism and cognitive aging, as well as the incredible variability of the bilingual experience across sociocultural contexts, may explain prior mixed findings. First, bilingualism's definition is inconsistent across studies. Some studies define bilingualism based on a single dichotomous item of whether a participant knows or speaks another language, 15 while others define bilingualism based on level of self‐reported proficiency, dominance, or other bilingual history factors in a given language. 16 , 17 Second, studies largely focus on establishing group differences between bilingual and monolingual speakers in ADRD risk, centering monolingualism, and treating bilingual adults as a monolithic group, instead of deconstructing bilingualism into components that best represent the cognitively enriching experience inherent to bilingualism. Bilingual individuals can demonstrate significant within‐group heterogeneity in terms of their bilingual experiences—including age and context of second language (L2) acquisition, types and degree of proficiencies, frequency of language use, number and type of languages spoken, among others—that remain largely overlooked and therefore remain uninformative with respect to the aspect(s) of bilingualism that may contribute to cognitive aging. 18 Third, there has been an inconsistent control for potential confounders which limits our ability to infer whether bilingualism has a direct effect on cognition or whether these associations are due to environmental and sociocultural factors (e.g., educational experiences, socioeconomic status, and history of migration) that may influence the ability or opportunity to become bilingual. 19 Fourth, it is unclear whether best practices for the neuropsychological and cognitive assessment of bilingual individuals have been used. Studies of bilingualism and cognitive aging may have bilinguals who speak a diversity of languages, yet are only evaluated in the region's primary language. Is the language of test administration their dominant language? Even when language assessments are administered in a bilingual individual's dominant language, interpreting the results of certain cognitive tests necessitates additional considerations. And if the assessment is available in multiple languages, has the validity of the instruments been established for each language in a population that is representative of the demographics of the participants? Lastly, theoretical and conceptual frameworks on how bilingualism may influence cognition and confer cognitive or brain reserve are inconsistently included across studies of bilingualism and cognitive aging.

Throughout this perspective, we will address each of these issues by providing a brief background and suggest recommendations to improve research on bilingualism and cognitive aging. This perspectives piece was developed within the Diversity and Disparities Professional Interest Area—Bilingualism, Languages and Literacy Special Interest Group of the Alzheimer's Association, an interdisciplinary working group composed of researchers with expertise spanning bilingualism, cognitive aging, neuropsychology, linguistics, speech‐language pathology, and ADRD research (see Approach in Supporting Information). We will focus on: the operationalization of bilingualism and the need to deconstruct bilingualism to move away from a dichotomous classification; a conceptual framework on how to incorporate key sociocultural confounds; discuss best practices in the cognitive assessment of and interventions for aging bilinguals; and a review of current theories on the cognitive mechanisms of bilingualism and their potential relationship with CR. Overall, we posit that aligning and improving the operationalization and measurement of bilingualism will not only help resolve conflicting evidence on its role in cognitive aging, but also advance best practices in the clinical assessment and intervention of bilingual older adults—two aims that are often interdependent, as research on the cognitive consequences of bilingualism often relies on clinical evaluation of bilingual adults.

DEFINING BILINGUALISM

Bilingualism has long been viewed through a monolingual lens, sometimes referred to as “the monolingual yardstick”. 20 This has resulted in exclusionary practices where only people who were perfectly balanced’ or defined as “true bilingualism” were considered to be bilingual. For instance, a recent study evaluating the impact of bilingualism on late‐life cognitive functioning classified only participants who reported speaking two or more languages daily as bilingual speakers. 21 Yet, participants who reported speaking multiple languages once a week or less were classified as monolingual speakers. It seems reductionist to classify someone as a monolingual if they are using two languages weekly. Although these studies provide valuable information regarding the associations of bilingualism and cognitive aging, their bilingual participants may not in fact represent all bilinguals but perhaps a select group of bilinguals (i.e., highly proficient and daily dual language users). Similarly, their monolingual comparison group may not actually include purely monolingual participants if they had acquired a second language at some point in their lifetime and use it occasionally. A different, emerging point of view conceptualizes bilingualism as a spectrum, placing what may be operationalized as “strict” monolingualism and “perfectly balanced” bilingualism on its extremes and acknowledging that most people would fall somewhere in between. 22

However, there is no consensus on a standard process to classify and group individuals into these categories (i.e., monolingual and bilingual) which has led to inconsistent operationalizations of bilingualism across cognitive aging studies. 23 For instance, prior studies evaluating differences in cognitive performance between bilingual and monolingual older adults categorized the groups based on either a self‐reported knowledge and/or use of a second language, 15 , 21 or on self‐reported L2 proficiency above a certain level (i.e., able to communicate like a native speaker). 17 , 21 While different research questions might require specific and diverse operationalizations of bilingualism, it is critical to make these criteria explicit to best ascertain the type of bilingual adult a particular study may be generalizable to.

In the context of cognitive aging and ADRD research, we propose that investigators start from an inclusive definition of bilingualism in which investigators obtain a rich characterization of the participants’ linguistic background (i.e., type and degree of bilingualism) and then classify participants as needed per the scientific aims of the study. At its broadest definition, a bilingual person is someone who has acquired two or more languages at any point in their life. In addition, it is important to distinguish bilingual individuals from second‐language learners. A bilingual has acquired and can functionally use a second language, while a second‐language learner is still in the process of learning and may not use it frequently. Importantly, a monolingual person is an individual who has only acquired one language (see Table 1).

Different definitions of bilingualism may be required for different scientific disciplines and focus, such as psycholinguistics, linguistics, education, and policy research. However, for the context of cognitive aging and ADRD research, adopting an inclusive definition may provide a common starting point for investigators interested in evaluating the effect of bilingualism in cognitive aging and ADRD risk. If a given study chooses to study a certain type of bilingualism, then their nomenclature for their groups should reflect that (i.e., active and highly proficient bilingualism instead of just bilingualism) as well as for the comparison group (i.e., monolingual individuals only, monolingual individuals with limited proficient bilingual individuals grouped together, etc.). Similarly, investigators should provide a strong rationale for excluding certain types of bilingual speakers and avoid grouping them with monolingual adults. Researchers might also consider capturing bilingualism on a continuum as opposed to splitting participants into groups. Through this approach, we might start to create greater consensus on the operationalization of bilingualism across studies and improve the generalizability of findings by clearly defining and well characterizing our bilingual and monolingual cohorts.

Deconstructing bilingualism

Treating bilingual speakers as a monolithic group can be restrictive to hypothesis testing and examining mechanisms, potentially leading to overgeneralized findings across diverse bilingual populations. This is because bilingual speakers can differ on a variety of linguistic characteristics that may influence cognitive functioning and brain structure, including age and context of L2 acquisition, frequency of bilingual language usage, degree of proficiency across modalities (reading, writing, understanding, speaking), number of languages known, and linguistic similarity or dissimilarity between languages, among others (Figure 1). If bilingualism is hypothesized to influence cognitive test performance and/or serve as a factor that can confer protection in late‐life cognitive functioning, studies should endeavor to understand what aspects and under what conditions bilingualism might impact cognition to fully understand the bilingual experience.

Conceptual model of different aspects of bilingualism that may confer resilience to cognitive aging and ADRD.ADRD, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias.

Few studies have approached bilingualism through this deconstructive lens in the context of cognitive aging. Gollan and colleagues have reported that a greater degree of bilingualism was associated with a delayed age of AD onset, particularly among individuals with low education. 24 Bilingualism was operationalized with an objective bilingual naming test, where higher scores suggested greater balanced bilingualism. Studies among older adults in Spain have reported that active bilingualism delayed the onset of mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 16 Bilingualism was operationalized on a continuum that ranged from “passive bilingualism” meaning being able to understand another language yet with little to no usage of it, with earlier age of L2 acquisition, higher L2 proficiency, and greater daily language on the other end suggesting an “active bilingualism”. In another cohort of bilingual adults in Spain, there was an association between bilingualism and AD biomarker profile, such that earlier age of acquisition was associated with a lower p/tau ratio. 25 More recently, greater L2 usage was linked to increased neuronal connectivity among bilingual people diagnosed with AD residing in Italy and Switzerland, 26 and less balanced bilingualism was associated with worse phonemic fluency performance and worse Aβ 42/40 plasma biomarker profile among a well characterized cohort of bilingual Mexican‐American adults in Texas. 27 As such, this growing body of studies strongly suggest that deconstructing bilingualism and measuring its different components can provide novel insights into the association of bilingualism and cognitive aging and ADRD risk, which may in fact differ by sociocultural context.

Measuring bilingualism

It may be challenging to determine which measure of bilingualism to incorporate into studies of cognitive aging and ADRD. Studies of ADRD, either clinical or observational, are time intensive as they generally may require extensive medical and neuropsychological assessments, collection of ADRD biomarkers, functional evaluations, and assessment of social determinants of health. As such, instruments to include in cognitive aging studies to evaluate bilingualism should be feasible and efficient to minimize participant burden. We recommend that, in studies of bilingualism and cognitive aging, the following aspects should be assessed. This information is also summarized in Table 2. We note that from these different core components of bilingualism's measurement, clinical researchers may also opt to create indices that reflect degree of dominance, a relatively common practice in the literature.

Age of acquisition

Studies have operationalized age of acquisition (AoA) based on the age in which fluency of the second language was obtained, 28 or the age of active daily usage of the second language. 29 However, other investigators have operationalized AoA as the start of language learning or exposure based on the initial exposure to the second language (e.g., parents, friends, listening to music and television) or through the start of the formal classroom learning. 30 , 31 Given that more research is required across different bilingual populations to understand which aspect of AoA is more relevant for cognitive aging, we recommend that operationalizations of AoA measure both age of first exposure in a trajectory of subsequent learning as well as age in which fluency was obtained. Fluency is a multidimensional construct that can encompass abilities across speaking, comprehension, reading, and writing. Here, we operationalize fluency in terms of spoken production. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that alternative operationalizations could emphasize other modalities dependent on the research goals.

Place of acquisition

In addition to AoA, studies should collect data on place of acquisition. While some studies have reported that place of acquisition (i.e., at home, school, immersive learning experiences) may be associated to differential fluency gains, 32 , 33 it is unclear whether context of learning is related independently to late‐life cognitive health. As such, this presents a significant gap in the literature that requires further study.

Proficiency

Proficiency is typically a self‐reported measure in Likert scale format (e.g., 1–7 or 1–10) assessed via self‐report across multiple modalities (i.e., speaking, understanding, reading, writing). This is one of the most widely available measures of bilingualism in published questionnaires. While some studies find a moderate to high correlation between self‐reported L2 proficiency and standardized language tests, 34 other studies have found over‐and under‐estimation of self‐reported proficiency when compared to objective language proficiency tests. 35 , 36 Ideally, proficiency measures of bilingualism should be assessed in a combination of self‐reported and objective measures. In addition, the use of multiple objective measures that represent different aspects of language proficiency (e.g., receptive language proficiency vs. production, spoken vs. written language proficiency) will highlight the multi‐dimensional nature of proficiency.

Language use and exposure

Bilingual speakers can also differ in degree of language use and exposure. While frequency of dual language use, in it of itself, has not been consistently associated with better cognitive functioning, 37 bilingual speakers can demonstrate substantial variation in the manner and contexts in which they use their languages (i.e., speaking both languages in a given context vs. use one language exclusively in one context), which could place different cognitive demands on the speaker. 38

Language use and exposure are frequently measured using standard bilingual background questionnaires in both general and interactional (e.g., language use at home, work, in social setting, etc.) contexts. An additional dimension of language use concerns internally directed language, such as the language one reports thinking in. Some language background questionnaires approximate this construct through items assessing the language used for counting or dreaming, which may reflect automatic or default language selection. Raw scores (percentage exposure, Likert scale) or normalized scores are commonly used as a measure of bilingualism to predict cognitive performance. Given that more research is required to further understand the potential relationship between language use and cognitive outcomes in late‐life, we recommend that investigators measure a bilingual speaker's use of their languages across various contexts.

Language distance

There are an estimated 7139 living languages worldwide with significant heterogeneity in their linguistic features. 39 Numerous studies have shown a certain level of separate neural representations between L1 and L2 languages driven in part by linguistic distance. 40 Linguistic distance represents the quantitative measures of the typological distance between languages. However, scientists currently lack adequate consensus on how to represent linguistic distance between languages. The most commonly adopted approach is the lexicostatistics method, which measures distances based on mutual intelligibility or proportion of shared cognate words. 41 Other studies have grouped bilingual adults depending on whether the languages known are from the same or different language family. 42 Further research is required to validate these different markers of linguistic distance as it relates to cognitive aging among bilingual populations. Additionally, with the increasing availability of large language models, these technological advances might provide exciting avenues with which to measure language distance. 43

Self‐report measures

Table 3 and Supporting Information provide a summary of available self‐report measures of bilingualism that capture various of the aspects described previously. Careful consideration should be given to which questionnaire is more appropriate for the socioeconomic and educational background of the individual, and which measures contain questions that are easiest to understand in the face of cognitive decline. Lastly, given that bilingualism is a dynamic phenomenon where an individual's proficiency and use in each language can fluctuate over time, longitudinal assessment of these features provides a more accurate representation than cross‐sectional, nominal measures. 44 In the face of cognitive decline, we recommend these features should be assessed both pre and post symptom onset.

Objective measures

While performance‐based measures of bilingualism have been developed that focus on assessment through picture naming (e.g., Multilingual Naming Test [MINT]) 45 or verbal fluency tasks, 46 there are several limitations to these objective measures that require further study. First, it is challenging to disentangle measures of linguistic abilities that tap into cognitive processes that can be impacted by aging from independent assessments of degree of bilingualism. For instance, verbal fluency and naming measures assess executive functions and lexical retrieval abilities as much as they may be measuring linguistic proficiency 47 and these abilities are impacted early in the ADRD process. 48 To our knowledge, the MINT has been shown to be less vulnerable to the effects of cognitive aging and can be a valid measure of linguistic proficiency even among aging populations. 49 However, a second limitation is that these measures can be biased by educational, linguistic, and cultural factors. 50 As such, additional research is required to determine the validity of these performance‐based measures across culturally and linguistically diverse bilingual populations.

SOCIOCULTURAL CONFOUNDS AND BILINGUALISM

Bilingualism is not randomly distributed in a population, nor does it occur in a vacuum. Certain life experiences increase the ability and/or opportunity for someone to become bilingual (Figure 2). For instance, in monolingual dominant societies, bilingualism is often associated with migration and can overlap with the socioeconomic challenges faced by minority groups. 9 , 19 Conversely, many non‐English speaking countries teach English as a foreign language, due to its status as a lingua franca, in which case bilingualism could covary with higher educational attainment and socioeconomic status. These diverse sociocultural and socioeconomic factors, which can be confounded with bilingualism (i.e., childhood socioeconomic status, history of immigration, etc.), are also associated with late‐life cognitive health. 19 These different reasons for and experiences with becoming and being bilingual greatly challenge its study. Despite these confounds, we aim to isolate the independent contribution of bilingualism on cognitive aging as to make causal inferences. While randomized control experiments would be ideal for establishing causality, they are unattainable in studying bilingualism. Therefore, researchers must carefully account for the factors that may confound its relationship with cognition.

Methodological framework on sociocultural factors across the life course that may confound the association of bilingualism with late‐life cognitive outcomes.

When aiming to evaluate the effect of bilingualism on cognitive aging and ADRD risk, one must take a life‐course approach to understand which childhood and adulthood contextual factors may be relevant confounds to consider. Figure 2 describes a conceptual framework for investigators to use and adapt as needed, depending on the bilingual population under investigation. Factors that influence an individual's ability or opportunity to become bilingual early in life could be factors such as childhood socioeconomic status, years and quality of education, neighborhood environment, among others. Across the lifespan, experiences of migration have been suggested but not proven to underlie observed bilingual associations (e.g., the healthy migrant effect). 51 In this case, comparisons with other bilingual contexts may offer an alternative approach (e.g., India and Spain, where migration is not always a confound). Extending our investigation of bilingual associations to diverse global contexts may further reduce the impact of confounds on study outcomes. Moreover, adulthood contextual factors such as socioeconomic status and healthcare access/utilization might interact with bilingualism's effects on cognitive outcomes.

Since life‐course sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors have been found to influence cognition and often covary with bilingualism, 9 , 19 their inclusion makes for more accurate comparisons between mono‐ and bilingual‐groups and ultimately better inferences. However, given that these differences in confounding factors between bilingual and monolingual groups may be non‐trivial and systematic, statistically controlling for relevant confounds might not be appropriate as it may lead to potentially spurious results. 52 As such, statistical techniques such as investigating interactions with/stratification of other relevant “protective” factors, residuals approaches or use of propensity scores may address concerns of inadequate control of confounders. Carefully navigating the sociocultural and sociodemographic considerations and contextual nuances of bilingualism may enable us to further isolate the experience of bilingualism to cognition and help elucidate its relationship and implications for cognitive aging.

COGNITIVE ASSESSMENT OF BILINGUAL ADULTS

Determining language of administration for the assessment of cognitive decline and dementia in bilingual populations presents both theoretical and methodological challenges. Some argue for testing in the dominant or preferred language, as it might be least confounded by premorbid language proficiency. 8 Determining the most proficient and preferred language, however, is not straightforward, and methods can differ widely. A recent study outlined this difficulty clearly and demonstrated that applying a systematic approach to test language selection, which includes both subjective and objective assessment of bilingualism could improve accuracy in the cognitive assessment of bilingual adults. 53 Others propose testing in both languages, for a more comprehensive picture. Testing in both languages could reveal subtle differences not apparent in just one. 54 , 55 In fact, some studies have reported that progression of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD or certain PPAs might impact non‐dominant language abilities earlier in the disease process than dominant languages. 56 Ultimately, the choice depends on the aims of the assessment. For instance, to assess clinical progression, the dominant language might be sufficient. But if it is to understand how bilingualism affects the disease, testing in both languages could provide richer data.

Although language preference is typically a choice made by bilingual people themselves, their choices can be influenced by the perceived social status and prestige that may be associated with their languages. 57 These social attributions can impact non‐native speakers primarily, as they may experience discrimination due to their language proficiency which can exacerbate barriers to healthcare access and satisfaction in bilingual people. 58 In addition, people typically do not know what clinical assessments entail and language proficiency may be highly contextual. For example, bilingual people who acquire English as a second language may be highly proficient but have obtained numeracy skills in their first language. As language may be linked to number processing 59 this may impact their performance on often used assessments such as Digit Span and Serial 7s. Moreover, the availability and feasibility of administering the assessment in the preferred testing language may be largely constrained by access to resources (such as interpreters, validated cross‐cultural or translated tests, etc.) and/or the language experience of researchers and clinicians themselves.

A neuropsychological assessment should reflect reliable and valid measurement of true cognitive abilities, not systematic bias due to cultural–linguistic differences. Ensuring the construct validity of cognitive tests across languages with measurement invariance techniques is crucial for accurate and meaningful cognitive aging/ADRD research involving bilingual populations. Imagine a test designed to assess memory. If the test itself is culturally biased or the translation process alters its meaning, a bilingual participant's score might reflect these issues rather than their actual memory function. Measurement invariance techniques help verify that the test measures the same cognitive construct (e.g., memory) equivalently, regardless of language. This allows researchers to compare scores from different language groups with confidence, leading to more reliable conclusions about how ADRD impacts cognition across diverse populations. Prior studies have successfully determined measurement invariance across languages among cohorts in the United States 60 , 61 and internationally. 62 , 63 However, because most cognitive batteries are administered in one language, even if measurement equivalence is determined across language versions, it remains unknown whether construct invariance is maintained between bilingual and monolingual individuals.

Developing new culturally–linguistically tailored tests comes with the caveat of a potential lack of normative data. Normative data are required to develop cut‐off scores for classification of cognitive impairment (i.e., MCI, dementia), 64 yet normative data are not available for many of the most commonly used clinical and research tests. 65 This paucity introduces an additional source of bias in the use of neuropsychological tests, rendering their use in culturally–linguistically diverse populations questionable until the influence of bilingualism has been examined or normative data developed. Developing study‐specific robust neuropsychological norms may improve the sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic classification. 64 However, most current normative approaches are biased towards monolingualism (i.e., norms are based on single‐language administration in primarily monolingual individuals). As such, future studies are needed to determine the influence of bilingualism in the normative reference group on sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic classifications.

SPEECH–LANGUAGE AND COGNITIVE INTERVENTIONS

Speech–language interventions for bilingual speakers require consideration of both practical and theoretical issues related to language availability and treatment prioritization. Ideally, intervention is provided in the languages in which the individual routinely communicates in daily life across contexts. For bilingual individuals living with dementia, communication support across both languages is often essential to maintain participation and connection with family and caregivers. Speech–language intervention in ADRD may include restitutive treatment, strategy training, multimodal communication supports (including augmentative and alternative communication [AAC]), and counseling/education for both patients and their care partners. Although providing treatment in non‐majority or less commonly spoken languages can be challenging, collaboration with multilingual speech‐language pathologists, interpreters, translators, and family members can allow both languages to be meaningfully supported in practice.

In addition, theoretical factors may influence designs for bilingual intervention. While some studies report greater vulnerability of later‐acquired languages, 66 other work suggests that patterns of language loss are influenced by dominance and age of acquisition, 55 or demonstrate similar decline across languages. 54 Evidence in bilingual dementia remains limited and primarily from PPA. Case studies have shown treatment‐related gains and cross‐language transfer effects, including improvement in naming despite overall decline in other domains and maintenance following prophylactic treatment. 67 Importantly, a group study examining lexical retrieval intervention in bilingual speakers with lvPPA and svPPA demonstrated significant gains for trained items within and across languages, with cross‐linguistic transfer especially evident for cognates. 68 These promising, but emerging findings suggest that dual‐language intervention may produce meaningful change even in the context of neurodegenerative disease, and future work should investigate broader validation across language contexts.

Many nonpharmacological interventions used in ADRD, beyond speech‐language intervention, are also inherently language‐mediated and therefore raise similar considerations for bilingual adults. For example, cognitive stimulation approaches may be delivered through structured group discussion and language‐based activities intended to enhance cognitive and social functioning. In practice, this means that the language(s) of delivery, the cultural familiarity of materials, and the participant's communicative strengths can meaningfully influence engagement and outcomes, much like in speech–language interventions. Importantly, some cognitive stimulation therapy studies have included bilingual individuals (e.g., Moroccan Arabic–French or Spanish–English speakers) and demonstrate feasibility in linguistically diverse samples. 69 , 70 Work on cognitive stimulation therapy implementation emphasizes the importance of translation and cultural adaptation (rather than literal translation alone), as well as collaboration with relevant stakeholders (e.g., people with dementia, caregivers, community providers, and clinicians) when adapting interventions to new linguistic and cultural contexts. 71 Continued research is needed to expand cognitive–linguistic interventions to additional language pairs, integrate culturally informed treatment adaptations, to evaluate whether bilingualism moderates intervention responses, and improve access to bilingual intervention for individuals currently living with dementia and their families, who continue to face significant barriers to accessing these intervention services. 72

BILINGUALISM'S EFFECTS ON COGNITION IN COGNITIVE AGING/ADRD RESEARCH

Within the context of cognitive aging and ADRD risk, bilingualism has been proposed as a factor that may contribute to cognitive and/or brain reserve 73 . However, reserve, along with resilience and resistance, has been variably defined across bilingualism research. While both CR and brain reserve (BR) relate to how individuals cope with brain changes, they represent distinct constructs. CR reflects maintenance of cognitive processes despite pathology, whereas brain reserve represents structural capacity. Related constructs include resilience, which refers to preserved cognition despite existing pathology or neurodegeneration, and resistance, the capacity to prevent or slow the development or accumulation of pathology itself. 74 To evaluate whether bilingualism confers reserve, resilience, or resistance, studies must consider both cognitive performance and indices of brain structure or AD‐related biomarkers.

Many previous studies investigating the protective effects of bilingualism in aging have not included structural brain measures or markers of AD neuropathology. If bilingualism contributes to CR or resistance, then older bilinguals may display greater neuropathology than monolinguals when equivalent in cognitive performance or disease severity. 9 Cross‐sectional findings remain mixed: some studies report greater atrophy among bilinguals, 11 , 12 whereas others show greater brain integrity. 75 Even fewer studies have included AD‐specific biomarkers such as amyloid and tau. For example, one study found lower abnormal total‐tau in bilinguals compared to monolinguals, but could not establish that this was directly related to cognition. 25 It remains critical for future research to demonstrate that the biomarker being evaluated is meaningfully associated with cognitive functioning, as recommended in current reserve/resilience frameworks. 73

Although there is no single dominant theoretical model to explain potential cognitive advantages associated with bilingualism, most theories posit that managing two languages requires constant language control, resulting in increased engagement of attentional and executive control systems. Other frameworks suggest bilingualism may promote more efficient neural network organization or quantitative differences in lexical‐level competition. 76 However, these mechanistic accounts remain debated, and evidence for broad domain‐general transfer remains inconsistent. 77 As methodological rigor increases (e.g., improved bilingual measurement, specification of confounds), future studies will better clarify which mechanisms may confer modifiable protection relevant to ADRD risk. 78

CONCLUSION

Bilingualism has been identified as a potential protective factor in cognitive aging and ADRD. It is crucial that researchers strive to use similar methodological approaches in the measurement of bilingualism to increase replicability of study findings. To achieve this, we propose that future studies, at a minimum, capture age of acquisition, place of use, proficiency, language use and exposure, and, when possible, language distance. By decomposing bilingualism into its core components, we not only enable meaningful cross‐study comparison, but also clarify how these components collectively contribute to a more comprehensive and holistic understanding of bilingual language experience.

We also propose that clear operationalized definitions of bilingualism be provided in future work, coupled with a rationale given the specific characteristics of the population being studied. In parallel, we emphasize that the study of bilingualism must explicitly account for sociocultural context, as language experience is embedded within social structures. Ultimately, without this level of precision, we cannot determine how or why bilingualism may be protective in aging and ADRD, nor identify which aspects of bilingual experience are truly modifiable targets for intervention.

Finally, we emphasize that this manuscript is intended as a narrative Perspectives piece rather than a systematic or exhaustive synthesis of the literature. As such, the evidence highlighted reflects the authors’ collective expertise and aims to illustrate central conceptual and methodological issues in the field. We acknowledge that this approach may be subject to selection bias and that alternative perspectives or additional relevant studies may not have been comprehensively included. It is our hope that this article serves as a catalyst for continued discussion, refinement of measurement practices, and future consensus‐building efforts to advance rigor and clarity in bilingualism and aging research.
