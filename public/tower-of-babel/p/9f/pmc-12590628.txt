A novel enzymatic approach for a targeted fungal growth inhibition
Authors: Angelina Ivanova, Valeriia Buzova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDandruff represents a complex, multifactorial disorder characterized by impaired barrier function, altered lipid composition, and microbial imbalance. The principal pathogenic mechanism, however, involves excessive colonization by Malassezia species, notably M. furfur, M. restricta and M. globosa, on the scalp. Standard antifungal options often lead to undesirable effects, including itching and irritation, and may negatively impact the scalp microbiome. This study evaluates combination of chitinase and chitosanase, targeting fungal cell wall chitin and chitosan, as a promising targeted approach for scalp Malassezia spp. control.MethodsThe in vitro antifungal activities of chitinase (100 U/g) and chitosanase (200 U/g) were evaluated against Malassezia furfur, Malassezia restricta, and Malassezia globosa at concentrations ranging from 0.125% to 5% w/w. Their efficacy was compared to that of conventional antifungal agents, including climbazole, piroctone olamine, selenium sulfide, zinc pyrithione, and propanediol caprylate. Cytotoxicity was assessed using fibroblast cell lines via MTT assay and fluorescence microscopy. A clinical study (n = 18) evaluated the impact of a solution containing 0.25% chitinase and 0.25% chitosanase on scalp M. furfur and M. restricta DNA and RNA levels using quantitative PCR (qPCR). A metagenomic analysis was conducted to assess the impact of enzymatic treatment on bacterial composition and diversity.ResultsIndividually, 0.25% chitinase and 0.25% chitosanase inhibited Malassezia spp. growth by 23.85% and 26.15%, respectively (p < 0.05). When combined at 0.25%, they achieved 98.38% inhibition (p < 0.05), with complete suppression observed at 0.5%. In a clinical study, a 3-hour scalp treatment with a solution containing 0.25% chitinase and 0.25% chitosanase followed by quantitative PCR of post-treatment samples demonstrated significant reductions in DNA and RNA levels of M. furfur and M. restricta. DNA content decreased 2.4- and 1.9-fold, and RNA levels declined 2.4- and 4.6-fold, respectively. Cytotoxicity was detected only at concentrations ≥ 7.6%, well above the effective antifungal doses. The metagenomic analysis demonstrated that a three-hour scalp treatment with chitinase - chitosanase solution increased alpha diversity (Chao1 index) and doubled the number of identifiable operational taxonomic units (OTUs).ConclusionsThe chitinase-chitosanase combination offers a promising targeted approach for scalp Malassezia spp. control without broad antimicrobial effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04453-y.Keywords: Malassezia spp., Chitinase, Chitosanase, Dandruff treatment, Antifungal agents, Scalp microbiome, Climbazole, Propanediol caprylate

Background

Dandruff, affecting up to 50% of the global adult population over their lifetime, is characterized by scalp flaking and itching, transepidermal water loss, elevated scalp pH, and disrupted barrier function, indicating compromised stratum corneum integrity [1]. The precise etiology of this complex, multifactorial disorder remains difficult to determine. Nevertheless, several predisposing factors that increase the likelihood of dandruff development have been identified. These include the abundance of Malassezia yeasts [2], host epidermal conditions and the integrity of the epidermal barrier, sebaceous gland activity and secretion [1], and the bacterial composition of the scalp microbiota [3], whose main representative members are Cutibacterium acnes, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Malassezia spp [4]. Lipid alterations can serve as an initiating factor. Under androgenic stimulation, sebaceous glands secrete large quantities of triglycerides, wax esters, and squalene [5, 6]. This shift creates an ideal niche for the lipophilic yeasts Malassezia spp., which encode multiple secreted lipases that hydrolyze triglycerides into free fatty acids, especially oleic acid, raising surface pH to 6.0–6.5 [7, 8]. Approximately half of the population exhibits an inherited hypersensitivity to oleic acid, resulting in pruritus, erythema, and accelerated keratinocyte proliferation that underlies visible flaking [9].

Concurrently, elevated pH suppresses Cutibacterium acnes, the key follicular commensal responsible for producing short chain fatty acids and the antibiotic cutimycin [10]. Short-chain fatty acids help maintain acidic scalp pH and inhibit staphylococcal biofilm formation, whereas cutimycin limits colonization of skin hair follicles by Staphylococcus species [11]. In dandruff, the population of Cutibacterium acnes declines, disrupting the acidic barrier and relieving antagonistic pressure on another ubiquitous skin colonizer, Staphylococcus epidermidis [12]. This shift leads to an increase in the ratio of S. epidermidis to C. acnes, a change commonly seen in the scalp microbiome of individuals affected by dandruff [4, 12]. Research indicates that S. epidermidis can become an accelerator of dandruff once the ecological balance is lost. It does so through metabolic opportunism, biofilm formation and protease-mediated damage to the stratum corneum. S. epidermidis exploits glycerol released by fungal lipolysis, and under these alkaline and nutrient-rich conditions, its agr quorum-sensing circuit induces expression of the icaADBC operon, responsible for polysaccharide intercellular adhesin production, as well as the cysteine protease EcpA. Biofilm matrices formed in this milieu shield the bacteria from host defences, while EcpA degrades desmoglein-1 and the antimicrobial peptide LL-37, compromising corneodesmosomes, elevating transepidermal water loss and triggering cytokine cascades that intensify pruritus and flaking [13]. Concurrently, Malassezia species, in the absence of bacterial competitive constraints, proliferate to densities three times greater than those observed on healthy scalps [14]. Although dandruff is a multifactorial condition, numerous studies have demonstrated that Malassezia spp. represent a principal causative factor in its pathogenesis [2, 14].

The most effective first-line therapies remain agents with antifungal activity- climbazole, piroctone olamine, selenium sulfide, zinc pyrithione (ZnPy), and propanediol caprylate, whose shared mechanism is the inhibition or removal of Malassezia spp. However, all of these agents have significant limitations. Climbazole is an imidazole antifungal agent whose primary mechanism involves inhibiting ergosterol biosynthesis by interfering with fungal cytochrome P-450 enzymes, resulting in disruption of the fungal cell membrane [15]. Climbazole is regarded as a standard treatment for dandruff, however, its dose-dependent inhibition of M. furfur occurs predominantly at higher concentrations (greater than 1%) [16]. Treatment of primary keratinocytes with climbazole has been shown to upregulate the expression of genes encoding proteins involved in the formation of keratinized membranes, thereby improving the skin barrier [17]. Nevertheless, climbazole may induce localized skin irritation, presenting as redness, rash, itching, and allergic reactions, which limits its use as a first-line therapy. It is also important to note that Malassezia spp. tend to develop resistance to antiseptics, including azoles like climbazole. This resistance arises from mutations in the ERG11/CYP51 gene and the overexpression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter proteins, which utilize ATP hydrolysis or electrochemical gradients to efflux antifungal drugs, thereby reducing susceptibility [18]. Additionally, azoles inhibit flavohaemoglobin (flavoHb), negatively affecting the growth of gram-positive bacteria [19]. This issue has been exacerbated by the prolonged and uncontrolled antifungal drugs use, leading to the microorganisms’ adaptation [20]. Piroctone olamine functions as a chelating complex that binds iron ions, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial function. It disrupts fungal metabolism, preventing sebum triglyceride degradation into oleic and arachidonic acids. Additionally, piroctone olamine exhibits antibacterial activity against gram-positive and gram-negative scalp bacteria and its reported side effects include irritation and oedema [21, 22]. Selenium sulfide has a multifaceted action against dandruff. It interferes with keratin structure in the stratum corneum, which exerts a keratolytic effect by reducing cell adhesion and promoting the shedding of dead skin cells along with fungal elements. Selenium sulfide also exerts moderate antifungal activity against Malassezia spp. and antibacterial effect [22]. However, it is associated with a range of adverse events, including scalp irritation, dryness, burning, increased hair loss, and in some cases notable discoloration of the hair or scalp [23]. Zinc pyrithione acts mainly through two mechanisms: disrupting fungal membrane transport by blocking proton pumps and acting as a copper ionophore that facilitates toxic copper accumulation, thereby damaging critical iron-sulfur cluster-containing metabolic enzymes in fungi [24, 25]. This dual action results in potent inhibition of Malassezia but also other scalp microbiota, including gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, even at low concentrations [26]. Zinc pyrithione also impedes fungal cell division and induces cellular stress signaling, which can cause DNA damage in both fungal and human skin cells. Concerns about this DNA-damaging potential, together with evidence of reproductive toxicity in animal studies, led to a complete prohibition of its use in cosmetic products in the European Union as of March 1, 2022 [27]. Propanediol caprylate is a monoester derived from caprylic acid and propanediol. The effectiveness of this compound is related to the dependence of the fungus on sebum lipids (esters). Malassezia’s endogenous lipases cleave the ester to release caprylic acid which exerts antifungal effects by disrupting fungal membranes and metabolic processes, reducing viability and hence the severity of dandruff [28]. Unlike older antifungals, this strategy is auto-regulatory and self-limiting, targeting only those areas where Malassezia is abundant. However, research demonstrates that caprylic acid, which inhibits fungal growth, also exerts antibacterial effects [29].

Most agents used as dandruff remedies are non-selective antimicrobials that destroy much of the scalp microbiome, thereby disrupting beneficial bacterial effects, such as modulation of the immune response and provision of vitamins and amino acids to the scalp [26, 29–32]. This, along with Malassezia’s tendency to develop resistance to antiseptics (as azoles), forces researchers to seek and develop new dandruff treatments. In this regard, the use of enzymes targeting different cell wall structures holds promise. The Malassezia cell wall, comprising 5% chitin, 20% chitosan, and 75% glucans [33], offers a selective target for antifungal therapies. Chitinase and chitosanase- glycosyl hydrolases that degrade chitin and chitosan, respectively, target compounds absent in bacteria, potentially allowing selective inhibition of Malassezia spp.

Our hypothesis posits that upon suppression of Malassezia metabolism by chitinase and chitosanase, the scalp surface pH re-acidifies toward physiological levels, Cutibacterium acnes populations recover their protective function, and staphylococcal populations regress, thereby restoring normal barrier lipid composition and corneocyte cohesion. This study investigates the antifungal efficacy of chitinase and chitosanase against Malassezia spp. both in vitro and in vivo, compares their performance to conventional treatments, and assesses their safety on human fibroblasts alongside their influence on bacterial composition and diversity.

Materials and methods

Test ingredients

The following commercially available compounds with antiseptic activity, climbazole (Symrise AG, Holzminden, Germany), piroctone olamine (Shanghai Cosroma Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), selenium sulfide (Shanghai Cosroma Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), ZnPy (Salicylates and Chemicals Pvt. Ltd., Hyderabad, Telangana, India), and propanediol caprylate (Symrise AG, Holzminden, Germany), were used as positive controls. Solutions containing these compounds are summarized in Table S1.

The enzymes chitinase (100 U/g, 30% - pure enzyme from Aspergillus niger, 70%- Magnesium sulfate) and chitosanase (200 U/g, 30% - pure enzyme from Aspergillus niger, 70%- Magnesium sulfate), obtained from Shaanxi Pioneer Biotech Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China, are promising agents for use as anti-dandruff agents. The original data are presented in Table S2.

Test microorganisms and growth conditions

The following fungal strains were used for the enzyme studies and as positive controls for the growth inhibition of Malassezia spp.: M. furfur CBS 1878, M. restricta CBS 7877, and M. globosa CBS 7966 were purchased from the Westerdijk Institute in Utrecht, Netherlands. The strains were cultured in Sabouraud dextrose agar and then incubated in CHROMagar Malassezia® with Tween-40 and glycerol for visible growth at 37 °C for 72 h. An aliquot of the cell culture was diluted, and the cells were counted using a Neubauer chamber to ensure they reached the desirable inoculum concentration of 1.0 × 106 colony-forming unit (CFU)/mL. All microbiological analyses were performed in a microbiology safety cabinet (Faster, Italy).

Antifungal screening by determining Log10CFU reduction

The decimal logarithm of the colony-forming units per milliliter (Log10CFU) reduction in fungi induced by the tested antifungal substances was used to evaluate their inhibitory effect on M. furfur, M. globosa, and M. restricta. Briefly, following exposure of the fungal strains to the substances, they were quantitatively evaluated by determining the viable total colony counts. The test substances were prepared in water containing 0.3% (w/w) polysorbate 80, at final concentrations of 50.0, 30.0, 20.0, 10.0, 5.0, 2.5, and 1.25 mg/mL (equivalent to 5.00, 3.00, 2.00, 1.00, 0.50, 0.25, and 0.125% w/w). Polysorbate 80 was included in the aqueous solution to enhance the solubility and stability of the test substances, some of which exhibit limited water solubility. As a non-ionic surfactant, polysorbate 80 ensures homogeneous dispersion of the compounds. The benchmark antifungal drugs climbazole, piroctone olamine, ZnPy, propanediol caprylate and selenium sulfide, were used as controls. First, 1 mL mixture of the test microorganisms at a density of 1.0 × 106 CFU/mL was cultured with 9 mL of each sample concentration.

The experimental procedure involved incubating the substances with a known suspension of M. furfur, M. restricta, and M. globosa on substrate culture medium (Mueller-Hinton broth) at 37 °C for 1 h, which is the average time required for the potential anti-dandruff activity of ingredients to exert its effects. Next, the viable fungal population was determined by counting the viable cells on an agar plate (CHROMagar Malassezia). A suspension of the microorganism inoculum without any test substance was used as the negative control, and all experiments were performed in triplicate. The results were expressed as a decimal logarithm of the reduction in colony-forming units per milliliter (log10CFU/mL) and then converted to antifungal efficacy as a percentage of inhibition. All analyses were conducted in triplicate for statistical data processing.

Cytotoxity evaluation of the tested substances by MTT test and fluorescence microscopy with live/dead staining

Cytotoxicity of substances was assessed using cell lines of non-immortalised fibroblasts in the MTT metabolic test. This method is based on the reduction reaction of the yellow tetrazolium salt (3’-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide, MTT) by mitochondrial dehydrogenases of living cells to purple formazan crystals, which are insoluble in aqueous medium [34].

For the assay, cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 20% fetal calf serum, L-glutamine and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C until monolayer formation. To perform the test, 1000–3000 cells in 200 µl of culture medium are added to the wells of a 96-well plate depending on the proliferative potential. Cells are thoroughly suspended before introduction into the plate. The cell suspension was introduced into the wells of the plate using a multichannel pipette and sterile tips. The plates with cells were then incubated for 24 h in a CO2 incubator for cell adhesion to the substrate. Using a multichannel pipette and sterile tips, aliquots of prepared solutions of compounds at the tested concentrations were added to the wells of the plate with cells. After addition of the test substances, the cells were cultured in a CO2 incubator to maintain a stable physiological pH in the culture medium through a CO2-bicarbonate buffering system under standard conditions for 48 h. At the end of the incubation time, the culture medium with the test substances was removed from the plate. A mixture was prepared in a multi-channel pipetting tub: 9 ml of culture medium + 1 ml of MTT reagent (5 mg/ml in Hanks’ solution) per 96-well plate. The prepared reaction mixture was added in a volume of 100 µl into each well of the plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator for 3.5 h.

At the end of the incubation time, the culture medium with the tested compounds was removed, 100 µl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added and incubated for 5–10 min. The resulting violet staining was detected at a wavelength of 550 nm on a Tecan infinite 200 Pro microplate reader (Switzerland).

The ability of chitinase and chitosanase to exert cytotoxic effect on cells was also evaluated by microscopy with counting the area of viable cells in ‘BCAnalyzer’, semi-automated tool for the quantification of cell monolayer from microscopic images [35] on fibroblast cell lines.

Quantitative PCR evaluation of Chitinase and Chitosanase antifungal effects on scalp mycobiome

The primary objective of this study was to characterize the effects of chitinase and chitosanase solutions on the scalp mycobiota of 18 volunteers, with a focus on mitigating the microbial factors associated with scalp flaking in conventionally individuals. The study aimed to elucidate the potential of these enzymatic treatments in addressing the fungal causes of dandruff formation.

A transparent aqueous solution containing 0.25% chitinase and 0.25% chitosanase was utilized in this study. The research protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Kazan (Volga Region) Federal University (Protocol No. 48, dated May 23, 2024) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki following written informed consent from all participants. The study enrolled 18 volunteers (9 females and 9 males), aged 18 to 60 years, who met the following exclusion criteria: pregnancy or lactation; kidney or liver diseases; severe autoimmune disorders within the last 5 years; chemical treatments such as perming, keratin straightening, bleaching, or permanent waving within the last month; hair coloring within the last 14 days; use of antifungal medications or supplements within the last 60 days; use of anti-dandruff shampoos within the last 60 days; history of skin diseases within the last 3 years; systemic retinoid use within the last 60 days; and use of hair loss treatments within the last 60 days. Participants completed a questionnaire and underwent a dermatological examination of the scalp prior to the study.

A 4 × 4 cm area was selected on the hairy part of the scalp. Pre-treatment samples were collected from these areas using sterile swabs and placed in microtubes with lysis buffer. Subsequently, 1 mL of the enzyme solution was added to this area. After a 3-hour incubation, post-treatment samples were collected using sterile swabs and transferred to microtubes with lysis buffer for further analysis.

Nucleic acids were extracted using the LIRA reagent (BioLabMix, Russia). Swabs collected from the scalp surface were placed in 1 mL of LIRA reagent and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Chloroform (200 µL) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to the lysate, followed by vigorous shaking for 5 s and incubation for 5 min with intermittent manual mixing. The swab was removed, and the mixture was centrifuged at 10.000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting phases—organic (lower), interphase, and aqueous (upper)—were separated. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube for RNA extraction, while the organic phase and interphase were retained for subsequent DNA isolation.

For RNA extraction, an equal volume of isopropanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to the aqueous phase, mixed by inversion, and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The sample was centrifuged at 12.000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with 1 mL of 80% ethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After centrifugation at 12.000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was air-dried for 10 min. RNA was resuspended in 15 µL of RNase-free water and stored at −20 °C.

For DNA extraction, the organic phase and interphase were combined, homogenized, and mixed with 300 µL of 96% ethanol. After inversion and incubation for 5 min at room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 2.000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of 0.1 M sodium citrate in 10% ethanol (pH 8.5). Following incubation for 30 min at room temperature and centrifugation, the pellet was washed with 1 mL of 80% ethanol, incubated for 20 min, and centrifuged again. The dried pellet was dissolved in 15 µL of 40 mM NaOH, neutralized with 0.5 µL of 1 M HEPES, and stored at −20 °C.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using a BioRad CFX96 thermocycler (BioRad, USA) with the Biomaster HS-qPCR mix (BioLabMix, Russia) under manufacturer-recommended conditions [36]. The 50 µL reaction mixture contained 1× RT-qPCR buffer, 0.1 µM of each primer (Table S3), 0.1 µM dNTPs, 2.5% DMSO, 5% RT-qPCR mix, and DEPC-treated water. The ITS region of 26 S rRNA was used as reference gene. The primers were designed based on 26 S rRNA genes of Malassezia species.

The PCR protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 7 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 40 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 35 s [37]. Primer annealing temperatures were calculated using the Tm Calculator [38]. Real-time monitoring was performed after each elongation step.

qRT-PCR was performed using the BioRad CFX96 thermocycler (BioRad, USA) with the qOT-PCR SYBR Blue mix (BioLabMix, Russia), a one-step reverse transcription and real-time quantitative PCR mix containing the SYBR Green I fluorescent dye, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The 50 µL reaction mixture consisted of 1× RT-qPCR SYBR Blue buffer, 0.1 µM of each primer (Table S3), 0.1 µM dNTPs, 2.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 5% RT-qPCR mix, and DEPC-treated water. The primers were designed based on 26 S rRNA genes of Malassezia species.

The qRT-PCR protocol included a reverse transcription step at 45 °C for 30 min, followed by initial denaturation at 95 °C for 7 min and 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 55 °C for 40 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 35 s. Primer annealing temperatures were calculated using the Tm Calculator (https://tmcalculator.neb.com). Real-time fluorescence monitoring was conducted after each elongation step.

Statistical analysis

The research findings are presented as mean ± standard deviation, calculated from parallel replicates for each validation parameter. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, version 2016, Redmond, WA, USA, www.microsoft.com), Student’s t-test with the software Statistica (StatSoft, Inc. STATISTICA, version 9.0, Tulsa, OK, USA, www.statsoft.com) and GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, version 6.0, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com), which offers a comprehensive suite of statistical tools. This approach provides a robust method for assessing treatment-induced changes in gene expression and nucleic acid content. Results were considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05.

Metagenomic analysis of scalp Microbiome response to enzymatic treatment

The objective was to characterize the impact of chitinase-chitosanase enzymatic treatment on scalp bacterial composition in healthy volunteers.

The chitinase–chitosanase solution preparation, volunteer selection criteria, and scalp treatment protocol were identical to those used in the PCR experiments. A transparent aqueous solution containing 0.25% chitinase and 0.25% chitosanase was utilized in this study. The research protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Kazan (Volga Region) Federal University (Protocol No. 48, dated May 23, 2024) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki following written informed consent from all participants. The study enrolled 4 volunteers (2 females and 2 males), aged 18 to 60 years, who met the following exclusion criteria: pregnancy or lactation; kidney or liver diseases; severe autoimmune disorders within the last 5 years; chemical treatments such as perming, keratin straightening, bleaching, or permanent waving within the last month; hair coloring within the last 14 days; use of antifungal medications or supplements within the last 60 days; use of anti-dandruff shampoos within the last 60 days; history of skin diseases within the last 3 years; systemic retinoid use within the last 60 days; and use of hair loss treatments within the last 60 days. Participants completed a questionnaire and underwent a dermatological examination of the scalp prior to the study.

A 4 × 4 cm area was selected on the hairy part of the scalp. Pre-treatment samples were collected from these areas using sterile swabs and placed in microtubes. Subsequently, 1 mL of the enzyme solution was added to this area. After a 3-hour incubation, post-treatment samples were collected using sterile swabs and transferred to microtubes for further analysis.

DNA was extracted using the FastDNA™ SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Metagenomic libraries were prepared following the “Preparing 16S Ribosomal RNA Gene Amplicons for the Illumina MiSeq System” protocol. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the following primers: forward (5’-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’) and reverse (5’-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3’). Amplicons were indexed and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina, USA) in 2 × 300 bp paired-end mode.

Raw sequences were quality-checked with FastQC, and low-quality reads plus adapter/linker sequences were removed using Trimmomatic [40]. Filtered reads were processed in QIIME 2 (v.2022.8) [41] and denoised with DADA2 algorithm [42]. Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA 138 database clustered at 99% sequence identity [43]. Alpha diversity metrics (Shannon index, Simpson index, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, and Chao1) were calculated to assess bacterial diversity.

Statistical analysis was performed in Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, USA) and GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Group differences were evaluated by multiple t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U test.

Specifically, at a 3% concentration, the Log10CFU was 4.86, and it was < 1 only at a 5% concentration, indicating complete inhibition of fungal growth. Other positive controls, piroctone olamine, selenium sulfide, ZnPy, and propanediol caprylate, exhibited similar inhibitory effects on fungal growth, achieving complete inhibition at 0.25% (Fig. 1A). Surprisingly, the most innovative agent to date (propanediol caprylate) did not yield better results. At a concentration of 0.125%, selenium sulfide and propanediol caprylate showed a Log10CFU of 1.83 and 1.63, which is the same as the activity of piroctone olamine.

At a 5% concentration, chitinase inhibited Malassezia spp. growth by only 63.8% (log10CFU 0.44), while chitosanase achieved 67.8% inhibition (log10CFU 0.49). Such high enzyme concentrations may cause toxicity, indicating that neither chitinase nor chitosanase alone is optimal for inhibiting Malassezia spp.

However, the use of a mixture of both enzymes (chitinase and chitosanase) had a remarkable synergistic effect. The two enzymes at a 1:1 ratio and concentration of 0.25% inhibited the growth of Malassezia spp. by 98.38% (Log10CFU 1.79), which was comparable to the results obtained by commercially available products with proven antifungal effects. Furthermore, fungal growth was completely suppressed (Log10CFU < 1) at a concentration of 0.5%. The advantages of climbazole over classic antifungal products are evident. However, while climbazole suppressed fungal growth by 100% (Log10CFU < 1) at a concentration of 5%, the enzymatic combination of chitinase and chitosanase achieved this effect at a concentration that was 10 times lower (0.5%) (Fig. 1). Thus, significant synergy was observed, with a synergy index of 1.93, when chitinase and chitosanase were used in combination.

The cytotoxicity of chitinase and chitosanase against non-immortalized fibroblast cell lines was investigated at a concentration range of 0.0% to 15.6%. Figure S1 illustrates the residual metabolic activity of the cells in the presence of the tested compounds as a result of the MTT assay; however, it was not possible to determine the IC50 from these results.

Fibroblasts start to lose their shape, indicating the cytotoxic action of the compounds at 7.6% concentration of chitinase and chitosanase, while the most active inhibition of Malassezia spp. was observed from the concentration of 0.125%. These findings indicate that, within the range of concentrations evaluated for in vitro antifungal activity (0.125%–5% w/w) and for clinical study (0,25%), the enzyme combination does not exhibit cytotoxic effects.

The clinical study employed quantitative PCR to evaluate the antifungal efficacy of chitinase and chitosanase solution (0,25%) on the scalp mycobiome (specifically, M. furfur and M. restricta) of healthy volunteers (n = 18). Scalp samples were treated with a control solution and incubated for 3 h, followed by post-treatment sample collection. We selected two Malassezia species, M. restricta and M. furfur, as our first priorities for investigation due to specific structural and physiological characteristics that, compared with other dandruff-associated fungal species, could potentially impede enzymatic activity. M. restricta, according to recent studies, accounts for more than 80% of fungal sequences detected on dandruff-affected scalps [12] and exhibits a distinctive cell wall architecture enriched in chitosan in addition to chitin, with both polymers covalently cross-linked with β-glucans [45]. This highly cross-linked polymer network may reduce enzyme accessibility and thereby limit antifungal efficacy. The second species, M. furfur, was included in the PCR experiments despite its generally lower abundance on the scalp (although some articles indicate its prevalence over other Malassezia species [14, 46] because it possesses distinct resistance mechanisms, such as biofilm-forming capabilities [47]. The biofilm matrix may provide a physical barrier against enzyme activity.

The results revealed significant reductions in the DNA and rRNA content of key fungal species, M. furfur and M. restricta, relative to the total mycobiome, as normalized by the ITS region: a 2.4-fold reduction in M. furfur DNA content (Fig. 2A) and a 1.9-fold reduction in M. restricta DNA content (Fig. 2B) were observed after the treatment. These results suggest that combination of chitinase and chitosanase effectively degrades fungal cell walls, leading to a decrease in fungal DNA load. This is consistent with the enzymatic activity of chitinase and chitosanase, which target chitin and chitosan, respectively, key structural components of fungal cell walls.

Fold reduction in M. furfur and M. restricta DNA (A, B) and RNA (C, D) after treatment with enzyme solution (0.25% chitinase + 0.25% chitosanase), normalized to total mycobiome ITS region. Data represent medians (n = 18)

The reduction in rRNA content was more pronounced, with a 2.4-fold decrease for M. furfur (Fig. 2C) and a 4.6-fold decrease for M. restricta (Fig. 2D). The greater reduction in RNA levels compared to DNA may reflect the enzymes’ impact on fungal viability and metabolic activity, as RNA serves as a more dynamic indicator of cellular function than DNA. A substantial body of work demonstrates that ribosomal RNA quantification by RT-qPCR provides a reliable indicator of metabolically active fungal cells [48, 49]. While rRNA may be less responsive than mRNA to immediate transcriptional changes, it offers greater stability during sample processing and is more reliable than DNA for viability assessment. Analysis of rRNA molecules specifically targets metabolically active fungal cells, given that rRNA is continuously synthesized in viable organisms and predominantly incorporated into ribosomes, the essential machinery for protein synthesis. The reduction in rRNA content suggests that the treatment not only reduces fungal load but also suppresses fungal activity.

However, it is important to acknowledge that a subset of subjects did not exhibit a favorable response. In several cases, DNA and RNA levels of Malassezia spp. remained unchanged or even showed modest increase following treatment. Such non-response likely reflects intrinsic differences in scalp physiology. Despite these individual disparities, the use of median fold-change values demonstrates an overall treatment effect. Median values are less influenced by outliers or extreme values, providing a more robust representation of central tendency in datasets with potential skewness. This is particularly relevant in clinical studies involving human subjects, where inter-individual variability in mycobiome composition and response to treatment is expected. By using medians, the results better reflect the typical response across the study population, enhancing the reliability and interpretability of the findings.

Subsequently, we conducted a metagenomic analysis to assess bacterial diversity and scalp microbiota structure in volunteers (n = 4) before and after treatment with a chitinase-chitosanase enzyme solution. A total of 434,251 raw reads were obtained, yielding 257,143 merged non-chimeric sequences after processing, representing an average of 32,143 valid sequences per sample.

Figure 3 summarizes bacterial diversity metrics between pre- and post-treatment groups, including Chao1, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Faith PD), Shannon diversity, Simpson diversity, and the number of identified operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Significant differences in microbiota biodiversity before and after treatment were detected for Chao1 and OTU metrics. Treatment of the scalp with chitinase and chitosanase enzymes increased alpha diversity (Chao1 index) and the number of identifiable operational taxonomic units (OTUs) by twofold after 3 h of incubation.

Species-level identification was not included due to low confidence in species-level determination. At the phylum level, significant differences were identified for Firmicutes representatives, with further class-level differences observed for Bacilli (increased abundance), and at the order and family levels for Bacillales, including the genus Bacillus and Enterococcus. Discussion

The combination of chitinase and chitosanase enzymes demonstrated antifungal efficacy against Malassezia spp. that was comparable to, or exceeded, that of standard positive controls (selenium sulfide, zinc pyrithione, propanediol caprylate, and piroctone olamine) at the same 0.25% concentration, yet without broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects on the scalp microbiome. By specifically cleaving chitin and chitosan in the fungal cell wall, polymers absent from bacterial membranes, these enzymes selectively inhibit Malassezia growth while sparing beneficial bacteria. The observed reductions in M. furfur and M. restricta DNA and RNA content highlight the potential of chitinase and chitosanase as effective antifungal agents for modulating the scalp mycobiome. The greater impact on RNA levels suggests that these enzymes not only reduce fungal load but also inhibit fungal metabolic activity, which could be crucial for managing conditions associated with Malassezia overgrowth, such as dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis.

Metagenomic analysis further revealed that within three hours of enzymatic treatment, bacterial alpha diversity (Chao1) and OTU richness doubled, reflecting rapid ecological niche release and subsequent recolonization following fungal suppression. Although the canonical Cutibacterium/Staphylococcus ratio was not restored within the limited observation period, we detected a marked increase in the relative abundance of Bacillus spp., taxa not traditionally associated with dandruff pathogenesis but recognized for secreting lipases and proteases that modulate sebum composition and skin pH, as well as producing antimicrobial peptides capable of suppressing inflammatory taxa such as Staphylococcus spp [50, 51]. Thus, the transient expansion of Bacillus may serve as an ecological “bridge,” creating favorable environmental conditions for the eventual recovery of commensal bacteria. Indeed, the initial decrease in Cutibacterium appears counterintuitive given the established role of these bacteria in maintaining scalp microbiome stability, but likely reflects a normal biphasic recovery pattern in which resident populations temporarily decline after ecosystem disturbance before rebounding under optimized conditions.

Taken together, these data support the potential use of a chitinase–chitosanase combination as a targeted, resistance-avoiding dandruff therapy that inhibits Malassezia spp. growth and metabolism and fosters long-term microbiome restoration without the broad-spectrum antiseptic effects of conventional products.

Limitations of the study include the small sample size (n = 18) and the lack of quantitative PCR measurements of M. globosa DNA and RNA. In addition, assessment of clinical parameters such as flaking, itching, dryness, and changes in scalp pH, as well as a detailed assessment of potential side effects, were not incorporated into the current experimental design. The antifungal activity of the chitinase- chitosanase combination was examined for a limited set of Malassezia species (M. furfur, M. globosa, M. restricta), meaning that further work could broaden this investigation to other species potentially implicated in dandruff.

Future studies should address these limitations by collecting comprehensive clinical data to assess both the long-term efficacy and safety profile of the enzymatic formulation. In addition, subsequent research should aim to elucidate critical aspects of the chitinase–chitosanase mechanism of action, including the kinetics of Malassezia inhibition, effects on the fungal cell wall assessed by scanning electron microscopy, and potential impacts on epidermal barrier integrity and function.
