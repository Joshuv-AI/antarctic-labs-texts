The association between serum follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone levels and congestive heart failure in postmenopausal women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hangyu Zhou, Saiyaremu Xierzhati, Diliyaer Adili, Tingting Wang, Tiantian Wang, Zhao Wang, Wenzheng Zhou, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to explore the relationship between follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, serum luteinizing hormone (LH) levels, and the risk of congestive heart failure (CHF) in postmenopausal women. While earlier investigations have looked into the possible connection between serum sex hormones and heart health, the results have not provided clear conclusions. This study included a total of 2,853 postmenopausal women who visited the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region. The associations between FSH, LH, and CHF were explored through univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. To analyze the dose-response relationship, a generalized additive model (GAM) was utilized. Additionally, sensitivity analysis, mediation analysis, and subgroup analysis were performed to confirm the reliability of these findings. The discriminatory ability of FSH and LH for distinguishing CHF was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). Their incremental value was further evaluated with continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Clinical utility was examined using decision curve analysis (DCA). Findings from the comprehensive logistic regression analysis indicated a notable inverse relationship between FSH and LH levels and the occurrence of CHF. The odds ratios (OR) [95% confidence intervals (CI)] corresponding to each standard deviation increase in FSH and LH were 0.75 (0.67 ~ 0.85) and 0.88 (0.78 ~ 0.99), respectively. The GAM identified critical thresholds for FSH and LH. When FSH was below 69.21 mIU/mL, each increase of one standard deviation was associated with a 36% reduction in the risk of HF, OR: 0.64 (0.52–0.78). When LH levels were below 26.49 mIU/mL, each increase of one standard deviation was associated with a 40% reduction in the risk of HF, OR: 0.60 (0.45–0.80). Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis both confirmed robustness. Mediation analysis revealed that the association between LH and heart failure was influenced by estrogen and progesterone, whereas FSH was not mediated by these hormones. In summary, this research uncovers a complex relationship between the levels of FSH and LH and the occurrence of CHF in women after menopause. These results emphasize the critical role of hormonal balance in the cardiovascular well-being of postmenopausal females, shedding light on the connection between gonadotropins and CHF. These findings could inform future approaches to the prevention and management of CHF, while also stressing the necessity for extensive prospective studies to confirm these findings and investigate their potential clinical significance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43697-0.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a severe manifestation or advanced stage of various heart diseases, with persistently high mortality and readmission rates1. According to statistics, the global prevalence of HF is approximately 1–2%, with a total patient population exceeding 64 million, and it continues to rise with the aging population and the increasing burden of chronic diseases2–4. This condition poses a major public health issue worldwide, making it essential to identify high-risk HF patients early for effective treatment and management. HF is also the main cause of cardiovascular death among postmenopausal women5. After women enter menopause, the risk of HF significantly increases, which is not only associated with advancing age but also related to changes in ovarian hormone levels during and after menopause6. Therefore, assessing the sex hormone levels in postmenopausal women and incorporating them into risk stratification may better prevent and manage HF.

Traditionally, endogenous sex hormones in women have been viewed primarily as regulators of the reproductive system. However, ongoing scholarly research is gradually elucidating the relationship between endogenous sex hormones and cardiovascular diseases, along with their associated risk factors. Despite these advancements, the function of sex hormones in the onset and advancement of heart disease among postmenopausal women remains unclear, as most research has focused on the impacts of estrogen (estradiol, E2) and testosterone7. The protective effects of estrogen on the heart are significant, particularly as estrogen levels decline in postmenopausal women, leading to a marked increase in the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD)8. Elevated testosterone levels in women are associated with an increased risk of coronary heart disease and HF9. However, few reports have focused on the role of gonadotropins in the progression of heart disease in postmenopausal women. FSH is involved in stimulating the growth and development of ovarian follicles and inducing estrogen production. However, apart from its presence in reproductive tissues, FSH receptors are also expressed in blood vessels, liver, and adipose tissue10–12. LH, which triggers ovulation, promotes and maintains the corpus luteum, and regulates the secretion of progesterone, is also expressed in receptors outside of reproductive tissues, such as in the adrenal glands13, mammary glands14 and tumor cells15. These provide the molecular basis for their non-reproductive functions. Previous studies have found that FSH and LH are negatively correlated with the risk of diabetes, insulin resistance, obesity, and metabolic syndrome16–19. Currently, there is limited research on the association between LH and FSH and heart failure in postmenopausal women.

Therefore, our research is a large-scale cross-sectional study that utilizes case data from a major tertiary hospital in Xinjiang, China, aiming to provide new insights into the relationship between FSH and LH levels in postmenopausal female participants and heart failure.

Materials and methods

This study, conducted in a cross-sectional format, drew upon data from the electronic medical records at the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, spanning from January 2018 to December 2023. A total of 4947 subjects were included in the study, of which 2853 postmenopausal female patients were selected for final analysis, as shown in Supplementary Material Fig. S1. The inclusion criteria were defined as postmenopausal women, characterized as subjects who reported cessation of menstruation for at least 12 months, aged 55 years or older, or who had undergone hysterectomy or oophorectomy at least one year prior. Exclusion criteria included: pregnant women, FSH < 25.0 IU/L (as per the recommendations of the 2011 Reproductive Aging Workshop, late perimenopausal FSH levels are characterized by ≥ 25 IU/L)20, patients with renal impairment, severe liver disease, autoimmune diseases, or malignant tumors.This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. This study is a retrospective analysis. All data were anonymized before the start of the research. The Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, which presented minimal risk to subjects as defined in Article 32 of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Congestive heart failure assessment

In this study, the diagnosis of congestive heart failure was based on discharge diagnoses. It is defined by the presence of symptoms and/or signs of heart failure: dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, reduced exercise tolerance, easy fatigue, ankle swelling, elevated jugular venous pressure, hepatic jugular reflux, third heart sound (gallop rhythm), and left ventricular apical impulse displaced laterally, as detailed in Supplementary Material Table S1, along with objective evidence of heart failure: (1) NT-ProBNP ≥ 125 pg/mL or BNP ≥ 35 pg/mL, (2) abnormal echocardiographic findings.

Covariates

We extracted baseline information at admission from the electronic medical record system, including patient age, weight, height, smoking status, alcohol consumption, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), as well as reproductive history, diabetes history, coronary heart disease, and hypertension history. Additionally, based on laboratory data, we obtained the following laboratory parameters: albumin, creatinine (Cr), uric acid (UA), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), hemoglobin (Hb), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), FSH, LH, E2, testosterone, and progesterone. The calculation method for body mass index (BMI) is weight (in kilograms) divided by height (in meters) squared (m²). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the creatinine equation from the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration21: Scr (mg/dL) ≤ 0.7,eGFR = 142×(Scr/0.7​)−1.2 × (0.9938)age×1.012 ,Scr (mg/dL) > 0.7,eGFR = 142×(Scr/0.7​)−0.241 × (0.9938)age×1.012. Other biochemical indicators were measured using an automatic analyzer (Model 7600-010: Suzhou Hitachi Medical Systems Co., Ltd., China) by enzymatic methods. FSH and LH were determined using a fully automated chemiluminescent immunoassay analyzer (Access 2 Immunoassay System: Beckman Coulter, Inc., USA) through chemiluminescent immunoassay methods. Estradiol was evaluated with a fully automated electrochemiluminescent immunoassay analyzer (Cobas e 601: Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) through electrochemiluminescent immunoassay methods. Progesterone was determined using a fully automated chemiluminescent immunoassay analyzer (Centaur XPT: Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Inc., Germany) through chemiluminescent immunoassay methods. Testosterone was measured using a fully automated chemiluminescent immunoassay analyzer (UniCel DxI 800: Beckman Coulter, Inc., USA) through chemiluminescent immunoassay methods.

Statistical analysis

Missing data imputation was performed using linear and logistic regression models, with final imputed values derived through nearest neighbor matching based on the predictive mean matching (PMM) framework. The number (percentage) of missing covariates is displayed in Supplementary Material Table S4. Continuous variables that conform to a normal distribution are presented as mean (standard deviation), while those that do not conform are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed using variance tests, while those that do not conform were analyzed using non-parametric rank-sum tests. The differences in baseline characteristics of categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Both FSH and LH were analyzed as continuous and categorical variables in univariate and multivariate logistic regression models to determine the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for their association with CHF prevalence. Three regression models were developed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, BMI, and reproductive history), and Model 3 (adjusted for age, BMI, reproductive history, diastolic and systolic blood pressure, Hb, TG, TC, HDL, LDL, albumin, eGFR, uric acid, smoking, drinking, coronary heart disease, diabetes, and hypertension history). To address multicollinearity issues, we screened covariates using the variance inflation factor (VIF), including only variables with VIF < 5 (see Supplementary Material Tables S2 and S3). Generalized additive models (GAM) were utilized to investigate the nonlinear relationship between FSH and LH with CHF. A partitioning algorithm was used to identify the inflection points of the nonlinearity, and a segmented regression model was applied to describe the association characteristics on either side of the inflection points. The likelihood ratio test was used to compare the goodness of fit between the models containing nonlinear terms and traditional linear models. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the robustness of the results. First, to reduce the influence of extreme outliers on the results, we excluded data for LH and FSH that were below the 1st percentile and above the 99th percentile, and conducted analyses accordingly. Additionally, based on Model 3, testosterone, E2, and progesterone were included for multivariate logistic regression analysis. Stratified analysis examined the moderating effects of demographic and clinical variables such as age, reproductive history, lifestyle factors (alcohol consumption/smoking), cardiovascular and metabolic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease), and BMI on the outcomes, and assessed the interaction between subgroups using likelihood ratio tests. Mediation analysis was conducted to investigate whether estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone mediated the relationship between FSH and LH with heart failure, with the size of the indirect effects, the proportion of mediation effects, and the p-values for mediation effects all reported in the results. ROC analysis and AUC were employed to assess the discriminatory performance of LH and FSH for CHF. Continuous NRI and IDI metrics were used to quantify the improvement in the model’s capacity to distinguish participants after including LH and FSH. DCA was utilized to evaluate clinical utility.All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2, developed by the R Core Team, located in Vienna, Austria). Statistical tests were two-tailed, with p-values below 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical profiles

We investigated the history of CHF in 2853 postmenopausal female participants attending the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region. We found that 484 cases had a documented history of CHF. Participants with lower serum levels of FSH and LH had a higher proportion of CHF patients. Specifically, the prevalence of heart failure among the FSH tertile groups was 23.1%, 15.5%, and 12.3%, respectively. The prevalence of heart failure among the LH tertile groups was 22.2%, 14.8%, and 13.9%, respectively, as detailed in Fig. 1.

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics stratified by FSH tertiles. It can be observed that participants with lower FSH levels were younger and had lower levels of hemoglobin, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, albumin, and luteinizing hormone, as well as a lower proportion of having one child (P < 0.05). Table 2 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics according to LH tertile groups. It was found that participants with lower LH levels had significantly lower levels of hemoglobin, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, albumin, and FSH, as well as a lower proportion of having one child (P < 0.05). Conversely, they exhibited significantly higher levels of estradiol, progesterone, triglycerides, eGFR, and a higher prevalence of previous hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, and having two or more children (P < 0.05).

Associations of FSH and LH indices with prevalence of CHF

To explore the association between LH and FSH concerning the occurrence of CHF, we formulated three regression models and performed a logistic regression analysis. In Model 1 for FSH, the odds ratio (OR) was 0.70 (range: 0.63 to 0.77), which implies that every standard deviation rise corresponded to a 30% reduction in CHF prevalence. In the fully adjusted Model 3, the OR was found to be 0.75 (range: 0.67 to 0.85), indicating a 25% decrease in CHF prevalence for each standard deviation increase, with all p-values being less than 0.01. For LH, Model 1 reported an OR of 0.77 (range: 0.69 to 0.86), suggesting a 23% decrease in CHF prevalence with every standard deviation increase. In Model 3, the OR was 0.88 (range: 0.78 to 0.99), reflecting a 12% reduction in CHF prevalence for each standard deviation rise, with p-values all below 0.05, as displayed in Table 3. Although there were slight changes in OR values following the adjustment for confounding variables, they remained significant. Following this, we divided FSH and LH into three categories based on tertile rankings. The findings showed that in the fully adjusted Model 3, the ORs for FSH in tertiles T2 and T3 as compared toT1 were 0.68 (range: 0.52 to 0.88) and 0.55 (range: 0.41 to 0.73), respectively. Regarding LH, the fully adjusted Model 3 indicated that the ORs for T2 and T3 compared to T1 were both 0.75 (range: 0.58 to 0.98) and 0.75 (range: 0.57 to 0.99), respectively. Dose‒response relationships of FSH andLH with CHF

To determine the dose-response relationship between FSH and LH with heart failure, we utilized a GAM and identified a nonlinear correlation, as shown in Fig. 2. Specifically, when FSH levels are below 69.21 mIU/mL, each standard deviation increase is associated with a 36% reduction in the risk of heart failure, with an OR of 0.64 (0.52–0.78). Conversely, when FSH levels exceed 69.21 mIU/mL, no statistical association was observed, with an OR of 1.09 (0.76–1.58). Additionally, when LH levels are below 26.49 mIU/mL, each standard deviation increase correlates with a 40% reduction in the risk of heart failure, with an OR of 0.60 (0.45–0.80). However, when LH levels are above 26.49 mIU/mL, no association with heart failure was found. Further details can be found in Table 4.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analyses of the associations between FSH, LH and CHF

Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted on factors such as age, BMI, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, history of coronary heart disease, and reproductive history to assess whether baseline or demographic characteristics influence the association between FSH and LH with heart failure prevalence. In the subgroup and interaction analysis of FSH, it was found that there was no statistically significant interaction between FSH and any stratified subgroup, except for reproductive history, as shown in Fig. 3. Similarly, the results for LH indicated that only the reproductive history subgroup exhibited an interaction with LH, as detailed in Supplementary Materials Fig. S2. Specifically, in the FSH study, patients with a reproductive history of more than two children showed the strongest correlation between FSH and CHF, while no statistical association was observed in patients with one child or no reproductive history. The same results were obtained in the LH study. We also performed sensitivity analyses; first, to reduce the impact of extreme outliers on the results, we excluded data where LH and FSH were below the 1st percentile and above the 99th percentile, and the results remained robust, as indicated in Supplementary Materials Table S7. Furthermore, based on Model 3, we incorporated testosterone, estradiol, and progesterone into a multivariable logistic regression analysis, ultimately finding that the results remained unchanged, as shown in Supplementary Materials Table S8.

Mediation effect assessment

To assess the mediating effects of testosterone, estradiol, and progesterone, the downstream hormones regulated by FSH and LH, in the associations studied, we conducted a mediation analysis. The results indicated that no significant mediating effects of testosterone, estradiol, and progesterone were found in FSH (P > 0.05), as shown in the Supplementary Materials Table S5. In LH, we identified mediating effects of estradiol and progesterone, with mediation proportions of 17.14% and 13.8%, respectively (P < 0.05), while no significant mediating effect of testosterone was observed (P > 0.05), as shown in the Supplementary Materials Table 6.

Model evaluation for LH and FSH

ROC curves were generated to assess the association of LH and FSH levels with CHF status. The AUC (95% CI) for FSH was 0.79 (0.76–0.81), and for LH, it was 0.77 (0.75–0.80), as shown in Supplementary Materials Figs. S3 and S4. When added to Model 3, LH and FSH improved the model’s performance for distinguishing heart failure cases (Supplementary Materials Table S9). The continuous NRI for FSH was 0.0744 (0.0380–0.1139), and the IDI was 0.0202 (0.0132–0.0269). For LH, the continuous NRI was 0.0314 (0.0044–0.0604), and the IDI was 0.0089 (0.0036–0.0137). Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) indicated that incorporating LH and FSH could provide clinical net benefit in evaluating CHF prevalence (Supplementary Materials Figs. S5 and S6).

Discussion

We utilized data from 2853 postmenopausal female participants to analyze the correlation between FSH, LH, and the prevalence of CHF. Overall, our study is the first to reveal a negative correlation between FSH, LH, and the risk of HF in postmenopausal women. Specifically, (1) there is a significant negative correlation between FSH and LH levels and the prevalence of HF in postmenopausal women, and this association exhibits a non-linear relationship, with corresponding inflection points being explored. (2) After adjusting for E2, the association between FSH and heart failure remained significant, revealing that FSH may be independently associated with HF and is not influenced by E2. (3) The association between LH and HF may be influenced by the mediating effects of estrogen and progesterone. (4) The above associations were more pronounced in postmenopausal women with a history of more than two pregnancies.

CHF exhibits significant gender differences and is associated with various age groups. In younger individuals, the incidence of CHF is higher in men than in women, whereas in older age, the incidence in women far exceeds that in men. Therefore, changes in hormones in postmenopausal women may be related to the onset of cardiovascular diseases. Extensive research has explored the connection between natural sex hormones and cardiovascular disease (CVD), a major global health concern, yet the specific relationship between gonadotropins and CHF is still being studied. In research focusing on gonadotropins in cardiovascular diseases and risk factors, existing studies have produced inconsistent results, underscoring the need for additional research. In previous epidemiological studies, gonadotropins have shown gender differences in cardiovascular diseases and risk factors. In a retrospective case-control study, it was found that FSH levels were significantly positively correlated with arteriosclerosis in obese men, suggesting that FSH may increase the risk of cardiovascular disease by promoting vascular sclerosis in males22. Studies have shown that the correlation between LH and familial premature coronary heart disease remains significant after adjusting for traditional risk factors23. In a study investigating the relationship between serum FSH and estradiol levels and carotid intima-media thickness (IMT) in 587 postmenopausal women, a significant negative correlation was observed between FSH levels and subclinical atherosclerosis, independent of E224. Another study indicated that serum FSH levels in postmenopausal women are negatively correlated with the 10-year ASCVD risk, suggesting that low FSH could serve as a risk factor or biomarker for cardiovascular disease in this population25. A cross-sectional study found that in postmenopausal women with type 2 diabetes, FSH was negatively associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and this association was independent26. A study derived from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database revealed that both FSH and LH are negatively associated with metabolic syndrome and its severity in postmenopausal women27. NAFLD and metabolic syndrome are independent risk factors for the onset of cardiovascular diseases, which to some extent illustrates the association between FSH, LH, and cardiovascular health. However, a cross-sectional study from China revealed that in postmenopausal women with type 2 diabetes, elevated levels of LH were independently and positively associated with increased high-sensitivity troponin T levels28.

Current research has identified the mechanisms of FSH in cardiovascular diseases and risk factors, particularly in postmenopausal women and patients undergoing androgen deprivation therapy. Some mechanistic studies have proposed theoretical potential benefits, but evidence for cardiovascular protective mechanisms is limited. Other studies have found that FSH promotes inflammation, endothelial damage, and the progression of atherosclerosis. FSH can participate in lipid metabolism and energy balance regulation through its receptor (FSHR), theoretically contributing to the maintenance of lipid homeostasis and energy metabolism, thereby indirectly supporting cardiovascular health. FSH can regulate lipid metabolism in the liver and white adipose tissue through FSHR. Animal experiments have shown that FSH treatment can reduce hepatic triglyceride content and affect the expression of genes related to lipogenesis and carbohydrate transport in adipose tissue, suggesting that FSH contributes to the reduction of fat synthesis and storage29,30. FSH promotes lipid storage and energy supply by activating the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway, regulating lipid droplet formation and related gene expression, thereby improving the metabolic state of organs31. FSH can influence immune cell function and regulate inflammatory responses. Moderate levels of FSH may contribute to immune homeostasis, indirectly reducing the adverse effects of chronic inflammation on cardiovascular health32. In current research, the mechanisms of LH in cardiovascular function have been less studied. The indirect effects of LH on cardiovascular diseases are primarily achieved through the regulation of sex hormones, influencing vascular endothelial function, and altering the metabolic environment. Although some mechanisms may have adverse effects, the role of LH in maintaining sex hormone balance could potentially benefit cardiovascular health under specific physiological conditions33. Animal experiments have found that elevated LH inhibits the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, reduces eNOS phosphorylation and NO synthesis, leading to decreased vascular relaxation and aggravated atherosclerosis34. FSH and LH theoretically have certain beneficial effects on cardiovascular health through mechanisms such as regulating hormone balance, endothelial function, and immune responses. However, direct evidence is limited, and more research has focused on their adverse effects. The impact of LH and FSH on cardiovascular diseases and heart failure in postmenopausal women still lacks clinical evidence. Further exploration of their potential protective mechanisms and clinical significance is needed in the future.

Since LH and FSH are gonadotropins, they typically act on their respective target organs in the body to produce other sex hormones, thereby exerting their effects35. Readers may be concerned that the association between LH and FSH with HF is not direct, but rather may be mediated through their influence on estrogen levels, which in turn could be linked to heart failure. In other words, the observed negative correlation might actually stem from the protective effect of estrogen on heart failure in women36,37. First, we considered estradiol, testosterone, and progesterone as mediating factors and conducted a mediation effect analysis. The results showed no mediating effects of the aforementioned sex hormones. Subsequently, we performed a sensitivity analysis by treating them as potential confounding factors for adjustment. We included estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone in the model for analysis, and the results showed no significant changes. This suggests that the association between FSH and HF is not influenced by estradiol. Previous studies have also indicated that estradiol levels are not associated with the risk of CVD in non-users of postmenopausal hormone replacement therapy38. In LH, our mediation analysis revealed that its association with HF involves the joint effects of progesterone and estrogen. Sensitivity analyses adjusted for estradiol, testosterone, and progesterone also indicated that the association between LH and heart failure is not independent and may be influenced by estrogen and progesterone. Numerous studies have demonstrated that progesterone can modulate immune responses, suppress inflammation, and alleviate oxidative stress, which may contribute to reducing the risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases in women38,39.

It is noteworthy that our subgroup analysis revealed a more pronounced association between FSH and LH and heart failure in individuals with a reproductive history of more than two. In fact, the number of reproductive events (such as the number of pregnancies and live births) is positively correlated with the risk of cardiovascular disease in women; the greater the number of reproductive events, the higher the risk of heart failure, and it is an independent risk factor. This association has been confirmed in multiple large-scale cohort studies, genetic epidemiological studies, and cross-sectional studies40–42. The more pregnancies a woman has, the lower her FSH levels. A study on postmenopausal women found that the number of pregnancies is significantly negatively correlated with FSH levels, and this relationship is independent of factors such as age, hormones, and obesity2. Multiple pregnancies lead to a decrease in FSH levels, which may partially explain the mechanism behind the increased cardiovascular risk in multiparous women43. In our study, the results may also be attributed to the varying proportions of reproductive history. Subgroup analysis is considered exploratory, and future research may need to increase the number of individuals in other groups and conduct prospective longitudinal studies to validate our findings.

In our study, it was found that low levels of LH and FSH in postmenopausal women are significantly associated with an increased risk of CHF, potentially serving as biochemical indicators for this condition. Furthermore, the levels of LH and FSH exhibit a non-linear negative correlation with the prevalence of CHF, and this association exists only within specific threshold ranges; the inflection point of this relationship was identified through threshold effect analysis in this study. Additionally, our research revealed that this association is particularly pronounced in individuals with a reproductive history of more than two children. These results have important implications for clinical practice, as LH and FSH levels in postmenopausal women could serve as valuable markers for identifying heart failure. For those with low LH and FSH levels, further cardiovascular evaluations and interventions might be essential to avert the advancement of heart failure. Furthermore, sustaining adequate FSH levels could be crucial in preventing heart failure onset, particularly in women with a history of multiple pregnancies. However, to gain a deeper understanding of the connection between gonadotropins and heart failure, more research is necessary to confirm and investigate the underlying mechanisms. Thus, additional studies are needed to evaluate the potential therapeutic benefits of regulating LH and FSH in relation to heart failure development before these findings can be integrated into clinical practice.

However, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the cross‑sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between gonadotropin levels and CHF. Second, several potentially important confounders were not available in our dataset, including the use of hormone replacement therapy and medications that may influence both hormone profiles and heart failure risk (e.g., cardioprotective drugs). Third, the outcome definition did not distinguish between subtypes of heart failure, which may represent distinct pathophysiological entities and could obscure potential subtype‑specific associations. Fourth, FSH and LH levels can be influenced by various pre‑analytical and analytical factors, such as the timing of blood sampling and laboratory assay methods, which may introduce measurement variability. Finally, as the study was conducted within a single tertiary center in Xinjiang, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited. Future prospective, longitudinal studies that collect detailed information on medication use, hormone therapy, and heart failure phenotypes are warranted to validate and extend our observations.

Conclusions

This study investigated the correlation between FSH and LH and the prevalence of HF, revealing a significant negative correlation between LH and FSH levels and HF in postmenopausal women. This association exhibited a non-linear pattern, and corresponding inflection points were explored. After adjusting for estradiol, the association between FSH and HF remained significant, suggesting that FSH may be independently associated with HF and is not influenced by estradiol. The association between LH and HF may be mediated by the effects of estrogen and progesterone. The associations between LH and FSH and HF were more pronounced in postmenopausal women with a history of more than two pregnancies. These results carry significant implications for clinical practices and preventive strategies, offering new avenues for the prevention and management of CHF.
