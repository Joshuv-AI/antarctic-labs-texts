Zinc-phosphorus nanodomains with dynamic coordination for electrochemical CO2 reduction
Authors: Guiru Zhang, Ning Ji, Liuxuan Luo, Xiangrui Li, Peng Shen, Rui Xue, Shu Yuan, Junyi Huang, Congfan Zhao, Tianzi Bi, Xiaojing Cheng, Yangge Guo, Xiaohui Yan, Xiaozhi Su, Shuiyun Shen, Junliang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-atom catalysts (SACs) are favored owing to their well-defined motifs and notable CO selectivity in electrochemical CO2 reduction. However, the dynamic coordination evolution under working conditions and the structure-activity relationships remain highly puzzling. Herein, we suggest that flexible Zn–P3 nanodomains with weak-bond interactions may exhibit a potential-dependent structural modulation under working conditions compared to Zn–N4 configurations. A possible metastable state with reduced Zn–Px-like coordination may be formed from the Zn–P3 structure under CO2 reduction working conditions. Based on in situ spectroscopy analysis, deuterium isotope kinetic analysis, and theoretical calculations, the low-coordination Zn and the possibly perturbed P sites may serve as efficient active sites for the first step of sequential proton–electron transfer in the CO2 electroreduction. The collaborative assignments within this dynamic catalytic nanodomain, rather than at individual metal site, may contribute to the improved the intrinsic activity, which delivers a high turnover frequency (47,690 h−1) and a CO production rate (213 mL h−1 cm−2) at 500 mA cm−2 for 100 h. Our work elucidates the dynamic coordination-governed mechanisms of SACs and underscores the necessity of probing their structural basis under operating conditions.Subject terms: Catalytic mechanisms, Electrocatalysis, Electronic properties and materials

Introduction

The electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) powered by renewable electricity is a promising route for producing high-value chemicals to achieve carbon neutrality1–3. Among the various products of the CO2RR, highly reduced species such as methanol, ethylene4, and n-propanol5 have attracted considerable interest6–8. However, CO is widely considered the most viable candidate for industrial implementation because of its high selectivity and considerable industrial demand9,10. A pioneering study by Hori et al.11,12 showed that noble metal electrodes (e.g., Au and Ag) can effectively catalyze the conversion of CO2 to CO. However, the high cost and moderate selectivity of noble metal electrodes have limited their practical application as industrial catalysts13,14. Recent studies have demonstrated the exceptional performance of carbon-supported single-atom catalysts (SACs)15–17, which incorporate low-cost transition metals such as Ni18, Fe19, and Zn20. Although the bulk forms of these metals have negligible activity for CO production during the CO2RR, their atomically dispersed counterparts (e.g., M–N4–C configurations) achieve notably high CO Faradaic efficiencies (FEs)21.

Furthermore, although the formation of the 2e−-product CO is straightforward, it involves two relatively high energy barriers, that is, *CO2 adsorption and *COOH formation22. Various M–N4 motifs are still constrained by one of these two processes. Vijay et al.22 reported that the limiting step on Fe–N4 is *CO2 adsorption, whereas on Ni–N4, it is *COOH formation. Therefore, designing efficient active sites that simultaneously facilitate *CO2 adsorption and *COOH formation is the key to overcoming the limitations of CO2-to-CO conversion23. Moreover, the limitations of the relatively rigid M–N4 motif were made evident in the recent research on SACs16. The strong interactions between the host and doped heteroatoms stabilize the M–N4 motif and restrict its activity24,25. Consequently, various strategies such as unsaturated coordination26, second-shell doping27, and axial regulation28 have been used to modulate the local environment of SACs to improve their catalytic performance. However, it is unclear whether these structures maintain stability and serve as the activity origin because increasingly detailed studies have revealed the various transformations of SACs under working conditions29–31. Qiu et al.32 recently revealed the potential-dependent structural evolution of Fe–N–C active sites under CO2RR working conditions. Under such conditions, a novel transient dangling active site, which delivers superior activity to Fe–N4 motifs, is generated on Fe–N–C SACs, where the Fe single-atom leaves the anchoring site by breaking the Fe–C bond while maintaining stable binding with the substrate only using Fe–N bonds. This suggests that flexible dynamic catalytic nanodomains, driven by the electrochemical environment, may be the structural basis for the electrocatalytic structure–activity relationship.

Herein, we synthesized a Zn-P-C single-atom catalytic structure with excellent CO2RR properties, which benefits from the dynamic coordination effect between the single-atom center and the local environment. The distinctive Zn–P3 nanodomains exhibit a more flexible coordination environment than conventional Zn–N4. Due to the dynamic weak interaction between Zn and P, a plausible CO2-associated reconstruction pathway may lead to a *CO2–Zn–Px configuration with reduced coordination under CO2RR working conditions. Moreover, the observed transformation of the interfacial hydrogen bond network under these conditions likely provides a more favorable proton-transfer microenvironment for enhancing the CO selectivity of the CO2RR, compared to the competing hydrogen evolution pathway. Theoretical calculations also support that this coordination evolution may help mitigate the trade-off relationship between *CO2 adsorption and *COOH formation. This strategy improves the intrinsic activity of the Zn–P3 catalyst for the electrosynthesis of CO and realizes efficient operation in a zero-gap electrolysis system.

Results and discussion

Catalyst synthesis and characterization

We synthesized Zn–P and Zn–N SACs with different structural domains using a bottom-up strategy, as shown in Fig. 1a. Triphenylphosphine (PPh3) and urea were used as precursors to introduce phosphorus (P) and nitrogen (N) heteroatoms, respectively, into the graphene matrix. After freeze-drying and calcination, the final P- (Zn-P-C) and N-doped (Zn-N-C) carbon-supported Zn-based SACs were obtained. The zinc contents in these catalysts were determined via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), which were 2.38 wt.% and 2.52 wt.% for Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C (Supplementary Table 1), respectively.

The morphology of the catalysts was studied via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Fig. 1b). Two-dimensional (2D) energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to study the elemental distribution on the surfaces of both SACs (Supplementary Fig. 1). The SEM images of both catalysts exhibited a nanosheet structure. The nanosheets with a thickness of 10–30 nm were stacked together, exposing numerous steps and stacking faults. Spherical aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) of Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C revealed the atomic distribution of Zn single-atoms with bright contrast along the z-direction33 and without ordered lattice fringes (Fig. 1c, e). These results confirm the syntheses of both Zn-based SACs. As shown in Fig. 1d, f, P and N heteroatoms were uniformly distributed in Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C, respectively.

Raman spectroscopy was used to analyze the defect densities of Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C using pristine graphene as a reference (Fig. 1g). The peaks at 1350 cm−1 and 1580 cm−1 corresponded to the D and G bands of graphene, respectively34. Normally, the ratio of the intensity of the D band to that of the G band (i.e., ID/IG ratio) increases with the defect density. However, the ID/IG ratios of Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C were lower than that of graphene, indicating the occurrence of lattice self-repair during annealing. It also suggests that Zn atoms are anchored at defect sites in the carbon network. These findings are in agreement with the specific surface areas of both catalysts obtained via N2 Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis. Defect-engineered graphene exhibited a larger surface area than both Zn-based catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Table 2).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to confirm the structure and dispersion of Zn in both catalysts (Fig. 2a). The diffraction peaks at 26.6° and 54.8° corresponded to the crystalline structure of graphene (PDF#029-0661). No Zn-related signals (Zn, PDF#042-1014; ZnP2, PDF#001-8137; and ZnN, PDF#067-0710) were observed, consistent with HAADF-STEM results. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to analyze the valence states and atomic environments of catalysts. All XPS spectra were calibrated using the C 1 s signal (284.8 eV, Supplementary Fig. 3). As illustrated in Fig. 2b, a distinct P 2p signal (133.4 eV) appeared on Zn-P-C, along with a Zn 3 s signal at 139.5 eV. N 1 s region for Zn-N-C was observed notable signal convolution. The N species with higher binding energy are attributed to graphitic N anchored on the carbon network, whereas the pyridinic N species at lower binding energy are typically bonded to Zn atoms35. As shown in Fig. 2c, Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C exhibited the Zn 2p peak at 1021.8 eV, corresponding to Zn2+. Notably, the Zn LMM Auger peak of Zn-P-C was shifted by − 1.7 eV compared with that of Zn-N-C, indicating the distinct coordination environments for Zn in the two samples.

We further investigated the energy shifts induced by different coordinations using X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). The Zn K-edge XANES spectra of the two Zn-based catalysts and the reference are shown in Fig. 2d. The near-edge absorption positions of the two Zn-based catalysts were near that of ZnO. In addition, the intensity of the white line peak in Zn-N-C was higher than that in Zn-P-C, indicating the higher oxidation state of Zn species in Zn-N-C. Figure 2e shows the coordination environments of Zn-based SACs from their Fourier-transformed k3-weighted EXAFS spectra. The first-shell length of Zn-N-C was slightly shorter than the Zn–O bond length (1.5 Å) in ZnO (Supplementary Fig. 4). This was attributed to the Zn–N motif, as confirmed by a previous study36. In contrast, the first-shell signal of Zn-P-C was observed at 1.9 Å, which is longer than the Zn–C bond length (1.4 Å) but shorter than the Zn–Zn bond length (2.3 Å), implying the scattering of the Zn–P motif. Figure 2f and Supplementary Fig. 5 compare the fittings of the EXAFS spectra of the two SACs, and the detailed fitting parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The fitting result for Zn-N-C revealed a Zn–N4 nanodomain (coordination number = 4.33) with a phase-corrected distance of 1.98 Å. By contrast, for Zn-P-C, the Zn–P coordination number was 3.24, with a phase-corrected distance of 2.33 Å, indicating a Zn–P3 nanodomain. Electronic local function calculations showed that lower coordination numbers and higher bond lengths weakened the bonding strength within the Zn–P motif, with free electrons localized in a ring domain formed by the three P atoms (Supplementary Fig. 6). The relatively constant fluctuation of energy in ab initio molecular dynamics simulations at 10 ps confirmed the stability of the Zn–P3 structure (Supplementary Fig. 7), which was further supported by the fact that no Zn-Zn metal bonding signal was observed after calcination. As shown in Fig. 2g, the 2D WT-EXAFS plots provide more accurate coordination information. The 2D WT-EXAFS plots in the k-space exhibit a signal at 5.4 Å−1 for Zn–P, which is considerably higher than that (4.1 Å−1) for Zn–N, probably due to the stronger scattering capability of P atoms compared to N atoms8.

Electrocatalytic CO2RR performance

We compared the intrinsic activities of Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C in an H-type cell, and gaseous and liquid products were quantified using gas chromatography and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 8), respectively37. As shown in Fig. 3a, Zn-P-C exhibited a higher reaction current density than Zn-N-C in the wide potential window from − 0.1 to − 1.0 V. Zn-P-C exhibited a current density of 22.7 mA cm−2 at − 0.89 V. The gaseous products included CO and H2, with only trace amounts of formate observed in the catholyte (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 9–11). The CO FE on Zn-P-C remained above 90% in the potential window from − 0.44 to − 0.89 V, significantly outperforming Zn-N-C. The low CO FEs on the P/N-doped graphene samples confirms that the true electrochemical CO2RR sites originate from the Zn–P3 and Zn–N4 coordination structures (Supplementary Fig. 12). To account for the potential electrochemical surface area (ECSA) effect, we compared the double-layer capacitance-normalized jCO,ECSA of both SACs. As shown in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 13–14, the jCO,ECSA of Zn-P-C at − 0.89 V was 18.1 mA cm−2, which is approximately twofold higher than that on Zn-N-C. This suggests that the contribution of exposed Zinc active sites to CO electrosynthesis is limited, and P coordination could be responsible for the increase in Zn-P-C.

To explore the industrial potential of Zn-P-C SACs, we evaluated the CO2RR performance of Zn-P-C sprayed onto a commercial GDE (Zn-P-C/GDE) in a zero-gap electrolyzer (Supplementary Fig. 15). As shown in Fig.e 3d, the CO FE remained above 90% in the current density range of 100–1000 mA cm−2. The highest full-cell energy efficiency (EE) was 44.7% at 100 mA cm−2. Furthermore, Zn-P-C maintained 93% of the CO FE and 35% of the EE during a 100 h stability test at 500 mA cm−2 (Fig. 3e). After pro-longed operation, salt deposition was observed on the catalyst surface, and it was the key limitation for the durability of the zero-gap electrolyzer (Supplementary Fig. 16)38. The spent catalyst exhibited a consistent active site density and sustained > 80% of the CO FE in subsequent testing in an H-type cell (Supplementary Fig. 17). Water contact angle tests demonstrated the sustained hydrophobicity of the catalyst, and SEM and XPS analyses revealed the formation of a cesium salt after 100-h operation (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19 and Table 4). However, this degradation in system performance is unrelated to the catalyst structure, as HADDF-STEM imaging and XPS show that, compared to the fresh catalyst, the spent catalyst maintains good atomic-level dispersion of Zn, with its valence state and composition with P remaining essentially stable (Supplementary Figs. 20–21). In subsequent improvements, greater emphasis should be placed on enhancing the cation electromigration shielding capability of the anion exchange membrane or exploring stability testing within cation exchange membrane systems. Furthermore, operations such as designing a hydrophobic cathode gas flow channel and introducing volatile acids have been proven effective in preventing salt precipitation39,40. For a broader context, we compared the CO2-to-CO conversion performance of Zn-P-C with those of other reported state-of-the-art M-N-C motif SACs. The data are derived from stability tests. While these materials exhibited competitive CO FEs, Zn-P-C was the best N-coordinated SAC in terms of activity and stability, achieving a turnover frequency of 47,690 h−1 and a CO production rate of 213 mL h−1 cm−2 (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 22 and Tables 5, 6)24,41,42.

Considering the significant market potential for CO, we performed the technoeconomic analysis of the electrochemical CO2-to-CO conversion process (for detailed parameters, see Supplementary Note 1). Single-variable sensitivity analysis revealed that balancing renewable electricity costs and reaction current densities is critical for overcoming feasibility constraints (Supplementary Fig. 23). Waterfall analysis was used to identify a pathway to achieve competitive CO production costs ($440 ton−1). The initial step of the roadmap emphasizes the reduction of costs associated with renewable electricity and CO2 feedstock. These costs are influenced by the scale of renewable energy deployment and advancements in carbon capture technologies, and they are mostly independent of CO2RR performance. Increasing the current density from 100 mA cm−2 to 500 mA cm−2 can reduce CO production costs by ~ 30%. Further improvements in equipment, operation, and separation will have a smaller impact. This is because high current densities facilitate more compact systems and decrease electrolyte regeneration costs. We hypothesize that the outstanding performance of the Zn-P-C catalyst underscores its suitability for industrial CO production via the CO2RR. A reduction in electricity prices to $0.03 kWh−1 will also accelerate the commercialization of CO2-to-CO conversion technologies (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 24).

Mechanistic studies

To monitor the dynamic evolution of Zn-P-C during the CO2RR, we performed in situ XAS measurements. As shown in the inset of Fig. 4a, the Zn K-edge in Zn-P-C shows no significant change with increasing cathodic potential. Only a slight increase in the white line peak intensity is observed at relatively high overpotentials. This may be related to subtle changes in the coordination structure of Zn, though such a trend could be influenced by measurement errors and is therefore not conclusive. To more accurately examine the local microenvironment around the Zn, the k3-weighted Fourier-transformed EXAFS spectra are presented in Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 25. The Zn–P signal at 1.9 Å weakens noticeably as the cathodic potential increases. Based on previous in situ studies of the coordination environment of SACs, we believe this trend exceeds the fluctuations caused by systematic noise43,44. Accordingly, we performed fitting on these spectra within the given k‑space window (3–11 Å−1), with the relevant fitting parameters provided in Supplementary Table S7. It can be observed that the CN of Zn–P gradually decreases from 3.26 (OCP‑1) to 2.41 (– 1.2 V), suggesting a potential-dependent modulation of the local Zn–P coordination, possibly towards a partially reconstructed Zn–Px-like state during CO2RR. (Supplementary Figs. 26, 27). Furthermore, DFT calculations indicate that the stability of these two structures is thermodynamically comparable. Notably, when the potential returns to OCP‑2, the Zn–P coordination number (3.09 ± 0.31) mostly recovers to OCP-1, and no metal–metal bond signals are detected throughout the measurement process. This demonstrates that the dynamic evolution of the Zn–P structure is constrained, and thus reveals that the dynamic stability and antiagglomeration properties of Zn–P3 nanodomains14,29. Since EXAFS fitting results are statistically averaged and carry certain uncertainties, we further analyzed the valence states of Zn and P using quasi‑in-situ XPS. Compared to the Zn 2p orbitals, the Zn LMM spectrum is known to exhibit larger energy shifts in response to valence changes. As shown in Fig. 4c, the Zn LMM signal at 987.8 eV shifts toward higher kinetic energy with applied potential. This may result from Zn beginning to bond with highly electronegative elements, thereby decreasing electron density. Due to the current lack of suitable in situ characterization techniques at the sub‑nanometer scale, we suggest that a possible potential-dependent weakening of Zn–P bonds may be related to the formation of a *CO2-Zn-Px structure after carbon‑terminal adsorption of CO2, with supplementary support from theoretical calculations (Supplementary Note 2 and Fig. 28). Bonding analysis via the crystal orbital Hamilton populations (COHP) analysis after CO2 adsorption shows that approximately 0.72e⁻ are transferred to the *CO2 intermediates when the highly electronegative carbon terminal adsorbs onto the Zn surface. The Zn–P bonding orbitals shift above the Fermi level, and the antibonding orbitals further strengthen, resulting in an increase in the ICOHP value (– 0.65 eV). This is consistent with the high‑energy shift of the Zn LMM peak during CO2RR, indicating a weakening of the Zn–P bond energy. Furthermore, comparison with the Zn LMM spectrum of Zn-N-C reveals that this shift occurs only within the applied CO2RR potential window and recovers upon returning to OCP (Supplementary Fig. 29). This potential-dependent behavior is also consistent with the observed changes in Zn coordination number from EXAFS, suggesting that some Zn–P3 sites may undergo a dynamic coordination change, possibly towards a Zn–Px-like environment. In addition, the P 2p spectrum splits under CO2RR conditions (Fig. 4d), showing a new signal attributable to P–O species. This indicates that the detached P atoms formed after Zn–P bond cleavage may serve as adsorption sites for interfacial species such as *OH, which is generated from interfacial H2O or *COOH intermediates.

Subsequently, we employed in situ ATR-SEIRAS to investigate the catalytic mechanism under the dynamic Zn–P coordination (Supplementary Fig. 30). As shown in Fig. 4e, f, the peaks at 2118 cm−1, 1880 cm−1, and 1650 cm−1 corresponded to CO2, CO, and O–H vibrations from H2O, respectively45. The peaks at 1295 cm−1, 1381 cm−1, and 1420 cm−1 were associated with the interconversion of carbonate and bicarbonate on the electrode surface46. In the potential windows from − 0.8 to − 1.1 V, Zn-P-C exhibited a 2.5-fold higher CO signal intensity than Zn-N-C (Supplementary Fig. 31). For Zn-P-C, the intensity of the shoulder peak at 1705 cm−1 gradually increased, which is consistent with the formation of the *COOH intermediate and supported by vibrational frequency simulations (Supplementary Fig. 32). This *COOH peak was absent in Zn-N-C, probably due to the weak signal intensity and overlap with the water peak, as suggested by simulations. Theoretically, because the CO2/H2O ratio is inversely related to the reaction current density, Zn-P-C, with its higher CO2RR activity, should exhibit a stronger H2O signal with an increase in CO2 consumption47. However, we observed that the intensity of the water peak for Zn-P-C was notably lower than for Zn-N-C. We deconvoluted the water peak (2700–3700 cm−1), which remained unaffected by the adsorption of carbon-based intermediates (Fig. 4f). According to the previous studies, the deconvoluted vibrational frequencies corresponded to three distinct forms of water, that is, free water (f-H2O), hydrogen-bonded water (h-H2O), and protonated water (p-H2O)48. As shown in Fig. 4f, Zn-P-C exhibited a reduced total intensity of the water peak within the voltage range from − 0.2 to − 1.0 V, consistent with the signal observed at 1650 cm−1. Supplementary Fig. 33 summarizes the potential-dependent intensity proportion of three forms of water. The proportion of p-H2O increased at more negative potentials, consistent with spectroscopic results from previous studies. This demonstrated that p-H2O supplied protons for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), while f-H2O provided protons for the CO2RR. Zn-P-C exhibited a lower p-H2O proportion and a higher f-H2O proportion than Zn-N-C, explaining its higher CO FE within the H-type cell. Notably, the proportion of f-H2O on Zn-P-C increased at potentials below −0.6 V, unlike that on Zn-N-C. This trend has not been observed for previous metallic plate electrodes48. By analyzing the structure of interface water in different electrolyte environments, we found that the increase in the proportion of f-H2O at high overpotential is enhanced by larger cations, and it exists in the presence of bicarbonate or carbon dioxide (Supplementary Figs. 34–36). Previous work has indicated that bicarbonate ions can also serve as a carbon source for CO2RR at the interface, suggesting that the switching of the nano-domain needs to occur under the CO2RR conditions. Pulse electrolysis also showed that when no c CO2RR occurs, the proportion of free water does not undergo this reversal. Finally, in the deuterated electrolyte, tests on Zn–P–C also showed an f-H2O reversal trend, but the magnitude would be significantly reduced. This may be attributed to the denser hydrogen-bond network in deuterated water, which weakens the influence of the Zn–P interface on the hydrogen-bond network interaction (Supplementary Fig. 37). It is worth noting, however, that due to the limitations of interfacial spectroscopic techniques—which primarily infer the hydrogen-bond network indirectly based on vibrational frequencies—it remains difficult to directly determine whether this effect arises from changes in the catalytic structure, the presence of CO2RR intermediates, or a combination of both. Consequently, the proposed link between Zn–P coordination modulation and altered water structure should be viewed as a plausible interpretation consistent with the available data, rather than a definitive causal relationship.

The deuterium kinetic isotope effect (KIE) can distinguish whether protons are involved in the rate-determining step of the CO2RR process. Typically, KIE > 1 indicates that the reaction is limited by proton-involved elementary steps, while KIE ≈ 1 suggests that the rate-determining step (RDS) is not influenced by protons49,50. The RDS typically occurs during the initial proton and electron transfer processes, that is, before *COOH formation. For single-site materials, the electron and proton are generally considered to be transferred sequentially (sequential proton–electron transfer; SPET)51,52. We recorded the LSV curves of both catalysts in 0.1 M CsHCO3/H2O and 0.1 M CsDCO3/D2O electrolytes, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5a, Zn-N-C exhibited a significantly lower current density in D2O than in H2O. The inset in Fig. 5a shows that the KIE gradually increased from 1.5 to 2.1 in the potential range from − 0.6 to − 1.2 V, indicating that proton transport was the RDS in the CO2RR. Zn-P-C showed only a slight decrease in the current density in D2O, with the KIE remaining stable at ~ 1.2 in the range from − 0.6 to − 1.2 V (Fig. 5b). The smaller KIE value of Zn-P-C indicated that its RDS is CO2 adsorption that is not affected by proton transport. The Tafel slope of 145 mV dec−1 fitted on Zn-P-C is close to the theoretical Tafel slope for CO2 adsorption (118 mV dec−1; Supplementary Fig. 38)53.

The first-principles calculation was conducted for CO2-to-CO conversion based on the density functional theory (DFT). The influence of interface water is considered through an explicit solvent model. Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C models were constructed by introducing Zn and P/N atoms into graphene to form Zn–P3 and Zn–N4 motifs, with reference to EXAFS fitting results (Fig. 2f). While the precise causal relationship between CO2 adsorption and coordination changes remains to be fully established, a plausible CO2-associated reconstruction pathway is considered below. Figure 5c shows the reaction free energies for CO2-to-CO conversion (Supplementary Fig. 39, 40), which serve as supportive computational information for the experimental mechanistic understanding described above. *CO2 and *H co-adsorbed states (*CO2 + *H; yellow field) directly affected by proton transport were considered between Volmer and Heyrovsky steps. While calculated *CO2 + *H was metastable on Zn-N-C, the subsequent Heyrovsky step was expected to be exothermic. The RDS on the Zn-N-C catalyst is governed by proton transport, imposing an RDS energy barrier of 0.68 eV (or 0.59 eV for the direct *CO2 → *COOH conversion pathway). In contrast, *CO2 + *H on Zn-P-C was more facile (ΔG = 0.22 eV). Unlike the fixed Zn–N4 coordination during the CO2RR, the Zn–P3 site may undergo a structural modulation upon CO2 adsorption, with a possible transient weakening of one Zn–P bond, leading to a *CO2–Zn–Px-type configuration, as observed via in situ XAS measurements. During the process, the Zn–P motif split up into two adsorption sites, among which activated Zn enhanced CO2 adsorption (Supplementary Figs. 41, 42), whereas detached P facilitated the interfacial proton transport. Then, *CO2 on the Zn site and *H on the P site combined to form *COOH (ΔG = − 0.37 eV). Transition-state calculations (Supplementary Figs. 43, 44) for proton transport demonstrated that the P site showed a lower *H migration activation energy (0.52 eV) than the N site on Zn-N-C (1.49 eV). This dynamic evolution resulted in an RDS of 0.32 eV for Zn-P-C, occurring during *CO2 adsorption. Furthermore, we considered the possibility that Zn-P3 may evolve into other configurations during in-situ dynamic reconstruction, influenced by interfacial hydroxyl radical species (Supplementary Figs. 45, 46, Table 8 and Supplementary Data 1). Comprehensive thermodynamic pathway calculations for CO2RR were performed on these possible configurations. The results indicate that the Zn-PX structure exhibits a lower RDS energy barrier occurred at step (1) compared to other reconstructed configurations, and certain oxygen-containing groups can embed into Zn-P3 nanodomains, leading to irreversible reconstruction, which may be another contributing factor to the decay in catalytic activity observed during the durability test. Charge density difference and Bader charge analyses revealed that Zn and P were electron-deficient, with a considerably higher charge density around P than N (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 47). As shown in Figs. 5e, f, the COHPs of Zn–N and Zn–P bonds indicated that the antibonding orbitals of Zn–P lay above the Fermi level (Ef). Compared to the Zn–N bond ( − 0.86 eV), the Zn–P pair exhibits a weaker ICOHP contribution ( − 0.71 eV), which also explains its potential-dependent modulation of Zn–P coordination in CO2RR54. Furthermore, projected density of states (PDOS) analysis indicated asymmetric spin-polarized orbital distributions near the Ef for Zn–P3. These delocalized electrons promoted the evolution of the key intermediates (Supplementary Figs. 48, 49)55,56.

Discussion

In conclusion, we developed a unique Zn–P catalytic nanodomain driven by a dynamic weak bonding effect in carbon-based SACs. The Zn-P-C catalyst exhibited the best CO2-to-CO conversion performance among all reported catalysts in a zero-gap electrolyzer, achieving stable operation for 100 h at an industrial current density of 500 mA cm−2. Combining multiple in situ spectroscopic techniques and DFT calculations, we suggested that the high activity of the Zn‑P catalytic nanodomain is associated with its dynamic coordination. Under working conditions, a possible partially reconstructed Zn–Px-like state is suggested as a potential key structure for lowering the energy barrier of the first SPET in CO2RR. This study highlighted the role of dynamic catalytic nanodomains in electrochemical reactions, offering fundamental insights into the design of carbon-supported SACs.

Methods

Chemicals

Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(Ac)2·2H2O, 99.995%), urea(CO(NH2)2, 99.999%), triphenylphosphine (PPh3, 99%) and ethanol (CH3CH2OH, 99.8%) was purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd. Cesium carbonate (Cs2CO3, 99.9%), Cesium hydroxide monohydrate (CsOH·H2O, 99.9%), Deuterium oxide (D2O, 99.9%) and graphene nanosheets (5–10 μm) were purchased from Meryer Chem. Tech. Co., Ltd. High purity CO2 ( > 99.999%) and Ar ( > 99.999%) were purchased from Air Liquide Co., Ltd. Alkaline ionomer solution (Sustainion XA-9, 5% in ethanol) was purchased from SCI materials Hub. All chemicals were used directly without further purification.

Synthesis of P/N doping graphene

150 mg of GNs, 100 mg of PPh3, and 100 mL of ethanol were sequentially added to an open beaker. The GNs-PPh3 mixture was then stirred in the open beaker to accelerate the evaporation of ethanol. Once the solvent had evaporated, the GNs-PPh3 mixture was vacuum-dried for 24 h and transferred to a high-temperature tube furnace under an Ar atmosphere. The temperature was increased at a rate of 5 °C per minute to 800 °C and held for 1 h. After annealing, the phosphorus-doped graphene material was collected.

In a separate step, 100 mg of GNs and 1 g of urea were thoroughly ground using a mortar and pestle and then transferred to an alumina crucible. The GNs-urea mixture was annealed in a high-temperature tube furnace filled with Ar. The temperature was increased at a rate of 5 °C per minute to 800 °C and held for 1 h. After annealing, the nitrogen-doped graphene material was collected.

Synthesis of Zn-P-C and Zn-N-C

To prepare the zinc-doped materials, 100 mg of phosphorus- or nitrogen-doped graphene and 33.6 mg of Zn(Ac)2·2H2O were mixed and added to a centrifuge tube containing 20 mL of ultrapure water. The mixture was sonicated for 2 h. The solution was then frozen using liquid nitrogen and further dried in a freeze-dryer for 48 h. The dried samples were subsequently annealed in a tube furnace under an argon atmosphere, heated at a rate of 10 °C per minute to 600 °C, and held at that temperature for 2 h. After annealing, the Zn-P-C or Zn-N-C material was obtained.

Material characterizations

The sample morphologies were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, TESCAN MIRA3 LHM) and bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X G2). Spherical aberration corrected high-angle annular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (HADDF-STEM) images were captured by a probe-corrected STEM (Spectra 300) working at 200 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were acquired using an ESCALAB QXi instrument. Quasi-in situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was conducted of catalysts drop-casted on carbon paper, as same as mentioned in the electrodes section. Before testing, the electrolytes should be CO2-saturated to remove the dissolved oxygen in the solution. Samples were conditioned at different potentials for 30 min as one conditioning step. After that, the samples were rapidly transferred to the XPS vacuum chamber without exposure to air. That would prevent air from influencing the catalysts, thereby maintaining the authentic state during the CO2RR process. XPS binding energy was calibrated to 284.8 eV using the adventitious alkyl carbon C 1 s peak. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas were carried out using a nitrogen adsorption apparatus (BSD Instrument 3H-2000PS). Before measurement, the samples were degassed in a vacuum at 350 °C for 12 h. The liquid electrolysis products were analyzed by Bruker Avance-III by 1H-NMR through a water suppression mode. Raman spectra were measured on Renishaw inVia Qontor using a laser with a wavelength of 532 nm. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) information of the sample was analyzed on Bruker D8 and compared with PDF cards. The concentration of metal elements in the sample was quantified by ICP-MS from Thermo Fisher.

Electrochemical measurements

A customized gastight H-type glass cell separated by Nafion 115 membrane (Fuel Cell Store) was deployed for intrinsic activity evaluation, where a high-purity graphite rod (99.995%, Aldrich) and an SCE were used as the counter and the reference electrode, respectively37. Certain amounts of Cs2CO3 (99.99%, Adamas-Beta) were dissolved in Milli-Q water to prepare the 0.05 M electrolyte, which was further purified by electrolysis between two graphite rods at 0.1 mA for 24 h to remove trace amounts of metal ion impurities. Prior to bulk electrolysis, 50 sccm CO2 (99.995%, Air Liquide) was bubbled for at least 30 min to get the 0.1 M CO2-saturated CsHCO3 electrolyte. The solution resistance (Ru) was determined using potentiostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS) at frequencies ranging from 0.1 Hz to 200 kHz, and manually compensated as E (iR-corrected vs. RHE) = E (vs. RHE) − Ru × i (amps of averaged current). All potentials (if not specifically mentioned) in this work were converted to the RHE scale by E(vs. RHE) = E(vs. SCE) + 0.244 V + 0.0591 × pHbulk and E(vs RHE) = E(vs Ag/AgCl) + 0.197 V + 0.0591 × pHbulk.

For the zero-gap electrolysis system, a customized reactor consisting of a 1 cm2 Zn-P-C cathode and a 4 cm2 IrO2-coated Ti fiber felt anode. Cathode ink is prepared by mixing and dispersing 0.5 mg cm−2 Zn-P-C catalyst with Sustainion XA-9 ionomer in anhydrous ethanol solution. And manually spray onto a commercial Sigracet 28BC gas diffusion electrode after ultrasonic dispersion for 1 h. Use 9 cm2 QAPPT-25 μm as the anion membrane and activate it in 1 M KOH for 24 h beforehand. The anode electrolyte is 0.1 M CsOH. The electrolyte was circulated through a peristaltic pump (Longer Pump BT100-2J) at a flow rate of 3.6 mL min−1. A 20 sccm CO2 flow was delivered through the cathodic Ti flow field using an Alicat mass flow controller. A digital volumetric flowmeter detects the exhaust flow rate. All the electrochemical measurements were conducted at room temperature using a Biologic SP-200 potentiostat.

In situ XAS measurements

Electrochemical in situ Zn K-edge XAS of Zn-P-C was carried out at BL14W1 beamline of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) operated at 3.5 GeV under top-up mode with a constant current of 220 mA. The photon energy of the incident X-ray was calibrated using Zn metal foil as the reference spectrum. The Zn-P-C is sprayed on conductive carbon paper, and the ink is configured in the same way as the CO2 reduction measurement. Ag/AgCl and Pt plates were used as the reference and counter electrode, respectively. CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 is used as an electrolyte. The EXAFS spectra were obtained by subtracting the post-edge background from the overall absorption and then normalizing with respect to the edge jump step. Then, χ(k) data in the k-space ranging from about 3 to 12 Å−1 were Fourier transformed to real (R) space using Athena software. The quantitative information can be obtained by the least-squares curve fitting in the R space with a Fourier transform k space range of 3 to 12 Å−1, using the Athena and Aetemis software.

In situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements

Electrochemical in situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements were performed on a Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS20 infrared spectrometer equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled detector. The ~ 50 nm Au layer is deposited on the optical window surface of a single-crystal silicon by electroless plating. The Zn-based single-atom catalyst ink was then sprayed onto the surface of the Au layer and assembled in a spectroelectrochemical cell as a working electrode. The Ag/AgCl and graphite rod were used as reference and counter electrode, respectively. In a CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte, the background infrared spectrum was obtained at the open circuit potential. Subsequently, in situ ATR-SEIRAS spectra were collected within a potential window of 0 V to − 1.1 V.

Computational methods

We perform spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations using Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP, version 6.1.0) code57,58 with the projected-augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential method59 and the generalized gradient approximation revised Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional60. For geometry optimizations, 3 × 3 × 1 k-points meshes with a the original Monkhorst-Pack scheme was used, and convergence criteria for energy and force were set to 10−5 eV and 0.03 eV Å−1, respectively, and the kinetic energy cutoff was set to 450 eV. The solution environment of CO2RR was simulated by an explicit solvent model. Spin polarization and dipole correction are considered. A 15 Å vacuum layer in the z-axis direction was added to avoid the interfere of the layer image coupling caused by the periodic boundary conditions. 6 × 6 × 2 k-points meshes were used for static calculations. The Gibbs free energies of reaction were calculated based on the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) method that assumes the equivalent chemical potential between 0.5 H2 and a pair of proton and electron (H+ + e−) at 0 VRHE and the standard conditions61. All correction values for gaseous molecules and adsorbates are summarized in Supplementary Table 8, respectively. To obtain the temperature and energy state, constrained ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) has been used with the slow-growth method. At the beginning of the MD simulation, models have been heated up to 300 K by velocity scaling over 1.5 ps and then equilibrated at 300 K for 10 ps with a 1-fs time step.

Crystal Orbital Hamiltonian Population (COHP) analysis was performed using the LOBSTER package62. This analysis identifies bonding and antibonding states of the interaction between adsorbates and the catalyst surface. By integrating COHP up to the Fermi level (ICOHP), one can compare the binding strength of adsorbates on different catalyst surfaces quantitatively63.
