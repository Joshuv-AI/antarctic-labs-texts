Gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to diabetic nephropathy via affecting renal lipid deposition and inflammatory responses
Authors: Ping Liu, Ping Hu, Meiping Jin, Yemeng Wan, Jiajun Wu, Weiqian Sun, Yuyan Tang, Lusheng Huang, Dongliang Zhang, Danye Shi, Ting Xie, Yijing Tong, Hui zheng, Lishun Wang, Haidong He, Xudong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic nephropathy (DN) patients exhibit distinct patterns of gut microbiota dysbiosis. Nevertheless, the causal relationship between gut microbiota dysbiosis and DN pathogenesis remains to be established.MethodsFecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) was performed using donor samples from both DN patients and diabetic controls, with healthy donor microbiota serving as the control intervention in STZ/high-fat diet-induced DN model. Renal function was assessed by measuring biochemical indicators such as urinary protein and serum creatinine. Renal pathological changes were observed using PAS staining and Masson staining, and the composition of the intestinal microbiota was analyzed by 16 S rRNA sequencing. Furthermore, the effects of FMT-HC on renal gene expression and lipid metabolism were investigated through transcriptome sequencing, Western blotting, and Oil Red O staining.ResultsCompared with the control group, the renal function of mice with DN microbiota transplantation was significantly deteriorated, as evidenced by increased proteinuria and aggravated renal tubular interstitial fibrosis, accompanied by impaired intestinal barrier function and reduced abundance of Lactobacillus. After FMT-HC intervention, renal injury in DN mice was significantly alleviated, lipid deposition was reduced, inflammatory responses were downregulated, and beneficial bacteria such as L. johnsonii were significantly enriched in the intestine. In addition, RNA seq revealed that FMT-HC exert a protective effect on diabetic nephropathy by regulating the PPAR signaling pathway, thereby improving renal lipid metabolism disorders.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to diabetic nephropathy. The regulation of renal lipid deposition and inflammatory responses might involve the process of DN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07832-9.Keywords: Diabetic nephropathy, Fecal microbiota transplantation, Lipid metabolism, PPAR signaling pathway

Introduction

With the continuous global rise in the prevalence of diabetes, diabetic kidney disease has emerged as the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD). This condition significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular disease and end-stage renal disease, ultimately leading to dialysis, high mortality rates, and severe medical-economic burdens [1, 2]. Current first-line treatments for DN, such as nonsteroidal selective mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and sodium-dependent glucose transporters 2, have shown limited efficacy [3]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutics in clinical practice. Particularly, it is important to explore new methods to delay the progression of diabetic kidney disease and reduce the risk of its advancement to end-stage renal disease.

In recent years, the regulatory role of gut microbiota in metabolic diseases has emerged as a significant focus of scientific investigation [4]. Accumulating clinical evidence demonstrates that DN patients exhibit distinct patterns of gut microbiota dysbiosis. Notably, as renal function declines, DN patients experience a marked reduction in microbial diversity, characterized by decreased abundance of beneficial bacterial taxa (including Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia) alongside relative proliferation of opportunistic pathogens (such as Enterobacteriaceae and Desulfovibrio). Importantly, this microbial imbalance shows strong correlation with disease severity and progression [5–8].

The gut microbiota has recently emerged as a key regulator of lipid metabolism in metabolic diseases. Accumulating evidence links microbial dysbiosis to host lipid metabolic disturbances. Notably, transferring fecal microbiota from hyperlipidemic patients to ApoE⁻/⁻ mice elevates serum lipid levels, implying that altered microbial composition may drive hyperlipidemia through changes in lipid absorption and metabolism [9]. The pathogenesis of DN involves multifactorial processes, among which lipid metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation are key drivers. Impaired renal lipid homeostasis not only results from systemic metabolic stress but actively promotes tubular dysfunction and fibrosis. In diabetes, sustained hyperglycemia triggers metabolic reprogramming marked by reduced fatty acid oxidation (FAO), ATP depletion, and lipid accumulation within tubular epithelial cells [10, 11]. The “lipotoxicity hypothesis” posits that lipid imbalance leads to excessive triglyceride and free fatty acid accumulation, activating oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. These changes trigger the release of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors, cause apoptosis, and accelerate DKD progression [12, 13]. Together, these studies underscore the pivotal role of lipid metabolism abnormalities in DKD. Whether gut microbiota dysbiosis accelerates disease progression through disturbance of renal lipid homeostasis and activation of inflammatory pathways warrants further investigation.

Although existing studies have revealed a close association between gut microbiota and DN, direct evidence demonstrating the causal role of gut microbiota dysbiosis in the onset and progression of DN remains limited. Moreover, whether and how gut microbiota dysbiosis affects lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses in renal tissues has not been systematically investigated. This study aims to elucidate the mechanistic role of gut microbiota dysbiosis in the progression of DN, with a particular focus on its impact on renal lipid metabolism and inflammation. By integrating microbiological analysis, transcriptomics, and inflammatory signaling pathway profiling, this research seeks to clarify the molecular mechanisms through which gut microbiota dysbiosis promotes DN development by influencing renal lipid deposition and inflammatory responses, thereby providing new theoretical insights and potential therapeutic strategies for microbiota-targeted interventions in DN.

Methods

Reagents and antibodies

Streptozotocin (STZ) was purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Catalog No: S8050, dissolved in 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate buffer, pH 4.2 ~ 4.5). Kim-1, nephrin, ZO-1, SCD1, Perilipin 5, CPT2, and FABP1 antibodies were purchased from Proteintech (catalog numbers: 30948-1-AP, 22912-1-AP, 21773-1-AP, 28678-1-AP, 26951-1-AP, 26555-1-AP, 13626-1-AP; Wuhan, China), and the F4/80 antibody was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Catalog No: 70076T, Massachusetts, USA). Oil Red O was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Catalog No: sc-203749, Dallas, Texas, USA).

Preparation for gut microbiota transplantation

This study selected three groups of donors, comprising 5 healthy controls (HC), 5 patients with type 2 diabetes (DM) and 5 patients with type 2 diabetes-related nephropathy(DN). The inclusion and exclusion criteria for volunteers refer to Supplementary word 1. The specific baseline characteristics are detailed in Table S1. Frozen fecal samples were thawed in a 37.5 °C constant temperature water bath for 10 min and subsequently transferred to an anaerobic workstation for processing. The samples were mixed with sterile PBS solution and sequentially filtered through 400-mesh and 800-mesh sterile filters to eliminate undigested residues and fine particles. The filtrate was centrifuged at 7500 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was collected. The precipitate was then resuspended in a 30% sterile glycerol/PBS mixture, aliquoted, and stored in an -80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer for future use. Equal volumes of fecal supernatants from the 5 HC, 5 DM, and 5 DN donors were mixed separately to create single-source FMT preparations, which were utilized for subsequent mouse experiments in the FMT-HC, FMT-DM, and FMT-DN groups, respectively. Concurrently, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was conducted on fecal samples from the aforementioned three donor groups (HC, DM, DN, n = 5 per group) to analyze their microbial community characteristics (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Animals

This experiment strictly adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by Fudan University. 6 weeks, SPF-grade male DBA/2J mice utilized in this study were procured from Beijing Sibefu Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (License No.: SCX2024-001).

Establishment of diabetes mellitus (DM) model

After an adaptive feeding period, mice were intraperitoneally injected with streptozotocin (STZ) at a dosage of 40 mg/kg, dissolved in 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.2 ~ 4.5), for five consecutive days. Seventy-two hours following the last injection, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and random plasma glucose (RPG) levels were measured from the tail vein. The model was deemed successfully established if any of the following criteria were satisfied: fasting glucose levels ≥ 11.1 mmol/L or random glucose levels ≥ 16.7 mmol/L. On the 14th day post-STZ injection, blood glucose levels were re-evaluated, and mice exhibiting stable glucose levels for seven consecutive days were included in the experiment. Mice that did not meet the criteria were administered an additional STZ injection (20 mg/kg) and reassessed five days later. The group design consisted of five mice per group: (1) Normal control group (Control): regular diet; (2) DM model group (STZ): high-fat diet; (3) DM + FMT-DM group: high-fat diet with fecal microbiota transplantation from DM patients; (4) DM + FMT-DN group: high-fat diet with fecal microbiota transplantation from DN patients.

Establishment of diabetic nephropathy (DN) model

In this study, DNwas induced in DBA/2J mice using a combination of a high-fat diet (HFD) and low-dose STZ. This modeling strategy first involved feeding mice a HFD to induce insulin resistance, followed by intraperitoneal injection of low-dose STZ to impair pancreatic β-cell function and induce hyperglycemia, ultimately resulting in structural and functional renal abnormalities [14].DBA/2J mice were selected because this strain is known to be highly susceptible to DN, exhibiting more severe proteinuria and pronounced renal morphological changes compared with other mouse strains [15]. Therefore, this approach is expected to reliably reproduce the key metabolic disturbances and renal pathological features of diabetic nephropathy under controlled experimental conditions.

Mice were fed a HFD (45% fat) for 4 weeks to induce insulin resistance. Following this period, STZ was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 40 mg/kg for 5 consecutive days. Blood glucose levels were measured 72 h after the final STZ injection, using the same criteria as for the diabetes model, and rechecked on day 7. Mice with stable hyperglycemia were included in the study. Those that did not meet the criteria received an additional STZ injection (20 mg/kg) and were reassessed 5 days later.

The group design consisted of five mice per group: (1) Normal control group (N): regular diet; (2) HS group: Intraperitoneal injection of STZ combined with a high-fat diet. (3) HS + FMT-HC group (n = 5): Intraperitoneal injection of STZ combined with a high-fat diet, followed by fecal microbiota transplantation from HC patients.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) was performed according to a modified version of previously described methods [16, 17]. Fresh fecal samples were collected from HC (healthy control), DM (diabetes mellitus), and DN (diabetic nephropathy) patients. Under anaerobic conditions, the samples were filtered and centrifuged, then resuspended in sterile PBS to a final concentration of 100 mg/mL. The resulting fecal suspension was aliquoted at a dosage of 10 µL/g per administration and stored at − 80 °C until use.

Specifically, SPF-grade male DBA/2J mice were selected and acclimated for one week prior to gut microbiota depletion pretreatment. The antibiotic cocktail consisted of ampicillin (100 mg/kg), neomycin sulfate (200 mg/kg), metronidazole (100 mg/kg), and vancomycin (100 mg/kg), administered by oral gavage at a dose of 10 µL/g per day for five consecutive days.After completion of antibiotic treatment, mice received FMT by oral gavage three times per week until the end of the 14-week experimental period. All experimental procedures were conducted under sterile conditions to ensure the stability and reproducibility of gut microbiota transplantation.

Sample collection and biochemical analyses

Before the administration of a high-fat diet, mice in each group were fasted for 12 h. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was measured from the tail vein using Fast One Touch Ultra (Johnson), and the body weight of the mice was recorded. The 24-hour urine volume and 24-hour urinary protein of each mouse in the metabolic cage were measured. All indices measured before the establishment of the model were recorded as pre-model data. Two weeks after STZ injection, tail vein blood samples were collected. After the initiation of FMT treatment, the body weight, blood glucose, and 24-hour urine volume of the mice in the metabolic cage were measured every 4 weeks, and the 24-hour total urinary protein was determined using the sulfosalicylic acid method. At week 8 and week 12, tail vein blood was collected, and serum creatinine (Cr) was measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer. At the end of week 14, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation, and fresh fecal intestinal contents (ileocecal region) were collected under sterile conditions. Fresh fecal samples were collected using sterile, enzyme-free 2 mL EP tubes, and blood, urine, and kidney tissue samples were also collected. These samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in a -80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer for future use. Concentrations of Serum lipopolysaccharide (LPS) were determined using Mouse LPS ELISA Kit (CSB-E13066m, CUSABIO, https://www.cusabio.com/) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histological analysis of renal and intestinal

The renal and small intestine tissues were collected in 4% paraformaldehyde and then imbedded in paraffin.The renal tissues were cut into 3 μm sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining.The small intestine tissues were cut into 3 μm sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For renal lipid accumulation analysis, one section of renal was frozen and stored at 4 °C, and then were stained with Oil Red O to visualize neutral lipids.All microscopic observations were performed using an Olympus optical microscope (Tokyo, Japan), and quantitative analysis was conducted using Image J analysis software (Version 1.53) according to the standard method.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

The renal and small intestine tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and cut into 3 μm sections, which were then used for IHC and IF.IHC and IF procedures were carried out in accordance with the previously reported methods.The slides were stained with primary antibodies for F4/80 (1:100),Kim-1 (1:100), nephrin (1:100), and ZO-1 (1:50) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After the addition of horseradish peroxidase-streptavidin conjugate (SABC), the sections were incubated at room temperature for an additional 30 min. For IF, FITC-labeled anti-rabbit/mouse IgG (1:500) were incubated at room temperature for 1 h under light-protected conditions. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 5 min. The sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium and observed under a fluorescence microscope. For each section, 10 random fields of view were selected, and the integrated optical density (IOD) was measured using a fully automated image analysis system (Image-Pro Plus 6.0). The IOD served as a quantitative measure of the relative content of positive substances in each section.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

The total RNA from renal tissues of each group of mice was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, the extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR kit, for subsequent quantitative PCR analysis. The qPCR reactions were performed using the AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix premix reagent on a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. Using GAPDH as the internal reference gene, the relative mRNA expression levels of the target genes were calculated by the ΔCt method. The primers are shown in supplementary Table 2.

16 S rRNA sequencing

The 16 S rRNA sequencing was performed as previously described [16], with the following specific procedures:

Sample Collection and DNA Extraction Fresh fecal specimens were collected from mice immediately prior to euthanasia, rapidly preserved on dry ice, and maintained at -80 °C until analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using a combined CTAB/SDS protocol. DNA integrity and concentration were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by dilution to a standardized concentration of 1 ng/µL with molecular-grade water.

16 S rRNA Gene Amplification and Sequencing The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of bacterial 16 S rRNA genes were PCR-amplified using universal primers 338 F (ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). After purification of equimolar PCR products using a gel extraction kit, sequencing libraries were prepared according to Illumina’s TruSeq DNA protocol. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 250-bp paired-end reads.After quality filtering, trimming, and chimera removal, the average read length of the merged sequences was 430 bp, The average sequencing depth per sample was 48,500 high-quality reads.

Microbiome bioinformatic analyses were conducted using QIIME2 (version 2022.11) following the official tutorials (https://docs.qiime2.org/2022.11/tutorials/) with minor modifications. Sequence denoising was performed using the DADA2 plugin to remove low-quality sequences and correct sequencing errors. The resulting sequences were clustered at 100% sequence similarity to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) and corresponding abundance tables.Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was carried out by aligning representative sequences against the SILVA reference database (Silva v138), and taxonomic information for each ASV was obtained accordingly. Rare ASVs were removed to construct an optimized abundance matrix for downstream analyses.

Microbial community diversity was assessed in QIIME 2.0 using both alpha and beta diversity indices. Community structure differences were visualized through Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on UniFrac and Bray–Curtis distance metrics. To compare microbial compositions across samples at different taxonomic levels (genus and species), the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) method was applied to identify significantly enriched taxa among groups. These analyses and visualizations were performed using the vegan package (v2.15.3) in R.

Reference-based transcriptome sequencing

The transcriptome sequencing methodology followed our previously described protocol [18], with the following detailed procedures:

RNA Extraction and Library Preparation Total RNA was isolated from samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified mRNA was fragmented into short fragments using divalent cations at elevated temperature. cDNA libraries were prepared from the fragmented RNA using the mRNA-Seq sample preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA), generating a total of millon 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

The cDNA library, constructed from pooled mouse kidney RNA samples, was sequenced on Illumina NovaseqTM X Plus sequence platform (paired-end mode). Then sequence quality was verified using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/, 0.11.9). including the Q20, Q30 and GC-content of the clean data.Reads from each sample were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (Ensembl version 112). using HISAT2, which utilizes a splice-aware algorithm to map reads across exon junctions.Transcriptome Assembly and Differential Expression Analysis Mapped reads were assembled into transcripts using StringTie. Individual sample transcriptomes were merged to generate a unified transcriptome using custom Perl scripts. Expression levels of transcripts were quantified as FPKM using StringTie and statistically evaluated with Ballgown.Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the following thresholds: Fold change: |log2FC| > 1,Statistical significance: *p < 0.05.

Statistics analysis

All normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v9.5) and R (v4.3.1).Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA (for ≥ 3 groups) or unpaired Student’s t-test (for 2 groups).Non-normally distributed data were assessed via the Kruskal-Wallis H-test (for ≥ 3 groups) or Mann-Whitney U-test (for 2 groups).Differentially abundant operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were identified using DESeq2, with significance thresholds set at |log₂(fold change)| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05.Visualization was performe using CNSknowall (https://cnsknowall.com) and Figdraw(https://www.figdraw.com).

Transplantation of DN gut microbiota exacerbated renal injury in diabetic mice

To elucidate the causal relationship between gut microbiota dysbiosis and the progression of diabetic nephropathy, this study conducted fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments in diabetic model mice. The specific workflow is shown in Fig. 1A: pseudo-germ-free mice were first established using a quadruple antibiotic regimen, followed by a 14-day FMT intervention as described in the Methods section to stabilize the transplanted gut microbiota. Subsequently, diabetes was induced by consecutive STZ injections for 5 days. The microbiota transplantation groups continuously received gut microbial suspensions derived from donors with diabetes or diabetic nephropathy via oral gavage (10 µg/g, three times per week) until the end of the 14-week experimental period.

Figures 1C-F show the changes in body weight and blood glucose levels in each group, respectively.Following STZ injection, mice developed progressive hyperglycemia and weight loss. Beta diversity analysis (Fig. 2A) revealed significant differences in microbiota structure among the groups, indicating that DN fecal microbiota transplantation can significantly alter the balance of gut microbiota. At the genus level, we first presented a stacked bar plot of the distribution of the top 30 genera in relative abundance (Fig. 2B), and then focused on the changes in the top 10 genera (Fig. 2C). Notably, Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus, which possess probiotic properties, showed a significant downward trend across the groups. Specifically, compared to the normal control group, the STZ group exhibited a significant reduction in the abundance of Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus, Furthermore, the DN transplantation group showed a further decrease in Lactobacillus compared to the DM transplantation group. Gut microbiota of HC ameliorated renal injury in diabetic nephropathy mice

Previous research findings indicate that gut microbiota dysbiosis may play a significant role in the onset and progression of DN [19, 20]. To further investigate the mechanisms by which gut microbiota influences DN, we employed fecal microbiota transplantation from healthy donors (FMT-HC) to intervene in a diabetic nephropathy mouse model induced by STZ combined with a high-fat diet. As shown in Fig. 3A, the experimental design detailing the grouping and intervention procedures is presented. The results demonstrate that the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) and serum creatinine levels were significantly reduced in the FMT-HC group (Figs. 3B-C). Histopathological staining and semi-quantitative analysis of renal tissues revealed that FMT-HC intervention significantly ameliorated tubular vacuolar degeneration and markedly reduced the degree of renal interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 3D-E). FMT-HC intervention significantly downregulated the expression of kidney injury molecule-1 (Kim-1) in the tubular region (Fig. 3F) and restored the expression level of nephrin, a key protein in glomerular podocytes (Fig. 3G). These findings suggest that fecal microbiota transplantation from healthy donors can exert renal protective effects.

Gut microbiota of FMT-HC reversed the downregulation of the PPAR pathway in diabetic nephropathy

To further investigate the potential molecular mechanisms by which FMT-HC improves renal tubular injury in diabetic nephropathy, we performed transcriptomic analysis on renal tissues from each group of mice using RNA-seq technology (Fig. 5A). The results showed that compared to the normal control group (NC), a total of 503 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the high-fat diet combined with STZ group, including 342 upregulated genes and 161 downregulated genes. In the comparison between the FMT-HC intervention group and the HFD group, 112 significantly differentially expressed genes were found (38 upregulated and 74 downregulated). The heatmap of differentially expressed genes among the three groups displayed in Fig. 5B demonstrates that FMT-HC intervention significantly reversed the gene expression abnormalities induced by HFD. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the top 20 genes with the most significant differences revealed that, compared to the NC group, the PPAR signaling pathway (mmu03320) was the most significantly altered metabolic pathway in the HFD group (Fig. 5C-D). After FMT-HC intervention, the PPAR pathway remained the most significantly different pathway compared to the HFD group(Fig. 5E-F). These results suggest that FMT-HC may exert a protective effect on DN by regulating the PPAR signaling pathway, thereby improving renal lipid metabolism disorders.

Gut microbiota of HC alleviated renal lipid deposition in DN mice

The lipotoxicity hypothesis of DN suggests that disrupted lipid homeostasis triggers oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, pro-inflammator factor release, and apoptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, exacerbating DN progression [12, 13].We conducted a heatmap analysis on 12 lipid metabolism-related genes involved in the PPAR pathway from the RNA-seq data (Fig. 6A). The results showed that FMT-HC treatment significantly downregulated the expression of PCK1 and FABP1 genes, while upregulating the expression levels of key genes such as PPARγ, SCD1, and SCD2. Figure 6B provides a detailed classification of the functional categories of these pathway-related genes, which mainly include lipid synthesis, fatty acid transport, and fatty acid oxidation metabolic processes. Figure 6C further illustrates the expression trends of the component genes. Through RT-PCR validation (Fig. 6D-O), we confirmed that FMT-HC treatment significantly regulated the expression levels of multiple lipid metabolism-related genes, including SCD1, PPARγ, CYP27a1, Plin5, Fabp1, PCK1, and Cpt2, which is consistent with the RNA-seq data analysis.Western blot analysis showed that compared with the control group, the expression levels of Plin5 and CPT2 in the HS group were significantly increased, while SCD1 expression was decreased, and no significant change was observed in FABP1 expression. Notably, after treatment with FMT-HC, the expression of Plin5 was significantly downregulated (P = 0.042). Meanwhile, SCD1 showed an increasing trend, whereas CPT2 exhibited a decreasing tendency, although these changes did not reach statistical significance. These findings indicate that modulation of the gut microbiota may help maintain energy homeostasis and alleviate renal cell injury by reducing excessive lipid synthesis and accumulation (Fig. 6P). Oil Red O staining demonstrated that FMT-HC significantly attenuated renal neutral lipid accumulation compared to the diabetic nephropathy model group (HS) (Fig. 6Q).

Discussion

This study, based on recent findings highlighting the critical role of gut microbiota dysbiosis in metabolic diseases [21, 22], revealed the pivotal function of gut microbiota in the pathogenesis and intervention of DN through a fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) model. The results showed that recipient mice transplanted with microbiota derived from DN mice exhibited significant renal dysfunction, including increased proteinuria and aggravated renal tubular interstitial fibrosis. Meanwhile, their intestinal barrier function was impaired, accompanied by a marked decrease in Lactobacillus abundance. This suggests that DN-associated microbial dysbiosis may exacerbate renal inflammation and structural damage through the “gut–kidney axis.”In contrast, following transplantation of fecal microbiota from healthy donors (FMT-HC), renal injury in DN mice was markedly ameliorated, inflammatory responses were suppressed, and renal lipid deposition was reduced, indicating that remodeling of the gut microbiota can significantly alleviate DN progression. Moreover, fecal microbiota transplantation notably affected the expression of genes related to the PPAR signaling pathway, which may represent one of the key molecular mechanisms by which the gut microbiota regulates the progression of diabetic nephropathy ( Fig. 8).

Gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to diabetic nephropathy via affecting renal lipid deposition and inflammatory responses. CPT2:Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2; FABP1:Fatty Acid Binding Protein 1; SCD1:stearoyl-Coenzyme A desaturase 1

Previous studies support the findings of this research, indicating that gut microbiota dysbiosis plays a promoting role in the progression of DN. In both CKD patients and mouse models, a decline in intestinal Lactobacillus johnsonii has been found to significantly correlate with multiple clinical renal function indicators [23, 24]. As a strategy to remodel the gut ecosystem, FMT has recently been demonstrated to improve metabolic syndrome and diabetic complications [15]. In this study, FMT-HC significantly increased the abundance of probiotics such as Lactobacillus johnsonii, which has been reported to possess anti-inflammatory and lipid metabolism-improving functions [25]. This strain can promote the expression of intestinal epithelial tight junction proteins by producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), reduce intestinal permeability, and suppress systemic inflammatory responses.

Gut microbiota imbalance can lead to dysregulated lipid metabolism. Transplantation of gut microbiota from hyperlipidemic patients into ApoE−/− mice significantly elevates their blood lipid levels [26]. Transcriptome analysis revealed that FMT-HC may exert its effects by regulating the PPAR signaling pathway. The PPAR (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor) family plays a central regulatory role in lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and energy homeostasis [27]. In DN mice, the expression of PPARα and PPARγ is typically suppressed, leading to impaired lipid oxidation and renal lipotoxicity accumulation [28]. After FMT-HC treatment, the expression of PPAR pathway-related genes in the mouse kidneys was upregulated, accompanied by improved renal lipid deposition and attenuated inflammatory responses. This suggests that healthy microbiota may activate the PPAR pathway through metabolites, promoting lipid oxidation and metabolic remodeling. These findings reveal the potential interaction mechanism between gut microbiota and the PPAR-γ pathway in intestinal epithelial cells.Notably, the regulatory relationship between specific probiotic strains and the PPAR pathway has been partially confirmed [29]– [30]. For example, Lactobacillus plantarum can enhance intestinal barrier function through the conjugated linoleic acid (CLA)-PPAR-γ signaling axis, thereby exerting anti-colon cancer effects [29]. Therefore, FMT-HC establishes a multi-dimensional protective mechanism by restoring probiotic balance and improving host metabolic signaling pathways.

Furthermore, this study found that FMT-HC not only improved the gut microbiota structure but also suppressed local renal inflammatory responses. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that gut microbiota homeostasis can downregulate the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway and reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [31]. Based on our results, we propose an integrated mechanism: healthy donor microbiota restores intestinal ecological balance, enhances barrier function, reduces endotoxin entry into the bloodstream and systemic inflammation, thereby activating the PPAR signaling pathway and ultimately ameliorating renal lipid metabolism disorders and structural damage.Future studies should focus on two crucial next steps: identifying the protective metabolites produced by Lactobacillus johnsonii and validating the efficacy of FMT-HC therapy in other established models of diabetic nephropathy. These efforts will be instrumental in translating our findings into potential clinical applications.

It is important to note the limitations of this work. Our conclusions are based on a single mouse model (DBA/2J) and a sample size of n = 5 per group, and the influence of animal sex was not explored, all of which warrant caution in broader interpretation. To build upon these findings, a critical next step will be to employ a refined experimental design that includes an antibiotic-cleared group and an FMT-HC normal recipient group. This approach will allow us to move beyond the current limitations and directly investigate the causal mechanisms underlying the observed therapeutic effects.

In summary, this study reveals that during the early stages of DN, gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to renal tubular injury by regulating lipid metabolism in renal tubules. Healthy donor fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT-HC) can significantly ameliorate renal tubular lipid metabolism disorders and alleviate tubulointerstitial inflammatory responses by upregulating the renal PPAR signaling pathway. This study provides both theoretical and practical foundations for developing gut microbiota-targeted therapies for diabetic nephropathy.
