Vaccination coverage and influencing factors of four non‑National Immunization Program vaccines: A cross‑sectional survey among children aged 1‒6 years in Beijing, China
Authors: Qianli Yuan, Wei Zhang, Huai Wang, Pei Gao, Zhiqiang Cao, Luodan Suo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine, rotavirus vaccine (RV), and varicella vaccine (VarV) have been recommended by WHO for inclusion in National Immunization Program (NIP). However, their vaccination rate is low in China as self-funded vaccines. This study aimed to investigate the coverage of these four vaccines and to identify their influencing factors. Selected via multi-stage stratified sampling, 4,182 guardians participated in a February–March 2025 study. Guardians completed an online questionnaire capturing sociodemographic characteristics, attitudes toward vaccination, and indicators of immunization service accessibility. Individual-level vaccination histories for children were extracted from the Beijing Immunization Information System and linked to survey responses. The vaccination coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine, RV and VarV were 48.5%, 47.4%, 43.5% and 89.3%, respectively. Older age, residence outside urban areas (suburban, outer suburban), and greater number of children in the household were associated with lower coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV, with adjusted ORs ranging from 0.160 to 0.929 (all p values < .05). Conversely, higher household income (200,000–499,999 CNY and ≥500,000 CNY), guardians’ perceived vaccination necessity, and receipt of vaccination notifications were associated with higher coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.018 to 6.521 (all p values < .05). The results indicated the vaccination coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV remains suboptimal in Beijing, with evident regional disparities. Free vaccination policies for PCV, Hib vaccine, RV, and VarV, together with strengthened reminder systems and targeted health education interventions, may help improve vaccine coverage among children.KEYWORDS: Non-National Immunization Program, PCV, Hib, rotavirus, varicella, vaccination coverage, Beijing

Background

Vaccination is widely recognized as the most cost-effective intervention for disease prevention and public health protection. Since the launch of the Expanded Programme on Immunization by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1974, it is estimated that vaccination has prevented approximately 154 million deaths globally over the past five decades, with children under 5 y accounting for about 95% of those lives saved.1 According to the Vaccine Administration Law of the People’s Republic of China,2 vaccines were classified into the National Immunization Program (NIP) and non-NIP vaccines. NIP vaccines are provided free of charge by the Chinese government. Non-NIP vaccines, serving as substitutes and complements to NIP vaccines, are voluntarily administered and require individual payment. China’s NIP represents a major public health achievement, having achieved over 95% coverage for children under 6 y old with 14 vaccines targeting 15 diseases.3 Despite this great success, challenges remain in the uptake and integration of several WHO-recommended vaccines that are currently categorized as non-NIP vaccines in China.

The pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine, rotavirus vaccine (RV) and varicella vaccine (VarV) are critically important non-NIP vaccines for children, and their vaccination volumes have increased steadily in recent years in China.4 WHO recommends that all 194 Member States include PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV vaccine in their NIP. Additionally, it advises that countries or regions with a high varicella disease burden include VarV in their NIP and achieve a vaccination coverage exceeding 80%.5 All these vaccines have demonstrated high efficacy, safety, and effectiveness in preventing targeted infectious diseases including meningitis, pneumonia, septicemia, childhood diarrhea, and varicella.6

As of 2025, among the 194 WHO Member States, 193, 163, 131 and 56 countries have included PCV, Hib vaccine, RV, and VarV in their NIP, respectively.7–9 The four vaccines all remain outside China’s NIP, and China is the only Member State that has not incorporated the Hib vaccine into its NIP. Despite their notable public health importance, uptake of these non-NIP vaccines in China remains low.4,6,10 According to global vaccination related reports, the global third-dose vaccination rates of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV stand at 67%, 78% and 59%, respectively, in 2025.6 However, according to a 2019 national survey,10 the first-dose vaccination coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV was all reported to be below 50% among children aged 6 months to 5 y nationwide, and substantially lower in Beijing (10.2%, 1.6%, and 6.0% respectively).10 The coverage of VarV was higher than that of the other three non-NIP vaccines, reaching 34%, yet it remained relatively low compared to NIP vaccines.10

As a megacity with a permanent population exceeding 20 million, Beijing has a substantial population of migrant children, which increases the risk of rapid transmission of vaccine-preventable diseases (VDPs) among children under 6 y of age, who are primarily covered by the NIP. The current immunization program in Beijing is insufficient to fully address the needs of disease prevention and control for meningitis, pneumonia, septicemia, childhood diarrhea and varicella. Consequently, evaluating the feasibility of incorporating vaccines targeting these diseases into the NIP is of public health importance, with vaccination coverage serving as a key consideration in policy decision-making.

However, data on coverage and associated determinants of PCV, Hib vaccine, RV and VarV among children aged 1–6 y in Beijing were extremely limited. Existing studies have several limitations: (1) focused solely on a single non-NIP vaccine, (2) restriction to younger children aged 1–3 y, (3) overreliance on parental self-reported vaccination status, and (4) omission of vaccination service related factors in analysis. To address these gaps, this study uses data from a cross-sectional survey and IIS in Beijing to estimate the vaccination coverage and explore the influencing factors associated with uptake of four non-NIP vaccines among children aged 1–6 y in Beijing. The findings aim to provide recommendations for improving vaccination uptake, including potential expansion of the current NIP and strengthened health education on non-NIP vaccines.

Methods

Study design and participants

From February 7 to March 31, 2025, a multistage stratified sampling survey was conducted in Beijing. Eligible participants were guardians of children aged 1–6 y who had resided continuously in the survey area for at least 6 months, provided informed consent, and were able to understand and complete the questionnaire independently. Participants were excluded if they withdrew during the survey.

Sampling procedure

Based on geographical location and economic development level, the 17 districts of Beijing were categorized into three strata: urban, suburban and outer suburban districts. Sampling was conducted in three stages. In the first stage, two districts were randomly selected from each stratum (urban, suburban and outer suburban districts, respectively). In the second stage, ten vaccination clinics were conveniently selected from each sampled district in the first stage. In the third stage, guardians of children aged 1‒6 y who accompanied their children to the selected vaccination clinics for vaccination were recruited as study participants.

Sample size and power analysis

The sample size was calculated with the following formula based on an error α = 0.05, Zα/2 = 1.96, allowance error δ = 0.01, design effect (deff) = 1.5, and a non-response rate of 10%. According to the literature, the first-dose vaccination coverage of RV is the lowest (approximately 6%) among the four non-NIP vaccines in Beijing.10 Therefore, an estimated proportion (P) of 6% is used to maximize the sample size. The required sample size was determined to be 3,600 participants.

Survey method

A questionnaire survey was conducted among eligible guardians identified by uniformly trained vaccination service providers at vaccination clinics. Prior to data collection, investigators provided a detailed explanation of the survey purpose and procedures. After providing informed consent, participants scanned a quick response (QR) code with their mobile phones to access the questionnaire on Wenjuanxing platform and completed the survey under one-on-one guidance from vaccination service providers. The first page of the questionnaire contained informed consent information, and only guardians who selected “agree” were allowed to proceed to the formal survey.

To ensure data quality, several quality control rules were implemented, including restricting network IP addresses to Beijing, allowing only one questionnaire submission per mobile phone, confining the range of permissible options for each question to avoid logical errors, and requiring completion of all questionnaire items. Questionnaires completed in less than 1 min or exhibiting straight-lining behavior (i.e., selecting the same response option for all items) were considered low quality and were excluded from analysis.

Questionnaire development and content

Based on previous literature and expert consultations, we developed a structured questionnaire. A pilot study was conducted among 50 guardians from four vaccination clinics in the Dongcheng and Haidian districts to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire. Based on feedback from the pilot study, a few modifications were made to ensure that the questions were comprehensible and interpreted as intended. Data from the pilot study were not included in the final analysis.

The questionnaire consists of three sections. The first section collected demographic characteristics of both children and their guardians. Information on children included gender, vaccination identification number, date of birth, and district of residence Guardian-related information included gender, relationship to the children, age, educational level, and occupation. The Healthcare providers (HCP) category in occupational classification refers to professionals working in various medical and health institutions at all levels who are engaged in technical services related to medical treatment, prevention, health care, nursing, medical technology, public health, health administration, and other related fields. Additional household-level information included the number of children in the family and annual household income. The second section assessed guardians’ knowledge about and attitudes toward the above mentioned four vaccine-preventable diseases and corresponding vaccines. For each vaccine, four questions evaluated whether the guardians understood the target diseases and the corresponding vaccine, had confidence in the vaccines’ safety and efficacy, and perceived the vaccine as necessary for their child.

The third section collected information on the provision of immunization services. Vaccination convenience was assessed by one-way travel time, one-way transportation cost and vaccination waiting time. Vaccination notification measured whether guardians have been informed by vaccination service providers about the availability of non-NIP vaccines for their children. The module assessing vaccination service satisfaction comprised 15 questions in the questionnaire. Each question was scored using a 5-point Likert scale (0 = very dissatisfied, 1 = dissatisfied, 2 = moderately satisfied, 3 = satisfied, 4 = very satisfied), and a total satisfaction score was calculated by summing the scores of all 15 questions.

Ascertainment of vaccination history

After the questionnaire survey was completed, individual immunization records were retrieved from the IIS using each child’s unique vaccination identification number and were precisely matched to the corresponding questionnaire data. These linked records were used to investigate vaccination status and to estimate vaccination coverage.

Vaccination schedules and coverage definitions

In Beijing, PCV, Hib, and RV vaccines are administered according to manufacturers’ package inserts (Supplementary Table S1), with the earliest eligible age ranging from 6 weeks to 2 months. Accordingly, coverage for these vaccines was estimated among all surveyed children. For VarV, the first dose is administered at 18 months of age; thus, coverage for VarV was calculated among children aged ≥2 y.

Vaccination coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine or RV (%) = [Number of children vaccinated at least one dose of the vaccine / Total number of surveyed children] × 100%; Vaccination coverage of VarV (%) = [Number of children vaccinated at least one dose of VarV/Total number of surveyed children aged ≥2 y] × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were described as medians and Inter-Quartile Range (IQR). Univariable associations between potential influencing factors and vaccination uptake were examined using the Chi-square test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. A generalized estimating equation (GEE) with clinic-level clustering was used to identify factors associated with non-NIP vaccine uptake. Variables found to be statistically significant in the univariable analysis were included as independent variables in the GEE analysis. For multivariate analysis, one-way transportation cost was categorized as ≥50 CNY versus <50 CNY. Vaccination waiting time and one-way travel time were both classified as ≥30 min versus <30 min. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) for each variable were calculated. A two-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 4,185 questionnaires were collected. Of these, one questionnaire was excluded due to a completion time of less than 1 min. Two duplicate responses were identified and removed based on unique vaccination identification numbers. Finally, 4,182 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis.

Table 1 summarizes the socio-demographic characteristics of the 4,182 children and their guardians. Among the 4,182 children, the median age was 2 y (1.0, 3.0); 50.2% were boys; 36.4% resided in suburban districts and 32.0% in outer suburban districts. Among the 4,182 surveyed guardians, 68.7% were female; 58.2% had a single child; 87.1% were the children’s parents and 76.5% held college degree or higher. About 29.4% of guardians reported an income of less than 100,000 Chinese Yuan (CNY), 38.8% reported 100,000‒199,999 CNY, and 24.4% reported 200,000–499,999 CNY. The most common occupational category was workers and employees (33.1%), followed by civil servants and staff of enterprises/public institutions (16.7%), and HCPs (13.3%).

In contrast, higher household income [200,000–499,999 CNY] (PCV: OR = 1.325, 95% CI: 1.081‒1.623; Hib vaccine: OR = 1.539, 95% CI: 1.246‒1.901; RV: OR = 1.467, 95% CI: 1.137‒1.664) and ≥500,000 CNY (PCV: OR = 2.150, 95% CI: 1.538‒3.004; Hib vaccine: OR = 2.482, 95% CI: 1.684‒3.500; RV: OR = 1.872, 95% CI: 1.448‒2.242), guardians’ perceived necessity of vaccination (PCV: OR = 5.134, 95% CI: 4.042‒6.521; Hib vaccine: OR = 2.464, 95% CI: 1.985‒3.052; RV: OR = 3.503, 95% CI: 2.626‒4.671), and receipt of vaccination notifications (PCV: OR = 2.515, 95% CI: 2.004‒3.157; Hib vaccine: OR = 1.983, 95% CI: 1.606‒2.337; RV: OR = 2.433, 95% CI: 2.045‒2.895) were associated with increased uptake of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV. Beyond these shared determinants, each vaccine also exhibited distinct associated factors.

For PCV, one-way transportation time ≥30 min to vaccination clinics (OR = 0.790, 95% CI: 0.529‒0.951) were associated with lower vaccination coverage, whereas higher coverage was observed among children whose guardians had greater PCV-related knowledge (OR = 1.527, 95% CI: 1.295‒1.800), and expressed confidence in the vaccine’s safety and efficacy (OR = 1.383, 95% CI: 1.025‒1.846). For the Hib vaccine, lower coverage was associated with guardians working as HCP (OR = 0.578, 95% CI: 0.358‒0.933), while higher coverage was observed among guardians with a master’s degree or higher (OR = 1.736, 95% CI: 1.080‒2.791) and those with an annual household income of 100,000–199,999 CNY (OR = 1.292, 95% CI: 1.083‒1.541).

For VarV, older child age (OR = 1.564, 95% CI: 1.406–1.783), female child (OR = 1.229, 95% CI: 1.013–1.665), residence in outer suburban districts (OR = 1.776, 95% CI: 1.156–2.729), guardians’ perceived necessity of vaccination (OR = 2.253, 95% CI: 1.302‒3.898), and receipt of vaccination notifications (OR = 1.534, 95% CI: 1.112‒2.116) were independently associated with higher vaccination coverage.

Discussion

Among the four non-NIP vaccines in Beijing, the VarV has the highest vaccination rate (89.3%), followed by PCV (48.5%), Hib vaccine (47.4%) and RV (43.5%). The vaccination rates in this study were significantly higher than those (PCV: 7.7%; RV: 20.3%; Hib vaccine: 42.6%; VarV: 67.1%) from a national survey covering ten provinces in China in 2019.4,10 The vaccination coverage of VarV is the highest among the four vaccines, both nationwide and in Beijing. The high vaccine coverage may be attributed to free varicella vaccines provided by local governments. Currently, multiple provinces in China have fully or partially incorporated Varv into their local immunization programs, including Tianjin,11 Shanghai,12 Jiangsu,13 Guangzhou,14 significantly boosting VarV vaccination coverage.15

In Beijing, free emergency vaccination is offered to students in various schools where varicella outbreaks have occurred. Furthermore, Beijing has innovatively incorporated VarV as the only non-NIP vaccine into the scope of vaccination certificate verification for childcare facilities, kindergarten, and primary school enrollment since 2010, identifying children who missed the doses of VarV and notifying catch-up vaccination. Previous studies have shown that the vaccination certificate verification significantly boost vaccination coverage.16 The potential causal association between these policies and vaccination coverage warrants further validation via cohort, or interrupted time-series studies in future research. Although Beijing’s current immunization strategy for the VarV has significantly increased vaccination coverage, further optimization is still needed. First, given that integrating free VarV vaccination has been demonstrated to substantially alleviate the disease burden associated with varicella,17,18 public health policymakers may consider incorporating VarV into local childhood immunization schedules. Second, the age for the first dose should be set to 12 months. The most commonly adopted VarV immunization schedule worldwide recommends administration of the first dose at 12‒18 months of age. However, in Beijing, the first dose is scheduled at 18 months, which may increase the susceptibility to varicella among children aged 12‒17 months.18

The coverage of non-NIP vaccines among children is associated with multiple factors, such as vaccination policies, regional economic level, vaccine prices, vaccine supply, household payment capacity, parental awareness of vaccination necessity, and accessibility of vaccination services.9,10,19–22 This study showed that factors influencing coverage of the four non-NIP vaccines were not entirely consistent. Older children, those residing in suburban or outer suburban, those from multi-child households were found to have lower PCV, Hib vaccine and RV vaccination coverage.

Vaccination coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine and RV decreased with advancing age among children, which could be associated with birth cohort-specific immunization policies and age eligibility criteria outlined in the immunization schedule. For instance, the imported pentavalent RV introduced in Beijing in 2019 exhibited higher coverage than the monovalent vaccine,23 yet imposed stringent age restrictions requiring completion of all three doses by 32 weeks of age. In 2023, the licensed age indication for the imported 13-valent PCV13 was expanded from 6 weeks–15 months to 6 weeks–5 y. Additionally, Hib-containing vaccines such as the diphtheria, tetanus, acellular pertussis, inactivated poliovirus and Hib (DTaP-IPV-Hib) pentavalent vaccine are indicated exclusively for infants and young children aged 0‒3 y. Such age constraints may result in missed vaccination opportunities among older children, consequently contributing to lower coverage. Another contributing factor may be that younger children undergo more frequent visits to vaccination clinics owing to their intensive immunization schedule, which may enable more timely reminders for parents from vaccination providers.24

Children from households with an annual household income of 200,000–490,000 and ≥500,000 were found to have higher PCV, Hib vaccine and RV vaccination coverage. Family affordability is an important factor affecting vaccination coverage. In Beijing, the single-dose prices of PCV, RV and Hib vaccine produced by different manufacturers range from 458 to 698 CNY, 138 to 280 CNY, and 43 to 638 CNY per dose, respectively. Additionally, an extra vaccination service fee of 25 CNY is charged per dose. Completing all doses specified in the immunization schedules incurs high costs, which impose a financial burden on families with a lower income, potentially limiting their access to the vaccine and reducing the likelihood of full compliance and completion.25 Previous studies have shown that parents’ maximum willingness to pay for their children’s vaccination accounts for 2% of their monthly household income.26 Qualitative interview results indicated that if non-NIP vaccines were provided free of charge, parents would not only have their children vaccinated but also tolerate longer waiting times at vaccination clinics.27 These findings highlight the potential value of gradually integrating the three non-NIP vaccines into the NIP based on factors including disease burden, cost-effectiveness, fiscal capacity, and vaccine supply, or introducing government-funded subsidy programs, which may effectively reduce household economic burdens.

Beyond economic factors, the unbalanced allocation of high-quality medical and preventive health resources also contribute to the low vaccination rate observed in suburban and outer suburban areas.28 Families with multiple children have significantly lower vaccination coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV, which may be due to the higher time costs and economic pressure. These results underscore the importance of targeting multi-child families as a priority population for health education and promotion initiatives regarding non-NIP vaccines.

Notably, we observed relatively lower Hib vaccination coverage among children whose guardians were HCP. This pattern may be attributed to two potential factors. First, HCP often hold more nuanced risk-benefit evaluations, which may lead them to exercise greater caution regarding non-NIP vaccines. Second, HCP with low vaccination willingness for themselves may be less likely to have their children vaccinated. Previous studies have demonstrated suboptimal vaccination coverage among HCP themselves, mainly driven by misinformation, misconceptions about vaccine efficacy and safety, and a perceived lack of necessity.29,30 Moreover, some HCP recognize the importance of vaccination but fail to translate this awareness into actual behavior. Our findings suggest that implementing comprehensive educational programs that provide up-to-date, evidence-based information about non-NIP vaccines is needed to address vaccine hesitancy, boosting their willingness to be vaccinated themselves and vaccinate their children.

Demographic variables showed a distinct influence on VarV coverage compared to the other three vaccines. Specifically, older children, girls, and those residing in outer suburban areas had higher vaccination coverage. This difference may be partly explained by the implementation of vaccination certificate verification, which has mitigated urban-rural disparities. Furthermore, consistent with previous studies,31 the higher coverage observed in outer suburban regions may be attributable to a lower proportion of migrant children.

Vaccination service accessibility influences parental vaccination willingness, thereby affecting vaccination coverage.9,20,32–34 This study found that children from families with a one-way transportation time of ≥30 min to vaccination clinics had relatively lower PCV coverage. The service radius, obtained from the IIS and maintained by clinic staff based on actual geographic distance, exceeded 10 km for some vaccination clinics in outer suburban districts, which create inconvenience for childhood vaccination. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing the service radius of vaccination clinics by adding vaccination sites in outer suburban districts and improving vaccination convenience by offering weekend clinics.

Higher vaccination coverage of four non-NIP vaccines was observed among children whose guardians perceived vaccination as necessary and had received vaccination eligibility notifications from vaccination service providers. Vaccination service providers maintain direct contact with children’s guardians, acting as primary communicators of vaccine-related information. Their recommendation can significantly change people’s attitude toward vaccination, thereby inducing a positive belief in the effectiveness and safety of non-NIP vaccines.15 Nevertheless, existing studies have shown that vaccination service providers demonstrate low enthusiasm for vaccine recommendation. This phenomenon may be attributed to such factors as concerns about being misunderstood by parents as promoting vaccines, no work mandates regarding the recommendation of self-paid vaccines, heavy workload with high time costs for communication, and a lack of corresponding incentives.19,35–37 Therefore, the government should consider developing policy-based incentive mechanisms, such as financial or performance-related incentives, to improve the initiative of vaccination service providers in vaccine recommendation and notification. Previous study indicated that reminders with health education were the most effective intervention types for promoting vaccination uptake, while the most effective delivery platforms were e-mail and text message.38 This suggests that higher vaccination coverage may be achieved through a combination of measures such as health promotion and vaccination reminders delivered via e-mail, text messages, or social media.

Conclusion

This survey revealed suboptimal vaccination coverage of PCV, Hib vaccine, and RV among children aged 1–6 y in Beijing. Lower household income, suburban or outer suburban residence, having multiple children, inadequate perception of vaccination necessity, and insufficient notification services, were identified as key data-driven determinants of non-NIP vaccine uptake. These findings highlight the necessity of implementing targeted health education to enhance vaccination awareness, establishing notification and reminder mechanisms for non-NIP vaccines within routine NIP immunization services, and formulating a priority-ranked, phased strategy for integrating self-funded vaccines into the NIP.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, allowing only the analysis of correlations between variables rather than establishing causal relationships. Second, the use of convenience sampling for vaccination clinics and participants may introduce selection bias. As this population is inherently more accepting of immunization, such sampling could lead to an overestimation of vaccination coverage. Future studies should expand the recruitment scope to include a more diverse sample of child guardians. Third, guardians had varying levels of vaccine-related knowledge, and the study participants were not strictly limited to parents but also included non-parental caregivers. This may also result in selection bias; however, given that parents accounted for over 87% of the participants, the potential impact of this limitation on the study’s overall conclusions is likely to be limited. Fourth, defining vaccination coverage as “at least one dose” may overestimate full-series vaccination. Given potential dropout in subsequent doses, the first-dose rate was higher than the full-series rate. Further studies on full-series vaccination are thus warranted to assess vaccination service effectiveness and the proportion of children with full immune protection. Lastly, online surveys may yield incomplete or inattentive responses, which could compromise data representativeness and result accuracy. Future studies should adopt diverse data collection methods to mitigate survey bias.
