Pericardial Decompression Syndrome After Large-Volume Pericardiocentesis in Systemic Sclerosis
Authors: Ahmed Al Mubaid, Martha Plasencia, Amir Abusharif, Muhammad Nazir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPericardial decompression syndrome (PDS) is a rare and underrecognized complication of pericardiocentesis, characterized by paradoxical hemodynamic deterioration after relief of pericardial effusion.Case SummaryA 32-year-old woman with systemic sclerosis presented with chest pain and was found to have a large pericardial effusion with early tamponade physiology. She underwent rapid, large-volume pericardiocentesis. Hours later, she developed acute respiratory failure and cardiac arrest. Repeat echocardiography demonstrated new severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction without recurrent effusion, consistent with PDS. Misinterpretation as recurrent tamponade led to emergent repeat pericardiocentesis complicated by right ventricular perforation requiring surgical repair. Ventricular function fully recovered with supportive care.DiscussionPDS is infrequently recognized and may be precipitated by rapid drainage in patients with chronic effusions and underlying myocardial disease, such as systemic sclerosis.Take-Home MessagesPostpericardiocentesis deterioration should prompt consideration of PDS rather than reflexive repeat drainage. Slower or staged pericardial drainage may reduce the risk of catastrophic complications.

History of Presentation and Past Medical History

A 32-year-old woman with a history of systemic sclerosis and hypertension presented with 1 week of progressive chest pain associated with intermittent nausea, vomiting, and palpitations. Her medications included irbesartan and mycophenolate mofetil. On presentation, vital signs revealed a heart rate of 106 beats/min, blood pressure of 199/140 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air.Take-Home Messages•Hemodynamic deterioration after pericardiocentesis should prompt consideration of pericardial decompression syndrome, particularly in patients with chronic pericardial effusions and underlying myocardial disease, and it should not be assumed to represent recurrent tamponade.•Large-volume and rapid pericardial drainage may increase the risk of pericardial decompression syndrome; staged or slower drainage strategies may reduce complications and prevent unnecessary repeat pericardiocentesis.

Differential Diagnosis

Based on the patient's presentation, the initial differential diagnosis included but was not limited to the following: acute coronary syndrome given chest pain and palpitations; pulmonary embolism due to acute symptoms and tachycardia; acute pericarditis in the setting of systemic sclerosis; atrial fibrillation or other tachyarrhythmias; hypertensive emergency; and, less likely, aortic pathology or esophageal disease.

Investigations

Laboratory evaluation was notable for a serum creatinine of 1.8 mg/dL and elevated high-sensitivity troponin I (108 ng/L, repeat 96 ng/L). Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly without pulmonary infiltrates (Figure 1). Computed tomography angiography of the chest excluded pulmonary embolism but revealed a large pericardial effusion. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a large circumferential pericardial effusion, measuring up to 2.9 cm posteriorly, with atrial diastolic collapse and a dilated inferior vena cava, consistent with early tamponade physiology. Severe concentric left ventricular hypertrophy was present with preserved systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF]: 60%) (Video 1).Figure 1Initial Chest Radiography on Presentation Showing Cardiomegaly Without Pulmonary Infiltrates

Management

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for management of suspected scleroderma renal crisis and close monitoring prior to pericardiocentesis. She subsequently underwent ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis with agitated saline confirmation. A total of 1,200 mL of bloody pericardial fluid was removed continuously over approximately 10 minutes, resulting in near-complete resolution of the effusion on postprocedural imaging. She returned to the intensive care unit in stable condition.

Approximately 3 hours later, the patient developed acute dyspnea with increasing oxygen requirements. Repeat echocardiography demonstrated newly reduced LVEF, without re-accumulation of pericardial effusion (Video 2). Shortly thereafter, she became tachycardic and hypotensive, with mean arterial pressure falling below 60 mm Hg. Vasopressor support was ordered; however, she acutely lost consciousness and was found pulseless. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated per advanced cardiac life support guidelines, and the patient was intubated. During resuscitation, given the abrupt hemodynamic collapse, recurrent tamponade was suspected. Emergent bedside pericardiocentesis was performed, yielding approximately 600 mL of bright red blood with return of spontaneous circulation. Owing to continued sanguineous drainage, cardiothoracic surgery was emergently consulted. The patient was taken to the operating room for a pericardial window, which revealed hemopericardium, prompting conversion to median sternotomy. Intraoperatively, a right ventricular free wall perforation was identified and repaired.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperatively, the patient required vasopressor support and mechanical ventilation. Chest radiography demonstrated severe bilateral pulmonary edema (Figure 2). A hemodialysis catheter was placed, and continuous renal replacement therapy was initiated for oliguric renal failure. Over the ensuing 48 hours, she demonstrated rapid clinical improvement, allowing discontinuation of vasopressors and extubation. Repeat echocardiography showed complete recovery of left ventricular systolic function (LVEF: 55%).Figure 2Chest Radiography Showing Severe Bilateral Pulmonary Edema

The patient was transferred to the medical ward for management of scleroderma renal crisis and was subsequently discharged to inpatient rehabilitation. One month later, outpatient cardiology follow-up revealed excellent functional recovery without recurrent symptoms.

Discussion

This case illustrates 2 distinct and rare complications occurring sequentially: pericardial decompression syndrome (PDS) following the initial pericardiocentesis, and right ventricular perforation resulting from a second, emergent pericardiocentesis performed during cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Differentiating these events is critical, as each carries unique pathophysiologic mechanisms, management considerations, and prevention strategies.

The patient's initial hemodynamic collapse occurred hours after successful large-volume pericardial drainage and in the absence of recurrent effusion, strongly supporting PDS as the primary etiology. PDS is defined by paradoxical hemodynamic compromise after pericardial fluid drainage, most commonly manifesting as acute left ventricular failure, pulmonary edema, or shock.1, 2, 3 Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain PDS. The most widely accepted hypothesis involves sudden removal of pericardial constraint, leading to increased venous return and acute right ventricular expansion. This can result in interventricular septal shift, impaired left ventricular filling, and reduced cardiac output.2 Additional theories include autonomic imbalance, myocardial stunning, and coronary hypoperfusion due to abrupt changes in transmural pressures.3 Patients with chronic pericardial effusions appear particularly vulnerable, as their myocardium may have adapted to long-standing external constraint.4

In this case, approximately 1,200 mL of pericardial fluid was removed over a short interval of approximately 10 minutes. Sudden removal of pericardial constraint may overwhelm a myocardium that has adapted to chronically elevated pericardial pressures. Staged or slower drainage with indwelling catheter placement has been suggested as a preventive strategy to mitigate this risk, especially in high-risk populations.4,5 Also, in this patient, systemic sclerosis likely played a central role. Scleroderma is associated with myocardial fibrosis, microvascular dysfunction, and concentric hypertrophy, all of which reduce ventricular compliance.6 The severe left ventricular hypertrophy observed on echocardiography suggests underlying restrictive physiology, rendering the heart unable to accommodate sudden increases in preload after large-volume pericardiocentesis. Importantly, repeat echocardiography obtained after clinical deterioration showed reduced systolic function without recurrent effusion, supporting PDS as the primary etiology rather than tamponade recurrence. However, given the rarity of PDS and the acuity of the patient's collapse, clinicians focused on tamponade physiology, prompting repeat pericardiocentesis.

A second, separate complication occurred when the patient's postprocedural shock was misinterpreted as recurrent tamponade, prompting emergent repeat pericardiocentesis during cardiac arrest. This second pericardiocentesis directly resulted in right ventricular perforation, as confirmed intraoperatively. Importantly, echocardiography performed prior to clinical decompensation demonstrated no re-accumulation of pericardial effusion, further indicating that the initial collapse was due to PDS rather than tamponade recurrence.

This case also highlights a rare hemodynamic presentation of cardiac tamponade with severe hypertension, rather than the classic hypotensive profile. The markedly elevated blood pressure (199/140 mm Hg) was likely driven by scleroderma renal crisis, underscoring the importance of recognizing that tamponade physiology may coexist with hypertensive emergencies in select clinical contexts.

This sequence underscores a critical learning point: Hemodynamic collapse after pericardiocentesis is not synonymous with recurrent tamponade. Awareness of PDS may prompt more cautious fluid removal, staged drainage, or heightened surveillance in high-risk patients. Some investigators advocate limiting initial drainage volume and leaving indwelling catheters for gradual decompression, particularly in chronic effusions.5 Despite the severity of complications, this patient's rapid myocardial recovery further supports a transient functional phenomenon consistent with PDS rather than irreversible myocardial injury.

Conclusions

PDS is a rare but life-threatening complication of pericardiocentesis that requires heightened clinical awareness, particularly in patients with chronic pericardial effusions and underlying myocardial disease such as systemic sclerosis. Misinterpretation of postprocedural shock as recurrent tamponade can lead to unnecessary and dangerous interventions. This case highlights the importance of recognizing PDS as a distinct clinical entity, using repeat imaging judiciously, and adopting cautious drainage strategies to prevent avoidable morbidity and mortality.
