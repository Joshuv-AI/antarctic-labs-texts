Complete chloroplast genome of Crotalaria juncea, a bast fibre crop with late-acting self-incompatibility, reveals evolutionary insights and phylogenetic relationships within Fabaceae
Authors: Dipnarayan Saha, Subhojit Datta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCrotalaria juncea, a member of Fabaceae family, offers significant agricultural, commercial, and environmental benefits. The bast fibres from C. juncea stems are utilised across diverse industries for the production of ropes, threads, twines, fishing nets, and paper. This fast-growing legume enhances soil fertility through nitrogen fixation when applied as green manure. It exhibits a late-acting self-incompatibility trait, promoting outcrossing and resulting in a heterogeneous plant population. The maternally inherited chloroplast sequence is ideal for species identification, understanding the evolution of self-incompatible species, and analysing population dynamics.ResultsThe chloroplast genome of C. juncea was identified using whole-genome skimming data. The chloroplast genome was 152,635 base pairs in length and contained 36.62% GC content. It comprises 127 genes, including 82 protein-coding genes, 37 tRNA genes, and 8 rRNA genes. Comparative analysis revealed significant collinearity and synteny between the chloroplast genomes of C. juncea and other Crotalaria spp. Despite conserved codon usage and repetitive sequences distributions across Crotalaria spp., most protein-coding genes exhibited higher Ka/Ks values, suggesting positive evolutionary selection with adaptive significance. Phylogenetic analysis of shared chloroplast genes, compared to the whole chloroplast genome, yielded superior clade resolution within the Fabaceae family. The analysis also identified highly diverse genes—clpP, matK, ndhF, and rpoC2—based on nucleotide diversity estimation, underscoring their potential utility for species identification within the Crotalaria genus and broader Fabaceae family members.ConclusionsThis study presents a comprehensive analysis of the C. juncea chloroplast genome. The variable sequence and gene features offer substantial potential for applications in species- and population-level identification, phylogenetic studies, and the exploration of adaptive evolution in C. juncea and other Fabaceae species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08702-8.

Background

Crotalaria juncea L., commonly known as sunnhemp or Indian hemp, is a rapidly growing annual plant in the Fabaceae family that plays a crucial role in sustainable agriculture and ecological restoration [1]. It is the only plant species from the large pantropical Crotalaria genus that is used in agriculture. Originating in the Indo-Pakistan region, this species is particularly valued as a cover crop in agroforestry systems. It improves soil fertility through green manuring, which in turn boosts crop yields and reduces the need for synthetic fertilisers [2, 3]. It also improves the physical, microbiological, and physicochemical properties of soil and shows promise in phytoremediation, notably in heavy metal uptake, biomass production, bioenergy feedstock production, and fodder crops [4]. Certain species of Crotalaria in Asia and Africa are utilised in traditional medicine due to their anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and analgesic properties. Beyond its agricultural applications, C. juncea is recognised for its commercial product, bast fibre [5, 6]. The fibre extracted from sunnhemp stems is a lignocellulosic material utilised in various small- to medium-sized industrial products, including ropes, twines, floor mats, fishing net strings, handmade paper, high-quality tissue paper, and currency paper.

Crotalaria juncea possesses hermaphroditic papilionaceous flowers characterised by a banner petal, two wings, two fused keel petals, and dimorphic stamens. The flowers attract specialised insect pollinators, specifically Megachile sp. and Xylocopa sp., for natural cross-pollination [7, 8]. The intricate structure of C. juncea flowers results in varying degrees of sexual interference and late zygote abortion, which impede self-fertilisation. Reproductive barriers present significant challenges in genetically improving C. juncea, as effective breeding measures often require controlled pollination for specific crossings and self-pollination to maintain the purity of the breeding lines. Genetic studies on important traits and genetic diversity assessments in C. juncea are limited because of the lack of genomic resources [5]. Recently, the genetics of late-acting self-incompatibility (LSI) and its associated genomic sequences have been analyzed [9–11]. Many molecular markers, such as expressed sequence tag simple sequence repeats (EST-SSRs), intron-linked polymorphic (ILP) markers, and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) allele-specific markers have been developed in C. juncea for genetic analysis and breeding purposes [12].

Crotalaria is a highly diverse genus comprising more than 700 species. It is considered monophyletic, i.e., it descended from a common ancestor and diverged from closely related taxa approximately 23–30 million years ago [13]. There is not much information on how these plants evolved, especially regarding their interactions with rhizobia, fibre production, and reproduction, because there are not enough genetic studies. Phylogenetic studies of Crotalaria spp. have been conducted through selective amplification and sequencing of plastid and ribosomal ITS regions [13]. A complete cp. genome sequence as a reference sequence offers a more comprehensive genetic perspective and is therefore considered more reliable than studies based on single genes. Before this study, only a few Crotalaria spp., had their full chloroplast (cp.) genomes published, like C. albida [14], C. pallida [15], C. assamica [16], and C. sessiliflora [17], which are often used in traditional medicines. This study presents the complete cp. genome sequence of C. juncea, a species extensively utilised for commercial bast fibre production and as a source of green manure. Additionally, C. juncea typically exhibits the biological phenomenon of LSI.

Results

Features of the Crotalaria juncea chloroplast genome

The cp. genome of C. juncea cv. SUIN 053 was assembled by low-coverage genome sequencing and genome skimming. In the assembly process, the published cp. genomes of C. albida (NC_061361) and C. pallida (NC_053562) served as references for ‘seed sequences’ and annotations. The cp. genome was assembled from 970,814 mapped read pairs, resulting in an average sequence coverage depth of 1889.92x (Fig. S1). The total length of the assembled C. juncea cp. genome was 152,635 base pairs (bp) and had a typical quadripartite circular structure (Fig. 1). The large single copy (LSC) and small single copy (SSC) regions are separated by a pair of inverted repeat (IRA and IRB) regions, respectively. The lengths of the nucleotide sequences for LSC and SSC were 83,587 bp and 18,092 bp, respectively, whereas the IRA and IRB regions measured 25,478 bp each. Annotation of the C. juncea cp. genome revealed an overall GC content of 36.6%, with the LSC region having 32.2% GC, the SSC region 35.0%, and the IR region having 42.9% GC (Table 1). C. juncea cp. genome annotation revealed 127 genes, comprising 82 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 37 tRNA genes, and eight rRNA genes. Most of the PCGs were predicted to be involved in photosynthesis and self-replication (Table 2). Six PCGs (ndhB, rpl2, rpl23, rps7, rps12, and ycf2), nine tRNA genes, and four rRNA genes were duplicated. Two PCGs, psbL and rpl22, which are usually present in the cp. genomes of other Fabaceae spp., were absent in the C. juncea. The rps16 gene possessed an internal stop codon and was excluded from the NCBI GenBank file (PP998656). Twenty genes contained intron(s), of which 18 possessed a single intron (10 PCGs and 8 tRNAs), whereas two genes (clpP and ycf3) contained multiple introns (Table 3). Most intron-containing PCGs are cis-spliced, except for rps12, which has three distinct exons and undergoes trans-splicing. Two exons were duplicated as they were located in the IR regions (Fig. S2). A comparison of PCGs in the cp. genome of C. juncea and four other published cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp. (C. albida, C. pallida, C. assamica, and C. sessiliflora) revealed 71 PCGs that were commonly shared among the five cp. genomes (Fig. S3A) (Table S1). A comparative analysis of the C. juncea cp. genome and the mitochondrial genome of C. pallida revealed 78 homologous sequences (Fig. S3B). These homologous sequences vary in length from 40 to 1,125 nucleotides and are classified as mitochondrial plastid DNAs (MTPTs). The combined size of the homologous sequence was 14,333 nucleotides, constituting approximately 9.4% of the C. juncea cp. genome. Two homologous fragments, each 1,125 nucleotides in length (identity percentage of 97.8%), were located within the rps7 and trnV-GAC intervals, as well as the ndhC-ndhK-ndhJ-trnF-GAA regions of the C. juncea cp. genome, and were represented in the rps7-adjacent region of the C. pallida mt genome.

Identification of repeat sequences in the chloroplast genome

Repetitive DNA sequences, encompassing both long repeats and short simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in the C. juncea cp. genome and four other Crotalaria cp. genomes, were analysed using the REPuter, TRF, and MegaSSR tools. SSRs in all five cp. genomes, including those of C. juncea, showed similar distribution patterns. A total of 153 SSRs were identified in the cp. genome of C. juncea, whereas the number of SSRs ranged from 147 to 163 in the cp. genomes of other Crotalaria spp. (Fig. 2A). Dinucleotide SSRs were the predominant category (49.7%) of SSRs, followed by mononucleotide repeats (39.9%) in the cp. genome of C. juncea and other Crotalaria spp. Tri- and tetranucleotide repeats were also found in all Crotalaria cp. genomes, but at lower frequencies. However, some penta- and hexa-nucleotide repeats were identified exclusively in C. sessiliflora and C. juncea, respectively. In all Crotalaria cp. genomes, A/T repeats constituted the predominant mononucleotide repeats, whereas AT/AT nucleotide combinations were the principal dinucleotide repeat types, followed by AG/CT repeats (Fig. 2B).

The total number of long repeat sequences in the cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp. ranged from 77 in C. juncea to 104 in C. albida. These long repeats were categorised into five distinct types: forward, reverse, complement, palindrome, and tandem repeats (Fig. 2C). The most prevalent type of long repeat was palindromic repeats, comprising 33.7% to 45.9% of the total, with 42.9% in C. juncea. This was closely followed by the forward and tandem repeat sequences. In C. juncea, the lengths of tandem repeats ranged from 20 to 48 nucleotides. The tandem repeat lengths in the other Crotalaria cp. genomes were similar, except for C. pallida, where the tandem repeat lengths ranged from 9 to 34 nucleotides.

Prediction of RNA editing sites and amino acid changes

For plant RNA-editing site prediction, the online PREPACT program was used to compare Crotalaria cp. PCGs with the RNA-editing database of the reference Pisum sativum cp. genome. A total of 227–234 cytosine-to-uracil (C→U) editing sites were predicted in 52 PCGs of Crotalaria cp. genomes, including 230 C→U edits in C. juncea (Table S2). The predominant codon change from ACU→AUU was observed for the conversion of the amino acid threonine (T) to isoleucine (I) (Fig. 3A). Gene-wise analysis of the RNA editing frequencies revealed that all ndh genes corresponded to approximately 18.6% of the total predicted RNA-editing sites (Fig. 3B). ycf2, ycf1, and rpoC2 individually contained the majority of RNA editing sites, accounting for approximately 13.9%, 10.5%, and 8.4%, respectively. Among the total number of RNA-editing sites, 33.8% were located within the first base of a codon, whereas 66.2% were located at the second base of a codon (Fig. 3C). The predicted RNA-editing sites would result in 17 types of amino acid conversions, including one instance of a change in the termination codon. Approximately 62% of the amino acid changes (T→I, S→L, H→Y, S→F, P→L, and T→M) resulting from RNA editing may have increased the hydrophobicity of the encoded amino acids (Fig. 3D).

Codon usage and amino acid frequencies

Codon usage frequencies, nucleotide diversity, and RNA editing site predictions were analysed in the cp. genome of C. juncea and compared with those of other Crotalaria spp. Each of the five Crotalaria spp. cp. genomes comprises 61 sense codons that encode 20 amino acids and three stop codons: UAA, UAG, and UGA. The total number of triplet codons in the five Crotalaria spp. strains varied from 50,878 in C. juncea to 51,030 in C. assamica. The predominant codon across all five Crotalaria spp. (2,335–2,415) is UUU (phenylalanine), whereas the least utilized codons are GCG (alanine) in C. juncea and C. pallida and CGC (arginine) in the C. albida, C. assamica, and C. sessiliflora cp. genomes (Table S3). Among the three types of stop codons, UAA was the most prevalent. Despite the changes in the overall codon count, the types of codons and amino acids were constant throughout all five Crotalaria cp. genomes (Fig. S4A). Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) statistics indicate a gene’s codon usage bias. An RSCU value of one signifies the absence of codon usage bias, whereas RSCU values greater than one denotes positive codon usage bias, and vice versa. Codons with RSCU values less than 0.6 are deemed underrepresented, whereas those with RSCU values greater than 1.6 are classified as overrepresented. The RSCU values of all protein-coding genes in the cp. genomes of the five Crotalaria spp. showed a trend similar to that of the C. juncea cp. genome (Fig. S4B). Thirty-three of the 64 codons presented RSCU values greater than 1.0, indicating the absence of codon usage bias, whereas 31 presented RSCU values of less than 1.0. Among the 33 codons exhibiting an RSCU > 1.0, 27 (81.8%) ended with A or U, specifically 15 codons ended with U and 12 ended with A. Only one codon, AGA (arginine), had an overrepresented codon usage bias (> 1.6), whereas seven codons were underrepresented, with an RSCU value < 0.6. Overall, the RSCU values suggest that the cp. genomes of C. juncea and other Crotalaria spp. are conserved, with few changes among related species.

Comparison of rearrangements in the IR boundary regions

The IRScope tool was employed to analyse the expansion and variations in gene rearrangements at the boundaries of the junction regions, specifically in IRA, IRB, LSC, and SSC, in the five Crotalaria cp. genomes (Fig. 4). The lengths of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions were relatively stable, with the LSC region ranging from 83,587 to 84,032 bp, SSC region ranging from 17,977 to 18,092 bp, and IR region ranging from 25,478 to 25,535 bp in length. The junction sites of the IR regions in the five Crotalaria cp. genomes presented consistent gene arrangement patterns, except for the absence of rpl2 and presence of the pseudogene ycf1. The genes trnH and rlp2 were located adjacent to the junction region between the LSC and IRA in C. juncea, C. pallida, and C. albida; however, rpl2 was absent from the LSC-IRA junction in the cp. genomes of C. assamica and C. sessiliflora. No alterations were observed in the arrangement of psbA located proximal to the LSC-IRA junction. rps19 and rpl2 were identified in the LSC-IRB junction sites of the cp. genomes of C. juncea, C. pallida, and C. albida; however, rpl2 was absent from the IRB region of the cp. genomes of C. assamica and C. sessiliflora. Within the junction boundary of SSC and IRA, the ndhF gene exhibited minor alterations. The ndhF gene spans the SSC-IRA junction and extends to the IRA by only four bases in C. sessiliflora, but in the other four cp. genomes, ndhF was extended to 27 bases in the IRA. Additionally, a copy of the pseudogene ycf1 was present across the SSC region (inside the IRA) of the C. pallida cp. genome. The gene arrangement of the SSC and IRB junction regions followed the same pattern as that of the LSC-IRA/IRB junction site. The pseudogene ycf1 spanned the SSC-IRB junction sites of the C. juncea, C. pallida, and C. albida cp. genomes but was absent from the SSC-IRB junction sites of the C. assamica and C. sessiliflora cp. genomes.

Genomic variants of Crotalaria chloroplast genomes

The mVISTA and AliTv tools were used to analyse global sequence variation among the five Crotalaria cp. genomes (Fig. 5). The cp. genome of C. albida was used as the reference for alignment. The alignment spanned 152,742 nucleotides. Based on the 70% variable sequence criterion, the number of variable regions in the aligned cp. genome pairs of C. albida-C. pallida, C. albida-C. juncea, C. albida-C. assamica and C. albida-C. sessiliflora were 294, 300, 283, and 294, respectively. In general, PCGs were more conserved than intronic and intergenic regions. Genomic variations were particularly noticeable in the intronic and intergenic spacer regions (also known as conserved non-coding sequences or CNS). Most genomic variations are insertions/deletions (InDels). The significantly divergent non-coding regions in these five cp. genomes were probably trnH-psbA, matK-rbcL, rbcL-atpB, trnL-trnT, trnG-trnR-atpF, psbM-trnY, trnG-trnfM, trnQ-rps16, rps12-clpP, rps8-rpl14, trnR-rrn5S-rrn4.5 S-rrn23S, and trnL. Most of these sequences are found in the LSC region. The chloroplast genome alignment of Crotalaria (LASTZ all-vs-all alignment) indicated a high degree of genomic collinearity, indicating that the genomes were highly conserved.

Selective pressure on protein-coding genes

The selection pressure estimated through the Ka/Ks ratio was compared among the 44 shared PCGs among the five Crotalaria spp. cp. genomes (Table S4). The average Ka/Ks ratio was 1.549, and the highest Ka/Ks ratio was 4.137 for ndhA (Fig. 7). Three genes, ndhH, psbZ, and rps18, had average Ka/Ks ratios close to 1.0, suggesting that these genes are highly conserved and neutral. Thirty-six genes displayed average Ka/Ks ratios exceeding 1.0, of which 20 were significant (p < 0.05), indicating that these genes were under a positive selection pressure. Among them, nine genes showed a significantly high level of positive selection, with Ka/Ks > 2.0 and p < 0.05. The positively selected genes were essential for critical chloroplast function and gene expression. For example, accD encodes acetyl-CoA carboxylase, which is necessary for fatty acid synthesis and plays a significant role in plant viability, chloroplast function, and development. Some of these genes, such as atpA, atpB, atpE, and atpI, encode ATP synthase components and are involved in energy synthesis. Similarly, genes such as psaA, psaB, and psbA are integral to the formation of photosystems I and II, whereas ndhA, ndhD, and ndhF are crucial for the electron transport chain within the photosystems. Other positively selected genes included rbcL, which is required for the formation of the Rubisco large subunit; rpoC2, which is necessary for the formation of Plastid-Encoded RNA Polymerase (PEP); and rps2-8, which is essential for the assembly of ribosomal small subunit proteins. Five genes exhibited an average Ka/Ks ratio of < 1.0, indicating that these genes were under negative or purifying selection pressure.

Phylogeny of Crotalaria and other Fabaceae cp. genomes

The phylogenetic relationships among 28 representatives of the major clades of Fabaceae spp. (Papilionoideae), including five Crotalaria spp., were analysed using shared CDS and complete cp. genome sequences. The cp. genomes of A. thaliana and P. trichocarpa were chosen as outgroup sequences to root trees. Phylogenetic relationships were assessed using both the ML and BI methods, and the results are presented in (Fig. 8A-D). Overall, the tree topologies were well resolved in both the ML and BI trees, based on the complete cp. genome or 57 PCG sequences as source data, respectively. The deeper nodes in all cases separated the major lineages and revealed monophyletic groupings. The trees constructed from the complete cp. genomes effectively resolved the clustering of the respective IRL, Millettioid, and Dalbergioid representatives in both ML and BI trees. Nonetheless, the cp. genomes of the Robinioid and Genistoid representatives, especially S. flavescens, were incorrectly clustered. Notably, all other cp. genomes of the Genistoid clade and Crotalaria spp. were grouped together, indicating their phylogenetic similarity. Conversely, the ML and BI trees constructed from 57 concatenated PCGs common to all cp. genomes exhibited a markedly clear clustering pattern, effectively resolving all clades without any mismatches. According to the cp. genome and PCG sequences, C. assamica was identified as the closest neighbour of C. juncea in both the ML and BI trees. All the phylogenetic trees presented strong node support, indicating the robustness and reliability of the constructed trees. In general, the phylogenetic trees from this study revealed that the Genistoids, encompassing all Crotalaria spp. represent the earliest diverging clade. All species within this clade consistently cluster together indicating a close evolutionary relationship and a shared common ancestor distinct from other Fabaceae taxa. The IRL clade and Robinioids appear as successive lineages, followed by Dalbergioids and Millettioids, which were resolved as later-diverging clades. Notably, the Millettioids formed a particularly well-supported and cohesive clade, with short internal branch lengths suggesting relatively low genetic divergence among its member taxa. Branch length variation across the tree indicated heterogeneous levels of molecular divergence among lineages; however, divergence times were not estimated because of the lack of time calibration.

Discussion

The significance of C. juncea is widely recognised in various domains, including commercial bast fibre crops, green manure, and cover crops in agriculture, bioenergy feedstock, and forage crops. Some Crotalaria spp. are used in traditional medicine [2–6]. This underscores the need for advanced research to understand the biology of this crop and to leverage this knowledge for its genetic enhancement and effective use in agriculture. A major challenge in breeding C. juncea is the genetic impact of LSI, which promotes outcrossing and results in heterogeneous plant populations [11]. The lack of adequate genomic resources, such as organelle and nuclear genomes, in C. juncea and other Crotalaria spp. limits a thorough understanding of phylogenetic and evolutionary knowledge, as well as improving them through breeding. Prior to this study, the cp. genome sequences of C. albida, C. pallida, and C. sessiliflora, which are used in traditional Chinese medicine, were reported [14, 15, 17]. This study provides a comprehensive cp. genome sequence map and comparative genomic analysis of C. juncea derived from genome skimming next-generation DNA sequencing data. The complete cp. genome of C. juncea was assembled into a 152,635 bp circular bipartite molecule comprising the LSC and SSC regions, which are typically divided into two inverted repeats (IRs). The size of the C. juncea cp. genome was similar to those of C. albida and C. pallida, with comparable GC content and SSR repeats. Among the various long-repeat types, palindromic repeats are the most common in the cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp., unlike other Fabaceae spp., which predominantly have forward repeats [19]. The C. juncea cp. genome contains 127 genes, including 82 protein-coding genes, similar to the C. albida cp. genome. However, it only marginally surpassed the gene count of the C. sessiliflora cp. genome. The overall GC content in the cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp., similar to other Fabaceae spp., showed minor variations (35–37%). The IR regions have a higher GC content (42–44%) than the LSC and SSC regions, a pattern commonly observed in plastid genomes [20]. The high GC content in the IR regions may be due to the presence of highly conserved and duplicated rRNA genes (rrn16S, rrn23S, rrn4.5S, and rrn5S), which are characterised by a high GC percentage. Regarding expansion and contraction within and around the IR boundaries, the IR regions of the Crotalaria spp. cp. genomes displayed relatively consistent structural arrangements, with minimal changes in the position and presence or absence of genes within the IR regions. Previous analyses of plastids in the inverted repeat-lacking clade (IRLC) of Fabaceae-Papilionoideae have revealed significant rearrangements in the IR regions [21], and these regions have remained relatively conserved in other papilionoid families, such as the cp. genomes of Dalbergiods [22] and Crotalaria spp [17]. The structural and feature similarities of the cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp. indicate a significant degree of sequence conservation, albeit with some variation.

The chloroplast genomes of different Crotalaria spp. have similar numbers and arrangements of PCGs, indicating that these genomes have undergone minimal evolutionary changes. This pattern has also been observed in the cp. genomes of other Fabaceae spp [19]. This explains why important cp. genes from endosymbionts are preserved, because they are required for life-sustaining functions such as photosynthesis, energy (ATP) synthesis, and gene expression [23, 24]. Similar to other Fabaceae spp., the rpl22 gene is typically missing from the cp. genomes of C. juncea, C. albida, and C. pallida. This is likely because it is transferred to the nuclear genome [25]. Although many Fabaceae spp. have lost the rps16 gene from their cp. genomes [25], it was retained in all five Crotalaria spp. Among the common intron-containing PCGs, introns were absent in petB, petD, rpl2, and rpl16 of the chloroplasts of C. assamica and C. sessiliflora. However, the introns of these genes were retained in the cp. genomes of C. juncea, C. albida, and C. pallida. Similarly, introns of the rps16 gene are missing in the cp. genome of C. sessiliflora, and the gene has become a pseudogene [17]. The dynamic nature of intron loss within genes and the conversion of genes into pseudogenes has been reported in the chloroplast genomes of various plant species. Although identifying the precise causes of the loss of specific genes across different chloroplast genomes is challenging, these phenomena may indicate species divergence and evolutionary significance [26]. These may serve as important phylogenetic markers for distinguishing and identifying closely related species within the family Fabaceae - Papilionoideae.

Repeat units, such as SSRs, play a pivotal role in genetic recombination. They are important for genomic variation, including organelle genomes and their evolution [27, 28]. SSR repeats are mostly conserved in the cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp., with mono (A/T) and dinucleotide (AT/AT) repeats being the predominant classes. These A/T- and AT-rich repeats are also found in other IRL members, such as Vigna sp. and Onobrychis gaubae [21, 29]. In addition to SSRs, forward repeats and palindromic repeats are major long repeat types in Crotalaria chloroplasts, similar to other IRL clade members [21]. These repeats are important for phylogenetic and population studies because they are related to genomic changes in otherwise conserved plastid genomes [28].

RSCU analysis shows the non-random use of synonymous codons in a genome, which is key for organelle genomic variations with evolutionary implications [30, 31]. In Crotalaria spp. cp. genomes, RSCU analysis found both positive (RSCU > 1.0) and negative codon (RSCU < 1.0), with many codons ending in A/U. Among the preferred amino acids, Arg (AGA) was the predominant amino acid in the cp. genomes of all Crotalaria spp. This pattern is similar to that observed in most dicotyledonous plant chloroplasts, including Fabaceae spp [19, 32].

Hypervariable cp. regions with informative sequences are considered as mutation hotspots. They are useful DNA barcodes for identifying closely related plant species, constructing phylogenetic trees, and conducting evolutionary studies [33]. The cp. genome is maternally inherited, highly conserved, and has a low rate of mutation. Thus, variable cp. genetic markers are often better than nuclear genetic markers for genetic discrimination studies [19]. In Crotalaria spp., several divergent sequence hotspots have been identified in the intergenic regions and PCGs. Intergenic regions such as trnK-rbcL, trnL-trnT, rps12-clpP, rps8-rpl14, and trnR-rrn5S-rrn4.5S, along with the coding regions of ycf1, rps15, and ndhH, were found to be significant. The intergenic region trnL‒trnT and coding gene ycf1 were also found to be highly informative in the cp. genome of O. gaubae, a Fabaceae–Papilionoideae family member [21]. Among the total variable sites in cp. genomes, parsimony informative sites are a subset of variable sites characterised by the presence of at least two distinct nucleotides, each occurring in a minimum of two different sequences [22, 34]. These sites are particularly valuable because they provide substantial phylogenetic information for reconstructing evolutionary relationships and distinguishing between species, thereby serving as potential barcodes. Among the various PCGs of Crotalaria spp., rpoC2, ndhF, matK, and clpP produced a high number of variable and parsimony informative sites, rendering them suitable for barcoding. Similar parsimony informative hotspots have also been identified in the plastomes of Dalbergia sp. of the Papilionoideae family [22].

Assessment of potential RNA editing events indicates that evolutionary processes have facilitated corrections of missense mutations in genes through insertions, deletions, and modifications of transcript sequences [35]. In the cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp., RNA editing events were predominantly observed in ndh genes. The most frequent editing event was the conversion of ACU (threonine) to AUU (isoleucine). C-to-U editing of ndh genes has been widely reported in plants. This type of RNA editing is likely associated with critical functions in plants, such as maintenance of photosystem activity and stress tolerance under adverse conditions. It often exhibits positive selection during evolutionary processes [36]. The Ka/Ks ratio helps us understand how genes evolve and adapt [37]. Genes with Ka/Ks ratios < 1 are subjected to strong purifying selection to maintain functionality, as mutations altering their amino acid sequence are often deleterious and eliminated by natural selection. Conversely, genes with Ka/Ks > 1 are either under positive selection or are significant for adaptation to specific environments, as alterations in amino acid sequences enhance an organism’s survival under varying environmental conditions. A Ka/Ks ratio close to one suggests that changes in the amino acid sequence occur randomly, resulting in neither beneficial nor detrimental effects [37]. In Crotalaria spp., 36 plastidial genes exhibited a Ka/Ks > 1.0, with nine displaying high Ka/Ks values of > 2.0, indicating that they were predominantly under positive selection. These genes may have played significant roles in enhancing the adaptations of Crotalaria spp. during evolutionary processes.

The conserved nature and high copy number of cp. genome sequences provide valuable insights into the evolutionary history, species identification, and phylogenetic relationships among plant families [24]. Phylogenetic analysis employing complete cp. genome sequences and coding sequences from 28 representative Fabaceae spp. across various clades, including five Crotalaria spp., revealed highly similar topologies in both ML and Bayesian analyses. Most clusters were consistent with the known clades and were resolved using complete cp. genome sequences. However, representative genomes from the Robinioid and Gensitoid clades (particularly S. flavescens) were misclustered in this study. Conversely, phylogeny based on the coding sequence of the shared plastid genes exhibited a stringent resolution of clades, suggesting that plastidial protein-coding gene sequences are reliable for robust phylogenetic analysis. The five Crotalaria spp. belonging to the Crotalarieae tribe clustered together, closely followed by the Genisteae tribe, perhaps owing to the significant similarities in their chloroplast genome sequences. The divergence of Genistoid clade relative to the other Fabaceae clades may have resulted from a deep split within the Fabaceae, potentially associated with early ecological or morphological differentiation, although the present analysis did not test this hypothesis. However, these findings, in terms of clade resolution, corroborate the previous results of phylogenetic analyses of legumes using matK gene [38] and protein-coding genes [39]. One of the notable findings from the phylogenetic analyses in the present study was the obvious pattern indicating that using combined sequences from protein-coding genes is more effective for understanding evolutionary relationships than using the entire cp. genome. Protein-coding genes in chloroplasts serve as reliable markers for examining deep evolutionary divergence because they maintain crucial functions and preserve ancient evolutionary traits. Conversely, phylogeny based on complete chloroplast sequences, including non-coding regions, may be suitable for obtaining finer resolution of recent-speciation events. For instance, phylogenetic studies employing shared chloroplast protein-coding genes have revealed well-supported clades and resolved systematic relationships across various plant taxa, including monocots, Zingiberaceae, and mangroves [40–42]. Currently, there is no evidence to suggest that chloroplast genes play a role in the self-incompatibility mechanism of LSI in C. juncea. However, examining phylogenetic frameworks, based on chloroplast genes, may yield valuable insights into the evolutionary history of plant species that exhibit this trait. Such analyses may also aid in elucidating the patterns of maternal inheritance in species with LSI traits. Notably, a similar pattern of correlated evolution between self-incompatibility and clonal reproduction has been observed in Solanum spp., as demonstrated by phylogenetic studies utilising ndh genes [43].

Conclusions

Crotalaria juncea is recognised for its commercial stem fibre and is utilised as a green manure for soil reclamation. It exhibits a characteristic LSI trait, rendering it a highly cross-pollinated species with diverse plant populations. This necessitates the implementation of reliable plant identification tools such as chloroplast-specific markers. Consequently, a comprehensive reference chloroplast genome of 152,635 bp was identified from the genome sequence of C. juncea, and its genes and structural features were analysed. The chloroplast genome of C. juncea was compared with those of other Crotalaria spp. to identify significant mutational hotspots and variable nucleotide sequences in the clpP, matK, ndhF, and rpoC2 genes. These genes may serve as barcode sequences for species and population differentiation. Although the chloroplast genomes are relatively conserved in Fabaceae spp., phylogenetic analysis using protein-coding gene sequences, compared to whole-cp genome sequences, successfully differentiated and clustered representative species according to their clades. Notably, the chloroplast genomes of Crotalaria spp. contains numerous protein-coding genes with high Ka/Ks values (> 1). These findings indicate that most of these genes have undergone positive evolutionary selection, potentially contributing to highly adaptive mechanisms that warrant further investigation in future studies. For instance, it would be intriguing to explore whether these positively selected genes play a role in the adaptation of the LSI traits in C. juncea. The reference chloroplast genome of C. juncea offers valuable resources for effectively identifying plant populations and studying their evolutionary history, both within the Crotalaria genus and the Fabaceae family.

Materials and methods

Plant samples, DNA extraction and sequencing

Seeds of C. juncea cv. SUIN 053, obtained from the ICAR-Central Research Institute for Jute and Allied Fibres, Barrackpore, West Bengal, India, was grown in pots filled with garden soil. Leaf samples (~500 mg) from 30-day-old plants were harvested fresh, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and extracted for genomic DNA using cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) [44]. Approximately 150 ng of high-quality genomic DNA was enzymatically digested and size-selected to enrich 250–450-bp DNA fragments through gel purification. The DNA was amplified in a thermal cycler with HiFi PCR master mix (Qiagen Inc.), and the amplified products were purified using 1X Beckman Coulter™ Agencourt AMPure XP beads. Illumina sequencing platform-specific adapters were ligated to both ends of the size-selected DNA fragments, and a whole-genome DNA sequencing library was prepared using the QIAseq FX DNA Library Kit (Qiagen) for Illumina sequencing. The library concentration and quality were determined using a Qubit 3 fluorometer with the dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and an Agilent D5000 ScreenTape System in a 4150 TapeStation (Agilent). Single-end sequencing data of approximately 4.08 gigabytes with 151-nucleotide read length were generated using the Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Chloroplast genome assembly and annotation

Adapter sequences were filtered from raw reads using fastp [45] and AdapterRemoval v2 [46]. The cp. genome of C. juncea was assembled using GetOrganelle (v1.7.7.0) [47] and the merged cp. genomes of C. pallida (NC_053562) and C. albida (NC_061361) were used as seed sequences. The default parameters were used, except for -F embplant_pt, SPAdes k-mer settings of 21, 45, 65, 85, and 105, and --reduce-reads-for-coverage of 500. The assembled C. juncea cp. genome was annotated using GEseq [48] and CPGAVAS2 [49], with the C. pallida and C. albida cp. genomes serving as references. A comprehensive cp. genome map of C. juncea was generated using the Chloroplast Genome Viewer (CPGview) package [18].

Prediction of plastidial DNA transfer to mitochondria

The transfer of mitochondrial plastid DNA (MTPT) is one of the most prominent examples of horizontal gene migration between organelles, particularly from the cp genome to the mitochondria and has evolutionary significance [50]. Because the mitochondrial genome of C. juncea is not available, the cp. genome of C. juncea was mapped to the mitochondrial genome of C. pallida (NC_086918.1) using LASTZ v.1.04.00. Homologous sequences corresponding to cp. and mitochondrial genes were identified manually, and a homology map of the mitochondria and cp. sequences was created using the Advance Circos module of TBtools-II [51].

Analysis of nucleotide diversity, codon usage, and RNA editing sites

DNA sequence polymorphism software (DnaSP v.6) [52] was used to compute the nucleotide diversity (Pi) and total mutation count in both the coding and non-coding regions. The cp. genome sequence of C. juncea was aligned with those of C. albida (NC_061361), C. pallida (NC_053562), C. assamica (GWHAZXA01000000), and C. sessiliflora (GWHBHJB01000000) using MAFFTv7 [53] and used as an input file in DnaSP. The cp. genomes were obtained from the Chloroplast Genome Information Resource (CGIR; https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/cgir/). The sliding window length was set to 800 bp, with a step size of 100 sites. The synonymous (Ks) and nonsynonymous (Ka) changes in the shared protein-coding genes of the five Crotalaria spp. cp. genomes were analysed using KaKs_Calculator 3.0 [54]. For the Ka/KS calculations, the Nei-Gojobori (NG) method and Bacterial, Archaeal, and Plant Plastid Code were used as the genetic code. Genes with a Ks value of 0 were excluded from the analysis to avoid the potential for an ambiguous (NA) Ka/Ks ratio. CodonW v1.4.4 (https://galaxy.pasteur.fr/) was used to calculate the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) in the cp. genomes of C. juncea and four other Crotalaria spp. An RSCU value of 1.0 signifies no bias in codon usage, whereas an RSCU value of less than 1.0 indicates infrequent codon usage, and an RSCU value greater than 1.0 reflects positive codon usage bias. The identification of RNA editing sites (C-to-U) in the cp. genome of C. juncea was conducted using the Plant RNA Editing Prediction and Analysis Computer Tool (PREPACT 3.0) [55] and using the cp. genome of Pisum sativum (GenBank accession no. NC_014057.1) as reference.

Comparative analysis of Crotalaria cp genomes and IR regions

A comparative analysis of the cp. genomes of C. juncea and other Crotalaria spp. retrieved from the CGIR database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/cgir/) was performed using the mVISTA program in the Shuffle-LAGAN mode. The cp. genome of C. albida was used as the reference genome for alignment with the other cp. genomes. The Alignment Toolbox and Visualization (AliTV) software was used for whole-genome alignment and visualisation of linear maps [56]. The number of protein-coding genes was compared and depicted using TBtools-II [51]. The C. juncea cp genome was compared with those of other Crotalaria spp. strains using the default parameters of the online AlignStatPlot tool (https://bioinformatics.um6p.ma/AlignStatPlot/) [57]. The expansion and contraction of the inverted repeat regions within the cp. genomes of Crotalaria spp. were analysed and compared using the IRscope web application (https://irscope.shinyapps.io/irapp) [58].

Repeat sequence identification

Different types of repeat sequences, including microsatellite repeats across all five Crotalaria cp. genomes, were also compared. Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) and their features were identified using MegaSSR software [59]. The minimum number of repeats was set at 10, 4, 4, 3, 3, and 3 for mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides, respectively. Large repeats were detected using the REPuter program [60] to identify forward, palindromic, reverse, and complementary repeats. The parameters were set to a minimum repeat size of 30 bp and a Hamming distance of three. Tandem repeats were detected using Tandem Repeat Finder v4.09.1 [61] with advanced options, including alignment parameters of match, mismatch, and InDels set to 2, 7, and 7, respectively; a minimum alignment score of 80 to consider a repeat; and a maximum period size of 500.

Phylogenetic analyses

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the complete cp. genomes and concatenated protein-coding genes (PCGs) from the cp. genomes of 28 selected Fabaceae family plants and two outgroup species, employing both the maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. The selected plant species from the Fabaceae-Papilionoideae family included representatives of published cp. genomes from various clades, including Millettioids (Cajanus cajan: NC_031429, Cyamopsis tetragonoloba: NC_037714, Mucuna macrocarpa: NC_044116, Glycine max: NC_007942, Glycine soja: NC_022868, Phaseolus vulgaris: NC_009259, Vigna radiata: NC_013843, Vigna unguiculata: NC_018051), the IRL clade (Lathyrus sativus: NC_014063, Lens culinaris: NC_027152, Cicer arietinum: NC_011163, Medicago truncatula: NC_003119, Pisum sativum: NC_014057, Trifolium repens: NC_024036, Vigna sativa: NC_02725155, Wisteria frutescens: NC_060811), Robinioids (Lotus japonicus: NC_002694, Robinia pseudoacacia: NC_026684), Dalbergioids (Arachis hypogaea: NC_037358, Dalbergia sissoo: NC_049043. Additionally, the Crotalaria cp. genomes of C. assamica (GWHAZXA01000000) and C. sessiliflora (GWHBHJB01000000), collected from the CGIR database, and the C. juncea from the current study were incorporated, along with two outgroups: Arabidopsis thaliana (NC_000932) from the Brassicaceae family and Populus trichocarpa (NC_009143) from the Saliceae family.

Phylogenetic ML and BI trees based on the complete cp. genome sequences were reconstructed using the web services of IQ-TREE (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) and the à la carte options of NJPhylogeny.fr (https://ngphylogeny.fr/) [62, 63]. Before reconstructing the phylogenetic trees, the complete cp. genomes of all species were aligned and curated using the default parameters of MAFFTv.7 [53] and Gblocks [64]. Appropriate phylogenetic model selection criteria, such as the Akaike information criterion (AIC) or Bayesian information criterion (BIC), were assessed using the ‘Model Selection’ tool of the IQ-TREE web service. The substitution model for the ML tree was GTR+I+G, and the IQ tree was reconstructed using a nonparametric bootstrap with 100 replicates. The Bayesian tree was reconstructed using MrBayes tree inference of NJPhylogeny.fr, which employs 1,00,000 generations, four chains, the GTR model, and a 0.25 burn-in fraction.

The coding sequences of all PCGs from the 30 cp. genomes were extracted using the ‘Extract’ function in Phylosuite v.1.2.3 [65] for phylogenetic analysis. The selected sequence type and codon table were ‘Chloroplast genome’ and ‘Bacterial, Archaeal, and Plant Plastid Code,’ respectively. Multiple sequence alignment of the coding sequences (CDSs) of the extracted PCGs was performed using the ‘MAFFT’ option, ‘Codon’ was used in the alignment mode, ‘Bacterial, Archaeal, and Plant Plastid Code’ was used as the codon table, and the strategy was set to ‘--auto’. The aligned sequences were curated using the tool ‘Gblocks’ in Phylosuite, with the data type chosen as ‘Nucleotide.’ The curated and aligned CDSs of 57 common PCGs from all 30 plastomes were concatenated into a single, interleaved Phylip/Fasta/Nexus formatted file. The ‘ModelFinder’ feature of Phylosuite was employed to identify appropriate substitution models for both ML-based IQ-TREE and BI-based MrBayes tree reconstruction. The parameters utilised for model selection were as follows: sequence type, DNA; Akaike information criterion (AIC) for the IQ-TREE model; Bayesian information criterion (BIC) for the MrBayes model; and partition mode specified as ‘Edge-linked’ and ‘Merge’. The 'IQ-TREE' option of Phylosuite was employed to construct the ML phylogenetic tree, utilising the 'GTR' model, with a rate of heterogeneity across sites set to +G and +I, and four rate categories. A standard bootstrap of 1000 with 1000 replicates was used to statistically support the phylogenetic branches. The Bayesian tree was constructed using the ‘MrBayes’ tool within Phylosuite, employing the GTR model, four gamma categories, rate of variation: invgamma (+I+G), the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm calculated for 2,00,000 generations, a sampling frequency of 1000, a number of chains of 4, and a burn-in fraction of 0.25. All consensus phylogenetic trees were visualised using the Interactive Tree of Life web server (https://itol.embl.de/) [66] and the trees were rooted in outgroups.

Candidate gene selection for DNA barcoding in Crotalaria

A total of 57 PCGs shared among the 28 Fabaceae spp. cp. genomes were extracted and aligned using the MAFFT tool in Phylosuite v.1.2.3. The number of InDels for each PCG dataset was determined by analysing insertion-deletion (InDel) polymorphisms using DnaSP v6.12 [52]. Polymorphic sites, genetic diversity indices, and neutrality tests, including Fu & Li's D, Fu & Li's F, and Tajima's D, were conducted using the DnaSP v6.12. Potential barcoding genes were identified from the aligned PCGs of Fabaceae chloroplasts, including those of C. juncea, and from the five Crotalaria spp. cp. genomes. This identification was based on the total number of variable sites and the number of parsimony informative sites using the online 'P-Utils' program from the PlastidHub website (https://www.plastidhub.cn/) [67].
