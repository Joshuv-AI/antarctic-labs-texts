Microbiome therapeutic PMC101 inhibits the translocation of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella while enhancing eubiosis in antibiotic-induced dysbiosis mice
Authors: Hanieh Tajdozian, Hoonhee Seo, Sukyung Kim, Md Abdur Rahim, Hyun A Park, Faezeh Sarafraz, Youjin Yoon, Hokyoung Kim, Indrajeet Barman, Chae-eun Park, Fatemeh Ghorbanian, Soyeon Lee, Hwal Rim Jeong, Ho-Yeon Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), known for their extensive antibiotic resistance, pose a severe global medical threat. Therefore, developing novel therapeutics beyond conventional antibiotics is urgently needed, and the importance of microbiome therapeutics is increasingly being recognized. This study explores the expanded systemic efficacy of PMC101, a microbiome therapeutic, beyond intestinal CRE infections and investigates its mechanism of action from a microbiome perspective. First, the genetic characteristics of the novel strain were identified through whole-genome analysis, and a scalable cultivation process was established as part of the overall development of this microbiome therapeutic. PMC101 increased the survival rate to 100%, significantly reduced disease severity scores, and prevented weight loss in CRE-infected mice treated with antibiotics. These effects are attributed to the inhibition of CRE growth in stool and the reduced detection of CRE in the lungs and kidneys, indicating suppression of systemic translocation. Metagenomic analysis revealed that PMC101 prevented the reduction in microbial population caused by antibiotics and CRE infection, restored species diversity indices, and mitigated dysbiosis while promoting eubiosis. This CRE translocation suppression was closely associated with increased CRE translocation-microbiome index, defined as the ratio of Bacteroidetes to Proteobacteria. This relationship was further confirmed through simulations using a human intestinal microbial ecosystem model. Additionally, increases in short-chain fatty acids, reductions in excessive inflammatory responses, and decreases in tissue damage were observed, all of which contribute to preventing CRE translocation. Finally, pathogen inhibition effects and safety tests were conducted, confirming the prophylactic potential of PMC101 as a microbiome therapeutic. Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRK), a Gram-negative bacterium, has emerged as a significant global health challenge because it is a major cause of severe infections and high mortality rates in humans [1, 2]. Although carbapenems, polymyxins, and tigecycline have been used in combination antibiotic therapies to control CRK infections, they do not significantly impact clinical or microbiological outcomes or improve the likelihood of mortality in patients with CRK infections [3]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has classified CRK as an “urgent threat” to public health owing to its increasing resistance to nearly all available treatment options [4, 5]. Therefore, there is a need to develop a new class of therapeutics that extends beyond the scope of traditional medicines to overcome such crises [6, 7].

As researchers increasingly investigate the role of the microbiome in human health and disease, there is a growing interest in developing interventional strategies using microbiome-based therapeutics as alternative treatments [8–11]. Recent studies have demonstrated an association between disruptions in the original microbiota composition and the onset of various diseases, including non-infections [12, 13] and infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), Clostridium difficile, Escherichia coli, Shigella spp., Streptococcus mutans, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus [14–20]. In particular, the toxicity of antibiotic use and microbiome disruption are crucial challenges in developing new drugs [21]. Therefore, the close relationship between microbiota composition disruption and the occurrence of various diseases underscores the potential of microbiome-based therapeutics, which aim to introduce beneficial functions that maintain eubiosis in the human microbiome, thereby inhibiting pathogen colonization [22]. Microbiome-based therapeutics have emerged as promising alternative antibiotics because they can modulate the intestinal environment, influence microbiota composition and function, and enhance gut health [23–26].

According to the European Medicines Agency and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), microbiome-based therapeutics, such as probiotics and spore-forming organisms, can be viable candidates in clinical trials and drug development [27, 28]. These emerging agents aim to restore the human gut microbiota from a dysbiosis state to a more diverse, healthier, and balanced composition (eubiosis) through gut-modulatory mechanisms by improving gut integrity and producing key antibacterial compounds, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). This modulation reduces intestinal permeability and inhibits the colonization of pathogenic bacteria [29–32]. Based on recent research outcomes, our team has previously demonstrated the preventive and therapeutic effects of Lactobacillus fermentum against CRK infection in an in vivo model, as well as the limitations of this approach [33]. However, the therapeutic applications of microbiomes for CRK infection remain challenging. Therefore, we conducted this study to explore the prophylactic potential of microbiome therapeutic PMC101. In-depth investigations using mouse models of antibiotic-induced intestinal dysbiosis have been continuously conducted to advance the drug development process and address the limitations of our previous study. Therefore, we aimed to determine how the microbiome therapeutic PMC101 arrests dysbiosis, promotes eubiosis, reduces intestinal permeability, and consequently inhibits CRK infection and translocation to maintain gut health.

Materials and methods

Carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae

The clinical CRK isolate used in our study was obtained from Soonchunghyang University Hospital Pathogenic Resource Bank and cultured at the Probiotics Microbiome Commercialization Center [34]. The CRK isolate was grown in MacConkey broth (BD Difco, USA) for 24 h under aerobic conditions at 37 °C. The culture was then streaked onto MacConkey agar (BD Difco, USA) plates supplemented with 10 mg/mL imipenem (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. Next, colonies were picked, transferred to MacConkey broth, and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Subsequently, bacterial growth was checked using a spectrophotometer (DR 1900, Hatch, USA), and the optical density (OD) was measured at 600 nm. Following this, pathogens (OD: 1.0, 2 × 109 CFU/mL) were stored using sterile glycerol at − 80 °C until further use.

Whole genome analysis of microbiome therapeutic PMC101

The PMC101 strain was isolated from a vaginal sample of a healthy woman and subjected to whole-genome sequencing analysis. Genomic DNA from the strains was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen, Germany) and analyzed by CJ Bioscience (Gyeonggi-do, Korea) based on PacBio sequencing as previously reported [35]. PacBio sequencing data were assembled using the PacBio SMRT Analysis software (v.2.3.0) with the HGAP2 protocol (Pacific Biosciences, USA). The resulting contigs from the PacBio sequencing data were circularized using Circulator 1.4.0 (Sanger Institute, UK). Protein coding sequences (CDSs) were predicted using Prodigal 2.6.2 [36] and classified based on Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) (EggNOG; http://eggnogdb.embl.de). For taxonomic purposes, the average nucleotide identity was calculated using the orthologous average nucleotide identity (OrthoANI) method [37].

Establishment of a cell bank, food-grade media optimization, and scale-up production for microbiome therapeutic PMC101

After identifying the PMC101 strain, the Master Cell Bank (MCB) preparation was conducted. The MCB for microbiome therapeutic PMC101 was prepared using a cleaned and decontaminated biosafety cabinet, following ICH Q5D-compliant Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) guidelines [38]. Subsequently, the bacterial strain was cultured and transferred into cryovials containing 60% glycerol, which had been autoclaved beforehand aseptically following the previous study [39]. Following this, the cryovials were subjected to shaking to mix the bacterial culture and glycerol for 5 min and stored at − 80 °C for future use. Working Cell Banks (WCBs) were subsequently prepared from the MCB under defined culture conditions. Quality control tests were performed to confirm that the MCB and the WCB are genetically identical and to ensure the WCB is free of contaminants. The prepared WCB was also stored at − 80 °C following the earlier conditions.

Moreover, to discover an effective food-grade medium (FGM) suitable for the large-scale production of PMC101, seven different types of FGMs were prepared based on the commercial MRS medium composition, as described in our previous study [40]. FGMs compositions were yeast-peptone (MB cell, Korea), Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), d-glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), magnesium sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), sodium acetate 3 M (Biosesang, Korea), sodium acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Dipotassium phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and trisodium citrate (MB cell, Korea). After preparation, the pH values of FGMs were adjusted to 6.4 using NaOH 10 M (Sigma Aldrich, USA). Initial pHs of FGM 1, FGM 2, FGM 3, FGM 4, FGM 5, FGM 6, and FGM 7 were 6.4, 6.3, 6.2, 6.5, 6.1, 5.8, and 6.0, respectively. Next, all FGMs were sterilized at 120 °C for 15 min. The PMC101 strain was then inoculated into the prepared FGMs and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h to reach the endpoint of growth. All cultures were initiated with the same OD600 value (0.043 ± 0.01) at the start (0 h). Subsequently, the growth of PMC101 and pH were checked, and the FGM with the highest bacterial growth rate was selected for the forthcoming experiments. Accordingly, PMC101 was cultured in 5 L of fresh, selected FGM using a lab-scale fermenter system (FMT-STS07, Fermentec, South Korea), achieving an OD600 of 1.0 under aerobic conditions. Cells were then collected via centrifugation at 4000 RPM for 10 min at room temperature and adjusted to an approximate concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mouse/200 μL for the in vivo experiment.

Ethics approval and experimental design for assessing PMC101 efficacy in mice models with CRK infection

Nine-week-old specific-pathogen-free female BALB/c mice were purchased from Doo Yeol Biotech, Korea. The mice were acclimated for 1 week prior to the experiments. They were housed individually in cages within a temperature-controlled environment (23 °C ± 2 °C) under a 12 h light–dark cycle and with a relative humidity range of 30–70%. To disrupt the gut microbiota, mice were administered vancomycin solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 150 mg/kg (200 µL per mouse) on days − 2 and − 1. Cyclophosphamide (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 450 mg/kg (200 µL per mouse) was also administered 3 days prior to infection to induce neutropenia [34]. Three days after neutropenia, mice were infected with 200 µL of CRK suspension (2 × 109 CFU/mouse) orally as a single dose on days 0, 2, and 7. CRK bacterial colonies obtained from the 18 h culture were suspended in a sterile saline solution (0.9% sodium chloride) to achieve the desired concentration of CRK in each mouse model. Additionally, a 200 µL solution of 0.2 M NaHCO3 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered after infection to increase the severity of infection by neutralizing stomach acidity [34]. Following this, the antibiotic-induced dysbiosis mice were randomly divided into two groups: an infection group and a treatment group, with free access to food and water. The treatment group received PMC101 culture, whereas the infection group only received sterile saline through oral gavage under the same conditions. The prophylactic dosage in mice models was determined according to previous studies [37, 41]. All animal experiments were conducted in a biosafety level 2 room (LML 20-591) at the Probiotic Microbiome Center (PMC) animal facility, Soonchunhyang University, in accordance with the World Health Organization’s guidelines. The facility is registered with the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (Registration No. 657). The animal experiments of this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Soonchunhyang University (IACUC Number SCH23-0003).

Efficacy of microbiome therapeutics PMC101 against CRK infection in antibiotic-induced gut microbiome dysbiosis mouse model

The effectiveness of microbiome therapeutics PMC101 was evaluated against CRK infection in an antibiotic-induced dysbiosis mouse model (n = 6 mice per group). The mice in the probiotic treatment group received 200 µL of PMC101 via oral gavage (1 × 109 CFU/mouse) four times a week on days − 3, − 2, − 1, 0, 2, 6, 7, and 9, respectively. The remaining mice’s survival rate, illness severity score, and body weight were evaluated until the end of the experimental period. Additionally, fecal samples were collected from each cage, resuspended in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and homogenized for 60 s. The total aerobic bacterial burden and CRK were then quantified using nutrient agar (consisting of 5.0 g/L peptone, 3.0 g/L beef extract, and 15.0 g/L agar, BD Difco, USA) and MacConkey agar containing imipenem, respectively.

Investigating the efficacy and mechanistic insights of microbiome therapeutic PMC101 in preventing CRK translocation in mice

The prophylactic mechanism of PMC101 in preventing CRK translocation was evaluated using an antibiotic-induced dysbiosis mouse model (n = 12 per group). The mice in the PMC101 treatment group received 200 µL of PMC101 via oral gavage (1 × 109 CFU/mouse) for 6 days starting three days before CRK inoculation. At the end of the experiment, mice from each group were sacrificed, and blood and organ samples, such as the lungs, intestines, and kidneys, were freshly collected. Cytokine analysis was performed using blood samples, and CRK in the lungs and kidneys was quantified by utilizing MacConkey agar containing imipenem. Histopathological and tight junction gene expression analyses were conducted on the intestinal epithelium. Additionally, fecal samples were collected during the experimental period to assess the impact of PMC101 on SCFA production. Moreover, metagenomics analysis was conducted using the collected fecal samples to assess the effect of PMC101 on gut microbiome composition.

Comparative anti-infective activity of Lactobacillus fermentum strains in a murine CRK colonization model

A murine colonization model was established to evaluate the anti-infective efficacy of two different Lactobacillus fermentum strains (L.f 1 and L.f 2) compared to PMC101 in vivo. Mice were randomly divided into infection and treatment groups (n = 6 per group). From day − 3 to day − 1, mice received a daily oral gavage of a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail consisting of metronidazole (150 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), kanamycin (150 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and vancomycin (150 mg/kg) in a total volume of 200 µL per mouse. On day − 3, neutropenia was induced via a single intraperitoneal injection of cyclophosphamide at 450 mg/kg (200 µL per mouse). On day 0, after oral administration of bicarbonate to mice, they were challenged with 200 µL of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella (CRK) suspension containing 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mouse. Prior to CRK infection, mice were treated with 9 × 109 CFU/mL of L. fermentum strains, including PMC101, in 150 mL of drinking water twice a week for 2 weeks. On day 7 post-infection, 5-fluorouracil was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 400 mg/kg (200 µL per mouse). Stool samples were collected during the study to quantify the levels of colonization. For CFU determination, stool samples were serially diluted, and 10 µL of each dilution was plated on MacConkey agar supplemented with 10 mg/mL imipenem. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Bacterial load was calculated and expressed as log10 CFU/mL.

Histopathological assessment

Moreover, to evaluate the efficacy of PMC101 on the intestinal epithelium of the antibiotic-induced dysbiosis mouse model, the intestinal samples from each cage were sliced into three segments corresponding to the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum on day 7. The extracted organs were fixed in neutral buffered formalin, and Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted at the Korea Pathology Technical Center (KPNT, Cheong-si, Korea). Following this, samples were processed for 13 h using the STP120 Spin Tissue Processor (Myr, Spain), and tissue sections were cut into approximately 4 μm slices before being mounted on slides. The slides were then visualized using an Easyscan Pro6 slide scanner (Motic, Hong Kong). Histopathology was scored on H&E-stained ileum sections, and pathological changes and mucosal inflammation were measured.

Cytokine analysis of blood serum

Blood samples (1 mL) were collected from sacrificed mice’s hearts on day 7 using a syringe (Korea Vaccine, Korea). Subsequently, blood samples were transferred to the lab immediately after collection and kept at room temperature for 20 min to facilitate clotting. After clotting, the blood samples were centrifuged at 4000 RPM for 10 min to separate the blood serum. Then, the blood serum samples were transferred to a fresh Eppendorf tube and stored at − 80 °C for further analysis. Before starting the cytokine analysis, frozen serum samples were thawed, and cytokine levels were evaluated using IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ LEGEND MAX Mouse ELISA Kit (BioLegend, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Analysis of tight junction protein expression using western blot

Following previous studies, the integrity of the mice’s intestinal epithelium was evaluated during PMC101 treatment on day 7 using a western blot assay [36–38]. For protein extraction, the intestinal tissue was collected freshly and homogenized in 1× RIPA lysis buffer (Abcam, UK) and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). The mixture was then centrifuged at 14,000 RPM at 4 °C for 15 min. Extracted proteins were then quantified, transferred into polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies, including ZO-1 (Protein Tech, USA) and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Membranes were then visualized using a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, USA), and densitometric measurements of the obtained bands were subsequently conducted with ImageJ software (version 1.8.0).

Assessment of the impact of microbiome therapeutic PMC101 treatment on SCFA levels

The levels of acetic, propionic, butyric, and valeric acids in the gut content of mice were determined using gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC–MS/MS, GCMS-TQ8040, Shimadzu, Japan) according to previous studies [42, 43]. Briefly, the fresh fecal samples were homogenized in 5 mL of PBS-distilled water (1 g/sample) and then centrifuged at 4000 RPM for 5 min. Afterward, 20 mg of gut particulate matter was placed into an Eppendorf tube and then extracted with 30 µL of 0.1 M NaOH and 430 µL of methanol. The mixture was then incubated at − 20 °C for 20 min to facilitate protein precipitation. After centrifugation at 4000 RPM for 10 min at 4 °C, the collected supernatant was dried at 37 °C. Following this, methoxyamine hydrochloride (MeOX, Sigma, USA) was dissolved in pyridine (Samchun, Korea) to a concentration of 20 mg/mL, and the dried mixture was added to this solution prior to the incubation (60 °C for 90 min). Subsequently, 60 µL of N-tert-Butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide (MTBSTFA; Sigma, USA) was added to the mixture, which was then incubated for an additional 30 min at 60 °C. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 RPM for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and mixed with 140 µL of pyridine to prepare the final test solution for detection by GC–MS. The quantification of SCFA was assessed by measuring the peak areas for propionate, acetic, butyric, and valeric acids. The mixture of acetic acid (TCI, Japan), propionic acid (TCI, Japan), butyric acid (TCI, Japan), and valeric acid (Sigma, USA) was prepared at a concentration of 1000 ppm and used under the conditions mentioned above.

Evaluating the effect of microbiome therapeutic PMC101 on the human intestinal microbial ecosystem

To investigate the effect of microbiome therapeutic PMC101 on intestinal eubiosis in greater depth, a human intestinal microbial ecosystem simulator (SHIME, ProDigest, Belgium) was used, inoculated according to the manufacturer’s instructions and a previous study [44]. The system consisted of 10 double-jacked vessels that mimic the in vivo gut microbial ecosystem (stomach, small intestine, ascending, transverse, and descending colon) by controlling environmental conditions such as pH, temperature, inoculum, and residence time [30]. In brief, fresh fecal samples of four healthy children volunteers without a history of antibiotic treatment for 6 months before this study were collected and homogenized with 100 mL of sterilized phosphate buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 7.0) (8.8 g/L K2HPO4, 6.8 g/L KH2PO4, 0.1 g/L sodium thioglycolate, and 15 mg/L sodium thionite) (ProDigest, Ghent, Belgium) for 10 min using a Bag Mixer 400 (Interscience, Louvain-La-Neuve, Belgium). Next, the samples were inoculated into the colon vessel, both control and treatment groups, where the microbial inoculum was stabilized for 2 weeks in a carbohydrate-based medium and allowed to adapt to the specific environmental conditions of the colon vessels. Subsequently, 50 mL of PMC101 culture (1 × 109 CFU/mL) was added to the small intestine vessel on day 15 in one set. Probiotics were not used in another set as a control. Subsequently, samples were collected from the three colon vessels (ascending, transverse, and descending colon vessels) on days 0, 1, 5, and 7. The collected samples were then utilized to quantify the normal flora, beneficial bacteria, and CRK using nutrient agar, MRS agar, and MacConkey agar, respectively. Additionally, the samples were analyzed through metagenomics to assess the effect of PMC101 treatment on gut microbiota composition. The Pediatrics Department of Soonchunhyang University Cheonan Hospital collected fresh human fecal samples. The experimental method was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Pediatrics Department at Soonchunhyang University Cheonan Hospital IRB (No. SCHCA 2019-12-016), and informed consent was obtained from all subjects and their parents.

Metagenomics analysis of the gut microbiome

To explore the impact of PMC101 on gut microbiota composition, metagenomics analysis was conducted using the stool samples collected from the second animal model and human intestinal microbial ecosystem simulator samples. Briefly, genomic DNAs were extracted from these samples using a QIAamp DNA Fast Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA was then quantified with a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) using a dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA). The quality of extracted DNA was also analyzed using a ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad, USA) after subjecting it to 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. Following this, the amplicon PCR was performed to amplify the V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene using standard Illumina 16S primers (forward primer: TCG GCA GCG TCA GAT GTG TAT AAG AGA CAG CCT ACG GGN-GGC WGC AG, reverse primer: GTC TCG TGG GCT CGG AGA TGT GTA TAA GAG ACA GG-ACT ACH VGG GTA TCT AAT CC) following the optimized conditions [45]. Agencourt AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter, UK) beads were used after each PCR step to purify the PCR products, which had been quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit. Next, metagenomic libraries were prepared using a Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA). Later, the samples were normalized, pooled, and mixed with PhiX Control v3 (Illumina, USA) before being sequenced on the iSeq™ 100 platform (Illumina, USA), following the standard Illumina protocol previously used by our team [13, 45]. Finally, the sequencing data were analyzed, and the entire microbial compositions of the samples were exhibited according to a previously described protocol [13]. The Fast Length Adjustment of Short Reads (FLASH) software (version 1.2.11) was used to merge pairs of reads from the initial DNA sections [46]. The Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME) software (version 1.9.1) was employed for sequence analysis [47]. The richness and diversity of the bacterial populations within the samples were assessed using α indices, i.e., ACE and Shannon [48, 49].

Monitoring the antimicrobial properties of microbiome therapeutic PMC101

A time-kill assay was conducted against the CRK pathogen using the cell-free culture supernatant (CFCS) of PMC101, as described in a previous study with some modifications [50]. To serve this purpose, the PMC101 strain was cultured in MRS broth (BD Difco, USA) and FGM broth overnight, and then CFCS was prepared. In this assay, the initial amount of CRK was set at 1 × 108 CFU/mL, and the pH was maintained at 6.0 ± 0.01. Following this, 300 μL of CRK suspension was added to 15 mL of CFCS derived from MRS broth and FGM, respectively, and incubated at 37 °C. Control groups included CRK pathogen suspended in MRS broth and FGM without CFCS inoculum. At predefined intervals, aliquots were withdrawn from each tube, serially diluted, and plated onto MacConkey agar to determine the viable CFU of CRK. The assay was executed in triplicate, and the mean log CFU/mL of total surviving cells was recorded. Additionally, the pH level of each aliquot’s interval was measured during the experiment using an electronic pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Korea).

Safety evaluation of microbiome therapeutic PMC101

To assess the safety properties of PMC101, hemolytic activity and antimicrobial susceptibility (E-test) were evaluated using a previously established protocol based on data from the official websites of the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) [51–53]. Briefly, the hemolytic activity of PMC101 was evaluated on blood agar plates (KisanBio, South Korea) under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h. Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus KCTC 5033 (Korean Collection for Type Culture, Daejeon, South Korea) and S. aureus (NCCP 14780) (National Culture Collection for Pathogens, Cheongju, South Korea) served as negative and positive controls [54]. Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using the E-test, following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI), and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were compared to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) standards [55]. All experiments adhered to EFSA safety assessment guidelines.

Results

Whole-genome analysis results of the microbiome therapeutic strain PMC101

The primary genomic characteristics of the PMC101 strain are illustrated in Fig. 1. PMC101 had only one circular chromosome, with an average GC content of 51.6% and a length of 2,086,671 bp, as generated by CLC Genomics (Fig. 1A). The genome contains 2075 CDSs and 1934 predicted proteins organized by COG functional classifications (Fig. 1B). Biological functions were identified for 1450 proteins. In comparison, 528 CDSs corresponded to conserved proteins of unknown function, and the remaining 141 (6.8%) were hypothetical proteins with no matches in existing databases. Additionally, 15 rRNA genes and 58 tRNA genes were predicted. The complete genome sequence of PMC101 is available in the NCBI database under the following accession number: PRJNA860760.

The circular map also shows tRNA and rRNA, respectively, indicated by red and blue. Furthermore, the OrthoANI approach was used to conduct a similarity analysis for strains with substantial similarities in 16S rRNA analyses, employing the whole-genome sequencing data of PMC101 (Fig. 1C). These similarities were confirmed by comparing PMC101 to all publicly available Lacticaseibacillus fermentum genomes. For instance, PMC101 exhibited 99.29%, 99.22%, 99.02%, and 99.09% similarity with strains 47-7, B44, ACA, and GR1103, respectively, which were significantly above the cutoff value of 95% for species delineation [56]. In contrast, comparisons with other Lactobacillus species, such as L. panis MT-01, L. reuteri SD-RD830, and L. gastricus LG045, showed markedly lower similarities of 70.36%, 68.37%, and 68.64%, respectively. These results strongly suggest that the newly discovered strain PMC101 is L. fermentum. Moreover, to investigate whether the isolated strain is newly discovered, the genomic information of PMC101 was compared to that of other L. fermentum strains, such as GR1008, GR1009, GR1007, GR1103, and B44 (Fig. 1D). Although they belong to the same species, their genome size, GC content, and CDS (coding sequence) composition were distinct. These findings identified PMC101 as a novel strain with unique genetic features that set it apart from previously identified strains.

Optimization of food-grade medium for the lab-scale production of PMC101

The growth conditions of PMC101 were optimized using various FGMs (Fig. 2). Figure 2A shows the composition of 7 FGMs modified based on the MRS medium. However, after inoculating the bacterial strain and incubating for 18 h, the growth was checked by measuring the optical density (OD), which was recorded as 0.37, 0.87, 1.08, 0.39, 0.94, 0.91, and 0.64 for FGM1 through FGM7, respectively (Fig. 2B). Based on the growth pattern, PMC101 demonstrated the highest growth rate in FGM3, followed by FGM2, FGM5, FGM6, and FGM7. In contrast, substantially lower growth was observed in FGM1 and FGM4. This result indicates that PMC101 can effectively utilize the components of FGM2, FGM3, FGM5, FGM6, and FGM7 for proliferation, supporting their suitability as a growth medium formulation.

B Following this, the growth rate was assessed based on optical density (OD) values, where the highest growth rate was observed in FGM3, followed by FGM2, FGM5, FGM6, and FGM7. In contrast, substantially lower growth rates were observed in FGM1 and FGM4. At the same time, the pH levels of the bacterial culture were also checked (Fig. 2C). The pH values recorded for FGM1, FGM2, FGM3, FGM4, FGM5, FGM6, and FGM7 were 4.7, 3.9, 4.6, 5.8, 4.1, 4.2, and 4.0, respectively. FGM4 exhibits the highest pH level, whereas all other FGMs have a pH below 5. Among these, the lowest pH level was determined in the FGM2. Based on these observations, FGM2 was selected as the most effective medium for culturing PMC101, which exhibits high bacterial growth while maintaining the lowest pH level required for the proper function of the probiotic strain.

Evaluation of the impact of PMC101 on gut microbiota based on metagenomic analysis

The impact of PMC101 on gut microbiota during CRE infection and treatment was evaluated using metagenomic analysis (Fig. 5). The richness of the bacterial community was explored utilizing ACE metrics to estimate the α-diversity of the gut microbiota (Fig. 5A, B). The results showed that the richness of the bacterial community was significantly increased in the PMC101-treated group compared to the CRK group on day 2 (p < 0.05) and day 7 (p < 0.05). Notably, by day 7, the degree of microbial richness in the PMC101-treated group was higher than on day 2, indicating the ability of microbiome therapeutics to restore the gut microbiota balance gradually over time. Additionally, the species diversity was investigated employing the Shannon metrics. The data revealed a significant enhancement of species diversity in the PMC101-treated samples compared to the CRK samples on day 2. No statistically significant difference was found between the groups when comparing them on day 7. The taxonomic compositions of the two groups were analyzed (Fig. 5C). The data revealed that the Proteobacteria increased, while the Bacteroidetes decreased, due to CRK infection. However, the group treated with PMC101 showed a lesser degree of these changes compared to the untreated group. By day 7 post-infection, the untreated group maintained these taxonomic shifts, whereas the PMC101-treated group exhibited a reversal to a normal range. Specifically, the previously increased Proteobacteria levels decreased, and the reduced Bacteroidetes levels increased significantly. This suggests that the Bacteroidetes-to-Proteobacteria ratio is crucial during CRK infection and treatment. In conjunction with translocation to the lungs and kidneys observed in CRK infection animal models, this ratio was newly defined as the CRK translocation-microbiome index (CRK-TMI) (Fig. 5D). Mice with CRK translocation to the lungs and kidneys exhibited a low CRK-TMI. In contrast, those treated with PMC101 and showing improved conditions had an increased CRK-TMI. These findings highlight that PMC101 is pivotal in reducing gut microbiota dysbiosis, preventing systemic CRK translocation from the gut, and maintaining a healthy gut microbiome.

Assessing the antimicrobial properties of microbiome therapeutic PMC101

The antimicrobial efficacy of PMC101 against CRK infection was evaluated using a time-kill assay (Fig. 7). The findings revealed a gradual reduction in CRK burden after 5 min of treatment with FGM-derived PMC101 cell-free culture supernatant (CFCS). The reduction was more pronounced after 30 min (p < 0.001), 40 min (p < 0.001), 50 min (p < 0.001), 60 min (p < 0.001), 70 min (p < 0.001), 80 min (p < 0.001), and 90 min (p < 0.001) of treatment compared to the FGM control group. A gradual reduction in CRK was also observed after 15 min of treatment with MRS-derived PMC101 CFCS, with a significant decrease at 80 min (p < 0.001) and 90 min (p < 0.001) of treatment. Simultaneously, pH changes were also measured over time. Data showed a significant reduction in pH while treated with FGM-derived PMC101 CFCS or MRS-derived PMC101 CFCS from 0 to 90 min (p < 0.001). The initial pH was 6.0 ± 0.01, and it was reduced to below 4 after the treatment. However, the reduction in the FGM medium was lower than in the MRS medium. These findings suggest that the ability of PMC101 to combat CRK infection may be linked to its antibacterial activity, which is driven in part by the acidification of the gut environment during treatment.

Determining the safety properties of microbiome therapeutic PMC101

The safety profile of PMC101 was evaluated through hemolytic activity and antimicrobial susceptibility (E-test) assays (Fig. 8). In the hemolysis assay using blood agar plates, PMC101 demonstrated no clear zone surrounding the colonies, indicating the absence of hemolytic activity, consistent with the negative control L. rhamnosus GG (Fig. 8A). In contrast, the positive control, S. aureus, exhibited a distinct clear zone, confirming hemolytic activity. Furthermore, in antibiotic susceptibility testing, PMC101 was susceptible to the most commonly used antibiotics, including ampicillin, erythromycin, clindamycin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol (Fig. 8B). These findings confirm the safety profile of PMC101, supporting its potential development as an effective microbiome therapeutic.

Effects of different L. fermentum on intestinal colonization in a murine model

The efficacy of different L. fermentum strains against CRK pathogens were evaluated using an in vivo model (Fig. S2). The bacterial loads in fecal samples were measured on days 1, 7, and 14 post-infection. Among the tested strains, PMC101 demonstrated the most pronounced inhibitory effect. During the study, mice treated with PMC101 showed a reduction of approximately 4.5 log10 CFU/mL compared to the infection group on day 14 (p < 0.001). Other L. fermentum strains also showed reductions in CRK colonization, particularly on days 7 and 14. However, their effects were less consistent and not statistically significant. In contrast, the control group maintained high and relatively stable CRK counts throughout the experiment. These findings indicate that the preventive administration of strain PMC101 can effectively reduce intestinal colonization by CRK compared to other strains in a murine model.

Discussion

CRK is considered one of the most serious drug-resistant bacteria in the ongoing antibiotic-resistance pandemic, as it confers resistance to all currently available antibiotics [57–60]. Therefore, developing a new generation of effective therapeutic options is urgently needed to combat the spread of this deadly pathogen. In our previous study, the probiotic PMC101 strain demonstrated significant efficacy against CRK infection in an in vivo model with no notable side effects [39]. Based on this study, the current research builds upon previous findings, expanding beyond the focus on gut infections to address the prevention of systemic translocation. A key focus is placed on exploring the mechanisms of action from a microbiome perspective. Moreover, this research advances the scientific understanding and lays the groundwork for developing a microbiome therapeutic.

The strain PMC101 was identified as L. fermentum based on a 95% similarity threshold in whole-genome sequencing [61, 62]. Additionally, this strain represents distinct genomic characteristics compared to other L. fermentum strains, positioning it as a novel strain. Accordingly, master and working cell banks (MCB and WCB) were established for the strain to ensure the integrity of the experiments [63]. Moreover, the growth conditions of PMC101 were optimized using a laboratory-scale fermenter with various food-grade mediums. A food-grade medium is a culture medium composed of safe ingredients suitable for human consumption [64]. Considering future drug development, food-grade media with scalable cultivation conditions were utilized in this study. However, ultimately, one medium was selected that showed the optimal growth of PMC101 while maintaining a minimal pH level. A series of detailed analyses was then performed to investigate its effects.

The efficacy of PMC101 against CRK was evaluated using a mouse model with cyclophosphamide and vancomycin. Cyclophosphamide is a potent immunosuppressive agent used in animal models to enhance disease induction [65]. This agent depletes the immune cells by interfering with their replication and function, making the animals susceptible to infection [66]. At the same time, the antibiotic vancomycin was administered to the animals to increase their susceptibility to CRE, thereby enhancing the severity of the disease. Research indicates that vancomycin treatment has adverse effects on the host, altering the normal microbiota composition and rendering the host highly susceptible to intestinal colonization by antibiotic-resistant pathogens [67]. However, the study findings show that the group left untreated had the highest mortality rate. In contrast, the microbiome therapeutics-treated group survived until and beyond the end of the experimental period. An increased illness severity score and remarkable weight loss were also observed in the CRE group, whereas these conditions improved in the treated group. This is particularly concerning as CRK resists nearly all available beta-lactam antibiotics, enabling them to survive and thrive within the host, making the infection difficult to treat. However, recent studies demonstrate the potential of microbiome therapeutics to mitigate the severity of CRE infections, aligning with our current findings [68]. Additionally, the decreased level of normal flora in the nontreated group indicates that CRE caused their disturbance, whereas the treated group maintained the baseline level throughout the experiment. The study also reveals a decreased level of CRK in the microbiome therapeutic group. Recent evidence suggests that probiotics can regulate intestinal flora and inhibit the colonization of pathogenic bacteria, findings that are consistent with our own [69]. A similar study, conducted by Sassone-Corsi et al. [70], showed that microbiome therapeutics could protect mice from Enterobacteriaceae infections, revealing the potential of this novel approach for infection management.

Microbiome therapeutics PMC101 also demonstrated its ability to prevent the translocation of CRK from the gut to other organs. This finding is supported by the heightened expression of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) protein in the PMC101-treated group. ZO-1 is the first tight junction-associated protein that interacts with occludin and with several other tight junction-associated proteins, including ZO-2, ZO-3, β-catenin, paxillin, talin, and the perijunctional actin ring, playing a crucial role in maintaining barrier function [71]. The potential of PMC101 to prevent CRK translocation was further demonstrated by histopathological analysis results, which showed severe mucosal damage, villus atrophy, and extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in the CRK group. In contrast, these conditions were remarkably improved in the treated group. These findings align with previous studies that showed the prophylactic effect of probiotics in regulating mucosal tissue homeostasis and protecting against infectious and inflammatory diseases [72]. Moreover, PMC101 demonstrates its potential to activate the immune system by increasing cytokine production, including IFN-γ and IL-10. IFN-γ is a cytokine that enhances the activation of macrophages, neutrophils, and other immune cells, which are crucial for killing pathogens [73]. IL-10 is a key anti-inflammatory cytokine that modulates immune responses and prevents excessive inflammation, thus minimizing tissue damage [74]. PMC101 treatment significantly enhanced the production of SCFAs. SCFAs are a subset of fatty acids now considered potential health and/or disease biomarkers [75]. They are primarily found in the proximal colon of the gut and can be transported across the gut epithelium, thus playing a crucial role in maintaining gut integrity and homeostasis [76]. They can modulate chemotaxis and phagocytosis, induce reactive oxygen species (ROS), change cell proliferation and function, and possess antimicrobial effects [76]. A study by Ye et al. [77], shows a close association between SCFAs and the establishment of gut eubiosis in a T2D mouse model. Similarly, another recent study using a TB infection mouse model revealed a higher production of butyric acid in the microbiome therapeutic group compared to the infection group [78].

Microbiome therapeutics PMC101 treatment improves dysbiosis conditions, as evidenced by a significant increase in microbial richness and diversity in the treated group compared to the infected group. Additionally, the Bacteroidetes population was found to increase remarkably, whereas the Proteobacteria population decreased in the PMC101-treated group. The opposite trend was observed in the CRK group. Bacteroidetes are the largest group of Gram-negative bacteria in the gut microbiome, playing crucial roles in functions such as metabolism, immune modulation, and regulation of the gut-brain axis. They are considered the key players in a healthy state [79]. Conversely, Proteobacteria have been linked to inflammation and dysbiosis [80]. Mitigating dysbiosis is a key strategy for preventing pathogenic colonization, particularly with Klebsiella [81–83]. Based on this, a novel concept, the CRK-translocation microbiome index (CRK-TMI), was introduced, considering the importance of the Bacteroidetes-to-Proteobacteria ratio in relation to CRK infection and treatment. Data show a higher degree of translocation of CRK from the gut to the lungs and kidneys in the CRK group. In contrast, the translocation level of microbiome therapeutic groups was significantly low. Linear regression analysis further demonstrates that PMC101 increases CRK-TMI, indicating its ability to minimize the translocation of this pathogen. Research indicates that CRK can translocate from the gastrointestinal tract to other host organs, leading to systemic infections and thereby complicating the treatment of the disease [84]. However, our findings are consistent with previous results, showing the effectiveness of microbiome therapeutics in reducing pathogenic colonization and translocation [85, 86]. Furthermore, a human intestinal microbial ecosystem simulator was conducted to strengthen our findings regarding the efficacy of PMC101 in reducing the CRK burden and improving dysbiosis conditions. The multicompartment dynamic simulator has emerged as a key in vitro model for studying interactions between gut microbiota [87]. The data revealed that PMC101 did not disturb the normal gut microbiome flora but reduced CRK burden, which aligns with our in vivo findings. Moreover, the metagenomics findings support the animal model’s data in which an enhanced population of Bacteroidetes was noticed in the PMC101-treated group, whereas Proteobacteria was found to be decreased. These observations aligned with recent studies and further confirmed the ability of PMC101 to maintain microbial diversity toward eubiosis conditions [88, 89].

When synthesizing these data, it is suggested that PMC101 enhances CRK-TMI, suppressing CRK translocation, alleviating infection in affected animals, and improving survival rates. Furthermore, as shown in time-kill assays, our findings, consistent with previous studies, demonstrate that PMC101 exhibits superior antimicrobial properties against CRK, including its efficacy against multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae [90]. Based on its excellent efficacy against CRK infections, PMC101 was evaluated for its safety profile to assess its potential as a prophylactic candidate for developing a novel microbiome therapeutic. Following EFSA guidelines, PMC101 was shown to be susceptible to antibiotics, enhancing its safety for clinical application and minimizing the risk of promoting antibiotic resistance. Additionally, this strain was identified as non-hemolytic, a critical safety indicator that mitigates the risk of adverse effects on red blood cells, providing further assurance of its preliminary safe use in human therapy [91].

Microbiome therapeutic development has become a significant research focus, aiming to limit the damage caused by antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and promote overall health [92]. With the increasing focus on microbiome therapeutics as a next-generation approach for maintaining gut health, recent FDA approvals of innovative microbiome-based treatments like VOWST (oral-fecal microbiota spores product (capsule), formerly known as SER-109) and REBYOTA (rectally administered fecal microbiota suspension, formerly known as RBX2660) used for recurrent Clostridium difficile infections underscore the potential of these therapies in personalized medicine. These advancements present promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics, targeting dysbiosis and related pathogenic infections [93, 94]. Although research into microbiome-based therapies for the prevention and treatment of CRK infections is still in its early stages, the prophylactic potential of PMC101 is evident. PMC101 exhibits direct inhibitory effects on CRK and its translocation, promoting gut health by strengthening gut microbiome balance. Altogether, these findings suggest that PMC101 holds great potential as a promising candidate for further development.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study emphasizes the potential of PMC101 as a novel microbiome therapeutic for restoring gut eubiosis and mitigating the adverse effects of antibiotic-induced dysbiosis, particularly in combating CRE infections. The graphical abstract highlights the dual role of PMC101 in reducing microbial disruption and promoting microbial restoration, effectively lowering infection severity and supporting gut health. These findings align with FDA guidelines for microbiome therapeutics, establishing PMC101 as a promising and safe microbiome therapeutic agent for managing multidrug-resistant infections. However, further studies, including clinical trials and pharmaceutical-grade research, are essential to optimize dosage and ensure safety. Additionally, while our findings highlight the enhanced prophylactic potential of PMC101, we acknowledge the need for further evaluation of its therapeutic efficacy, as well as the necessity of conducting comprehensive comparative analyses of PMC101 with other strains. Despite these limitations, our findings can serve as primary data for clinical applications and provide a solid foundation for developing PMC101 as a next-generation microbiome therapeutic to address the global challenge of antibiotic resistance.
