Assessing the Costs of Intravenous Push Waste in Intraoperative Areas Through Observation: A Multi-site Study
Authors: John B. Hertig, Les Louden, Blake Shay, Armando Soto, Garrett Robbins, Tatyana Kornilova, Prachi Arora
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe costs associated with proper disposal, management, and regulatory compliance of controlled substances in healthcare systems are substantial. In the context of the current opioid crisis, and given the high abuse potential of controlled substances, it is imperative that waste is minimized and waste procedures are followed to ensure safe disposal of controlled substances. This study aims to quantify the costs associated with fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam, and ketamine waste in intraoperative areas through a multi-site observational analysis.MethodsThe study used an observational design across various hospital procedural and post-procedural units in the Southwest Florida region of the United States. Automated and non-automated workflows for wasting controlled substances were compared. As with a previous study conducted by Hertig et al., waste was evaluated as (1) the quantity (mg/μg) of medication disposed defined as ‘pharmaceutical waste’ or ‘product waste’ (PW); and (2) workforce time associated with the waste disposal process defined as ‘workforce time waste’ (WTW). Secondary measures include workforce costs associated with the waste disposal process. The product waste analysis was conducted between October and December 2023. The workforce time waste analysis was examined over a 10-day period in January and February 2024. A yearly extrapolation model was applied to cost data.ResultsThe findings revealed substantial costs linked to both PW and WTW, emphasizing the financial burden of controlled substance waste. Study data validated previous literature describing the extent of fentanyl, hydromorphone, and morphine waste while documenting significant amounts of midazolam and ketamine waste. The combined annual waste cost for the two study hospitals was estimated at US$56,557, with workforce time accounting for 36%–50% of this total cost.ConclusionThis study provides vital insights into the financial and operational impact of medication waste in procedural and post-procedural areas, supporting ongoing efforts to minimize waste, ensuring the safe and effective use of controlled substances. Future research should explore the impact of medication waste across diverse healthcare settings and the cost implications associated with pharmacy professionals in the waste compliance process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40801-024-00456-9.

Introduction

Background

Injectable medications are widely used across many health systems and are common root causes for medication errors. About one-third to one-half of such medication errors that occur with intravenous medications are preparation and administration errors. These errors lead to more than one million hospitalizations annually, adding around US$2.7 billion to US$5.1 billion of medical costs to the United States healthcare system [1, 2]. In addition to preparation and administration errors, controlled substances are uniquely associated with additional risks. Proper disposal is an essential best practice, as controlled substances including fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam, and ketamine are associated with particularly high abuse and diversion risk. Current federal statute dictates the appropriate disposal of controlled substance medications must occur immediately with documentation and be witnessed by two licensed healthcare professionals [3]. Meanwhile, health system policies and procedures for handling and disposing of controlled substances vary. The Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) estimates that prescription drug diversion in the United States is a US$25 billion-a-year industry [4]. Depending on the patient care unit, the quantity and variety of controlled substances administered can create a compliance and practice burden on healthcare professionals. Policies requiring thorough documentation, checks and balances, and audits necessitate an institutional investment of time and resources.

There are significant costs associated with proper controlled substance disposal, management, and regulatory compliance. In the context of the current opioid crisis, and given the high abuse potential of controlled substances, it is imperative that waste is minimized, and waste procedures are followed to ensure safe disposal of controlled substances. Previous research has been conducted in operating rooms demonstrating significant costs associated with both product waste and the workforce time associated with compliance [5–7]. Previous research has also examined health system costs associated with pharmaceutical or product waste (PW) and workforce time waste (WTW) resulting from compliance with controlled substance disposal and unoptimized controlled substance dosing (i.e., not matching product size with practice needs) [8]. This prior study specifically focused on the intravenous push (IVP) administration of controlled substances such as fentanyl, hydromorphone, and morphine. The study employed a time motion study design to gather data on waste amounts and the time associated with waste disposal processes in two inpatient general medicine hospital sites. Results highlighted significant costs associated with the disposal of controlled substances in terms of both wasted medication and the time healthcare professionals spend on waste compliance (average of US$2.40 wasted per dose administered). Additional recent studies have also explored the safety of IVP administration, examining both ready to administer (RTA) IVP medications and traditional vial and syringe, as well as patient outcomes when administering the lowest necessary dose of controlled substances in procedural areas. These data suggest RTA is safer and more cost effective than traditional vial and syringe, with an estimated savings of US$182.61 per administration, while using lower doses of opioids is associated with fewer National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention (NCCMERP) Category ‘E’ events (temporary harm requiring intervention) in patients. This large retrospective study included 170,607 patients, emphasizing the importance of matching product with practice not only for waste reduction, but also decreasing patient harm [9–11]. Furthermore, sustainability measures were examined in a study that evaluated the difference in waste between aseptically batched RTA syringes prepared by the hospital versus commercially prepared RTA syringes of the equivalent medication. From this study, it was concluded that commercial RTA syringes improved sustainability efforts through reduction in overall drug waste and workforce time waste [12]. While this study did not utilize controlled substances, the takeaways can be extrapolated to the stricter and time-consuming handling and disposal of controlled substances in health systems. In a healthcare system that is frequently impacted by quality and production-induced drug shortages, it is imperative that optimization of drug use in health systems is improved.

Given this growing body of peer-reviewed evidence, there remains a gap in understanding the extent to which medications other than fentanyl, hydromorphone, and morphine are also wasted. Further, previous studies have either been extrapolated models, or were conducted in general medicine units in hospitals and health systems. A prospective, multicenter study in Italy focused specifically on observing this phenomenon specifically in intensive care unit (ICU), emergency area, and operating room (OR) settings. The study observed that the estimated yearly waste amounted to 139,531 syringes, for a total estimated financial cost of €78,060 (US$92,569) [5]. Limited data exist for controlled substance waste in operating rooms, post-procedural areas, and in women’s and pediatric settings. Given the high-volume use of controlled substances in these areas, coupled with the increasing use of medicines like ketamine, research is needed to support a holistic understanding of various costs associated with use of these medications. Importantly, this information can help health systems make more informed decisions around safe medication use, diversion prevention, and the expanded adoption of waste minimization practices in these various healthcare settings.

Purpose

The purpose of this study was to perform a waste assessment and workforce cost analysis associated with intravenous (IV) opioid products in procedural and post-procedural areas (e.g., operating room, same-day surgery) in adult, women’s, and pediatric settings. The study was conducted in a major health system in Southwest Florida and included four separate hospitals—St. Joseph’s Hospital (SJH), St. Joseph’s Women’s (SJWH) & Children’s Hospital (SJCH), and Morton Plant Hospital (MPH).1 Given the expanding use and high abuse potential of controlled substances, it is imperative product waste is minimized and waste procedures are followed to ensure safe disposal [13]. Research is needed to better understand the financial and workforce impacts of controlled substance medication waste in expanding healthcare settings [14].

Primary Objective

The primary aim of this study is to quantify the total waste associated with administering IVP controlled substances (fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam, and ketamine). Two categories of waste will be evaluated: (1) the quantity (mg/μg) of medication disposed, defined as ‘pharmaceutical waste’ or ‘product waste’ (PW); and (2) workforce time associated with the waste disposal process, defined as ‘workforce time waste’ (WTW). Secondary measures include workforce costs associated with the waste disposal process using mean Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) salary data for those health professions involved in respective waste procedures.

Methods

Study Design

This observational study was conducted in varying hospital procedural units, with differing mechanisms for tracking controlled substance waste, with a focus on fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam, and ketamine. The product waste analysis was performed for each site in October, November, and December 2023. Workforce time waste observations were collected over 10 nonconsecutive days during January and February 2024. The specific procedural care areas at SJH/SJCH included 18 main operating rooms, two cardiovascular surgery operating rooms, and six endoscopy rooms, while SJWH consisted of 12 main labor and delivery (L&D) operating rooms. MPH procedural areas consisted of 20 main operating rooms, six endoscopy rooms, five cardiac operating rooms, three labor and delivery operating rooms. The PACU (post-anesthesia care unit) at MPH was also assessed to best evaluate controlled substance waste management within an automated workflow model.

The WTW was evaluated by comparing hospitals utilizing an automated workflow (MPH) with those using a non-automated (traditional) workflow (SJH) model. The practice of using automated dispensing technology includes time waste practices since anesthesia personnel are required to log into automated dispensing cabinets (ADCs) as well as pulling required medications and documenting any necessary waste. In comparison, in a non-automated process, time waste practice involves both pharmacy and anesthesia personnel. Since automated dispensing processes are not available, anesthesia personnel are provided controlled substance kits and use controlled substance reconciliation sheets (see Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]) to document medications used and wasted. Additionally, pharmacy personnel are responsible for reviewing returned controlled substance kits along with reconciliation of any waste and reporting of discrepancies.

For automated workflow at MPH, time waste observations were collected from ADC data. The data was analyzed to review time waste for anesthesia personnel related to the amount of total time involved removing controlled substance(s) from ADCs until waste was documented or reconciled within the ADC. Additionally, the PACU was evaluated to best correlate to actual ‘waste’ times as this practice area tends to follow traditional methods of pulling and wasting medications compared with other procedural areas where controlled substances are pulled at the beginning of a case and wasting occurs at case end.

Non-automated workflow (SJH) time waste was assessed for both pharmacy and anesthesia personnel. For pharmacy, the time it takes a pharmacy technician to reconcile product waste was documented on an ‘Anesthesia Controlled Drug Record’ (ESM). For anesthesia, We reviewed how long it took for anesthesia providers to document product waste (see ‘Anesthesia Controlled Drug Record’—ESM). While these models draw comparisons, they were analyzed independently as different documentation and reconciliation methods are utilized. Statistical Analysis

Consistent with previous related studies, data measures such as number of assessments, product waste, workforce waste, etc. will be reported for each drug (fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam, and ketamine) separately as well as grouped data. For continuous variables, mean and total will be calculated. For categorical variables, frequency tables, including ‘n’ and percentages will be calculated. Wherever appropriate, standard deviation, median (min and max), and 95% confidence intervals may also be calculated.

In the absence of validated national estimates or substantive research in the literature, opioid waste estimates reported by Hertig et al. and Rothenburg et al. [10, 15] were considered to best represent the targeted population and were used to calculate the sample size for this study. The past study by Hertig et al. reported opioid waste associated with administering a 100-μg vial of fentanyl and a 1-mg vial of hydromorphone. On average, 48% of fentanyl doses and 41% of hydromorphone doses were reported as being wasted, with limited waste associated with morphine. The past study by Rothenburg et al. [2] reported the probability of opioid waste including morphine in a specific scenario of waste optimization as 23%. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no past study estimating the prevalence of ketamine and midazolam waste in the United States. Based on the past studies, a minimum of 2164 instances of fentanyl waste, 1206 instances of hydromorphone waste, and 273 instances of morphine waste were required to be reported in both the settings (total) to attain a power of 80% at the significance level of 5% for this study.

Primary Objectives

Pharmaceutical Waste Analysis

Fentanyl

Two vial sizes were assessed for fentanyl: 100 μg/2 mL and 250 μg/5 mL. In the SJH non-automated practice settings, the cumulative fentanyl waste was 93,315.5 μg. The cumulative waste in the MPH automated practice setting was similar at 90,803 μg. The average waste of the fentanyl 100 μg/2-mL vial was 65.10 μg in the nonautomated practice setting and 59.22 μg in the automated practice setting. The average waste of the fentanyl 250 μg/5-mL vial was 125.68 μg in the nonautomated practice setting and 124.68 μg in the automated practice setting. Based on the number of transactions for fentanyl 100 μg/2 mL (1250 and 1367, respectively, in the automated and non-automated settings), this formulation had the largest total waste of any studied drug and formulation.

Ketamine

Two sizes were assessed for ketamine: 500 mg/10-mL vial and 50 mg/5-mL syringe. In the SJH non-automated practice settings, the cumulative ketamine waste was 20,257 mg. The cumulative waste in the MPH automated practice setting was less at 13,015 mg. The average waste of the ketamine 500 mg/10-mL vial was 458.14 mg in the nonautomated practice setting and 414.81 mg in the automated practice setting. The average waste of the ketamine 50 mg/5-mL syringe was 28.79 mg in the nonautomated practice setting and 24.86 mg in the automated practice setting.

Hydromorphone

One vial size was assessed for hydromorphone: 2 mg/1 mL. In the SJH non-automated practice settings, the cumulative hydromorphone waste was 457.03 mg. The cumulative waste in the MPH automated practice setting was similar at 456.95 mg. The average waste of hydromorphone 2 mg/1 mL is 1.19 mg in the nonautomated practice setting and 1.17 mg in the automated practice setting.

Midazolam

Two vial sizes were assessed for midazolam: 5 mg/5 mL and 2 mg/2 mL. In the SJH non-automated practice settings, the cumulative midazolam waste was 267.7 mg. The cumulative waste in the MPH automated practice setting was higher at 808.75 mg. The average waste of midazolam 5 mg/5 mL is 3.27 mg in the nonautomated practice setting and 2.08 mg in the automated practice setting. The average waste of midazolam 2 mg/2 mL is 1.23 mg in the nonautomated practice setting and 1.06 mg in the automated practice setting.

Morphine

Two vial sizes were assessed for morphine: 4 mg/1 mL and 5 mg/10 mL (preservative-free). In the SJH non-automated practice settings, the cumulative morphine waste was 1,419.73 mg. The average waste of morphine 4 mg/1 mL is 2.05 mg in the nonautomated practice setting. The average waste of morphine 5 mg/10 mL is 3.94 mg in the nonautomated practice setting. The two assessed vial sizes were not utilized in the automated practice setting.

Workforce Time Waste Analysis

The automated and non-automated compliance processes involving the workforce is detailed in Fig. 1. The average workforce time associated with waste compliance (WTW) observed in this study is outlined in Table 3. For the MPH facility, the average time to document and comply with controlled substance waste (automated) was 23.84 s (95% confidence interval: 21.64, 26.04). For the SJH facilities including the main, women’s and children’s hospitals, the average workforce time per each PW waste (non-automated) was 65.58 s (95% confidence interval: 59.45, 71.71). Using a t-test comparing means of the two populations (Automated vs. Non-automated) the p-value was <0.0001 indicating a statistically significant difference between automated and non-automated compliance processes. Based on health system data, the primary individual involved in the waste process was a certified registered nurse anesthetist (CRNA) at 56% of documented waste transactions. The remaining individuals involved in the wasting of controlled substances (as primary user or witness) were registered nurses (RNs). For the purposes of reporting results and data analysis, the time for anesthesia personnel to waste controlled substances using the automated anesthesia stations was not included in the final analysis and discussion. The average of 116.6 minutes for this specific area is not reflective of time spent wasting, but instead is indicative of the total time for a procedure or case. Therefore, this data set was determined to be out of scope for the purposes of the study objectives. Similarly, time spent by the pharmacy department (8.47 s) as part of the non-automated process at SJ hospitals was collected, but deemed out of scope and was not included in the data analysis. Secondary Objective

Cost Analysis

The combined cost analysis included relevant waste costs, including both PW and WTW. To calculate the WTW costs, median salary data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics was used for RNs, CRNAs, and anesthesiologists [17]. A weighted average for RNs and CRNAs was calculated from the by the percentages reported (e.g., 56% of observations were CRNAs and 44% were RNs). As demonstrated in Table 4, the total waste cost, including both product and workforce time, was approximately US$13,945.43 with PW over 90 days (October to December 2023) and 10 days for WTW (January to February 2024) across all four hospitals. At MPH, over 36% of the total cost of waste was associated with workforce time (WTW), while at SJH 50% of the total waste cost was associated with WTW. When a yearly extrapolation model was applied (Table 5), the total combined waste cost for product and workforce time at MPH was estimated at US$15,612, and at SJH it was US$40,945. Combining total costs of waste at both hospitals using the extrapolation model results in US$56,557 worth of fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam and ketamine waste in the health system areas studied. Discussion

The findings of this study highlight significant financial and operational burdens associated with the administration and disposal of intravenous push (IVP) controlled substances in various hospital procedural and post-procedural areas. Consistent with previous studies conducted in other hospital care areas, our results underscore the considerable extent of pharmaceutical waste (PW) and workforce time waste (WTW), emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to optimize controlled substance use and disposal practices. This research validates previous peer-reviewed evidence of fentanyl, hydromorphone and morphine waste, while revealing additional novel data concerning the extent of midazolam and ketamine waste in US-based health systems [8]. Importantly, the time spent by our valuable healthcare workforce continues to be a significant factor and cost in the waste process, with between 36% and 50% of the total cost of waste associated with the use of workforce to comply with wasting processes and procedures. In total, when accounting for both costs associated with the product and the use of workforce time, the cost of waste remains significant with US$56,557 worth of fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam and ketamine waste per year in the health system units studied.

Notably, these data are likely a conservative estimate considering the salary calculation associated with workforce time waste only includes CRNAs/RNs and not anesthesiologists. Anesthesiologists are likely to be involved in the waste process depending on institutional staffing/workflow processes and therefore would create higher overall workforce time waste costs. Further, the time waste calculations measure only the time taken for healthcare staff to travel to document or witness waste, but do not include any time taken to return to previous tasks. Additionally, product waste costs studied are only associated within intraoperative settings. While these products have high use in intraoperative areas, they are also highly utilized in other hospital units (critical care, emergency department, etc.), leading to considerable total product and workforce time waste costs for hospitals. The total cost of product and workforce waste is likely even higher than the data in this study suggest, stressing these wasting practices are non-value added for hospital workers and only introduce safety, diversion, and regulatory concerns for hospitals.

Waste Analysis

Pharmaceutical Waste (PW)

The study revealed substantial pharmaceutical waste across the investigated medications. For instance, at Morton Plant Hospital (MPH), fentanyl and midazolam exhibited notably high waste percentages. Specifically, 50 μg of the fentanyl 100 μg/2-mL vial was wasted 52.15% of the time, and 1 mg of the midazolam 2 mg/2-mL injection was wasted in 81.75% of administrations. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating significant waste associated with controlled substances, although our study extends these observations to include ketamine and midazolam, which had not been extensively studied in this context [8]. Further, study results indicate that the average waste for fentanyl and ketamine was substantial, with cumulative waste figures at both automated (MPH) and non-automated (SJH) practice settings being alarmingly high. The similar cumulative waste figures between these settings suggest that both automated and non-automated processes are currently insufficient in effectively minimizing medication waste. As such, this research builds upon the prior evidence base, suggesting urgent action is needed to improve protocols and technologies aimed at matching product availability with clinical practice, thereby reducing unnecessary waste in our hospitals and health systems. Specifically, the excessive wasting of over half of ketamine products substantiates anecdotal reports of heavy ketamine use and waste across the United States. Under-studied areas including the emergency department are associated with high volumes of ketamine use, particularly for sedation and intubation. Health professionals in this area retrieve a large-format ketamine product (ketamine 500 mg/10 mL), administer a relatively small dose and waste the majority of the vial, leading to large PW (e.g., 450 mg of waste in 23% of administrations). There is a clear need for improvement in the utilization of ketamine in the hospital setting. Additionally, midazolam 2 mg/2-mL vials demonstrated the highest percentage of total waste at both MPH (81.75%) and SJH (61.24%) for the most commonly wasted dose of 1 mg. Therefore, the manufacturing of midazolam 1-mg vials/syringes should be evaluated to best match actual utilization practices within intraoperative areas.

Workforce Time Waste (WTW)

This analysis of WTW revealed a clear distinction between automated and non-automated workflows. The time required for compliance with waste disposal was significantly lower in the automated workflow setting (23.84 s per waste event at MPH) compared with the non-automated workflow (65.58 s per waste event at SJ facilities). This considerable difference underscores the efficiency gains that can be achieved through the implementation of automated dispensing technologies. Although many inpatient hospital areas may have now adopted automation, over half of ambulatory surgery centers (53%) remain on paper records [16]. Given the continued shift to outpatient surgery/care, this extent of non-automation wastes additional workforce time in our already stressed healthcare system. The involvement of CRNAs in most waste transactions (56%) highlights the critical role these professionals play in the waste disposal process. Regardless of whether CRNAs or RNs are involved in compliance with wasting, their time is valuable. Increasing salaries paired with widespread healthcare worker shortages highlight the continued importance of reallocating valuable workforce time to more direct patient care activities [17].

In addition to anesthesia and nursing staff, pharmacy professionals are also heavily involved in the medication use and compliance process associated with the study medications. The limited pharmacy data collected as part of this research was out of scope, yet clearly the operational and financial impact of waste on pharmacy departments is significant. Focused research is needed to fill this evidence gap, adding necessary data in the calculation of total costs of wasted medication for health systems. Importantly, these research findings validate the need to include workforce time in holistic cost analyses. Further, it is imperative that health systems invest in streamlining workflow to improve the efficiency of care. For example, the use of ready-to-administer (RTA) pre-filled products, optimizing product sizes to match practice, and implementing intuitive automated systems are all evidence-based approaches to improve care delivery and achieve substantial cost savings [18].

Safety, Diversion and Regulatory Considerations

This study underscores the importance of implementing effective waste minimization practices and technologies in healthcare settings. Given the high abuse potential and regulatory requirements associated with controlled substances, reducing waste is not only a matter of cost savings but also a critical aspect of patient safety and compliance. Although this study was conducted at two hospitals in the same health system, these issues are widespread. Hospitals should consider adopting automated dispensing technologies and optimizing dosage sizes to align more closely with clinical needs. Additionally, regular training and audits may help ensure adherence to best practices for controlled substance disposal, further reducing waste and enhancing overall operational efficiency. Previous studies have explored the potential for patient harm associated with the medications in the context described [9]. Patient safety remains an essential consideration when using all medications, especially intravenous (IV) controlled substances. Risks associated with improper labeling and packaging of IV medications are well documented, yet labeling issues remain a threat to patient safety, particularly in procedural and post-procedural areas [19]. The United States Pharmacopeia (USP) in their Chapter <797> standards dictate “approved labeling includes information for the diluent, the resultant strength, the container closure system, and storage time.” Large-format products coupled with poor labeling practices has increased potential to result in incorrect dosing and patient harm [20].

Medication diversion remains a constant threat to health systems and the public health. As detailed in the introduction, the DEA has attempted to quantify this multi-billion-dollar issue in the US. Controlled substance waste is a significant source of potential diversion in hospitals. The larger the amounts and the greater the volume/frequency of controlled substance waste, the more of these medications go undocumented and potentially diverted. Previous studies have measured the time between removal from automated dispensing cabinets and documentation of waste, noting specific and dramatic increases in waste of controlled substances during staff shift changes [10]. Inconsistent documentation and witnessing of waste remains a risk to patients, staff, and public health.

Finally, in addition to diversion risk mitigation and controlled substance compliance, health systems must also ensure compliance with sterile compounding regulations. Specifically, USP Chapter <797> standards have been adopted by State Boards of Pharmacy to assist in setting requirements for the safe compounding of medications. From the USP briefing on the updated 2022 standards, “any unused starting component from a single-dose container must be discarded after preparation is complete. Single dose containers must not be used for more than one patient.” [20]. This clear directive has the potential to increase medication waste if products are not matched with clinical practice. Dispensing doses for multiple patients out of a single-dose product is prohibited, further reinforcing the impetus for health systems to purchase products in formats aligning with clinical need, including ready-to-administer pre-filled syringes, in order to provide cost-effective care.

Limitations

As with all research studies, it is acknowledged that this investigation has certain limitations. Although the observational study was conducted in multiple intraoperative units across two large community hospitals, every health system is unique. The technique and research approach outlined can be replicated at any facility, but the specific time, cost, and other data reported in this manuscript is unique to the study sites included in this research. Extrapolation should be conducted with the caveat that variations in care unit layout and practice, including the use of order sets, can influence study results, as they did in this research.

This study was limited to specific procedural and post-procedural areas within a single health system, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, there were differences in the study period of product waste and workforce time waste data collection. Although there is no reason to believe this affected the validity of the study, it is acknowledged as a limitation. Paper chart data regarding waste of controlled substances is subject to human error more so than digital recording due to differences in handwriting and reporting procedures among the staff. The paper chart data only tracks the waste per patient but does not report individual doses received. For example, one patient may have been administered a 0.3-mL dose followed by a 0.6-mL dose from the same 1-mL vial, but the waste would be reported as 0.1 mL. The doses administered were later extracted from Tableau® and analyzed separately. Future research should explore controlled substance waste across a broader range of healthcare settings, including outpatient and long-term care facilities. Additionally, investigating the impact of waste on pharmacy departments would add needed context in the attempt to understand the total cost of pharmaceutical waste to our health systems.

Conclusion

This study provides critical insights into the extent of pharmaceutical waste and workforce time waste associated with administration of IV controlled substances, specifically fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine, midazolam, and ketamine, in procedural and post-procedural hospital settings. When extrapolated annually, the combined waste cost for both study hospitals was estimated at US$56,557. The substantial proportion of waste costs associated with workforce time (36–50%) emphasizes the financial impact of inefficient waste disposal processes. These findings validate current literature, while highlighting the need for targeted interventions to optimize controlled substance use and disposal practices.
