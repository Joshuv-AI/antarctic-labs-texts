Multimodal non-surgical management of isolated tracheobronchial mucormycosis: a case series of 8 patients
Authors: Zeqin Fan, Liqiong Liu, Na Yang, Mengli Li, Panpan Qian, Shuanglan Xu, Xiqian Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated tracheobronchial mucormycosis (ITBM), a distinct subtype of pulmonary mucormycosis (PM), is a relatively rare but life-threatening infection. Here, we present our experience in the multimodal comprehensive treatment of ITBM.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective descriptive case series of ITBM patients at the Affiliated Hospital of Yunnan University from 2020 to 2023. Demographic characteristics, underlying diseases, clinical manifestations and details of treatment were collected.ResultsA total of 8 patients (3 males, 37.5%) with a mean age of 50.8 years were included. Diabetes was the most common risk factor and ITBM tended to involve the left main bronchus. All 8 patients started with intravenous antifungal therapy and transitioned to oral therapy once stable. Five (62.5%) received posaconazole injection, and six (75%) received posaconazole tablets. The average durations were 5.5 weeks for intravenous antifungal therapy, 17.4 weeks for oral therapy, and 22.9 weeks for total systemic therapy. Regarding topical antifungal therapy, a total of 7 (87.5%) patients were administered atomized inhalation of amphotericin B deoxycholate (AmBD) (2.5-5 mg/dose, twice daily), and the mean treatment time was 27.3 days. Similarly, seven (87.5%) patients received bronchoscopic instillation of AmBD (2.5–20 mg/dose, twice a week), with an average number of 5.6. All patients underwent bronchoscopic interventional treatment. The most frequently used interventional treatments were argon plasma coagulation (APC), cryoresection, and balloon dilatation. To maintain airway patency, 3 patients underwent tracheal silicone stent implantation. The main complication of interventional therapy was mild hemoptysis (50%). Finally, all patients achieved complete remission, with a mean time from diagnosis to complete remission of 5.3 months.ConclusionIn this case series of ITBM, a non-surgical multimodal management strategy was applied, and favorable clinical outcomes were observed during follow-up.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14022-x.

Background

Pulmonary mucormycosis (PM) is a relatively rare invasive fungal infection with a significant mortality rate. It is usually caused by inhaling spores of mucor. This disease primarily affects individuals with diabetes and compromised immune systems [1]. In recent years, there has been a notable increase in the incidence of PM, particularly during the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic [2]. However, only a small number of patients exhibit isolated involvement of the central airway (trachea and main bronchus) without parenchymal or segmental bronchial involvement, a condition referred to as isolated tracheobronchial mucormycosis (ITBM) [3]. The pathogen invades the airway wall and blood vessels in ITBM patients, leading to stenosis, obstruction, and potentially severe hemoptysis. The management of this disease is challenging, and the mortality rate is high, particularly due to the limited feasibility of surgical intervention.

Given the limited number of case reports [3, 4], the unique nature of ITBM-related central airway stenosis and the high mortality rate, the objective of this study was to describe the clinical characteristics and management of ITBM using a non-surgical multimodal approach, encompassing risk factor management, systemic and local antifungal therapy, and bronchoscopic interventions.

Methods

This study was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Yunnan University. We retrospectively collected baseline medical data from the hospital’s electronic medical records system between January 2020 and October 2023. The study was approved by the institutional ethics review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The term ITBM refers to the detection of Mucor through histopathology of the trachea or/and bronchi, and bronchoscopy reveals the presence of a lesion in the trachea or/and bronchi. It is important to note that patients with pulmonary parenchyma and/or segmental bronchial involvement were excluded from this definition. The degree of airway stenosis was graded according to the benign central airway stenosis classification published by Freitag et al. [5]. Cases were retrieved by searching the electronic medical records for pulmonary mucormycosis using ICD codes, followed by manual review of inpatient records, chest CT, and bronchoscopy reports. All consecutive patients who met the diagnostic criteria for ITBM during the study period were included. In our study, a total of 35 patients with pulmonary mucormycosis were initially identified. Of these, 27 were excluded because of concomitant pulmonary parenchymal and/or segmental bronchial involvement, yielding a final cohort of 8 patients with ITBM.

Upon confirmed diagnosis, all patients immediately received a standardized multimodal regimen, including intravenous antifungal therapy, concurrent nebulized amphotericin B deoxycholate (AmBD) inhalation, bronchoscopic interventional debridement, and endoscopic local antifungal instillation. Intravenous antifungal therapy was administered as the initial treatment. Switch to oral sequential therapy was made when patients met all of the following: (1) stable vital signs; (2) continuously improving symptoms; (3) > 50% regression of endobronchial lesions; and (4) normal gastrointestinal function with tolerance to oral intake. Bronchoscopic intervention was performed for all patients with confirmed ITBM who could tolerate intravenous sedation and airway manipulation, regardless of baseline airway stenosis grade. The core objectives were to debride necrotic airway tissue, reduce local fungal burden, relieve airway obstruction, and maintain airway patency. The endoscopic therapeutic modalities employed in this study included argon plasma coagulation (APC), cryoresection, balloon dilatation, electric snare resection, laser ablation, high-frequency electrotomy, high-frequency electrocoagulation ablation, stent placement, mechanical expansion, and cryoablation. The choice among available modalities was individualized based on the nature and location of lesions, the degree of airway stenosis, and the judgment of the treating bronchoscopist. All procedures were performed by experienced endoscopists following standardized protocols. All endoscopic AmBD instillation procedures were performed under intravenous sedation and analgesia, with routine oxygen inhalation and continuous electrocardiographic monitoring. The drug was instilled via the working channel of the bronchoscope or a dedicated Olympus delivery catheter to the affected airway segment. Treatments were administered twice weekly, with a single dose ranging from 2.5 mg to 20 mg.

Clinical outcomes were defined according to the MSG/EORTC consensus criteria [6], adapted for ITBM as follows: (1) Complete response: simultaneous fulfillment of (i) complete resolution of all attributable symptoms and signs, (ii) complete regression of tracheobronchial lesions on chest CT and bronchoscopy (airway stenosis was permissible, as it represents post-infectious sequelae rather than active disease), (iii) mycological eradication confirmed by negative follow-up histopathology and culture, and (iv) sustained remission on long-term follow-up (≥ 6 months) without clinical, radiological, or bronchoscopic evidence of recurrence. The time from diagnosis to CR was calculated based on the initial fulfillment of criteria (i)–(iii); the 6-month confirmatory period (criterion iv) was not added to this calculation. All patients underwent standardized evaluations at each follow-up visit, including clinical symptom assessment, chest CT, and bronchoscopy. The primary efficacy evaluation time point was set at 6 months after CR; actual follow-up intervals ranged from 6 to 8 months due to routine clinical scheduling and patient-related factors, while follow-up was extended in patients requiring prolonged management (e.g., those with silicone stent placement). (2) Partial response: ≥50% improvement in attributable symptoms and ≥ 50% reduction in intraluminal lesions, with evidence of mycological clearance or reduction. (3) Failure: disease progression (clinical worsening, new or enlarging lesions, or persistently positive microbiology) or death. Continuous variables were expressed in terms of the mean and standard deviation, and classified variables were represented by numbers and percentages.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 8 patients (3 males, 37.5%) with a mean age of 50.8 ± 11.9 years were included in this study (Table 1). At diagnosis, cough, expectoration, and dyspnea were present in all 8 patients (100%), while fever was observed in 2 patients (25%). All eight patients had type 2 diabetes mellitus. Two developed acute diabetic complications: one had diabetic ketoacidosis and the other diabetic ketosis. Five patients had been on glucose-lowering therapy before admission, while the other three had no treatment history. Admission HbA1c, available in 5 of the 8 patients, ranged from 8.0% to 11.3%, indicating poor long-term glycemic control. The mean fasting plasma glucose in the three days before the initial admission ranged from 6.00 to 18.3 mmol/L (11.9 ± 4.1mmol/L); after standardized hypoglycemic intervention, the value decreased to 4.97–8.53 mmol/L (7.0 ± 1.3 mmol/L) during the three days before the last hospitalization, suggesting improved glycemic status. Three patients (37.5%) had a history of COVID-19 infection prior to the diagnosis of ITBM (Supplementary Table S1).

CT and Bronchoscopy Findings

Computed tomography (CT) findings included tracheobronchial stenosis (87.5%), pleural effusion (25%), atelectasis (25%) and tracheal spike-like protrusion (12.5%). The bronchoscopy results revealed that mucosal necrosis and ulcers were the most prevalent findings (100% and 50%, respectively), followed by obstruction, stenosis, mucosal edema, purulent secretion, and granulation tissue. In addition, the trachea (62.5%) and left main bronchus (40%) exhibited the highest frequency of involvement (Table 1).

Management

All 8 patients (100%) initially received intravenous antifungal therapy and were subsequently transitioned to oral sequential therapy upon stabilization of their conditions. During the intravenous therapy period, five patients (62.5%) were administered posaconazole injection. Among these, two patients experienced severe and intolerable vomiting following treatment with amphotericin B colloidal dispersion (ABCD) and amphotericin B deoxycholate (AmBD), respectively. Consequently, their treatment regimens were modified to posaconazole injection. Additionally, 2 (25%) patients received Isavuconazole injection, and 1(12.5%) patient was treated with AmBD. The mean duration of intravenous antifungal therapy was 5.5(3.6) weeks. For the sequential oral therapy, 6 patients (75%) were prescribed posaconazole enteric-coated tablets, while 2 patients were administered posaconazole oral suspension. The mean duration of oral and total systemic antifungal therapy was 17.4 (6.1) and 22.9 (8.2) weeks, respectively (Table 2). In terms of topical antifungal therapy, a total of 7 (87.5%) patients were administered atomized inhalation of AmBD (2.5-5 mg/dose, twice daily), and the mean treatment time was 27.3 (14.5) days. Similarly, seven (87.5%) patients received bronchoscopic instillation of AmBD (2.5–20 mg/dose, twice a week), with an average number of 5.6 (4.1) (Table 2). Systemic antifungal-related adverse events included severe vomiting in 2 previously described patients, posaconazole-associated QT prolongation in 1 patient, and mild nausea in 1 patient. No significant adverse events were noted in the remaining patients or those receiving topical antifungal therapy.

To clean necrotic tissue and mitigate airway stenosis, all patients underwent bronchoscopic treatment. A total of 87.5% (7/8) received at least 2 bronchoscopy interventional treatments. The most frequently used interventional treatments were argon plasma coagulation (APC) (6 patients, 14 times), cryoresection (4 patients, 13 times), and balloon dilatation (4 patients, 11 times) (Table 3; Fig. 1). Discussion

In this case series of 8 patients with ITBM treated with a multimodal approach, complete remission was observed in all patients during follow-up. Multiple studies have confirmed that patients with diabetes mellitus have significantly increased susceptibility to respiratory mucormycosis [7, 8]. Damaraju et al. reported that diabetic patients accounted for as high as 79.2% of cases with ITBM [3]. The results of the present study are highly consistent with these findings: all eight enrolled patients with ITBM had a history of diabetes mellitus, and without exception, all had poor glycemic control. Hyperglycemia and ketoacidosis have been demonstrated to foster a microenvironment conducive to Mucorales proliferation through multiple mechanisms, including upregulation of the fungal CotH protein and host GRP78 receptor, release of free iron, and impairment of phagocyte function [9–11]. In line with standard care principles, intensive glycemic control was implemented as a foundational component of management for all patients in this cohort. Additionally, COVID-19 infection and its treatment can exacerbate hyperglycemia and immunosuppression, and are recognized as significant predisposing factors for mucormycosis [12, 13]. In this series, all three patients with a documented history of COVID-19 prior to ITBM diagnosis also achieved complete remission. However, given the limited number of cases, the compounding effect of COVID-19 on the prognosis of ITBM cannot be independently assessed, and larger studies are warranted. Notably, the prevalence of ITBM in the left main bronchus was higher in our cohort (50%), a predilection that parallels the known vulnerability of the left main bronchus to other infectious diseases, most notably tuberculosis [14, 15]. This pattern may be explained by the anatomical features of the left main bronchus. As described by Ashour in his concept of “left bronchus syndrome,” the left main bronchus is considerably longer, narrower, and takes off at a more horizontal angle from the trachea compared with the right [14]. Moreover, its anatomical course traversing the mediastinum between the aortic arch and the left pulmonary artery subjects it to potential extrinsic compression, which may further compromise local lymphatic drainage and mucociliary clearance. These anatomical disadvantages collectively impair the effective expulsion of infected secretions and facilitate pathogen retention, thereby creating a localized microenvironment conducive to Mucorales proliferation.

According to the literature, mortality rates of mucormycosis vary widely (10–90%), reflecting marked heterogeneity across infection sites and underlying host conditions [16, 17]. In a comprehensive review of 929 zygomycosis cases, Roden et al. further quantified this variability: with active treatment, localized cutaneous disease had a mortality of approximately 16%, pulmonary mucormycosis 76%, and disseminated disease over 90% [17]. For ITBM, the systematic review by Damaraju et al., which included 26 cases, reported an overall mortality of 50% [3]. Although this rate is lower than that of disseminated or pulmonary parenchymal mucormycosis, it still underscores the substantial lethality of ITBM even when confined to the airways.

In comparison, all patients in our cohort achieved complete remission during follow-up. Two factors may help explain the favorable outcomes observed in this series, though no causal inference can be drawn. First, all patients underwent early and repeated bronchoscopic interventions. Previous case series have associated early bronchoscopic debridement with better outcomes, as it may reduce fungal burden and prevent transmural extension [3]. Second, the multimodal regimen applied in this study integrated underlying risk factor control, combined systemic and topical antifungal therapy, and bronchoscopic intervention, which aligns with the three pillars of mucormycosis management recommended by current guidelines [18]. Notably, the treatment approaches in the ITBM cases reviewed by Damaraju et al. were highly heterogeneous: some patients received systemic antifungal therapy alone, some underwent surgical resection, and only a minority received bronchoscopic topical treatment [3]. In contrast, the standardized multimodal regimen in our study was uniformly applied, and whether this difference in management is associated with the observed outcomes warrants further investigation. However, it must be acknowledged that, given the extremely high mortality of untreated mucormycosis and the rarity of ITBM, isolating the independent effect of any single treatment component is extremely challenging, both ethically and practically. The relative contributions of these individual components therefore cannot be readily distinguished.

Regarding medication, European guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of mucormycosis strongly recommend using liposomal amphotericin B (LAmB) as the first-line drug treatment [18]. However, due to the unavailability of LAmB and patient concerns regarding the toxicity, side effects, and costs of other antifungal agents, posaconazole injection was administered to five patients during the intravenous antifungal phase, while posaconazole enteric-coated tablets were given to six patients during the oral sequential phase. This deviation from guideline recommendations was necessitated by a critical lack of drug availability and is supported by emerging evidence. A systematic review of 1,601 patients with mucormycosis reported an overall favorable clinical outcome rate of 74.8% with posaconazole-based therapy, and 78.6% in the subgroup with COVID-19-associated mucormycosis; the same review demonstrated that posaconazole monotherapy was effective in both first-line and second-line settings [19]. Notably, all patients in our study who received posaconazole-based treatment showed favorable safety and clinical outcomes. In this small series, posaconazole-based regimens were well tolerated and were associated with complete remission in all patients. While no efficacy conclusions can be drawn, these descriptive observations provide preliminary real-world reference for the use of posaconazole in resource-limited settings. Mucor’s extensive infiltration of blood vessels and tissues results in vascular invasion, tissue necrosis, and ischemia. Consequently, the local drug concentration in systemic antifungal treatment becomes inadequate, making it challenging to achieve favorable clinical outcomes. Several studies have indicated that patients’ clinical symptoms and imaging did not improve after systemic antifungal therapy alone but did improve after the addition of endoscopic instillation of AmBD [20, 21]. Additionally, a study conducted by Feng et al. demonstrated that bronchoscopic instillation of AmBD is a safe and effective method for treating pulmonary mycosis [22]. In our study, 7 patients received repeated bronchoscopic instillation of AmBD, with an average number of 5.6 (4.1). Furthermore, 7 patients underwent both bronchoscopic instillation and inhalation of AmBD. Compared to intravenous medication, endoscopic instillation and inhalation therapies offer the advantages of fewer side effects and higher local drug concentrations.

Although guidelines and the literature consistently emphasize the critical role of early surgical intervention in improving outcomes for patients with mucormycosis, surgery is often impractical in ITBM, a distinct subtype of pulmonary mucormycosis. In this study, none of the 8 ITBM patients underwent surgical resection; instead, favorable clinical outcomes were observed with a non-surgical multimodal approach. Bronchoscopic interventions were performed to debride necrotic tissue, restore airway patency, reduce fungal burden, and facilitate topical drug delivery, which has been proposed as a minimally invasive alternative to surgical resection [23]. Additionally, in our study, 8 patients underwent bronchoscopic interventional therapy, and 87.5% received at least 2 bronchoscopic interventional treatments. APC, cryoresection, and electric snare resection were commonly employed interventional techniques for clearing necrotic tissue. However, caution must be exercised when using cryoresection to prevent airway wall tearing and subsequent bleeding. It is advisable to perform these procedures under the guidance of experienced bronchoscopists. Additionally, during the recovery phase of ITBM, some patients may develop airway malacia or cicatricial stenosis. Stent placement is generally considered for airway malacia, and may also be used for cicatricial stenosis when balloon dilatation fails to maintain airway patency. The airway stenosis observed during the recovery period of ITBM is comparable to tuberculous bronchial stenosis, and therefore, bronchoscopic therapy is also applicable.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a small sample size of only 8 cases. By design, we restricted the cohort to ITBM without parenchymal or segmental bronchial involvement. This introduced selection bias, as this localized form may be inherently more treatment-responsive, limiting generalizability to all pulmonary mucormycosis. Case identification through ICD-code searches could have missed miscoded or undocumented diagnoses, adding to selection bias. Additionally, patients who died before a definite diagnosis could be established may have been missed, leading to survivorship bias. These biases, together with the small sample and retrospective design, may affect the representativeness of the results. Second, given the high mortality and rarity of the disease, as well as the inherent difficulty of separating the multimodal strategy into individual components for comparison, no comparator group was established in this study; consequently, the independent contribution of each factor to the treatment outcomes cannot be distinguished. In the future, animal models under controlled experimental conditions may help isolate the independent effects of each treatment component. Third, this study was limited by constraints in laboratory diagnostics and clinical data completeness. Due to constraints in institutional testing conditions and the retrospective design, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for posaconazole was not performed. All cases were diagnosed merely based on the histopathological detection of characteristic hyphae, without accurate species-level identification of Mucorales. In addition, clinical data were partially incomplete, and admission glycated hemoglobin data were only available in five patients. For the remaining patients and for assessing in-hospital glycemic changes, daily capillary blood glucose monitoring was used as a surrogate; however, this method is less standardized than HbA1c and may be influenced by acute illness and in-hospital dietary changes, introducing measurement variability. These limitations have important implications for both internal and external validity. Regarding internal validity, the incomplete baseline data and the absence of TDM may introduce information bias and residual confounding. Therefore, this study is strictly presented as a descriptive case series, and no causal inferences regarding treatment effectiveness can be drawn. Regarding external validity, this study was conducted in a single-center, resource-limited setting where TDM and molecular fungal identification are not routinely available. Thus, our results may not be generalizable to well-resourced centers with standardized intensive care and routine TDM. However, they provide practical, real-world evidence for clinicians practicing in similarly constrained settings. In conclusion, large-sample, prospective, multicenter controlled studies with routine therapeutic drug monitoring and molecular identification of fungal species are warranted to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case series describing a non-surgical multimodal comprehensive management approach for ITBM. All 8 patients in this series achieved complete remission. Given the study’s descriptive nature and small sample size, these findings warrant further investigation in multicenter, prospective studies.
