Anisakid nematode larvae in freshwater fishes in the St. Lawrence River (Québec, Canada)
Authors: Catherine Brisson-Bonenfant, Yves Paradis, David J Marcogliese, Guillaume Côté, Sarah Aubé, Simon Bernatchez, Philippe Brodeur
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnisakid nematodes were observed in freshwater and anadromous fishes in the St. Lawrence River, Québec, Canada. Fish were caught at different locations across a 250 km gradient in the St. Lawrence ecosystem covering freshwater and estuarine habitats. Depending on the sampling sites, nematode prevalence ranged from 30% to 58% for sauger (Sander canadensis) and was 43% for Atlantic tomcod (Microgadus tomcod). For walleye (Sander vitreus), parasites were found only in a fluvial Lake (Lake Saint-Pierre) of the St. Lawrence River, with a prevalence of 23%. In all sampling sites, mean abundance of nematodes in the flesh was higher in sauger than in walleye. Presence of nematodes was significantly correlated with fish total length for walleye, sauger and Atlantic tomcod. Sealworm, Phocanema decipiens s.s., and whaleworm, Anisakis simplex s.s., were found and identified by molecular analysis in sauger and walleye in a fluvial lake (Lake Saint-Pierre), the fluvial estuary and the upper estuary of the St. Lawrence River, while Atlantic tomcod were found infected in a freshwater river tributary (Sainte-Anne River) during spawning.This is the first record of Ph. decipiens s.s. in a non-anadromous freshwater fish. The presence of sealworm and whaleworm in walleye and sauger is attributed to their forays into the brackish waters of the fluvial estuary and suggest large scale migrations between the brackish and the fresh waters of the St. Lawrence River.Keywords: Phocanema decipiens, Walleye, Sauger, Anisakis simplex, Atlantic tomcod

Introduction

The presence of parasites in fish affects fisheries globally, causing, among other things, increased fish mortality and reductions in recruitment, body condition, and/or economic value (Dobson and May, 1987). Anisakid nematodes that occur as larval stages in numerous species of marine fish are of particular concern in marine fisheries. Parasites in the genera Anisakis (whaleworms), and Phocanema (sealworms, formerly known as Pseudoterranova; Bao et al., 2023), are infectious to humans when consumed in raw, undercooked, or lightly marinated fish (Mattiucci and Nascetti, 2008; Measures, 2014). The genus Anisakis comprises nine species of which two (A. simplex sensu stricto and A. pegreffii) have been confirmed as zoonotic for humans (Mattiucci and D’Amelio, 2014), while the Phocanema decipiens sensu latu complex consist of six sibling species, of which three (Ph. decipiens sensu stricto, Ph. azarasi and Ph. cattani) have also been confirmed as zoonotic (see review by Koutsoumanis et al., 2024). In humans, infections caused by parasites of the genus Anisakis can be either asymptomatic or symptomatic. Symptoms, which can be varied, include gastrointestinal manifestations, such nausea, vomiting and gastric or intestinal pain. Misdiagnosis as other conditions, including cancer, occurs frequently before a correct diagnosis is made (see review by Shamsi and Barton, 2023). Consuming anisakid larvae can lead to an infection from live larvae, an allergic reaction to anisakid allergens (even when the larvae are dead), or both (Pozio, 2013; Mattiucci et al., 2013). More than 2000 cases of infection are diagnosed in humans annually (Pozio, 2013) and anisakidosis cases have been reported in 34 countries (Shamsi and Barton, 2023). Additionally, sealworms are a further economic burden on fisheries because they are large and easily visible in the flesh of commercial fish (Bowen, 1990; McClelland, 2002). In Atlantic Canada, costs of removal of sealworm from Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) and other fishes were estimated to $27–50 million CAD annually (McClelland, 2002). Over the years, several methods have been developed and implemented to reduce the risks associated with parasites in fishery products (Koutsoumanis et al., 2024).

Anisakid nematodes have complex life cycles that involve invertebrate intermediate hosts (mainly crustaceans), fish intermediate and paratenic (transport) hosts, and marine mammal definitive hosts. Members of Anisakis require cetaceans as definitive hosts, while those of Phocanema use pinnipeds (Mattiucci and Nascetti, 2008; Measures, 2014). Whaleworms tend to infect pelagic fishes, while sealworms are more common in demersal fishes (McClelland et al., 1990). While these parasites occur globally in all the world's oceans (Mattiucci and Nascetti, 2008), Anisakis spp. and Phocanema spp. have been found in more than 75 fish species each in North Atlantic waters alone (McClelland et al., 1990; McClelland, 2002). Anisakis simplex sensu lato and Ph. decipiens s.l. also have been found in freshwater in anadromous fishes returning from sea (Frimeth, 1987; Due and Curtis, 1995), while Anisakis sp. has been observed on rare occasions in salmonids in lakes and reservoirs (Wootten and Smith, 1975).

The St. Lawrence River is among the largest rivers on the North American continent (El-Sabh and Silverberg, 1990), connecting the freshwater Laurentian Great Lakes basin to the salt water of the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the North Atlantic Ocean. With more than 800 000 fishing days per year (MELCCFP, unpublished data), the freshwater portion of the St. Lawrence system is a popular recreational fishing area, where walleye (Sander vitreus) and sauger (Sander canadensis) are two of the most targeted species. Walleye and sauger in this area are also commercially exploited with a value of landings around $40k CAD/year (Dumont et al., 2023). Atlantic tomcod (Microgadus tomcod), which lives in salt water and spawns in brackish or freshwater tributaries of the St. Lawrence River, notably the Sainte-Anne and Batiscan Rivers (Landry-Massicotte, 2022), is targeted by a winter recreational fishery during spawning (Landry-Massicotte et al., 2023). The Atlantic tomcod serves as prey for various marine mammals (Lesage et al., 2001) and is known to be infected with at least 15 species of metazoan parasites in Canadian waters, including sealworm (Margolis and Arthur, 1979; McDonald and Margolis, 1995). Atlantic tomcod can also be a potential prey for walleye and sauger. In a preliminary study, we detected sealworm infections in walleye and sauger from Lake Saint-Pierre, a fluvial lake in the St. Lawrence River (Brisson-Bonenfant et al., unpublished data). To our knowledge, no species of sealworm has been reported in a non-anadromous freshwater fish.

In this study, we examined the prevalence and abundance of anisakid nematodes in the flesh of walleye and sauger captured at different freshwater and estuarine locations of the St. Lawrence ecosystem. Because of their potential role in the transmission of anisakid nematodes and their spawning migrations between marine and freshwater habitats, Atlantic tomcod, which belong to the Gadidae whose members are commonly infected by sealworms (McClelland, 2002), were also collected and examined for anisakid nematodes in the musculature. Finally, a subset of nematodes found in the flesh of walleye, sauger and Atlantic tomcod was identified using molecular analyses.

Materials and methods

Study area

The St. Lawrence River system is composed of different hydrographic and ecological sectors, each with its own characteristics. The fluvial section, which is the southernmost part of the St. Lawrence ecosystem, starts as a long corridor of freshwater and then broadens to form several large shallow lakes including Lake Saint-Pierre (Fig. 1). The fluvial estuary section, which extends from Lake Saint-Pierre to the Île d'Orléans in the Québec City area (Ingram and El-Sabh, 1990; Simons et al., 2010, Fig. 1), is subject to low to moderate tides. The upper estuary extends from the western tip of Île d'Orléans to the mouth of the Saguenay River, forming a transition zone where fresh water from the river mixes with salt water from the Atlantic Ocean (Ingram and El-Sabh, 1990; Simons et al., 2010). The upstream limit of salt intrusion occurs in the Québec City region during winter and downstream of Île d’Orléans at other times of the year (Ingram and El-Sabh, 1990). Due to its combination of complex bathymetry and strong tidal and river flows, this zone displays large longitudinal and lateral salinity gradients (Simons et al., 2010). Salinity ranges from zero at Québec City to 2‰ at the eastern tip of Île d’Orléans and at the eastern tip of Île aux Grues, to 14‰ at the western tip of Île aux Coudres (Simons et al., 2010), and around 26‰ at Rivière-du-Loup (Ingram and El-Sabh, 1990, Fig. 1). The increasing salinity downstream of Île d’Orléans combined with the high turbidity up to Île aux Coudres and the influence of large tides make conditions inhospitable to most freshwater fish species (St. Lawrence Centre, 1996).

The fish community of the St. Lawrence River system consists of approximately 129 species, including those that live exclusively in fresh water, those that live exclusively in salt water, and diadromous species that migrates between salt and fresh water (García Machado et al., 2021). The St. Lawrence River is also home for many marine mammals such as belugas (Delphinapterus leucas), harbour seals (Phoca vitulina), harp seals (Phoca groenlandica), and gray seals (Halichoerus grypus) that live in the brackish estuary and Gulf of St. Lawrence (Lesage et al., 2001).

Fish samples and parasitological examination

Sampling was carried out in the Lake Saint-Pierre archipelago between May 10 and October 31, 2014, and between September 27 and October 31, 2019 (Fig. 1). Walleye and sauger were caught by recreational anglers under the supervision of a professional fishing guide. Fish were kept in a live well and processed at the end of each fishing day. Another sampling event was carried out between September 1 and September 8, 2019, in Lake Saint-Pierre, the fluvial estuary, and the upper estuary. Walleye and sauger were caught using a bottom trawl (Mingelbier et al., 2019) at various sampling stations along a 250 km gradient between Sorel-Tracy and Île aux Grues (Fig. 1). Due to time constrains, some fish were frozen at −20 °C on the boat after capture and subsequently processed after thawing. Examination of thawed fish for anisakid nematodes is a common practice that has been regularly employed in large-scale surveys of parasites in Canadian waters and has been shown to be effective (McClelland et al., 1990; McClelland and Martell, 2001). Finally, Atlantic tomcods were caught by line in the Saint-Anne River on February 20, 2020, during the recreational winter fishing season. Fish were frozen and stored at −20 °C after capture.

For each sampling, fish total length (TL) was measured to the nearest millimeter. Parasite counts by a visual inspection (without candling) were performed in the right and left fillet. This technique has been shown to be effective for sealworm, although less so for whaleworm (McClelland et al., 1990; McClelland and Martell, 2001). For sampling conducted in Lake Saint-Pierre by a fishing guide during fishing trips with clients, parasites counts per fillet were stopped at a maximum of 20 due to logistical and time constraints. Only walleye within the legal-size range (370 mm–530 mm) were processed to comply with fishing regulations, as walleye outside this range must be released by anglers. On a subsample of fish of each species, 116 parasites were randomly extracted and preserved in 95% ethanol for further molecular identification. Handling of the fish, counting, and extraction of the parasites were carried out by the fishing guide for Lake Saint-Pierre and by biologists and wildlife technicians from the Ministère de l’Environnement, de la Lutte contre les changements climatiques, de la Faune et des Parcs (MELCCFP) for other sites.

Statistical analysis

For walleye and sauger, sampling stations were grouped into three different hydrographic and ecological sectors to allow statistical comparison between these different environments: 1) Lake Saint-Pierre and its archipelago, 2) fluvial estuary, and 3) upper estuary (Fig. 1). Fresh and frozen walleyes and saugers were combined for all analyses within each species (Table 1).

The prevalence of nematodes, defined as the number of fish with at least one parasite divided by the number of fish examined, was compared between walleye and sauger, as well as among different sampling sites, using Fisher's exact test to account for unbalanced sample sizes. To investigate key factors explaining the presence of nematodes at the individual host level, the occurrence of nematodes in walleye, sauger and Atlantic tomcod was modelled using a generalized linear model (GLM) fitted with a binomial distribution and a logit link. For sauger, five candidate models were built to explain the observed variation in the probability to detect at least one nematode at the individual host level: 1) null model, 2) length only, 3) sampling site only, 4) the additive effects of length and sampling site, and 5) the multiplicative effects of length and sampling site by the inclusion of an interaction term. For walleye and Atlantic tomcod, since all specimens with nematodes came from the same sites (Table 1), and sampling site variation was therefore absent in the data set, only two models were tested: 1) null model, and 2) length. Sampling year was not included as a potential factor in the model selection because the size distributions of walleye and sauger in Lake Saint-Pierre differed between years. The adequacy of each model was first assessed using the Osius and Rojek (1992) goodness-of-fit test. The Stukel's score test was also used to verify that the logit link was correctly specified (Stukel, 1988). Models exhibiting a lack-of-fit and/or link misspecification were discarded prior to perform the next step of model selection. An information-theoretic approach based on Akaike weights according to the Akaike's information criterion corrected for small sample sizes (AICc) was used to identify the most statistically supported, adequate model (Burnham and Anderson, 2002).

Abundance, defined as the number of parasites in an individual host, was calculated by combining the total number of nematodes in right and left fillets. Mean abundance refers to the average number of parasites in the fish sample. The relationship between abundance of nematodes and TL of walleye, sauger and Atlantic tomcod was modelled using a generalized linear model (GLM) fitted with a negative binomial (type 2) distribution. For sauger, following the same statistical approach as for the occurrence of nematodes, five candidate models were built to explain the observed variation in the abundance of nematodes at the individual level (see above). Since first modelling attempts for sauger did not yield appropriate adequacy, only sauger with a TL ≥325 mm were used in the modeling process of nematode abundance. This allowed to model the relationship between the abundance of nematodes and length of sauger on the size range where parasites were observed (Fig. 2, Fig. 4) which in turn, allowed a better model adequacy. For walleye and Atlantic tomcod, since sampling site variation was not present in the data set, only two models were tested to explain the abundance of nematodes in fillets: 1) null model, and 2) length. The adequacy of all the models that were tested for each species was assessed based on the behaviour of the Pearson residuals by using the R package hnp (Moral et al., 2017). Model adequacy was verified visually by using the goodness-of-fit diagnostic plot and by calculating the percentage of residuals falling outside the envelope out of 100 simulations following the proposed categorization of Mainguy and Moral (2021). Models exhibiting a lack-of-fit were discarded prior to perform the next step of model selection. An information-theoretic approach based on the AICc was used to identify the most statistically supported, adequate model according to their Akaike weight (Burnham and Anderson, 2002). All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2022).

Molecular analysis

DNA was extracted from individual whole specimens using the standard Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit. The DNA was eluted with 100 μl of sterile water. After the extraction, 1.5 μl of DNA was added to the following PCR mix: 7.5 μl of AccuStart™ II (GelTrack™ PCR SuperMix, Quantabio), 2.25 μl (10 mM) of each primer per gene/region of interest, 1.5 μl of water and 1.5 μl of DNA (10–20 ng/μl). Two genes were used for species discrimination, cytochrome c oxidase subunit II (COX2) and the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), region of the ribosomal DNA. Primers were obtained from Mattiucci et al. (2014), 211F (5′-TTTTCTAGTTATATAGATTGRTTYAT-3′) and 201R (5′-CACCAACTCTTA AAATTATC-3′) FOR COX2 and NC5 (5′-GTAGGTGAACCTGCGGAAGGATCATT 3′) NC2 (5′- TTAGTTTCTTTTCCTCCGCT-3′) for ITS. For COX2 amplification, the PCR conditions were 94 °C for 3 min, for initial denaturation, followed by 34 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 46 °C for 60 s and 72 °C for 90 s, and 72 °C for 10 min (Mattiucci et al., 2014). For the ITS rDNA region, the PCR conditions were 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 5 min (Mattiucci et al., 2014).The amplicons were then migrated for 45 min on a 1% agarose gel, the gel was soaked in an ethidium bromide solution (0.5 μg/ml) and then visualized under UV light. The amplified sequences were sent to the IBIS genomic analysis platform (Laval University, Québec) The forward and reverse sequences were aligned using Geneious Prime 2022.0.1. The nematodes were identified by using the NCBI BLAST tool using nt database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).

Results

A total of 88 walleyes, 254 saugers and 30 Atlantic tomcods from different sites of the St. Lawrence River was examined for nematodes in their fillets (fresh and frozen samples combined; Table 1). Nematode prevalence differed significantly between walleye and sauger across sampling sites (Fisher's exact test, P < 0.001). For walleye, the presence of nematodes in fillets contrasted between sampling sites. Parasites were only found in Lake Saint-Pierre, with a prevalence of 23% (Table 1). Prevalence of nematodes in sauger was higher than in walleye in Lake Saint-Pierre, in the fluvial estuary, and in the upper estuary (Table 1).

The probability of observing the presence of a nematode was modelled at the individual host level. For walleye, sauger and Atlantic tomcod, models using fish TL best explained the presence of nematodes (Supp. mat. 1). Presence of nematodes was significantly correlated with fish TL for walleye (z-value = 3.19, P = 0.001; Fig. 2), sauger (z-value = 7.25, P < 0.001) and Atlantic tomcod (z-value = 2.76, P = 0.006). At a TL of 485 mm (±29 mm) walleye had a 50% chance of being infected by at least one nematode. Sauger and Atlantic tomcod had a 50% chance to be infected by nematodes at a TL of 376 mm (±5 mm) and 239 mm (±8 mm), respectively (Fig. 2).

In all sampling sites, mean abundance of nematodes in the flesh was higher in sauger than in walleye (Fig. 3). In Lake Saint-Pierre, the only sector where parasites were found in walleye, abundance of nematodes in the fillets was not significantly correlated with length of walleye (z-value = 1.77, P = 0,076; Fig. 4). For sauger with a size ≥325 mm, the model using an additive effect of length and sector was the most statistically supported model (Supp. mat. 1) to explain the observed abundance of nematodes, but the total explained variance was low (R2 = 0.13). Length showed a positive relationship with the abundance of nematodes in the flesh of sauger, especially in larger fish (all sectors combined, z-value = 12.55, P < 0.001; Fig. 4). For a given size of sauger, the abundance of parasites was higher in the fluvial estuary (z-value = 2.125, P = 0.03) and lower in the upper estuary (z-value = −1.977, P = 0.04) compared to Lake Saint-Pierre. For Atlantic tomcod, the abundance of nematodes was significantly correlated with the length of specimens (z-value = 3.186, P = 0.001), explaining 18% of the observed variation in nematodes abundance (Fig. 4).

Mean abundance (S.D.) of anisakid nematodes (right and left fillets combined) in walleye (Sander vitreus), sauger (Sander canadensis) and Atlantic tomcod (Microgadus tomcod) in different sectors of the St. Lawrence River.

The amplified ITS region and COX2 gene sequences were 850 and 629 bp, respectively. ITS analyses demonstrated nucleotide similarity of 99.5%, 99.3%, 98.3%, 98.2%, and 97.9%, when compared to GenBank sequences corresponding to Ph. azarasi, Ph. decipiens s.s., Ph. bulbosa, Ph. krabbei, and Ph. cattani, respectively. COX2 had 99.0%, 98.5%, 94.6%, 94.3% and 93.8% of nucleotide similarity when compared to sequences of Ph. decipiens s.s., Ph. azarasi, Ph. cattani, Ph. bulbosa and Ph. krabbei, respectively. For Anisakis sp., the COX2 and ITS fragments had 99.7% and 98.4% of nucleotide similarity with sequences of A. simplex s.s. Results from BLAST yield conclusive molecular identification on 116 nematodes. The majority of nematodes (>97%) found in the flesh of walleye and sauger across the different sampling sites were identified as Ph. decipiens s.s., while those in the Atlantic tomcod were a mix of Ph. decipiens s.s. (56%) and A. simplex s.s. (44%; Table 2). Given that all our sequenced worms (n = 116) belong to one of two anisakid species, and considering the relatively high number of worms sequenced, we can assume with some degree of confidence that all the nematodes found in the flesh of the fish sampled in this study are indeed anisakids species.

Discussion

This study reveals a natural occurrence of the parasitic nematode Ph. decipiens s.l. in the St. Lawrence River in two non-anadromous freshwater fish species (sauger and walleye) and one anadromous species (Atlantic tomcod). To our knowledge, this is the first record of any species of sealworm in non-anadromous freshwater fish.

The use of COX2 was not sufficient to determine the species but makes it possible to exclude Ph. cattani, Ph. bulbosa and Ph. krabbei as being the species of nematode. The use of ITS makes possible the confirmation of the species since Zhu et al. (2002) identified a single nucleotide in the ITS gene that unequivocally distinguishes between Ph. decipiens s.s. and Ph. azarasi. The G in position 407 of ITS alignment is unique to Ph. azarasi while we find a C in this position for Ph. decipiens s.s. Our results demonstrate the presence of this characteristic Ph. decipiens s.s. nucleotide in all of our samples. Therefore, molecular analyses confirm that the analyzed nematodes belong to the species Ph. decipiens s.s.

This molecular identification is further supported by the known geographic distribution of other potential nematode species. Specifically, Ph. azarasi is predominantly found in the Pacific, along the west coast of North America and in the Japan Sea (Mattiucci and Nascetti, 2008). Near the province of Québec, Ph. bulbosa has been recorded off Labrador (Mattiucci and Nascetti, 2008) in bearded seals (Erignathus barbatus), a species uncommon in the St. Lawrence Estuary (Gosselin and Boily, 1994). In the Gulf of St-Lawrence, all nematodes from seals that were genetically identified by Brattey and Davidson (1996), belonged to Ph. decipens s.s. (=Ph. decipens B). Additionally, two specimens of Ph. decipens s.s. were genetically identified in the St. Lawrence Estuary: one from a gray seal at Île aux Lièvres and another from a Greenland halibut (Reinhardtius hippoglossoides) in the Saguenay River (Brattey and Davidson, 1996). Ph. cattani has exclusively been identified in Chile, within the Pacific region (Mattiucci and Nascetti, 2008) and uses fur seals (Arctocephalus sp.) as its definitive host, which are absent in the Atlantic Ocean. Ph. krabbei has been identified in Northeast Atlantic Ocean and Faeroe Islands, in harbour seals and gray seals (Mattiucci and Nascetti, 2008).

In our study A. simplex s.s. was also found in sauger, a non-anadromous freshwater fish, and more especially in Atlantic tomcod, an anadromous fish. The more pronounced presence of A. simplex s.s. in Atlantic tomcod compared to walleye and sauger, is likely because Atlantic tomcod is an anadromous species that feeds in a marine environment where whales, the final host of A. simplex s.s, are present (Scott and Crossman, 1973). Sealworms and whaleworms have been found previously in tomcod; in saltwater: Anisakis sp. from the Baie de Chaleurs (Québec) and Phocanema (=Porrocaecum) sp. from Newfoundland, Canada (Heller, 1949; Templeman et al., 1957). In freshwater, records of these two nematodes are typically confined to anadromous fishes returning from sea, such as Anisakis simplex s.l. in brook charr (Salvelinus fontinalis) from New Brunswick, Canada and Arctic charr (Salvelinus alpinus) from Greenland (Frimeth, 1987; Due and Curtis, 1995). Infections of Anisakis sp. were observed in freshwater species, namely brown trout (Salmo trutta) and rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), from a landlocked freshwater reservoir in the United Kingdom (Wootten and Smith, 1975). As these were hatchery-reared fish, the authors concluded that the infections in landlocked freshwater were derived from feeding captive fish with untreated offal, which was common practice in Europe at the time. Another species of Sander, the pike-perch (Sander lucioperca), was infected with A. simplex s.l. in a brackish lagoon in Poland, as well as in the Baltic Sea (Rolbiecki and Rokicki, 2000, and references therein). The pike-perch presumably acquire the parasite by feeding on infected herring (Clupea harengus) in both areas (Rolbiecki and Rokicki, 2000). However, while considered a freshwater fish, pike-perch are known to inhabit brackish and estuarine waters (Froese and Pauly, 2024). While A. simplex s.s. is the only species of the genus found in eastern Canadian waters, no worms were sampled previously from the Gulf of St. Lawrence (Mattiucci et al., 1997). Nevertheless, given the previous records of molecularly identified sealworms and whaleworms from eastern Canadian waters, we consider our identifications of worms from a subsample to be representative of the anisakid nematodes found in the musculature of walleye, sauger and tomcod.

The presence of any species of sealworm on fish caught as far inland as Lake Saint-Pierre is surprising, considering that this fluvial lake is located far upstream of the salt front. Probably, freshwater fish might enter into the brackish waters of the upper estuary, where they feed on infected prey (smaller fish or crustaceans) and can exhibit large scale migrations upstream to Lake Saint-Pierre. This hypothesis is the most likely and it is supported by telemetry data (MELCCFP, unpublished) and results of a marking study conducted near Québec City. Indeed, this study highlighted large-scale migrations for more than 20 freshwater fish species including walleye and sauger of the St. Lawrence River. They move seasonally over a distance of approximately 200 km along the river section between the Lake Saint-Pierre archipelago and the eastern tip of Île d’Orléans (de Lafontaine et al., 2006). During the mission in 2019 conducted by the MELCCFP, walleyes were caught in a station in the upper estuary where conductivity was recorded at 1471 μS/cm indicating that this species can use brackish water habitats (Suppl. mat. 2). Walleye and sauger are known to forage occasionally in brackish waters of the upper estuary (Supp. mat. 3).

A number of possible scenarios could explain the presence of sealworm in these freshwater fishes. First, stranding events can be used as an indicator of marine mammal distribution. From 1994 to 2008, there were a small number of strandings in the freshwater portion of the St. Lawrence River, as far upstream as Montréal (Truchon et al., 2013). While relatively few, they do indicate that marine mammals periodically may enter fresh water and could excrete anisakid eggs during their entry into freshwater, conceivably contributing to the spread of anisakids into Lake Saint-Pierre and other areas of the river. In addition, sealworm eggs can hatch in freshwater (Burt et al., 1990b; Measures, 1996) and are infective to freshwater copepod intermediate hosts (Burt et al., 1990b). Thus, theoretically the life cycle could be completed in fresh water. However, survival of hatched larvae in freshwater is of limited duration, being approximately 12% of that in brackish water and seawater at temperatures ranging from 5 to 15oC (Measures, 1996). Furthermore, given the limited number of marine mammals strandings in freshwater over a 15-year period (Truchon et al., 2013) and the high infection levels in some of our freshwater samples, this scenario seems unlikely.

Second, walleye and sauger could potentially acquire infections by consuming Atlantic tomcod in fresh water during the tomcod spawning migration, which occurs in winter months. Both Anisakis sp. and Ph. decipiens s.l. can be passed serially from fish to fish (Wootten and Smith, 1975; Burt et al., 1990a). Although the size of the Atlantic tomcod is comparable to other species known to be prey for sauger and walleye, we lack data to confirm or refute this hypothesis. Additionally, such transmission would require the fishes to prey on tomcod during periods of low activity in winter. Consequently, this scenario seems less likely.

Third, although walleye and sauger are non-anadromous freshwater species, they can venture into the brackish waters of the upper estuary (MELCCFP, unpublished telemetry data). For instance, walleye and sauger tagged near Québec City have been re-captured in Lake Saint-Pierre and in brackish waters near Île d’Orléans (de Lafontaine et al., 2006). Moreover, walleye and sauger are occasionally captured in brackish waters of the upper estuary up to Rivière-Ouelle (Supp. mat. 3). Given that both seals and beluga whales are known to inhabit the upper estuary (Lesage et al., 2001), they could potentially release parasite eggs into these waters, resulting in transmission to fish such as rainbow smelt (Osmerus mordax), a prey fish found in stomach contents of walleye in the Québec City area (L’Hérault, 2013). Thus, the hypothesis that walleye and sauger acquire anisakid nematode infections when feeding in the estuary appears to be the most plausible scenario.

Sealworm may be a good indicator of the use of St. Lawrence Estuary habitats by walleye and, especially, sauger. Indeed, the presence of sealworm in sauger caught at Lake Saint-Pierre suggests a significant movement of these fish to the downstream sectors of the St. Lawrence River where seals are present (Lesage et al., 2001; Truchon et al., 2013). Given that the parasite is long-lived in fish (McClelland et al., 1990; McClelland, 2002), high infection levels in an individual fish may indicate an accumulation of the parasite over time due to repeated and frequent incursions into the upper estuary where the fish has fed. For several years, anglers have reported that in the St. Lawrence River sauger are more parasitized than walleye. In this study, the prevalence of nematodes in musculature was higher in sauger than in walleye across all sampled sites, supporting these observations. This difference could be explained by the movement of sauger into the brackish waters of the upper estuary during the summer months (MELCCFP, unpublished telemetry data). For walleye, nematode parasites were only found in fish caught in Lake Saint-Pierre, which is surprising since this sampling site is the farthest from salt water. However, this result must be interpreted with caution, as it may be attributed to the small sample sizes in the fluvial and upper estuary areas.

The numbers of larval anisakid nematodes in fish typically increases with fish size (McClelland et al., 1983, 1990). This was also observed in the present study, as nematode abundance was related to size in both sauger and Atlantic tomcod. Moreover, the probability of being infected by a nematode was significantly correlated with fish length for walleye, sauger and Atlantic tomcod. Additionally, the slope of parasite numbers versus size increases drastically at TL of approximately 30 cm in both walleye and sauger. A similar pattern is observed in Atlantic cod and other fishes, which reflects a change when fish become piscivorous (McClelland et al., 1983, 1990; McClelland, 2002). The lack of relationship of nematode abundance with size in walleye is unusual and may be a consequence of the relatively low prevalence in Lake Saint-Pierre combined with the low sample sizes in the fluvial and upper estuaries, where no nematodes were detected. As previously mentioned, we can confidently assume that all the nematodes found in the flesh of the sampled fish are anisakids, since all our sequenced worms (n = 116) belong to one of two anisakid species, and the number of worms sequenced is relatively high.

Our nematode infection levels are probably slightly underestimated, because the visual inspection technique may miss a few sealworms in fillets but will miss many more whaleworms, due to their smaller size and light colouration (McClelland et al., 1983, 1990). Whaleworm is usually found in the viscera, and infection levels in the musculature of fish are usually low, except in extremely heavy infections (McClelland et al., 1990). Despite this limitation of the study regarding the assessment of the total abundance of nematode parasites in the entire fish, overall, we are confident that our numbers of parasite observed in the flesh are reliable and reflect real patterns.

This study reports only the second occurrence of sealworm, Ph. decipiens s.s., in Atlantic tomcod, an anadromous species that resides in salt water and reproduces in winter in the mouths of brackish and freshwater rivers, such as the Sainte-Anne River. Conceivably, Atlantic tomcod could play a role in transmitting sealworm to walleye and sauger, as these fishes could acquire the parasite by feeding on tomcod in freshwater during the winter, or, more likely, during excursions into the upper estuary at other times. Atlantic tomcod is, in turn, known to feeds on various preys, including small crustaceans, which can be infected by parasitic nematodes. Both Anisakis sp. and Ph. decipiens s.l. are known to be transmissible from fish to fish (Wootten and Smith, 1975; Burt et al., 1990a). In fish, these parasites remain as third-stage larvae and migrate out of the stomach to the viscera and musculature. They only remain in the digestive system, where they mature to adulthood, if ingested by the definitive host (in this case, a mammal; Anderson, 2000). Other prey fishes in the upper estuary, such as rainbow smelt, which are known intermediate hosts of Ph. decipiens s.l. (Landry and Hare, 1990; Möller and Klatt, 1990) could also play a role in sealworm transmission to walleye and sauger.

Parasites are used to aid in fish stock discrimination and the migration of commercial fishes (Williams et al., 1992; MacKenzie and Abaunza, 1998). The use of parasites as biological tags has been applied mainly to marine fisheries, but also to a limited extent to freshwater fishes in North America (Marcogliese and Jacobson, 2015). Discovery of anisakid nematodes in the fillets is interesting, from a biological perspective, because it demonstrates that walleye and sauger migrate into the upper estuary of the St. Lawrence River, where they feed on prey resources found in those waters. However, infections could lead to potential problems for recreational fisheries, due to a general contempt and disdain for parasitic organisms among the public (Marcogliese, 2005). Additionally, since both whaleworms and sealworms are transmissible to humans, awareness among anglers, commercial fishermen and the public is required to ensure safe consumption of catches (Measures, 2014; Ángeles-Hernández et al., 2020). Moreover, exposure to Anisakis spp., even if cooked or frozen, is known to induce sometimes serious allergic reactions (Rahmati et al., 2020). Given the potential consequences of these unusual infections, it becomes important to better understand the extent of infections in other freshwater fishes, the life cycle of sealworm in the upper estuary and the interactions between its different hosts in these and neighboring waters.
