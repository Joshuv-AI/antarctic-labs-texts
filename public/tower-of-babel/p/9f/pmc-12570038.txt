Mechanical energy-charged navigating pump repair natural vascular scaffolds to reverse osteoporosis
Authors: Fengkun Wang, Shuai Yang, Yanbin Zhu, Yiran Zhang, Wei He, Shuo Zhang, Jiheng Xiao, Chunxu Fu, Peng Wang, Jianpeng Chen, Jiahao Wang, Haiyune Zhao, Wei Chen, Yingze Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bone vasculature plays a critical role in maintaining bone health. A reduction in mechanical loading-induced vascular deficiency plays a critical role in osteoporosis progression by disrupting the bone microenvironment and impairing intercellular communication among endothelial cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts. A bioactive nanoparticle, formed by fusing mechanically loading-induced preosteoclasts (POCs)-derived exosomes (ML-Exo) with (DSS)6 peptide-modified endothelial cell membrane vesicles (DMVs), reconstructs vascular networks and the bone microenvironment, reversing osteoporosis. Mechanical loading enhances communication capacity of ML-Exo, enabling mechanical signal transmission to endothelial cells and promoting angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling. Additionally, the CXCR4 protein and (DSS)6 peptide on DMVs facilitate bone tissue localization (soil entry), while endothelial cell membrane homologous chemotaxis ensures precise targeting of bone vasculature (direct irrigation). This “Mechanical Energy-Charged Navigating Pump” (MLE/DMV), formed by fusion of ML-Exo and DMVs, restores the “natural vascular scaffold network,” improving blood supply and nutrient delivery to the bone microenvironment. It also restores intercellular communication among endothelial cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts, slowing osteoporosis progression.

Introduction

Bone vasculature plays a critical role in maintaining bone health. Reduced mechanical loading-induced vascular dysfunction is a key factor in the progression of osteoporosis [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In normal bone tissue, mechanical loading directly or indirectly influences endothelial cells, thereby regulating their proliferation and differentiation [4,5]. Inspired by the “root-tree symbiosis” theory, which posits that tree roots absorb nutrients from the soil and distribute them throughout the tree to maintain homeostasis, we propose that in the bone microenvironment, intercellular communication among endothelial cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts, orchestrated by the vascular network, constitutes a critical pathway for maintaining bone homeostasis [6,7]. In the progression of osteoporosis, reduced mechanical loading leads to disruption of the vascular network and dysregulation of the endothelial cell-osteoblast-osteoclast axis, thereby exacerbating bone loss. Therefore, improving the bone microenvironment through modulation of the bone vasculature and intercellular communication emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy for osteoporosis [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

To restore intercellular communication and nutrient supply and remodel the bone vasculature, mechanical signaling transduction is essential. In vivo, the vascular network is exposed to a complex mechanical environment, where mechanical loads regulate its structure, function, and the rate and direction of angiogenesis through direct or indirect mechanisms, including cyclic stretch induced by blood pulsatility and physiological loads caused by tissue deformation during body movements [12,13]. Preosteoclasts (POCs) serve as a critical component of the endothelial-osteoblast-osteoclast intercellular communication axis [14,15]. As a non-bone-resorptive subtype of osteoclasts, they are closely associated with the formation of H type vessels (a subtype of osteogenesis-associated vascular structures) within bone tissue, referring to them as vascular-associated osteoclasts [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Their exosomes, as natural biomolecules, play a pivotal role in restoring intercellular communication [20]. However, these exosomes exhibit inconsistent efficacy and yield, which limits their functional potential [21,22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the production and release of exosomes are highly dependent on mechanical loading [[23], [24], [25]], and mechanical signals are transmitted to neighboring cells via exosomes, thereby regulating the biological activities of target cells [26,27]. However, whether mechanical loading can enhance the functional effects of exosomes derived from POCs remains to be determined.

Additionally, during drug delivery via systemic circulation, precise targeting of exosomes to bone tissue remains a challenge [28]. The highly complex bone microenvironment poses significant limitations to effective exosome uptake by target cells, thereby restricting their capacity to regulate intercellular communication [29]. To address these limitations, various molecular modifications, such as bone-targeting peptides and bisphosphonates, have been incorporated onto exosome surfaces [30]. These modifications enhance targeting specificity and improve therapeutic outcomes in osteoporosis treatment. Nevertheless, most current studies are largely confined to single-level targeting strategies, failing to achieve precise localization around bone vasculature due to the complexity of the bone microenvironment. Moreover, given the heterogeneity of cellular populations within this microenvironment, enhancing target cell uptake of exosomes remains a pressing challenge. Therefore, developing spatially precise delivery systems for engineered exosomes capable of targeting bone vasculature represents a critical research direction in this field. The cell membrane, as a natural carrier, is widely utilized in drug delivery and other applications owing to its excellent biocompatibility [31]. The CXCR4 protein on the surface of endothelial cell membranes naturally endows bone-targeting capability [32]; however, its efficacy remains suboptimal. We sought to enhance the targeting capability of nanoparticles by modifying the cell membrane surface with the (DSS)6 peptide [33]. Through the CXCR4 protein and (DSS)6 peptide on the surface of DMVs, nanoparticles can be guided to localize to bone tissue via systemic circulation, achieving “soil entry” and initial localization near the root system. Meanwhile, upon arrival at the bone tissue, nanoparticles achieve ultra-precise spatial delivery to endothelial cells by leveraging homologous chemotaxis mediated by endothelial cell membranes, thereby accomplishing “direct irrigation” of the root zone. Through the fusion of ML-Exo and DMVs to form MLE/DMV, we achieved multi-level targeting. This fusion enabled the remodeling of the bone vascular network and initiated the domino effect of intercellular communication among endothelial cell, osteoblast, and osteoclast within the bone microenvironment. Consequently, the balance between osteogenesis and bone resorption was restored.

This study focuses on the remodeling of endothelial-osteoblast-osteoclast intercellular communication and the reconstruction of the vascular network within the osteoporotic microenvironment, aiming to alleviate osteoporosis. MLE/DMV targeting bone vasculature were generated via membrane fusion technology by combining ML-Exo and DMVs (Scheme 1). MLE/DMV serves as a full-API nanodrug (FAND) [34], is composed entirely of active ingredients, exhibiting high biocompatibility and characterized by low toxicity. Additionally, MLE/DMV demonstrates precise spatial targeting capability through a novel “Mechanical Energy-Charged Navigating Pump.” MLE/DMV first generates a “soil entry” via (DSS)6 and CXCR4 peptide, achieving initial localization. Subsequently, by leveraging the homologous chemotaxis mediated by endothelial cell membranes, it employs the “irrigation needle” to directly target the root zone, realizing “precision irrigation.” Following this, the microRNAs within MLE/DMV act as nutrients and fertilizers, regulating root growth, which equates to modulating the vascular network. On one hand, blood vessels can deliver oxygen and nutrients to tissues, enhance the microenvironment, and regulate the behavior of osteoblasts and osteoclasts through intercellular communication. Materials and methods

Materials

DMEM and α-MEM were purchased from Gibco (California, USA). The endothelial cell-specific medium was purchased from ScienceCell (San Diego, USA). The m-CSF and Rankl were bought from Novoprotein (Su Zhou, China). GW4869, DiD, DiR and Dil were obtained from MCE (New Jersey, USA). The collagenase I was purchased from Worthington Biochemical (Worthington, USA). Dispase II and PKH67 dye solution were bought from Sigma-Aldrich (Missouri, USA). DNase I was purchased from Invitrogen (Grand Island, USA). DAPI was purchased from BD (California, USA). Phalloidin was bought from Cayman (Michigan, USA). Microfil was obtained from Flow Technology (Arizona, USA). Anti-CD31 antibody was purchased from R&D systems (Minnesota, USA). Anti-EMCN antibody was purchased from Santa Cruz (Texas, USA). Anti-Ki67 antibody, anti-OCN antibody and anti-OSX antibody were purchased from abcam (Cambridge, UK). Anti-TSG101 antibody, anti-CD81 antibody, anti-CD9 antibody, anti-VEGFR2 antibody and anti-CXCR4 antibody were purchased from proteintech (Wuhan, China). DSPE-PEG2000-(DSS)6 was purchased from Ruixibio (Xi An, China). BioFlex plates (BF-3001C) coated with type I collagen were purchased from Flexcell International Corporation (USA).

Animal care and study approval

8-week-old C57BL/6J mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Animal Center of Hebei Medical University. All experiments involved in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethical Welfare Committee of Hebei Medical University (IACUC Number: Hebmu-2025024).

Isolation and culture of preosteoclasts (POCs)

Bone marrow cells were isolated from 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice. Following euthanasia, femurs were aseptically excised, and bone marrow cells were collected by flushing the marrow cavity. The harvested cells were filtered through a 70 μm filter, erythrocytes were lysed, and the cells were centrifuged. The resulting cells were resuspended in fresh α-MEM containing 10 % exosome-free fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Gibico, 15070063), and 30 ng/mL m-CSF (Novoprotein, CB34). The cells were seeded onto BioFlex plates coated with type I collagen (Flexcell International Corporation, USA) and cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 48 h. Non-adherent cells were removed by replacing the medium, and the cells were further cultured for an additional 24 h. The cells were then cultured for 72 h in the presence of 60 ng/mL Rankl (Novoprotein, C28A) to generate POCs, which were identified using TRAP staining. A FX-4000 T Tension Plus System (Flexcell International, McKeesport) was used to apply mechanical cyclic sinusoidal tensile strain (4 % strain amplitude, 0.5 Hz) to POCs for 4 h. In contrast, the control group was placed in the bioreactor without mechanical loading. After that, fresh medium was added, and the cells were further cultured for 48 h to collect conditioned medium for exosome extraction.

Isolation and characterization of exosomes

Exosomes derived from POCs were isolated through a series of ultracentrifugation steps. The conditioned medium was sequentially centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, 3000 g for 20 min, and 10000 g for 30 min to pellet cellular debris and unbroken cells. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove any remaining particulates. Ultracentrifugation at 120,000 g (Himac, Japan) for 90 min at 4 °C was performed to harvest the exosomes, which were then resuspended in PBS. To ensure purity, the sample was centrifuged at 120,000 g for an additional hour to remove residual medium. The exosomes were subsequently resuspended in PBS, and their protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0009). The structural integrity of the exosomes was examined using TEM (Hitachi, Japan). The size, distribution, and specific protein content of the exosomes were analyzed using NTA (NanoFCM, China).

Cellular uptake

To assess cellular uptake, PKH67-labeled exosomes were prepared by mixing 30 μg of exosomes, 400 μL of Diluent C, and 2 μL of PKH67 dye solution (Sigma-Aldrich, PKH67GL), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was quenched with 400 μL of 5 % BSA. The labeled exosomes were then resuspended in PBS and co-cultured with HUVEC for 8 h. After co-culture, the HUVEC were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, stained with DAPI (BD, 564907) and Phalloidin (Cayman, 20478), and imaged using a confocal microscope (Nikon AX) to evaluate cellular uptake. Subsequently, the cells were digested and dissociated into a single-cell suspension using trypsin, and fluorescence intensity was detected via flow cytometry. In the multi-cellular context for assessing cellular uptake, MC3T3 and HUVEC were first separately labeled with DiD and Dil, respectively. PKH-67-labeled MLE/DMV were then co-cultured with the two cell types for 8 h. Afterward, the cells were digested and dissociated into a single-cell suspension using trypsin, and fluorescence intensity was separately detected in the two channels via flow cytometry.

In vitro tube formation

HUVEC were seeded onto 48-well plates pre-coated with 150 μL Matrigel (ABW-0827045) and treated with exosomes from different groups or an equivalent volume of PBS. After 6 h, cells were stained with calcein-AM, and fluorescence microscopy (Olympus BX51) was used to observe and quantify the total length and number of junctions per well.

Migration assay

HUVEC were seeded into the upper chambers of 24-well transwell plates (BIOFIL-TCS-003-024). Serum-free medium containing exosomes from different groups was added to the lower chambers. Following a 24-h incubation, cells on the upper surface of the transwell membrane were removed using a cotton swab. Cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4 % PFA and stained with 0.5 % crystal violet for 10 min. Finally, three random fields on the lower surface of each membrane were selected, and cell counts were performed twice.

Wound healing assay

HUVEC were seeded into 6-well plates. A linear wound was created by scratching the monolayer of cells using a sterile pipette tip (200 μL). Floating cells were washed away with PBS. Subsequently, cells were incubated with an equal volume of exosomes from different groups. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h using microscopy, and statistical analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

EdU staining assay

HUVEC were seeded into 24-well plates and treated with exosomes from different groups. After 24 h of culture, HUVEC were co-incubated with the EdU working solution for 2 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime). HUVEC were fixed, permeabilized, and incubated with Click Additive Solution for 30 min. Following DAPI staining of the cells, fluorescence microscopy was used for observation.

Total RNA was isolated using trizol (Invitrogen, 15596026). For the qPCR assay, raw data were obtained using PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). During data analysis, the expression of the control group was normalized, and the relative expression of each sample was calculated using the 2−△△Ct method. Bone marrow endothelial cells (BMECs) isolation

The isolation method for BMECs was performed as previously described [29]. After euthanasia, the bilateral femurs were removed, and the ends of the femurs were excised, followed by flushing out the bone marrow. The cells were cultured overnight in DMEM containing 20 % FBS. Subsequently, the adherent cells were digested, and BMECs were isolated using CD31 microbead-based magnetic sorting. The cells were then cultured in endothelial cell-specific medium (ScienceCell-1001).

Synthesis and characterization of DMV

BMECs were harvested when the cell density in the dish reached 80–90 %. Cells were centrifuged at 1500g for 5 min and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, cells were resuspended in double-distilled water and subjected to cell membrane lysis using an ultrasonic processor (QSonica-Q500, USA) under conditions of 20 kHz and 130 W, with 5s on and 5s off for 5 min, while kept on ice. The cell membrane was extruded through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane using an Avanti mini extruder (Avanti, USA), followed by 20 cycles to standardize the size of membrane particles. Cell membrane vesicles (MVs) were then obtained by centrifugation at 15,000g for 30 min. Protein content was determined using the BCA assay kit, and the samples were stored at −80 °C for further use. To enhance bone-targeting ability, DSPE-PEG2000-(DSS)6 was synthesized. First, 100 mg of DSPE-PEG2000-mal was weighed and dissolved in 3 mL of DMF. (DSS)6 peptide (1.1 equivalents) was added and dissolved completely, followed by reaction at room temperature for 12 h. The reaction mixture was then transferred to a dialysis bag and dialyzed in deionized water for 24 h. The dialysate was collected and freeze-dried to obtain the product DSPE-PEG2000-(DSS)6. To form DMVs, MVs were decorated with DSPE-PEG2000-(DSS)6 via the lipid insertion method. Specifically, 100 μg of MVs was incubated with 100 μg of DSPE-PEG2000-(DSS)6 at 37 °C for 1 h. To further assess the binding between (DSS)6 peptide and MVs, confocal microscopy-based fluorescence colocalization imaging was employed.

Preparation and characterization of MLE/DMV

MLE/DMVs were prepared via membrane fusion using a co-extrusion method. ML-Exo and DMVs were mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio for fusion. The suspensions of DMVs and ML-Exo were combined, incubated in a 37 °C ultrasonic bath for 20 min using an ultrasonic cleaner, and then subjected to sequential extrusion through polycarbonate membranes of 400 nm and 200 nm, respectively, each performed 20 times to obtain MLE/DMVs. Fluorescence colocalization imaging was used to evaluate the fusion between ML-Exo and DMVs, with Dil-labeled DMVs and PKH67-labeled ML-Exo subjected to fusion or non-fusion treatments, followed by imaging using a confocal microscope. For characterization, the morphologies of the formulations were observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and particle size distribution and zeta potential were analyzed using a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer (BeNano180ZetaPro). Additionally, SDS-PAGE and western blotting were performed to evaluate and compare the protein components in ML-Exo, DMVs, and MLE/DMVs.

Stability test

The stability of MLE/DMV was assessed by measuring particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) at various time points over a 7-day period. MLE/DMV samples were suspended in PBS containing 50 % FBS to mimic physiological conditions.

In vitro HA binding evaluation

To evaluate the bone-targeting ability of exosomes in vitro, the hydroxyapatite (HA)-binding assay was conducted. HA discs (Aladdin, China) with a diameter of 1.1 cm were used to assess the in vitro HA-binding affinity of exosomes from different groups. The HA discs were incubated with 2 mL of FITC-labeled ML-Exo, MLE/MVs, and MLE/DMVs for 12 h. After incubation, the HA discs were washed three times with ultrapure water, and fluorescence imaging of the HA discs was performed using an IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer Inc., USA).

Animal models and treatment

To investigate the therapeutic effects of exosomes from different groups, 8-week-old OVX mice were randomly assigned to receive different treatments. Exosome injections commenced in the second week following OVX surgery, administered every 5 days for a total of 30 days, at a dose of 100 μg per mice.

In vivo biodistribution study

To evaluate the biodistribution and targeting properties of ML-Exo, MLE/MVs, and MLE/DMVs in vivo, an animal-based fluorescence imaging technique was employed. A total of 100 μg of DiR-labeled exosomes from different groups were injected into the tail vein of mice in a 200 μL PBS solution. The biodistribution of exosomes in the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, and lower limb bones was observed and quantified. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an IVIS small animal imaging system (PerkinElmer Inc., USA). The relative fluorescence intensity was acquired and analyzed using IVIS Spectrum imaging software (PerkinElmer Inc., USA).

Micro-fil perfusion

To visualize the femoral vasculature in mice, microCT imaging was performed. Mice were euthanized, and the thoracic cavity was opened to expose the heart. A needle was inserted into the left ventricle, and the vasculature was flushed with heparinized saline (0.9 % normal saline containing 100 U ml−1 heparin sodium). Subsequently, the mice were perfused with 10 % formalin for fixation, followed by a final flush with heparinized saline. Radiopaque silicone compounds containing lead chromate (Microfil MV-122, Flow Tech) were then injected into the vascular system. The mice were preserved at 4 °C for 24 h, after which the femurs were removed, fixed, decalcified, and subjected to microCT imaging. The 3D images of the vasculature were reconstructed using CTVox software. The total vessel volume was calculated using CTAn software.

Histology, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

For histological analysis, fresh femurs were obtained from experimental and control groups, followed by fixation and subsequent decalcification using 0.5M EDTA (Servicebio, G1105) for 21 days. The specimens were then processed through an ethanol series for dehydration, followed by xylene clarification and paraffin embedding. Sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared for H&E staining (Solarbio, G1121). For immunofluorescence staining, the samples were dehydrated with 35 % sucrose and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek). Sections of 20 μm thickness were prepared for further staining. The staining protocol involved the following steps: sections were rinsed three times with PBS to remove OCT compound and treated with 0.1 % Triton X-100 (Biosharp, BS084) for 10 min. Subsequently, sections were incubated with 10 % donkey serum at room temperature for 1 h to block nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies were then applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were CD31 (R&D Systems, AF3628; 1:100), Endomucin (Santa Cruz, sc-65495; 1:100), and Ki67 (Abcam, ab15580; 1:1000). After washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. For immunohistochemistry, the sections were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the HRP-streptavidin detection kit (Report biotech; RK9000). Nuclei were counterstained with Mayer hematoxylin solution (Solarbio, G1080). Images were captured using a Nikon AX confocal microscope or Olympus BX 51 microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry assay

To quantify the number of CD31hi and EMCNhi endothelial cells in bone, mice were euthanized at the end of the intervention, and femurs were carefully excised. Soft tissues were removed, and the metaphyseal and diaphyseal regions of the bones were ground in cold PBS. Then the bone fragments were then digested in a digestion buffer containing 3 mg/mL collagenase I (Worthington, LS004196), 4 mg/mL dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich, D4693), and 2 U/mL DNase I (Invitrogen, 18047019) at 37 °C for 25 min. The digested samples were filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to obtain a cell suspension. Red blood cells were lysed using a red blood cell lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation. Finally, the cells were resuspended in PBS containing 2 % FBS and incubated with antibodies. The antibodies used were as follows: APC-conjugated EMCN (eBioscience, 50-5851-80; 1:100), FITC-conjugated CD31 (BioLegend, 102405; 1:100), APC–Cy7-conjugated CD45 (BD Biosciences, 557659; 1:100), and Violet-eFluor 450-conjugated Ter119 (Tonbo, 75–5921; 1:100). Finally, the labeled cells were analyzed using a BD Celesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, America; 660344), and data were processed using FlowJo software.

μCT analyses

Following euthanasia, femurs were excised, surrounding soft tissues were removed, and the bones were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Subsequently, μCT scans were performed using a SkyScan 1176 scanner (Bruker, Luxembourg, Belgium). Image reconstruction was performed using NRecon software. For data analysis, CTAn software was utilized to evaluate parameters such as BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Sp. Representative images were generated using CTvox software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Based on the number of transfection groups, 293T cells were seeded into 24-well plates (6∗104 cells per well) one day before transfection. Cells were transfected when their density reached 50 %–70 %. For transfection, 50 μL of DMEM was mixed with 0.4 μg of WT/MUT plasmid and 1 μL of 20 pmol/μL mimics/negative control to prepare solution A. Then, 48 μL of DMEM was mixed with 2 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 reagent to prepare solution B. Solutions A and B were thoroughly mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The transfection mixture was added to the 24-well plate, gently mixed, and incubated in the culture chamber. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. Luciferase activity was measured 24–48 h after transfection.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism v10.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. All experiments were repeated at least three times, and a P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

ML-exo facilitated angiogenesis for activating pump system

Preosteoclasts, a non-bone-resorbing subtype within the osteoclast lineage, secrete extracellular vesicles (EVs) that have demonstrated the capacity to promote angiogenesis [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that cells exposed to mechanical loading (ML) can transmit mechanical signals via exosomal pathways, thereby modulating the biological behavior of target cells [23]. For example, ML has been shown to enhance the regenerative potential of mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes, thereby improving their role in maintaining extracellular matrix homeostasis and mitigating chondrocyte senescence [36]. Nonetheless, whether ML-Exo derived from POCs exhibit superior angiogenic activity remains to be determined.

Initially, the POCs were extracted and cultured. Bone marrow cells were isolated from the bilateral femurs of 8-week-old mice via flushing the marrow cavity, followed by culturing in BioFlex plates coated with type I collagen. These cells were stimulated with 30 ng/ml m-CSF for 72 h to generate bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM). Subsequently, BMDM were treated with 30 ng/ml m-CSF and 60 ng/ml Rankl for 72 h to induce POCs. TRAP staining was used to confirm successful induction of POCs (Fig. S1). Next, we subjected POCs to mechanical stimuli under varying loading conditions (intensity, frequency, duration) and treated HUVECs with different sets of conditional medium to identify the optimal mechanical loading parameters. Our results demonstrated that a loading regimen of 4 % strain amplitude at 0.5 Hz applied for 4 h most effectively enhanced tube formation and migration of HUVECs (Figs. S2–4). Consequently, this specific loading condition was adopted for subsequent experiments. Subsequently, experimental POCs were subjected to 4 h of mechanical cyclic sinusoidal tensile strain (4 % strain amplitude, 0.5 Hz), one group was treated with medium containing GW4869 to inhibit exosome secretion, while control cells were cultured in the bioreactor without mechanical loading for 4 h. After 4 h, all groups were replaced with fresh medium and further cultured for 48 h. The conditional medium was then collected, and co-cultured with HUVEC. Then the tube formation, migration assay, wound healing assay EdU staining assay were conducted to assess their angiogenic capacity. The mechanical loading conditional medium (ML-CM) exhibits superior potential in promoting the migration, tube formation, and proliferation of HUVECs compared to control conditional medium (Con-CM). Moreover, this effect was abolished upon pretreatment with GW4869, suggesting that exosomes played a critical role in this process (Figs. S5–S6).

Next, we isolated Con-Exo and ML-Exo derived from POCs according to the outlined experimental design (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, a series of experiments were conducted to characterize the exosomes. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the vesicles from both groups exhibited the characteristic cup-shaped double-membrane structure (Fig. 1B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) indicated that the vesicle diameters ranged from 50 to 120 nm (Fig. 1C). NanoFCM further confirmed the expression of exosomal protein markers CD9 and CD81 (Fig. 1D). Additionally, PKH67-labeled exosomes could be uptake by HUVECs (Fig. 1E). To investigate the role of ML-Exo in HUVEC migration, proliferation, and tube formation, HUVECs were pretreated with PBS, Con-Exo (100 μg/ml), or ML-Exo (100 μg/ml). First, a Transwell assay was conducted to analyze changes in HUVEC migratory capacity. As shown in Fig. 1F and G, both Con-Exo and ML-Exo promoted HUVEC migration; however, ML-Exo exhibited a more pronounced effect, significantly enhancing the migratory capacity of HUVECs. Furthermore, a wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the role of exosomes in wound healing. At 24 h, wound closure was observed to varying degrees across all groups, with the ML-Exo group demonstrating the highest degree of wound closure (Fig. 1J–H). Additionally, EdU staining was used to assess HUVEC proliferation. Compared to Con-Exo, pretreatment with ML-Exo resulted in a significant increase in the number of EdU-positive nuclei, indicating that ML-Exo possesses a stronger capacity to promote HUVEC proliferation (Fig. 1I–L). Moreover, tube formation analysis revealed that ML-Exo exhibited superior performance in terms of tube length and the number of junctions (Fig. 1K–M). qPCR revealed the expression of angiogenesis marker genes, including VEGF, ANGPT1 and HIF1A significantly increased in the ML-Exo group (Fig. 1N).

Mechanical stimulation enhances exosome secretion and alters their cargo, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways that are crucial for bone repair, immune regulation, and microbial homeostasis. Mechanical loading can also enhance the function of exosomes from preosteoclasts. These results confirm the successful isolation of ML-Exo and demonstrate its superior angiogenic capacity compared to Con-Exo, thereby serving as a critical component to activate the “pump system”.

Preparation and characterization of MLE/DMV

Exosomes play a critical role in intercellular communication. However, their low targeting efficiency limits the functional effectiveness [21]. Engineering modifications of exosomes represent a key strategy to enhance their functional efficiency [37]. Cellular membranes, with their excellent biocompatibility and ability to retain donor cell characteristics, are widely utilized for in vivo drug delivery [38]. In this study, we developed an “mechanical energy-charged navigating pump” (MLE/DMV) through membrane fusion technology. First, bone marrow endothelial cells (BMECs) were isolated using previously described methods [35], and membrane vesicles (MVs) were prepared via ultrasonic disruption and extrusion. MVs exhibited a circular and bilayered membrane structure with an average size of 139.12 nm (Fig. 2B) and a zeta potential of −19.8 mV (Fig. 2E). The CXCR4 protein on their surface conferred them with inherent bone-targeting activity. To further enhance their “navigation” functionality, we synthesized (DSS)6 peptide and co-incubated it with MVs to generate DMVs. The successful synthesis of DSPE-PEG2000-(DSS)6 was confirmed by the nuclear magnetic resonance spectrum (Fig. S7). Fluorescence co-localization of FITC-labeled (DSS)6 peptide and Dil-labeled MVs confirmed the successful coupling of (DSS)6 peptide to MVs (Fig. 2C and D). DMVs retained a circular shape, with an average size of 154.38 nm and a zeta potential of −20.5 mV. Subsequently, the suspension of ML-Exo and DMVs was subjected to ultrasound fusion and co-extrusion to achieve membrane fusion, resulting in MLE/DMV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that MLE/DMV exhibited a spherical structure with a lipid bilayer membrane, an average size of 121.83 nm, and a zeta potential of −14.5 mV. Fluorescence co-localization of Dil-labeled DMVs and PKH67-labeled ML-Exo demonstrated successful fusion between the two components (Fig. 2F and G). Compared to simple physical mixing of ML-Exo and DMVs, MLE/DMV exhibited significantly increased fluorescence overlap. The total protein content of MLE/DMV was analyzed via SDS-PAGE. The results clearly demonstrated that MLE/DMV exhibited a combined protein profile characteristic of both DMVs and ML-Exo (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, Western blotting was employed to evaluate characteristic protein markers. Positive expression of the exosome markers TSG101, CD81, and CD9 was observed in both ML-Exo and MLE/DMV. Additionally, VEGFR2 and CXCR4 from DMVs were also detected in MLE/DMV. These protein analyses confirm that ML-Exo and DMVs have successfully merged, resulting in a fused MLE/DMV that retains the characteristic proteins of both components (Fig. 2I). Finally, the stability of MLE/DMV was assessed by suspending it in PBS containing 50 % FBS for 7 days, with particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) measured throughout the experiment. Minimal changes in size and PDI were observed, indicating that MLE/DMV exhibits high stability (Fig. 2J).

In summary, we successfully synthesized MLE/DMV by fusing DMVs and ML-Exo. DMVs, acting as navigators, first guide the system into bone tissue, achieving “soil entry,” and then precisely localize to the “vascular root” by leveraging homologous chemotaxis mediated by endothelial cell membranes. Meanwhile, ML-Exo, as the core of the pump system, plays a critical role in “vascular irrigation”. Moreover, the biosafety of MLE/DMV in vivo is guaranteed by its composition, which consists entirely of biological components. Therefore, MLE/DMV is expected to exert enhanced functionality in vivo.

MLE/DMV equipped with a navigator is capable of efficiently initiating the pump system

Precise targeting capability is a prerequisite for exosomes to exert their therapeutic functions via systemic circulation. Furthermore, due to the inherent complexity of cellular components within the bone microenvironment, the ability of exosomes to specifically recognize and bind to target cells is critical for achieving therapeutic efficacy [39]. Therefore, we systematically investigated the targeting capability and cellular uptake efficiency of engineered MLE/DMV in vitro.

To evaluate bone-targeting ability, hydroxyapatite (HA) binding assays were conducted. FITC-labeled MLE/DMV was incubated with HA discs for 12 h, and fluorescence intensity was quantified to assess HA binding capacity. The results demonstrated that MLE/DMV exhibited significantly stronger fluorescence intensity compared to other groups, indicating enhanced HA binding ability (Fig. 3A–D). Given that HA is the main inorganic component of bone tissue, the superior performance of MLE/DMV can be attributed to the successful incorporation of the (DSS)6 peptide, which imparts bone-targeting properties. To further assess cellular uptake efficiency, PKH67-labeled MLE/DMV was co-cultured with HUVECs. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. Fluorescence imaging demonstrated that MLE/MV and MLE/DMV exhibited greater accumulation in HUVECs (Fig. 3B–E). Flow cytometry further demonstrated that MLE/MV and MLE/DMV were more efficiently internalized by endothelial cells (Fig. 3C–F). These findings confirm that endothelial membrane modification enhances HUVEC uptake due to its homologous chemotaxis. However, the bone microenvironment is complex and not limited to a single-cell context. Thus, to simulate the in vitro bone microenvironment, a co-culture system was established using DiD-labeled MC3T3 cells and Dil-labeled HUVECs. PKH67-labeled MLE/DMV was co-cultured with both cell types, and fluorescence-based flow cytometry was used to quantify exosomes uptake (Fig. 3G). The results demonstrated that HUVECs exhibited significantly higher uptake of MLE/DMV compared to MC3T3 cells, highlighting the selective targeting of MLE/DMV to endothelial cells within a multi-cellular context (Fig. 3H). Functional assays were then performed to evaluate the effects of exosomes in different groups on HUVEC tube formation, migration, and proliferation. Tube formation assays revealed that MLE/DMV and MLE/MV induced more distinct vascular-like network structures compared to exosome without endothelial cell membrane modification, with quantitative analysis showing increased tube junctions and total tube length (Fig. 3I and J). Transwell assays further demonstrated that MLE/DMV and MLE/MV significantly enhanced HUVEC migratory capacity (Fig. 3K and L). EdU assays indicated that MLE/DMV and MLE/MV promoted HUVEC proliferation (Fig. 3M and N). In contrast to traditional single-level targeting strategies, such as bisphosphonate modification, the multi-targeting functionality of MLE/DMV enables superior efficacy. Bone is a highly vascularized tissue, and bone vasculature is indispensable for maintaining bone homeostasis. The aging and dysfunction of vasculature are key contributors to osteoporosis [41]. H type vessels, an important vascular subtype, mediate the differentiation of perivascular osteoprogenitor cells and couple angiogenesis with osteogenesis [[42], [43], [44]]. During osteoporosis, the number of H type vessels significantly decreases [45,46]. Therefore, modulating vascular generation represents an important approach to alleviate osteoporosis.

To investigate the effects of ML-Exo, MLE/MV, and MLE/DMV on bone vasculature during osteoporosis, 8-week-old female mice were subjected to OVX surgery. Starting from the second week post-surgery, the mice were administered different groups of exosomes via the tail vein every 5 days for a total of 30 days, followed by relevant experiments (Fig. 4G). First, after Micro-fil perfusion, the femurs of mice were decalcified and subjected to μCT scanning to observe intramedullary vascular distribution, followed by three-dimensional vascular reconstruction and quantitative analysis. The results demonstrated that bone vasculature was significantly reduced in OVX mice, and administration of ML-Exo, MLE/MV, and MLE/DMV all delayed the progression of vascular reduction, with MLE/DMV showing the most pronounced effect (Fig. 4H and I). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of EMCN+Ki67+ revealed that MLE/DMV maximally promoted the proliferation of endothelial cells beneath the growth plate in osteoporotic femurs (Fig. 4J and K). Additionally, changes in H type vessel numbers were assessed using IF and flow cytometry. IF results showed that the CD31hiEMCNhi area was highest in the MLE/DMV group (Fig. 4L and M). Then the flow cytometry results were analyzed according to the described gating strategy (Fig. S8) and flow cytometry also demonstrated the most significant increase in H type vessel numbers in this group (Fig. 4N and O).

The results above demonstrate that MLE/DMV, acting as an “mechanical energy-charged navigating pump”, precisely targets the area near the epiphyseal plate, providing precise irrigation to the surrounding vascular network. This achieves a dual-stage spatial delivery system, thereby promoting vascular growth under osteoporotic conditions. Vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients to surrounding tissues, improving the microenvironment and regulating the behavior of osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Additionally, biomolecule in the microenvironment promote further vascularization, forming a “natural vascular scaffold” that holds promise for slowing the progression of osteoporosis.

Vascular endothelial cells act as a hub for molecular crosstalk among various cell populations in the bone microenvironment by regulating the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts through intercellular communication [[47], [48], [49]]. The vasculature system is a highly active metabolic and endocrine organ that regulates skeletal growth balance through a series of complex signaling pathways. To maintain bone homeostasis, osteogenesis and angiogenesis are coupled in a spatiotemporally dependent manner: osteoblasts and osteoclasts secrete angiogenic factors to promote vascular growth and guide its function, while endothelial cells release secretory signals to regulate bone remodeling and repair [50]. Natural vascular scaffolds are defined as innate structures formed by osteogenesis-associated vascular subtypes, including H type vessels. While their function in promoting cell migration and differentiation is analogous to that of traditional metal/polymer scaffolds, a key advantage of natural vascular scaffolds is the elimination of rejection risks. Additionally, natural vascular scaffolds facilitate a more prolonged duration of action and hierarchical regulation through the modulation of the bone microenvironment.

MicroRNAs are small, single-stranded non-coding RNAs present in various organisms, and the post-transcriptional regulation they mediate is considered one of the most critical cellular regulatory mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that microRNAs are involved in the regulation of angiogenesis and osteogenesis coupling [51,52]. Additionally, exosomes serve as a key medium for microRNA-mediated long-range regulation, and their composition and cargo are modulated by mechanical loading, resulting in distinct biological effects [53]. These findings confirm that mechanical loading enhances the expression of miR-25-3p in ML-Exo, acting as a core functional element of MLE/DMV and playing a crucial role in promoting angiogenesis.

miR-25-3p exhibited a therapeutic effect via targeting CUX1

To investigate the potential mechanisms by which miR-25-3p carried by MLE/DMV promotes angiogenesis in HUVECs, we utilized the TargetScan, miRDB, and miRWalk databases to identify target genes of miR-25-3p (Fig. 7A). Among the candidate genes, CUX1 exhibited a strong correlation with endothelial cell senescence, leading us to select it as a target gene [54]. Previous studies have shown that CUX1 plays a critical role in regulating the replicative and stress-induced senescence of endothelial cells by modulating the expression of p16INK4a, thereby promoting the development of atherosclerosis [55,56]. Therefore, targeting CUX1 represents a promising approach to alleviate vascular degeneration. Based on the potential binding sites of miR-25-3p, WT CUX1 and mutant MUT CUX1 constructs were established (Fig. 7B). Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that the relative luciferase activity of WT CUX1 was significantly different compared to the NC group, while MUT CUX1 showed no significant difference compared to the NC group (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, qPCR results demonstrated that transfection of HUVECs with miR-25-3p mimics reduced CUX1 expression, whereas transfection with inhibitors increased CUX1 expression (Fig. 7D). These findings collectively confirmed that CUX1 is a direct target of miR-25-3p. To further explore the relationship between miR-25-3p and CUX1, in vitro rescue experiments were conducted. HUVECs were co-transfected with miR-25-3p inhibitors and either siNC or siCUX1, and CUX1 expression levels were analyzed via qPCR. Compared to HUVEC transfected with miR-25-3p inhibitors and siNC, cells transfected with miR-25-3p inhibitors and siCUX1 exhibited significantly reduced CUX1 expression (Fig. 7E). Additionally, the expression of angiogenesis-related genes, including VEGF, ANGPT1, and HIF1A, was significantly higher in the miR-25-3p inhibitor and siCUX1 group compared to the miR-25-3p inhibitor and siNC group. Silencing CUX1 rescued the reduced angiogenic capacity caused by miR-25-3p inhibitors (Fig. 7F). EdU staining revealed that the miR-25-3p and siCUX1 group exhibited enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. 7G–J). Transwell and wound healing assays demonstrated that inhibiting CUX1 offset the negative effects of miR-25-3p on HUVEC migration (Fig. 7I–K, L, M). These results demonstrate that inhibiting CUX1 in HUVECs can counteract the negative effects of miR-25-3p inhibitors on cell proliferation and angiogenesis. In conclusion, miR-25-3p in MLE/DMV can be directly transferred into HUVECs, promoting angiogenesis by silencing CUX1, thereby exerting therapeutic effects. This suggests that CUX1 represents a potential therapeutic target for the clinical treatment of osteoporosis.

Conclusion

In this study, the Mechanical Energy-Charged Navigating Pump exerts its osteoporosis-relieving effects by remodeling the natural vasculature scaffold, thereby improving the bone microenvironment and restoring intercellular communication. Upon entering the circulation, the MLE/DMV utilizes its surface CXCR4 protein, (DSS)6 peptide, and endothelial cell membrane for precise navigation to the bone vasculature. Subsequently, the ML-Exo act as the core of MLE/DMV delivers mechanical signals via miR-25-3p to target the CUX1 in endothelial cells, thereby regulating vasculature formation and intercellular communication. The vasculature facilitates the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to tissues, improving the bone microenvironment and intercellular communication to alleviate osteoporosis. Based on these findings, we propose that precise mechanical signal transduction to regulate endothelial cell behavior represents a novel therapeutic perspective for osteoporosis treatment. With a more refined understanding of mechanotransduction pathways, future research holds promise for the development of intelligent drug delivery systems based on mechanical energy modulation, enabling more precise and personalized remodeling of the bone microenvironment. Furthermore, the endogenous regulatory mechanisms underlying this strategy offer favorable biosafety and biocompatibility, laying a solid foundation for the development of anti-osteoporosis therapies with minimal side effects and long-term efficacy. This mechanoregulatory approach, which targets the vascular microenvironment, may also be extendable to other vasculature-related bone diseases, such as osteonecrosis and impaired bone healing, demonstrating broad translational potential. Key limitations of this study include the OVX mouse model’s potential inability to fully mimic human conditions, the assessment of only short-term efficacy without post-cessation data, and complex nanoparticle preparation that challenges scalable production. Future work requires larger animal models, advanced microenvironment simulation, and scalable manufacturing.
