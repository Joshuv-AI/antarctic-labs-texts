Cardiovascular Health in Sexual Minority Adults: Life's Essential 8 in The PRIDE Study
Authors: Nicole Rosendale, Andrew J. Wood, Billy A. Caceres, Carl G. Streed, Rose Wong, Annesa Flentje, Juno Obedin‐Maliver, Mitchell R. Lunn
Published: 2026

A 2020 American Heart Association scientific statement “Assessing and Addressing Cardiovascular Health in LGBTQ Adults” emphasized the need for cardiovascular health (CVH) studies in sexual and gender minority communities. 1 This cross‐sectional study evaluated CVH across sexual orientations (SO) using the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 (LE8) score within The PRIDE (Population Research in Identity and Disparities for Equality) Study. 2

Between May 5 and July 21, 2023, The PRIDE Study participants were invited to complete the “Heart Health Study” questionnaire. This 35‐ to 57‐item questionnaire (depending on skip logic) collected demographics, cardiometabolic history, and LE8 components (questionnaire and data available upon request from qualified investigators at https://pridestudy.org/collaborate). SO and gender identity were self‐reported and linked from the most recent annual questionnaire.

The primary outcome was the overall LE8 score (0–100, higher values indicating more favorable CVH). Secondary outcomes included scores for LE8 components, categorized as low (0–49), moderate (50–79), or high (80–100) following American Heart Association recommendations.

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics across SO. LE8 components were compared using chi‐square tests (permutation chi‐square where appropriate for small cell sizes). Missing data were addressed through multiple imputation by chained equations (50 imputations) and ANCOVA tested LE8 scores (alpha <0.05, Stata 18) for each group in 4 models: unadjusted; age adjusted; age and sex adjusted; and age, sex, and gender identity adjusted. Sensitivity analyses included complete‐case evaluations. The institutional review boards at the University of California San Francisco, Stanford University, and WIRB‐Copernicus Group (WCG) approved this study. All participants provided informed consent.

Among 4670 adults (median age 36.4 years; interquartile range, 28.9–51.9), participants identified as gay/lesbian (41.8%), bisexual/pansexual (24.7%), queer (22.6%), asexual (8.9%), straight (1.3%), and another SO (0.7%). Most respondents were White and had access to their health records. LE8 component missingness was highest for lipids (64.2%) and lowest for glucose (2.4%; FigureFigure1). Participants with missing components were younger (median 35.3 years, interquartile range, 28.2–50.1 versus 39.1 years, interquartile range, 30.6–54.9).

Mean overall LE8 scores indicated moderate CVH across SO, ranging from 68.2 (95% CI, 63.4–73.0) in those with another SO to 74.4 (95% CI, 73.1–75.6) in asexual adults. Adjusting for age, sex, and gender identity, and the complete‐case analysis yielded comparable findings.

Across components, participants had favorable glucose, physical activity, and sleep scores but unfavorable body mass index and diet scores. Significant differences emerged across SO (Figure1). Unfavorable body mass index scores were common among asexual (41.1%), bisexual/pansexual (46.1%), and queer (40.7%) participants. Diet scores were least favorable among asexual (39.6%) and bisexual/pansexual (39.3%) participants. Gay/lesbian participants had unfavorable blood pressure scores (15.5%). Bisexual/pansexual (19.9%) participants had a high prevalence of unfavorable nicotine scores.

In this cross‐sectional investigation, sexual and gender minority adults exhibited moderate CVH across SO categories, paralleling the general US population. Differences in LE8 components suggest community‐specific disparities. Similar to data from the 2013 to 2018 NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey), 3 our cohort's mean scores (68.2 to 74.4) reflect midrange CVH.

Our study described CVH patterns in groups not studied in NHANES, namely asexual, pansexual, and queer individuals. Asexual participants showed a mixed profile of unfavorable body mass index and diet but favorable nicotine and glucose scores. Queer participants demonstrated favorable sleep and glucose scores, whereas bisexual/pansexual participants had more unfavorable patterns across several components, potentially reflecting unique stressors experienced by this group. 4 Further research should explore whether similar mechanisms contribute to disparities in other underrepresented sexual minority populations.

The prevalence of missing lipid and blood pressure data, despite patient portal access, may indicate limited preventive screening. Guidelines recommend lipid screening begin between ages 20 and 35 and hypertension screening from age 18. Given the sample's median age of 36, many should have received such evaluations. Sexual and gender minority individuals may avoid or delay care out of fear of discrimination or due to restrictive health policies. 5 Ensuring access to affirming health care is therefore essential.

Diet quality was unfavorable across all groups, consistent with prior findings. 3 Food insecurity disproportionately affects sexual minority adults and may contribute to these outcomes. Most published interventions to improve diet quality do not investigate differences across sexual orientations. 1 Future interventions must address the multilevel contributors to poor diet quality in sexual minority adults.

This study's limitations include (1) sampling bias due to convenience sampling, (2) misclassification bias due to self‐reported data, (3) response bias due to lack of verification of portal access in reporting health factors, (4) high missingness of some LE8 variables, and (5) limited generalizability in the primarily White, English‐speaking sample. Future research should assess intersectionality with sociodemographic factors. Although not a limitation, straight participants in this study were transgender or gender diverse, precluding direct comparison with cisgender, straight populations.

In conclusion, overall CVH among sexual and gender minority adults was moderate across sexual orientations, similar to the overall US population. Disparities in specific risk factors underscore the need for affirming, community‐specific interventions. Longitudinal research is needed to identify causal pathways and inform strategies to promote CVH equity across these communities.
