Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis‐like syndrome after CD19‐directed CAR T‐cells for B‐cell lymphoma and B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia: A LYSA, SFCE, and GRAALL study from the DESCAR‐T registry
Authors: Nicolas Gower, Roch Houot, Cécile Pizot, André Baruchel, Marie‐Emilie Dourthe, David Beauvais, Thomas Gastinne, Gabriel Brisou, Rémy Dulery, Stéphanie Guidez, Jean‐Jacques Tudesq, Adrien Chauchet, Laura Herbreteau, Arnaud Campidelli, Jacques‐Olivier Bay, Pierre Sesques, Edmond Chiche, François‐Xavier Gros, Amandine Durand, Marion Lubnau, Arthur Sterin, Roberta Di Blasi, Alexandra Marquet, Elodie Gat, Florence Rabian, Thibaut Leguay, Gabrielle Roth‐Guépin, Nicolas Boissel, Franck Morschhauser, Jérôme Paillassa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) are well‐recognized toxicities of CAR T‐cell therapy, immune effector cell‐associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis‐like syndrome (IEC‐HS) remains poorly characterized. We aimed to describe the incidence, clinical features, management, and outcomes of IEC‐HS using real‐world data from the DESCAR‐T registry (NCT04328298). We conducted a multicenter retrospective study of patients registered in DESCAR‐T who received standard‐of‐care CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy for relapsed or refractory B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (B‐NHL) or B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) and fulfilled ASTCT criteria for IEC‐HS. We analyzed 42 patients who developed IEC‐HS. Most had a high CAR‐HEMATOTOX score (≥2, n = 36) and prior high‐grade CRS (grade ≥3, n = 20). IEC‐HS was characterized by marked hyperinflammation, with high median ferritin (18,561 µg/L), lactate dehydrogenase (825 IU/L), and triglycerides (4.5 g/L), and low fibrinogen (0.9 g/L). Median time to IEC‐HS onset was 9 days (IQR 6–21). Patients received a median of two treatment lines. Observed response rates were 77% with etoposide, 70% with corticosteroids, 70% with corticosteroids plus anakinra, and 50% with tocilizumab, with combination therapy more frequently used in severe cases. After a median follow‐up of 24.1 months, overall mortality was 81%. One‐year overall survival was 15.6% for B‐NHL and 47.1% for B‐ALL. In this real‐world cohort, IEC‐HS was a rare but life‐threatening complication of CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy, often occurring after severe CRS and associated with poor outcomes. Despite initial inflammatory control, mortality remained high, underscoring the need for improved risk stratification and management strategies.

INTRODUCTION

CAR T‐cell therapy has emerged as a pivotal treatment for patients with relapsed or refractory B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (B‐NHL) and B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The remarkable efficacy of anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cells in these populations led to their approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA).

However, despite these clear benefits, anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cells are associated with significant adverse effects, with Cytokine Release Syndrome (CRS) and Immune Effector Cell‐Associated Neurotoxicity Syndrome (ICANS) being the most notable and well‐characterized complications. 5 Immune Effector Cell Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis‐like Syndrome (IEC‐HS) is another adverse event of CAR T‐cell, but less common than CRS and ICANS. Data on IEC‐HS is limited. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Both CRS and IEC‐HS are characterized by hyperinflammatory conditions that can range from mild fever to life‐threatening multi‐organ failure, sharing clinical and biological features such as fever, cytopenia, hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, and hypertriglyceridemia. However, the traditional diagnostic scores for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, such as the HLH‐2004 score and the H‐Score, are not well‐suited for these contexts. 11 Recently, the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) proposed new diagnostic criteria for IEC‐HS, including markedly elevated ferritin levels (≥10,000 µg/L), coagulopathy, cytopenia, and multi‐organ dysfunction 7 (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Although CRS and IEC‐HS share some characteristics, they appear to be distinct conditions. IEC‐HS typically emerges later, around Day 14 postinfusion, with higher ferritin levels and elevated IL‐1 and IFNγ levels compared to those in CRS. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 , 13 Drivers of IEC‐HS associated with CAR T‐cell therapy, its management, and patient outcomes remain unclear. 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 Here, patients with IEC‐HS were investigated using real‐world data from the DESCAR‐T registry (NCT04328298), describing the clinical and biological characteristics, treatment approaches, and outcomes of these patients with B‐ALL or B‐NHL.

METHODS

Study design

The DESCAR‐T registry is a French national study including all patients eligible for commercially available CAR T‐cell therapies in the context of hematologic malignancies. Classified as Research Not Involving the Human Person under French law, DESCAR‐T is registered under the ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04328298 and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were individually informed upon inclusion in the registry. For this study, patients with IEC‐HS were identified from the DESCAR‐T registry based on the data export of September 2023. In addition, a few extra cases were identified between September 2023 and September 2024, in participating centers with identified IEC‐HS cases. Eligible patients were those with a diagnosis of IEC‐HS, treated for relapsed or refractory B‐cell neoplasia (B‐ALL or B‐NHL) with standard‐of‐care CD19 CAR T‐cell and registered in DESCAR‐T. Studied CD19 CAR T‐cells were axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel), tisagenlecleucel (tisa‐cel), lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso‐cel), and brexucabtagene autoleucel (brexu‐cel). Data collected from DESCAR‐T included baseline patient and disease characteristics, type of cellular therapy, biological data at lymphodepletion (LD) (LDH, triglyceride, fibrinogen, hemoglobin levels, platelet, and absolute neutrophil counts, ASAT, ALAT, and bilirubin), toxicities, and treatment outcomes. Additional biological and clinical data were retrospectively collected at IEC‐HS onset by contacting all participating centers with IEC‐HS cases.

Definition of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis‐like syndrome (IEC‐HS)

Cases of IEC‐HS occurring between the day of CAR T‐cell infusion and the last follow‐up were extracted. Each IEC‐HS case was reviewed and confirmed by each center and centrally reviewed according to the ASTCT criteria published by Hines et al. 7 (Supporting Information S1: Table S1) and the revised version of the ASTCT criteria. This revised version included all IEC‐HS after CAR T‐cell infusion, and the attribution criterion (“is attributable to CAR T‐cell therapy”) was not applied, allowing inclusion of cases with concurrent triggers to reflect real‐world practice. All HLH‐like events occurring after CAR T‐cell infusion were included, including those associated with infection, disease progression, and/or CRS, because the etiology of IEC‐HS is difficult to establish and is often multifactorial. All patients who developed IEC‐HS after CAR T‐cell infusion were included, even if it occurred late at the time of relapse.

Definition of response to IEC‐HS treatment

Complete response (CR) was defined as normalization of all quantifiable symptoms and biomarkers of IEC‐HS. Partial response (PR) was defined as at least a 25% improvement in two or more quantifiable symptoms and biomarkers. Overall response rate (ORR) was defined as the sum of CR and PR.

Statistical analyses

Quantitative variables were summarized in tables displaying the number of observations, median, quartiles (Q1 and Q3), or range, and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Qualitative variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ 2 test. Response rates are expressed as percentages with their 95% Exact Clopper Pearson confidence interval (CI). The median follow‐up throughout the study was calculated using a reverse Kaplan–Meier plot of time to death with 95% CIs. Median follow‐up from first infusion is presented. Overall survival (OS), progression‐free survival (PFS), and event‐free survival (EFS) were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method. OS (used for patients with B‐ALL and B‐NHL) was defined as the time between CAR T‐cell infusion and death from any cause. PFS (used for patients with B‐NHL) was defined as the time between CAR T‐cell infusion and first progression or death from any cause. EFS (used for patients with B‐ALL) was defined as the time between CAR T‐cell infusion and primary refractory disease confirmation (i.e., the date when failure to achieve a response to induction therapy was determined), relapse, second malignancy, or any death, whichever occurred first. Cumulative incidence of NRM was measured from the first infusion to the date of death without progression and not due to lymphoma/leukemia. Cumulative incidence of disease‐related mortality is measured from the first infusion to the date of death due to lymphoma or leukemia. Both are presented based on a competing risk analysis. The event rates together with 95% CIs, patients at risk, and censored at specific time points (e.g., at 12 months) were computed.

Continuous variables measured at lymphodepletion and at the time of IEC‐HS were compared using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. All outputs were produced using SAS version 9.4 and AdClin version 3.5.

RESULTS

Incidence and patients' characteristics

Among 2176 patients (137 B‐ALL and 2039 B‐NHL) treated with CD19 CAR T‐cell and registered in DESCAR‐T as of September 2023, 38 cases of IEC‐HS were identified according to ASTCT criteria, 7 including 11 with B‐ALL (incidence of 8.0%) and 27 with B‐NHL (incidence of 1.3%). These incidence estimates should be interpreted with caution, given the retrospective design and potential ascertainment bias related to case identification. Four additional cases (3 B‐ALL and 1 B‐NHL) were subsequently identified in participating centers with previously reported IEC‐HS cases, which may have led to preferential inclusion of centers with recognized IEC‐HS and to an enrichment in more severe or clinically overt cases, resulting in a total of 42 patients included in the analysis (14 B‐ALL and 28 B‐NHL). After case review, 26/42 (62%) fulfilled ASTCT criteria for IEC‐HS without other contributing factors such as progression or infection (10 B‐ALL and 16 B‐NHL) (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Median age at CAR T‐cell infusion was 18.5 years (range 1.0–45.0) in B‐ALL, and 61.5 years (range 33.0–76.0) in B‐NHL patients. B‐NHL subtypes included diffuse large B cell lymphoma (n = 12), transformed lymphoma (n = 10, 9 indolent and 1 Poppema), mantle cell lymphoma (n = 5), and one follicular lymphoma (Table 1). Most B‐ALL patients received tisa‐cel (n = 12, 85.7%), whereas B‐NHL patients were treated with axi‐cel (50%), tisa‐cel (32.1%), or brexu‐cel (17.9%) (Table 1).

Refractory after the first line of therapy.

The majority of patients had advanced‐stage disease, received multiple prior lines of therapy, and were primary refractory (B‐ALL) or refractory to their last treatment (B‐NHL) (Table 1). Most patients received bridging therapy, which was frequently ineffective in both B‐ALL and B‐NHL patients (Table 1). At lymphodepletion, and compared to DESCAR‐T patients, most patients exhibited a pronounced inflammatory profile characterized by elevated LDH, ferritin, and CRP levels (Table 1 and Figure 1), as reflected by a high CAR‐Hematotox score ≥2 (n = 36, 85.7%). Median CAR‐HEMATOTOX scores were comparable between B‐NHL and B‐ALL patients. No major differences in clinical or biological features were observed between patients fulfilling ASTCT criteria and those meeting the revised criteria (Supporting Information S1: Table S2).

Clinical and biological characteristics of patients at IEC‐HS onset

At IEC‐HS onset, fever was reported in 78.6% of patients (n = 33). Most patients (92.5%, n = 40) experienced CRS and/or ICANS prior to IEC‐HS onset, including 15 who developed both. Due to the retrospective design, we were not able to distinguish between new‐onset and persistent fever at IEC‐HS onset, which may limit the ability to differentiate IEC‐HS from ongoing CRS with HLH‐like manifestations. According to the physician declaration, the median time from CAR T‐cell infusion to IEC‐HS was 9 days (IQR 6–21), and the median time from prior CRS to IEC‐HS was 6.5 days (IQR 4–21). At the time of IEC‐HS diagnosis, CRS had fully resolved in 37.5% of patients (n = 15) (Table 2), highlighting an overlap between CRS and IEC‐HS with few resolved CRS before new clinical/biological worsening. The interval between CRS and IEC‐HS was calculated using the respective onset dates of each syndrome. As 62.5% of patients had ongoing CRS at IEC‐HS onset, the latter was defined based on clinical and biological criteria according to ASTCT definitions. The median time from CAR T‐cell infusion to IEC‐HS onset was longer in B‐NHL than in B‐ALL patients (13.5 vs. 6 days, P < 0.001). In addition, CRS was more frequently resolved at IEC‐HS onset in B‐NHL compared with B‐ALL patients (48.1% vs. 15.4%, P = 0.045).

Bone marrow trephine biopsy/aspirate and/or tissue biopsy were performed in 47.6% of cases (n = 20), revealing hemophagocytosis in half of the evaluated cases. According to ASTCT grading, most patients (n = 24, 57.1%) experienced severe IEC‐HS (grade ≥3) (Table 2). In 61.9% of cases, CAR T‐cell therapy was the only contributing factor (n = 26). In the remaining patients, severe CRS, early disease progression, or refractory disease, and infections were considered as possible contributors (Table 2). When comparing patients fulfilling strict ASTCT criteria with those meeting modified criteria, the only observed difference was a longer time to IEC‐HS onset in B‐NHL patients with additional contributing factors (Supporting Information S1: Table S3).

Biological parameters at IEC‐HS onset significantly differed from those measured at LD, with significant increases in ferritin and LDH levels and decreases in fibrinogen, hemoglobin, platelet counts, and absolute neutrophil count (Figure 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table S4). Most patients showed an inflammatory profile at LD with a high median ferritin level of 2341 µg/L (IQR 1500–4145) for B‐ALL and 1572 µg/L (IQR 846–2906) for B‐NHL. Ferritin level was significantly higher at IEC‐HS onset than at LD (P < 0.0001), with a median of 55,000 µg/L at IEC‐HS onset (IQR 18,612‐87,147) for B‐ALL patients and 17,061 µg/L (IQR 7035–30,177) for B‐NHL patients. At IEC‐HS onset, high levels of triglyceride (median of 4.5 g/L for both B‐ALL and B‐NHL patients) and LDH (median of 1741 and 644 UI/L for B‐ALL and B‐NHL, respectively) but low fibrinogen levels (median of 0.8 and 0.9 g/L for B‐ALL and B‐NHL, respectively) were observed. At IEC‐HS onset, patients exhibited profound cytopenia (Figure 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table S4). These biological characteristics were also found in patients with IEC‐HS as per original ASTCT criteria (i.e., without other contributing factors) (data not shown). When comparing patients with B‐ALL and patients with B‐NHL, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was more frequent in B‐ALL patients at IEC‐HS onset (92.9% vs. 46.4%, P = 0.003) (Table 2).

Immune effector cell‐specific toxicities and infections

CAR T‐cell therapy was associated with high toxicity in our cohort. Twenty‐six patients (61.9%) required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), with a median stay of 6 days (range 2–33). One‐third of patients (n = 12, 28.6%) required vasopressors, and 5 patients (11.9%) had mechanical ventilation.

Most patients developed CRS prior to IEC‐HS (92.9% of B‐ALL and 96.4% of B‐NHL), with a median time between infusion and CRS of 2 days (range 1–9) (Table 3). High‐grade CRS (grade ≥3) was common, occurring in nearly half of patients (n = 20, 47.6%). ICANS prior to IEC‐HS onset were less frequent in B‐ALL patients (16.7%) than in B‐NHL (46.4%). When present, ICANS was severe (grade ≥3) for half of the cases, with a median onset of 8 days postinfusion (range 3–20).

Immune effector cell‐associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT) was common, with 76.2% of patients (n = 32) experiencing at least one grade ≥3 cytopenia. Grade ≥3 anemia occurred in 26 patients (61.9%) who frequently required red blood cell transfusion. Thirty patients (71.4%) had grade ≥3 neutropenia, of whom 24 (80.0%) required G‐CSF. Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia occurred in 28 patients (66.7%) who frequently required platelet transfusions. At 3 months, most patients alive still had persistent cytopenias. Overall, cytopenias appeared more frequent and prolonged in B‐NHL than in B‐ALL patients (Table 3).

Infectious complications were observed in 71.4% of patients (n = 30) during follow‐up, mainly grade ≥3 infections (n = 27, 90.0%). Infections were observed before IEC‐HS onset in 56.7% of patients and after onset in 66.7%, with an overlap of patients experiencing infections both before and after IEC‐HS (n = 7, 16.7%). Infection post‐IEC‐HS included bacterial infection (n = 12, 28.6%), fungal infection (n = 6, 14.3%), viral infection (n = 5, 11.9%), and microbiologically undocumented infections (n = 6, 14.3%). Fungal infections were invasive candidosis (n = 4, 66.7%) and invasive aspergillosis (n = 2, 33.3%). Viral infections were mainly due to cytomegalovirus (CMV; n = 3, 60.0%). Comprehensive data on infections are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S5 in the supplementary material.

IEC‐HS management (treatment and response)

Overall, 37 patients (88.1%) received at least one treatment for IEC‐HS, whereas five patients did not. Among untreated patients, four had low‐grade IEC‐HS (two grade 1 and two grade 2) and experienced a favorable outcome without specific therapy. Among treated patients, most (67.6%) required more than one line of treatment to manage IEC‐HS (Figure 2A). Patients receiving a single line of therapy (n = 12) had less severe IEC‐HS (8 grade 1–2, 66.7%), whereas those requiring ≥2 lines more often had grade ≥3 IEC‐HS (Figure 2). No statistically significant differences were observed between patients who received 0–1 line of therapy and those who received ≥2 lines across baseline characteristics, features at CAR T‐cell infusion, and clinical and biological characteristics of IEC‐HS, although these findings should be considered exploratory given the limited number of patients (Supporting Information S1: Table S8A and B).

(B) IEC‐HS severity (grade 1–2 vs. grade ≥3) according to the number of treatment lines (1 vs. ≥2). (C) Treatment used to manage IEC‐HS for B‐ALL, B‐NHL, and all patients. (D) Response to treatment used to manage IEC‐HS according to IEC‐HS severity/grade (grade 1–2, grade ≥3, and all patients grade 1–5), blue corresponding to response and red to failure.

Treatment intensity appeared to increase with IEC‐HS severity, with patients with low‐grade disease less frequently exposed to intensified immunosuppressive therapies such as anakinra, JAK inhibition, or siltuximab (Figure 2B–E). Corticosteroids were the most frequently used treatment, given to 71.4% (n = 30/42, Figure 2C) of patients for IEC‐HS and were associated with an ORR of 70% and a CR of 47% (Figure 2D). Combination therapy with Anakinra (anti‐IL1R) and corticosteroids was used in 24% of patients (n = 10) and was associated with ORR/CR of 70% and 40%, respectively. No patient received Anakinra as monotherapy.

Etoposide was administered to 31.0% of patients for IEC‐HS (n = 13) and was associated with an ORR/CR of 77% and 31%, respectively. Tocilizumab (anti‐IL6R) was specifically used for IEC‐HS in 28.6% (n = 12/42) of patients with observed ORR/CR rates of 50% and 25%, respectively. In patients with grade 1–2 IEC‐HS, etoposide, corticosteroids, and anakinra–dexamethasone were associated with high response rates (ORR 90%–100%). In contrast, responses appeared lower in patients with grade ≥3 IEC‐HS (Figure 2D). These findings should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size and the inability to perform formal comparisons between treatments. All IEC‐HS treatments and corresponding response rates are summarized in the supplementary material (Supporting Information S1: Table S6). The small number of patients precluded formal comparisons between patients with non‐revised ASTCT criteria and patients with revised ASTCT criteria in terms of response to IEC‐HS treatment.

Outcomes following CD19‐directed CAR T‐cell and IEC‐HS

For B‐NHL patients, the BORR to CAR T‐cell therapy was 60.7% with a best complete response rate (BCRR) of 42.9% and a median time to best response of 1.1 months (Figure 3). At 1 month, 39.3% of patients achieved a CR and 21.4% a PR. Three B‐NHL patients (10.7%) with ongoing IEC‐HS but without obvious disease progression died of infection before PET‐CT scan evaluation and were considered as nonresponders to CAR T‐cell. In B‐ALL patients (n = 9, 64.3%), most achieved a complete hematologic response with undetectable minimal residual disease (MRD) at 1 month, whereas 2 patients (14.3%) were refractory to CAR T‐cell therapy, and 2 patients died of infectious complications before response assessment.

Despite these early responses, long‐term outcomes were poor. With a median follow‐up (mFU) of 36.5 months since infusion, B‐ALL patients had a median EFS of 5.5 months (95% CI 0.9–11) and median OS of 9.0 months (95% CI 1.3–21.7) with 1‐year OS of 47.1% (95% CI 19.9–70.5) (Figure 3B,D). In B‐NHL patients, after an mFU of 24 months since infusion, median PFS and OS were 2.1 months (95% CI 1.1–2.9) and 3 months (95% CI 1.4–5.4), respectively, with 1‐year OS of 15.6% (95% CI 5–31.6) (Figure 3A,C). Survival events during FU are summarized in a swimmer plot in the supplementary material (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Survival outcomes appeared similar between patients fulfilling strict ASTCT criteria and those meeting revised criteria, although the sample size limited meaningful comparisons.

Overall mortality reached 81%. The main cause of death was disease progression (n = 23; 67.6%), infection (n = 6; 17.6%), including 1 invasive fungal infection, CAR‐T cells toxicity, including IEC‐HS (n = 3; 8.8%), or other reasons (n = 2; 5.9% [stroke n = 1, multiple organ failure n = 1]). For these patients, death was often multifactorial. IEC‐HS remained unresolved at the time of death in nearly one‐third of patients (28.6%). Among patients with unresolved IEC‐HS, the main cause of death was also disease progression for one‐third of them (n = 4/12), followed by infection (n = 3/12), and 3 patients experienced grade 5 IEC‐HS, including one patient who experienced grade 5 CRS, ICANS, and IEC‐HS despite Tocilizumab, dexamethasone, and anakinra, and died from multiple organ failure (Supporting Information S1: Table S7). Estimated 6‐month disease‐related mortality (DRM) and nonrelapse mortality (NRM) were 34.6% [95%CI 20.2%; 49.4%] and 24.1% [95% CI 12.3%; 38.1%], respectively (Figure 3G–H and Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). Infections accounted for a major proportion of NRM (55%), with 17.6% of patients succumbing to infections and 20% of patients who developed infections during follow‐up ultimately died (Supporting Information S1: Table S7).

IEC‐HS severity appeared to be associated with outcome. Among B‐NHL patients, those with high‐grade IEC‐HS had inferior OS compared with patients with grade 1–2 disease (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

This study represents one of the largest real‐world cohorts, including both B‐ALL and B‐NHL patients, treated with anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy who developed IEC‐HS. However, with only 42 patients, the study remains underpowered for most comparative analyses, and subgroup findings should be considered exploratory. Regarding incidence estimates, we acknowledge that the additional identification of four cases outside the September 2023 registry export may have introduced ascertainment bias, potentially favoring more clinically overt cases while missing less recognized presentations. Therefore, the reported incidence should be interpreted with caution. In this cohort, IEC‐HS was observed more frequently in B‐ALL than in B‐NHL. One possible explanation is the higher circulating tumor burden and more immediate antigen exposure in B‐ALL, which may lead to stronger CAR T‐cell activation and a more pronounced inflammatory response. However, given the limited sample size, this observation remains hypothesis‐generating and requires confirmation in larger studies.

Our findings highlight the rarity of IEC‐HS and its severity, with an 81% mortality rate that may impact expected OS. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Deaths were mainly related to disease progression or infectious complications; however, in many cases, these events occurred in the context of ongoing or unresolved IEC‐HS (28.6% of patients), highlighting the multifactorial nature of mortality in this population. In this context, NRM was strikingly high with a 6‐month cumulative incidence of 24.1%, mainly related to infections in this small population. NRM is 4 to 5‐fold higher than that observed in clinical trials and in retrospective data from meta‐analyses of patients treated with anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cell. 18 , 19 The progression‐related mortality rate of 68%, with a median OS of 3 months for B‐NHL and 9 months for B‐ALL, as well as the NRM rate, appears to be in line with prior survival data published for IEC‐HS patients. 8 , 12 , 20

In this cohort, patients developing IEC‐HS displayed several features known to be associated with both CAR T‐cell failure and severe toxicity, including high‐risk or refractory disease, advanced disease stage, and elevated inflammatory markers at LD with high CAR‐HEMATOTOX score (Table 1). 21 A marked temporal and clinical overlap was observed between CRS, ICANS, and IEC‐HS, with most IEC‐HS cases occurring after severe CRS. These findings support the hypothesis that IEC‐HS may belong to a broader spectrum of CAR T‐cell‐related hyperinflammatory toxicities, although this concept remains speculative and requires confirmation in future studies. This observation was supported by the high proportion of prior grade ≥3 CRS observed in IEC‐HS patients compared with the overall DESCAR‐T population, although this comparison should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size. However, specific risk factors for IEC‐HS remain unclear and warrant further investigation in larger cohorts. 8 , 10

Diagnosis of IEC‐HS remains challenging. We applied revised ASTCT criteria (Hines et al. 7 ) to capture a broad spectrum of IEC‐HS, including cases with possible contributing factors such as infection, disease progression, or severe CRS. This reflects real‐world practice, in which IEC‐HS is often multifactorial. In clinical practice, the diagnosis relies primarily on biomarkers, such as hyperferritinemia and severe cytopenia, often accompanied by organ dysfunctions. In our cohort, hyperferritinemia appeared as a key diagnostic feature, with ferritin levels frequently exceeding 10,000 µg/L, which may help differentiate IEC‐HS from CRS and could be significantly higher according to some studies. 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 In contrast, the inclusion of cytopenia in IEC‐HS diagnosis is challenging due to confounding factors, such as chemotherapy‐induced cytopenia, cytopenia related to the underlying disease, or ICAHT. Indeed, one‐third of patients in this IEC‐HS cohort exhibited severe and prolonged cytopenia. IEC‐HS occurred early after infusion, with a median onset around Day 9, which may reflect increased awareness and earlier recognition compared with previous reports. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 , 13 When comparing patients fulfilling strict ASTCT criteria with those classified under the revised criteria, the main difference observed was a longer time to IEC‐HS onset in B‐NHL patients. This may reflect the inclusion of cases occurring in the context of concurrent contributing factors, particularly disease progression or infection, which often arise later after CAR T‐cell infusion. Some of these presentations may overlap with malignancy‐associated HLH and could have implications for treatment strategies, including consideration of etoposide‐based approaches. However, these observations remain exploratory and should be interpreted with caution.

Management of IEC‐HS remains challenging due to limited retrospective and no prospective data on the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressive therapies. Given the small number of patients in our registry‐based study, no comparative analysis could be performed regarding the optimal therapeutic strategy for IEC‐HS. The key is balancing symptom control to prevent multiorgan failure, preserving CAR T‐cell viability and expansion, and minimizing immunosuppressant toxicity, particularly infection risk. The distinct immune and cytokine profiles, based on the presumed key role of IL‐1 and IFNγ rather than IL‐6, suggest that corticosteroids and/or Anakinra, commonly used in severe CAR T‐cell complications, may represent a reasonable first‐line option in this setting. 9 , 10 , 13 , 22 However, patients receiving combination immunosuppressive therapies generally had more severe disease, underscoring substantial indication bias. Moreover, the poor prognosis of this population, partly due to high relapse rates, may suggest inadequate disease control, potentially exacerbated by multiple immunosuppressants. Indeed, the use of high‐dose corticosteroids or Anakinra may affect CAR T‐cell persistence and thus the risk of relapse. Moreover, accumulated immunosuppression, alongside IEC‐HS‐ or chemotherapy‐induced cytopenia, contributes to high NRM, particularly from infections. Beyond bacterial infections, there is a high proportion of fungal (e.g., invasive aspergillosis and invasive candidosis) and viral (e.g., CMV) infections both before and after IEC‐HS onset. Evaluating letermovir for primary CMV prevention, systematic mold‐active prophylaxis (posaconazole or voriconazole) for invasive fungal infection, and early polyvalent immunoglobulin substitution for encapsulated bacteria may help mitigate infection‐related mortality.

Based on our observations, existing ASTCT and EBMT algorithms, 7 , 23 , 24 and current pathophysiological insights 9 , 10 , 13 and case reports, 6 , 8 , 12 , 22 , 25 we propose a pragmatic therapeutic algorithm for IEC‐HS management (Figure 4). This algorithm should be viewed as a hypothesis‐generating framework rather than a guideline and requires prospective validation. Emerging therapies targeting IFNγ, such as emapalumab, are biologically plausible, but data remain insufficient to support their routine use in this setting. 9 , 10 , 13 , 22

Although this study is one of the largest cohorts examining IEC‐HS, it has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including the relatively small number of patients and marked heterogeneity in patients' characteristics and CAR T‐cell products, precluding formal comparison with patients treated with CAR T‐cell but without an IEC‐HS diagnosis. Although no matched control group was available, descriptive comparisons with the overall DESCAR‐T population suggested that patients developing IEC‐HS more frequently had refractory disease, higher tumor burden, and elevated inflammatory markers at baseline. In addition, subgroup analyses, including comparisons between B‐ALL and B‐NHL, should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, these data provide important insights into the clinical course, management challenges, and dismal outcomes associated with IEC‐HS.

In conclusion, IEC‐HS remains a rare but life‐threatening complication of anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy, occurring predominantly in patients with high‐risk inflammatory disease and associated with profound immune‐mediated toxicity. Our findings underscore the urgent need for prospective studies to better define risk factors and to develop optimized management strategies aimed at reducing the high morbidity and mortality.
