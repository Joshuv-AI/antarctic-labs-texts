Influence of Operator Board-Certification Status on Outcomes of Mechanical Thrombectomy: A Single-Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Yuhei Ito, Tsuyoshi Ichikawa, Chisae Tamogami, Megumi Koiwai, Kyouichi Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMechanical thrombectomy (MT) is the standard of care for acute ischemic stroke, and its outcomes are highly dependent on operator experience. While integrating junior physicians into MT practice is crucial for sustainable workforce development, it poses a challenge in maintaining procedural quality during their training. Although the Japanese Society for Neuroendovascular Therapy (JSNET) provides a board certification system in Japan, the impact of an operator’s certification status on MT outcomes remains uncertain. Therefore, this study aimed to validate the safety and efficacy of MT performed by noncertified operators under specialist supervision by comparing their outcomes with those achieved by board-certified specialists.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective study of patients undergoing MT between January 2020 and December 2024. Patients were stratified into 2 groups based on the primary operator's certification status. Primary outcomes were assessed across 3 domains: efficacy (successful reperfusion, defined as a modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction score ≥2b, and favorable functional outcome, defined as a 90-day modified Rankin Scale score of 0–2), safety (symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage [sICH]), and efficiency (puncture-to-reperfusion time). Secondary efficacy measures included the modified 1st-pass effect (mFPE) and the number of passes. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate whether operator certification status was independently associated with clinical outcomes. In all procedures performed by noncertified operators, a JSNET board-certified specialist was scrubbed in and provided direct supervision.ResultsEighty-seven patients met the inclusion criteria. Forty-three were treated by board-certified specialists and 44 by noncertified operators under direct supervision. The groups did not differ significantly in efficacy outcomes, including successful reperfusion (97.7% in board-certified vs. 93.2% in noncertified; p = 0.62), favorable functional outcome (51.2% vs. 43.2%; p = 0.46), and the achievement of mFPE (65.1% vs. 52.3%; p = 0.22). The primary safety outcome of sICH was also comparable (7.0% vs. 9.1%; p = 1.00). However, the efficiency outcome, puncture-to-reperfusion time, was significantly shorter in the board-certified group (median, 29 vs. 38 minutes; p = 0.02). In the multivariable analysis, only younger age and mFPE achievement were independently associated with favorable outcome, whereas the operator’s certification status was not (p = 0.75).ConclusionUnder direct supervision, MT performed by noncertified operators demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety to procedures performed by board-certified specialists, albeit with lower efficiency. These data endorse structured, supervised training pathways that protect patient outcomes while developing the next generation of neurointerventionalists.

Introduction

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has been established as a standard treatment for acute ischemic stroke, and its global use has been expanding rapidly due to broadened indications. Given that MT is frequently performed in emergency settings, it requires rapid decision-making and considerable technical expertise. Consequently, securing enough skilled operators remains a major challenge in many institutions.

At the same time, early exposure to practical MT experience is essential for younger physicians to develop into independent neurointerventionalists, which is critical for long-term workforce development. Previous studies have suggested that procedural outcomes in MT are influenced by the operator’s experience, and balancing educational opportunities with the maintenance of procedural quality and safety has become a pressing issue.1–5)

In Japan, the Japanese Society for Neuroendovascular Therapy (JSNET) has operated a board certification system for over 2 decades. This system requires candidates to meet rigorous criteria, including a minimum number of procedural cases and the successful completion of written, oral, and technical examinations.6)

While the JSNET board certification system is well-established, the specific impact of an operator’s certification status on procedural outcomes within this supervised training framework remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the validity of MT performed by noncertified operators under direct supervision. To achieve this, we compared the key domains of efficacy, safety, and efficiency between procedures performed by board-certified specialists and their noncertified counterparts.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patient population

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at a single center. We reviewed consecutive patients who underwent MT for acute ischemic stroke at our institution between January 2020 and December 2024. Cases that required angioplasty during the procedure and those with missing 90-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score data were excluded from the analysis.

Baseline and procedural variables

Baseline variables included age, sex, premorbid mRS score, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and atrial fibrillation), occlusion site, stroke subtype, and admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score. Large-vessel occlusion (LVO) was defined as occlusion of the internal carotid artery, the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery, or the basilar artery.

The extent of ischemia was assessed using the diffusion-weighted imaging-Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (DWI-ASPECTS) when available, with DWI-ASPECTS regarded as equivalent to ASPECTS. If DWI was not obtained, conventional ASPECTS was used. For posterior circulation strokes, the posterior circulation Acute Stroke Prognosis Early CT Score (pc-ASPECTS) was applied. For analysis and reporting, this was summarized as a single variable (ASPECTS/pc-ASPECTS).

Acute treatment with intravenous alteplase was recorded. Procedural variables included the 1st-line thrombectomy technique, categorized as stent-retriever thrombectomy, contact aspiration, or a combined technique (stent-retriever plus contact aspiration), as well as the number of passes, and time metrics (onset-to-reperfusion [O2R], puncture-to-guiding catheter placement [P2G], and puncture-to-reperfusion [P2R]).

Operator groups and supervision

A total of 4 board-certified specialists (including 1 JSNET instructor) and 7 noncertified trainees served as primary operators during the study period.

The noncertified operators were postgraduate year (PGY) 4–7 residents, with prior experience ranging from their 1st-ever thrombectomy case to approximately 20 cases.

Specialists were PGY 9–24, with thrombectomy experience ranging from 20 to 150 cases. All supervising physicians were JSNET board-certified, and 1 held instructor credentials. The noncertified operators were physicians actively pursuing JSNET board certification. As operational benchmarks, we prioritized independent performance of diagnostic cerebral angiography, experience assisting in approximately 10 MT procedures, and prior operator experience in extracranial endovascular procedures (e.g., carotid artery stenting), although exceptions could occur due to the emergent nature of acute ischemic stroke cases. In all procedures performed by noncertified operators, a JSNET board-certified specialist was scrubbed in and provided direct supervision, including intraoperative guidance and decision-making. The degree of direct intraoperative intervention (e.g., hands-on manipulation vs. verbal instruction) was not systematically recorded.

Definition of operator

In this study, the “primary operator” was defined as the physician documented as such in the operative record. During the procedure, key steps included guiding catheter navigation, intracranial microcatheter placement, and stent retriever and/or aspiration catheter deployment and retrieval. Even when verbal instruction or assistance from the supervising specialist was provided, if the noncertified operator was unable to advance the procedure at these critical steps, the supervising board-certified specialist took over and completed the procedure. In such cases, the physician who completed the remaining procedure was recorded as the primary operator in the operative note.

Outcomes and definitions

We assessed primary outcomes across 3 key domains:

Efficacy: The primary efficacy outcomes were successful reperfusion, defined as a modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) grade of 2b or higher, and favorable functional outcome, defined as an mRS score of 0–2 at 90 days. We additionally evaluated the modified 1st-pass effect (mFPE), defined as achieving mTICI 2b–3 with the 1st thrombectomy pass. We used mFPE rather than the original FPE definition (mTICI 2c–3)7) because (i mTICI ≥2b is the conventional threshold for “successful reperfusion” in clinical practice and registries, and (ii in a modest single-center cohort, mFPE more parsimoniously captures both reperfusion efficacy and procedural efficiency without further stratifying 2b versus 2c. Previous systematic reviews have also reported that both FPE and mFPE are associated with favorable functional outcomes.8)

Safety: Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) was defined as any hemorrhage identified on postoperative neuroimaging, including subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), parenchymal hemorrhage, and intraventricular hemorrhage. Symptomatic ICH (sICH) was defined as ICH accompanied by a ≥4-point worsening in NIHSS score. In addition, SAH was tabulated as a separate item irrespective of symptom status because SAH is considered to strongly reflect the technical quality of the procedure; accordingly, SAH contributes to the “any ICH” category and is also displayed independently in the tables.

Efficiency: The primary efficiency outcome was groin P2R. Other time metrics, such as P2G and O2R, were also analyzed. For patients with an unknown onset time, the last known well time was used as the reference.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between the 2 groups using the Mann–Whitney U-test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables as counts (percentages).

To examine the association between operator’s certification status and clinical outcomes, we first performed univariable logistic regression analyses for baseline and procedural variables. In principle, all variables were considered; however, the number of passes was substituted by mFPE. The site of occlusion was analyzed as LVO versus non-LVO, rather than by individual vessels, and stroke subtype was evaluated as cardioembolic versus non-cardioembolic. In addition, because the aim of this study was not to detect differences according to the thrombectomy technique employed, procedure type was not included in the analysis. Subsequently, we built a priori, forced-entry multivariable logistic regression models, selecting covariates for clinical relevance while maintaining an events-per-variable ≥10 to evaluate whether operator’s certification status was independently associated with clinical outcome. The primary model for favorable outcome (mRS 0–2) included age, admission NIHSS score, mFPE, and operator’s certification status. Covariates were not selected based on univariable p-values.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Japanese Red Cross Fukushima Hospital (Approval No. 2507), and informed consent was obtained via an opt-out method.

Efficacy: There were no significant differences in the primary efficacy outcomes. The rate of successful reperfusion was 97.7% in the board-certified group versus 93.2% in the noncertified group (p = 0.62), and the rate of 90-day favorable functional outcome was also similar (51.2% vs. 43.2%; p = 0.46). Additionally, the rates of complete reperfusion, achievement of mFPE, and number of passes, a secondary efficacy measure, did not differ between the groups.

Safety: The primary safety outcome, the incidence of sICH, was comparable between the board-certified (7.0%) and noncertified (9.1%) groups (p = 1.00). Similarly, there was no significant difference in the rate of any ICH (14.0% vs. 27.3%; p = 0.13) or SAH (7.0% vs. 6.8%; p = 1.00). Periprocedural complications occurred in 4 cases (9.3%) in the board-certified group (asymptomatic vessel dissection, asymptomatic carotid–cavernous fistula, transfusion-requiring puncture site complication, and nonocclusive mesenteric ischemia) and in 3 cases (6.8%) in the noncertified group (2 asymptomatic vessel dissections and 1 dissection requiring stent placement). No significant difference in overall periprocedural complication rates was observed between the groups.

Efficiency: The primary efficiency outcome, P2R, was significantly shorter in the board-certified group compared to the noncertified group (median, 29 vs. 38 minutes; p = 0.02). Other time metrics, such as O2R and P2G, showed no significant differences.

Association between operator certification and outcomes

A univariable analysis was performed to identify factors associated with a favorable functional outcome. As shown in Table 4, younger age, male sex, lower premorbid mRS score, the absence of hypertension, lower admission NIHSS score, shorter P2R and P2G, achievement of mFPE, and the absence of any ICH/sICH were significantly associated with favorable outcomes. The operator’s certification status was not associated with the outcome (p = 0.46).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we evaluated the validity of MT performed by noncertified operators under a supervised training system, focusing on the key domains of efficacy, safety, and efficiency. Our primary finding was that while procedural efficiency, as measured by P2R, was lower in the noncertified group, there were no significant differences in the crucial outcomes of efficacy and safety. These findings indicate that the temporal overhead imposed by intraoperative teaching (e.g., instructional pauses, deliberate pacing) does not undermine overall procedural quality. In line with the focus on technical performance, we separately reported procedure-related SAH in addition to any ICH. Importantly, no significant differences were observed between groups regarding the frequency of SAH and other periprocedural complications (including dissections, carotid–cavernous fistula, and systemic events), suggesting that under direct supervision, technical complication rates are not higher for noncertified operators.

This key finding was further reinforced by our univariable/multivariable analysis, which revealed that the operator’s certification status was not an independent predictor of a favorable outcome. This indicates that the final clinical outcome is determined by patient-specific factors and the technical quality of the reperfusion, rather than the operator’s title alone.

Our results align with previous studies reporting that operator experience correlates with MT outcomes but also extend them by providing a specific focus on a structured, supervised educational setting.1–5) Unlike studies on independently practicing operators, our findings demonstrate that direct intraoperative supervision by board-certified specialists can effectively bridge the experience gap. This structure, which allows for immediate expert guidance, appears pivotal in maintaining procedural quality and preserving patient outcomes, even with a modest increase in procedure time. These findings support the feasibility of integrating hands-on training into clinical practice without compromising patient safety.

In Japan, the JSNET board certification system has been maintained for over 2 decades and is widely regarded as a rigorous credentialing process.6) To obtain board certification, candidates must complete at least 5 years of training in a core specialty (e.g., neurosurgery, radiology, neurology, or emergency medicine), followed by at least 1 year of dedicated neuroendovascular training. Certification requires participation in a minimum of 100 neuroendovascular procedures, including at least 20 cases as the primary operator, across a range of disease categories, in addition to passing written, oral, and practical examinations.

At the instructor level, candidates must demonstrate even greater expertise, with at least 200 procedures performed as the primary operator, including higher volumes of aneurysm, revascularization, arteriovenous fistula, arteriovenous malformation, and tumor cases, along with academic achievements such as conference presentations and publications. Both certifications require renewal every 5 years based on continued case experience and academic participation. These requirements emphasize that JSNET-certified specialists and instructors represent physicians with substantial procedural experience and formal evaluation, reinforcing the validity of comparing outcomes based on certification status in this study. In contrast, while fellowship programs and professional society certifications exist in the United States and Europe, a nationally standardized credentialing system is not universally established, and training quality can vary widely among institutions.9,10) The fact that noncertified operators in our study were able to perform MT safely and effectively under the direct supervision of highly trained specialists highlights the potential of Japan’s structured certification system to support consistent and effective operator development.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, its findings may have limited generalizability. Second, the modest sample size restricted the number of variables that could be included in our multivariable logistic regression model. Although baseline ASPECTS is widely recognized as a predictor of outcome, the indication for thrombectomy in patients with low ASPECTS was gradually expanded during the study period, which may have confounded its prognostic value in this cohort. Therefore, as baseline covariates we selected age and admission NIHSS score, which were more stable indicators across the study period. Third, because of the retrospective design, the degree and frequency of intraoperative supervisory intervention and whether operator switching occurred were not systematically captured and therefore could not be quantified. This unmeasured intervention may have influenced trainee independence and may also have contributed to the comparable safety or efficiency outcomes between groups, underscoring the importance of direct on-site supervision. Fourth, the retrospective design carries an inherent risk of selection bias and measurement error due to inconsistent documentation; residual confounding cannot be excluded. Lastly, the publication of major trials supporting thrombectomy for large-core infarcts during the study period may have introduced some heterogeneity into our patient cohort over time.11–13) Future prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that a structured training framework with direct supervision allows noncertified operators to achieve comparable efficacy and safety outcomes in MT to their certified counterparts. The observed trade-off in procedural efficiency underscores the “cost” of training, but, critically, does not compromise patient outcomes. These findings provide strong support for specialist-led, supervised training programs as a valid and safe pathway for developing the future neurointerventional workforce.
