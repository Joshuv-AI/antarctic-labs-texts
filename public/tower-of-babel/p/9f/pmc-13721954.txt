Reactions to quit smoking campaign messages by US adults who smoke menthol cigarettes: A focus group study
Authors: Rhyan N Vereen, Mayank Sakhuja, Shayna Farris, Jennifer M Sheldon, Chisom Ojukwu, Emmanuel Saint-Phard, Sarah D Mills, Carrie Rosario, Kurt M Ribisl, Allison J Lazard, Paschal Sheeran, Noel T Brewer, Marissa G Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:State and local menthol cigarette bans are increasingly common, but strategies to boost their impact remain unclear. A campaign that encourages people who smoke menthol cigarettes to quit smoking altogether could amplify the benefits of bans. This study examined reactions to campaign messages in a situation where menthol cigarettes are no longer being sold among adults who smoke menthol cigarettes.Methods:We conducted 6 semi-structured focus groups, each with 7–9 adults ages 21+ years, who smoked menthol cigarettes (n = 50). Four of the focus groups were among populations with higher menthol cigarette use (ie, Black and lesbian, gay and bisexual adults). Participants shared reactions to 12 campaign messages that aimed to encourage quitting smoking (eg, “You can start your quit journey today” and “You’ve got this!”). We conducted qualitative thematic analyses.Results:Messages that used supportive language (eg, “Every attempt counts”) motivated participants to want to quit. Nuanced messages about money (reward vs. saving) and family (quit to spend time with family vs. quit for family), as well as encouragement to set small attainable goals also received positive reactions. Messages about mental health benefits of quitting received mixed feedback. Phrases like “quit smoking now” and mention of menthol cigarettes, as opposed to cigarettes in general, were described as punitive and negatively targeted.Conclusions:Our study provides guidance for campaign developers seeking to encourage adults who smoke menthol cigarettes to quit smoking. Findings highlight the need for nuanced health communication to support quitting smoking among priority populations.Implications:Most smoking cessation health communication campaigns are not designed for people who smoke menthol cigarettes. Findings suggest that campaign developers should consider creating clear and direct messages that incorporate supportive and positive language, emphasize rewards and setting realistic and attainable goals, and avoid potentially stigmatizing language about menthol cigarettes.Keywords: cessation, health disparities, public health, qualitative research, tobacco control

Introduction

Menthol cigarette use has led to an additional 10 million people starting smoking and 378 000 premature deaths from 1980 to 2018.1 Menthol cigarette use is associated with greater nicotine dependence2,3 and can be harder to quit than non-menthol cigarettes, particularly among minoritized racial and sexual groups.4–7 Around 81% of Black adults who smoke cigarettes use menthol cigarettes, compared to 34% of white adults who smoke cigarettes.8 Use patterns also differ by sexual orientation, where 55% of lesbian or gay and 49% of bisexual people who smoke cigarettes use menthol cigarettes, compared to 42% of straight adults who smoke cigarettes.8 Reducing the prevalence of menthol cigarette use may therefore reduce the disproportionate burden of tobacco-related morbidity and mortality among Black and lesbian, gay, or bisexual (LGB) adults who smoke cigarettes, priority populations for tobacco control.

To date, two US states (Massachusetts and California) and more than 200 cities have banned the sale of menthol cigarettes.9 States and cities will likely continue to adopt menthol cigarette bans. In 2022 the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) proposed a rule to prohibit menthol as a characterizing flavor in cigarettes (ie, a menthol ban) nationally in an effort to eliminate menthol cigarette use and harms.10 Although FDA withdrew the rule in early 2025, the agency could purse banning the sale of menthol cigarettes in the future. Regardless of whether a federal menthol ban is implemented, a tailored quit smoking campaign as a standalone intervention may aid in smoking cessation and potentially shift more people toward quitting smoking altogether, supporting the calls for culturally sensitive messages to support cessation of menthol cigarettes.11,12 Social marketing campaigns are a powerful tool for changing attitudes, norms, and, ultimately, behaviors.13,14 National anti-smoking campaigns such as FDA’s The Real Cost, CDC’s Tips from Former Smokers, and the Truth Campaign have been associated with more negative smoking beliefs and reductions in smoking among youth and adults.15–19 Recent quit smoking campaigns have been developed to raise awareness about the harms of menthol cigarettes and/or discourage use, prioritizing racial and ethnically minoritized groups.20,21 However, neither campaign focused solely on adults who smoke menthol cigarettes and their reactions to quit smoking messages.

The purpose of this qualitative study was to assess responses to potential smoking cessation messages designed specifically for people who smoke menthol cigarettes, with a focus on Black and LGB people who use menthol cigarettes at disproportionate rates.

Methods

Message development

The study team developed 12 original messages intended to encourage quitting smoking among people who smoke menthol cigarettes. Each message contained a headline and 1–2 sentences about quitting. Messages were newly developed for this study. They were informed by established health behavior theories, particularly Protection Motivation Theory,22 that highlight three constructs that align with evidence-based cessation intervention components: self-efficacy, response efficacy, and motivation. First, we reviewed existing campaigns prioritized for Black and/or LGB people who smoke cigarettes (eg, “Fresh Empire,”23 “This Free Life”24) to identify overlapping concepts and messaging across the campaigns. Then, we conducted literature reviews on quit smoking campaigns, focusing on those that embedded self-efficacy, response efficacy, and motivation principles. Message development was highly collaborative and iterative. Over the course of multiple team meetings, over 50 candidate messages were developed. During these meetings, study team members reviewed, discussed, refined, and consolidated draft messages. Next, we gathered feedback on messages from our tobacco control and communication expert advisory board, as well as two adults who smoke menthol cigarettes who served as advisors. Through this process, the team selected four draft messages that best reflected each construct. Thus, messages reflected these themes: self-efficacy (4 messages; eg, “You can. When you identify your triggers, you can better resist the urge to smoke.”), response efficacy (4 messages; eg, “Save money. Menthol cigarettes will not be sold in stores anymore. Quit now and save over $1,000 each year.”), and motivation (4 messages; eg, “What’s your reason? Keep your reasons for quitting smoking in mind. They will carry you through the rough spots”). Prior to the focus groups, the team vetted and further revised draft messages using feedback from an expert advisory board comprised of an interdisciplinary team of researchers with expertise in tobacco use and smoking cessation among minoritized groups, and community advisors who were adults who smoke menthol cigarettes from our priority populations. Participants

Eligible participants were age 21 years or older, lived in the United States, and self-reported current use of menthol cigarettes (defined as smoking on most or all days, having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime, and having smoked menthol-flavored cigarettes in the past 30 days). Virtual focus groups were planned. Therefore, eligibility also included being able to speak English and being willing to participate in a video conference.

Procedures

In February and March 2024, we partnered with Ewald & Wasserman Research Consultants LLC, a research consultancy, to recruit a purposive sample of primarily Black and/or LGB participants largely from three major US cities (Atlanta, Chicago, and New York City) using Craigslist. We selected these cities because they have large and diverse populations that would enhance our ability to recruit for our target samples of Black and LGB adults. Potential participants completed an eligibility screener online. We contacted eligible respondents to obtain verbal informed consent and schedule their participation in a virtual focus group. Individuals who were both Black and LGB could have been assigned to either the Black or LGB group based on scheduling preferences and available focus group slots. Virtual focus groups were held via Zoom, a videoconferencing software, and lasted about 90 minutes, and allowed participants to build on each other’s ideas in voiced agreements and disagreements, sparking ideas that might not have emerged in one-on-one interviews. Focus groups were held in two rounds, with unique participants in each round. In between rounds, we refined or replaced messages based on participant feedback. Each round included one focus group with only Black or African American participants, one with only LGB participants, and one with a general population group open to people of any race and sexual orientation (including Black and/or LGB participants). Groups were separated by race and/or sexual orientation to encourage open discussion about social identity and potential stigma-related issues related to menthol cigarette use.

Six focus groups were held, with each group including 7–9 participants. We decided a priori to conduct 2 rounds of focus groups and anticipated that 3 groups would be sufficient in each round. Between the two rounds of focus groups, we assessed for thematic saturation before concluding that we had indeed reached saturation of themes within each round. Three study team members were present at each focus group in the roles of moderator, note taker, and technical support person. Participants received a $110 electronic gift card for participating in the focus group and were offered a list of resources for quitting smoking (provided in the Supplemental Materials). Study procedures were approved by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Institutional Review Board.

Measures

The moderator of each session (CO or ES) used a semi-structured guide to facilitate discussion. The first part of the guide assessed perceptions of a menthol cigarette ban.25 The second part of the guide assessed reactions to smoking cessation messages developed by the study team. The moderator told participants that the study team was developing a campaign to help people who smoke menthol cigarettes to quit smoking. To mimic message reactions in the context of a potential menthol ban, the moderator also instructed participants to imagine that menthol cigarettes would no longer be sold in stores beginning the following month. The moderator guide is shared in Supplemental Materials.

When directed, the technical support person presented four messages at a time (within one of the three themes) in the focus group. The viewing order of the themes was randomized for each focus group. The moderator instructed participants to identify the message that would most motivate them to quit smoking. Follow-up probes inquired about their reasons for selecting or rejecting specific messages in that message set, elements of the message that did or did not resonate with them, and suggestions for wording changes. This process was repeated for the remaining two themes. A total of 12 messages were reviewed.

After the first round of focus groups, the study team met to review message feedback and revise the messages, as needed, in preparation for the second round of groups. Messages that participants expressed concerns about (eg, were seen as confusing, offensive, or irrelevant) were removed and replaced with new messages and messages with proposed wording changes were updated. Some messages stayed the same. The three groups in round 1 reviewed the original 12 messages, while the three groups in round 2 reviewed the 12 adapted messages. Participants in the second round of focus groups reacted to the messages using the same process as in the first round, with probes focusing on whether concerns from the first round had been adequately addressed. All focus groups were audio recorded. A professional transcription service transcribed all recordings verbatim.

Analysis

We organized and analyzed the data using Dedoose (version 9.0.107). We developed an initial codebook of deductive codes using the study’s research questions.26 Three coders (RNV, MS, SF) independently coded the first transcript, adding inductive codes as they arose, to form a revised codebook. Then, they convened to discuss coding discrepancies, reached consensus, and refined the codebook. Two members of the research team (MS, SF) coded each of the remaining five transcripts using the same process of independently coding the transcript and then meeting to refine the codebook and reach consensus before proceeding to the next transcript. The codebook was finalized after coding of the final transcript. The larger study team was consulted when consensus could not be reached. At the completion of coding, we grouped the codes into meaningful categories to identify shared reactions to the messages among focus group participants (ie, thematic analysis).26 The COREQ27 was used for reporting. We integrated feedback across all groups to develop messages that had been evaluated by populations most affected by menthol cigarette use, ensuring that final messages would be both relevant and not cause harm to these communities. We report quotes that are representative of the identified theme, reflective of the patterns of discussion among the groups, and succinct (to limit the need to truncate or paraphrase participant statements).28,29

Results

The sample included 50 participants with a mean age of 40 years (Table 2). About half were Black or African American (48%) and over one-third (38%) were lesbian, gay, or bisexual. Most participants reported smoking cigarettes every day (88%). Language and tone

Messages that used supportive language motivated participants to want to quit smoking. Specifically, phrases like “every attempt counts,” “you’ve got this,” and “celebrate” in message 23 were described as empowering and encouraging. One participant detailed their selection of this message as the most motivating by sharing the following statement:

“I chose [message 23] because I found the bold, ‘you’ve got this,’ first of all, encouraging and motivational. Beyond that, I appreciate the wording where it helps you not beat up yourself when it’s saying, ‘every attempt to quit smoking counts.’ Whatever efforts you have done thus far counts. Keep going. In other words, don’t stop, but celebrate your success.”

On the contrary, some messages were demotivating to participants. Phrases like “quit smoking now” (eg, messages 5 and 6) and a message about wishing they never started smoking (eg, message 10) were described as demanding, punitive, and guilt or regret-inducing. The negative language made some participants want to disengage with the messages. For example, a Black participant described the overall feeling from a set of messages as, “… It’s more of, like, a blackmail and, like your conscience speakin’ … and tryin’ to force you to do something that you don’t wanna do. And you’re coming up, like to me, with that kind of statements, I would totally ignore you ‘cause it’s more of, like angering …”.

Several participants also had a strong negative reaction to the messages that explicitly mentioned menthol cigarettes, as opposed to cigarettes in general. Participants felt that messages about menthol cigarettes (eg, message 4, 6, and 12) were inherently not about the product, but instead about the people who smoke menthol cigarettes. When asked why they opposed the messages, an LGB participant said, “… When it goes on to say the end of menthol as opposed to just the end of cigarettes, … I again take that to say, ‘Black folks, you know? You and your menthol cigarettes … ’ I still feel that there is something-something just ain’t resonating with me, with that, with the menthol thing …”.

Participants further noted their frustration with menthol-specific messages by detailing their beliefs that controlling access to menthol cigarettes was an attempt to control the agency of minoritized racial and sexual groups. Multiple participants spoke about feeling like these messages were authoritarian. An LGB participant described it as, “…It’s your White Big Brother, your White, straight Big Brother is telling you what to do, and this is how you do it. To me, getting back to rebellions, like who are you to tell me what to do? I’m gonna do what I want…”

Quitting motivations

Different reasons for quitting also garnered different levels of motivation. Most participants were not motivated by a message about quitting to save money (message 6). Participants shared that they did not mind spending money on cigarettes and that, if desired, there were other ways to save money by changing their spending habits in other areas (eg, food, entertainment, discretionary spending). A participant in a LGB focus group said, “… I can get off Hulu and Netflix and other streaming to save money. That in and of itself is not motivating. It may be true. I don’t dispute the truthfulness of it, but there are other ways that we can save.”

However, many participants were motivated by a message that focused instead on rewarding themselves with the money saved if they quit smoking (message 18). This message allowed some participants to dream about how they could use the money they spent on cigarettes on other things, as exemplified by the following statement from a participant in one of the Black focus groups: “… If I spent less on, you know, this pack of cigarettes, um, how much money that I would have for the week? And that would go into different aspects of my life that I would feel even more fulfilled. Like, actually getting’ more time for self-care that I really love, takin’ that vacation that, you know, I really wanted to take because I have the money to do it …”.

Some of the messages focused on quitting for the sake of friends and family (messages 3, 5, 17); these messages received mixed reactions. Despite the noted importance of these networks, many participants were not motivated by the thought of quitting solely for family and friends and mentioned their social networks extended beyond only friends and family (eg, coworkers and neighbors). An LGB participant described their thoughts with the statement: “Family, they’re a motivation, but I pay my own bills. They’re not the say-all-be-all for me, you know, but they are definitely a factor …” Another LGB participant added, “I know what it’s not for gay men is ‘fresh start for your family,’ [laughter] because most of the gay culture is abandoned from their family ‘cause they’re gay.”

Some participants also reported mixed reactions to a message encouraging them to reach out to family and friends for support with quitting (message 3). In response to this message, a few participants mentioned strained relationships with family. Others talked about the possibility of friends having a negative influence on their quit attempts because they smoke too or simply preferring to speak with a neutral third party. For example, an LGB participant stated: “ … at that point when you are trying to quit, it is better to have a disinterested party, to have somebody who is a therapist, or who is a counselor. Because your friends, I don’t know how much help. Even if they’ve quit smoking themselves, I don’t know how much, you know, your friends are quit to help you with that necessarily. You might get judgment, or they may enable you.”

Finally, two messages focused on the relationship between quitting smoking and decreased stress and anxiety (message 8 and 20). Most participants had a desire to decrease stress and anxiety and identified with the headlines “Clear your mind” (message 8) and “Build a happier life” (message 20). For example, one LGB participant noted: “Clearing your mind, and just really, that just identifies with the most self-peace, self-love, self-with-self, and doing it for yourself, I feel like.” Most participants, however, were confused by these messages because participants felt that smoking cigarettes (rather than quitting) lowered stress and anxiety. They did not believe that quitting smoking could improve mental health. Many participants reported using cigarettes to cope with everyday challenges and negative life experiences. One LGB participant stated: “…feels like they’re – its like a magic cure or something. Like, ‘oh, you quit smoking. Now, you’re gonna be less anxious and stressed’ But I-I know that’s uh, not gonna be the case ‘cause life is stressful, right?… Uh, it feels a little, um, unrealistic.”

Quitting strategies

Several messages encouraged specific strategies for quitting smoking (message 1, 2, 13, 14, 15, 16). One strategy was related to avoiding smoking triggers. For several participants, the concept of avoiding triggers (message 13) was not motivating and was perceived as unrealistic because many triggers, such as driving or taking a break at work, could not be completely avoided. Participants, however, did respond favorably to the part of the messages about identifying triggers (messages 1 and 13). Identifying triggers was something that many participants had already done or felt that they could do. Participants were well aware that it is an important step toward making a quit attempt. For example, a participant from one of the general population groups explained: “…If anything, I would say some kind of language that says, you know, ‘Most likely, you can’t avoid all your triggers, but for the ones that you can avoid,’ or somethin’ like that, that sounds more realistic. It sounds like a longshot that you’re gonna be able to eliminate everything…”

Another strategy mentioned in a message (message 16) was setting a quit date. Many participants were not motivated by this message because they felt that setting a quit date alone was not enough to help them make a quit attempt. They said that a quit date was just another date on the calendar, a date was easy to miss and easy to move and that they would feel like a failure if they did not quit on the quit date. Some participants noted that if they did quit on the quit date, they may relapse and smoke again, and if any of these events took place they would be discouraged from making a quit attempt in the future. One Black participant stated: “I don’t think settin’ dates and plans work. I think those are—I think that never works. Like, I think it-it’s, like, a—you know, runs with new year’s resolution. Like, it only works for, like, a certain time. And then all of us in our own minds’ll be like, ‘Well, let me just smoke a cigarette right now because it’s—I haven’t smoked a cigarette in a while. I-I stuck to the calendar,’ but it’s, like-it’s, like, we don’t—we’re not gonna really stick to it.”

Finally, use of the Quitline was recommended as a strategy in two messages (messages 2 and 14). Participants were intrigued by the Quitline resource but had questions about its utility. They wanted the message to include information about how to contact the Quitline and who they would be speaking to if they called. Participants wanted more information about how they would be helped and how the Quitline was different than other smoking cessation resources. Participants preferred the wording quit “coach” over “counselor” and a clear statement that this person could provide support, resources, and help them make a quit plan for free (message 14). One LGB participant stated the following: “…I like that because, one, I’m—they’re free. And I love free. So it really enticed me. And I would hope that, maybe, along the lines of me talking with the free counselor and they’re tryin’ to help me with resources… You know, and, along the lines of them also connecting us with, like, things that we can do to help quit, they can also try to help figure out what may be the reason why an individual started smokin’ in the first place, you know, type thing. So that’s why that one stood out to me. I like that one.”

Discussion

In this study adults who smoke menthol cigarettes were motivated by supportive language that encouraged taking steps toward quitting. Messages were designed to reflect self-efficacy, response efficacy, and quitting motivation, aligning with theory-driven approaches that have been shown to support behavior change, smoking cessation messaging.30–32 Participants responded positively to messages that included language about setting goals that felt attainable and provided information about smoking cessation resources, like those provided by the free Quitline. These findings provide guidance for campaign developers seeking to encourage adults who smoke cigarettes, and particularly those who smoke menthol cigarettes, to quit smoking.

Our study reinforces the importance of language and tone in quit smoking messages. Participants reacted positively to messages with supportive language and negatively to messages that felt guilt-inducing or punitive. Findings are supported by a review of studies that point to a preference for gain-framed cessation messages.33 Individuals who smoke may already be exposed to and reminded of the negative effects of smoking and crave the new and positively framed information that is presented in gain-framed messages.33 Ongoing research should seek to identify message characteristics that amplify or explain the effectiveness of gain-framed messages.

Echoing findings from Wang et al.34 explicit mention of menthol cigarettes in quit smoking messages was problematic. Participants reacted negatively to menthol-specific messages and were confused by the explicit mention of menthol cigarettes, given their knowledge that all cigarettes, not just menthol-flavored, are harmful. These messages felt overly targeted and stigmatizing to Black and LGB adults given that they are minoritized communities that are already disproportionately targeted by menthol cigarette marketing.35 Notably, messages that did not include the word “menthol” did not receive the same pushback, with many messages without the word “menthol” receiving positive feedback. Stigma can be an unintended consequence of smoking cessation campaigns and campaigns need to be designed to minimize stigma to the extent possible.

Our findings revealed that reactions to messages about motivations for quitting smoking varied based on wording choice. The money-related messages around rewarding oneself garnered more positive reactions than a message about saving money. One possible explanation is because rewards aid in cessation.36 In telling participants that they could reward themselves, the messages encouraged them to trade one reward (ie, smoking a menthol cigarette) for an unspecified reward of their choice.37 Telling adults who smoke that they would save money by quitting smoking could be motivating for some adults who smoke cigarettes.38 However, in this study participants quickly thought of other ways that they could save money and still have adequate funds to support their smoking habits. Another example was seen in reactions to messages about mental health. Quitting smoking improves mental health.39–41 However, many participants reported smoking to cope with daily stressors. They did not believe that quitting smoking could be associated with a positive mental health outcome and were, therefore, not motivated by these messages. Further research is needed to determine whether campaign messages about mental health can effectively motivate adults who smoke menthol cigarettes to quit smoking.

In this study, participants were motivated by messages that outlined actionable goals that felt achievable (eg, identifying triggers) and were demotivated by goals that felt impractical or overwhelming (eg, avoiding triggers altogether or setting a quit date). Goals are important because the ability to set clear and measurable goals is associated with more successful quit attempts.42 The level of detail in the message was also important. For example, the original message encouraging participants to contact the Quitline was met with many questions about the resource and how it could benefit them. For effective messages, health communicators should balance the length of the message while also including enough information about the resources provided. Quit smoking messages that aid in setting achievable goals can help communicate resources in a more accessible and appealing way for individuals who smoke.

Our study is one of the first to focus on understanding reactions to cessation messages among adults who smoke menthol cigarettes. Participants self-reported menthol cigarette use. We acknowledge the inherent limitation of self-report data, though note that self-reported smoking status is commonly used as an eligibility criterion in similar studies.43 We oversampled members of priority populations for tobacco control (ie, Black and LGB individuals) to ensure adequate representation. Participants were instructed to react to the messages in the context of a national menthol cigarette ban that was under consideration at the time of the study. Though the specific policy context has evolved, the findings from our study remain relevant for ongoing state and local cessation efforts for people who smoke menthol cigarettes. Virtual focus groups may have limited the ability to build rapport among study participants. However, virtual focus groups have been shown to draw out a comparable number of ideas, analytic themes, and overall findings as compared to in-person focus groups.44,45 Also, we did not seek to reach saturation in themes related to our research questions specifically within each priority population group and therefore did not analyze the data separately by priority population. Thus, we are unable to determine whether findings differ by race or sexual orientation. We also did not assess the effect of message exposure on quitting behavior, but rather on the perceived effect of the message. It is possible that participants reacted positively to messages that may not actually change behavior or reacted negatively to messages that would change behavior. The real-world effect of the messages should be assessed experimentally before use in large scale campaign efforts.

Conclusions

In this qualitative study with adults who smoke menthol cigarettes, participants reacted differently to messages based on their language and tone, quit motivation, and type of quit strategy. Findings from this study suggest that campaigns for individuals who smoke menthol cigarettes should incorporate supportive and positive language, avoid focusing on menthol cigarettes explicitly, and emphasize rewards and setting realistic and attainable goals.
