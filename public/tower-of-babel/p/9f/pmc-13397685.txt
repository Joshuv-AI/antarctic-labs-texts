Critical care nurses’ experiences of managing patients receiving continuous kidney replacement therapy in intensive care units: a qualitative study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mohammed Yousef Almulhim, Abdulrhman Khaled Al Abdulqader, Mohamed Naeem Badr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury requiring kidney replacement therapy is a common and life-threatening complication among critically ill patients. Continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT) is frequently used in haemodynamically unstable ICU patients and places substantial technical, cognitive, emotional, and relational demands on critical care nurses. Although nurses are central to CKRT delivery, limited qualitative evidence explores their lived experiences of providing technology-embedded kidney support in Middle Eastern ICU contexts.MethodsA qualitative study using reflexive thematic analysis was conducted in adult ICUs affiliated with university healthcare settings in Al-Ahsa, Eastern Province, Saudi Arabia. Twenty critical care nurses with direct experience of caring for patients receiving CKRT or other kidney replacement therapies were recruited using purposive sampling. Semi-structured interviews were conducted face-to-face or through secure encrypted video conferencing, in Arabic, English, or bilingually according to participant preference. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, translated where required, and analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis approach.ResultsFour interrelated themes were developed: (1) Caught between the machine and the person; (2) On the edge: the emotional weight of kidney failure in critical care; (3) A delicate balance: uncertainty, advocacy, and interprofessional dynamics; and (4) Growing through the challenge: competence-building and resilience. Nurses described CKRT as a form of technology-embedded relational care requiring simultaneous machine vigilance, humanistic presence, moral judgement, family support, and interprofessional advocacy.ConclusionsManaging patients receiving CKRT in ICUs requires more than technical competence. It involves emotionally intense, morally complex, and relationally significant nursing work. Structured CKRT education, simulation-based training, advocacy preparation, routine debriefing, psychological support, and staffing models that reflect CKRT workload are needed to sustain safe and person-centred critical kidney care.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04793-8.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most frequent and serious complications encountered in critically ill patients, affecting an estimated 20–35% of ICU admissions worldwide and associated with substantially increased morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospital stay, and healthcare costs [1, 2]. Among patients with severe AKI, approximately 5–10% require kidney replacement therapy (KRT), most commonly delivered as continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT), which has become the preferred modality in haemodynamically unstable patients due to its gradual, controlled fluid and solute removal [3]. Despite advances in KRT technology, the outcomes of dialysis-dependent critically ill patients remain poor, with in-hospital mortality rates frequently exceeding 50% in those with multiorgan failure [4, 5].

CKRT management in the ICU is a highly complex, technology-intensive nursing responsibility. Critical care nurses are the primary providers of CKRT at the bedside, responsible for circuit priming, assembly, and initiation; real-time anticoagulation management; continuous monitoring for filter clotting, air embolism, and line disconnection; precise fluid balance management in haemodynamically unstable patients; recognition and management of acute CKRT-related complications; and detailed documentation of therapy delivery and patient response [6]. This technical workload occurs simultaneously with the full complexity of ICU nursing, including ventilator management, vasoactive drug titration, continuous haemodynamic monitoring, and family communication, in patients who are typically the most acutely ill in the hospital [7].

Intensive care nursing is consistently identified as one of the most demanding healthcare occupations. ICU nurses work within an environment characterised by persistent noise, rapidly shifting priorities, high patient acuity, frequent exposure to death and dying, and pervasive moral uncertainty [8, 9]. Burnout rates among ICU nurses are among the highest in any healthcare specialty, with compassion fatigue, emotional exhaustion, and depersonalisation widely reported [10, 11]. The addition of CKRT management to this demanding context introduces further layers of complexity: nurses must simultaneously operate and troubleshoot a continuous extracorporeal circuit, interpret alarm systems, make rapid clinical judgements under time pressure, and maintain the humanistic presence that defines person-centred critical care [12, 13].

Despite this considerable burden, the lived experiences of critical care nurses managing patients receiving CKRT / patients requiring kidney replacement therapy remain inadequately explored. Most existing literature on CKRT nursing focuses on technical competency, workload quantification, or knowledge assessment, employing quantitative approaches that are poorly suited to capturing the nuanced, experience-near dimensions of this role [14, 15]. Qualitative research is uniquely positioned to illuminate how nurses make sense of the dual demands of technological management and relational care; how clinical uncertainty, moral distress, and interprofessional dynamics are navigated at the bedside; and what sustains or depletes nurses in this challenging specialty [16]. A small body of qualitative work has explored nurses’ experiences of CKRT management, identifying themes of technical insecurity, emotional burden, and inadequate support, but most studies have been conducted in high-resource settings outside the Middle East and none have specifically focused on the Saudi Arabian context [17].

Saudi Arabia presents a distinctive context for ICU nursing. The Kingdom’s healthcare system is undergoing rapid transformation under Vision 2030, with significant investment in tertiary and critical care infrastructure [18–20]. ICUs in Saudi Arabia manage high-acuity patients including large proportions requiring RRT, within a cultural framework shaped by collectivist family values, Islamic interpretations of suffering and death, and a workforce that includes substantial proportions of internationally recruited nurses [21]. The Al-Ahsa region of the Eastern Province hosts a growing network of university-affiliated hospitals and tertiary facilities, including the King Faisal University healthcare system, that serve a large and diverse population. Understanding how nurses in this context experience and navigate the management of dialysis-dependent ICU patients is essential for informing locally relevant education, workforce support, and care quality initiatives.

Aim of the study

This study aimed to explore critical care nurses’ experiences of managing critically ill adult patients receiving continuous kidney replacement therapy in intensive care units in the Al-Ahsa region of Saudi Arabia.

Research question

How do critical care nurses experience and navigate the management of critically ill adult patients receiving continuous kidney replacement therapy in intensive care units?

Methods

Study design

A generic qualitative descriptive design informed by reflexive thematic analysis was used to explore critical care nurses’ experiences, meanings, and practices when caring for patients receiving continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT) in intensive care units [22, 23]. A qualitative descriptive approach was appropriate because the study sought to generate a clinically grounded and experience-near account of nurses’ perceptions, challenges, and support needs rather than to develop formal theory. Reflexive thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke, was selected because it supports an active, interpretative, and epistemologically flexible approach to identifying patterned meanings across interview data while acknowledging the role of researchers in knowledge production.

The study was underpinned by a contextualist orientation. Nurses’ accounts were treated as meaningful descriptions of practice that were also shaped by organisational routines, ICU hierarchies, cultural expectations, professional identities, and the technological environment of CKRT. Reflexivity was therefore treated as integral to analytic quality rather than as a strategy for eliminating researcher influence. The manuscript was prepared with attention to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), and a completed COREQ checklist was submitted as supplementary material.

Study setting and recruitment

The study was conducted in adult intensive care units within university-affiliated healthcare settings in the Al-Ahsa region, Eastern Province, Saudi Arabia. These settings included adult mixed medical-surgical, cardiac, and trauma/surgical ICUs that provide care for critically ill adults with multiorgan failure, including patients requiring CKRT. The phrase “university-affiliated healthcare settings” refers to clinical sites affiliated with King Faisal University and involved in clinical service, education, and training; it does not indicate that all participating nurses were employed directly by the university.

Recruitment was conducted in collaboration with ICU charge nurses and nursing managers, who circulated the study information sheet to potentially eligible nurses during staff briefings and through secure institutional electronic communication channels, including work email and approved encrypted staff messaging groups. Managers were not informed which nurses contacted the research team or participated. Interested nurses contacted the lead researcher directly to reduce the possibility of perceived managerial pressure or coercion (Table 1).

Purposive sampling was used to achieve variation in gender, ICU type, current nursing role, years of ICU experience, years of CKRT experience, and level of formal CKRT preparation. Recruitment decisions were reviewed iteratively during data collection. For example, after early interviews were mainly with staff nurses from mixed medical-surgical ICUs, subsequent recruitment intentionally included charge nurses, clinical educators, nurses from cardiac and trauma/surgical ICUs, and nurses with both limited and extensive CKRT experience. Recruitment continued until thematic sufficiency was achieved, meaning that later interviews added depth and nuance but did not generate substantially new patterns of meaning relevant to the research question [24].

A total of 35 nurses were approached or received the study invitation. 28 nurses expressed interest, 26 were eligible and consented, and 20 completed interviews. 5 nurses declined participation, mainly because of workload or scheduling difficulties. No participant withdrew after consent, no interviews were repeated, and no completed interviews were excluded from analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were registered nurses currently working in adult ICUs in the participating university-affiliated healthcare settings in Al-Ahsa who had at least 12 months of ICU nursing experience, had directly cared for at least five patients receiving CKRT or another form of kidney replacement therapy, and were able to participate in Arabic and/or English. Nurses in exclusively administrative roles without direct ICU patient care were excluded, as were nurses on extended leave during the recruitment period.

To protect participants from avoidable emotional burden, nurses were also informed that they should not participate if they felt that discussing experiences related to critically ill patients, death, moral distress, or end-of-life care would be personally distressing at the time of recruitment. This was based on participant self-assessment rather than managerial judgement or formal psychological screening. Managers were not asked to identify distressed nurses, and no information about individual nurses’ emotional status was shared with managers or clinical leaders.

Data collection

In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted between February and April 2025 at times convenient to participants. Interviews were scheduled outside official duty hours or during approved break periods to avoid interfering with patient care and to minimise any perception that participation was part of work duties. Interviews took place either in private meeting or education rooms within or adjacent to the ICU, or through secure encrypted video conferencing when face-to-face meetings were not feasible because of shift patterns or participant preference.

No repeat interviews were conducted. All interviews were conducted by the lead researcher, a nursing academic with formal qualitative research training and no supervisory or managerial authority over participants. Each interview began with an informal conversation to build rapport, confirm voluntary participation, and remind participants of confidentiality, the right to pause or stop the interview, and the right to withdraw without consequences.

Interviews lasted approximately 45–90 min and were digitally audio-recorded with written consent. Field notes were written after each interview to capture contextual observations, emotional tone, non-verbal cues where relevant, and early analytic reflections.

The full interview guide is provided as Additional file 1, and was designed to elicit concrete, experience-near narratives about CKRT management and its emotional, relational, and organisational dimensions. Interviews lasted between approximately 45 and 90 min and were digitally audio-recorded with participants’ written consent. Field notes capturing contextual details, non-verbal observations, and initial analytic reflections were written by the lead researcher after each interview.

Interview structure and conduct

The semi-structured interview guide was developed from the study aim, relevant literature on ICU nursing and CKRT, and qualitative methodological guidance. The guide was reviewed by two senior ICU nurses for cultural relevance, clarity, and clinical appropriateness, and was pilot-tested with two eligible nurses whose interviews were not included in the final analysis. Minor revisions after piloting improved the wording of prompts and encouraged participants to describe concrete experiences rather than general opinions (Additional file 1).

The guide was used flexibly rather than as a fixed questionnaire. Each interview began with broad, open-ended questions inviting participants to describe their everyday experiences of caring for ICU patients receiving CKRT. More targeted prompts about emotional burden, moral distress, advocacy, technology, family communication, faith, coping, and training were introduced only after participants had first described their experiences in their own terms. This approach was intended to reduce the risk of directing participants prematurely toward predefined concepts. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the interview structure may have shaped the data generated, particularly because the guide included prompts about moral distress, technology-mediated care, and faith-based coping.

Recording and transcription

All interviews were digitally audio-recorded using encrypted devices and transferred immediately to a password-protected institutional server. Original files were deleted from recording devices after secure transfer. Arabic interviews were transcribed verbatim in Arabic, English interviews were transcribed verbatim in English, and bilingual interviews were transcribed in the language used by the participant at each point in the interview. Transcripts were checked against the audio recordings by members of the research team to ensure accuracy, completeness, and fidelity to tone and meaning.

Arabic quotations used in the manuscript were translated into English by a bilingual researcher and independently checked by another bilingual team member (M.Y.A). Translation aimed to preserve participants’ intended meaning, emotional tone, and culturally embedded expressions rather than to produce literal word-for-word translation. Where participants used religious or culturally specific expressions, such as references to du‘aa, divine will, patience, or acceptance, these were retained or explained where needed to preserve cultural meaning. Quotations were lightly edited for readability by removing repeated words, fillers, and identifying details; however, the substantive meaning of participants’ accounts was not altered. Participants were not asked to approve every translated quotation, but preliminary thematic summaries were shared with a subset of participants to explore resonance with their experiences.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis approach. First, the lead qualitative researcher read and re-read all transcripts to become immersed in the dataset and wrote familiarisation notes after each reading. Second, initial codes were generated across the full dataset using NVivo 14 for data management (M.N.B). Coding was primarily conducted by the lead qualitative researcher, while a second team member independently reviewed a purposive subset of transcripts to support reflexive discussion and enhance interpretive depth. Codes were developed inductively while remaining attentive to the study aim and the clinical context of CKRT nursing.

Third, codes were clustered into candidate themes that captured patterned meanings across participants’ accounts. Fourth, candidate themes were reviewed against coded extracts and the full dataset, with particular attention to divergent cases, differences by role or experience level, and alternative interpretations. Fifth, themes were named and defined through iterative team discussions. Interpretive disagreements were not treated as coding errors requiring statistical agreement; rather, they were used as opportunities to examine assumptions, refine theme boundaries, and consider competing readings of the data. Sixth, the final analytic narrative was developed by integrating thematic interpretation with illustrative quotations.

Preliminary thematic summaries were shared with a subset of participants to explore whether the interpretations resonated with their experiences. Participant feedback was used reflexively to refine theme descriptions and improve clarity, rather than to validate the analysis as objectively correct.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant institutional review board. All study procedures complied with institutional guidelines, national research ethics regulations, and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participants were informed of the study aims, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality measures, and their right to decline any question, pause the interview, stop the interview, or withdraw without consequences for employment or professional standing.

Because interviews involved potentially sensitive experiences, including patient death, suffering, moral distress, end-of-life care, and perceived futile treatment, several safeguards were used. At the beginning of each interview, participants were reminded that they could avoid discussing any event that felt uncomfortable. During interviews, the interviewer monitored verbal and non-verbal signs of distress and offered pauses when emotionally difficult experiences were discussed. At the end of each interview, participants were asked a brief closing question about how they felt after the discussion. When needed, participants were reminded of available institutional psychological support resources and encouraged to seek support through appropriate staff wellbeing or counselling channels. No participant requested termination of the interview because of distress. Pseudonyms were used in transcripts and reporting, and identifying details about patients, colleagues, units, and incidents were removed or altered to protect confidentiality.

Rigour and reflexivity

Rigour was supported through strategies consistent with trustworthiness in qualitative research and with the assumptions of reflexive thematic analysis [25]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the dataset, iterative reading, reflexive memoing, peer debriefing, and the use of vivid quotations to demonstrate links between participants’ accounts and analytic interpretations. Transferability was supported through description of the study context, participant characteristics, recruitment strategy, and CKRT care environment. Dependability was strengthened through an audit trail documenting recruitment decisions, interview procedures, coding development, theme refinement, translation decisions, and team discussions. Confirmability was supported through reflexive journaling and transparency about analytic decisions.

The lead interviewer was a nursing academic with experience in critical care nursing education and qualitative research, but had no supervisory authority over participants and was not involved in their employment evaluation. The wider research team included clinicians and nursing academics with backgrounds in nephrology, internal medicine, medical-surgical nursing, and critical care nursing. These backgrounds provided clinical sensitivity to CKRT practice but also carried assumptions about the complexity and emotional burden of critical care. To manage these assumptions, the interviewer maintained a reflexive journal before and after interviews, documenting expectations, emotional responses, emerging interpretations, and possible areas of over-identification with participants. Team meetings were used to challenge early interpretations, examine alternative explanations, and ensure that themes were grounded in participants’ accounts rather than in researchers’ prior clinical assumptions.

Member reflection was used cautiously and consistently with reflexive thematic analysis. Preliminary thematic summaries were shared with a subset of participants to explore resonance, clarity, and whether important aspects of experience had been overlooked. Feedback suggested that the themes were recognisable and meaningful to participants, particularly the tension between machine vigilance and human connection. This feedback informed refinement of theme descriptions but was not treated as objective verification of the analysis.

Findings

Reflexive thematic analysis developed four interrelated themes that illuminate CKRT nursing as a form of technology-embedded relational care (Table 2). Participants did not describe CKRT merely as an additional technical procedure; rather, they described a practice in which machine vigilance, physiological interpretation, patient comfort, family communication, interprofessional negotiation, and moral responsibility were continuously intertwined. Although there was strong convergence across participants’ accounts, the analysis also identified variation by experience level, role, and prior CKRT training. Less experienced nurses emphasised fear, uncertainty, and dependence on senior colleagues, whereas experienced nurses and charge nurses more often described anticipatory judgement, advocacy, and informal mentoring responsibilities. Some participants framed CKRT primarily as emotionally burdensome, while others also described it as a source of professional confidence and specialist identity.

Themes, sub-themes, and descriptions

Caught between the machine and the person

This theme captures the defining tension at the heart of nurses’ experiences: the simultaneous, often competing demands of technological vigilance and humanistic care. Participants consistently described the management of dialysis-dependent ICU patients as a practice that demanded their full clinical attention on two distinct but inseparable fronts: the CKRT circuit, with its complex alarms, pressures, fluid balances, and filter management; and the patient, who lay behind the machine in a state of critical vulnerability. Far from being reducible to technical proficiency alone, caring for dialysis-dependent ICU patients was experienced as a constant negotiation between the relentless pull of the technology and the irreducible humanity of the person attached to it.

The relentless demand of the CKRT circuit

Nurses described CKRT management as a continuously active clinical responsibility that demanded a form of sustained hypervigilance that was qualitatively different from other ICU nursing tasks. Unlike discrete interventions such as medication administration or physical assessment, CKRT placed a continuous demand on nurses’ cognitive attention throughout the shift.CKRT is not like a ventilator that you set and it runs. It talks to you constantly. Every hour, the balance, the pressures, the anticoagulation. You have to listen to it, or it will clot and the patient will miss hours of therapy. (N07).

CKRT is not like a ventilator that you set and it runs. It talks to you constantly. Every hour, the balance, the pressures, the anticoagulation. You have to listen to it, or it will clot and the patient will miss hours of therapy. (N07).

Participants described the experience of managing CKRT alarms as one of the most persistently demanding aspects of the role. Alarms signalled potential filter clotting, access problems, air detection, or pressure deviations, and distinguishing clinically significant alarms from false or minor alerts required experience, pattern recognition, and rapid assessment. Nurses described learning to ‘read’ the machine’s alarm patterns as a form of tacit clinical knowledge acquired over time.In the beginning, every alarm scared me. I would call the physician for everything. Now I can hear an alarm and already know from the sound and the pressure numbers whether it is serious or just a small adjustment. But it took years to learn that. (N04).

In the beginning, every alarm scared me. I would call the physician for everything. Now I can hear an alarm and already know from the sound and the pressure numbers whether it is serious or just a small adjustment. But it took years to learn that. (N04).

The burden of CKRT was not experienced simply as “workload” in the task-based sense, but as a relentless demand that continuously competed for nurses’ attention, judgement, and emotional presence. Participants explained that the circuit required repeated checking, interpretation, and intervention throughout the shift. This demand became especially intense when a nurse cared for more than one unstable patient, when several patients in the unit were receiving CKRT simultaneously, or when alarms occurred alongside vasoactive drug titration, ventilation changes, family questions, or emergency admissions. In such moments, nurses felt that CKRT narrowed their field of attention and made it difficult to step back, think holistically, and provide the relational aspects of care they valued.On a night shift when three patients are on CKRT and you are the nurse for two of them, you are running between machines all night. You barely have time to assess properly, to talk to the family, to step back and think about the whole patient. (N12).

On a night shift when three patients are on CKRT and you are the nurse for two of them, you are running between machines all night. You barely have time to assess properly, to talk to the family, to step back and think about the whole patient. (N12).

Keeping humanity at the bedside

Despite the overwhelming technical demands of CKRT management, nurses consistently described a deep commitment to maintaining a humanistic presence at the bedside of patients receiving CKRT / patients requiring kidney replacement therapy, many of whom were sedated, intubated, delirious, or unable to communicate. Participants spoke about the importance of treating patients as whole persons rather than as ‘cases’ or ‘circuits,’ even when clinical conditions made conventional patient interaction impossible.Even when a patient is sedated, I talk to them. I tell them what I am doing when I check the machine, when I turn them. Maybe they cannot hear, but I believe they can feel presence. They are still a person, not just a body connected to tubes. (N03).

Even when a patient is sedated, I talk to them. I tell them what I am doing when I check the machine, when I turn them. Maybe they cannot hear, but I believe they can feel presence. They are still a person, not just a body connected to tubes. (N03).

Nurses described consciously constructing small moments of human connection within the technical landscape of CKRT management: using touch when assessing access sites, speaking reassuringly when patients appeared distressed despite sedation, including family members in explanations of the therapy, and attending to comfort measures alongside circuit management. These were understood not as ‘extras’ but as integral to the quality of care.The family sees all these machines and they are terrified. I always take time to explain: this one cleans the blood, this one is for the heart, this is for breathing. When they understand, they relax a little, and that helps the patient too. (N09).

The family sees all these machines and they are terrified. I always take time to explain: this one cleans the blood, this one is for the heart, this is for breathing. When they understand, they relax a little, and that helps the patient too. (N09).

Several nurses described a sense of professional identity anchored in this integration of technical and humanistic care. Being a ‘good CKRT nurse’ was not solely about machine competence; it also meant seeing and honouring the patient behind the extracorporeal circuit.I am proud of knowing how to manage CKRT well. But my goal is always to give the best to the patient, not just to the machine. The machine serves the patient. Not the other way. (N16).

I am proud of knowing how to manage CKRT well. But my goal is always to give the best to the patient, not just to the machine. The machine serves the patient. Not the other way. (N16).

On the edge: the emotional weight of kidney failure in critical care

This theme reflects the profound emotional burden experienced by nurses caring for dialysis-dependent ICU patients, who are among the most critically ill and clinically precarious patients in the hospital. Participants described an emotional landscape shaped by daily proximity to suffering, clinical deterioration, and death, in a context where the limits of medicine were frequently visible and moral complexity was routine. The emotional demands of this work were experienced as distinct and cumulative, residing not only in individual dramatic events but in the everyday accumulation of witnessing, absorbing, and responding to the fragility of life.

Bearing witness to clinical deterioration

Nurses described clinical deterioration in patients receiving CKRT as emotionally powerful because it was experienced from three overlapping perspectives: immediate, delayed, and cumulative. The immediate dimension involved watching deterioration unfold at the bedside while actively managing the CKRT circuit and other life-sustaining therapies. Nurses described this as particularly distressing because they were not passive observers; they were adjusting fluids, responding to alarms, monitoring pressures, and escalating concerns while seeing the patient continue to decline.You set up the circuit, you optimise the filter, you manage the balance perfectly. And the patient still deteriorates. The kidneys fail, then the liver, then the heart. You do everything right and it is still not enough. That is the hardest part. Feeling that all your effort was not enough to save them. (N05).

You set up the circuit, you optimise the filter, you manage the balance perfectly. And the patient still deteriorates. The kidneys fail, then the liver, then the heart. You do everything right and it is still not enough. That is the hardest part. Feeling that all your effort was not enough to save them. (N05).

The delayed dimension referred to the emotional impact that often emerged after the shift, when nurses were no longer required to maintain professional composure. Several participants described continuing to function during the clinical crisis but processing the emotional meaning only later, at home, during prayer, or when speaking with colleagues.The patient dies, we do the care after death, complete the documentation, and fifteen minutes later I am assessing a new admission. There is no moment to breathe. You carry it inside and keep working. Sometimes I go home and finally feel what happened that day. (N11).

The patient dies, we do the care after death, complete the documentation, and fifteen minutes later I am assessing a new admission. There is no moment to breathe. You carry it inside and keep working. Sometimes I go home and finally feel what happened that day. (N11).

The cumulative dimension reflected the way repeated exposure to deterioration, death, and perceived therapeutic failure accumulated over time. This was particularly evident among nurses who had cared for the same patient across several days or weeks. Participants described forming intense short-term attachments because CKRT required constant bedside presence and detailed knowledge of the patient’s minute-by-minute physiological changes.In ICU, you can get very attached to a patient in just three or four days. You are with them twenty-four hours, you know every number, every change, you fight for every hour. And then they go. It leaves a mark on you, even if it is short. (N18).

In ICU, you can get very attached to a patient in just three or four days. You are with them twenty-four hours, you know every number, every change, you fight for every hour. And then they go. It leaves a mark on you, even if it is short. (N18).

Together, these immediate, delayed, and cumulative forms of witnessing show that the emotional burden of CKRT nursing is not limited to dramatic deaths or isolated crises. It is produced through sustained proximity to physiological decline, repeated responsibility for life-sustaining technology, and the need to continue caring for others while carrying unresolved emotional residue from previous patients.

Moral distress and the limits of technology

A striking and emotionally significant dimension of nurses’ experiences was moral distress arising from situations in which CKRT was perceived as prolonging suffering rather than supporting meaningful recovery. Nurses described the painful experience of caring for patients in whom they could see no realistic prospect of renal or overall recovery, yet in whom aggressive CKRT was continued at the direction of physicians or families.Sometimes you look at the patient and you know. The kidneys are not coming back. The liver is failing. CKRT is just keeping them alive for the family to say goodbye, maybe. But we run the circuit every day, and inside I ask myself: is this helping him, or are we prolonging his suffering? (N13).

This moral distress was compounded by nurses’ limited perceived authority to voice such concerns formally within the ICU hierarchy. Several participants described a tension between their clinical observations and ethical intuitions on one hand, and the expectations of physicians and families on the other, which left them feeling morally implicated in care they sometimes disagreed with.I can say something informally to the doctor, suggest gently that maybe we talk to the family about goals of care. But the final decision is not mine. Sometimes I feel like I am carrying the weight of a decision that was made above me, but I live with it at the bedside every hour. (N08).

Nurses called for greater inclusion in goals-of-care discussions, recognition of their clinical expertise in assessing patients’ responses to CKRT, and access to ethics consultation and debriefing resources to help process the moral complexity of this work.

A delicate balance: uncertainty, advocacy, and interprofessional dynamics

This theme captures the navigational demands nurses faced in managing clinical uncertainty in CKRT, advocating for patients within complex ICU team structures, and sustaining productive interprofessional working relationships. Managing patients receiving CKRT / patients requiring kidney replacement therapy required not only technical competence but also sophisticated clinical judgment, communication, and relational skill within a hierarchical environment where nurses’ knowledge and expertise were sometimes undervalued.

Navigating clinical uncertainty in CKRT

Participants described substantial clinical uncertainty in several key domains of CKRT management, including anticoagulation strategy selection, filter timing and circuit dose, the management of anticoagulation failures, and the interpretation of CKRT-derived data in haemodynamically unstable patients. This uncertainty was particularly prominent for nurses earlier in their CKRT careers, but was never fully resolved even with experience, given the inherent complexity and patient-to-patient variability of critical AKI management.There are many protocols, but the patient does not always follow the protocol. The anticoagulation that works for one patient causes bleeding in the next. You have to adjust, read the patient, ask questions. There is no single right answer, and that is uncomfortable at first. (N02).

Nurses described developing personal frameworks for clinical decision-making in CKRT through accumulated experience, peer consultation, and deliberate self-directed learning. However, they frequently expressed that formal educational preparation for CKRT had been insufficient, and that much of their competence had been built through trial, error, and informal mentorship rather than structured training.When I started in ICU, nobody gave me a course on CKRT. I watched a senior nurse, I practised on the machine, I read by myself at night. Gradually I learned, but I think a lot of mistakes happened in the process. We need proper training, not just watching and hoping. (N14).

Speaking up and being heard

Nurses described a complex range of interprofessional dynamics in the ICU, spanning collaborative and hierarchical relational patterns that shaped their experiences of advocacy, decision-making, and professional recognition. Most participants valued their relationships with physicians and other ICU team members, describing positive experiences of collegial respect and shared commitment to patient welfare. However, many also described barriers to raising concerns, particularly regarding CKRT management decisions, patient comfort, or goals of care.If I see a problem with the circuit, the doctor listens. For technical things, they trust us completely. But if I want to say something about the patient’s comfort or about whether we should continue, it is harder. You have to say it carefully, at the right moment, with the right person. (N17).

Nurses developed strategies for advocacy that included building trust with specific physicians over time, timing conversations thoughtfully, framing observations in clinical rather than emotional language, and using structured handover formats to ensure concerns were recorded and communicated. Several described the importance of nurse-to-nurse solidarity in supporting each other’s advocacy efforts.If I think the patient is suffering and the family does not understand the situation, I will speak to the charge nurse first, then together we approach the physician. Positive experiences of interprofessional collaboration were also prominent. Nurses described ICU teams in which their CKRT expertise was genuinely valued, their clinical assessments were incorporated into medical decision-making, and their concerns were heard and acted upon. These experiences of being recognised as knowledgeable clinical partners were deeply meaningful to participants and were associated with greater job satisfaction and sense of professional worth.

Growing through the challenge: competence-building and resilience

This theme captures the developmental and coping dimensions of nurses’ experiences: how competence in managing dialysis-dependent ICU patients was constructed over time through experience, mentorship, and reflection, and how nurses sustained themselves in the face of the significant technical and emotional demands of this specialty. Participants were not passive in the face of challenge; rather, they actively invested in their own development and sought to build the resources, both within themselves and in their work communities, necessary to continue providing high-quality CKRT care.

Learning the craft of renal support in the ICU

Nurses described a trajectory of professional development that typically began with anxiety, uncertainty, and a sense of inadequacy in the face of CKRT complexity, and progressed through accumulated experience to a position of genuine specialty confidence and competence. This trajectory was rarely smooth or linear; it was marked by moments of critical failure or near-miss events that generated powerful learning, alongside periods of consolidation, mentorship, and increasing mastery.My first time setting up CKRT alone, my hands were shaking. The senior nurse was watching but I had to do it. I made an error in the priming and she corrected me quietly. I was embarrassed, but I never made that mistake again. You learn it in your body, not just in your head. (N10).

Simulation-based training, when available, was highly valued by nurses as a means of building procedural confidence in a psychologically safe environment. Several participants described how access to simulation prior to first-time ICU CKRT management had significantly reduced their anxiety and improved their initial performance. However, simulation access was inconsistent, and most nurses called for greater investment in formal CKRT competency programmes within Saudi ICU nursing education.We have simulation for intubation, for CPR. Why not for CKRT? It is just as complicated, maybe more so, because you have to manage it continuously, not just for three minutes. I think if new nurses had simulation training, they would be safer and less afraid. (N15).

Experienced nurses described finding deep professional satisfaction in their accumulated CKRT expertise, and many took pride in acting as informal mentors to junior colleagues. This pedagogical role was not always formally recognised or compensated, but was seen as a meaningful contribution to team competence and patient safety.

Faith, colleagues, and coping in adversity

Nurses drew on a range of personal, social, and spiritual resources to cope with the emotional and cognitive demands of caring for dialysis-dependent ICU patients. Faith emerged as a central coping mechanism for the majority of participants, consistent with the Islamic cultural context of the study setting. Nurses described drawing comfort and motivation from spiritual practices, including prayer, supplication, and religious interpretation of suffering and death, that provided both a framework for accepting clinical outcomes and a source of personal renewal within the shift.After a patient dies, I go to the prayer area, I make du’aa for them and for their family. It gives me peace. I believe their suffering is over and they are in God’s hands now. This helps me return to the next patient with a clean heart. (N19).

Peer support from colleagues was equally prominent as a coping resource. Nurses described informal check-ins, shared humour between colleagues during quieter moments, and the powerful reassurance of being understood by someone who shared the same experience. The ICU team was frequently described as a ‘second family’ whose emotional solidarity was essential to sustaining nurses through difficult shifts.Only my colleagues in ICU understand what I feel after losing a patient on CKRT. My family at home, they try, but they don’t know. My colleagues, they know. One look, one coffee together, and we can continue. (N20).

Despite the importance of these informal coping strategies, nurses were consistent in identifying the limitations of relying solely on personal and peer-based resources. They called for formal organisational supports including structured debriefing after critical events or patient deaths, access to psychological or counselling services normalised within the ICU culture, formal recognition of the emotional dimensions of CKRT nursing in performance appraisal and professional development, and adequate staffing to prevent the chronic overwork that depleted coping reserves.I have faith and I have my colleagues, and these carry me far. But there are days when this is not enough. When the moral weight of the work is too heavy for one person to carry. We need systems, not just strength. We need our hospitals to see what we carry and share it with us. (N01).

Variation and divergent cases

Although many participants described CKRT nursing as emotionally and morally demanding, the dataset also contained divergent and more positive accounts. Nurses with longer CKRT experience, formal training, or educator/charge nurse roles were more likely to describe CKRT as a source of professional confidence and specialist identity. For these participants, the machine was not only a source of anxiety but also a clinical space in which they could demonstrate advanced judgement, mentor junior colleagues, and contribute visibly to patient stabilisation.

At first CKRT was frightening, but now it is one of the skills that makes me feel I am really an ICU nurse. When others ask me for help with the machine, I feel proud because I know I can protect the patient. (N16).

At first CKRT was frightening, but now it is one of the skills that makes me feel I am really an ICU nurse. When others ask me for help with the machine, I feel proud because I know I can protect the patient. (N16).

A small number of participants also reported feeling adequately supported when senior nurses, educators, and physicians were accessible and collaborative. These accounts did not negate the wider concerns about workload and emotional burden, but they showed that institutional culture and team relationships shaped how CKRT demands were experienced.

When the team is good, CKRT is not a lonely responsibility. The doctor listens, the charge nurse checks on you, and the educator is available. In those shifts, even if the patient is very sick, you feel supported. (N04).

These divergent cases suggest that distress was not an inevitable consequence of CKRT itself. Rather, it was intensified when technical responsibility, emotional exposure, limited authority, and insufficient support converged. Conversely, structured training, accessible senior support, collaborative physicians, and recognition of nursing expertise helped transform CKRT from an isolating burden into a domain of professional growth.

Discussion

This study explored critical care nurses’ experiences of managing critically ill adult patients receiving CKRT in ICUs in Saudi Arabia. The central conceptual contribution of the study is the interpretation of CKRT nursing as technology-embedded relational care. Participants did not experience CKRT as a purely technical therapy added to routine ICU work. Rather, they described a distinctive form of nursing in which continuous extracorporeal technology, physiological instability, patient vulnerability, family uncertainty, interprofessional negotiation, and moral responsibility were inseparably connected at the bedside.

The theme “Caught between the machine and the person” provides the conceptual centre of the findings. Nurses were required to sustain uninterrupted attention to the CKRT circuit while also preserving the patient’s humanity, dignity, comfort, and relational presence. This finding extends existing literature by showing that the emotional and moral burden of CKRT is not separate from its technical demands. The machine’s relentless alarms, pressures, fluid balances, and risk of clotting created a continuous cognitive pull, while the patient’s critical illness required comfort, advocacy, family explanation, and ethical sensitivity. In this sense, CKRT nursing can be understood as a practice of holding together two forms of vigilance: technological vigilance toward the extracorporeal circuit and relational vigilance toward the person whose life is being sustained through that circuit.

The first theme, Caught between the machine and the person, reflects a fundamental experiential tension in CKRT nursing that has received limited attention in the literature. Consistent with prior work on technology in the ICU [26], nurses in this study described the extracorporeal circuit as a presence that continuously competed for their attention alongside the patient. The relentless demand of CKRT alarms, fluid balance management, and anticoagulation adjustments created what participants described as a form of sustained hypervigilance that was qualitatively distinct from other ICU tasks [27]. This resonates with findings from a Chinese qualitative study on nurses’ CKRT experiences that identified ‘machine pressure’ as a dominant source of stress, but extends those findings by examining how nurses actively sought to maintain humanistic care within this technological landscape [17]. The strategies nurses described, talking to sedated patients, involving families in explanations, attending to comfort alongside circuit management, reflect a commitment to whole-person care that is often invisible in quantitative assessments of ICU nursing competency [28].

The second theme, On the edge: the emotional weight of kidney failure in critical care, confirms and extends the substantial literature on burnout, compassion fatigue, and moral distress among ICU nurses [29]. The subtheme of bearing witness to deterioration resonates with earlier descriptions of nurses’ grief in the face of repeated patient death [30], but highlights the particular acuteness of loss in the ICU context, where clinical attachment develops rapidly within the intensity of life-threatening illness. The subtheme of moral distress and the limits of technology is especially significant. Nurses described experiencing a form of moral distress [31] in which they felt implicated in care they perceived as disproportionate or futile, yet lacked the formal authority or organisational mechanisms to voice their concerns effectively. This pattern has been documented in critical care settings internationally, but its specific intersection with CKRT management, in which nurses’ hands-on expertise gives them a uniquely intimate view of therapeutic futility, has not previously been described in the Saudi context.

The third theme, A delicate balance: uncertainty, advocacy, and interprofessional dynamics, highlights the communication and relational demands of CKRT nursing that are often underestimated in technical competency frameworks [32]. Nurses’ descriptions of navigating clinical uncertainty in anticoagulation management, filter timing, and CKRT dosing are consistent with published literature documenting the complexity of CKRT clinical decision-making [33]. The subtheme of speaking up and being heard reflects well-documented patterns of hierarchical constraint in ICU advocacy [34], but also highlights positive experiences of interprofessional collaboration that are sometimes absent from critical accounts. The strategies nurses developed for effective advocacy, including timing, framing, and collective action through charge nurses, align with the concept of ‘safety-oriented advocacy’ in critical care [35] and represent important practical knowledge that deserves greater attention in nursing education.

Although clinical uncertainty is common across ICU practice, participants’ accounts suggest that uncertainty in CKRT has particular characteristics. In ventilation, vasoactive support, sepsis management, and other ICU interventions, nurses also navigate instability and rapid change. However, CKRT uncertainty was experienced as distinctive because it involved continuous interpretation of extracorporeal circuit performance alongside the patient’s changing haemodynamic, metabolic, coagulation, and fluid status. Nurses had to judge whether an alarm reflected access dysfunction, filter clotting, anticoagulation problems, patient movement, pressure changes, or evolving clinical deterioration. They also had to anticipate the consequences of interruptions in therapy, fluid balance errors, or delayed escalation. Thus, CKRT did not create uncertainty in isolation from other ICU care; rather, it intensified and layered uncertainty by adding a continuously active technological system to an already unstable patient.

The fourth theme, Growing through the challenge: competence-building and resilience, highlights the developmental trajectory of CKRT nursing competence and the active agency of nurses in building and sustaining their capabilities. The finding that most nurses described their CKRT competence as primarily self-constructed through informal experience and mentorship, rather than formal education, is consistent with broader evidence that CKRT nursing training is frequently inadequate worldwide [36]. The strong call for simulation-based training in CKRT echoes growing consensus in critical care nursing education, which has demonstrated the value of high-fidelity simulation in building procedural confidence and reducing clinical error [37]. The coping strategies described, particularly the centrality of faith and peer support, are consistent with broader evidence on coping among Muslim-majority nurses [38] and highlight the importance of culturally situated understandings of resilience in workforce support initiatives.

Taken together, the findings suggest that the management of patients receiving CKRT / patients requiring kidney replacement therapy in ICUs places demands on critical care nurses that are simultaneously technical, emotional, moral, and interpersonal, and that current institutional responses in this context are insufficient to meet those demands. Educational preparation for CKRT is inconsistent and inadequately formal; emotional and psychological support is largely individual and informal; and the moral complexity of critical renal care is rarely acknowledged in organisational structures or performance frameworks. These gaps are not unique to the Saudi context but are particularly salient given the Kingdom’s rapid healthcare expansion and the ongoing workforce development challenges associated with ICU nursing under Vision 2030.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted within one regional university-affiliated healthcare context in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia, which may limit transferability to other regions, countries, or organisational models. Second, purposive and volunteer-based recruitment may have favoured nurses who were more reflective, engaged, or willing to discuss emotionally difficult experiences, potentially under-representing nurses who were highly distressed, disengaged, or reluctant to participate. Third, interview-based data are subject to recall and social desirability influences, particularly in professional cultures where emotional control, resilience, and competence are highly valued.

Fourth, interviews were conducted in Arabic, English, or bilingually. Although bilingual checking and team discussion were used to preserve meaning, some emotional, religious, or culturally specific nuances may have shifted during transcription, translation, or quotation editing. Fifth, the semi-structured interview guide included prompts about moral distress, technology, and faith-based coping. Although broad open-ended questions were asked first and the guide was used flexibly, these prompts may have shaped the data generated and the prominence of some themes. Finally, as a qualitative study, the findings are interpretative and context-bound. They are not intended to estimate the prevalence of particular experiences or establish causal relationships.

Implications for nursing education and practice

The findings have important implications for nursing education, clinical practice, and workforce policy. In nursing education, CKRT-specific preparation should be integrated into undergraduate, postgraduate, and continuing critical care nursing programmes. Education should move beyond machine operation to include fluid balance reasoning, alarm interpretation, anticoagulation-related risk, patient comfort, family explanation, ethical uncertainty, and escalation of concern. Simulation-based CKRT training is particularly important because it allows nurses to practise circuit setup, troubleshooting, emergency responses, and interprofessional communication in a psychologically safe environment before managing unstable patients independently.

Because many participants found advocacy challenging, education should also include explicit preparation for speaking up in hierarchical ICU environments. Useful strategies include graded assertiveness, structured communication tools such as SBAR, case-based ethics discussions, role-play of goals-of-care conversations, and simulation scenarios in which nurses practise escalating concerns about patient comfort, treatment burden, or CKRT continuation. In clinical practice, advocacy can be strengthened by ensuring that nurses contribute routinely to multidisciplinary rounds, documenting nursing concerns about CKRT tolerance and patient comfort, involving charge nurses or clinical educators in difficult conversations, and creating clear escalation pathways when nurses perceive that treatment may be disproportionate or poorly aligned with patient-centred goals.

ICUs should also invest in structured CKRT competency pathways that include supervised practice, formal assessment, refresher training, and regular reassessment. Structured debriefing after patient deaths, circuit-related adverse events, or morally distressing cases should become part of routine ICU culture rather than an exceptional response. Psychological support services should be visible, confidential, and normalised by ICU leadership. At the policy level, staffing models should recognise that CKRT adds continuous cognitive, technical, and emotional demands to ICU care. Adequate nurse-to-patient ratios, formal recognition of CKRT expertise, and protected time for training and debriefing are essential for sustaining safe, person-centred critical kidney care.

Conclusion

This study shows that caring for critically ill patients receiving CKRT is a technically demanding, emotionally intense, morally complex, and relationally significant area of critical care nursing. Nurses were caught between the machine and the person: they maintained extracorporeal therapy, interpreted physiological instability, supported families, preserved patient dignity, and carried moral responsibility at the bedside. Their experiences demonstrate that CKRT nursing should not be understood only as a technical competency, but as a form of technology-embedded relational care.

The findings highlight the need for structured CKRT education, simulation-based competency development, formal advocacy preparation, interprofessional communication frameworks, routine debriefing, psychological support, and staffing models that reflect the real workload of CKRT. Strengthening these supports is essential for protecting nurses’ wellbeing, improving patient safety, and sustaining humane critical kidney care in Saudi ICUs and similar high-acuity setting.
