Introduction

Ventricular tachycardia (VT) is one of the leading causes of sudden cardiac death in patients with structural heart disease. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a common inherited cardiac disorder, with a prevalence of approximately 0.2%–0.5%.1,2 When ethnic differences and diagnostic criteria discrepancies are disregarded, the prevalence of midventricular HCM is estimated to be nearly 5% of all HCM cases.1 Although a less common phenotype, midventricular HCM is associated with a higher risk of ventricular arrhythmias.1,3,4

Because endocardial ablation alone may have limited efficacy in patients with HCM, a combined endocardial-epicardial approach should be considered.3,5, 6, 7 However, epicardial access carries the risk of procedural complications, such as inadvertent right ventricular free wall puncture, liver injury, or diaphragmatic damage.8, 9, 10 Transatrial CO2 insufflation is commonly used to facilitate a safer subxiphoid approach by separating the right ventricular free wall from the epicardium and enlarging the anterior pericardial space.11, 12, 13 However, the use of transatrial air insufflation instead of CO2 has not previously been described in the literature.

In this report, we describe a case of transatrial air insufflation for epicardial access and management of a right atrial thrombus, which was detected on follow-up echocardiography.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old man diagnosed with midventricular HCM was transferred from a referring hospital to our institution for management of an electrical VT storm probably exiting the left ventricular apex (Figure 1). He had previously received a diagnosis of atrial fibrillation and midventricular HCM–related heart failure with reduced ejection fraction earlier. There were no abnormal findings on physical examination and in the laboratory results other than elevated creatinine levels. Echocardiography showed no enlarged left ventricular cavity, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 35%, apical thinning, and mild-to-moderate aortic regurgitation, mitral regurgitation, and tricuspid regurgitation.

Ventricular tachycardia during electrical storm.

Despite high-dose β-blocker, amiodarone, and multiple electrical cardioversions, the VT storm could not be terminated, and the patient was intubated.

The treatment strategy was discussed in a multidisciplinary heart team meeting that included electrophysiologists, heart failure specialists, and cardiac imaging physicians. Institutional review board approval was obtained according to the institutional policy. Written informed consent for both the procedure and the publication of anonymized clinical data and images was obtained from the patient and his family.

After sinus rhythm was restored under sedation (Figure 2), a combined strategy of VT ablation and secondary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation was planned.

Termination of ventricular tachycardia with an atrial fibrillation rhythm.

Under general anesthesia, right ventricular (quadripolar) and coronary sinus (decapolar) catheters were positioned, and a Judkins Right 4 (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) coronary artery catheter was advanced through an Agilis sheath (Abbott, Chicago, IL) and was parked inside the right atrial appendage (RAA) (Figure 3). RAA angiography was performed in the right anterior oblique 30° view, and the RAA was punctured at its distal anterior tip using a Hornet 14 0.014-in guidewire (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) inside a 1.5 × 15 mm over-the-wire (OTW) balloon (Emerge, Boston Scientific). With a gentle forward thrust, the wire tip easily punctured the RAA, and the wire and OTW balloon were advanced together inside the pericardial sac. Then, the wire was removed and the balloon was connected to a 50-mL syringe after which 50 mL of room air was insufflated, while progressive separation was observed on the left lateral view (Supplemental Video 1). Once 2–3 cm of separation was achieved, epicardial puncture was performed with a 17-G Tuohy needle, the OTW balloon was retracted, an Agilis sheath was inserted inside the pericardial sac, and the air was rapidly aspirated out through its side port. After epicardial mapping was completed, intravenous heparinization was initiated to maintain an activated clotting time >300 seconds and endocardial left ventricular mapping was performed via a retrograde transaortic approach (Figure 4). Both endocardial and epicardial mapping were performed using a PENTARAY NAV ECO high-density mapping catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) with the CARTO 3 system. Late, low-voltage abnormal fractionated potentials were detected at both endocardial and epicardial apical regions. Irrigated ablation was delivered using a THERMOCOOL SMARTTOUCH SF catheter (Biosense Webster) at 50 W/40°C, with an irrigation rate of 8 mL/min in the epicardial space. After ablation, the pericardial sheath was exchanged for a pigtail catheter, and 80 mg of methylprednisolone and 10,000 IU of tranexamic acid were administered into the pericardial space while confirming that no air was left inside the pericardial sac, and the pigtail catheter was retracted 2 hours later.

Transatrial access via the right atrial appendage to facilitate epicardial access.

Electroanatomic mapping of the left ventricle.

Throughout the procedure, continuous arterial pressure monitoring was used to detect any hemodynamic changes suggestive of pericardial tamponade. In addition, intraprocedural echocardiographic assessments were performed approximately every 15 minutes by a second operator to evaluate for pericardial effusion or bleeding. No pericardial effusion was detected during or immediately after the procedure.

Follow-up was uneventful until routine transthoracic echocardiography 24-hour postprocedure revealed a 2.9 × 2.8 cm thrombus in the RAA septal region (Figure 5). Given the patient’s acute kidney injury, contrast computed tomography was initially deferred. After multidisciplinary discussion, low-dose, slow-infusion alteplase (1 mg/h for 48 hours) was initiated without a loading dose, followed by apixaban 5 mg twice daily for 1 week.10,14 Once creatinine improved, contrast computed tomography confirmed thrombus shrinkage (Supplemental Video 1), after which implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation was performed. At 1-month follow-up, echocardiography confirmed complete resolution of the thrombus (Figure 6).

Discussion

In this case, we report combined endocardial and epicardial VT ablation in a patient with midventricular HCM presenting with electrical storm. Notably, we used transatrial air insufflation for epicardial access—an approach not previously described in the literature to our knowledge.11,13 Postprocedurally, the patient developed a right atrial thrombus, a rare complication, which was successfully treated with conservative medical therapy without complications.10,14

Safety remains a major concern during epicardial access. Subxiphoid access carries risks of right ventricular puncture, coronary injury, or diaphragmatic complications.8, 9, 10 CO2 insufflation is widely used to separate the pericardial layers and thus reduce these risks.11,13 In our case, we used air instead of CO2 to evaluate its feasibility and safety as an alternative.

Although transatrial room-air insufflation for facilitating epicardial access has not previously been specifically described, the controlled use of intrapericardial air itself is not entirely unprecedented in the electrophysiology literature. Di Biase et al15 previously demonstrated that controlled and progressive introduction of air into the pericardial space, particularly in combination with saline, could increase separation within the pericardial cavity and facilitate epicardial ablation while preventing phrenic nerve injury. In that study, careful hemodynamic monitoring and complete evacuation of intrapericardial air were emphasized as key procedural safety measures. Our approach similarly relied on controlled low-volume transient room-air insufflation with immediate aspiration after successful epicardial access.

Although CO2 insufflation remains the preferred and better-established approach, our center has also been exploring the feasibility of transient room-air insufflation under controlled procedural conditions on the basis of preliminary institutional experience with this technique. Parts of this experience were previously presented at the European Heart Rhythm Association 2026 scientific meeting.16

In our institution, temporary limitations in the availability of medical CO2 have occasionally been encountered, prompting the evaluation of alternative methods for facilitating epicardial access. Within this context, room-air insufflation has been explored as a potential alternative technique under strict procedural safety precautions. Our center has preliminary experience with this approach within the framework of an institutional study assessing the noninferiority of room-air insufflation compared with CO2 insufflation.

Several anatomical and procedural factors further support the safety of our approach. During trans-right atrial access, the microcatheter used was of very small caliber and positioned entirely within the venous system, making clinically significant air entry highly unlikely. After pericardial access was established, the use of a large lumen Agilis sheath enabled complete aspiration of insufflated air. To further reduce the risk of systemic air embolism, insufflated air was rapidly aspirated under negative pressure immediately after epicardial access was obtained. Complete evacuation of air from the pericardial space was confirmed by fluoroscopy and transthoracic echocardiography. In addition, the fact that the heart and great vessels are routinely exposed to ambient air during open-heart surgery without clinically meaningful embolic events provides further physiologic reassurance that transient, fully extravascular air exposure—when followed by complete evacuation—does not inherently pose a significant embolic risk.

Because the gas kinetics and tissue absorption properties of air are different from those of CO2, its use may theoretically increase the risk of air embolism, elevated pericardial pressure, or other procedure-related adverse events.13,17 Nonetheless, our observation in this case—where no periprocedural air embolism or infectious complications occurred—supports the potential feasibility and safety of air as an alternative to CO2.

Alternative strategies, including surgical epicardial access via mini-thoracotomy, were considered during multidisciplinary evaluation. However, after discussion with the patient and his family, the patient declined surgical intervention. Therefore, a percutaneous approach was preferred, and transatrial insufflation-assisted epicardial access was considered the safest feasible alternative in this clinical scenario.

Bleeding and vascular complications are relatively common during ablation procedures, whereas intracardiac thrombus formation remains an uncommon but clinically significant event.8,10,14 This risk may be amplified in the setting of combined endocardial and epicardial ablation because of prolonged catheter manipulation and strict anticoagulation requirements. Intracardiac thrombi are most frequently reported within the ventricular cavities.8,10 Our review of the available literature revealed no previously published case of right atrial thrombus formation after such procedures.10,14 In this patient, the thrombus was detected in the early postoperative period and was most likely related to RAA wire puncture and temporary withholding of systemic heparinization during epicardial mapping. Contributing mechanisms likely included local endothelial trauma at the puncture site, delayed initiation of systemic anticoagulation before endocardial left ventricular mapping, and procedural manipulation within the right atrium.

The thrombus formation completely resolved with low-dose, slow-infusion alteplase followed by oral anticoagulation, without the need for surgical intervention. This approach may represent a safe and effective treatment option for hemodynamically stable patients.14

Although our experience suggests that transatrial air insufflation may represent a feasible alternative to CO2 for facilitating epicardial access, this technique should currently be considered exploratory. Further studies are required to better define its safety profile and clinical applicability before routine use can be recommended.

Conclusion

This case highlights the potential efficacy of combined ablation for midventricular HCM, demonstrates that air insufflation may be a safe alternative to CO2 for epicardial access, and illustrates the successful conservative management of a postprocedural right atrial thrombus. These findings underscore the importance of diversifying epicardial access techniques and performing early echocardiographic surveillance to promptly identify thromboembolic complications.
