First case of fatal leucocytozoonosis in domestic ducks in a zoological garden in Japan
Authors: Kaho UEGAKI, Ayako OHKAWARA, Miyou YOSHIDA, Yuuto KAWASHIMA, Masaki ITO, Keita KAKISAKA, Akihiro SADO, Masateru MITSUZAKI, Yukita SATO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leucocytozoon spp. are common avian haemosporidian parasites that can cause clinical symptoms with lethal cases in poultry worldwide. However, leucocytozoonosis has not been reported in domestic ducks (Anas platyrhynchos domesticus) in Japan. Two adult domestic ducks maintained in a zoological garden in Japan became lethargic, exhibited labored breathing, and died in July 2024. These ducks had hemorrhages in the visceral organs with splenomegaly and hepatomegaly. Megaloschizonts of haemosporidia were histologically observed in the spleen and other organs. Partial sequences of Leucocytozoon mitochondrial cytochrome b gene DNA were determined and classified as lineages known to infect wild waterfowl (Anseriformes). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first known fatal case of leucocytozoonosis in domestic ducks in Japan.

Leucocytozoon spp. are haemosporidian parasites of wild and domestic birds with a worldwide distribution excluding the polar regions [23]. L. caulleryi, which cause sporadic infections in chickens, is transmitted by biting midges (Culicoides spp.) [11, 13, 15]. In contrast, other Leucocytozoon spp. that infect birds mainly other than chickens, are transmitted by black flies (Diptera: Simuliidae). Black fly–transmitted Leucocytozoon spp. infections are typically subclinical in wild birds, while clinical disease has been reported in domestic ducks [8, 9, 12, 23]. In Canada, infected 6-week-old ducks have exhibited anorexia, lethargy, dyspnea, green diarrhea, and respiratory distress. Death can occur within a few hours to overnight [9]. In Sweden, similar cases have been documented in ducks aged 10 days to 2 months [8]. In Japan, Leucocytozoon spp. transmitted by black flies have been detected through surveillance of asymptomatic wild birds [7, 18]. However, excluding L. caulleryi, no cases of clinical disease attributable to Leucocytozoon spp. have been reported in either free-ranging wild birds or domesticated poultry to date. Here, we described the first fatal case of leucocytozoonosis in domestic ducks caused by Leucocytozoon in Japan.

In 2018, five newborn call ducks (Anas platyrhynchos domesticus) were introduced to a zoological garden in Sapporo, Hokkaido, Japan. Two of the ducks were relocated to a zoological garden in Tokyo, Japan, on June 24, 2024. The remaining three ducks, 2 females and 1 male, had been housed in a hut but were then exhibited outdoors from May to September alongside a 12-year-old Chinese goose (Anser cygnoides domesticus) and two 12-year-old chickens (Gallus gallus) in the garden. Their enclosure was near a river running through the garden often visited by wild waterfowl. Mandarin ducks and mallards have been observed near the zoological garden during summer [17].

On July 15, 2024, the two female ducks rapidly became lethargic, exhibited labored breathing, and subsequently died. However, the male duck, Chinese goose and two chickens showed no clinical signs of illness. Tracheal samples collected from the deceased birds were tested for influenza A virus antigens using the ESPLINE® INFLUENZA A&B-N rapid detection kit (Fujirebio, Tokyo, Japan), but the results were negative. To identify protozoan species detected in three ducks, DNA was extracted from the formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) spleen tissue blocks of the deceased ducks using a QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN N.V., Venlo, Netherlands) and from the blood of the surviving duck using a QIAamp DNA blood mini kit (QIAGEN) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cytochromeb gene of avian haemosporidian parasites comprised of Plasmodium, Haemoproteus, and Leucocytozoon spp. were amplified separately by world-standard nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) primers, as previously described [5]. PCR was performed using EmeraldAmp PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). An approximately 500-bp gene fragment of the cytochromeb gene of Leucocytozoon spp. was obtained from samples derived from all three infected ducks, and then purified using a QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (QIAGEN). PCR products were subsequently sequenced by Hokkaido System Science (Sapporo, Japan) using the BigDyeTM Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit and ABI 3730×l DNA Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Consequently, three cytochromeb gene sequences from Leucocytozoon spp. isolated from the two deceased ducks and the surviving duck were determined and named ANAPLA04-1, ANAPLA04-2, and ANAPLA05 (GenBank accession no. PV392647-PV392649). No differences were found among these three sequences except one synonymous substitution; thus, the three ducks were considered simultaneously infected with the same Leucocytozoon spp.

A phylogenetic tree of the cytochromeb gene of avian haemosporidian parasites was constructed using the maximum-likelihood (ML) method in MEGA11 [20]. ML analysis revealed that the GTR+G model was chosen as a best-fit model. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 cycles) was performed to assess the tree topology. Parasite lineages obtained from GenBank/EMBL/DDBJ were used for the phylogenetic analysis, which revealed that ANAPLA04-1, ANAPLA04-2, and ANAPLA05 were identical to a Leucocytozoon lineage previously detected in a mallard duck in northeastern China in 2015 (GenBank accession no. MW882309). To determine the existence of vectors that possibly transmit the parasites, black flies were collected from two sampling sites in the zoological garden on August 2024. Sampling site A was located in the wooded area along the Maruyama River (43.050407°N, 141.308491°E), while sampling site B was located in the outdoor enclosure for ducks and chickens (43.050254°N, 141.307452°E). A carbon dioxide-baited light trap (black light type, Fujihira Industry, Tokyo, Japan) was placed in sampling site A on August 15, 2024, and in sampling site B on August 23 and 24, 2024. A trap was operated between 4:00 p.m. and 8:00 a.m. The captured dipterans were stored in plastic vials containing 70% ethanol for further analysis. Black flies were then separated from the captured dipterans and identified to the species level based on morphological features under a stereomicroscope [19, 21]. For the molecular detection of Leucocytozoon spp. and host blood meal identification, individually extracted DNA from black flies was subjected to PCR targeting the partial mitochondrial cytochrome b gene of Leucocytozoon spp. and birds as described previously [6]. Amplified PCR products were sequenced as described above. In total, only two black flies were collected during the sampling period, and both specimens were female. One specimen collected at sampling site A on August 15, 2024, was morphologically identified as Simulium aokii, whereas the other specimen collected at sampling site B on August 24, 2024, was identified as S. japonicum. Neither specimen was positive for Leucocytozoon DNA. However, the S. aokii specimen was positive for avian blood, and the detected sequence was identified as chicken (Gallus gallus).

This is the first report of leucocytozoonosis identified in domestic ducks in Japan. In the present case, proliferation of Leucocytozoon organisms was observed in multiple organs of the acutely deceased ducks, consistent with previous fatal cases reported in domestic ducks in Canada and Sweden [8, 9]. Furthermore, no pathological findings suggestive of other diseases were observed. Therefore, the results indicate that Leucocytozoon spp. caused the death of the ducks. Leucocytozoonosis-related mortality is typically high in juvenile ducks but decreases with age [8, 10, 12]. Adult ducks are generally tolerant to this infection, except for fatal cases of co-infection with fungi [8, 14]. To our knowledge, no fatal cases of Leucocytozoon spp. infection in adult ducks have been reported; thus, our case may be extremely rare. Susceptibility to Leucocytozoon spp. has been reported to vary among duck breeds [10]. Call ducks may be more susceptible than other breeds.

We tried to identify the Leucocytozoon spp. by observing the gametocytes isolated from the blood smear of the surviving duck. Generally, the main species potentially pathogenic to ducks is L. simondi [9, 10, 12, 23]. The characteristic feature of L. simondi gametocytes is the dumbbell-like thickening at both ends; however, the morphology of the gametocytes observed in the present study differs from that of L. simondi [22].

Because Leucocytozoon spp. transmission commonly occurs during the summer when black fly activity is high and the prepatent period ranges 4–15 days, the infection in the ducks may have occurred between late June and early July 2024 [3]. One of the ducks that was transferred to another zoological garden underwent a blood smear examination due to lethargy in July 2024; however, no Leucocytozoon gametocytes were detected.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the detected Leucocytozoon lineages clustered together with Leucocytozoon lineages isolated from Anseriformes in Alaska and Eastern Asia including Japan and China (Fig. 4). Anseriformes migrating to Japan use the East Asian–Australasian Flyway, which covers the Western Pacific. Incidentally, Sapporo City, the study site, is located on the Eastern Palearctic Flyway, which goes between the southwest coast of Japan and the far north of Russia [1, 2, 24]. Leucocytozoon spp. may have been transmitted from infected migratory birds to domestic ducks either directly or through non-migratory Anseriformes.

Black flies, such as Simulium venustum, S. croxtoni, S. euryadminiculum, and S. rugglesi are known vectors of Leucocytozoon spp. in ducks [23]. Although none of these species of black fly species as found in the zoological garden, S. aokii and S. japonicum were captured. S. japonicum is associated with Leucocytozoon spp. infections in grouse [16, 18]. Although no reports have described S. aokii transmission of Leucocytozoon spp., it is known to feed on birds [16]. Indeed, in the current case, chicken blood was detected in captured S. aokii individuals. Based on these findings, S. aokii and S. japonicum are considered potential vectors of Leucocytozoon spp., although Leucocytozoon DNA was not detected in any of these black flies. The proportion of Leucocytozoon DNA in blood-feeding black flies was 62% in the previous study. Thus, the number of black flies examined in the present study was insufficient to obtain conclusive results regarding the vector [4].

Our case emphasizes that leucocytozoonosis should be established as a cause of mortality in ducks. To control leucocytozoonosis in Japan, species of Japanese black flies that are capable of transmitting Leucocytozoon spp. among waterfowl should be identified. Furthermore, continued surveillance of Leucocytozoon infections in waterfowl should be conducted.
