Kidney function in newly diagnosed myeloma patients: factors associated with kidney impairment and recovery
Authors: Bruno Nogueira Cesar, Walter Moises Tobias Braga, Nelson Hamerschlak, Marcelino de Souza Durão Junior
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKidney disease is a common complication of multiple myeloma (MM) and a risk factor for increased morbimortality. In this retrospective cohort study based on medical records, we analyzed the kidney function of patients with renal disease related to MM during the first year of treatment. All patients included were consecutively admitted to the outpatient services of two hospitals between January 2009 and January 2019 and met the diagnostic criteria for MM regardless of the reason for seeking medical help. We excluded patients who had kidney disease or who were on dialysis before MM diagnosis. We investigated the factors associated with renal function recovery using multivariate analysis. We evaluated 167 patients (median age of 66 ± 11.49 years). Almost half of the patients had arterial hypertension (76; 45.5%). The majority had International Staging System (ISS) grades 3 (73; 43.7%) or 2 (60; 35.9%). Seventy-four (44%) patients had an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min/1.73 m² at the time of MM diagnosis. Fifty-two patients (31%) underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). After 12 months, 4 (2.3%) patients needed dialysis, and 18 (10.7%) died. The factors associated with an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m² were anemia, hyperuricemia, 24-hour proteinuria > 1.0 g, and extramedullary plasmacytoma. However, only baseline renal function (eGFR > 60 ml/min/1.73 m2) and HSCT were associated with greater recovery of renal function at 12 months of follow-up.Keywords: Multiple myeloma, Acute kidney injury, Antineoplastic agents, Epidemiology

Introduction

Annually, multiple myeloma (MM) accounts for approximately 1.8% of new cases of cancer and accounts for 2% of cancer deaths in the United States [1]. It has a prevalence of 13 to 17% among hematological neoplasms in adults [2]. It is more common in men and in elderly individuals (median age of 66 years) [3–5]. In 2014, the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) updated the diagnostic criteria for MM and for diseases that usually precede it, such as monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and smoldering myeloma (latent myeloma) [6]. The IMWG defines kidney impairment in myeloma as a serum creatinine > 2 mg/dL or a creatinine clearance (CrCl) < 40 ml/min, as long as it is attributed to MM (light-chain cast nephropathy). According to some studies, kidney disease can occur in up to 50% of patients diagnosed with MM. In the era of conventional chemotherapy (CT), the average survival of patients with renal failure is 2 years. New therapies have increased survival, but kidney dysfunction remains an important risk factor for death [6].

Kidney impairment in patients with multiple myeloma is driven by multiple mechanisms: hypercalcemia, hyperuricemia, direct toxic effects of light chains on renal epithelial cells, cast nephropathy (myeloma kidney) and even kidney impairment due to comorbidities such as arterial hypertension and diabetes [6].

Other additional risk factors, not directly linked to myeloma, could contribute to renal impairment. Dehydration, facilitated not only by hypercalcemia but also through gastrointestinal losses, loop diuretics, infections, and sepsis, poses a significant threat. Furthermore, the use of nephrotoxic drugs such as NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, and contrast agents, coupled with concurrent administration of ACE inhibitors and ARBs, is widely acknowledged as a contributor to myeloma-related kidney impairment during presentation. Importantly, there is an increased prevalence of earlier stages of chronic kidney disease (CKD) among affected individuals, resulting in adverse outcomes such as the loss of kidney function [7].

To improve the disease and, consequently, kidney prognosis in these patients, it is imperative to initiate volume resuscitation and, when present, treat hypercalcemia with bisphosphonates immediately after diagnosis. Zoledronate and pamidronate could be associated with acute tubular necrosis and collapsing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, so they are another important reason for the close follow-up of renal evolution during this treatment [8]. Nevertheless, due to the possible rapid progression to irreversible kidney injury, other interventions must be administered early after diagnosis [7]. High doses of corticosteroids and protease inhibitors, such as bortezomib, are part of the standard regimen of treatment for patients with MM and renal failure. The inclusion of these inhibitors reduced the dependence on dialysis compared to that of conventional chemotherapy, improving the renal prognosis of these patients [9–15]. High-dose chemotherapy followed by HSCT seems to be the best treatment for MM and has led to the recovery of renal function even in patients on dialysis [16, 17]. The combination of high permeability dialyzers or plasmapheresis was associated with a greater reduction in the serum level of light chains, however, it did not clearly improve dialysis independence or survival [18–20].

Despite all of the known risk and prognostic factors associated with kidney impairment in the literature, the majority of cohort studies evaluating risk factors are for myeloma disease rather than for myeloma kidney disease in particular. In addition, the majority of the literature involved American and European populations, with few observational studies from other regions.

Objectives

In patients with renal disease related to multiple myeloma (MM), we investigated the factors associated with kidney insufficiency at presentation and the factors associated with kidney recovery during the first year of MM treatment.

Methods

Study design, setting, and ethics

This was a retrospective cohort study based on demographic, clinical and laboratory data collected from the medical records of two hospitals: Hospital Sao Paulo (HSP), a university public hospital and Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein (HIAE), a private hospital, both in Sao Paulo, Brazil.

The Institutional Review Boards of both hospitals approved the study protocol. All records were anonymized before analysis.

Participants: inclusion and exclusion criteria

The International Myeloma Work Group criteria have been used for the diagnosis of MM [2]. All newly diagnosed patients admitted between January 2009 and January 2019 were included. The data collected cover the period from diagnosing MM to completing the first year of treatment.

We excluded patients who had only one medical assessment and those who did not have laboratory or clinical data recorded in the electronic medical registries, as it would not be possible to track clinical evolution. We excluded chronic kidney disease patients (CKD 5-D) who were on dialysis due to renal disease not related to MM. We also excluded patients with concurrent neoplastic diseases.

Variables and measurements

All variables analyzed in this study were evaluated from medical records of hospitals. This study evaluated patient data only at two points in time: at initial clinical presentation and at 12 months.

At the time of initial presentation, we analyzed sociodemographic variables such as age and sex. We also assessed the following comorbidities: diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (coronary artery disease, stroke or peripheral vascular disease), heart failure and smoking.

The following laboratory data at clinical presentation were collected: Calcium, creatinine, hemoglobin, lactic dehydrogenase, uric acid, beta-2-microglobulin, albumin, ratio of involved and noninvolved light chains and 24-hour proteinuria.

At the time of diagnosis of MM, we defined kidney insufficiency as an eGFR < 60 ml/min according to KDIGO criteria (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) as in other studies [21–24]. Because this was a retrospective study and there was no histologic analysis, it is difficult to assess the onset of kidney disease and the main component of the injury involved. The baseline creatinine value was considered as the lowest value available between 8 and 365 days before MM diagnosis. Previously known CKD was defined for patients who had an abnormal baseline creatinine (that corresponds to an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1,73 m²) level for at least 3 months before the newly diagnosed patient. The eGFR was calculated by the CKD EPI equation excluding race. Creatinine and the respective eGFR were evaluated at clinical presentation and at 12 months.

We verified some clinical data specifically related to MM at diagnosis, including the presence of hypercalcemia, kidney impairment, anemia and bone lesions according to the criteria of the International Myeloma Work Group (IMWG), prognostic classification by the International Staging System (ISS) of the IMWG, isotype of myeloma (divided into IgG, non-IgG, light chain), presence or absence of extramedullary plasmacytoma, and associated amyloidosis.

Furthermore, we evaluated the presence of the following factors that could interfere with the recovery of patients with ongoing kidney injury: antineoplastic treatment with or without proteasome inhibitors, the use of antiresorptive drugs, bone marrow transplantation, radiotherapy, hospitalization, sepsis and the use of nephrotoxic drugs (amphotericin, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, antivirals and NSAIDs). Regarding antineoplastic treatment, when patients were not treated with a proteasome inhibitor-based regimen, they received treatment based on alkylating agents (such as cyclophosphamide, thalidomide and corticosteroids) or solely thalidomide and corticosteroids due to poor performance status. In the bortezomib group, only five patients received lenalidomide instead of thalidomide as the immunomodulatory agent.

Study size and dates

This study used a convenience sample covering all consecutive patients admitted to two centers over 10 years (2009–2019). There was no preliminary sample size calculation.

Statistical methods

Comorbidities and clinical and laboratory data were compared according to the presence of kidney insufficiency at clinical presentation, defined as an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m². Subgroup analysis was performed only for patients with an eGFR < 50 ml/min/1.73 m2 to assess the renal response at 12 months. The IMWG criteria for defining renal response are based on baseline eGFR and the best achieved response. The criteria are as follows: complete response (baseline eGFR < 50 ml/min with improvement to ≥ 60 ml/min), partial response (baseline eGFR < 15 ml/min with improvement to 30–59 ml/min), and minor response (baseline eGFR < 15 ml/min with improvement to 15–29 ml/min or baseline eGFR 15–29 ml/min with improvement to 30–59 ml/min) [6]. To simplify the analysis, patients with a complete or partial response were grouped into a group called “increased renal response”, and patients with a lower or absent response or patients who were on dialysis or who had progressed to death by the end of follow-up were considered to have a “reduced renal response”. The two groups were compared at 12 months according to events that could affect renal function, such as interventions received by the patient (chemotherapy, hematopoietic cell transplantation or other), and clinical events (sepsis, admission to an intensive care unit, dialysis) in the last year. These exposures were evaluated as risk or protective factors in univariate and multivariate analyses.

In multivariate analysis, binary logistic regressions were performed, based on the variables selected by the univariate analysis (those with p-values of 0.2 or less) and that had clinical relevance in the literature. The collinearity between these selected variables was also verified. The full and parsimonious model was used (through the stepwise process). The regressions yield Nagelkerke’s R2.

Continuous variables are expressed as the means, medians, standard deviations, and maximum and minimum values, and groups were compared using Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described using simple frequencies and percentages, and the two groups were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. An alpha significance level of 5% was used in inferential analyses.

The data were entered into Excel 2010 spreadsheets. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS version 22.0 statistical program.

Results

The median age of the 167 patients was 66 years, and 54% were male (Table 1). The most common comorbidities were hypertension (45%), diabetes (16%) and cardiovascular disease (16%). More than 40% of patients presented with kidney insufficiency according to the criterion of glomerular filtration rate lower than 60 ml/min/1.73 m2. Hypercalcemia was observed in 24% of patients. According to IMWG, more than half of the patients were anemic and 66% had bone lesions. With respect to MM characteristics, 91 (54%) had an IgG isotype, 42 (25%) had non-IgG immunoglobulin and 34 (20%) had light chains. Seven patients (4.2%) had associated amyloidosis. Most patients had advanced disease, with 73 patients (44%) with ISS III and 60 patients with ISS II (36%). Data from complementary exams, such as the ratio of free light chains and cytogenetic alterations, were only included for some patients’ medical records, 68 (40.7%) and 56 (33.5%), respectively. The baseline eGFR was available for 139 patients (83%).

Factors associated with greater renal response at follow-up

Overall, 74 patients (44.3%) had kidney insufficiency (eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m²), 54 (32.3%) had an eGFR < 50 ml/min/1.73 m2, and 50 (29.9%) had an eGFR < 40 ml/min/1.73 m2 at the time of diagnosis. For the analysis of the recovery of renal function, 54 individuals were included since the criterion that evaluates the renal response is based on the IMWG (eGFR < 50 ml/min/1.73m2). In this group (n = 54), 18 (33%) patients used a regimen of drugs based on a proteasome inhibitor (bortezomib). Thirteen (24%) patients underwent hematopoietic cell transplantation. At the end of the 12 months of follow-up, 4 (7%) had evolved to the need for renal replacement therapy, and 18 (33%) had died.

To simplify the analysis, we compared the group with a reduced renal response (comprising a minor renal response according to the IMWG, dependence on renal replacement therapy and death) and an increased renal response (comprising a complete and partial response according to the IMWG criteria) at 12 months of follow-up. Table 4 shows the univariate analyses of factors associated with renal response. According to the multivariate analysis, the variables associated with increased renal response were a baseline glomerular filtration rate > 60 ml/min/1.73 m² and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in the first year (Table 5).

Discussion

In this cohort, we observed that the risk factors associated with renal insufficiency (eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m²) at the time of MM diagnosis were anemia, elevated serum uric acid levels, 24-hour proteinuria greater than 1 g, and the presence of extramedullary plasmacytoma. At the end of 12 months of follow-up, a baseline eGFR ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73 m² and HSCT were associated with an increased renal response.

Risk factors linked to kidney insufficiency at presentation

We observed that anemia was associated with renal insufficiency at the time of MM diagnosis. Anemia is a common complication of myeloma and results from several underlying pathophysiological mechanisms such as the replacement of medullary progenitor cells by abnormal myeloma plasma cells, suppression of erythropoietin production by cytokines (IL1 and IL6, TNF alpha, CCL3), abnormal iron metabolism, and the anemia typical of chronic disease. In renal failure, there is a relative deficiency of erythropoietin and a decrease in the average life of red blood cells [25–27]. On the other hand, anemia can predispose patients to renal failure, as shown in hematological diseases [28, 29]. It is difficult to assess whether anemia is just a consequence of the disease or whether could play a role in the genesis of kidney injury. Hypoxia, which is associated with anemia, is one of the common mechanisms involved in the progression of chronic kidney disease and in the establishment of acute kidney injury [25].

Initially, we noticed that a higher blood level of uric acid was associated with a lower GFR. As the kidney excretes uric acid, hyperuricemia is naturally seen as a consequence of a decrease in the GFR. On the other hand, in addition to lithiasis, hyperuricemia causes acute kidney disease (especially in tumor lysis syndrome) and chronic kidney disease (due to interstitial urate deposits in the kidneys) [30]. However, even in the absence of these deposits, hyperuricemia seems to cause kidney damage through different mechanisms, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of TOLL-like receptors, endothelial dysfunction, activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and tubular injury, resulting in glomerulosclerosis, intraglomerular hypertension, acute kidney injury and tubulointerstitial disease [31–34].

Another risk factor linked to kidney insufficiency in our sample was proteinuria ≥ 1 g/24 h. Paraproteins, mainly free light chains, affect the kidneys through several mechanisms such as the formation of casts (cast nephropathy), damage to proximal tubular cells (Fanconi syndrome, acute tubular necrosis), activation of inflammatory pathways (acute and chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis, glomerulonephritis) and deposit diseases caused by intact or modified monoclonal proteins (amyloidosis) [35]. Thus, the proteinuria observed in myeloma may be due to excessive production, filtration and excretion of the monoclonal component, tubular proteinuria due to injury to proximal tubular cells, and proteinuria due to increased glomerular capillary permeability. In our study, it was not possible to assess the type of main protein present in the urine. Regardless of its origin, higher levels of proteinuria are associated with greater impairment of kidney function [36].

In our study, the presence of extramedullary plasmacytoma was associated with kidney insufficiency at the time of myeloma development. Plasmacytoma and myeloma occur in up to 17% and 20% of patients, respectively, during their presentation and disease progression [37]. Impaired kidney function and shorter survival have been observed in patients with high-risk myeloma associated with extramedullary disease [38–40]. This is the first observational study that has directly linked extramedullary plasmacytoma to worse kidney presentation in these patients.

Factors associated with better kidney prognosis at follow up

In this study, a baseline eGFR > 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 was associated with a better renal response at the end of 12 months of follow-up. Obviously, a greater renal reserve determines a greater chance of renal recovery in different scenarios of kidney injury and acute kidney disease. Hydration, withdrawal of nephrotoxins and treatment of hypercalcemia are interventions that must be implemented promptly. A rapid and early reduction in the concentration of the monoclonal component is essential for the recovery of renal function and greater survival. The use of new therapeutic regimens, such as immunomodulatory agents, proteasome inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies, increased the response and extended the progression-free survival time, in addition to increasing renal recovery and survival. A study of 3107 patients newly diagnosed with MM in the UK between 1980 and 2002 revealed early mortality in patients with renal dysfunction [41]. In a Chinese cohort, a glomerular filtration rate < 30 ml/min at presentation and a lack of renal response were associated with shorter survival [42]. In another study with 1,038 patients newly diagnosed with MM, the best hematological response and the absence of previous chronic kidney disease were associated with the best renal response [43]. However, in this cohort, only one-third of the patients who were evaluated for renal response after MM treatment received proteasome inhibitor based therapy (bortezomib), which may explain why we did not find an association between this therapy and the recovery of renal function.

Autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (AHSCT) during the first year of follow-up was an independent factor associated with good renal response in this cohort of patients. Currently, after careful analysis of patients and adjustment of the therapeutic scheme during induction, AHSCT is performed in individuals with renal dysfunction, even those on dialysis. In a study that evaluated 81 patients with MM and renal failure, 38 were on dialysis. Renal failure had no impact on the collection of stem cells nor did it compromise grafting [44]. In another study with 46 patients with MM and renal failure who were undergoing chemotherapy and HSCT, 1/3 of patients had a 25% increase in the glomerular filtration rate in relation to the initial rate [16]. In an evaluation of 54 patients with MM undergoing dialysis, other authors observed that patients who underwent ablative chemotherapy followed by AHSCT had a greater renal response and increased survival. Thirteen (24%) of these patients recovered renal function and became dialysis-independent a median of 4 months after treatment [16]. Among 370 patients with MM who underwent their first HSCT, across different levels of renal function, no differences were observed in terms of n procedure-related mortality, progression-free survival, or overall survival. Furthermore, a decline in the glomerular filtration rate greater than approximately 9% in one year was associated with lower overall survival [45].

Study limitations and strengths

This was an observational retrospective study. We do not have baseline creatinine levels or kidney function over time for all patients prior to myeloma diagnosis. Therefore, when we look at patients with impaired kidney function at the time of myeloma diagnosis, it is impossible to differentiate with certainty between those with AKI, AKD (Acute Kidney Disease), and those with AKI on CKD. Only 54 patients with acute clinical failure at presentation according to the IMWG criteria could be included in the 12-month follow-up analysis, according to the IMWG response criteria, which limits the generalizability of the findings. A small number of patients with kidney failure were treated with proteasome inhibitors, which is concurrently considered a standard initial approach.

This is the first cohort study in Latin American population to assess factors associated with kidney disease in myeloma patients. In addition, we found that extramedullary plasmacytoma is associated with kidney insufficiency at myeloma presentation and reinforced that AHSCT could be associated with better kidney outcomes in these patients.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the complex interplay of factors influencing renal insufficiency and renal recovery in patients with multiple myeloma (MM). In scenarios where the use of proteasome inhibitors is limited, high-dose chemotherapy followed by early HSCT may be the best option for greater kidney function recovery, even for those with severe disease according to ISS. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first cohort study in the Latin American population with myeloma, we hope that more observational and experimental studies involving not only specific regions but in a multicentric approach could provide a more comprehensive overview of the kidney disease in these patients.

Informed consent

It was a study mainly based on medical records. We tried to contact all patients to obtain their consent for the data to be used. However, the telephone numbers and addresses in the registries were not valid anymore for most older cases. Informed consent was obtained from the patients we were able to contact directly. All records were anonymized before analysis. The waiver of informed consent for ancient patients that we have not been able to contact has been approved by the ethics committee.
