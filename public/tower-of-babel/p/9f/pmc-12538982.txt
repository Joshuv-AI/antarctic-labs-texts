Feasibility of whole-body vibration exercise and its immediate effect on executive and physical functions in older adults with probable mild cognitive impairment

Background

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a condition characterized by cognitive decline that is often associated with aging [1]. It is characterized as a decline of mental abilities or skills, but it does not lead to significant functional impairment, distinguishing it from dementia [1, 2]. The prevalence of MCI is approximately 6% in individuals aged 60–64 and increases to approximately 25% in those aged 80–84 [1]. A meta-analysis of 41 cohort studies found that 39.2% of the cumulative proportion of MCI cases progressed to dementia [3]. In this context, MCI is considered a potential ‘target’ for interventions aimed at delaying the progression of dementia [1].

Previous studies have shown that physical exercise benefits executive and physical functions in older adults with MCI [4–8]. However, it is important to recognize that some older adults (aged 80 years of age or older) with MCI may encounter challenges in adhering to these programs [9]. These challenges may include significant limitations related to the complexity of the instructions and restrictions on free movement, often caused by excessive concern from family members on risks of getting lost or falling [10]. As a result, alternative and supplementary strategies, such as whole-body vibration training (WBV), may be needed to prevent functional decline in these older individuals [5, 11].

A recent systematic review [7] found that WBV training can effectively enhance the strength, balance, mobility, walking ability, and overall physical performance of nursing home residents aged 80 and older. The WBV training has been described as a safe, easily performed, and acceptable alternative to other exercise regimens in this group of older who cannot undertake more conventional evidence-based exercises [7, 12]. Notwithstanding, the response to WBV training may vary compared to community-dwelling older adults [8]. To date, no studies have investigated whether WBV training is well-tolerated and free from discomfort for community-dwelling individuals over 80 with MCI.

This study aimed to investigate the feasibility of a WBV training protocol in community-dwelling older adults with probable MCI. In addition, we assessed the immediate effects of a single WBV session on executive and physical functions to contribute to future strategies for health promotion and dementia prevention in this population. This study and its findings may contribute to existing literature and knowledge by enhancing the understanding of the changes promoted by an immediate (acute) WBV session on executive and physical functions, thereby providing substantial insights for establishing therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Design study

The present study was a single-group clinical trial with a pre and post-intervention design aimed at investigating the feasibility of the WBV training. The research was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal dos Vales do Jequitinhonha e Mucuri (number 5.587.874), registered in the Brazilian Clinical Trials Registry (REBEC: RBR-9sbdbg7) https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-9sbdbg7). Moreover, it was carried out according to the Declaration of Helsinki [13]. All participants signed a written informed consent. The present study was edited following the checklists for randomized pilot and feasibility trials CONSORT [14].

Participants

The study's target population comprised community-dwelling older adults (80 years or older) who were registered in primary care units in Diamantina, Minas Gerais, Brazil. The inclusion criteria were: Indication of mild cognitive impairment based on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), considering cutoff point values ≤ 26 [15], and being considered insufficiently active (< 150 min of physical activity per week) according to the Active Australia Questionnaire (AAQ) [16]. The exclusion criteria were participants with any contraindications to WBV, including knee or hip prosthesis, cardiac pacemaker, recent fracture, recent major surgical operation, acute thrombosis or its high risk, acute musculoskeletal inflammation, gall or bladder stones, hernia, cardiac or other systemic disease not well balanced with medical treatment, tumor, diabetic neuropathy, severe vertigo, or epilepsy. Those who had a hearing disorder and/or severe visual impairment, Illiterates, and individuals with walking problems that could interfere with physical function tests were also excluded. As this is a feasibility study, sample size calculation is not required since one of the objectives is to estimate the recruitment rate and synthesize data that determine the sample size for a more conclusive study.

Procedures

Older adults were recruited directly in their homes and invited to participate in the study. Thereafter, applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the eligible participants underwent the scheduled assessments in the laboratory on five different days. On the first day, participants signed the written informed consent and answered the clinical health questionnaire (supplementary materials). In addition, the participants underwent body composition measurements using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry – DXA (Lunar Radiation Corporation, Madison, Wisconsin, USA, model DPX), to assess of lean mass, and bone mineral density. On the second day, participants were assessed for executive and physical functions (baseline). On the third day, participants familiarized themselves with the vibratory platform and adopted the positioning performed during the experimental session. This was done to avoid interference caused by the anxiety generated by performing an exercise that is different from the conventional one. The experimental protocol and executive function tests were conducted over four days following a 48-h familiarization period., The acceptability scale was administered immediately after the WBV training (Post-intervention 1). On the fifth day (48 h later), the experimental protocol was repeated, and physical functional tests and acceptability were applied immediately after the WBV training (Post-intervention 2). The same evaluator performed all outcome assessments and the WBV training.

Measures

The feasibility (primary outcome) involved the analysis of recruitment, intervention, acceptability, safety, measurement, and time [17, 18]. The recruitment feasibility was determined by calculating the eligible participants with probable MCI who consented to participate from the population admitted to local community health centers. The feasibility was assessed through records using the number of aged people with probable MCI at selected local community health centers for eligibility, the number of eligible individuals, and ultimately, the number of those included [18]. The intervention feasibility was evaluated by assessing participant adherence to the intervention (support for positioning to receive whole-body vibratory stimulation, feedback, and progression throughout the session), in addition to the average time spent during the session. "Participant acceptability of the intervention was assessed using a survey conducted after the intervention phase. Participants classified four statements about the intervention on a five-point acceptability scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Safety was measured by documenting fatigue, muscle pain, non-injury falls, injury-causing falls, and death. The feasibility of the measurement protocol was determined by measuring clinical outcomes in all participants.

Executive functions (secondary outcomes) were measured with 1) the Stroop Color and Word Test (SCWT), 2) the Trail Making Test (TMT), and 3) the verbal fluency test (VF). The SCWT assesses the capacity to inhibit executive function. Participants were instructed to name the ink color instead of indicating the word/character as quickly as possible within a limited time. The number of correct answers in 45 s (SCWT number) represents the results [19]. The TMT reflects components of executive function, such as visual attention and task switching. The test was applied as proposed by Wang et al. [19]. The VF provides information about the storage capacity of the semantic memory system. This test involves generating the highest number of words starting with the letters F, A, or S and the most considerable number of animal names in a fixed time [20].

Physical functions (secondary outcomes) were assessed by measuring functional performance, global muscle strength, and mobility through the application of three functional measures: 1) Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), 2) handgrip strength (HGS), and 3) dual-task. The SPPB is a composite test that includes an assessment of gait speed, a balance test, and a chair stand test. In this test, the maximum score is 12 points, and a score of ≤ 8 indicates poor physical performance [21]. The HGS was assessed using a Jamar® dynamometer, measured in kg, by an isometric contraction applied on its handles, according to the American Society of Hand Therapists (2002) [22]. The Dual-task tests were adapted from the study by Liao et al. [23], where participants were asked to walk ten meters under three conditions. The spatial–temporal parameter, including the velocity (m/s), was documented in each trial, and the data was calculated based on the average of the three trials.

Intervention

Each day, all participants received the intervention at the same time, ensuring it was administered simultaneously. The training protocol was based on previous studies that utilized WBV in the aged population or groups with chronic conditions who faced challenges and/or limitations in physical activity [24, 25]. The training protocol consisted of five sets of 1-min static squats (isometric knee flexion at 20 degrees) on a vertical vibration platform (FitVibe® Excel Pro, GymnaUniphy, Belgium) (Fig. 1). The knee flexion angle was measured with a handheld goniometer (Baseline Plastic 360 Degree Isom [Stfr], Winterville, GA) before each testing session. Between sets, the subjects were instructed to rest for 40 s in an orthostatic position on the platform while it was turned off.

Protocol Intervention of WBV: static squats, simulating the movement of sitting in a chair

Participants were instructed to remain with their feet on the platform and their spine, arms, and head in the instructed position (simulating the movement of sitting in a chair) [26] to minimize the resonant catastrophe. A predetermined distance between the feet was established, 14 cm to the right and 14 cm to the left of the vibration center of the platform. This position the vibration mechanical stimulation parameters consisted of 35 Hz in a frequency, an amplitude of 2 mm, and an acceleration of 3.26g gravity [27]. A physical therapist measured blood pressure, heart rate, saturation, and perceived exertion before, during, and after each intervention session.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 22.0 (SPSS Statistics; IBM, Armonk, NY). Data distribution was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive analyses were reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (continuous variables) and absolute number and percentage (categorical variables). The t-test for paired samples (normal distribution) and the Wilcoxon test (non-normal distribution) were used to compare pre and post-intervention results with a significance level of 5%.

Results

Feasibility

Between September and December 2022, three hundred twenty patients aged 80 and older were screened for eligibility. After the evaluation, forty-one (12.81%) were excluded due to some contraindication. The presence of kidneys and/or gallstones, pacemakers, or stents were the main reasons for exclusion. Four participants declined to continue after the familiarization stage with the vibratory platform. Eighteen (5.63%) participants were evaluated (Fig. 2). It is noted that all older adults completed the executive and physical tests without difficulty.

Discussion

This study evaluated the feasibility of WBV training among community-dwelling older adults with probable MCI. Although this population represents a promising target group for rehabilitation using WBV training, it is crucial to regard the considerable heterogeneity within this group, particularly regarding the medical conditions, disabilities, and ongoing treatments. The broad spectrum of alleged contraindications to vibration loading is the most significant barrier against using WBV training among older adults. Many individuals were excluded due to the ineligibility of WBV training. The reasons ranged from cardiac involvement to kidney and biliary calculi, which were the most common among the older adults in the sample used for this study. Another feasibility study involving nursing home residents identified similar issues with contraindications to vibration. The high prevalence of severe cognitive and/or mobility issues among older adults reduced the number of potentially eligible participants [12]. Thus, the findings of this study demonstrated that WBV training should be considered an alternative and supplemental strategy among healthy community-dwelling older adults with probable MCI, provided the patients have no contraindications to vibratory.

The success of any exercise intervention heavily depends on adherence levels to exercise programs in real-world settings [4]. Each participant followed the study protocol precisely without problems or interruptions. These results are consistent with other studies that used WBV training in older adults [28, 29] and showed that low intensity is well tolerated in the population [12]. However, future research should investigate long-term adherence to WBV training.

The experimental procedure was shown to be secure. No further adverse events were noted, although one participant could not tolerate the exercise, and three older adults experienced minor dizziness on the familiarization day. It was already anticipated that exposure to WBV training could have specific typical side effects, including dizziness, nausea, fatigue, and pruritus. The WBV parameters utilized, however, have already been discussed in other studies [24, 25] and are also consistent with previous studies' suggestions that safe vibrations occur within the range of 30 to 50 Hz and 2.25 to 7.98 g [30]. Furthermore, the squatting/knee-bending position significantly reduced the transmissibility of the vibration stimulation to the brain.

The time required to complete the intervention was 10 min compared to the average time spent on a physiotherapy session with conventional exercises, which is around 30 to 60 min daily. The duration of WBV training represents a small fraction of time; it stands out as a short-duration exercise alternative [24]. Additionally, the duration is under 15 min, which is considered the safe threshold for daily exposure [30, 31]. The intervention offers a distinct advantage over traditional exercise programs due to its simple instructions and movements, making it particularly suitable for individuals aged 80 and older with cognitive deficits.

Instruments were used to evaluate executive and physical functions and measure the outcomes. Both proved to be effective for measuring clinical outcomes. Screening instruments were also applied, and the AAQ showed the participants' difficulty recalling the time spent on activities performed the previous week. This difficulty can be explained once the population is studied, which consists of older adults with probable MCI. Therefore, one of the functions that can be impaired due to both the natural aging process and the cognitive state is memory, particularly the ability to recall recent information [1].

Among the clinical outcomes, only two tests showed significant improvement in physical and executive functions: the dual motor task and the Trail Making Test—part A, once comparing pre and post-intervention (immediate effect). Due to the small sample size, this data should be interpreted cautiously. These findings are consistent with recent studies, demonstrating that WBV can promote improvements in older adults' cognitive and functional aspects [32–36]. Specifically, a study showed that one WBV training session improved students' cognition and inhibition in the Stroop Color Word Test [37]. Although other studies have shown that WBV training has immediate effects on executive function, applying the tests before the intervention may have introduced a learning effect, which could have influenced the performance of the post-intervention test. Therefore, future studies should explore the efficacy of WBV training on parameters of executive and physical functions in older adults with probable MCI.

The present study was the first to demonstrate the feasibility of WBV training in community-dwelling older adults with probable MCI. Other studies have already described the use of WBV training among older adults who are under 80 years of age with MCI. The studies presented positive physical and executive outcomes [5, 6]. Given only the main point, the studies realized that very old (≥ 80 years) were institutionalized individuals [7]. Older adults aged 80 or older with MCI face certain restrictions on traditional exercise programs [10]. These restrictions include significant limitations related to the complexity of the instructions and restrictions, often due to excessive concern from family members about the risk of getting lost or falling. Therefore, for old patients, especially those with MCI, the WBV training may provide a supplemental and alternative therapeutic approach [28]. Notably, WBV training could serve as a low-intensity, short-duration exercise method that is safe for older adults and effective for MCI patients [11]. The meticulous characterization of this study's participants set our investigation apart. The gold-standard methods were used to measure physical and functional variables rather than relying on less precise indirect estimates. Despite these strengths, it is essential to acknowledge certain limitations. The main restriction identified in this study was the refusal of volunteers to participate due to the timing of the assessments, which occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic since the target population was a risk group, which may have underestimated adherence. This study's researchers emphasized the importance of conducting studies with large participants to confirm the findings and ensure broader population coverage. Furthermore, the researchers of this study performed the diagnosis of probable MCI with a screening instrument, the MoCA [15]. Although validated and presented with adequate diagnostic accuracy, it is not considered the gold standard for diagnosing MCI. The present study evaluated only the immediate effect of the intervention; therefore, investigating the effects of accumulated consecutive sessions should be a focus of future studies. Additionally, future studies must compare different age groups to determine if the intervention is more beneficial for one specific age group.

Conclusion

Older adults with probable cognitive impairment can safely engage in WBV training, provided there are no contraindications to vibration. The evaluation of the feasibility criteria demonstrated that the intervention was brief, safe, easy to perform and comprehend, and was well-received and appreciated by the participants. Additional studies with appropriate designs are required to assess the efficacy of the proposed intervention procedure for the studied population, with a focus on clinical outcomes.
