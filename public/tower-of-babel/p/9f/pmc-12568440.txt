Antenatally Diagnosed Ventriculomegaly: Postnatal Clinical Course and Neurodevelopmental Insights
Authors: Sungbo Shim, Jin Hwan Lyu, Sumin Lee, Ching-yu Lin, Yoonmi Jeong, Joo Hi Kim, Seung Hwan Baek, Jungho Han, In Gyu Song, Hoseon Eun, Min Soo Park, Jeong Eun Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAntenatal ventriculomegaly (VM), a prevalent fetal central nervous system (CNS) abnormality, ranges from isolated VM to VM with additional CNS abnormalities. This study aimed to analyze postnatal diagnoses and clinical outcomes of patients with antenatal VM to identify key prenatal factors.Materials and MethodsNeonates born at ≥35 weeks of gestation between 2010 and 2022 with antenatal VM were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were categorized as VM with other CNS abnormalities (group A, n=95), isolated VM (group B, n=40), and resolved VM (group C, n=80). CNS abnormalities identified postnatally but not prenatally were classified as group A2 (n=38). Imaging findings, clinical features, and 2-year neurodevelopmental outcomes were compared.ResultsGroup A showed higher rates of bilateral VM (78.9% vs. 50.0% vs. 27.5%, p<0.001), severe VM (>15.0 mm) (47.4% vs. 17.5% vs. 0%, p<0.001), and larger initial ventricle size [12.1 (10.0–37.5) vs. 11.4 (10.0–16.0) vs. 11.0 (10.0–14.0) mm, p<0.001] than groups B and C. In group A2, hemorrhage was the most common abnormality (34%). At 2 years of age, group A had higher rates of epilepsy, developmental delay, and cerebral palsy compared to group B. Developmental delay was noted in 40.2% of group A and 17.5% of group B.ConclusionLarger initial ventricle size in antenatal VM was associated with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes. Normal antenatal findings can obscure postnatal CNS abnormalities. Advanced imaging and postnatal evaluation are essential for timely diagnosis and perinatal counseling and management.

INTRODUCTION

Fetal ventriculomegaly (VM), defined as enlargement of the brain's ventricles, is diagnosed by prenatal ultrasound when the atrial diameter of the lateral ventricle is ≥10.0 mm.1 VM is the most commonly diagnosed fetal central nervous system (CNS) abnormality, with a reported prevalence of 0.3–1.5 per 1000 live births.2 The severity of VM is categorized based on the ventricle size measured by neuroimaging [sonography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)] as mild (10.0–12.0 mm), moderate (12.1–15.0 mm), or severe (>15.0 mm).34

The etiology of VM is highly diverse and can be categorized as abnormal turnover of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) due to obstruction, CSF overproduction, or cerebral parenchymal loss.35 Obstructive causes include aqueductal stenosis,intraventricular hemorrhage, or inflammatory processes subsequent to infection. Brain malformations, such as agenesis of the corpus callosum, migration disorders (characterized by abnormal neural placement, as seen in lissencephaly, pachygyria, and heterotopia), and brain masses (including tumors or cysts), can also contribute to VM.67 VM resulting from brain volume loss (atrophy) may be due to metabolic diseases, vascular injuries, infection, hypoxia, or infarction.6 Isolated VM refers to VM diagnosed without associated structural CNS abnormalities or genetic abnormalities.3

VM has a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations in terms of neurodevelopmental outcomes. Severe VM is often associated with CNS structural abnormalities or genetic disorders, along with poor neurodevelopmental outcomes.89 It may also be associated with hydrocephalus, a pathological condition characterized by ventricular dilation due to increased intracranial pressure.3 Conversely, even when detected incidentally, mild VM may be associated with underlying CNS abnormalities. While isolated VM generally has a more favorable prognosis than non-isolated VM,1011 several studies have reported neurodevelopmental issues when compared to the general population.1213

In this study, we reviewed more than 10 years of clinical data from a single tertiary hospital to analyze and compare prenatal imaging findings, postnatal clinical characteristics, and neurodevelopmental outcomes in infants diagnosed with VM antenatally. We aimed to evaluate the prenatal factors and postnatal outcomes in patients diagnosed with VM during the prenatal period based on the etiology of VM, and to identify factors associated with postnatal prognosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This retrospective, single-center observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital (approval no. 4-2024-0357). Newborns delivered at Severance Children's Hospital between 2010 and 2022, with a gestational age (GA) ≥35 weeks and a prenatal diagnosis of VM in the second or third trimester, were included. Infants born before 35 weeks were excluded to minimize the confounding effects of prematurity on neurodevelopmental outcomes.

VM was diagnosed by prenatal ultrasound during the second or third trimester when the atrial diameter of either lateral ventricle measured ≥10.0 mm at the level of the glomus of the choroid plexus in the axial plane. A total of 258 cases of prenatal VM were identified during the study period. After excluding 32 infants born at <35 weeks of gestation and 11 who were not evaluated postnatally, 215 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Patients were categorized into three groups based on their postnatal diagnosis: VM with other CNS abnormalities (group A, n=95), isolated VM (group B, n=40), and resolved VM without other abnormalities (group C, n=80). Within group A, patients with CNS abnormalities not identified prenatally were further categorized as group A2 (n=38).

Etiology of CNS abnormalities

The etiology of the CNS abnormalities was categorized as follows: brain malformations, hemorrhage, ischemia, volume loss, neural tube defects, congenital infections, and vascular abnormalities. In cases with multiple abnormalities, categorization was based on the most likely underlying cause.

Antenatal, postnatal, and longitudinal follow-up clinical characteristics

Antenatal information included GA and ventricle size at the time of first diagnosis, as well as the GA at which the ventricles were most enlarged. The difference between the initial and maximum ventricle sizes was also calculated. Additionally, the location of VM (bilateral or unilateral) and its severity based on its maximum size were recorded. Severity was categorized as mild (10.0–12.0 mm), moderate (12.1–15.0 mm), or severe (>15.0 mm) according to the largest ventricle measurement during pregnancy.

Postnatal characteristics included GA at birth, birth weight, head circumference at birth, Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, and Respiration (APGAR) score (at 1 and 5 minute), need for respiratory support at birth, postnatal diagnoses of the neurologic system, and the presence of other congenital anomalies unrelated to the CNS. The postnatal diagnosis of VM was confirmed by neuroimaging findings, including ultrasound and/or brain MRI. All patients underwent ltrasonography within the first 3 days of life, and follow-up scans were performed as clinically indicated. Brain MRI was generally performed during the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission period. However, when the initial postnatal ultrasound was normal or showed only mild isolated VM, MRI was occasionally omitted at the clinician's discretion.

Neurodevelopmental follow-up data included diagnoses of epilepsy, hearing impairment, visual impairment, developmental delay, and cerebral palsy assessed by the age of 2 years. As patients with VM were considered high-risk infants, follow-up was conducted until at least 2 years of age according to our institutional protocol, which allows for reliable assessment of most major neurodevelopmental domains.

Study outcomes and statistical analyses

The primary outcome of this study was to identify clinical and imaging factors, such as ventricular size, laterality, and severity of VM, that correlate with postnatal diagnosis. Secondary outcomes included investigating the clinical differences between isolated VM and VM with other antenatally undiagnosed CNS abnormalities, clarifying the etiology of CNS abnormalities related to VM, and exploring the neurological prognosis of the study population. Comparisons of antenatal ultrasound findings, clinical characteristics, and neurological outcomes among the three groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. For continuous variables, Kruskal-Wallis one-way analysis and Mann-Whitney U test were applied, as most variables were not normally distributed. Data are presented as the number of cases (percentage in each group) and median values (minimum-maximum). Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Comparison of antenatal characteristics

Antenatal ultrasound findings for each group are presented in Table 1. The median (range) GA of initial detection of VM and the GA at which ventricle size was largest were both significantly lower in group C than in groups A and B [27.0 (19.0–38.0) vs. 28.7 (16.0–38.6) vs. 28.9 (20.0–37.1) weeks, p=0.005; and 28.0 (20.0–38.0) vs. 34.0 (20.0–39.0) vs. 32.5 (20.0–37.0) weeks, p<0.001, respectively]. The median (range) initial size of VM was greater in group A than in groups B and C [12.1 (10.0–37.5) vs. 11.4 (10.0–16.0) vs. 11.0 (10.0–14.0) mm, p<0.001]. The median (range) value of maximum VM size was significantly larger in group A, followed by groups B and C [15.0 (10.0–43.0) vs. 12.0 (10.0–24.5) vs. 11.0 (10.0–15.0) mm, p<0.001]. The median (range) difference between the initial and maximum ventricle size was also greater in group A than in groups B and C [1.0 (0.0–32.5) vs. 0.6 (0.0–10.1) vs. 0.0 (0.0–3.5) mm, p<0.001]. The differences between the initial and maximum ventricle sizes in each group are shown in Fig. 2. Statistically significant differences were observed in the location and severity of VM among the three groups. Group A had a higher proportion of bilateral cases than groups B and C (78.9% vs. 50.0% vs. 27.5%, respectively, p<0.001), whereas group C had a predominance of unilateral cases. The proportion of patients with severe VM (>15.0 mm), as measured by the maximum size, was also higher in group A than in the other two groups (47.4% vs. 17.5% vs. 0.0%, p<0.001). Mortality rates did not differ among the three groups.

Comparison of postnatal characteristics

Postnatal characteristics of each group are presented in Table 2. The median (range) GA at birth was significantly higher in group C than in groups A and B [38.6 (36.1–41.3) vs. 37.9 (35.0–40.9) vs. 38.3 (35.0–40.9) weeks, p<0.001]. The median (range) birth weight was also significantly lower in group A than in group C [3125 (1960–4610) vs. 3380 (2140–4370) grams, p<0.001]. The median (range) APGAR scores were lower in group A than in group C at both 1 min [6 (1–9) vs. 7 (3–9), p=0.001] and 5 min [8 (3–10) vs. 8 (5–10), p=0.013]. The proportion of patients requiring invasive respiratory support at birth was the highest in group A, followed by groups B and C (33.7% vs. 15.0% vs. 7.5%, p<0.001). Head circumference at birth was smaller in group A than in group B [35.0 (30.5–42.0) vs. 35.6 (28.0–38.0) cm, p=0.049]. Macrocephaly was more frequently observed in group B, followed by groups A and C (60.0% vs. 42.1% vs. 36.3%, p=0.045). Other characteristics, including sex and extracranial anomalies, did not differ among the groups.

Comparison between isolated VM and CNS abnormalities without antenatal diagnosis

A total of 79 patients had no specific intracranial abnormalities detected antenatally other than VM, of which 38 had CNS abnormalities identified after birth (group A2), and 40 had isolated VM without CNS abnormalities after birth (group B). Antenatal and postnatal characteristics of groups A2 and B are compared in Tables 3 and 4. The median initial ventricular size was significantly greater in group A2 than in group B (12.5 mm vs. 11.4 mm, p=0.017). There were no other significant differences, including the median (range) difference between the initial and maximum sizes of ventricle. Postnatal characteristics did not significantly differ between the two groups in terms of GA, sex, birth weight, head circumference, APGAR score, need for respiratory support at birth, or extracranial anomalies, except for whether an MRI was performed (Table 4).

Neurodevelopment outcome at 2 years of age

Neurodevelopmental outcomes at 2 years of age were compared between groups A and B (Table 5). Patients in group A had a significantly higher prevalence of epilepsy (22.1% vs. 0.0%, p=0.001), developmental delay (40.2% vs. 17.5%, p=0.011), and cerebral palsy (20.7% vs. 0.0%, p=0.002) compared to those in group B. There were no significant differences in the incidence of hearing or visual impairment between the two groups. Group C was excluded from this analysis, as its inclusion would have limited the accuracy of prognostic comparisons.

Etiology of CNS abnormality

Of the patients diagnosed with VM, 95 had accompanying brain abnormalities (group A), representing approximately 44.2% of all patients. The classification of CNS abnormalities is shown in Fig. 3A. Brain malformations were subdivided into corpus callosum agenesis, brain masses (tumors and cysts), and other malformations, including migration disorders. Agenesis of the corpus callosum was the most common abnormality, identified in 25 patients (26.3%), followed by brain malformations and hemorrhage (24 patients each, 25.3%). White matter volume loss was identified in 9 (9.5%), congenital infection in 7 (7.4%), neural tube defect in 3 (3.2%), and ischemic damage, brain mass, and vascular malformations in 1 patient each (1.1%).

Among the 95 patients in group A, 38 were further classified as group A2. Etiologies of CNS abnormalities in group A2 included hemorrhage in 13 patients (34.2%), brain malformation in 9 (23.7%), white matter volume loss in 7 (18.4%), corpus callosum abnormalities in 4 (10.5%), infection in 3 (7.9%), ischemia in 1 (2.6%), and a brain mass in 1 (2.6%) patient (Fig. 3B).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated prenatal imaging findings, postnatal clinical characteristics, and neurodevelopmental outcomes of neonates diagnosed with VM antenatally over a decade at a single tertiary hospital. These results provide critical insights into the differentiation of VM and emphasize the significance of both prenatal imaging and multidisciplinary follow-up in predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes.

In our study, VM in group A showed a larger initial size and a maximum size that often persisted into the later stages of gestation. Additionally, patients in group A frequently presented with bilateral and severe VM in terms of ventricular size. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, such as Melchiorre, et al.,14 who reported that bilateral and severe VM cases are associated with worse outcomes.

Consistent with previous research, our study observed that infants in group A had significantly worse neurodevelopmental outcomes than those in the other groups. Pagani, et al.15 similarly reported that progression is strongly correlated with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes in VM cases. The neurodevelopmental outcomes of infants in group B, who had isolated VM, were typically better than those in group A, but they still exhibited a certain degree of risk compared to the normal population. This result aligns with the findings of Chang, et al.,10 indicating that although isolated VM typically has a better prognosis, there remains a heightened risk of developmental delays compared to infants without VM.

In this study, we classified patients with VM whose etiology was not identified prenatally but was diagnosed postnatally as group A2. Notably, hemorrhage was the most frequent abnormality detected after birth despite the initial prenatal diagnosis of isolated VM. This finding is supported by Melchiorre, et al.14 and Pagani, et al.,15 who highlighted the limitations of prenatal imaging in detecting certain CNS pathologies. Prenatal ultrasound remains the primary diagnostic tool, although it has limitations in detecting subtle CNS abnormalities. Although our study did not include in utero MRI, its use in cases of isolated VM could potentially improve prenatal diagnostic accuracy.1617 These results underscore the need for postnatal imaging and clinical evaluation to identify and manage these potential complications early.

The initial ventricular size in the A2 group was significantly larger than that in group B, suggesting that even when VM appears isolated on prenatal imaging, a larger ventricular size may indicate a higher likelihood of underlying CNS abnormalities. This observation is consistent with that of Melchiorre, et al.,14 who emphasized that larger initial ventricular sizes are associated with poorer outcomes and a greater probability of postnatal abnormalities.

Although group B had a higher mean head circumference and a greater proportion of macrocephaly than group A, ventricular size was significantly larger in group A. This suggests that increases in ventricular size may more strongly indicate underlying pathological processes, such as obstructive hydrocephalus, than head circumference alone. These findings highlight the importance of detailed neuroimaging in assessing the clinical significance of VM.

Our study observed significant differences between the initial and maximum ventricular sizes. A difference greater than 5.0 mm was noted in 23 patients (24.2%), with 5 (5.3%) exhibiting a difference greater than 10.0 mm. Among these, 1 patient was in the isolated VM group, whereas the other 4 patients had associated CNS abnormalities. The CNS abnormalities in these 4 patients included myelomeningocele, agenesis of the corpus callosum, porencephalic cysts, and brain hemorrhage. These findings suggest that significant changes in the ventricular size during pregnancy may indicate severe pathological conditions. These findings align with those of Pilu, et al.,18 who suggested that changes in ventricular size are a critical marker of underlying pathologies. This highlights the importance of routine monitoring and early intervention, particularly in cases of increasing ventricular size, to improve long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

The progression of ventricular size varied significantly among the groups. Group C showed almost no changes, group B showed minimal change, and group A exhibited the most significant differences. This pattern indicates that although the absolute size of the ventricles is important, the degree of change in size over time may be more critical in identifying pathological cases. A more significant increase in ventricular size is commonly associated with severe CNS abnormalities, reinforcing the need for close monitoring during pregnancy. Despite these differences, there were no significant differences between groups A2 and B in the change from initial to maximum ventricular size. This finding highlights the importance of in utero MRI in cases initially diagnosed as isolated VM. MRI can identify subtle CNS abnormalities not visible on ultrasound and provide more accurate predictions of neurodevelopmental outcomes.19 This approach may improve the prediction of outcomes and guide better management of affected neonates.

This study had several limitations. First, its single-center retrospective design inherently limits generalizability. The findings may not be fully generalizable to other settings or populations, as they reflect the practices and outcomes of a single institution. Although retrospective in nature, this study attempted to mitigate potential bias through consistent imaging protocols and multidisciplinary follow-up assessments, thereby enhancing data reliability. Second, neonates born before 35 weeks of gestation were excluded to minimize the confounding effect of prematurity on neurodevelopmental outcomes. However, this criterion may have excluded some severe VM cases that resulted in early delivery, introducing selection bias and potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to all severities of VM. Additionally, the limited sample size within each group hindered the ability to perform multivariate analyses to control for confounding factors, which may have influenced the interpretation of the key findings. Agarwal, et al.20 also highlighted the challenges of assessing long-term outcomes of fetal VM due to its heterogeneous nature and limited data. Moreover, differences in MRI utilization across groups may have introduced detection bias, particularly in group A, where MRI use was more frequent. As mentioned above, the decision to perform MRI was at the clinician's discretion and depended on the presence of suspected CNS abnormalities, other systemic abnormalities, or neurological problems. Due to the higher number of MRI scans performed in group A, more CNS abnormalities may have been detected compared to the other groups. This difference could have affected the results, making the abnormalities in group A appear more common. Future studies should standardize imaging protocols across groups to avoid this type of bias and ensure more accurate comparisons. Barzilay, et al.16 demonstrated that MRI could detect additional abnormalities in fetal VM, particularly in moderate or progressive cases, highlighting its utility in specific cases. Recent studies by Wang, et al.21 reported that MRI was more likely to identify additional abnormalities in early-onset VM, emphasizing the importance of thorough evaluations in progressive cases. Lastly, this study did not analyze the genetic aspects of VM, although some patients were later diagnosed with genetic syndromes associated with CNS abnormalities. This limitation arose as the rate of genetic testing varied depending on the year of birth, with most tests performed only in more recent years. Future studies should incorporate genetic analyses alongside clinical and imaging data to enable a more comprehensive understanding of VM.

Future research should focus on prospective multicenter studies to validate these findings. Considering the inherent limitations of single-center retrospective studies, multicenter collaborations would enable the data collection from a larger, more diverse patient population, thereby improving statistical power and reducing regional biases.

Furthermore, tracking long-term outcomes beyond 2 years, particularly cognitive, motor, and social development, would provide deeper insights into the impact of VM on the neurodevelopmental outcomes in children. Estrin, et al.1 identified early alterations in white and cortical matter as critical indicators of neonatal neurodevelopmental outcomes, emphasizing the necessity of longitudinal studies. Additionally, the discovery of CNS abnormalities in group A2, which were initially prenatally diagnosed as isolated VM, suggests the importance of performing postnatal MRI in cases of isolated VM. Even when brain MRIs do not reveal abnormalities, methodologies such as volumetry can aid in predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes. This observation supports the need for detailed examinations of isolated VM cases.22 These imaging techniques may uncover abnormalities not detectable prenatally. Previous studies have highlighted that third-trimester MRI, with its high diagnostic accuracy, should be strongly considered to strengthen prenatal diagnosis, particularly for detecting additional abnormalities in isolated VM cases and those suspected of progressive abnormalities.52324

This study highlights the importance of prenatal imaging and postnatal follow-up in managing and evaluating VM diagnosed during pregnancy. Larger initial ventricular sizes in patients with isolated VM were associated with a higher likelihood of additional CNS abnormalities, underscoring the need for close monitoring and comprehensive follow-up. In group A2, where isolated VM was diagnosed prenatally, postnatal MRI revealed additional CNS abnormalities, reinforcing the need for MRI in such cases.

Significant increases in ventricular size during pregnancy are associated with severe CNS abnormalities, emphasizing the importance of both absolute size and progression in clinical evaluations. Clinicians should prioritize postnatal neuroimaging and multidisciplinary assessments of VM with larger ventricular sizes or progressive changes during pregnancy. Such measures aid in the early identification of underlying pathologies and facilitate timely interventions.

Future studies, particularly multicenter prospective designs, should validate these findings and investigate long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes beyond 2 years. A comprehensive understanding of these outcomes could inform clinical guidelines and improve care for affected infants and their families.
