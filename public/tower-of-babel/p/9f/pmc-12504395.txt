Potential associated factors of postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction in elderly patients: precursor of BDNF and mature BDNF in serum
Authors: Yushan Dong, Qiaoyan Wang, Die Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the acceleration of population aging in China, the incidence of postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction (PND) will continue to rise.AimWe aim to evaluate whether the precursor of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (proBDNF) and mature brain-derived neurotrophic factor (mBDNF) are associated with PND in elderly patients.MethodsEighty-six patients who were scheduled for elective orthopedic surgery under general anesthesia were ultimately enrolled. Referring to previous studies, the patients were divided into the PND group and the non-PND group with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scale on postoperative day 7. The Spearman test was used to determine the correlations between mBDNF, proBDNF, and PND.ResultsAll the participants possessed normal cognitive function before surgery. Compared with those in the nPND group, proBDNF, mBDNF, proBDNF*mBDNF, and MoCA score (all P < 0.001) were lower in the PND group after surgery. The Spearman test showed PND was correlated with postoperative proBDNF (correlation coefficient = -0.391; P = 0.001), mBDNF (correlation coefficient = -0.295; P = 0.006), proBDNF*mBDNF (correlation coefficient = -0.479; P < 0.001), and the ratios of proBDNF (correlation coefficient = -0.388; P < 0.001) and mBDNF (correlation coefficient, -0.375; P < 0.001) from before to after surgery.DiscussionIn this study, PND was significantly associated with postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF; the diagnostic value of postoperative mBDNF combined with proBDNF was superior to that of either index alone.ConclusionsThe PND was significantly associated with postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1800017415, registered date: 07/29/2018), http://www.chictr.org.cn.Keywords: Orthopedic surgery, Elderly, Postoperative neurocognitive disorder, Brain-derived neurotrophic factor

Introduction

Postoperative neurocognitive disorder (PND) is a common complication of the central nervous system, especially in elderly patients, and mainly manifests as a decline in learning, memory, attention, and executive ability [1, 2]. Recently, Gordon et al.. reported that cognitive dysfunction was significantly associated with the new dementia, and delirious individuals could possess a 2-fold increased risk of the latter compared with those without cognitive dysfunction [3]. With the acceleration of population aging in China, the incidence of PND continues to rise, which could increase the burden on families and society to a certain extent [4–7]. It is meaningful to conduct predictive screening for PND and identify the high-risk population by related factors in advance, which aligns with the concept of enhanced recovery after surgery.

As is known, objective biological markers are more convincing than assessments based on subjective scales, and the exploration and discovery of biological markers of PND are also important for its diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. According to the previous studies, cerebrospinal fluid and blood contained a variety of biomarkers associated with impaired cognitive function. Lozano-Vicario et al.. found that lower preoperative levels of CXCL9 in serum and cerebrospinal fluid were associated with postoperative delirium in elderly patients with hip fractures [8]. Guo et al.. found that a higher preoperative level of neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in blood or a lower level of NPTX2 in cerebrospinal fluid was associated with impaired postoperative cognitive function, which may be modulated by T-tau and P-tau [9, 10]. In addition, increased levels of Aβ42, granuloprotein precursor, and alpha-synuclein in preoperative cerebrospinal fluid, as well as neurofilament light chain sNfL in serum, may also be associated with impaired cognitive function [11–14]. Recently, Nikolac et al.. found that the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) level was significantly related to the speed of cognitive decline, which could be used as a potential biomarker to predict the transformation of mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer’s disease [15].

BDNF is a protein that promotes the development, repair, and functional maintenance of neurons by regulating the growth, development, induction of differentiation, and synaptic connections of embryonic neurons. BDNF also plays an important role in maintaining normal cognitive function, in the form of precursors of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (proBDNF) and mature brain-derived neurotrophic factor (mBDNF), which are widely distributed in the central nervous system (especially in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex). It is proven that BDNF can regulate the growth of axonal branches and dendrites, improve the transmission efficiency of synapses, and help reduce the occurrence of PND [16, 17]. Chang et al.. found that patients with higher serum BDNF levels at admission had a lower risk of cognitive impairment at 3 months post-morbes [18]. Previous studies have suggested that the two common subtypes of BDNF, mBDNF and proBDNF, are closely related to cognitive functions. Moreover, proBDNF and mBDNF seem to exert two opposite effects (mechanistically) in the human body [19–22], and the mutual conversion between proBDNF and mBDNF was also previously suggested to be related to cognitive functions [23]. There were still few studies on the correlation and diagnostic value of proBDNF and mBDNF in PND after orthopedic surgery among the elderly, in which population the incidence of PND is often considered to be increased [24, 25]. It is also beneficial for more accurately diagnosing PND in elderly orthopedic patients promptly, as well as for early intervention or prevention, by involving postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF into the assessment of PND. Therefore, we designed this clinical trial to evaluate the correlation between proBDNF, mBDNF, and PND, and hypothesized that postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF are correlated with the PND after orthopedic surgery among the elderly population with high diagnostic value.

Methods

Study design and patients

This case-control study was approved by the hospital ethics committee (TJ-IR20180509) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Center (ChiCTR1800017415). Elderly patients (> 65 years old) who were scheduled for elective orthopedic surgery under general anesthesia from October 2021 to May 2022 were enrolled, regardless of gender, with ASA physical status I-III, and signed informed consent. The sites of surgery involved the hip, knee, and femur, and all the surgeries were performed by the same team. The exclusion criteria were as follows: preoperative cognitive dysfunction; contraindications of nerve block; severe heart, lung, liver, or kidney dysfunction; taking psychotropic drugs before surgery; uncooperative; massive bleeding; or transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). Referring to previous studies [26–29], the patients were divided into the PND group and non-PND group with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scale on postoperative day 7.

Anesthesia procedure and perioperative management

All the patients routinely fasted for 8 h and abstained from drinking water for 4 h before anesthesia. After the patients entered the operating room, venous access to the upper extremity was opened, and ultrasound-guided radial artery puncture was performed. ECG, SpO2, temperature, and BIS were also monitored.

The standardized anesthesia management strategy was adopted in all patients and was strictly performed by the same investigator (Qiaoyan Wang). Anesthesia was induced by intravenous injection of midazolam 0.02 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.4–0.6 µg/kg, propofol 1–2 mg/kg, and cisatracurium 0.2–0.3 mg/kg. Then, tracheal intubation was performed, and PETCO2 was maintained at 35–45 mm Hg during the anesthesia. After the anesthesia induction, the ultrasound-guided femoral nerve block (0.375% ropivacaine, 15 mL) and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block (0.375% ropivacaine, 5 mL) were routinely performed in all patients. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol 2–4 mg/kg/h, remifentanil 0.1–0.3 µg/kg/min, and sevoflurane 1%-2%, and the investigator maintained the BIS at 40–60, the blood pressure within ± 20% of the baseline value, and the heart rate at 50–100 beats/min. When the mean arterial pressure (MAP) decreases to more than 20% of the baseline value, phenylephrine 40–80 µg or ephedrine 3–6 mg was intravenously administered. When the increase in MAP was more than 20% of the basic value, urapidil 10–15 mg was intravenously administered. When the HR was less than 50 beats/min, atropine 0.3–0.5 mg was intravenously administered.

At the end of the surgery, flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg was intravenously administered, and patient-controlled intravenous analgesia was subsequently performed (sufentanil 100 µg and tropisetron 4 mg were diluted to 100 mL with normal saline). Then the patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). When the tracheal tube was removed and the Steward recovery score was > 4 points, they were transferred to the ward. If the pain score on the numeric rating scale (NRS) was ≥ 4 points (moderate-to-severe pain, which could affect the patient’s normal life, such as sleep, emotions, etc.), intravenous injection of flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg (PACU) or intramuscular injection of tramadol 100 mg (ward) was administered and repeated if necessary.

BDNF assay methods

In our study, proBDNF and mBDNF were measured one day before and one day after the surgery, respectively. Peripheral blood samples were collected and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 10 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was transferred to new, corresponding EP tubes (pyrogen- and endotoxin-free), and the serum was stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

The mBDNF and proBDNF levels were quantified using commercially available human brain-derived neurotrophic factor enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Shanghai Lanpai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., catalog numbers: mBDNF, LP-H05669; proBDNF, LP-H05671). The assays were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The detection procedures for both proBDNF and mBDNF were essentially identical. In brief, all standards and samples were loaded in duplicate into the microplate wells. For standard wells, 50 µL of standard solution was added; for sample wells, 10 µL of test sample and 40 µL of sample diluent were added, while blank wells remained unspiked. Subsequently, 100 µL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibody was added to each well. The plate was sealed with adhesive tape and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Following five wash cycles, the plate was inverted and the exterior surface was carefully dried using clean absorbent paper. Then, 50 µL of chromogen A and 50 µL of chromogen B were added sequentially to each well, followed by gentle mixing and incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of stop solution to each well. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm within 15 min using a fully automated microplate reader (Sunrise, Switzerland, model F50). The concentrations of proBDNF and mBDNF were calculated based on the corresponding OD values. The sensitivity of this assay was 1.0 pg/mL.

Evaluation of PND

In this study, the PND was evaluated by the MoCA scale. The MoCA scale mainly evaluates delayed verbal recall, visuospatial/executive function (including the clock drawing test), language ability, attention/concentration, and orientation. The total score ranges from 0 to 30 points. One point is added if the year of education is ≤ 12 years, and the maximum score should be ≤ 30 points. A total score ≥ 26 points was considered normal, and a score lower than this threshold was considered to indicate PND. All the patients were routinely evaluated on postoperative day 7 by face-to-face interview or video telephone. The PND assessment was carried out by specially trained researchers and the assessors were unaware of the occurrence situation (i.e., the grouping situation) of PND beforehand. Each researcher in our team received uniform training on the use of the cognitive function scale before enrollment and was specially guided by a specific neurologist (Hongmei Ding).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the correlations between mBDNF, proBDNF, and the PND. The secondary outcome measures were as follows: intraoperative remifentanil dosage, LOS in PACU and after surgery, MoCA scores on postoperative day 7, perioperative mBDNF and proBDNF, postoperative NLR, incidence of postoperative adverse events (including nausea and vomiting, rescue analgesia, and sleep disorders), etc.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated by PASS 15.0. According to the preliminary survey, the correlation coefficient between BDNF and PND after surgery was approximately − 0.341. A total of 86 patients included had a power of 90%, with an α set to 0.05. Considering the loss to follow-up (about 10%), 96 patients were ultimately included.

Statistical analysis

For quantitative data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed for normality, and the Levene test was performed for homogeneity of variance. The normally distributed data were analyzed by the Student’s t test, and were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation; the other data were analyzed by the Mann-Whitney U test, and were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Qualitative data were analyzed by the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, expressed as the number of patients (%).

The correlations between proBDNF, mBDNF, proBDNF/mBDNF, proBDNF*mBDNF, and PND were analyzed by the Spearman test. The diagnostic value of mBDNF, proBDNF, and proBDNF*mBDNF for PND was determined by generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated. Also, the univariate logistic regression analysis was performed based on education duration, anesthesia duration, remifentanil dosage, and postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF, and then variables with P < 0.05 were included in the multivariate logistic regression models to explore the influencing factors for the PND, expressed as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidential interval (95% CI). Multicollinearity was analyzed by the variance inflation factor (VIF) method, with a VIF > 5 indicating the presence of multicollinearity. Bilateral tests were used in all analyses, with P < 0.05 indicating statistically significant differences. SPSS 25.0 software was used for all the statistical analyses.

Results

In this study, 169 patients were evaluated for eligibility, and 73 patients were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria (35 patients refused to participate in the trial, 1 patient took psychotropic drugs before surgery, 1 patient had preoperative cognitive dysfunction, and 36 patients were younger than 65 years old). During the trial, 3 patients were transferred to the ICU after surgery, 4 patients refused to continue postoperative follow-up, and 3 patients did not complete the cognitive evaluation. Finally, 17 patients in the PND group and 69 patients in the nPND group were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart

All the participants possessed normal cognitive function before surgery. There were no significant differences in age, gender, body weight, BMI, ASA physical status, preoperative comorbidities, preoperative MMSE score, surgery duration, operative site, postoperative NLR, and postoperative adverse events between the two groups (all P > 0.05). However, the education duration (9.0 [6.0, 9.0] year vs. 6.0 [6.0, 6.0] year; P = 0.002), anesthesia duration (192.7 ± 82.2 min vs. 144.4 ± 53.5 min; P = 0.033), and intraoperative remifentanil dosage (2.5 ± 1.0 mg vs. 1.8 ± 0.8 mg; P = 0.008) in the PND group were significantly higher than those in the nPND group. The length of stay (LOS) in PACU (45.0 [40.5, 45.0] min vs. 25.0 [25.0, 30.0] min; P < 0.001) and after surgery (12.9 ± 2.0 day vs. 8.7 ± 3.6 day; P < 0.001) in the PND group was significantly longer than that in the nPND group (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, we found that postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF were significantly correlated with the incidence of PND after orthopedic surgery among the elderly population. In addition, the ROC curve analysis showed a high diagnostic value of postoperative proBDNF, mBDNF, and proBDNF*mBDNF for PND, and the logistic analysis suggested postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF were the independent influencing factors for PND.

The MMSE and MoCA scales are most commonly used to assess cognitive function status. Pinto et al.. reported that the MoCA scale was more sensitive to diagnose mild cognitive impairment than the MMSE scale [30]. In our study, the MoCA scale was used for the evaluation of the PND. We considered the following possibilities: (i) Compared with the MMSE scale, the MoCA scale also includes object recognition tests and number span tests to evaluate attention, and adds connection tests and clock drawing tests to evaluate executive function; (ii) The weight of cognitive function in the MMSE scale is reflected in orientation, computation, and immediate memory, and the impairment of these functions may not be obvious in the early stage of cognitive dysfunction, while the weight of these items is reduced in the MoCA scale. In this study, the postoperative MoCA score was found to be significantly correlated with postoperative proBDNF, mBDNF, and proBDNF*mBDNF. This suggests that the greater the reduction in BDNF levels after surgery, the more severe the occurrence of PND is expected to be. In addition, the decline in BDNF levels after surgery was significantly greater in the PND group. This phenomenon is interesting and may be attributed to the neuroinflammatory process [31, 32], hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysregulation, or impaired neurotrophic signaling [33].

Neuroinflammation induced by surgery and anesthesia could over-activate N-methyl-D-aspartic acid receptors and calpsins, downregulate BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway, and lead to dendrite loss, apoptosis, and cognitive impairment [34, 35]. Active neurogenesis in the hippocampus (the process of generating functional neurons from precursor cells) could generate neural stem cells, maintain an enriched environment (EE) for neuronal proliferation and differentiation, promote neuronal repair, and help improve cognitive function. Persistently elevated levels of IL-1β in the hippocampus could also lead to elevated levels of TRPM2 by enhancing the inflammatory response, which promotes impaired cognitive function [36]. Previous studies have shown that surgery and anesthesia can induce microglia to activate and release IL 1β, causing the downregulation of BDNF in the hippocampus and a decline in cognitive function [37]. We speculate that BDNF may be an important mediator between neuroinflammation and neurocognitive impairment. In this study, PND appeared to be associated with higher NLR, which is considered a simple, low-cost marker of inflammation [38].

The BDNF family consists of three main subtypes: proBDNF, mBDNF, and the pro-peptide of brain-derived neurotrophic factor. The level of the BDNF subtype could provide a more accurate diagnostic method for PND [39]. According to the previous study, midbrain astroglia-derived neurotrophic factor could improve the cognitive impairment associated with sevoflurane anesthesia by inhibiting microglial activation and neuroinflammation [40]. Hovens et al.. found the expression of BDNF in the peripheral blood of patients with PND was significantly lower than that in non-PND patients [41]. In our study, we also found that proBDNF and mBDNF after surgery were significantly correlated with the incidence of PND. We speculate that this phenomenon may be related to BDNF promoting neural repair and improving cognitive function by nourishing nerves, regulating synaptic plasticity, and alleviating neuroinflammation [42]. To exclude the influence of PND-related confounding factors, such as education duration, anesthesia duration, and remifentanil dosage, we constructed the multivariate regression models and further verified that postoperative mBDNF and proBDNF were the independent risk factors for the PND. In view of the conditions and costs of monitoring proBDNF and mBDNF, we suggest that medical staff give priority to the screening of people at high risk of PND by the MoCA scale, and proBDNF and mBDNF can be monitored early after surgery as conditions permit. With the introduction of postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF in the evaluation of PND, the related prevention, early intervention, or timely diagnosis of PND could be more accurate.

BDNF is distributed in both the brain and peripheral blood and plays an important role in maintaining normal cognitive function [16]. BDNF in the brain is mainly synthesized in neurons and glial cells, can enter the peripheral blood through the blood-brain barrier, and is positively correlated with changes in serum BDNF. Therefore, the peripheral BDNF level could reflect changes in the level of BDNF in the brain to a certain extent [43, 44]. Compared with cerebrospinal fluid, peripheral blood is easier to obtain and safer. In our study, people who developed PND had lower serum levels of proBDNF and mBDNF, which may suggest that BDNF in the brain was also significantly reduced in this group of people. Analysis of the changes in proBDNF and mBDNF before and after surgery suggested that the decreases in proBDNF and mBDNF levels were associated with the increase in the occurrence of PND. However, Lu et al.. found proBDNF could participate in the induction of LTD through p75NTR and in the negative regulation of synaptic plasticity and cognitive function, leading to reduced synaptic formation and neuron apoptosis, and promoting the occurrence of cognitive dysfunction [19]. Moreover, the injection of TAT-Pep5, an intracellular p75NTR inhibitor, into the hippocampus of older mice blocked the interaction between proBDNF and the guanosine diphosphate dissociation inhibitor, a homolog of the Ras gene family, and significantly improved cognitive function [20]. This phenomenon was interesting. Considering that both mBDNF and proBDNF showed varying degrees of decline in the perioperative period, this may be related to the surgical stress experienced from the surgery [39]. Our study found proBDNF was more valuable than mBDNF in the diagnosis of PND. However, there is no clear biological mechanism for reference or explanation of the phenomenon that the correlation between mBDNF and PND after surgery is stronger than that of proBDNF. Given that proBDNF and mBDNF are inseparable and mutually balanced in the same time and space, we believe that proBDNF/mBDNF may play a more important role in the occurrence of PND, and postoperative proBDNF combined with mBDNF seemed to be more scientific in the diagnosis of PND [45].

The signaling pathways of proBDNF and mBDNF involved in the occurrence and development of cognitive dysfunction are different. The proBDNF is mainly bound by the p75 neurotrophic factor receptor, which significantly inhibits the proliferation of neural stem cells, reduces neuronal differentiation, causes the collapse of synaptic structure, induces neuronal apoptosis, and inhibits neurogenesis [21]. However, the mBDNF is mainly bound by the tyrosine kinase B receptor, which promotes neuronal survival, increases synaptic plasticity, and promotes learning and memory processes [22]. When neurons in the brain function normally, proBDNF and mBDNF are in a state of homeostasis, promoting synaptic growth and regulating the process of nerve regeneration. However, the unbalanced conversion of mBDNF to proBDNF may lead to cognitive decline, which may provide a more precise target for improving cognitive function [23]. In our study, we found proBDNF/mBDNF seemed to be lower (not significantly) in the non-PND group compared to the PND group. Referring to the study of Zhang et al.., we are more inclined to believe that the unbalanced conversion of proBDNF to mBDNF was significantly associated with the early stage of PND, although both mBDNF and proBDNF were lower after surgery than before surgery, as previously mentioned [45].

Limitations

Firstly, the sample size of this study was not large, which may affect the reliability and generalizability of our results. In the future, large-scale, multicenter clinical trials should be carried out to further verify the effects of proBDNF and mBDNF on the PND. Secondly, considering that compared to cerebrospinal fluid, peripheral blood is easier to obtain and safer to handle. Therefore, the BDNF in the brain was not detected in this study, and the influence of proBDNF and mBDNF on PND and its specific mechanism needs to be further explored in the future. Thirdly, previous studies have shown that BDNF plays a key role in the pathogenesis and treatment of depression [46]. This study did not explore the interaction between BDNF and the psychology of the elderly population, although the excluded patients involved individuals who took psychotropic drugs before surgery. The relationships among mental diseases, BDNF, and PND should be further explored in the future. In addition, this study did not conduct an in-depth analysis of other influencing factors of PND( such as nerve block, anesthetic exposure, educational level, anesthesia duration, gender, surgical site or baseline inflammatory status, etc.), as well as not detect the neurofilament light chain and the tau protein, which were mainly associated with long-term cognitive dysfunction [47]. Finally, the development of cognitive dysfunction at 30 days or 1 year after surgery was not tracked. The relationship between early postoperative BDNF and long-term postoperative cognitive impairment remains to be further studied. Considering the relatively limited time points for monitoring proBDNF and mBDNF in this study and the fact that they are not completely consistent with the PND assessment time points, it is only reasonable to consider proBDNF and mBDNF to be strongly correlated with PND. However, regarding proBDNF and mBDNF as biomarkers of PND requires further exploration in the future, especially regarding the time points for detecting proBDNF and mBDNF and evaluating PND, and these need to be increased and kept as consistent as possible.

Conclusions

In the context of this study, we found that the PND after orthopedic surgery among the elderly population was significantly associated with postoperative proBDNF and mBDNF, which presented a high diagnostic value for PND. In addition, the diagnostic value of postoperative mBDNF combined with proBDNF was superior to that of either index alone. Our study may provide a new idea for the early detection or screening of PND high-risk groups.
